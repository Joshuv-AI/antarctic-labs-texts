Identifying the Factors With Later Standing Acquisition in Patients With a Right-Hemisphere Stroke: A Cross-Sectional Study Using POSTIM Data
Authors: Karim Jamal, Stéphanie Leplaideur, Chloé Rousseau, Etienne Foussat, Solène Painchaud, Lisa Viallard, Isabelle Bonan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify factors associated with later standing acquisition in subacute post-stroke patients with right-hemisphere stroke.MethodsThis cross-sectional study used baseline data from the POSTIM multicenter randomized controlled trial (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT01677091), involving eight rehabilitation units in France. Included patients were patient aged 18–80 with a first right supratentorial stroke (within 9 months), who could stand unassisted for at least 30 seconds with eyes closed but had <40% body weight bearing on the paretic leg during posturography. Evaluations included motor weakness, spasticity (triceps surae, quadriceps, adductors), epicritic and arthrokinetic sensitivity, spatial neglect, body representation, and balance. Logistic regression identified factors associated with later standing acquisition (>3 months).ResultsAmong 86 participants (mean age 59.7±10.3 years; 68.6% male), 49 (57%) acquired standing capacity within 3 months, while 37 (43%) did so later. Those with later standing acquisition showed greater spasticity in all muscle groups (p<0.02), more severe motor weakness (p=0.003), and impaired arthrokinetic sensitivity (p=0.02). Balance was significantly worse in this group (p<0.005). Multivariate analysis identified three independent factors of later standing acquisition: spatial neglect (odds ratio [OR]=3.22; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.09–9.51; p=0.03), ischemic stroke (OR=3.27; 95% CI, 1.08–9.90; p=0.04), and quadriceps spasticity (OR=1.98; 95% CI, 1.17–3.35; p=0.01).ConclusionIn right-hemisphere stroke, ischemic etiology, quadriceps spasticity, and spatial neglect are significantly associated with later standing acquisition. These factors may be useful for identifying patients at risk of later standing acquisition and for guiding future interventional research.Keywords: Stroke, Perceptual disorders, Muscle spasticity, Standing position

GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

INTRODUCTION

Among the various consequences of stroke, the challenge of regaining the ability to stand is particularly significant, as it is essential for restoring independent mobility [1]. Difficulties in achieving this standing capacity can lead to several adverse consequences, including impaired transfers and walking ability, increased risk of falls, and reduced participation in daily activities [2]. Understanding how post-stroke deficits interact with later standing acquisition is essential for optimizing rehabilitation protocols and patient outcomes.

Several studies have investigated different factors influencing post-stroke standing capacity, i.e., ability to stand unassisted, at the acute stage or sub-acute, including lower limb motor weakness [1,3], spasticity [4], spatial neglect [5-7]. However, discrepancies exist in the literature regarding the relative impact of these factors, likely due to variations in outcome measures, sample sizes, and patient heterogeneity and stroke stage. Moreover, patients with right-hemisphere stroke (RHS) appear to face unique challenges. Notably, they require more time to achieve a stable standing position compared to those with left-hemisphere stroke (LHS) [8,9]. This delay can be attributed to the crucial role of the right hemisphere in multisensory integration and spatial perception [10-12]. Lesions in the right hemisphere are frequently associated with disturbances in spatial body representation, manifesting as both egocentric (subjective straight ahead [SSA], longitudinal body axis [LBA] [12,13]) and allocentric reference (subjective visual vertical [14]) distortions and spatial neglect impairments [6], which can affect standing and balance control [5,13], increasing the complexity of rehabilitation.

To date, no study has specifically focused on the factors affecting later standing acquisition in subacute patients with RHS. In this context, our study aims to identify key factors associated with later standing acquisition using data from the POSTIM database. A deeper understanding of these factors could optimize rehabilitation strategies and improve personalized care. Recognizing that the first three months post-stroke represent a critical period for motor neuroplasticity and recovery [15,16], our population was stratified into two groups: (1) those who regained standing capacity within three months, and (2) those who achieved it later. We hypothesized that persistent disturbances in spatial body representation and spatial neglect would be major factors of later standing acquisition.

METHODS

Design and ethics

This study is a secondary cross-sectional analysis of baseline data from the multicentric randomized controlled trial conducted in adult physical and rehabilitation medicine units across eight hospitals in France. The original trial (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT01677091) evaluated the effects of prism adaptation and neck-muscle vibrations on weight-bearing asymmetry (WBA) in patients with RHS. This analysis used only pre-intervention data. The participants were stratified retrospectively based on the timing of standing capacity acquisition, and all independent variables were measured prior to this classification. The study adheres to the STROBE guidelines for cross-sectional studies and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Poitiers-France III West (N°05/12/16) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Each participant received information about the protocol and signed a consent form before the study began in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

Patients aged between 18 and 80 years who were admitted to one of the eight physical and rehabilitation medicine units with a first right unilateral supratentorial stroke within 9 months before study inclusion were eligible for inclusion. Patients were included in the original study as soon as they were to be able to stand upright unassisted for at least 30 seconds with their eyes closed and have less than 40% of their body weight supported by the paretic lower limb during a posturographic assessment. Patients with orthopedic, rheumatologic, or visual disorders affecting their postural balance were excluded. The original study’s primary outcome was WBA.

Outcomes

The outcomes analyzed in this study were based solely on data collected at baseline, prior to the intervention, in the original trial. All assessments were performed as soon as the patients were eligible to be included, rather than at a fixed post-stroke time point.

Assessment of post-stroke deficiencies

Age (in years), gender (male/female), post-stroke delay (in days) and etiology of stroke lesion (ischemic/hemorrhagic) were reported. The Motricity Index ranging from 0 to 100 [17] with higher scores indicate better motor strength was used to assess motor weakness of the paretic lower limb, and spasticity scores of the paretic triceps surae, quadriceps and adductor according to the Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS) [18]. The MAS was treated as a continuous variable in the logistic regression model, consistent with the analytical approach applied to all clinical variables in order to preserve the full range of variability and maintain a uniform statistical framework. Scores were coded numerically from 0 to 5, with grade 1+ assigned a value of 2, grade 2=3, grade 3=4, and grade 4=5. Two clinical examinations were performed to assess the patient’s sensitivity. The examinations included epicritic and arthrokinetic sensitivity tests on the knee, ankle, and toes of the lower paretic limb. Each test was scored on a scale of 0 to 2, with 0 indicating anaesthesia, 1 indicating impairment and 2 normal sensitivity, after comparison with the non-paretic side. The total score for the lower paretic limb was calculated by summing the sub-scores.

Spatial neglect and body representation assessments

Four tests were conducted to assess spatial neglect: the Bell Cancellation Test [19], the 20-cm Line Bisection [20], the Fluff Test [21], and the Catherine Bergego Scale [22]. Spatial neglect was defined by the presence of at least three positives out of four tests. This conservative criterion was chosen to increase diagnostic specificity by requiring consistent impairment across multiple complementary assessments (paper-and-pencil, personal neglect, and functional evaluation), in line with recommendations advocating multimodal assessment of neglect rather than reliance on a single test [23,24]. Two tests were performed to assess spatial body representation: the SSA [25], and the LBA [13]. The SSA was conducted with patients in a seated position in front of a measuring table. Patients were blindfolded and instructed to indicate the direction of “straight ahead” with their right hand, dividing the space into two parts. This was done in six starting positions, sequenced in the following order: 30°, -20°, 10°, -30°, 20°, and -10°. LBA was performed with the patient lying supine in complete darkness. A light strip was placed in front of them, and their head, trunk, and lower limbs were aligned and supported by cushions. Patients were instructed to indicate when the strip was parallel to their body axis using the same sequence of starting positions. No visual reference other than the wand was available. For both the SSA and LBA, results were recorded in degrees. The Algebraic error (mean of signed deviations) was calculated.

Balance assessments

Balance was evaluated using the Postural Assessment Scale for Stroke (PASS) [26], the Timed Up and Go (TUG) [27], the Berg Balance Scale (BBS) [28], and WBA, which is defined as the percentage of body weight supported by the affected lower limb, was measured using posturography (Feetest6©; Techno Concept). The participants stood with both feet on the platform, looking straight ahead (or closing their eyes, depending on the instructions given). The eyes-closed condition was used to remove visual input in order to assess visual dependence and the ability to maintain postural control without visual information [29]. They had to remain as still as possible during the 30-second recording. The average of the two trials with eyes open and the two trials with eyes closed was used to calculate WBA [30].

Statistical analysis

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Quantitative variables were expressed as means±standard deviation or medians with interquartile ranges, and categorical variables as numbers and percentages. Group comparisons (standing acquired ≤3 months vs. >3 months) were performed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and t-tests or Mann–Whitney tests for continuous variables. Logistic regression identified factors associated with later standing acquisition. Balance assessments were not entered into the multivariate logistic regression model due to their conceptual proximity to the outcome (standing capacity), in order to avoid collinearity and to focus on independent underlying impairments associated with later standing acquisition. Variables with p<0.20 in univariate analysis were included in a multivariate model with stepwise backward selection. Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis. For each univariate analysis, all available data were used, leading to variable sample sizes across analyses. Analyses were performed using SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute).

RESULTS

Participants

Eighty-nine individuals were enrolled between May 2013 and May 2018. After excluding three patients due to missing data on post-stroke delay, 86 participants (68.6% male; mean age 59.7±10.3 years) were included. Ischemic stroke was observed in 56 (65.1%) of cases and hemorrhagic stroke in 30 (34.9%). Mean Motricity Index for the paretic lower limb was 55.3±21.1 and other assessments are detailed in Table 1. Out of the 86 participants, 49 patients (57%) achieved standing capacity within three months, while 37 (43%) did so after three months (Fig. 1).

Standing capacity acquisition

Univariate analysis

The later standing acquisition group exhibited significantly higher spasticity of the triceps surae (1.4/5±1.1 vs. 2.1/5±1.4; p=0.02), the quadriceps (0.6/5±0.8 vs. 1.2/5±1.2; p=0.01) and the adductor (0.7/5±1.1 vs. 1.5/5±1.4; p=0.003), greater motor weakness (61.0/100±17.5 vs. 47.4/100±23.3; p=0.003), and more impaired arthrokinetic sensitivity (4.9/6±1.6 vs. 3.9/6±2.0; p=0.02) compared to the early group. Additionally, balance assessments (BBS, PASS, TUG, WBA) were significantly worse in the later standing acquisition group (all p<0.005) (Table 1).

Multivariate analysis

The multivariate model included several variables such as age, stroke etiology, spasticity, motor weakness, epicritic and arthrokinetic sensitivity tests, spatial neglect assessment (Table 2). Three factors were independently associated with later standing acquisition group: the ischemic stroke etiology (odds ratio [OR]=3.27; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.08–9.90; p=0.04), spasticity of the quadriceps (OR=1.98; 95% CI, 1.17–3.35; p=0.01), and spatial neglect (OR=3.22; 95% CI, 1.09–9.51; p=0.03) (Fig. 2). No other variables were independently associated with the delay of standing acquisition.

DISCUSSION

This study investigated factors associated with later standing acquisition in patients with RHS. Our first finding indicates that only fifty percent of our cohort achieved standing capacity within three months (Fig. 1), and 3 out of 4 did so by four months. This recovery period is notably longer than the one-month timeframe generally reported in the literature for cohorts similar to ours neurorehabilitation unit [31,32]. While the eyes closed condition during the assessment may partially explain this extended recovery time, it does not fully account for the delay. A more plausible explanation lies in our inclusion of RHS patients, who are known to demonstrate more pronounced WBA and require more time to achieve postural stability then patients with LHSs [8,9].

Patients with ischemic stroke were three times more likely to experience later standing acquisition (OR=3.27). Moreover, a one-unit increase in spasticity of the quadriceps is significantly associated with a higher probability of prolonged recovery time (OR=1.98). Lastly, the presence of spatial neglect is linked to a threefold higher likelihood of later standing acquisition beyond three months post-stroke (OR=3.22).

Our results regarding stroke etiology diverge from previous studies [33,34], which reported more severe functional impairments in patients with hemorrhagic stroke compared to those with ischemic stroke. However, our sample appears to be representative of stroke etiology, with approximately two-thirds of participants having ischemic stroke and one-third hemorrhagic stroke [33]. Notably, in our sample, patients with ischemic stroke took longer to acquire an upright posture. This discrepancy is not entirely unexpected, as although hemorrhagic stroke often presents with more severe deficits at admission, these patients tend to have a greater recovery potential over time due to the resolution of edema [34]. Our assessment performed at the time patients attained the standing position, rather than at the onset of rehabilitation may have allowed the patients with hemorrhagic stroke to reach a similar level of performance as the ischemic stroke group. From a clinical perspective, although stroke etiology itself cannot be modified, these findings highlight the importance of careful monitoring of patients with ischemic stroke, who may be at higher risk of later standing acquisition.

Spasticity emerged as an unfavorable factor for later standing acquisition in our study, consistent with Singer et al. [4], who reported that lower-limb spasticity can impede the recovery of standing balance control over time. While our cohort with later standing acquisition exhibited increased spasticity across triceps surae, quadriceps, and adductor muscle groups, multivariate analysis identified only the spasticity of the quadriceps as a statistically significant factor. This finding contrasts with Singer et al.’s [4] observation that ankle spasticity primarily compromised balance recovery. This discrepancy may stem from methodological differences, as Singer et al. [4] specifically included patients with ankle spasticity regardless of quadriceps involvement. Interestingly, although quadriceps hypertonicity may initially appear to promote upright stability by maintaining knee extension, it may paradoxically limit the controlled knee flexion required for weight transfer, resulting in abnormal joint mechanics during standing [35]. Spasticity fundamentally disrupts voluntary motor control and limits the generation of compensatory postural adjustments essential for maintaining upright posture [36]. While some heterogeneity exists in the literature, Genthon et al. [37] did not identify spasticity as a principal factor, potentially reflecting differences in study populations, assessment protocols, or evaluation timing, our findings emphasize the clinical relevance of early spasticity assessment as a factor associated with later standing acquisition and support further investigation of its role in postural control during subacute stroke rehabilitation.

Consistent with prior studies [6,7], our results indicate that spatial neglect is significantly associated with later standing acquisition in patients with RHS. Although our cross-sectional design cannot establish a direct causal relationship, this association underscores the need to systematically assess and address spatial neglect in rehabilitation programs for these patients. It is also important to note that only individuals who could eventually stand unassisted were included; therefore, patients who never achieved standing (potentially due to severe deficits in body representation or other factors) were not captured. In contrast, our measures of spatial body representation (SSA and LBA) were not associated with later standing acquisition, likely because they primarily evaluate egocentric space. In addition, these tests particularly the LBA have been reported to correlate with WBA [13]. They assess the perceived midline of the body, which may result in uneven weight distribution, however this does not appear to be a prognostic factor for the time required to acquire standing position, especially once the initial postural deficits are compensated for. By comparison, the visual vertical is crucial for postural control, requiring individuals to accurately perceive their environment and rely on verticality for balance [38]. Future research should therefore incorporate visual vertical assessments and consider a longitudinal design that captures patients throughout the recovery trajectory including those who do not attain standing to more definitively clarify how spatial perception deficits influence both the timing and overall potential for standing acquisition.

Study limitations

This study has certain limitations. First, the primary criterion for assessing standing acquisition required patients to maintain an upright posture unassisted for at least 30 seconds with their eyes closed. Consequently, our findings are specific to this subset of patients, which may not fully reflect the earlier stages of recovery. Future research should adopt a longitudinal design with repeated assessments, capturing the transition from sitting to independent standing and eventually walking, in order to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the recovery process. A second limitation is the inclusion of only patients with RHS, as the right hemisphere is strongly associated with spatial deficits. Although our cohort represents the largest population studied on this topic, we acknowledge that these findings may differ in a population with LHSs, and future studies should explore their applicability in such populations. However, it is also important to note that studying a homogeneous sample of patients with RHSs allows us to evaluate more precisely the specific factors influencing recovery of standing ability, making the findings more robust in this context. In addition, our sample included relatively young patients admitted to specialized rehabilitation units, which may limit the wider applicability of our results. Further studies are needed to determine whether these findings extend to older populations or those receiving less intensive rehabilitation services. Third, the relatively small number of patients in the later standing acquisition group may have increased the risk of overfitting in multivariate analyses, despite efforts to limit the number of included factors. Future studies with larger samples are warranted to confirm these findings and allow more robust multivariable modelling. Finally, spatial neglect was defined using a conservative multimodal criterion, which may have underestimated milder or task-dependent forms of neglect [39,40], future studies should consider more sensitive or continuous measures to capture the full spectrum of neglect severity. In addition, spatial neglect was assessed using standardized tools that, although valid, may not fully capture the complexity of this condition in real-world settings. Therefore, ecological assessments in dynamic environments should be considered to better understand spatial neglect and its impact on functional recovery [6].

Conclusion

Our study found that only fifty percent of patients with RHS, who were able to maintain an upright posture unassisted for at least 30 seconds with their eyes closed, achieved standing capacity within three months. It also confirms that persistent spatial neglect is associated with the time needed to regain standing capacity and further demonstrates that ischemic etiology and increased spasticity of the quadriceps are factors associated with later standing acquisition. These findings highlight clinically relevant factors associated with later standing acquisition which may help guide clinical assessment and identify targets for future hypothesis-driven rehabilitation research.
