Quantum neural networks meet federated learning for DNA mutation prediction
Authors: Sotirios Messinis, Ioannis Rallis, Nikolaos Temenos, Dimitrios Kalogeras, George A Kastis, Nikolaos Doulamis, Nicholas E Protonotarios
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this work, we introduce QuanGAT, a hybrid framework integrating quantum neural networks (QNNs), graph attention networks (GATs), and federated learning to tackle DNA mutation prediction in biomedical graphs in a privacy-preserving, noise-aware setting. Motivated by quantum biology’s potential to describe mutation-level mechanisms, our approach is able to simulate decentralized genomic environments in order to protect sensitive data, while capturing complex biological variability. QuanGAT employs a QNN encoder based on parameterized quantum circuits, incorporating quantum noise, via the so-called depolarizing and amplitude damping channels. Furthermore, in order to enforce differential privacy, QuanGAT utilizes an attention-based architecture enhanced with Laplace noise. We evaluated the model on mutation-enriched protein–protein interaction networks, partitioned across simulated clients under centralized, federated, and noisy scenarios. In particular, we employed QuanGAT in three different datasets, namely PPI, STRING, and OBGN-Proteins. Especially in federated learning settings, QuanGAT consistently outperformed certain state-of-the-art graph neural networks up to 4.5 % in terms of accuracy and up to 6.3 % in terms of macro F1 score. Our findings demonstrate that integrating QNN encodings with attention-based graph learning may improve DNA mutation prediction in decentralized, privacy-aware settings.Keywords: Quantum neural networks, Federated learning, Graph attention networks, DNA mutation prediction, Biomedical graphs

Introduction

Quantum biology examines quantum mechanical phenomena, such as superposition, entanglement, and tunneling, in biological processes and systems [1], [2]. Recent advances suggest that quantum effects may govern a range of biological processes, namely photosynthesis [3], enzyme catalysis [4], DNA mutation [5] and neural signaling [6]. Simultaneously, the emergence of quantum computing and quantum-inspired algorithms has led to the development of new computational models able to represent and analyze biological phenomena with enhanced expressiveness and efficiency [7].

In parallel, machine learning, particularly graph neural networks (GNNs), has shown remarkable potential in the analysis of biological data, especially in contexts where interactions and relationships are naturally represented as graphs [8], [9]. Among GNN variants, graph attention networks (GATs) [10] employ attention mechanisms that dynamically prioritize relevant neighbors, improving both interpretability and performance of graph-based models in complex domains such as protein-protein interaction (PPI) [11], gene expression [12], and mutation analysis [13].

Mutations in PPI regions can significantly alter interaction strength and specificity, usually by modifying interface geometry and protein stability. Such perturbations range from complete loss of function, due to protein misfolding, to more targeted effects that disrupt only specific interactions affecting cellular phenotypes [14]. These mutation-driven disruptions frequently underlie diverse disorders: for instance, oncogenic mutations may prove capable of rewiring interaction networks, altering protein complex composition and binding partners, as observed in mutations affecting the kinase DYRK2, which altered its interaction network composition [15]. Computational tools, such as structural modeling and deep learning, can nowadays help predict how mutations affect PPIs and support the discovery of therapeutic targets [16]. However, the application of such powerful models in sensitive domains like genomics presents challenges related to data privacy, distribution, and heterogeneity [17]. In this direction, federated learning addresses these challenges by enabling decentralized training across multiple institutions or data silos without sharing raw data [18]. This makes federated learning particularly well-suited for biomedical applications, where data regulations and ethical constraints usually restrict centralized model training.

Motivated by these advances, in this work we explore how the synergy between GNN-based modeling and privacy-preserving learning can be enhanced by incorporating the principles of quantum computing. Specifically, we investigate a hybrid approach that leverages the strengths of GATs, federated learning, and quantum neural networks to tackle the complex task of DNA mutation prediction. To this end, we propose QuanGAT, a novel framework that combines quantum neural networks (QNNs), GATs, and federated learning to address the complex task of DNA mutation prediction. Our approach simulates mutation-aware, graph-based clients using protein-protein interaction data and evaluates our hybrid quantum-enabled attention architecture under centralized, federated, local-only, and noisy conditions, emulating quantum decoherence and biological variability. Our results reveal key trade-offs between gene expression, data privacy, and model robustness. They demonstrate that QNNs and attention mechanisms can enhance decentralized genomic analysis by leveraging quantum encodings and attention-based propagation. Across all federated learning configurations, QuanGAT consistently outperformed certain GNNs, achieving up to 4.5 % higher accuracy, while maintaining greater robustness to noise and client heterogeneity.

Related work

Recent advances in machine learning, graph representation, and quantum computing are already reshaping biomedical analysis. In this section, we review the evolution of federated learning frameworks, graph neural architectures, and quantum-enhanced models, focusing on their impact within sensitive domains such as genomics and molecular medicine.

Federated learning in biomedical applications

Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a promising paradigm for privacy-preserving model training across decentralized entities, particularly in sensitive fields such as healthcare and drug discovery. Recent advances include hybrid quantum-classical methods for optimizing participant selection in FL systems [19], generative models such as MolCFL that enhance molecular diversity and personalization through federated GANs [20], and structure-aware frameworks such as FedGrAINS, which address graph heterogeneity using generative flow-based sampling and adaptive regularization [21]. These works demonstrate FL’s capability to balance privacy, scalability, and personalization, especially when applied to biological graph data.

At the same time, GNNs have become essential for learning over structured biological data such as molecular graphs and PPI networks [22]. However, applying GNNs in federated contexts introduces challenges due to graph fragmentation, non-IID (non-independent and identically distributed) distributions, and privacy constraints [23]. Federated GNN approaches address these challenges by enabling subgraph-level training with graph convolutional networks (GCN) [24] or GAT layers [25], while research in PPI prediction moves beyond sequence-based models to incorporate interpretable structural information [26]. Among recent efforts, CGMega leverages graph attention networks and model-agnostic interpretability to integrate multi-omics data, enabling accurate cancer gene prediction and the discovery of high-order gene modules [27]. Adaptive message-passing mechanisms, such as those in FedGrAINS [21], further improve robustness by dynamically tuning receptive fields to client-specific graph characteristics. Complementing this, the FedGraphNN benchmark evaluates federated graph neural networks under non-IID conditions, revealing persistent performance gaps between federated and centralized training in heterogeneous graph scenarios [28]. Collectively, these developments highlight the importance of privacy-aware, structure-sensitive GNN frameworks for biomedical applications.

Quantum neural networks and DNA mutation prediction

The integration of quantum computing into machine learning pipelines, particularly through QNNs, has opened new research directions in secure, efficient, and high-capacity learning. QNNs exhibit potential in processing complex high-dimensional biomedical data, especially where conventional models often struggle. In particular, QNNs have shown promising results in lung cancer subtype classification using multi-omics integration with limited training samples [29]. To scale QNNs to collaborative learning settings, quantum federated learning (QFL) frameworks like FedQNN have been proposed, offering distributed quantum model training while ensuring data privacy across healthcare and genomics use cases [30]. Further research explores hybrid quantum-classical architectures, such as QuAFIDA, that leverage quantum eigensolvers within 5 G-connected medical IoT environments, combining privacy protection with intelligent diagnosis [31]. Lastly, quantum approaches to FL optimization, such as hybrid quantum-classical Benders’ decomposition, demonstrate the value of quantum resources in solving NP-hard scheduling problems in FL workflows [32]. These developments point to a rapidly evolving landscape where quantum technologies support both the privacy and capacity demands of next-generation federated learning systems.

The QBG-QCNN model introduces a quantum-convolutional neural network that combines Boruta-GA-based feature selection with quantum feature maps to enhance breast cancer classification accuracy, showcasing the benefits of hybrid quantum-classical architectures in clinical data interpretation [33]. Furthermore, hybrid quantum-classical GNNs have been applied to tumor classification tasks in digital pathology, integrating structural graph representations with quantum circuits to improve predictive performance [34]. Collectively, these works illustrate the potential of integrating quantum encoding, graph reasoning, and federated learning to address the unique demands of biomedical data analysis. A separate study demonstrates how quantum neural networks can uncover mutation-relevant biomarkers in CTLA4-activation pathways, even under limited sample regimes, offering a novel approach to genomic signal extraction [35].

Methods

This section outlines the key components of our approach, including the federated training protocol, the design of quantum-enhanced GAT architectures, and the mechanisms for modeling biological variability through quantum noise injection. To address the challenges of decentralized genomic analysis under privacy, heterogeneity, and noise, we design a hybrid framework that integrates federated learning, graph attention networks, and quantum neural encoding.

Federated graph learning

To address the challenges of privacy-preserving training on sensitive biological data, we implement federated graph learning (FGL) [36], as shown in Fig. 1. Unlike traditional centralized training paradigms, FGL enables multiple decentralized clients, each holding private, mutation-aware graph data, to collaboratively train a global model without exchanging raw features or labels. This approach is particularly relevant for biomedical research, where data governance policies and patient confidentiality prohibit direct data sharing.

Federated graph learning setup.

In our framework, each client represents an independent graph, simulating biological environments such as patient-specific protein interaction networks or mutation-driven genomic subgraphs. These clients locally train their own instances of a graph neural model and periodically communicate model parameters to a central aggregation server. The server applies federated averaging (FedAvg) as an averaging scheme to update a global model, which is redistributed to all clients for the next training round [37]. Specifically, our clients employ either a baseline GAT or a hybrid GAT with appended QNN encoders [38]. During local training, node features are passed through the QNN encoder and are concatenated with classical inputs, thus enriching the corresponding learned representation space.

The resulting embeddings are then processed by the GAT layers to exploit structural dependencies and attention-based neighborhood interactions. Each client uses its local training mask to compute the loss and update its local parameters [39]. After a predefined number of federated rounds, the globally shared model is evaluated across all clients using their test sets. This federated graph learning pipeline serves as the foundation for our experimentation, enabling comparisons between local-only, centralized, and federated training regimes under both classical and quantum-enhanced configurations.

Quantum neural network encoding

QNNs provide a powerful mechanism for learning expressive representations in noisy and complex domains such as DNA mutation prediction [40], [41]. In our hybrid architecture, QNNs are used to encode biologically meaningful node features prior to classical graph attention processing [42], enriching the input with quantum-informed representations.

Each QNN encoder comprises parameterized quantum circuits built with rotation and entanglement gates [43], operating on node vectors derived from mutation-sensitive features. Using angle encoding (RY rotations), the circuits translate classical inputs into quantum states, followed by optional noise channels to emulate decoherence and biological variability. Trainable quantum gates then evolve these states, producing expectation values, such as Pauli-Z, that serve as quantum-learned features [44]. Implemented via PennyLane [45] and PyTorch, the QNN outputs are concatenated with classical features and passed to a GAT layer for attention-based aggregation. This design introduces quantum inductive biases and allows evaluation under varying noise levels to assess model robustness in mutation-rich settings.

QuanGAT: a quantum-enhanced variant of graph attention architectures

The backbone of our model architecture is GATs, representing a class of graph neural networks that dynamically compute edge importance using learned attention coefficients. This allows the model to focus on relevant neighbors in a graph, which is crucial in biological networks where interaction strengths vary across entities such as genes, proteins, or mutations. In this study, we explore both standard and quantum-enhanced variants of GATs. Our baseline GAT model follows the standard two-layer design, with multi-head attention in the first layer and single-head aggregation in the second [46]. Building upon this, we introduce QuanGAT, a novel quantum variant of GANs, described graphically in Fig. 2. QuanGAT consists of two key enhancements, namely quantum-conditioned attention and differential privacy via attention perturbation.

Mutation-aware data simulation

To create a realistic experimental setting for DNA mutation prediction in decentralized environments, we construct a mutation-aware dataset by transforming the protein-protein interaction benchmark into multiple client-specific graph partitions [52]. Each partition simulates the data environment of an independent biomedical institution, such as a hospital or research laboratory, where patient-specific molecular graphs are observed locally and privately. The considered mutation-aware simulation pipeline provides a principled way to test quantum-augmented, federated learning methods under biologically grounded conditions. It offers a controllable and extensible testbed to study the interaction between graph attention, quantum encoding, and differential privacy in the context of decentralized genomic analysis.

Quantum noise injection and variability modeling

To simulate the stochastic behavior of biological systems and account for uncertainties in mutation-related variability, we incorporate quantum noise into the QNN encoding process [53]. Inspired by foundational principles in quantum biology, such as proton tunneling and coherence phenomena usually involved in DNA mutations, we employ a noise-injected quantum encoding layer as a proxy for modeling biological randomness.

In our framework, we draw an analogy between quantum noise channels and DNA mutation variability. While heuristic, this mapping captures essential aspects of mutational variability, offering simplified abstractions rather than literal representations of molecular mechanisms.

Training configurations

For the purposes of our experiments, we employ the QuanGAT framework, where each layer incorporates quantum-conditioned attention and optional differential privacy. We evaluate the QuanGAT architecture in centralized and federated settings, comparing its performance to classical GAT. The dimension of the node feature vector hi is set to 50, and the QNN encoder uses 4 qubits with quantum noise controlled via depolarizing (p) and amplitude damping (γ) channels. We initialize p=0.05 and γ=0.05 to simulate moderate decoherence, consistent with values commonly reported in the relevant literature [62]. The hidden layer contains 64 units with ReLU activation. The output layer produces multi-label logits for each node, and the final loss is computed using binary cross-entropy with logits. Optimization is performed using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.01. Each local training step consists of 5 epochs for centralized experiments and 1 epoch per federated round.

We simulate federated settings with K=8, 15, 25 clients. Each client is assigned a mutation-enriched subgraph. Federated learning proceeds for 50 communication rounds, using FedAvg. The privacy-preserving variants of QuanGAT are tested with Laplace noise applied to the raw attention logits, using privacy budgets ϵ∈{0.1,1.0,∞}, where ϵ→∞ denotes the non-private baseline. All models are implemented in PyTorch Geometric, and quantum circuits are constructed using PennyLane [63]. In our experiments, we vary the parameters p and γ to test the robustness of the hybrid model to quantum noise, and assess the trade-offs between representational expressiveness and decoherence. In federated settings, each client may use a different (p,γ) pair, reflecting heterogeneous biological environments or data collection conditions. This enables us to evaluate QNN resilience in non-uniform and potentially adversarial noise regimes.

Furthermore, we vary the privacy budget ϵ across experiments, effectively controlling the noise scale injected into the QNN layers. Lower ϵ values correspond to stronger privacy guarantees and higher uncertainty, which simulate greater biological diversity or instability across distributed clients. The presence of quantum noise allows our model to capture hidden stochasticity arising from mutation dynamics in biological networks.

The above models were selected for their relevance to protein-level classification, mutation prediction, and structure-aware learning. All models are evaluated under both centralized and federated settings using the PPI, STRING, and OGBN-Proteins datasets to maintain fairness across experiments.

We organize our experiments into three distinct evaluation cases (see below). Each case is evaluated using average multi-label classification accuracy across all nodes in the test masks.

Centralized training

In this setting, a representative subgraph consisting of 300 nodes is extracted from the global dataset and used as a centralized training and evaluation set. The model is trained with full access to both node features and topology, simulating an ideal scenario without distributional or privacy constraints.

Federated learning

The graph is partitioned into K=8, 15, 25 client subgraphs, each representing a distinct biological context with its own mutation-aware node features. Each client trains the QuanGAT model locally using its own data for one epoch per communication round. After each round, model parameters are averaged at a central server using the FedAvg algorithm. We simulate privacy-preserving distributed learning across heterogeneous biomedical institutions.

Privacy budget sensitivity

To study the impact of privacy preservation on performance, we vary the differential privacy parameter ϵ in the Laplace mechanism applied to the raw attention logits. We evaluate the QuanGAT model under three configurations: no privacy (ϵ→∞), moderate privacy (ϵ=1.0), and strong privacy (ϵ=0.1), and we quantify the utility–privacy trade-off introduced by attention perturbation.

Discussion

The evaluation across centralized and federated settings, client populations, and noise levels supports the effectiveness and robustness of QuanGAT. In the centralized setting, QuanGAT consistently outperformed all baseline models across datasets, as shown in Fig. 3. QuanGAT achieved the highest accuracy across all three datasets, with values over 0.85, consistently outperforming both classical and state-of-the-art graph models. It achieved macro F1 scores of 0.78 (PPI), 0.71 (STRING), and 0.67 (OGBN-Proteins). This translates to 4.6—6.3 % improvements over PGLCN (the strongest baseline), and up to 17.8 % over GCN, the least competitive model in the benchmark. Even against more sophisticated models like Net-DMPred and ProS-GNN, QuanGAT demonstrated clear advantages by achieving 4—8 % better macro F1 scores, highlighting the effectiveness of QNN-enhanced representations in extracting mutation-relevant graph features under ideal (non-private, non-distributed) conditions.

In the federated learning setting, model performance typically declined due to client heterogeneity and limited communication. Yet, as shown in Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6 QuanGAT achieved strong accuracy across all client configurations, exceeding 0.90 for 8 clients, 0.80 for 15 clients, and 0.75 for 25 clients, while also demonstrating faster convergence and lower final loss values compared to all baselines, particularly in early training rounds. On PPI, QuanGAT delivered macro F1 scores of 0.842, 0.815, and 0.781 for K = 8, 15, and 25 clients, respectively—showing a +0.4—1.6 % gain over PGLCN, and a +9—12 % margin over GCN. On STRING, the advantage over PGLCN was 0.7—1.3 %, and up to +11.6 % over GCN. On OGBN-Proteins, QuanGAT led PGLCN by +1.2—1.6 % and GCN by +11—13 %, confirming consistent generalization across datasets.

Performance differences indicated in Table 3, Table 4 are closely related to the imbalanced nature of each dataset investigated in this study. Considering this, we made further computations in order to enhance the clarity of model performance such as AUROC and AUPRC. The derived metrics highlighted a consistent imbalance effect in all datasets, with average values close to 0.7 for the centralized setting and 0.6 at the federated setting across all client populations, while all models achieved better performance in the PPI dataset. This can be justified by the large difference in class counts (e.g., 121 for PPI vs. 2 for STRING) and the highly skewed label distributions across nodes, which directly model training and evaluation. For OGBN-Proteins, despite having 132,534 nodes and 112 labels, each label is annotated on only a small fraction of proteins, making the dataset multi-label and highly imbalanced, with some functional classes being very frequent and others extremely rare.

A critical dimension of this study was the robustness of QuanGAT to quantum noise, introduced via depolarizing and amplitude damping channels. We show that, while performance degrades with stronger noise levels (lower privacy budgets ϵ), the QuantGAT model maintains robust accuracy and convergence. As shown in Table 5, when increasing the noise (i.e., reducing ϵ from ∞ to 0.1), the accuracy degraded from 0.7665 to 0.5696 (for 8 clients), but still outperformed non-quantum models under similar conditions. This confirms the hybrid model’s resilience in noisy environments. These results demonstrate that QuanGAT can tolerate moderate quantum noise while preserving both utility and privacy.

While the attention coefficients in GATs provide a certain degree of interpretability, the addition of quantum embeddings introduces an extra layer of complexity, making it harder to identify which features drive the predictions. To address this issue, we performed post-hoc interpretability analyses, namely saliency maps and attention heatmaps, see Fig. 7, Fig. 8, respectively. Saliency maps highlight which input mutations most influenced the output for a given protein, whereas attention heatmaps visualize which interactions the model prioritized.

QuanGAT was evaluated under simulated federated conditions with graph partitions representing independent biomedical institutions. Real-world deployment across physically distributed and asynchronous clients may introduce additional challenges, such as communication latency, hardware heterogeneity, and irregular data availability. It would be valuable for further investigations to explore the deployment of QuanGAT in a real-world federated infrastructure, potentially integrating edge devices or institutional compute nodes, to assess performance under non-ideal synchronization, connectivity issues, and varying computational resources.

QuanGAT simulations used 4-qubit circuits that are realizable on current NISQ devices [70], but are not yet scalable to datasets with thousands of biological features. To address this limitation, hybrid approaches such as feature grouping, dimensionality reduction, and parameter-efficient circuits can reduce qubit requirements. Although large-scale deployment depends on progress in hardware capacity and error correction, QuanGAT is designed to align with the gradual scaling of quantum devices in the near future.

Furthermore, we simulated federated learning by splitting benchmark datasets into subgraphs, which was common in previous studies but does not fully capture the heterogeneity of real-world genomic data across institutions. In practice, institutions may exhibit systematic biases, batch effects, or population-specific mutation patterns that challenge the generalizability of the model. In the future, we aim to validate our system on distributed genomic datasets across multiple institutions, incorporating privacy-preserving protocols to account for this heterogeneity and better reflect use in real-world scenarios.

Conclusion

The development of QuanGAT underscores the broader impact of combining quantum computing principles with federated graph learning in privacy-sensitive genomic analysis. By integrating a quantum neural encoder within a federated GAT framework, our approach improves mutation prediction performance, while upholding strong data privacy guarantees. The combination of QNN-based feature encoding and differentially private attention mechanisms illustrates how quantum noise can be harnessed to model biological uncertainty without compromising confidentiality. Future research can explore: (i) more advanced quantum architectures and noise modeling techniques, (ii) the deployment on emerging quantum hardware, and (iii) extend these methods to several other genomic or biomedical applications.
