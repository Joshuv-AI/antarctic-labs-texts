Providing continuity of care for people living with noncommunicable diseases in humanitarian settings: A qualitative study of health actors' experiences in Lebanon
Authors: C Akik, Z El Dirani, R Willis, C Truppa, C Zmeter, S Aebischer Perone, J Roswall, R Hamadeh, K Blanchet, B Roberts, MF Fouad, P Perel, É Ansbro
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Continuity of care (CoC) is essential for NCDs but is challenging in humanitarian crises.•Providers and stakeholders described disjointed NCD care within the Lebanese system.•Recent successive system shocks and the humanitarian response further impacted CoC.•Strengthening of information, referral and supply systems is key to improved NCD CoC.•Humanitarian actors could support CoC by aligning with Lebanon's NCD strategy.Keywords: Humanitarian, Conflict, Noncommunicable disease, Continuity of care, Integration, Health service, Person centred

Introduction

Low and middle-income countries (LMICs) bear most of the global NCD burden, including 77% of all NCD-related mortality (World Health Organization, 2022). They also shoulder the greatest burden of global conflict and displacement, and host 74% of the world's refugees (UNHCR, 2023). People living with NCDs are particularly vulnerable in humanitarian crises. Evidence suggests that the stresses of war and displacement and resulting interruptions to care may worsen existing NCDs (Slama et al., 2017; Jawad et al., 2019). Displaced populations may be exposed to other NCD risks, including increased levels of mental disorders and stress, exposure to alcohol and smoking, increased cardiovascular risk, and limited access to healthy food and exercise options (Hayman et al., 2015; Maconick et al., 2020; Willis et al., 2023; Charlson et al., 2019; Lo et al., 2017, 2016). It has, therefore, become increasingly necessary to include NCD services in humanitarian and health system responses, and, over the last decade, humanitarian organisations have gained operational experience and developed clinical guidance and tools to manage NCDs (International Rescue Committee, 2020; Medecins Sans Frontieres, 2016).

Continuity of care (CoC), that is following an individual over time and across levels of care, is recognised as essential to the provision of good quality NCD care. CoC is key to the prevention and management of serious NCD complications, such as amputations, blindness, kidney failure, and heart attacks. The term “continuity of care” is often used interchangeably with other concepts, such as integration of services, care coordination and continuum of care (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, 2018; Rossouw et al., 2021; Thapa et al., 2020). Two elements distinguish CoC from other concepts: the care of an individual patient, i.e. how individual patients experience integration of services and coordination, and care delivered over time (Haggerty et al., 2003). Four core dimensions of CoC have been described: the subjective experience of a caring relationship (interpersonal continuity), continuity of provider (longitudinal continuity), continuity of information (informational continuity), and the effective coordination of clinical care, including continuous access to equipment and medicines (management continuity) (World Health Organization, 2018).

Maintaining CoC has proven especially difficult in humanitarian crisis settings. The well documented challenges include patient mobility, loss of medical documentation, lack of care standardization, poor coordination between actors, interrupted supply chains, and destroyed or inaccessible facilities (Ansbro et al., 2022; Cross, 2018; Jobanputra et al., 2016; Rabkin et al., 2018). Experts also acknowledge that gaps in quality, standardization, and coordination of NCD care in crises, and limited integration with host systems also contribute to poor CoC (Ansbro et al., 2022). While recent operational guidance advocates for CoC for NCDs in humanitarian emergencies, empirical evidence on how to operationalize this is still limited, and clear definitions of what it entails are lacking in the humanitarian literature (Rabkin et al., 2018; Bausch et al., 2021; Jaung et al., 2021; World Health Organization Regional Office for South-East Asia, 2018).

Lebanon hosts over 1 million Syrians registered with the United Nations High Commission for Refugees (UNHCR) fleeing the Syrian conflict, the highest proportion of refugees to host population in the world, and half a million other protection-seekers (United Nations Refugee Agency, 2022; El Daif et al., 2021). Rates of NCDs are high and growing among both Lebanese and Syrian refugees in Lebanon, necessitating strengthened NCD care delivery (Doocy et al., 2016; El Haidari et al., 2024; Mansour et al., 2017). Since 2011, Lebanon integrated Syrian refugees into the national health system, with the cost subsidized by UNHCR (Akik et al., 2019). Prior to the Syrian crisis, decades of national political and sectarian conflict led to weakened governance in Lebanon, and a highly privatized, poorly integrated health system (WHO, 2017). The primary care system is underdeveloped and highly dependent on local actors (WHO, 2017). Early on in the Syrian crisis, there were clear gaps in the coverage, quality and continuity of NCD care in Lebanon (Akik et al., 2022). Working with the Lebanese Ministry of Public Health (MoPH), international humanitarian actors responding to the crisis supported Lebanon to strengthen NCD care over the last decade, through the integration of an NCD programme within primary health care centres (PHCCs) in 2012, health worker training, and rationalisation and strengthening of NCD drug procurement (Akik et al., 2022). However, the country experienced successive additional shocks since 2019, including a sharp economic recession, the August 2020 Beirut blast and the COVID-19 pandemic. These events have pushed Syrian refugees and other protection-seeking populations further into deprivation, poverty, and insecurity. Among Lebanese, socioeconomic vulnerability has sharply increased amidst widening inequity, such that one third of the population now lives below the poverty line (El Daif et al., 2021; World Bank, 2024; UNICEF, 2020). Much fewer Lebanese can afford to access private care and medications, and so more people are dependent on the public health system (Akik et al., 2022; Arakelyan et al., 2021; Hallit et al., 2020; Saleh et al., 2022). In turn, the system is further burdened by increasing demands, reduced funding, the exodus of medical professionals and shortages of supplies (UN ESCWA, 2021; United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees, 2022). Providing CoC for NCDs is, therefore, hugely challenging (Willis et al., 2023).

To our knowledge, there is limited evidence from humanitarian settings, such as Lebanon, on CoC for NCDs, including around referral pathways between different health system levels (Ansbro et al., 2022; Akik et al., 2019, 2022; Zablith et al., 2021; Ratnayake et al., 2021). This study was conducted within a wider research project exploring models of care for people with diabetes and/or hypertension (DM/HTN) in LMIC humanitarian settings. The project involved a partnership between academic, humanitarian and governmental partners and the ‘Partnering for Change’ (P4C) initiative, which includes the Lebanese Red Cross, the Danish Red Cross (DRC), the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), and Novo Nordisk. The partnership identified hypertension and diabetes as focal conditions due to their high disease burden, frequent co-existence, and amenability to management using a primary level public health approach. Lebanon was selected as one of two case study countries, since the humanitarian implementing partners were already providing NCD care there. The London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine (LSHTM) and the American University of Beirut (AUB) led the research in Lebanon.

We explored the experiences of people living with diabetes and hypertension during the evolving humanitarian crisis in Lebanon in a separate paper (Willis et al., 2023). This paper documents the impact of mass migration due to conflict on both refugee and host communities and on a national health system. The Lebanese government's decision to include Syrians within the public health system means that it was a key element of the humanitarian response to the Syrian crisis in Lebanon (Akik et al., 2022). Within this complex health delivery context, we aimed to explore health actors’ experiences of continuity of care for hypertension and diabetes for both Syrian refugees and vulnerable Lebanese in Lebanon.

Conceptual framework

We used a conceptual framework for the analysis of models of care for DM and HTN in humanitarian settings (Fig. 1) (Ansbro et al., 2022) in the conceptualization of models of care, development of tools, and in analysis and reporting. This conceptual framework was iteratively developed to guide the P4C research project and was previously published (Ansbro et al., 2022). It draws on (i) a mental health in health systems framework (Fuhr, 2020), which was adapted from the World Health Organization (WHO) model of health systems and a person-centred approach to healthcare and used in a mental health and psychosocial support intervention study in humanitarian settings (de Graaff et al., 2022); (ii) existing literature on models of NCD care in LMICs (Kane et al., 2017) and (iii) global experts’ experiences of NCD care in humanitarian settings (Ansbro et al., 2022). For this paper, the authors chose to focus on the health system inputs and intermediate health system goals in the context of Lebanon, a protracted humanitarian crisis. To organize the findings, we reported against each framework domain through the lens of CoC, focusing on interpersonal, longitudinal, informational, and management continuity. The patient and community factors elements of the framework were addressed in our linked paper (Willis et al., 2023), and the final goals are reflected on in the discussion.

Study setting and sites

Study sites were purposively selected from among facilities supported by our humanitarian implementing partners in Lebanon, aiming to include variation in health system level and geographical location. We selected four urban-based facilities that provide low cost or free-of-charge health services to Syrian refugee and vulnerable Lebanese patients with previously diagnosed HTN or DM: (1) two primary-level dispensaries in the North Lebanon governorate that were not part of the MoPH network at the time of the study; (2) one PHCC in Mount Lebanon that is part of the MoPH network, and (3) a governmental tertiary teaching hospital that serves patients from all regions. All provided low cost or free-of-charge health services to Syrian refugee and vulnerable Lebanese patients with previously diagnosed HTN or DM.

Interview participant selection and recruitment

Interview participants were purposively selected to meet our study and partners’ research objectives. Where relevant, this was followed by snowball selection. Identification of potential health care provider participants was initiated in discussion with the management team of the study sites, while other health actors representing governmental institutions, health system managers, and humanitarian actors involved in DM/HTM care delivery, were identified based on previous work conducted on Syrian refugees and a review of the literature on provision of health services to manage and treat non-communicable diseases among Syrian refugees and vulnerable Lebanese populations. Contact information was retrieved from publicly available directories. The study team contacted participants via email and/or telephone and/or an online platform using a recruitment script. Based on our experience of similar studies (Willis et al., 2023; Ansbro et al., 2021) and following the principles of theoretical saturation, we aimed to collect approximately 30 interviews (including patient and caregiver interviews, which were reported separately (Willis et al., 2023)).

Interview data collection

Data collection took place between September 2020 and February 2021. Health actors’ interviews were conducted either face-to-face at health facilities or using online platforms, by ZED, CA (both Lebanon-based, female public health researchers, MPH and DrPH qualified respectively), ÉA (female, UK-based, clinically trained public health researcher, MBBCh PhD) and RW (female, UK-based social scientist, PhD). All researchers involved in data collection have undertaken research ethics training and have experience in conducting qualitative research with vulnerable populations. All were involved in the study design and iterative design of study tools. Interviews were undertaken in English or Arabic, as preferred by the participant, and lasted between 0.5 and 1 h. Each started with an introduction of the researcher followed by obtaining verbal informed consent according to AUB approved procedures, since many were conducted remotely due to the Covid-19 related pandemic restrictions. Interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ permission and hand-written notes were taken.

Topic guides were developed based on the conceptual framework for a model of NCD care in humanitarian settings (Fig. 1). These topic guides were used in more than one humanitarian setting to explore models of NCD care delivery. They were built around the themes considered important by our partners including the patient pathway (the diagnosis, initial care-seeking and support, prior knowledge, current care and support, community-based care and condition management), patient-centeredness of current care (access and coverage, experience, respect and dignity, communication, user focus, quality of infrastructure) and integration and CoC, including referral systems. The first two concepts were reported in our linked paper, while this paper focuses on the final two concepts, which require a functioning health system, reinforced by robust health system building blocks (Table 1). The topic guides were adapted in light of COVID-19 to include questions about its impact (Supplementary Material 1). We conducted two pilot interviews; topic guides were adapted accordingly and iteratively throughout the data collection process.

Interview data management and analysis

The AUB/LSHTM research team conducted regular debriefings, providing opportunities for reflection and support, as needed, during data collection. Fortnightly update meetings were held with humanitarian partners to discuss emerging findings. The team concluded data collection when theoretical saturation was reached on the key themes we sought to explore. The research team developed a glossary of key terms in Arabic and their translation to English. Interviews were transcribed verbatim by the research team (ZED, CA, ÉA, RW) and then translated into English by two professional translators as required. To ensure accuracy, translated transcripts were checked against the original version by the interviewer. Transcripts were analysed using a thematic approach. A coding framework was developed iteratively using both inductive and deductive approaches, supported by the NVivo 2020 R1 software package.

A validation meeting was conducted in February 2021 with the research partners to review and discuss preliminary findings. Feedback informed the selection of priority themes in subsequent analysis and supported the formulation of key recommendations included in this manuscript. These discussions also informed partners’ future programmatic interventions.

Ethics

The study was approved by the MoPH Lebanon following submission of a letter of intent from study partners, and a Ministry of Public Health (MoPH) focal point was appointed to the study team. Ethical approval was obtained from the ethics committees at AUB (SBS-2019-0404) and LSHTM (16193 – 1). The study conformed to the principles embodied in the Declaration of Helsinki. We have reported our findings according to the COREQ checklist.

Findings

Interviews were conducted with 20 out of 22 potential participants whom we approached. Twelve participants were female and eight were male. Of these, 11 were HCPs and one was representative of a governmental institution, one was an international delegate and seven were humanitarian actors. Two representatives of international agencies declined to participate. Out of the 11 interviewed HCPs, three were practicing in a PHCC, five in dispensaries, and three in a governmental hospital. HCPs included a nurse and a nurse supervisor (n=2), social workers (n=2), general practitioners (GP) (n=2), cardiologists (n=2), an endocrinologist, an emergency medicine physician, and a midwife involved in the triage of patients attending a PHCC. Four of the five physicians were part-time visiting physicians within the facilities.

HCPs reported on their experience and expertise related to DM/HTN services provided at the facility level, whereas other health actors provided a national and international perspective on NCD care delivery, gaps, and challenges in the context of a national health system responding to a protracted humanitarian crisis, with support from humanitarian agencies. All health actors also suggested recommendations to overcome these challenges.

Here we report our results under the conceptual framework's domains related to health system inputs and intermediate health system goals (Table 1). We acknowledge that some elements may fall under more than one domain; to avoid repetition, they are reported under only one. In the discussion, we reflect on how these various elements contribute to or impede the four dimensions of CoC.

Health system inputs and their impact on continuity of care

Gaps in required health systems inputs including financing and governance challenges, the inconsistent medication supply, the lack of retention of the health workforce, and the absence of a centralized health information system (HIS), have meant that DM/HTN care is non-standardized and inconsistent. The influx of humanitarian actors, UN agencies, and international funders has made the landscape of NCD care delivery in Lebanon even more complex.

Governance and financing

The multiplicity of systems within the Lebanese health care system and dominant private sector that have grown in the shadow of the pluralistic power-sharing system have resulted in a lack of consistency and standardisation of care provided, and thus a lack of management continuity. Multiple informants commented that around two-thirds of PHCCs operated independently of the MOPH PHC network allowing for the establishment of parallel systems with limited communication and coordination with the MOPH.

“Why would you have all these centres and not all of them are part of the network? Therefore, if I go to a dispensary, I will not obtain the package that I would obtain somewhere else. Each one is following a different approach.” (HA19)

Furthermore, the PHCCs accredited by the MOPH are operated and managed by various entities such as local and international actors and municipalities, leading to the establishment of an array of models of NCD management and a lack of standardisation between facilities. In addition, the Lebanese fragmented system, according to two health actors, has resulted in a situation in which PHCC resources and quality of care are dependent on the presence and influence of a particular political party that invests in these PHCCs for political reasons, thus attracting mostly patients of a particular religious affiliation. This formula has contributed to the MoPH's “weak governance” and inability to play a central leadership and regulatory role in overseeing the overall health system.

“To have a national system, a perfect one, you should not have NGOs and foundations among others managing the centres. You should have the state and the MoPH, and you should say, these are centres where everyone goes.” (HA19)

A health actor also highlighted that health policy and implementation regarding healthy eating is very limited and, thus, humanitarian and development actors should advocate with the MoPH to act on NCD risk factors, including diet.

“You can't adapt the diet because you need environmental change, and we miss that. For example, I cannot lecture you on diabetes all day and then you go to the school or the university and see chips next to you. I think you need to have advocacy and to have environmental change on a smaller level, from a small scale to the biggest scale.” (HA14)

Financial challenges faced by the MoPH also impeded their agenda to promote better quality and continuity of primary level NCD care, particularly management continuity, as described by various participants. A representative of a UN agency stated that, ‘we are noticing in the last years and specifically the last year and this one, funds available for the health sector are decreasing.” The MoPH was also reported to have been unable to secure funds to purchase the NCD medications due to the economic crisis.

“The MoPH contributes to the YMCA budget to import chronic diseases medicines for the vulnerable Lebanese component while the humanitarian partners put out the remaining amount for the other population groups. […] At the end of 2019 and the beginning of 2020 […] the MoPH could not contribute to […] YMCA year budget, so this obviously created a deficit.” (HA20)

Linked with the variety of care models at primary healthcare facilities, different cost sharing structures exist, and these vary over time. The costs borne by patients who visit specific PHCCs are determined by the management of the PHCC and the supporting NGOs and actors. Some PHCCs provide free or discounted consultation costs or laboratory testing, which usually take place off-site in private laboratories and hospitals. Patients attending other facilities are required to pay for the tests themselves at external laboratories.

“In many NGO-supported PHCs […] the package of the laboratory tests is for free. […] We have 18 centres in Beirut, and in Mount Lebanon, they have packages for every patient who has diabetes, hypertension, or wellness issues. […] The patient pays only 3000LL [USD2]. There are also centres outside Beirut, and they are subsidized by [international NGOs]. […] But even in the centres that are not subsidized, the patients pay a maximum of 18,000LL [USD12] for the examination and EKG, and the medication is for free.” (HA15)

Similarly, for secondary and tertiary care cover, interviewees noted that mechanisms of financing and co-payment were highly variable. Uninsured Lebanese were described by one participant as the “conductor of their own orchestra”, trying to navigate their own care pathways, working within the constraints of their available resources. Health actors recognized that hospital care was not affordable for most lower-incomed patients who would attend PHCs. Affordable access to emergency care was particularly challenging for Syrian refugees and vulnerable Lebanese. One cardiologist explained that patients instead sought help from PHCs, which are ill-equipped to manage emergencies, because they could not afford to attend hospital emergency rooms:

“We cannot do anything because unstable angina cannot be treated in a dispensary. It is a case that requires a hospital. […] When it comes to Syrian refugees, the hospital does not cover it [unstable angina], and they barely have money to eat. How are they supposed to go to the hospital?” (HA05)

Medicines and equipment

All respondents indicated that medication scarcity, whether in community pharmacies, dispensaries, or PHCCs, is a major threat to CoC, particularly management continuity, and compromises adherence to treatment, putting patients at risk of complications. The YMCA is responsible for purchasing a standardised list of NCD medications for the PHCs in the MoPH network, funded via the MOPH (for Lebanese) and international donors (for Syrian refugees. The policy is that these purchased medications are then distributed equally among all nationalities. However, supply was unpredictable, leading to frequent stock-outs. This was reportedly due to limited government financing (as presented in the financing and governance section above), weakened governance with distribution of YMCA drugs to non-network PHCCs, as well as “medicines […] becoming very expensive [due to subsidy removal]. [Furthermore], at the beginning of the Covid-19 [pandemic], there was a blockage on all borders, for example, so there were fewer importations. [Also], due to the socio-economic situation, the number of vulnerable populations, and even more middle-class people, started also seeking some support from those health centres that receive drugs through the YMCA.” (HA17)

One interviewee also reported reasons related to poor communication between MoPH and PHCCs in terms of medication consumption monitoring and information technology fragmentation.

“The [MOPH] central [drug] warehouse where the medicines are being stored has been computerized and it has been upgraded …but …the bulk of the stock is in YMCA, so the IT infrastructure is still fragmented. The way it should be is that we have one platform that all the other platforms feed into… and then it will bring much more efficiencies.” (HA20)

In terms of equipment, HCPs reported that electrocardiogram machines were present at all three primary health facilities, (in one site functioning variably), but cheaper and lower technology microfilament and tuning fork for diabetic foot checks were absent.

Health workforce

CoC was also reported to be affected by workforce shortages, affecting interpersonal continuity. Clinical staff within PHCs were available to patients for a limited number of hours each week, and due to the economic crisis, HCPs were leaving the public sector and not being replaced at a time when more vulnerable Lebanese and Syrian refugees were seeking free or subsidized care. These factors led to increasing workload for remaining staff.

“[To improve the quality and continuity of NCD services], we need to have a [full-time] physician [based at the PHC] and that would be difficult because the [physician] cannot give a 100% of their time [as most physicians have their private practice or provide care in several health centres].” (HA01)

Many interviewees acknowledged the implications of workforce shortage and resource constraints on the public sector. This had led to a model whereby the same HCPs with the same skills and knowledge operate in both public and private sectors but provide different services. The time constraints in the public sector negatively impact on interpersonal continuity. Consultations were primarily focused on providing treatment and diagnosis to the largest possible number of patients, rather than providing patient-centred care.

“I cannot [spend more time with the patient]. I explain this [dietary recommendations, stress management, and foot examination] to my patients at the private clinic. But not to a patient for 3,000 or 10,000 LBP (USD 0.38 – 1.25), especially in these times, it is not even $1. To give 12 min, 30 or 45 min; that is not hard, but it is not fair.” (HA05)

Several health actors also identified inconsistency in use of guidelines, affecting management continuity. They report that specialists refer to their university training on the guidelines set by European countries or the U.S. and international associations such as the International Diabetes Federation, rather than adopting MoPH NCD guidelines.

Facilities and services

As discussed above, Syrian and vulnerable Lebanese have variable access to primary healthcare facilities, and the content of care provided, and cost-sharing structure are highly variable. While consultations with GPs and specialists, and medicines are available in PHCCs and dispensaries to a varying extent and with variable co-payments, most laboratory and diagnostic tests are provided in private laboratories and hospitals. Access to essential drugs through the YMCA programme is available at all MoPH-accredited PHCCs and a limited number of dispensaries. Our informants also confirmed the lack of integration between the different sites of care within the broader Lebanese health system.

Services at community level are limited. There is reportedly limited structured community-based support and activities, and an absence of formal peer-based support organisations. Outreach activities reported by HCPs included community-based awareness sessions, using WhatsApp as a platform to connect and tailor health messages to community members during the Covid-19 pandemic, and orienting newly diagnosed patients to their nearest health facilities through mobile clinics. Some humanitarian organisations reported commencing ad hoc home visit services targeting elderly people, many with NCDs, in response to the Covid-19 pandemic, and in part replacing the informal care provided by neighbours who could no longer help because of the economic crisis.

While targeted NCD screening was provided by some humanitarian actors, one interviewee from a humanitarian organisation reported that they did not support or engage in NCD screening, in line with their strategy.

“[At this organization], we do not [actively] screen [for NCDs], we do test people with existing diseases. […] Why would I do [screening] if I don't have enough medications for the already existing pool of patients?” (HA12)

Information

One of the main challenges to patients’ CoC, and coordination across health actors, is the absence of a unified health information system (HIS) between institutions that allows patients’ files/health records to be transferred between PHCCs, PHCCs and other services (such as laboratories), and between PHCCs and hospitals. The PHENICS – Primary Healthcare Network Information and Communication System – currently used by accredited PHCCs, serves as a centralized health information exchange model that aggregates data received from health facilities for programme management and service monitoring purposes. Humanitarian actors also monitor their projects, often using their own individualised electronic reporting systems. Generally, HCPs rely on paper-based medical records to view patient data. Thus, some facilities may have multiple, parallel data collection and reporting systems.

Appointment and defaulter tracing systems also support longitudinal continuity. None of our study sites had defaulter tracing systems to ensure the retention of patients. Two out of the three primary health facilities scheduled consultations with specialists using a paper appointment calendar, yet several HCPs expressed their frustration at patients not continuously following up on their conditions.

The call for a unified HIS that allows clinical data sharing and viewing between health providers practicing within and across health facilities, so tests/examinations are not repeated was a common recommendation across health actors, to support referral and return of information to referring site, quality monitoring and medication consumption monitoring.

“The [referral process’] challenges include the requirement for a unified referral system, and this is going to take time; we need an efficient and effective referral system. There must be a computer program without having too much paper…. I need [to be able to use] a database… so that everyone has all the contact information and every organization we work with. This is missing....” (HA14)

One interviewee highlighted the relevance of electronic health records to chronic disease care specifically:

“Since 2018, we were trying to push for, to introduce the electronic health records. […] This is because chronic disease [program] is a type of programs that is reliant on continuity of care. Unlike the others [types of programs], this patient will stay with you for quite long and having a sort of electronic health record that is linked between the PHCs will improve the efficiencies.” (HA20)

Intermediate health system goals and their impact on continuity of care

Gaps in required health systems inputs have influenced the various intermediate health system goals and, subsequently, continuity and integration of care.

Access and coverage

HCPs reported that co-payments for consultations and laboratory tests were often unaffordable for patients and forced them to “shop around” different centres, impeding management continuity, or prevented them from regular attendance or getting the recommended laboratory tests done:

“The examination of diabetes patients has definitely become almost free [for all diabetic patients] in these centres. But the patient tells you that he does not have 2,000 LL(USD 0.25) [the due co-payment] to buy bread, or he will wait for a free [examination] campaign.” (HA10)

However, according to one interviewee, some international NGOs provide a comprehensive NCD package that includes free-of-charge consultations, essential diagnostic tests, as well as medications. When this package was introduced, it led to improved follow-up rates, “so the main barrier for the patients (prior to introduction of the package) was cost, not knowledge (about available services).” (HA13)

To increase affordability, accessibility, and quality of primary healthcare, a flat fee model was established in some PHCs, supported by humanitarian actors. As part of this model, patients “were paying […] 3000 LL (USD 0.38) [for consultation] and they benefited from wide services from consultation, laboratory tests, radiology tests, hearing aid, eyeglasses, for persons with disability, assistive devices, from medication as well”. As an immediate response to the Beirut blast, this model has been adopted by the PHCs working in the affected areas. Further implementation of this model has been recommended by three health actors, one of which stated that it was effective in limiting the exploitation of patients by the private sector and better addressing patients’ needs. However, one interviewee perceived that physician practicing in the private sector “are not interested in the flat fee model” because of vested financial interests; “they want to make their own money” more than being concerned about “the wellbeing of the country” (HA19).

Quality and safety of care

Most participants confirmed having either provided or received training on the guidelines for management of NCD in primary healthcare, yet they were not necessarily the MoPH adopted guidelines. One health actor reported using clinical guidelines from other humanitarian organizations.

All participants agreed that a key priority for continuity of good quality care was to maintain a constant supply of medication to ensure patients’ adherence to treatment.

“You cannot have continuity of care and have YMCA that gets stockouts every three months. It doesn't work like this; these are chronic diseases, […] it's not even like [for] a one-year or two-year, it must be continuous.” (HA12)

Two physicians reported that the lack of affordable diagnostic testing also limited their ability to deliver quality treatment to their patients and required them to identify affordable (though less ideal) alternatives.

“The patient will definitely not do the laboratory tests [when they are expensive and not covered by any party]. We [physicians] are then forced to ask for the [urine] microalbumin [test] or we look for symptoms if the patient is showing diabetes or dyslipidaemia symptoms or things like that. It is a primitive method but at least we have something.” (HA05).

Most HCPs and two humanitarian actors indicated that patients trust their HCPs. Patients were described as loyal to particular physicians rather than to facilities:

“We know Lebanon and you know that people usually follow up with the doctor, not with the PHCC. So, if this doctor is no longer working in the public [clinic], these people will have to go to his private clinic at some point if they want to follow up.” (HA12)

Another health actor also highlighted that patients had "lost trust and confidence" in the public health sector and would "run to see a private practitioner" whenever they could afford it. One health actor stated that the primary healthcare's lack of visibility and publicity efforts of the services offered and their quality, as well as people's inability to distinguish between the services offered by a PHCC and a dispensary contribute to patients’ limited trust in the public health sector.

Responsiveness

In our conceptual framework, responsiveness refers to notions of autonomy, choice, communication, confidentiality, and dignity, quality of basic amenities, prompt attention, and access to family and community support. We cover quality of basic amenities, communication, and access to community support under other domains. The concepts of autonomy, choice, confidentiality, dignity, prompt attention and access to family support were not raised by the health actors and are rather concepts that would be addressed when exploring patient experiences, reported in our linked paper.

Integration and continuity

CoC was directly linked to how patients navigate the system over the disease course, how well the NCD services are integrated between sites and levels of care, and the structure of the referral system.

Absence of a formal referral system

One of the key features is that NCD Care is not coordinated across different facilities or levels of care in Lebanon, impeding longitudinal and management continuity. Another related key limitation is the lack of continuity of information. Without a unified electronic HIS, patients do not have a unique identifier to track them as they navigate the system. Patients need to carry their own paper-based health records and repeatedly supply this information when visiting a health facility or provider for the first time. To facilitate the transfer of paper-based information, the Lebanese MoPH issued a standardized referral document to be used between PHCCs or between PHCs and secondary or tertiary care facilities, but health actors reported that it is not used in practice. According to a general physician,

“[The referral system] does not exist. In other countries, the referral system is available. You take the document to the emergency; it is available and transferred; it has a serial number. This is it. The document is attached to the file, and the doctor has it. Everything is available. But we do not have it.”

Although some international NGOs tracked referrals made and the outcomes, most referrals in the system were not documented or tracked and the outcome was often not formally communicated back to the referrer.

Another key barrier to continuity, consistency and standardisation of care is the lack of formal referral pathways and mechanisms to connect PHC patients to hospitals for investigation or specialist input. This is noted by most HCPs. The gap in the referral system has been filled by informal referral or “orientation” to other services that are not formally linked or contracted, and patient self-referral:

"We tell them which access they might benefit from, and which organizations might cover them and which programs might include them. This is how we work. It is not a formal referral between NGOs; nothing is called a formal referral. It is not formal because it is not a transfer within a hospital or transfer between hospitals. It is not. It is more kind of help.” (HA06)

Nurses, GPs and social workers, who play a key role in bridging the referral gaps, acknowledged that they were not always aware whether the referral outcomes were successful and whether their patients received the needed treatment.

One GP reported ensuring CoC and a trust-based relationship with his patients by expecting them to visit him/her as well as the specialist.

“It is a parallel procedure [following up personally with the patient while the other physician whom the patient is referred to also provides another medical consultation] because usually there is a relationship between the patient and the doctor, which means that the patient goes back to the doctor, but not all patients do a follow-up.” (HA03)

Communication between private hospitals and other healthcare facilities is particularly problematic. Patients who seek care mainly in the public system may also visit private facilities e.g. for laboratory tests or other investigations not available in public sector. Many private hospitals have their own HIS and do not directly share patient-related information with other health facilities. Most of these hospitals use fee-for-service financing models, are not part of the public healthcare system, and treat each patient encounter as a new encounter, leading to duplication of care:

“Out of 180 hospitals, almost 80% of them are private hospitals. They work on a fee-for-services type of model. So, it is not easy to ask those providers to share back with anyone who requests information. […] If you are a patient who moves from one hospital to another, the other hospital will just assume that this patient is new; they cannot rely on the information provided by the previous hospital; they always start from scratch, which is problematic, and it just shows you the downside or the weakness of the health system because the fragmentation even exists within the hospital level.” (HA20)

In case of hospital discharge, patients admitted to the governmental hospital included in this study were reported to benefit from social workers informing them about services provided by other NGOs or PHCCs :

“Our weapon [for referral] is the team of social workers at [the tertiary hospital]. They are our database so sometimes we do have some questions, sometimes patients have questions […] then we would ask this team of social workers. They have a very large network because in [the tertiary hospital] they're connected to almost everyone, and so they would know that, for example, this person with an eye problem could benefit from something that this “X” NGO is giving and that we have never heard of but they would know who and where to refer so they're usually the people that we go to for external referrals.” (HA12)

However, this network of social workers was employed by the supporting NGO and was not standard within the Lebanese hospital sector. Most patients must navigate the fragmented system of care without this support. One humanitarian actor emphasized the need for mapping of services and establishment of formal referral pathways between organizations. They noted that ‘many organizations begin working on the field without knowing who is nearby [doing similar work in the area’ and that the lack of coordination was further exacerbated by ‘competition in the humanitarian field’, pointing out that ‘organizations need to start to follow-up and reply to each other’.

Standardization

The lack of a standardisation at primary level care and inconsistent referral pathways has been outlined above. Respondents from across KI groups recommended establishing standardized NCD service delivery packages and care processes aligned with the MoPH's policy and guidance. The MoPH efforts to improved standardisation have included the introduction of the accredited process, whereby in return for MoPH funding and support, PHCCs are expected to “offer a high level of services and be comprehensive”. They also introduced (i) the Patient Centred Primary Care Program (PCPC) and NCD programme, which specifies clinical guidelines, rationalised laboratory testing and testing intervals and frequency of consultation and dispensing; (ii) the YMCA coordinated drug procurement; and (iii) capacity building for nurses and physicians. However, as mentioned previously, fragmented governance, limited funding and repeated shocks, including the COVID-19 pandemic, has hampered the implementation of these programmes.

At hospital level, newly revised hospital accreditation standards have been developed, whereby hospitals are “obligated to work with the healthcare centres in their catchment area. They [hospitals] are obliged to work with PHCC, so there will be referral feedback for all these details.” (HA15)

While UN agencies, international funders and the MoPH have drawn up plans and policies that require humanitarian actors to align with the MoPH approach, in practice, the influx of international actors has exacerbated the lack of standardised NCD care delivery. According to interviewees, these entities may engage on a short-term project basis only and employ different models of support to both PHCCs and non-network dispensaries, for example via funding alone or procurement of medications or equipment, providing incentives for key staff, capacity-building for health professionals, or supporting outreach and education activities. While some health actors work independently and are not integrated with the public health system, others are supporting dispensaries to become accredited and use MoPH guidance and tools in their clinics.

Sustainability

The protracted nature of the crisis in Lebanon poses a challenge for the humanitarian actors who are engaged in NCD care. Traditionally, many take a short-term approach, are limited by short-term funding and seek a handover strategy:

“To have an exit strategy is a challenge for us because the current situation in the country is not getting any better. The chronic diseases program within the system is not getting any better although there is lots of funding coming, but still with the increased needs.” (HA17)

Most humanitarian agencies engage with the MoPH via short-term emergency mechanisms, such as the Lebanon Crisis Response Plan established during the beginning of the Syrian crisis and the Emergency Response Plan following the Beirut blast, rather than through a health system strengthening approach. Instead, participants recommended that humanitarian actors engage with development actors and strengthen existing systems rather than setting up parallel systems which contribute to further fragility and fragmentation of the system.

“This nexus approach, humanitarian approach needs to be improved and needs to be adopted in a more uniformed way inside and outside the network, on the primary and on the secondary healthcare level.” (HA19)

Interviewees reported the need for humanitarian actors to play a more prominent advocacy role; by forming and contributing to task forces and advocating within the facilities they support for the models of care which they perceive as the most efficient, especially models and tools aligned with MoPH strategy. Thus, they can use their positions supporting diverse primary level facilities to push them to engage with the MoPH NCD agenda and foster health system strengthening with a sustained impact.

Discussion

Our exploration of continuity of care for hypertension and diabetes in Lebanon showed that both the Lebanese health system's historical structure, and the more recent socioeconomic shocks impacting the system, have affected management, longitudinal, interpersonal and information continuity of care for both Syrian refugee and Lebanese patients. Our findings around the gaps in health system inputs and their negative impact on the intermediate health system goals of access and coverage, standardisation, and quality, are in keeping with previous studies from Lebanon and from other humanitarian contexts (Ansbro et al., 2022; Akik et al., 2019, 2022). Our study also adds to the limited literature on provider experiences of NCD care delivery in crises.

In high-income countries where models of continuous care are often delivered by primary healthcare physicians, the key role of relational (interpersonal) continuity between patient and provider has been long recognised (American Academy of Family Physicians, 2003; Gray et al., 2003; Ontario, 2013). Continuity of contact with a single provider has been shown to bring about better coordination of care, benefits in clinical outcomes and increased patient and provider satisfaction (American Academy of Family Physicians, 2003; Gray et al., 2003; Ontario, 2013). The latter is echoed in some of the patients and provider accounts from our study, where interviewees valued building trust and rapport, and maintaining longitudinal relationships (Willis et al., 2023). Studies of multi-disciplinary healthcare teams in high-income countries have highlighted how continuity can be facilitated by different health worker cadres using a joint care plan in a coherent, logical, timely and patient-centred fashion (Goodwin, 2016; World Health Organization, 2018). Humanitarian actors have some experience of employing multidisciplinary approaches for NCDs in relatively stable humanitarian settings (Ansbro et al., 2022, 2021; Murphy et al., 2022). For example, UNRWA have long provided this type of care for Palestinian refugees in the region (Santoro et al., 2016; Shahin et al., 2015). Currently, ICRC and their partners are also developing an integrated model of NCD care within a PHCC in northern Lebanon, delivering rehabilitation, mental health and primary level medical care in a person-centred way (Truppa et al., 2023). However, the health work force shortages and high attrition rates we highlighted in our study may threaten the viability of multidisciplinary care and limit the opportunity to develop and maintain interpersonal and longitudinal continuity of NCD care in Lebanon. While these challenges are common to many LMIC settings, they have been significantly worsened by Lebanon's recent political and economic crises (Hemadeh et al., 2020; Shallal et al., 2021).

Regarding specific health system inputs, such as governance, finance, medicines and equipment, it is notable that the Lebanese MoPH has tried to use the opportunity provided by the influx of international support and financing for the Syrian response to further build on their primary healthcare strengthening activities in Lebanon. Progress is being made despite the myriad challenges they are facing (Hamadeh et al., 2021). However, some problems remain, including the persistence of multiple parallel primary healthcare systems, a diversity of actors and financing mechanisms, and weaknesses in the new systems set up to bolster continuity and standardisation, such as the YMCA drug procurement mechanism. In Lebanon and similar contexts, inconsistent access to medicines and equipment, which hamper management continuity, have also been shown to increase the burden of care for patients and to diminish their trust in health systems (Ansbro et al., 2022; Arakelyan et al., 2021). Reduced patient trust may, in turn, negatively impact on patients’ healthcare seeking, which may then reduce longitudinal continuity (Arakelyan et al., 2021).

The health workforce challenges, the lack of services at community level and the limited role of community health workers that we illustrated in the Lebanon context, are not surprising given that community health worker role has been less developed in the Middle East compared to Asia and Sub-Saharan African contexts. The lack of patient empowerment interventions highlighted in this study is also common to many other humanitarian and LMIC settings, although this is changing. There are recent successful examples of interventions in Lebanon and Jordan involving community health workers and peer support for NCD patients (Ratnayake et al., 2021, 2022). The value and versatility of the community health workers’ role was highlighted as humanitarians adapted to the Covid-19 pandemic, minimising facility-based contact to protect their patients while ensuring continuity of care by other means (Ansbro et al., 2022). The lack of an integrated health information system (while moving in the right direction via the PHENICS system), the multiple parallel monitoring systems, and limited or absent referral pathways are certainly not unique to Lebanon (Ansbro et al., 2022, 2021). These gaps negatively impact on informational and management continuity of care, and, as highlighted by our own and by other studies from humanitarian settings, patients and families often bear the burden of managing their own data and navigating care pathways (McNatt et al., 2019).

Implications for practice and research

Improving continuity of NCD care for the Syrian and Lebanese populations in Lebanon requires strengthening of key health system inputs. This should, in turn, facilitate delivery of the final goals - an efficient and effective health system, improving people's health while protecting them from financial and social risk. Our interviewees suggested that humanitarian actors could further support the MoPH agenda of standardisation, and support sustainability by acknowledging the protracted nature of the Lebanese crisis, advocating with the facilities that they support to follow MoPH guidance, and by working with development actors in a humanitarian-development nexus approach (Kruk et al., 2018; Spiegel, 2017). This may mean moving from the traditionally short-term humanitarian approach to a longer-term development approach, supported by sustained financing, potentially linked to health system reform, to facilitate health system recovery and build resilience. The MoPH, in turn, may consider explicitly integrating NCDs into health system preparedness, response and resilience planning, in line with the WHO Eastern Mediterranean Regional Office's Regional Framework for Action on NCDs in Emergencies. This may include making crisis-affected populations eligible for NCD care as part of a basic universal healthcare package.

The health workforce shortages we identified may be alleviated, in part, by introducing incentives or boost retention or by exploring alternative care models. The shortage of physicians may require more focus on task sharing to other health care cadres. Task sharing entails delegation of some tasks to non-physician health professionals and lay workers and has been shown to produce positive health outcomes for NCD patients in LMICs (Joshi et al., 2014). Effective task sharing requires training, guidelines, supportive supervision and a favourable policy and regulatory environment. Strengthening community level services, potentially involving community health worker or volunteer networks, with links to PHCs is also recommended. We suggest that introducing multi-disciplinary, person-centred approaches to care and developing the social worker role may also balance physician shortages and improve continuity of care. Empowering patients and their families on self-management and the introduction of peer support groups could also support continuity of care (Ansbro et al., 2022; Truppa et al., 2023).

In the long term, establishing a digital national health information system, with unique patient identifiers, clear clinical guidelines and referral pathways is required (Khader et al., 2014). The UNRWA electronic medical record and cohort monitoring system may be one successful example to build upon. We acknowledge that this takes enormous and ongoing financial commitment. Lessons learned in other settings in response to the Covid 19 pandemic, whereby information systems were rapidly developed and adapted may prove useful (Miller et al., 2022; Negro-Calduch et al., 2021).

We note that Lebanon has been relatively stable compared to many other humanitarian settings and we must acknowledge that delivering continuity of care may be even more challenging in unstable or acute conflict settings. These contexts may involve mobile, sometimes traumatised populations, and disrupted or destroyed facilities, information systems and supply chains, severely hampering organisations’ capacity to delivery continuity of care for crisis-affected people living with chronic conditions. Different means to ensure continuity of care may be more important in such contexts, such as creating buffer stocks of medications and providing patients with runaway packs and patient-held medical records (Jobanputra et al., 2016).

Future research could include implementation research on integrated models of care, community care models, patient empowerment and on strengthening referral pathways to improve continuity of NCD care in humanitarian settings. It could also apply a health system resilience lens, exploring how the Lebanese NCD care system may show the absorptive, adaptive and transformative capacities required when responding to a surge in demand and shocks to the system (Kruk et al., 2017; Barasa et al., 2017; Blanchet et al., 2017).

Strengths and limitations

We have highlighted the provider experience of delivering NCD care in a humanitarian setting, which has had limited coverage in the literature. We used a conceptual framework specifically developed around models of NCD care in humanitarian settings that has been used and adapted by our team to guide the development of our data collection tools, analysis and the presentation of our results. Our study included a relatively small number of participants, attached to four facilities that were all supported by international NGOs. They may not be representative of the broad range of primary healthcare facilities and support structures in Lebanon. For example, we did not include private facilities. However, we felt that theoretical saturation was reached on the key themes we sought to explore and valuable lessons could be learned.

Conclusion

By examining health system inputs and their impact on intermediate health system goals, this study helped to understand the current capacity of the Lebanese healthcare system to address continuity of care for Syrian and Lebanese populations living with NCDs. Continuity of care has been challenged by the underlying health system structure, the refugee and economic crises, the Beirut blast, and the Covid pandemic. We recommend that humanitarian actors develop their work with the MoPH to align with and strengthen health system inputs, particularly the workforce, via task sharing and training, strengthening the medication supply chain, access to facilities and services via new funding mechanisms, and via the development, standardisation and interoperability of referral and information systems.
