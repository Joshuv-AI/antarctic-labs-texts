Valve-in-Valve Implantation for Early Failure of a Transcatheter Tricuspid Valve
Authors: Madjid Boukantar, David Aouate, Paul-Matthieu Chiaroni, Patrick Zamora, Tony Truong, Antoine Molho, Oueies Labidi, Clement Boyer, Emmanuel Teiger, Romain Gallet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTranscatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) is increasingly used for severe tricuspid regurgitation in high-risk patients. Early prosthetic dysfunction may occur, but management strategies remain poorly described.Case SummaryA 79-year-old woman presented with cardiogenic shock 6 months after Topaz TTVR. Echocardiography and computed tomography showed leaflet thickening involving 2 of 3 leaflets with a mean gradient of 9 mm Hg. Endocarditis was excluded, and anticoagulation optimization failed. Patient-specific bench testing improves procedural planning and procedural safety.Take-Home MessagesEarly dysfunction of TTVR prostheses requires prompt multimodality evaluation. Bench-guided transcatheter valve-in-valve implantation can safely avoid high-risk redo surgery.

History of Presentation

A 79-year-old woman was referred to our institution for cardiogenic shock with persistent right-sided heart failure symptoms and catecholamine dependence. She had undergone transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) with a Topaz valve (Tricares) 6 months earlier for severe symptomatic tricuspid regurgitation.1 Clinical presentation suggested severe prosthetic dysfunction requiring urgent reassessment.Take-Home Messages•Early dysfunction of orthotopic transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement prostheses may occur and requires careful multimodality evaluation.•Transcatheter tricuspid valve-in-valve implantation can be an effective alternative to redo surgery in selected patients.•Patient-specific bench testing can optimize tricuspid valve-in-valve strategy and safely avoid high-risk redo surgery.

Physical examination showed signs of systemic venous congestion and low-output heart failure. Hemodynamic instability persisted despite medical therapy.

Past Medical History

Her medical history included chronic atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and a prior nondisabling ischemic stroke.

Her chronic cardiovascular medications included rivaroxaban 15 mg daily, furosemide, and spironolactone.

Differential Diagnosis

Structural valve deterioration, prosthetic leaflet thrombosis, infective endocarditis, prosthetic underexpansion, or mechanical dysfunction.

Progressive right ventricular failure unrelated to prosthetic dysfunction.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated severe dysfunction of the tricuspid bioprosthesis with a mean transvalvular gradient of 9 mm Hg.

Cardiac computed tomography (CT) demonstrated marked thickening of 2 of the 3 prosthetic leaflets, consistent with hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) and strongly suggestive of prosthetic dysfunction (Figure 1). Because the patient was already receiving rivaroxaban, anticoagulation was temporarily intensified using heparin without clinical or hemodynamic improvement.

Endocarditis was excluded after evaluation by the infectious diseases team including blood cultures and complete clinical assessment.

Given persistent cardiogenic shock and inability to wean catecholamines, a definitive redo intervention was required.

Management

Redo surgery was considered prohibitive because of advanced age, cardiogenic shock, renal dysfunction, pulmonary disease, and global frailty.2 The heart team therefore selected transcatheter tricuspid valve-in-valve (ViV) implantation.

To the best of our knowledge, no prior ViV procedure had been reported in a failed Topaz transcatheter tricuspid valve. Given this novelty, and considering the original Topaz valve design—composed of a soft nitinol outer anchoring stent and a stiffer 28-mm inner stent containing the valve mechanism—preprocedural bench testing was performed using a patient-specific silicone 3-dimensional printed heart model derived from CT (Figure 2).Figure 2Patient-Specific Bench TestingBench testing in a patient-specific 3D-printed model showing (A) difficulty advancing the delivery system across the Topaz valve and (B, C, and D) stable positioning of the Sapien 3 valve within the inner stent.

Patient-Specific Bench Testing

Bench testing in a patient-specific 3D-printed model showing (A) difficulty advancing the delivery system across the Topaz valve and (B, C, and D) stable positioning of the Sapien 3 valve within the inner stent.

A Topaz valve was implanted in the model, and implantation of a Sapien 3 (Edwards) valve was simulated.

According to manufacturer specifications, the inner Topaz stent diameter measures 28 mm for both valve sizes. Preprocedural CT analysis using 3mensio software demonstrated an inner valve area of approximately 550 mm2 and an estimated diameter of 27 mm, supporting the selection of a 29-mm Sapien 3 valve (Figure 1). Bench testing further confirmed satisfactory expansion and opening of the 29-mm valve within the Topaz prosthesis, with stable anchoring and appropriate final positioning without migration (Figure 2).

Simulation demonstrated major stent–stent interaction during advancement of the Edwards delivery system across the Topaz prosthesis, with a potential risk of prosthetic damage and impaired valve advancement. Several strategies were evaluated including transjugular access, steerable delivery systems, and large-bore sheath protection. Bench simulation demonstrated that stent–stent interaction could be minimized by advancing a long 65-cm 24F DrySeal Flex sheath (Gore) across the failed Topaz prosthesis toward the pulmonary artery, allowing advancement of the Sapien 3 valve within the sheath up to the intended landing zone before sheath retrieval and valve deployment. Because this technique required advancement of a large and relatively stiff long sheath through the tricuspid prosthesis, the transfemoral approach was considered the most appropriate access route, providing the straightest trajectory toward the pulmonary artery.

The procedure was performed under conscious sedation using a right venous transfemoral approach. The failed Topaz prosthesis was initially crossed using a straight guidewire through an Agilis catheter (Abbott). A J-tip guidewire supported by a JR4 catheter was then advanced into the distal right pulmonary artery and subsequently exchanged for a Lunderquist extra-stiff guidewire (Cook Medical) to maximize support. Indeed, because the right ventricle was not severely dilated, we were concerned that positioning the guidewire only within the right ventricular apex would provide insufficient support. The 24F DrySeal Flex sheath was then advanced across the prosthetic valve into the right ventricle outflow tract. A 29-mm Sapien 3 valve already aligned with the balloon was inserted into the sheath at the level of the tricuspid prosthesis. The central balloon marker was positioned at the mid-height of the Topaz inner stent, and the upper edge of the Sapien 3 frame was aligned with the upper edge of the Topaz stent before deployment. The sheath was then retrieved over the prosthesis to allow the deployment.

Although the Sapien 3 valve was not initially fully coaxial with the Topaz inner stent, extremely slow and controlled deployment at nominal volume under rapid pacing allowed progressive coaxial realignment through gentle forward pressure on the Lunderquist guidewire and delivery system, resulting in optimal expansion and final alignment (Figure 3, Videos 1 to 5).Figure 3Valve-in-Valve Procedural StepsProcedural steps of valve-in-valve implantation: (A) guidewire placement, (B) advancement of a large-bore sheath across the Topaz valve, (C and D) positioning of the Sapien 3 valve, (E) deployment under rapid pacing, and (F) final result.

The mean echocardiographic transvalvular gradient immediately decreased from 9 mm Hg to 1 mm Hg, with no residual tricuspid regurgitation.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Catecholamine support was discontinued the following day with rapid hemodynamic improvement.

The patient was transferred to a cardiac rehabilitation unit 10 days later.

At 30-day follow-up, she remained clinically stable. Echocardiography showed a mean transvalvular gradient of 3 mm Hg with no residual tricuspid regurgitation.

Discussion

This case highlights three clinically relevant issues in contemporary transcatheter tricuspid intervention.

First, early dysfunction of orthotopic TTVR prostheses may occur despite recent implantation. As TTVR expands, structural valve deterioration, leaflet thickening, thrombosis, and prosthetic dysfunction will become increasingly important. Management strategies for failed TTVR prostheses remain poorly described. Recent reports have highlighted that HALT and leaflet thrombosis may occur after orthotopic TTVR and may be associated with elevated transvalvular gradients and recurrent heart failure symptoms.3,4 In our patient, CT showed marked leaflet thickening involving 2 of the 3 prosthetic leaflets, strongly suggesting prosthetic dysfunction. Although definitive leaflet thrombosis could not be confirmed, HALT-related dysfunction was strongly suspected. A lack of improvement after anticoagulation intensification and persistent cardiogenic shock justified urgent definitive intervention.

Second, ViV implantation may represent an effective alternative to redo surgery in carefully selected patients. Redo tricuspid surgery in elderly patients with cardiogenic shock, renal dysfunction, right heart failure, and frailty carries very high mortality. In such settings, a transcatheter strategy may provide rapid hemodynamic recovery with lower procedural burden. Most published tricuspid ViV experience concerns failed surgical bioprostheses or annuloplasty rings.5 Reports involving orthotopic TTVR failure remain extremely limited, particularly with newer dedicated systems such as the Topaz valve.

Third, patient-specific bench testing can significantly improve procedural planning in complex structural interventions. In our case, the original Topaz dual-stent architecture created uncertainty regarding delivery feasibility and final valve positioning. Bench simulation directly influenced procedural strategy by identifying the need for large-bore sheath protection and confirming the feasibility of transfemoral implantation. This planning step was particularly valuable because avoiding prosthetic damage during valve crossing was a major concern. Without simulation, alternative access routes such as transjugular access may have been selected unnecessarily. The bench model therefore contributed not only to procedural safety but also to simplification of the intervention.

This step substantially improved procedural safety and reproducibility. As TTVR use increases, ViV strategies and procedural simulation will likely become central components of long-term structural heart management.

Conclusions

Transcatheter tricuspid ViV implantation is feasible after early failure of a Topaz transcatheter tricuspid prosthesis.
