3D-printed core–shell scaffolds with a biphasic calcium phosphate core and GelMA hydrogel shell for bone tissue engineering
Authors: Amir Shadi, Amir Mostafapour, Behzad Asghari, Nima Beheshtizadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBone tissue engineering requires scaffolds that simultaneously provide mechanical stability, controlled biodegradation, and bioactivity to support bone regeneration. In this study, a novel core–shell composite scaffold was developed by integrating an extrusion-based 3D-printed alginate/ceramic lattice core with a bioactive gelatin methacrylate (GelMA) hydrogel shell. Biphasic calcium phosphate (BCP) systems with different hydroxyapatite (HA)/β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) ratios were incorporated into alginate-based bioinks and fabricated via robocasting to achieve well-defined, interconnected porous architectures. Following ionic crosslinking and lyophilization, the printed scaffolds were uniformly coated with GelMA and photo-crosslinked to form a stable hydrogel shell. Morphological analyses confirmed the preservation of interconnected porosity with pore sizes in the range of 450–650 µm, suitable for bone tissue ingrowth. Mechanical testing revealed that the incorporation of ceramic phases significantly enhanced scaffold stability, while GelMA coating further improved compressive performance, increasing the elastic modulus from 37.35 ± 0.73 MPa for pure alginate scaffolds to 82.04 ± 0.50 MPa for GelMA-coated BCP scaffolds. In vitro degradation studies demonstrated a controlled, time-dependent mass loss profile, indicating favorable scaffold stability under physiological-like conditions. Bioactivity evaluation in simulated body fluid (SBF) showed pronounced calcium phosphate deposition on BCP-containing scaffolds, particularly those coated with GelMA, as confirmed by FESEM and EDS analyses. Accordingly, the synergistic combination of a mechanically reinforced ceramic–polymer core and a bioactive GelMA shell resulted in scaffolds with enhanced mechanical integrity, tunable degradation behavior, and superior in vitro bioactivity. Introduction

Tissue engineering is an interdisciplinary field that integrates principles of biology, materials science, and engineering to regenerate damaged tissues and restore physiological function1. In this approach, a three-dimensional (3D) scaffold is employed in combination with therapeutic agents, cells, and growth factors to promote tissue regeneration at the defect site. The scaffold acts as a temporary extracellular matrix (ECM), supporting cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation until new tissue forms2. Accordingly, the development of tissue-engineered constructs relies on close collaboration between clinicians, biologists, and engineers, and has become a leading strategy for replacing or augmenting conventional treatment modalities3.

Bone tissue engineering (BTE), as a rapidly expanding branch of this discipline, focuses on the design and fabrication of biomimetic substitutes capable of restoring bone defects caused by trauma, malignancies, degenerative diseases, and congenital abnormalities4. Although traditional bone grafting approaches remain the clinical gold standard, they are associated with critical limitations, including donor site morbidity, limited graft availability, risk of infection, and immunological complications5. These shortcomings have motivated researchers to develop alternative, tissue-engineered solutions that are capable of accelerating bone regeneration, improving mechanical performance, and minimizing clinical complications.

Among the fabrication techniques utilized in BTE, extrusion-based 3D printing (robocasting) has emerged as a particularly promising technology6. This method enables precise control over scaffold architecture, pore geometry, and structural integrity, parameters of paramount importance for nutrient diffusion, vascularization, and mechanical resilience7. In robocasting, a hydrogel-based bioink is deposited layer by layer under computer control to produce scaffolds with highly tunable morphologies and tailored physicochemical properties, rendering this technique highly suitable for bone-regenerative applications8.

In recent years, composite hydrogels have gained considerable attention as bioink materials due to their favorable rheological behavior, biodegradability, printability, and mechanical performance9. Ceramic-based materials such as hydroxyapatite (HA), beta-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP), and biphasic calcium phosphate (BCP) exhibit excellent bioactivity and osteoconductive properties owing to their chemical similarity to natural bone mineral10. BCP composition ratio is another essential factor, influencing the physical and mechanical performance of the scaffolds. Based on the previous studies11,12 and marketed synthetic products13,14, two compositions of BCP were carried out in this study; HA: β-TCP of 60%: 40% and 20%: 80%.

Conversely, polymeric components such as alginate (Alg) provide desirable features including biocompatibility, controlled degradation, and efficient gel formation, thereby improving scaffold stability during the printing process15. The synergistic combination of these materials offers a means of overcoming the inherent limitations associated with their individual use . Despite their promising characteristics, single-component bioinks frequently suffer from limited cell adhesion, insufficient mechanical strength, inadequate degradation rate, or poor bioactivity, which restricts their applicability in bone scaffold fabrication16. Consequently, composite systems have been developed to integrate the advantages of both ceramic and polymeric constituents, enhancing material printability, biological performance, and structural stability17. The development of such composite hydrogels has therefore become a central strategy in advancing scaffold design for BTE and represents a major focus of current research18.

Recent studies showed that Gelatin Methacrylate (GelMA) could ameliorate the bone scaffolds’ biological and chemical performance, enhancing their cell attachment, proliferation, and differentiation capabilities8,19,20. Our recent study reveals that physical and mechanical performance of the tricalcium silicate (C3S)/ Alg scaffolds were improved via GelMA coating8. According to the hypothesis that this property could bring novel systems in bone scaffolding, this study was designed and perform.

In this work, several scaffolds were developed through composition of HA, β-TCP, and BCP and Alg using the robocasting technique. Then, a GelMA coating layer was applied as a shell on the 3D-printed Alg/ceramic core structures. The aim of the study was to analyze the mechanical and physical performance of the prepared scaffolds and determine the efficiency of GelMA coating on the Alg/ceramic scaffolds. Materials and methods

Materials

Fetal bovine serum (FBS, 11,570,636) and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 11,510,546) tablets, were purchased from Gibco Company, UK. Gelatin methacrylate and 2-Hydroxy-4′-(2-hydroxyethoxy)-2-methylpropiophenone (Irgacure 2959, Photo-initiator, PI, 106,797–53-9) were purchased from Zharf Andishan company, Iran. Sodium Alginate (Alg, 9005–38-3) was purchased from Sisco Research Laboratories Pvt. Ltd. Company, India. Hydroxyapatite (HA, , 1306–06-5) was purchased from Arminano company, Iran. Beta-Tricalcium Phosphate (β-TCP, , 7758–87-4) was purchased from Merk company, Germany.

Ink preparation

For the preparation of an extrusion-based 3D-printing bioink, a defined quantity of HA nanopowder was incorporated into an Alg solution. Initially, multiple formulations with different Alg-to-HA ratios were prepared and examined to determine the composition exhibiting the most favorable rheological and printing characteristics. The optimal formulation was obtained by dissolving 2 g of alginate in 30 ml of deionized water under continuous mechanical stirring at ambient temperature. Subsequently, 2 g of HA nanopowder was gradually added to the alginate solution, and the mixture was subjected to magnetic stirring for 24 h to ensure complete homogeneity and uniform particle dispersion. In order to enhance viscosity and improve extrusion stability, an additional 6 g of Alg was incorporated into the mixture and stirred until a well-dispersed and cohesive paste was formed. All remaining formulations were prepared following the same protocol. Table 1 summarizes the prepared bioinks for scaffold developing.

Extrusion-based 3D printing of lattice

An extrusion-based 3D printing machine (Pioneer V. X4, Omid Afarinan Company, Iran) was utilized to fabricate the composite scaffolds. A 10 ml printing syringe filled with ink was placed at 8 °C on the three-axis motion stage, which was controlled by computer-aided repeatire software. Our lattice design parameters were as strand diameter ≈ 450–500 µm and infill 8%. There was not any angle/rotation in this printing setting. Printing was carried out using a 450 µm nozzle at a deposition speed of 4 mm/s. The external dimensions of the printed scaffolds were 6 × 10 × 10 mm and the fabricated scaffolds were then immersed in a 70 w/v% calcium chloride solution for 30 min to facilitate cross-linking. They were frozen at -20°C for 48 h and then at -80°C for 24 h. This freezing step prepared them for lyophilization in a freeze dryer. After lyophilization, the scaffolds were ready for coating with GelMA. Finally, following the coating process, the scaffolds were dried again using the previously described method.

Coating with GelMA

To enhance the biological and physicochemical performance of the printed scaffolds, a surface coating step was carried out using a GelMA hydrogel. Based on our previous study, the optimum GelMA concentration and soaking time were 12% (w/v) and 5 min, respectively8. The scaffolds were first immersed in a 12% (w/v) GelMA solution for 5 min to ensure adequate surface coverage and hydrogel penetration into the scaffold microstructure. Subsequently, the samples were subjected to ultraviolet (UV) irradiation for 4 min using the UV module integrated in the bioprinter in order to induce photo-crosslinking and stabilize the GelMA layer. This process was implemented to improve the mechanical integrity, surface functionality, and biological interface of the scaffolds. All samples were coated under identical experimental conditions to ensure consistency and reproducibility.

Analyzing the composition ratio via FTIR

To analyze the composition ratio of the manufactured scaffolds and identify the functional groups, a Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis (BRUKER, USA) was utilized in the range of 500–3860 cm-1 with a resolution of 1 cm-1. All the 7 specimens listed in Table 1 were analyzed.

Mechanical performance test

The compressive mechanical properties of the fabricated scaffolds were evaluated using a universal testing machine (UTM-S250, Spico). A maximum compressive load of 50 kN was applied to determine the compressive strength of the freeze-dried specimens at a constant loading rate of 1 mm/s. Prior to testing, the scaffolds were cut into cubic specimens with dimensions of 10 mm × 10 mm and a height of 6 mm. For each experimental condition, three samples were tested to ensure statistical reliability and reproducibility of the measurements. This test configuration was intended to assess the structural integrity and load-bearing capability of the printed scaffolds under compressive stress, providing a quantitative comparison of mechanical performance between the different scaffold formulations.

In vitro bioactivity

The bioactivity of the fabricated scaffolds was evaluated in accordance with the protocol summarized in our previous study8, using a simulated body fluid (SBF) solution. In this assessment, the samples were examined with respect to their surface reactivity and ability to promote mineralization. Each scaffold specimen was immersed in SBF for predetermined periods of 1, 3, 7, 14 and 21 days at 37 °C inside an incubator in order to reproduce physiological conditions. Following immersion, the samples were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water and dried at room temperature. Subsequently, the morphology and surface chemistry of the mineralized layers were analyzed using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM).

This method enables the evaluation of scaffolds that are intended for contact with biological fluids, allowing their surface reactivity and apatite-forming capability to be examined. Bioactivity is typically defined as the ability of a material to induce the formation of a semi-crystalline HA layer on its surface in vitro, a mechanism that has been well established and widely documented in the literature21. The development of this calcium phosphate-rich layer is considered a strong indicator of the potential of the scaffold to bond with surrounding tissues in vivo. Consequently, the results of the SBF test are regarded as a preliminary predictor of in-body performance and osteoconductive capability of the scaffold.

In vitro degradation rate test

The degradation behavior of the scaffolds was evaluated in vitro by immersing the samples in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 1, 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days at 4 °C. The scaffolds that prepared for this test had dimensions of 10 × 10 mm and a height of 4 mm. Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate for each condition. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by the Holm–Sidak post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Results

Scaffold fabrication

In the present study, an extrusion-based 3D printing technique was employed for the fabrication of ceramic/polymer composite scaffolds. A square-shaped 3D model incorporating interconnected internal architectures was designed, consisting of 30 stacked layers. This design enabled the formation of uniformly distributed micropores with an average size of approximately 400 µm upon 3D printing procedure and before crosslinking. The calcium chloride crosslinking dictates a shrinkage in the scaffolds and the micropores between the strands get larger. Such a porous architecture provides a favorable spatial geometry that supports cell viability, proliferation, and differentiation, particularly for cell-based bone tissue engineering applications.

Following the preparation of various ink formulations, the rheological behavior of the synthesized bioinks was systematically evaluated to identify a composition suitable for extrusion-based printing. It was observed that ink formulations with a solid-to-solution ratio exceeding 25% exhibited a significant increase in viscosity, resulting in improved printability. Importantly, this formulation also demonstrated enhanced shape fidelity after deposition, which represents a critical requirement for maintaining structural integrity during and after the printing process. Unlike various fabricated bioinks, which suffered from poor dimensional stability, the bioink composed of 8 g alginate and 2 g ceramic particles dispersed in 30 mL of water exhibited optimal rheological properties, satisfactory extrusion behavior, and superior shape-retention capability. These characteristics indicate that the optimized composite ink formulation is well suited for the fabrication of mechanically stable and geometrically accurate 3D-printed scaffolds. Morphological Features

Figure 3 illustrates the scaffolds fabricated using extrusion-based 3D printing. To investigate the microstructure and internal architecture of the scaffolds, morphological analysis was performed using FESEM. As observed in Fig. 3, scaffolds composed solely of Alg were not suitable candidates for this study. Following rapid ionic crosslinking with calcium chloride, these samples exhibited significant distortion of their physical and geometric structure, leading to a loss of dimensional stability and collapse of the original scaffold architecture, along with growing several microcracks on the fabricated filaments.

Morphological features of developed 3D-printed scaffolds. FESEM observations at 500 µm scale after lyophilization procedure and their corresponded EDS analysis.

In contrast, the incorporation of ceramic fillers into the Alg-based scaffolds resulted in a marked improvement in structural integrity and shape preservation. The ceramic components enhanced the rigidity of the printed filaments and contributed to maintaining the predefined scaffold geometry after printing and crosslinking. However, increasing the ceramic content in the ink formulation led to a substantial rise in viscosity, which in turn introduced challenges during the printing process, including nozzle clogging caused by nanoparticle agglomeration.

To further enhance the physical, biological, and surface-related properties of the scaffolds, a GelMA hydrogel coating was applied. FESEM observations revealed that the GelMA layer covered the surface of the filaments without occluding the interconnected pore network, thereby preserving scaffold porosity. This surface modification is evident in Fig. 3 where a uniform coating can be distinguished along the strut surfaces. Based on the FESEM images, the pore diameters were estimated to be in the range of approximately 450–650 µm, a pore size considered favorable for bone tissue engineering applications due to its suitability for cell infiltration and nutrient transport. Note that the 450–650 µm pores are calculated after crosslinking and freeze-drying, in the dry state. The layer height was ≈350–400 µm.

FTIR analysis

Figure 4 shows the FTIR spectra of the developed scaffolds. Peaks observed at 1016–1117 cm-1 correspond to C–O–C and C–O stretching vibrations of the polysaccharide backbone. The HA spectrum exhibits phosphate (PO43-) bending bands at 566 and 605 cm-1 and a strong asymmetric stretching band at ~ 1036 cm-1 (Fig. 4B). Carbonate-related bands appear at 1385 and 1436 cm-1. Bands at 1635 and ~ 3440 cm-1 are attributed to adsorbed water and O–H stretching vibrations.

Mechanical performance

Specimens prepared for mechanical evaluation with dimensions of 6 × 10 × 10 mm were subjected to uniaxial compressive loading to assess their mechanical performance. The stress–strain curves for all the 7 groups are shown in Fig. 5. Also, Fig. 6A summarizes all the specimens’ data on a diagram. According to the obtained results, scaffolds composed solely of Alg exhibited a maximum elastic modulus of 37.35 ± 0.73 MPa. In contrast, composite scaffolds containing ceramic phases and subsequently coated with GelMA demonstrated a substantial enhancement in mechanical performance, reaching elastic modulus as high as 82.04 ± 0.50 MPa. This marked improvement indicates that the incorporation of ceramic fillers, combined with GelMA surface modification, significantly reinforced the scaffold structure under compressive stress. The observed increase in elastic modulus suggests that GelMA coating contributes to improved load distribution and filament bonding within the scaffold architecture. Therefore, GelMA surface modification can be considered a rational and promising strategy for enhancing the mechanical stability of extrusion-based 3D-printed bone scaffolds, particularly in applications where mechanical integrity is critical.

Biodegradability properties

The degradation behavior of the fabricated scaffolds was evaluated by immersing the samples in PBS for predetermined time intervals of 1, 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days. Throughout the degradation study, the mass loss of the scaffolds was monitored in order to assess their stability under physiological-like conditions. The average remaining mass of the scaffolds after each degradation period is summarized in Fig. 6B. The results indicate a time-dependent degradation behavior, with a gradual decrease in sample mass observed as the immersion duration increased. This controlled degradation profile is advantageous for BTE applications, as it allows the scaffold to maintain structural integrity during the early stages of regeneration while gradually degrading to accommodate new tissue formation.

Bioactivity properties

To assess the bioactivity of the fabricated scaffolds, the samples were immersed in SBF for predetermined time intervals. Subsequent examination of the scaffold surfaces using FESEM revealed the formation and deposition of calcium phosphate mineral phases on the scaffold surfaces (Fig. 7). FESEM observations obtained at immersion periods of 1, 3, 7, 14, and 21 days demonstrated a progressive increase in the amount of deposited mineral content over time, indicating the continuous nucleation and growth of calcium phosphate layers on the scaffold surfaces. Notably, scaffolds coated with GelMA exhibited a more pronounced and uniform mineral deposition compared to uncoated samples. This enhanced mineralization behavior suggests that the GelMA coating provided additional nucleation sites and improved surface bioactivity, thereby facilitating calcium phosphate precipitation. Hence, the increased mineral deposition observed on GelMA-coated scaffolds indicates superior in vitro bioactivity, which is considered a predictive indicator of improved in vivo bone-bonding capability and osteoconductive performance. Discussion

In this study, a novel core–shell bone scaffold was developed by integrating a robocasting-printed Alg/ceramic core with a UV-curable GelMA hydrogel shell. The extrusion-based 3D printing technique allowed for the creation of precise, interconnected architectures with controllable pore sizes (approximately 400–650 µm), which is essential for supporting cell infiltration, nutrient transport, and vascularization. Similar to the strategy described in the template, this architecture aims to mimic the natural bone ECM to provide an appropriate niche for tissue regeneration.

The incorporation of ceramic phases (HA, β-TCP, and BCP) into the Alg matrix was critical for overcoming the limitations of single-component bioinks. Results indicated that pure Alg scaffolds suffered from significant distortion and structural collapse following ionic cross-linking with calcium chloride. By adding ceramic fillers, the rigidity of the filaments was enhanced, maintaining the predefined geometry and minimizing microcracks. This aligns with our previous findings suggesting that ceramic nanopowders govern the printability and structural stability of polymer-based inks8,12. However, the study also noted that excessive ceramic content could lead to nozzle clogging due to nanoparticle agglomeration, necessitating an optimized solid-to-liquid ratio for effective robocasting.

One of the most significant findings was the reinforcement of the scaffold through the GelMA coating. While pure Alg scaffolds provided an elastic modulus of only 37.35 ± 0.73 MPa, the GelMA-coated BCP composite scaffolds reached values as high as 82.04 ± 0.50 MPa. This substantial improvement is attributed to the GelMA shell’s ability to improve load distribution and filament bonding within the lattice structure. As noted in the literature, the UV-cross-linked hydrogel shell forms strong intermolecular chains around the printed filaments, which significantly elevates the compressive strength and stiffness compared to bare scaffolds22.

The scaffolds exhibited a time-dependent degradation profile when immersed in PBS. This controlled mass loss is a vital attribute for BTE, as the scaffold must provide initial structural support while gradually degrading to make room for new bone formation. Studies suggests that hydrophilic functional groups (such as amide and hydroxyl groups) on the surface of GelMA-coated specimens can influence the degradation rate, facilitating the transition from artificial support to natural tissue23,24.

Bioactivity evaluation in SBF confirmed that the BCP-containing scaffolds promote the nucleation and growth of calcium phosphate mineral phases. Scaffolds coated with GelMA showed a more pronounced and uniform mineral deposition than uncoated samples. This enhancement is likely due to the functional groups in GelMA (carboxyl and amide moieties) providing additional nucleation sites that facilitate ion adsorption from the surrounding fluid25,26. The GelMA shell modifies the microenvironment to be more biologically favorable, ultimately predicting improved in vivo bone-bonding capability.

The bioactivity of the fabricated scaffolds was systematically investigated through EDS analysis following immersion in SBF at different time points (0, 3, and 21 days), as presented in Fig. 7. The evolution of elemental composition provides valuable insight into the surface reactivity, mineralization behavior, and apatite-forming capability of the different scaffold formulations.

The EDS spectra of the pure alginate scaffolds are dominated by carbon (C) and oxygen (O) peaks at all immersion times, reflecting the organic polymeric nature of Alg. At day 0, only trace amounts of calcium (Ca) and phosphorus (P) are detected. After 3 and 21 days of SBF immersion, a slight increase in Ca and P signal intensity is observed, indicating limited adsorption of ions from the SBF. However, the relatively weak and inconsistent Ca and P peaks suggest insufficient nucleation and growth of a calcium phosphate layer. This confirms the inherently low bioactivity of Alg, which lacks intrinsic bioactive sites capable of inducing apatite formation.

The incorporation of calcium phosphate phases significantly alters the elemental evolution of the scaffolds. In the Alg/HA group, distinct Ca and P peaks are already present at day 0 due to the intrinsic composition of HA. Following SBF immersion, the intensities of these peaks increase progressively, particularly at 21 days, indicating sustained deposition of calcium phosphate species and surface mineral maturation. The Ca/P ratio tends to approach that of stoichiometric or slightly calcium-deficient HA, which closely resembles the mineral phase of natural bone.

In contrast, the Alg/β-TCP scaffolds show a more rapid change in elemental composition at earlier immersion times. The higher solubility of β-TCP leads to accelerated ion exchange with the surrounding SBF, resulting in pronounced Ca and P peaks at 3 days. This dissolution–precipitation mechanism enhances early-stage bioactivity but may lead to less stable mineral phases over prolonged immersion, as evidenced by fluctuations in Ca/P peak intensity at 21 days. Such behavior is consistent with the known resorbable nature of β-TCP and its role in promoting early mineralization.

The BCP-containing scaffolds demonstrate a synergistic bioactivity profile that depends strongly on the HA/β-TCP ratio. The BCP60/40 scaffolds exhibit a balanced increase in Ca and P peaks over time, suggesting controlled ion release from β-TCP combined with the structural stability provided by HA. This composition promotes gradual formation of a calcium phosphate-rich layer, leading to a more uniform and mature apatite phase at 21 days.

Conversely, the BCP20/80 scaffolds display more pronounced Ca and P signals at earlier time points due to the higher β-TCP content. While this enhances initial mineral deposition, the relatively higher dissolution rate may influence long-term mineral stability. Nonetheless, both BCP systems outperform single-phase composites in terms of sustained bioactivity, highlighting the advantage of BCP in tailoring mineralization kinetics.

The GelMA-coated BCP scaffolds (GelMA-BCP60/40 and GelMA-BCP20/80) exhibit the most pronounced bioactivity among all groups. Although the presence of GelMA initially increases the relative intensity of C and O peaks at day 0, a substantial enhancement in Ca and P peak intensity is observed after 3 and 21 days of SBF immersion. This indicates that the GelMA layer does not inhibit mineralization; rather, it facilitates apatite nucleation by providing functional groups (e.g., carboxyl and amide moieties) that promote ion adsorption and heterogeneous nucleation27.

At 21 days, both GelMA-coated BCP scaffolds show strong and well-defined Ca and P peaks, with Ca/P ratios approaching those of bone-like apatite. The GelMA-BCP60/40 scaffold, in particular, demonstrates a more stable and homogeneous mineral phase, likely due to the optimized balance between HA stability, β-TCP reactivity, and GelMA-mediated ion binding. These findings suggest that GelMA coating enhances not only surface bioactivity but also the maturity and uniformity of the mineralized layer.

Collectively, the EDS results clearly demonstrate that scaffold composition plays a critical role in governing in vitro bioactivity. Pure alginate exhibits minimal apatite-forming ability, whereas calcium phosphate-containing scaffolds significantly enhance surface mineralization. BCP systems offer a tunable balance between early ion release and long-term stability, and GelMA coating further amplifies this effect by promoting biologically favorable mineral deposition. The formation of a calcium phosphate-rich layer with Ca/P ratios comparable to natural bone strongly suggests that the GelMA-coated BCP scaffolds possess superior osteoconductive potential and are promising candidates for bone regeneration applications.

The observed regenerative benefits of our scaffolds are likely attributable not only to their biochemical composition but only to their precisely engineered 3D architecture28. The interconnected porous network, fabricated via extrusion-based 3D printing, provides critical structural advantages for tissue regeneration. Firstly, the high porosity and interconnectivity facilitate efficient nutrient and oxygen diffusion throughout the scaffold, preventing the formation of necrotic cores and ensuring a viable microenvironment for seeded or infiltrating cells8. Secondly, the pore dimensions and open channels are designed to promote rapid cell infiltration and migration, enabling host cells to populate the entire construct and supporting uniform tissue ingrowth11,12. This open architecture also enhances waste product removal and allows for extensive neovascularization, which is essential for sustaining new bone formation. Consequently, the 3D lattice acts as a permissive physical guide that directs cell organization, matrix deposition, and ultimately, the functional integration of the newly formed tissue with the host environment.

Beyond the structural and biocompatibility properties investigated here, the composition of the developed BCP-alginate/GelMA scaffolds suggests potential for extending their application to antibacterial bone regeneration. Implant-associated infections, such as osteomyelitis, remain a significant clinical challenge. A promising strategy involves the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) at the implant site to disrupt bacterial membranes and biofilms. Notably, biphasic calcium phosphate ceramics like those used in this study serve as an excellent platform for incorporating antibacterial metal ions (e.g., silver, zinc, or cerium) via substitution within the crystal lattice29. Such doped ceramics can exhibit intrinsic ROS-generating capacity or enable light-activated ROS production. Furthermore, the alginate/GelMA hydrogel matrix could potentially be leveraged to encapsulate and locally release ROS-generating agents or antibiotics. Future work will focus on functionalizing the scaffold with ROS-active dopants and evaluating its efficacy against common pathogens like Staphylococcus aureus to develop a truly multifunctional, infection-resistant bone graft substitute.

The combination of a mechanically resilient ceramic-polymer core and a bioactive GelMA shell addresses the common trade-off between mechanical strength and biological functionality. By tailoring the HA/β-TCP ratios and applying a GelMA surface modification, these 3D-printed constructs offer a tunable platform for treating complex-shaped bone defects. While these in vitro results are promising, future studies in animal models are necessary to validate the scaffolds’ osteoinductive performance in a clinical setting.

Conclusions

In this study, a multifunctional core–shell scaffold was successfully engineered by combining extrusion-based 3D printing of alginate/ceramic composites with a bioactive GelMA hydrogel coating. The results demonstrate that incorporating calcium phosphate phases, particularly biphasic calcium phosphate, effectively overcomes the structural limitations of pure alginate scaffolds by enhancing printability, shape fidelity, and mechanical stability. Moreover, tailoring the HA/β-TCP ratio enabled control over mineralization behavior and degradation kinetics, offering a versatile strategy for optimizing scaffold performance.

The application of a GelMA hydrogel shell played a critical role in further improving scaffold functionality. GelMA-coated scaffolds exhibited significantly enhanced compressive properties, controlled biodegradation, and superior in vitro bioactivity compared to uncoated counterparts. The presence of GelMA facilitated uniform calcium phosphate deposition and promoted the formation of bone-like apatite layers, indicating improved osteoconductive potential. Importantly, the GelMA coating preserved scaffold porosity while providing a biologically favorable surface enriched with functional groups that support mineral nucleation.

Collectively, the integration of a mechanically robust ceramic–polymer lattice with a bioactive hydrogel shell addresses the inherent trade-off between mechanical strength and biological performance in bone scaffold design. This modular and tunable scaffold platform shows strong potential for the regeneration of complex bone defects. Future in vivo investigations are warranted to validate its osteogenic efficacy and long-term performance in clinical applications.
