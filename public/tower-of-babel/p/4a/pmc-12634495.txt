Letter to the Editor Regarding “Utility of Frailty Index in Predicting Discharge Disposition and Prolonged Length of Stay Following Enhanced Recovery After Surgery Protocol Total Hip and Knee Arthroplasty”
Authors: Parth Aphale, Himanshu Shekhar, Shashank Dokania
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the study by Issa et al. examining the comparative utility of the 5-item Modified Frailty Index (mFI-5) and the Risk Assessment and Prediction Tool in forecasting discharge disposition and prolonged length of stay (LOS) following total hip and knee arthroplasty within the context of an enhanced recovery after surgery protocol [1]. While the authors provide valuable data supporting the use of these tools in discharge planning, several aspects merit critical consideration regarding their clinical applicability, methodological scope, and implications for future research.

A central concern arises from the limited predictive performance of both mFI-5 and the Risk Assessment and Prediction Tool in relation to complications, readmissions, and prolonged LOS, with area under the curve values below 0.7. This raises the question of whether these tools, when used in isolation, are sufficient for robust preoperative risk stratification. Would integrating frailty indices with comorbidity measures such as the Charlson Comorbidity Index or employing multivariable machine learning models enhance the discriminatory capacity of risk predictions, particularly for clinically significant outcomes [2]? Moreover, the substantial proportion of patients categorized as “prefrail” or “moderate risk” presents a practical challenge. These intermediate categories, while statistically useful, may provide limited clinical guidance. Could continuous scoring models or frailty indices based on deficit accumulation offer a more nuanced and individualized risk profile [3]?

Another noteworthy limitation is the exclusive focus on administrative outcomes such as LOS and discharge disposition. Although these are relevant from a health system perspective, they may not reflect the patient-centered dimensions of postoperative recovery. The absence of functional outcomes, such as early ambulation or return to baseline activity, restricts the interpretability of the frailty scores in the context of rehabilitation planning. Would the incorporation of validated functional assessment tools, such as the Cumulated Ambulation Score or Activity Measure for Post-Acute Care “6-Clicks,” provide a more holistic evaluation of frailty’s impact on recovery [4]?

Furthermore, the single-center design and uniform enhanced recovery after surgery protocol limit generalizability. While standardization enhances internal validity, how might differences in discharge infrastructure, rehabilitation services, or patient social support across institutions affect the utility and thresholds of these indices? Finally, although the authors demonstrate that mFI-5 can aid in predicting discharge disposition, its potential as a multidimensional risk tool remains underexplored. Can frailty indices be expanded to inform perioperative interventions, such as prehabilitation, anesthesia planning, or postoperative monitoring, in a more comprehensive care model [5]?

In summary, while the study by Issa et al. contributes to the growing literature on frailty-informed surgical care, its findings highlight the limitations of existing indices and the need for more integrative, functionally oriented, and externally validated risk stratification frameworks. Addressing these considerations could substantially improve perioperative decision-making and enhance the personalization of care in arthroplasty.
