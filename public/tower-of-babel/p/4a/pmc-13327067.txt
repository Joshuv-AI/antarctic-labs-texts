Perioperative Management for Patients With Spinal Muscular Atrophy Undergoing Scoliosis Surgery: A Single‐Center Retrospective Study
Authors: Ai Hu, Xiuhua Zhang, Lulu Ma, Haisong Lu, Yulei Dong, Weiyun Chen, Yuguang Huang, Jianxiong Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveSpinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is a rare neuromuscular disorder frequently associated with progressive scoliosis, often requiring complex surgical intervention. Perioperative management for SMA patients remains challenging due to severe respiratory compromise, difficult airway anatomy, and a high risk of perioperative complications. This study aimed to analyze perioperative strategies and outcomes in SMA patients undergoing posterior spinal fusion for scoliosis correction.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed SMA patients who underwent posterior spinal fusion at our institution from January 2018 to December 2024. Primary outcomes included intraoperative blood loss, postoperative complications, and perioperative hypokalemia; secondary outcomes included postoperative mechanical ventilation duration, length of stay in the ICU and hospital, and radiographic outcomes. Subgroup comparisons between SMA type 2 and type 3 were performed using the independent‐samples t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables.ResultsForty‐three SMA patients (72.1% type 2, 27.9% type 3) with a mean age of 15.9 ± 4.9 years were included. Spinal deformity was severe, with a mean preoperative Cobb angle of 113.2° ± 22.8°. Pulmonary function was markedly impaired, with a median forced vital capacity (FVC) of 34.8% (IQR: 24.6%–46.4%) of the predicted value. Endotracheal intubation was challenging, with 93.0% (n = 40) requiring video laryngoscopy or awake tracheal intubation using flexible bronchoscopy. Blood loss was substantial, with a median intraoperative blood loss of 1200 mL (IQR: 1000–1550 mL) and a postoperative drainage of 1040 mL (IQR: 723–1293 mL). An extraordinarily high incidence of perioperative hypokalemia was observed, affecting 65.1% of patients (n = 28) intraoperatively and 79.1% (n = 34) postoperatively. All patients successfully underwent surgery with a mean Cobb angle correction rate of 49.0% ± 13.5%. There were no cases of operative mortality, and the overall complication rate was low (2.3%, n = 1). SMA type 2 and type 3 shared similar perioperative parameters.ConclusionsThis study represents the largest case series to date in Asia on the perioperative management of SMA patients undergoing scoliosis surgery. These patients present complex perioperative risks requiring meticulous multidisciplinary care. Advanced airway planning, hemodynamic support, blood management strategies, and proactive correction of electrolyte imbalances may help support safer perioperative care. With appropriate planning, scoliosis surgery can be safely performed in SMA patients, offering structural and functional benefits.

Introduction

Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is a rare autosomal recessive neuromuscular disorder caused by defects in the Survival Motor Neuron 1 (SMN1) gene, leading to an insufficient production of the survival motor neuron (SMN) protein [1, 2]. This deficiency results in progressive degeneration of anterior horn cells in the spinal cord and subsequent muscle atrophy, manifesting in various clinical phenotypes [3, 4]. Based on the age of onset and maximum motor function achieved, SMA is classified into four types. Type 1 is the most severe, presenting in infancy with profound muscle weakness and typically resulting in death during early childhood. Type 4 is the mildest form, with adult onset and patients attaining normal motor milestones [5]. Types 2 and 3 fall between these extremes, characterized by progressive motor decline and muscle weakness, often leading to the development of scoliosis that eventually requires surgical correction [6, 7].

The primary goals of surgical treatment for scoliosis in SMA patients are to prevent the progression of spine deformity, improve sitting balance and respiratory mechanics, thereby enhancing quality of life [8, 9]. These objectives are particularly important given that individuals with SMA usually have normal cognitive and emotional development, and may even demonstrate advanced verbal abilities compared to their peers [10]. However, spinal surgery in this population poses considerable challenges due to the complexity of surgical techniques required and the heightened risk of perioperative complications, particularly respiratory issues related to profound muscle weakness and severely impaired pulmonary function [11, 12]. Although a few small retrospective studies have offered valuable insights, there remains a lack of comprehensive data and no established evidence‐based guidelines for the perioperative management of these patients [13, 14, 15, 16].

In this study, we sought to summarize our institutional experience and provide practical insights into the perioperative management of SMA patients undergoing scoliosis correction. Building on prior small cohorts that have demonstrated the feasibility of surgery, this larger case series further characterizes the associated perioperative risks, particularly in patients with severe restrictive lung disease. We also highlight underrecognized clinical pitfalls, with particular attention to perioperative electrolyte disturbances. We hope these findings may help inform preoperative evaluation, anesthetic optimization, and postoperative care in this vulnerable population.

Methods

Participants

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (No. I‐25PJ3332). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal guardians prior to participation. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki as well as all relevant legal requirements and institutional guidelines.

Study Design

We retrospectively reviewed the electronic medical records of all patients diagnosed with SMA who underwent posterior spinal instrumentation and fusion for scoliosis correction between January 2018 and December 2024. The clinical diagnosis of SMA was initially suspected based on progressive motor weakness and definitively confirmed in all patients via genetic testing demonstrating biallelic pathogenic variants in the SMN1 gene. Data were extracted from the institutional database.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) definitively confirmed diagnosis of SMA via genetic testing; and (2) underwent posterior spinal instrumentation and fusion for scoliosis correction. The exclusion criteria were: (1) previous spinal fusion surgeries; (2) concomitant major surgical procedures during the same anesthesia; and (3) incomplete perioperative medical records.

Preoperative information, including patient demographics, cardiac and pulmonary assessments, and laboratory results, was documented. The use of mechanical ventilation support, spinal traction, and treatment with nusinersen was also recorded. Intraoperative surgical and anesthetic details were collected, as well as postoperative laboratory results, complications, and lengths of stay in the hospital and intensive care unit (ICU). Preoperative Assessment and Optimization

Standardized preoperative evaluations were conducted for all patients. Airway anatomy was assessed using the Mallampati classification system (Classes I through IV, based on the visibility of the soft palate, uvula, and faucial pillars with the mouth open and tongue protruded). Physical status was categorized using the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, where ASA class II indicates mild systemic disease and ASA class III indicates severe systemic disease that limits activity but is not incapacitating. Preoperative multidisciplinary optimization was routinely implemented, including targeted pulmonary rehabilitation, initiation of non‐invasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV) when indicated, and halo‐gravity traction (HGT) for severe, rigid deformities.

Standard definitions were utilized for perioperative laboratory variables: hypercarbia was defined as PaCO2 > 45 mmHg, and mild hypoxemia as 60 mmHg < PaO2 < 80 mmHg on room air. Anemia severity was classified as mild (90 ≤ Hb < 110 g/L for females, 90 ≤ Hb < 120 g/L for males), moderate (60 ≤ Hb < 90 g/L), and severe (Hb < 60 g/L). Hypokalemia was categorized as mild (3.0–3.4 mmol/L) and severe (< 3.0 mmol/L).

Anesthetic Management

Anesthesia was induced using target‐controlled infusion (TCI) of propofol, combined with fentanyl (2 μg/kg) and rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg). To facilitate continuous intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM), no additional neuromuscular blocking agents were administered after induction. Maintenance of anesthesia consisted of TCI propofol, titrated to a bispectral index (BIS) of 40–60, together with continuous remifentanil infusion (0.1–0.2 μg/kg/min). Intermittent fentanyl boluses were administered only when needed to treat transient intense surgical stimulation, such as incision or pedicle screw placement. Normothermia was maintained throughout surgery using forced‐air warming blankets and warmed intravenous fluids.

Intraoperative Fluid and Blood Management

Goal‐directed fluid therapy (GDFT) was implemented in all patients. Fluid management was primarily guided by dynamic hemodynamic indices, particularly pulse pressure variation (PPV) derived from continuous invasive arterial pressure monitoring. In selected patients, especially those with severe thoracic deformity or marked low body mass, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) was additionally used to assess cardiac filling and guide volume resuscitation. Lactated Ringer's solution and hydroxyethyl starch (HES) were used as the principal resuscitation fluids. Given the substantial blood loss anticipated during long‐segment spinal fusion, multimodal blood conservation strategies were routinely adopted, including administration of tranexamic acid (TXA) as a single 1 g intravenous bolus before skin incision and the use of intraoperative cell salvage in all patients.

Surgical Procedure

All patients underwent posterior spinal instrumentation and fusion. The surgical procedure involved a standard posterior midline exposure, pedicle screw insertion, rod contouring for deformity correction, and posterolateral bone grafting. The decision to extend fusion to the pelvis was individualized based on the severity of pelvic obliquity [8]. To accommodate disease‐specific postoperative treatment needs, unilateral interlaminar fenestration at the L2–L3 or L3–L4 level was routinely performed during fusion to preserve an accessible route for long‐term postoperative intrathecal nusinersen administration [17].

Postoperative Management

Following surgery, the need for ICU admission was determined individually based on the overall preoperative evaluation. In patients considered at higher risk, postoperative respiratory management in the ICU followed a step‐down approach, with high‐flow nasal cannula (HFNC) typically used immediately after extubation. In those who were already dependent on non‐invasive ventilation (NIV) preoperatively, NIV was resumed as maintenance therapy after transfer to the general orthopedic ward. Pain control was achieved with a multimodal approach, including local anesthetic infiltration at the wound site, intravenous sufentanil‐based patient‐controlled analgesia (PCA), and oral ibuprofen. Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) prophylaxis mainly consisted of passive lower‐limb movement and intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC), whereas pharmacologic prophylaxis and standard compression stockings were individualized in view of bleeding concerns and severe lower‐extremity muscle wasting.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome measures were intraoperative blood loss, postoperative complications, and the occurrence and severity of perioperative hypokalemia. Hypokalemia was monitored using serial arterial blood gas (ABG) measurements intraoperatively and serum biochemistry testing postoperatively, and was categorized as mild (3.0–3.4 mmol/L) or severe (< 3.0 mmol/L). Postoperative complications were defined as clinically significant adverse events occurring within 30 days postoperatively, including events requiring additional medical or surgical intervention.

Secondary outcome measures included postoperative mechanical ventilation duration, length of stay in the ICU and hospital, and radiographic outcomes. Radiographic evaluation focused on the Cobb angle of the major curve, measured on standard whole‐spine anteroposterior radiographs. The correction rate was calculated as follows: (preoperative Cobb angle − postoperative Cobb angle) / preoperative Cobb angle × 100%.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data and as median with interquartile range (IQR) for non‐normally distributed data. Categorical variables are presented as counts with corresponding percentages.

To explore potential differences in perioperative characteristics and outcomes between phenotypic subtypes, a subgroup analysis comparing patients with SMA type 2 and type 3 was performed. Variables with normal distributions were compared using independent samples t‐tests, while non‐normally distributed variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Two‐sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.3) within RStudio (version 2024.12.1 + 563, Posit Software, Boston, MA, USA).

Results

Demographics and Preoperative Evaluation

A total of 43 patients with spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) who underwent posterior spinal instrumentation and fusion for scoliosis correction were included in this study. The mean age of the cohort was 15.9 ± 4.9 years, including 26 female patients (60.5%) and 17 male patients (39.5%). The mean height and weight were 150.1 ± 12.8 cm and 39.3 ± 14.0 kg, respectively, with a mean body mass index (BMI) of 17.3 ± 5.0 kg/m2. Among these patients, 31 (72.1%) had SMA type 2, whereas 12 (27.9%) had SMA type 3. According to the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, the majority of patients (72.1%, n = 31) were ASA class III and the remaining 27.9% (n = 12) were ASA class II.

Pulmonary function was significantly impaired in most patients. The median forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) was 0.91 L (IQR: 0.63–1.40 L), with FEV1 as a percentage of the predicted value at 36.9% (IQR: 25.0%–51.0%). The median forced vital capacity (FVC) was 1.02 L (IQR: 0.67–1.56 L), corresponding to 34.8% (IQR: 24.6%–46.4%) of the predicted value, reflecting severe restrictive lung disease. Specifically, 58.1% of patients (n = 25) had either severe pulmonary dysfunction (FVC < 40%) or were unable to complete pulmonary function testing. Moderate dysfunction (FVC 40%–60%) was observed in 13 patients (30.2%), whereas only 5 patients (11.6%) demonstrated mild dysfunction (FVC 60%–80%) or normal pulmonary function (FVC > 80%). Eight patients (18.6%) were already dependent on nocturnal home NIPPV due to chronic respiratory insufficiency prior to surgery. However, arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis was largely within normal limits. The mean preoperative partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2) was 38.6 ± 4.1 mmHg, and the mean partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) was 88.6 ± 12.0 mmHg. Only 2 patients (4.7%) exhibited hypercapnia, and 8 patients (18.6%) had mild hypoxemia preoperatively.

Preoperative cardiac evaluation was normal in most patients (90.7%, n = 39), based on electrocardiography (ECG) and echocardiography. Mild abnormalities were noted in four patients (9.3%), including mitral valve anterior leaflet prolapse, mild thickening of the left ventricular wall or septum, and small pericardial effusions. Preoperative anemia was rare, identified in only one patient (2.3%) with a baseline hemoglobin of 106 g/L. The mean hemoglobin level across the cohort was 132.4 ± 13.3 g/L. Baseline preoperative serum potassium levels were within the normal range for all patients, with an overall mean of 4.07 ± 0.34 mmol/L. No patient presented with preoperative hypokalemia, with a minimum recorded value of 3.5 mmol/L.

The mean preoperative Cobb angle was 113.2° ± 22.8°, indicating severe scoliosis in all cases. Imaging studies of two representative SMA patients are shown in Figure 2. Halo‐gravity traction (HGT) was employed preoperatively in 14 patients (32.6%) to aid in spinal alignment. Additionally, 12 patients (27.9%) had received intrathecal nusinersen therapy, a disease‐modifying treatment for SMA, prior to surgery. Anesthesia Induction and Endotracheal Intubation

Preoperative airway assessment using the Mallampati classification revealed that 58.1% (n = 25) of patients were classified as Mallampati class III or IV, suggesting a high likelihood of difficult airway conditions. Additionally, 8 patients (18.6%) exhibited limited neck mobility, and 9 patients (20.9%) had restricted mouth opening, both of which further complicated endotracheal intubation. An example of a patient with both severely limited mouth opening and neck mobility is illustrated in Figure 3.

Airway assessment in a 20‐year‐old male with SMA type 2 scheduled for posterior spinal fusion surgery. (A) Frontal view demonstrating severely restricted mouth opening. (B) Lateral view illustrating markedly limited mandibular protrusion and neck mobility.

In terms of intubation strategy, most patients (86.0%, n = 37) underwent successful endotracheal intubation after general anesthesia induction. However, 6 patients (14.0%) required awake tracheal intubation using flexible bronchoscopy due to concerns about potential airway obstruction or respiratory compromise.

Various techniques were utilized to achieve safe endotracheal intubation. Video‐assisted laryngoscopy was the most commonly employed method, used in a total of 35 patients (81.4%). Based on blade design, these were classified into non‐channeled devices (Macintosh‐shaped blades, 34 cases) and channeled devices (Airtraq; Prodol Meditec S.A., Vizcaya, Spain, 1 case). Awake tracheal intubation using flexible bronchoscopy was performed in 6 patients (14.0%), including oral intubation in 2 cases (4.7%) and nasal intubation in 4 cases (9.3%), primarily in patients with severe airway limitations. Direct laryngoscopy was used in only 2 patients (4.7%). Preoperative airway assessments and the distribution of endotracheal intubation techniques used in this cohort are presented in Table 2.

Intraoperative Anesthesia Management and Surgical Information

Details regarding intraoperative anesthesia management and surgical information are presented in Table 3. All patients underwent invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring, and 72.1% (n = 31) received central venous catheter (CVC) placement. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) was utilized in one extremely cachectic patient (BMI 10.6 kg/m2) with severe thoracic deformity, primarily to guide safe prone positioning and monitor for cardiac compression. The mean duration of anesthesia was 552 ± 88 min, and the mean operation time was 447 ± 76 min. All patients underwent posterior spinal instrumentation and fusion, with a mean of 16.7 ± 1.4 vertebral segments fused. Pelvic fusion was performed in 34 patients (79.1%).

The median intraoperative blood loss was 1200 mL (IQR: 1000–1550 mL), and 93.0% (n = 40) of patients required allogeneic transfusion. The median number of packed red blood cell (RBC) units transfused was 4 (IQR: 2–6), while the median volume of fresh frozen plasma (FFP) administered was 600 mL (IQR: 400–800 mL). Intraoperative autologous blood transfusion using a cell saver system was performed in all patients, with a median autotransfusion volume of 527 mL (IQR: 367–828 mL). Median intraoperative fluid administration included 3500 mL (IQR: 3000–4350 mL) of crystalloids and 500 mL (IQR: 0–750 mL) of colloids. The median urinary output during surgery was 900 mL (IQR: 550–1300 mL). Vasoactive support was frequently required to maintain adequate intraoperative hemodynamic stability, with phenylephrine and norepinephrine used in 48.8% (n = 21) and 32.6% (n = 12) of patients, respectively. Severe hemodynamic instability occurred in three patients (7.0%). Two of these patients developed severe anaphylactic shock after fresh frozen plasma (FFP) transfusion and required rescue treatment with epinephrine. The third patient experienced transient circulatory collapse during prone positioning due to mechanical cardiac compression related to severe thoracic deformity [18]. Electrolyte imbalances, particularly hypokalemia, were commonly observed. Intraoperative hypokalemia occurred in 65.1% of patients (n = 28), necessitating potassium supplementation in 44.2% (n = 19) of cases.

Postoperative Management and Complications

Key postoperative outcomes and complications are summarized in Table 4. Most patients (95.3%, n = 41) required admission to the ICU immediately after surgery for close monitoring. The median ICU stay was 21 h (IQR: 19–25 h). Mechanical ventilation was continued postoperatively for a median of 15 h (IQR: 14–23 h) before extubation. Prolonged mechanical ventilation occurred in 5 patients (11.6%) who failed a spontaneous breathing trial in the first 24 h after surgery. All of these patients successfully weaned and extubated no later than POD 3, without pulmonary complications.

The median volume of postoperative drainage was 1040 mL (IQR: 723–1293 mL) and postoperative anemia was universally present in all patients (100%, n = 43) with varied severity. Moderate anemia was the most common, affecting 65.1% of patients (n = 28), whereas 34.9% (n = 15) had mild anemia. No cases of severe anemia were observed. The mean lowest postoperative hemoglobin level was 88.1 ± 8.7 g/L, with the nadir occurring on postoperative day (POD) 2 (IQR for day of nadir: 1–4 days post‐op). Allogeneic blood transfusion was required postoperatively in 62.3% of patients (n = 27).

Postoperative hypokalemia was highly prevalent, affecting 79.1% of patients (n = 34). Among these, 48.8% (n = 21) had mild hypokalemia and 30.2% (n = 13) developed severe hypokalemia. The first episode of low potassium typically occurred by POD 1 (median POD 1, IQR: 0–2 days), necessitating aggressive potassium supplementation in these affected patients. For the entire cohort, the lowest recorded postoperative potassium level was 3.1 ± 0.4 mmol/L observed on POD 2 (IQR: 0.5–3 days). In an exploratory effort to identify clinical features associated with this severe electrolyte disturbance, a preliminary comparison of baseline and intraoperative characteristics was performed between patients with and without severe postoperative hypokalemia (< 3.0 mmol/L), as shown in Table S1. Patients who developed severe hypokalemia appeared to have slightly lower baseline PaO2 (84.3 ± 6.1 vs. 90.5 ± 13.4 mmHg, p = 0.044) and a higher frequency of abnormal preoperative cardiac evaluations (23.1% vs. 3.3%, p = 0.041). Other factors, including BMI, operation time, and estimated blood loss, showed no statistically significant differences (all p > 0.05).

The overall rate of complications was low. There were no cases of operative mortality, thromboembolic events, neurologic, or pulmonary complications. One patient (2.3%) required reoperation for debridement due to surgical site infection.

The mean postoperative Cobb angle was corrected to 59.1° ± 22.3°, corresponding to an average correction rate of 49.0% ± 13.5%. Postoperative radiographs of the two representative patients are included in Figure 2.

The median length of total postoperative hospital stay was 15 days (IQR: 14–17 days). Patients were discharged after achieving stable respiratory function, adequate pain control, and correction of electrolyte imbalances.

Comparison Between SMA Type 2 and Type 3 Patients

Table 5 presents the results of a subgroup analysis comparing patients with SMA type 2 and type 3. Demographic characteristics, intraoperative parameters, and postoperative outcomes were evaluated between the two groups. Patients with SMA type 3 had significantly longer anesthesia durations (588.1 ± 58.3 vs. 537.7 ± 94.1 min, p = 0.042) and operative times (479.4 ± 54.2 vs. 434.1 ± 79.9 min, p = 0.041) compared to those with type 2. No statistically significant differences were observed in baseline demographics, airway assessment findings, transfusion requirements, incidence of hypokalemia, or postoperative complications between the groups.

Discussion

Previous studies reporting the surgical outcomes of SMA patients have demonstrated structural benefits of such surgery, including improved sitting balance and enhanced thoracic morphology [8]. Given the high‐risk nature of SMA, particularly due to severe muscle weakness, respiratory insufficiency, nutritional deficits, and a propensity for perioperative complications, ensuring the safety and success of the surgical intervention is of paramount importance. The novelty of our findings extends beyond confirming surgical feasibility. By analyzing this large cohort, we identified the extraordinarily high incidence of perioperative hypokalemia, highlighting a potentially underrecognized clinical issue. In addition, our data suggest that severely impaired pulmonary function may not represent an absolute contraindication to surgery. With tailored perioperative planning, these vulnerable patients can achieve safe outcomes.

Preoperative Respiratory Evaluation

Pulmonary function is a major concern in SMA patients, as severe restrictive lung disease is common due to progressive scoliosis and chest wall deformities [19]. Although pulmonary function test (PFT) results are commonly used to assess surgical risk, our findings suggest that severely reduced PFT values alone should not be considered an absolute contraindication for spinal surgery. In our cohort, baseline pulmonary function was severely compromised, with a median FEV1 of 0.91 L and a median FVC of 1.02 L. More than half the patients had an FVC below 40% of the predicted value, and 11 patients (25.6%) had an absolute FEV1 < 0.5 L or were unable to complete pulmonary testing. However, preoperative arterial blood gas analysis was with normal values in most patients, indicating largely preserved gas exchange despite spirometric parameters impairment. All patients underwent successful surgery without major pulmonary complications. This contrasts with studies such as Förster et al., in which patients with FEV1 < 0.5 L were excluded from surgical consideration [15]. Given these findings, we advocate for a more comprehensive, individualized approach to surgical candidacy that incorporates not only pulmonary function but also preoperative arterial blood gas analysis, anticipated surgical benefit, and the availability of perioperative respiratory support such as non‐invasive ventilation and pulmonary rehabilitation. Ongoing follow‐up remains necessary to determine whether scoliosis correction provides sustained benefits to respiratory function [20, 21, 22].

Altough comprehensive preoperative respiratory evaluation is essential, standard assessment modalities often require adaptation in this population. Although cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) is considered the gold standard for assessing physiologic reserve, it is often not feasible in non‐ambulatory SMA patients because of profound motor weakness. In severe cases, chest CT with three‐dimensional reconstruction may help identify potential tracheobronchial compression and mediastinal distortion. In addition, polysomnography (PSG) for sleep‐related breathing disturbances and quantitative assessment of cough strength for bulbar dysfunction may provide useful information for predicting extubation success. Preoperative halo‐gravity traction may also serve as an important adjunct in patients with severe, rigid deformities, as it can gradually reduce curve magnitude and improve thoracic compliance.

Beyond respiratory assessment, preoperative nutritional optimization is another important yet challenging component of perioperative management. In our cohort, malnutrition was common, with a mean BMI of 17.3 ± 5.0 kg/m2. Routine multidisciplinary optimization includes maximizing high‐quality protein intake and using calorie‐dense oral nutritional supplements to improve energy intake while minimizing gastrointestinal volume burden. However, surgery should not be postponed indefinitely in pursuit of idealized nutritional targets, as deformity correction itself may be an important prerequisite for longer‐term nutritional recovery.

Airway Management and Intubation Strategy

Airway management in SMA patients is particularly challenging due to restricted neck mobility, limited mouth opening, and altered airway anatomy [23, 24]. Consistent with previous reports, our data show a high reliance on advanced airway techniques, including video laryngoscopy (81.4%) and awake tracheal intubation using flexible bronchoscopy (14.0%) [11, 15, 16, 25]. Severe limitation in mouth opening, which is frequently encountered in SMA patients, is often related to temporomandibular joint (TMJ) contracture and mandibular hypoplasia secondary to chronic masticatory muscle atrophy. As these anatomical constraints are unlikely to be substantially improved within the short preoperative period, meticulous airway assessment remains central to perioperative safety. Continuous mouth‐opening and chewing exercises may be considered to help preserve bulbar function.

In addition to the technical challenges of intubation, airway management in SMA must also address the increased risk of perioperative aspiration. Bulbar dysfunction is common in these patients and may substantially increase aspiration risk [26]. Furthermore, severe spinal and thoracic deformities may cause compression and displacement of the gastrointestinal tract, thereby exacerbating gastroesophageal reflux and further increasing the likelihood of aspiration. For symptomatic patients, prophylactic oral proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) may be considered on the day before surgery.

The Challenge of Intraoperative Positioning

Another important consideration for SMA patients undergoing spine surgery is the challenge of intraoperative positioning, especially due to severe thoracic deformities and limb contractures (Figure 2). These anatomical constraints can complicate prone positioning, increasing the risk of hemodynamic instability and airway compromise. In cases of extreme deformity, improper positioning can lead to thoracic cavity compression, reduced venous return, and subsequent hemodynamic instability or hypoxemia. In our cohort, one patient experienced circulatory collapse related to prone positioning [18]. To mitigate these risks, positioning should be individualized according to each patient's anatomical characteristics. Advanced intraoperative monitoring, such as transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), may be considered to facilitate safer prone positioning by helping detect acute cardiac compression from the distorted thoracic cage. In addition, TEE can provide real‐time hemodynamic assessment, including detection of dynamic outflow tract obstruction, cardiac compression, and potential venous air embolism during extensive posterior fusion [27].

Blood Management and Hemodynamic Monitoring

SMA‐related scoliosis surgery typically involves long‐segment fusion, which predisposes patients to significant intraoperative blood loss. Our study reported a median estimated blood loss of 1200 mL, with nearly all patients requiring allogeneic transfusion. These results are consistent with prior studies [15, 16]. Though GDFT with multimodal blood conservation strategies has been applied in our cohort, several aspects of patient blood management could be further refined. For example, one patient had a preoperative hemoglobin level of 106 g/L, yet management was limited to dietary advice without targeted medical intervention, suggesting a potential opportunity for more proactive optimization. In future practice, more systematic screening and earlier correction of preoperative anemia, including consideration of intravenous iron or erythropoietin where appropriate, may further strengthen perioperative preparation for SMA patients undergoing scoliosis surgery. Also, point‐of‐care viscoelastic testing was not routinely used to guide transfusion therapy. Instead, fresh frozen plasma (FFP) was administered primarily on a prophylactic basis, reflecting concern about anticipated microvascular oozing from the extensive raw bony surfaces created during long‐segment decortication and fusion. Given the substantial bleeding risk associated with these procedures, incorporation of real‐time viscoelastic testing may be helpful in the future to support more individualized transfusion management.

In addition, vascular access also presents a challenge [15]. Due to the combination of extensive surgical trauma, high fluid and transfusion requirements, frequent administration of vasoactive medications, and the poor peripheral venous conditions caused by severe muscle wasting, reliable intravenous access is crucial. Furthermore, given the substantial need for perioperative potassium supplementation, especially in the context of the high incidence of hypokalemia observed in our cohort, proactive placement of a central venous catheter should be considered.

Perioperative Hypokalemia

One of the most striking findings in this study was the extraordinarily high incidence of perioperative hypokalemia, with 65.1% of patients developing this electrolyte imbalance intraoperatively and 79.1% postoperatively, which exceeds rates previously reported in the literature [15]. Notably, approximately one‐third of the cohort experienced postoperative nadir potassium levels below 3.0 mmol/L, a degree of hypokalemia that is rarely reported outside the context of gastrointestinal surgery. Interestingly, all patients in our cohort presented with normal baseline serum potassium levels preoperatively. The underlying mechanisms remain unclear but are likely multifactorial. We hypothesize it may be related to a significantly reduced total body potassium reserve. Because skeletal muscle is the primary storage site for potassium, the profound global muscle atrophy in SMA patients likely leaves them with a marginal intracellular potassium reserve. Although extracellular serum potassium can be maintained within a normal physiological range under stable baseline conditions, the extreme surgical stress, hemodilution, and massive fluid shifts during major spinal surgery may easily exhaust this limited reserve [28]. Furthermore, our preliminary exploratory analysis observed that lower baseline PaO2 and preoperative cardiac anomalies might be associated with a higher risk of severe postoperative hypokalemia. These findings potentially suggest that SMA patients with more compromised cardiopulmonary reserve, often reflecting more advanced disease, may be more susceptible to homeostatic disruptions and stress‐induced potassium shifts during major surgical trauma. However, we must emphasize the exploratory nature of these results. Future large‐scale, prospective studies are required to validate these findings. Nevertheless, hypokalemia is particularly concerning for its potential effects on cardiac function and neuromuscular stability. Moreover, hypokalemia‐induced muscle weakness can be misinterpreted for postoperative paresis, which is especially critical in spinal surgery, where timely differentiation between true neurological deficits and reversible metabolic disturbances is essential for appropriate management [29]. Rather than a rigid threshold‐based supplementation algorithm, these findings support a practical strategy centered on frequent perioperative electrolyte surveillance and individualized potassium replacement under dynamic reassessment. Proactive central venous access, frequent arterial blood gas monitoring, and meticulously titrated continuous intravenous potassium replacement can be considered.

Practical Perioperative Considerations

The overall incidence of complications in our cohort was low, and no operative mortality was observed. Although patients with SMA type 3 had statistically longer anesthetic and operative times than those with type 2 disease, there were no significant between‐group differences in baseline anthropometric characteristics or extent of fusion. This finding therefore most likely reflects statistical variation rather than a clinically meaningful difference. Most perioperative parameters were otherwise comparable between the two subgroups, suggesting that broadly similar perioperative management principles may be applicable to both SMA type 2 and type 3 patients, while individualized planning remains essential.

Although the retrospective design of this study precludes firm conclusions regarding optimal management strategies, our analysis of this relatively large cohort nevertheless offers useful clinical insights. To summarize the perioperative issues most relevant to SMA patients undergoing scoliosis surgery, we synthesized our key findings and institutional experience into a practical perioperative management framework, presented as take‐home messages in Table 6. This framework is intended to highlight important considerations in airway and ventilatory management, draw attention to perioperative electrolyte disturbances, and suggest a modified ERAS‐oriented perspective for this non‐ambulatory population.

Strengths and Limitations

A strength of this study is the inclusion of a unique consecutive cohort of SMA patients undergoing scoliosis surgery, which provides additional clinical data on perioperative management in this high‐risk population. Despite the insights gained from this study, several limitations must be acknowledged. As a single‐center retrospective analysis, the generalizability of these findings may be limited. Additionally, while we identified a high incidence of perioperative hypokalemia, its pathophysiology and optimal management warrant further investigation. Also, long‐term follow‐up is needed to evaluate the durability of surgical benefits, particularly in respiratory function, quality of life, and deformity recurrence.

Conclusions

This study provides the largest case series in Asia to date examining the perioperative management of SMA patients undergoing scoliosis surgery. Our findings highlight the unique challenges associated with this population, including the high prevalence of difficult airway conditions, severe perioperative hypokalemia, significant intraoperative blood loss, complex positioning, and the need for advanced hemodynamic monitoring. Nonetheless, with a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach, scoliosis surgery in SMA patients can be performed safely and effectively, offering meaningful improvements in spinal alignment, sitting balance, and quality of life.
