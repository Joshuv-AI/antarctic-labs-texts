EUS-guided hepaticogastrostomy versus EUS-guided hepaticogastrostomy with antegrade stent placement in patients with unresectable malignant distal biliary obstruction: a meta-analysis of individual patient data
Authors: Mahmoud Shaaban Abdelgalil, Ahmad Abdelrazek, Mahmoud Samir Mohamed, Amro Hassan, Manar Alaa Mabrouk, Mohamed Nasser, Eyad Khalil Alhmeid, Abdullah Nizar, Abid Wazir, Noha Ali, Adam Hraybi, Ryad iqbal, Sadia Paracha, Shray Amin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn patients with malignant biliary obstruction (MBO) where endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) fails, endoscopic ultrasound-guided hepaticogastrostomy (EUS-HGS) is an important rescue option. This study compared the safety and efficacy of EUS-HGS alone versus EUS-HGS with antegrade stenting (EUS-HGAS).MethodsPubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science were searched for studies comparing EUS-HGS and EUS-HGAS in MBO. Primary outcomes included reintervention rates, and time to recurrent biliary obstruction (TRBO). Secondary outcomes were technical success, clinical success, procedure duration, and adverse events. Individual patient data for TRBO were reconstructed from Kaplan Meier curves and analyzed using restricted mean survival time.ResultsFive observational studies including 451 patients were analyzed (250 EUS-HGS, 201 EUS-HGAS). Reintervention rates were significantly higher in the EUS-HGS group compared with the EUS-HGAS group (RR = 3.00; 95% CI: 1.69–5.34; P = 0.0002). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of technical success, clinical success, procedure duration, overall adverse events, cholangitis, or bleeding. TRBO was significantly longer with EUS-HGAS (mean 651 vs. 227 days; HR = 0.15; 95% CI: 0.03,0.83; P = 0.03), indicating improved stent patency. EUS-HGS was associated with a lower risk of pancreatitis (RR 0.14; 95% CI 0.03–0.60; P=0.008) but a higher risk of bile leakage (RR 5.58; 95% CI 1.03–30.09; P=0.05).ConclusionThis meta-analysis suggests that EUS-HGAS may provide longer time to recurrent biliary obstruction and lower reintervention rates compared with EUS-HGS, while maintaining similar technical and clinical success and procedure time. Overall adverse events were comparable, although bile leakage was more frequent with EUS-HGS and acute pancreatitis with EUS-HGAS. These findings may indicate a potential advantage of EUS-HGAS in terms of stent durability, warranting further studies for confirmation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-05102-y.Keywords: Malignant biliary obstruction, Endoscopic ultrasound-guided hepaticogastrostomy (EUS-HGS), Hepaticogastrostomy with antegrade stenting (EUS-HGAS), Biliary drainage, Recurrent biliary obstruction

Introduction

Malignant biliary obstruction (MBO) is a common clinical entity that presents both a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge. It may result in significant deterioration in the patient’s quality of life, particularly if patient becomes pruritic [1]. The most common cause of MBO is pancreatic cancer, and its incidence has increased over the last 30 years [2]. The gold standard procedure for biliary drainage (BD) is transpapillary drainage during endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP). However, ERCP sometimes fails in cases of difficult bile duct cannulation, duodenal obstruction, and surgical altered anatomy. In such cases, Endoscopic ultrasound-guided biliary drainage (EUS-BD) can serve as successful alternative to ERCP [3].

The EUS-BD techniques include EUS-guided choledochoduodenostomy (CDS), hepaticogastrostomy (HGS), antegrade stenting (AGS), and rendezvous stenting (RVS). Among these procedures, CDS and RVS require the echoendoscope to reach the duodenum. For this reason, HGS or AGS is usually indicated in patients with an inaccessible duodenum. However, recurrent biliary obstruction (RBO) is one of the major complications of HGS and AGS [4]. Considering that the survival rate of patients with pancreatic cancer has improved significantly with the advancements in chemotherapeutics, prolonging the time to recurrent biliary obstruction would improve these patients’ quality of life [5].

Alternatively, a method combining both AGS with HGS has been reported in some studies [6–8]. Hepaticogastrostomy with antegrade stenting (HGAS) is a modified technique in which AGS is performed via a transgastric approach, followed by HGS, using the same biliary access route. In terms of time to RBO, this procedure may have an advantage over HGS alone as it provides two biliary drainage routes: one to the stomach via HGS and the other to the duodenum via AGS [9]. We aimed in this study to systematically compare the efficacy and safety of EUS-HGS alone versus EUS-HGAS in patients with MBO.

Methods

We have conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis to compare the safety and efficacy of EUS-HGS alone versus EUS-HGAS in patients with unresectable malignant distal biliary obstruction. We adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA guidelines) [10] and Cochrane Handbook [11]. The study was registered on PROSPERO CRD420251064825.

Literature search & study selection

We conducted a comprehensive literature search across Scopus, PubMed, and Web of Science databases up to April 2025, using the following keywords: “endoscopic ultrasound-guided,” “hepaticogastrostomy,” “stent,” “bile duct obstruction,” and “bile duct neoplasms”. A detailed description of the search strategy is provided in the Supplementary File.

Based on our predefined selection criteria, we included both randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and observational studies that enrolled adult patients (> 18 years) diagnosed with unresectable malignant distal biliary obstruction, compared EUS-HGS alone versus EUS-HGAS, and reported at least one of the following outcomes: rate of recurrent biliary obstruction (RBO), need for reintervention, time to RBO, technical success, clinical success, procedure duration, or overall incidence of adverse events.

Studies were excluded if they were animal studies; focused on prevalence or cost-effectiveness; published in a language other than English; involved patients not diagnosed with unresectable malignant distal biliary obstruction; or used study designs such as review articles, case reports, case series, conference abstracts, or study protocols.

Screening and data extraction

All the studies were independently screened using Rayan software [12], we screened the studies in a two-step wise; the first titles and abstracts were screened then full-text screening. any disagreement was resolved using a common consensus by the lead author.

Data extraction was performed using a pre-defined Excel sheet by two authors independently. the extracted data included baseline characteristics, outcomes, and procedure details. the discrepancies were resolved by the lead auteur and discussion.

Quality assessment

We used the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) to assess the quality of the included observational studies, which evaluates studies based on three domains: selection, comparability, and outcomes [13]. The total core rating is 9 points, studies with a score ≥ 7 were considered high quality.

Study outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study were time to recurrent biliary obstruction (RBO) and the need for reintervention. Secondary outcomes included technical success, clinical/functional success, procedure time, and adverse events. Adverse events were classified as early if they occurred within 14 days after the procedure, late if they occurred more than 14 days after the procedure, or unspecified when the timing could not be determined. Reported adverse events included bile leakage, cholangitis, pancreatitis, and bleeding. Outcome definitions were adopted as reported in the individual studies. Definitions of the evaluated outcomes, including technical success, clinical/functional success, and RBO, were extracted from each included study and are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

We performed the meta-analysis using Review Manager (RevMan) software, version 5.4 (The Cochrane Collaboration, 2020). For dichotomous outcomes, pooled risk ratios (RRs) and hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. For continuous outcomes, including procedure time, mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs were computed. For time-to-event outcomes, we reconstructed Kaplan–Meier survival curves using the web-based Shiny application for graphic reconstruction of individual patient data [14]. This approach allowed us to estimate mean time to RBO under the same treatment condition. The accuracy of the reconstructed data was assessed using the following recommended thresholds: root mean square error (RMSE) ≤ 0.05, mean absolute error ≤ 0.02, and maximum absolute error ≤ 0.05, indicating sufficient precision for downstream analysis [15].We successfully reconstructed Kaplan–Meier curves for studies by Ishiwatari et al. (2022) [4] and Ishiwatari et al. (2024) [9]. The reconstructed individual patient data was then analyzed using SPSS version 24.

Additionally, we conducted a subgroup analysis of adverse events based on their timing, categorizing them as early (within 14 days), late (after 14 days), or of unspecified timing. Statistical significance was defined as a P-value < 0.05. A fixed-effects model was initially used for meta-analysis. In the presence of heterogeneity, a random-effects model was applied, and a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the robustness of the results. Heterogeneity was evaluated using the I² statistic, with values above 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity, and a P-value < 0.1 considered statistically significant [16]. We could not perform publication bias, due to the inclusion of less than 10 studies.

Results

Literature search results

A total of 547 records were initially identified through our comprehensive search strategy. Following the removal of duplicates, 362 unique records were screened based on titles and abstracts. Of these, 138 articles were assessed in full text for eligibility. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, five observational studies were deemed eligible and included in the final systematic review and meta-analysis [4, 6, 9, 17, 18]. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow diagram for included studies

Characteristics of included studies

Our analysis included five studies [4, 6, 9, 17, 18] comprising a total of 451 patients with malignant biliary obstruction: 250 patients were treated with HGS, and 201 with HGAS. The mean age in the EUS-HGS group was 69.9 years, compared to 71.94 years in the EUS-HGAS group. Four of the studies were conducted in Japan and one in China. The follow-up duration ranged from 17 to 108 months. The study summaries are presented in detail in Tables 1 and 2. Study quality assessed using the NOS indicated good methodological quality across all included studies (Supplementary File Table 2).

Primary outcomes

Time to RBO

Median Time to RBO derived from reconstructed KM curves aligned with the originally reported time to RBO in each study (Supplementary File Table 3), confirming the reliability of the method.

As shown in Fig. 2, mean time to RBO was longer in the EUS-HGAS group compared to EUS-HGS (651 vs. 227 days; P < 0.0001). EUS-HGAS significantly reduced the hazard of RBO (HR = 0.15; 95% CI: 0.03,0.83; P = 0.03), with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 60%; P = 0.11) (Fig. 3).

The need for reintervention

The EUS-HGS group showed significant increase in the incidence of reintervention in comparison to the EUS-HGAS group (RR = 3.00; 95% CI: 1.69,5.34; P = 0.0002). The analysis was homogenous (I² = 0%; P = 0.45) (Fig. 4).

Forest plot Illustrating the need for reintervention

Secondary outcomes

Clinical/functional success rate

There was no significant difference in clinical success between the EUS-HGS and EUS-HGAS groups (RR = 1.00; 95% CI: 0.94–1.06; P = 0.97). The analysis was homogenous (I² = 0%; P = 0.89) (Fig. 5A).

A Forest plot Illustrating clinical success rate, (B) Forest plot Illustrating technical success rate

Technical success rate

No significant difference was observed in technical success rates between the two groups (RR = 1.07; 95% CI: 0.97–1.18; P = 0.18). However, the analysis was markedly heterogeneous (I² = 75%; P = 0.003), and this heterogeneity could not be resolved by leave-one-out sensitivity analysis (Fig. 5B).

Procedure duration

No significant difference was observed in procedure duration between the two groups (MD = -9.31 min; 95% CI: -26.33 to 7.71; P = 0.28). However, substantial heterogeneity was present (I² = 94%; P < 0.00001). Sensitivity analysis excluding the study by Takenaka et al. (2022) reduced heterogeneity to I² = 0% (P = 0.32) and did not materially change the overall result, which remained non-significant (MD = -3.02 min; 95% CI: -7.14 to 1.09; P = 0.15) (Fig. 6).

Forest plot Illustrating procedure duration

Adverse events

There was no significant difference in the overall incidence of adverse events between the EUS-HGS and EUS-HGAS groups (RR = 0.88; 95% CI: 0.47–1.64; P = 0.68). Subgroup analysis based on the timing of adverse events also revealed no statistically significant differences. For early adverse events (< 14 days) after the procedure (RR = 0.70;95% CI: 0.39–1.24; P = 0.22). For late adverse events (> 14 days) (RR = 0.66;95% CI: 0.04–10.16; P = 0.76). In studies where the timing of adverse events was not specified (RR = 1.15;95% CI: 0.29–4.61; P = 0.84). Overall, the analysis was homogenous (I² = 43%; P = 0.12) (Fig. 7).

Forest plot Illustrating adverse events

EUS-HGS was significantly associated with a lower incidence of acute pancreatitis compared to EUS-HGAS (RR = 0.14; 95% CI: 0.03–0.60; P = 0.008) (Supplementary File Fig. 1). Conversely, EUS-HGS was linked to a significantly higher incidence of bile leak than EUS-HGAS (RR = 5.58; 95% CI: 1.03–30.09; P = 0.05) (Supplementary File Fig. 2). However, there was no significant difference between the two groups in terms of bleeding risk (RR = 0.47; 95% CI: 0.16–1.42; P = 0.18) (Supplementary File Fig. 3) and cholangitis(RR = 2.15; 95% CI: 0.62–7.40; P = 0.23)(Supplementary File Fig. 4) .The analyses were statistically homogeneous for all four outcomes (acute pancreatitis: I² = 0%; P = 0.73), (bile leak: I² = 0%; P = 0.85), (bleeding: I² = 0%; P = 0.41) and (cholangitis: I² = 0%; P = 0.95).

Discussion

This meta-analysis included five observational studies involving 451 patients with malignant biliary obstruction. The EUS-HGS group had a significantly higher rate of reintervention compared with the EUS-HGAS group. In addition, time to RBO was significantly longer in the EUS-HGAS group, suggesting better long-term stent patency. Both techniques showed comparable clinical and technical success rates, as well as similar procedure durations. Regarding safety outcomes, EUS-HGS was associated with a lower incidence of acute pancreatitis but a higher risk of bile leak. However, no significant differences were observed between the two groups in overall adverse events, bleeding, or cholangitis.

Regarding the need for reintervention, our findings are in contrast with those of Imai et al. (2017) [6] and Zhang et al. (2022) [17], Ishiwatari et al. (2022) [4],who reported no significant difference between the two techniques. However, our results align with those of Ishiwatari et al. (2024) [9], who found a significantly higher rate of RBO in the EUS-HGS group. The discrepancies between individual studies and the pooled analysis may be attributed to small sample sizes, which can limit statistical power and affect the detection of significant differences. To further investigate the mechanisms underlying RBO, Ishiwatari et al. (2022) analyzed the causes of stent dysfunction in each group [4]. They reported a higher incidence of stent hyperplasia in the HGS group (8 cases) compared with the HGAS group (3 cases) [4]. Stent hyperplasia, characterized by nonmalignant tissue ingrowth or overgrowth within the stent lumen, can obstruct bile flow and contribute to RBO. Tumor ingrowth or overgrowth was also an important mechanism, as progressive malignant disease may extend into the mesh of metallic stents or occlude the stent ends, leading to impaired drainage. Biliary sludge accumulation was also identified as a cause of stent occlusion, occurring in four cases in the HGS group (three involving metallic stents and one involving a plastic stent) and in one case in the HGAS group involving a plastic stent [4]. Despite these observations, multivariable analysis performed by Ishiwatari et al. (2022) did not identify stent type as an independent predictor of RBO [4]. The authors also highlighted stent migration and dislocation as potential causes of stent dysfunction [4]. In particular, EUS-HGS carries a risk of stent migration into the abdominal cavity or proximal displacement into the esophagus, complications that may contribute to recurrent biliary obstruction and necessitate reintervention [4].

However, regarding time to RBO, our findings are consistent with those of Ishiwatari et al. (2022) [4] and Ishiwatari et al. (2024) [9], both of which reported a significantly longer time to RBO with EUS-HGAS. This advantage may be attributed to the structural differences in drainage design between the two techniques [4, 6, 9, 17]. EUS-HGS provides a single drainage route into the stomach, which becomes a single point of failure if the stent is occluded. In contrast, EUS-HGAS creates dual drainage pathways: one via hepaticogastrostomy into the stomach and another via antegrade stenting through the malignant distal biliary obstruction into the duodenum [4, 6, 9, 17]. This dual-route configuration enhances bile flow and offers a backup mechanism, if one pathway becomes obstructed, the other may remain patent, thereby maintain biliary drainage and prolong the time to clinical RBO [4, 6, 9, 17]. However, our analysis of time to RBO needs to be interpreted with caution as it was based on only two studies.

Significant heterogeneity was observed in the analyses of RBO rate and technical success across the included studies. This variability is likely multifactorial and may reflect differences in operator expertise, procedural techniques, types of stents and devices used, patient selection criteria, and variations in study periods. In addition, differences in institutional experience and learning curves for complex endoscopic or interventional procedures may have contributed to inconsistencies in reported outcomes. Despite the use of sensitivity analyses, this heterogeneity could not be fully explained, suggesting the presence of underlying clinical and methodological diversity between studies. Therefore, the pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution, taking into account the inherent variability in real-world practice settings.

Regarding acute pancreatitis, our findings contrast with those of Imai et al. (2017) [6], Ishiwatari et al. (2022) [4], and Ishiwatari et al. (2024) [9], all of whom reported no significant difference in pancreatitis rates between EUS-HGS and EUS-HGAS. However, our results showed a significantly lower incidence of pancreatitis in the HGS group. This lower incidence may be explained by the anatomical implications of antegrade stent placement in HGAS [6]. Specifically, when the antegrade metal stent is placed across the duodenal major papilla, it can compress the orifice of the pancreatic duct [6]. This compression may block the outflow of pancreatic juice, thereby triggering acute pancreatitis [6]. Supporting this, Ishiwatari et al. (2022) reported that pancreatitis occurred exclusively in patients who had AGS placed across the duodenal papilla in the HGAS group, while no cases were observed in the HGS-alone group [4].

Regarding bile leakage, our findings contrast with those of Imai et al. (2017) [6] and Zhang et al. (2022) [17], who reported no significant difference in bile leak rates between HGS and HGAS. However, our results showed a significantly higher incidence of bile leakage in the HGS group. Bile leakage is a well-recognized cause of peritonitis, as the presence of bile in the sterile peritoneal space can trigger inflammation and infection [4]. This increase in bile leakage with HGS may be better understood in the context of both procedural physiology and drainage configuration differences between the two techniques [4, 9]. Although both HGS and HGAS require tract creation and dilation to achieve biliary access, HGAS adds an antegrade stent that restores bile flow toward the duodenum, thereby establishing a dual-drainage pathway through both the hepaticogastrostomy tract and the natural distal biliary route [4, 6, 9, 17]. This configuration may promote more effective biliary decompression, reduce intraductal pressure, and consequently decrease bile extravasation through the transluminal tract [4, 9]. In contrast, HGS relies solely on transmural drainage, which may sustain higher pressure within the system and increase the risk of leakage [4, 9]. Furthermore, the observed difference in bile leakage is likely not solely attributable to tract dilation avoidance, but rather to the overall drainage dynamics created by the addition of the antegrade stent [4, 9]. Supporting these mechanisms, Ishiwatari et al. 2022 reported peritonitis exclusively in the HGS group [4], while Ishiwatari et al. 2024 observed similar overall peritonitis rates but noted milder severity in the HGAS group [9]. It should also be acknowledged that, because the present study compares HGS with combined HGAS rather than isolated EUS-guided antegrade stenting, it remains difficult to disentangle the specific contribution of the antegrade component from the combined drainage strategy itself.

Strength and limitations

This study is the most comprehensive to compare HGS and HGAS in patients with MBO. A total of 451 patients were included in five observational studies: 250 patients underwent HGS, and 201 received HGAS. We evaluated 12 outcomes related to efficacy and safety, including a reconstructed Kaplan–Meier survival analysis for time to RBO. All included studies were assessed to have good methodological quality.

However, this meta-analysis has several limitations. First, only studies published in English were included, which may introduce language bias. Second, most of the studies were conducted in Japan and China, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, key outcomes such as procedure durations, time to RBO, and biliary drainage were based on only two studies, reducing the strength of our findings. Significant heterogeneity was observed in the analyses of RBO rate and technical success, and this could not be resolved through sensitivity analysis. Potential overlap between patient cohorts reported by Ishiwatari et al. (2022) and Ishiwatari et al. (2024) should be acknowledged. The 2022 study was a single-center retrospective analysis from the Shizuoka Cancer Center including patients treated between July 2016 and November 2020, whereas the 2024 study was a multicenter cohort including patients from five institutions with a broader inclusion period (January 2014 to December 2020). Although the 2024 study represents a multicenter dataset, partial inclusion of patients from the Shizuoka Cancer Center in both reports cannot be excluded based on the available information. This may have introduced a degree of population overlap, which could have influenced some outcomes. Moreover, a subgroup analysis according to stent type was not possible due to inconsistent and insufficient reporting of stent characteristics across the included studies, with several using mixed or inadequately specified stent types, which may represent a potential source of heterogeneity. Finally, all included studies were retrospective in design, making them susceptible to inherent biases such as selection bias and unmeasured confounding.

Implications and future directions

The findings of this meta-analysis have important clinical implications for selecting the optimal biliary drainage technique in patients with malignant biliary obstruction. Considering the observed trade-offs between EUS-HGS and EUS-HGAS, especially in terms of long-term patency and specific adverse events, treatment decisions should be tailored to each patient’s anatomical and clinical characteristics. These may include the risk of pancreatitis or bile leakage, the exact location of the biliary obstruction, and the patient’s overall prognosis. Future research should aim to conduct prospective, multicenter randomized controlled trials to generate more robust evidence. Moreover, standardizing procedural techniques and outcome reporting will help improve comparability across studies. Incorporating patient-reported outcomes and conducting cost-effectiveness analyses may also support more informed and personalized clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this meta-analysis suggests that EUS-HGAS is associated with improved stent durability, as reflected by longer time to recurrent biliary obstruction and a lower need for reintervention compared with EUS-HGS, while maintaining similar technical and clinical success rates and comparable procedure times. Although overall adverse event rates were similar between both techniques, EUS-HGS was associated with a higher risk of bile leakage, whereas EUS-HGAS showed a higher incidence of acute pancreatitis. These findings indicate that EUS-HGAS may offer a more favorable balance between efficacy and safety, although further high-quality studies are needed to confirm these results and better define patient selection.
