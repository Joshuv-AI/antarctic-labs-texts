The pervasiveness of racism in healthcare facilities in Latin America: a scoping review
Authors: Arachu Castro, Martha Silva, Eva Silvestre
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRacism in healthcare facilities across Latin America systematically affects Indigenous, Afrodescendant, and migrant populations. Yet, no comprehensive synthesis has mapped its scope across different populations, healthcare settings, and countries in the region. This scoping review followed PRISMA guidelines and searched PubMed, EBSCOhost, and EMBASE for peer-reviewed studies published between January 2015 and June 2025. We included studies addressing racism in healthcare facilities where clinical encounters between populations and healthcare workers occur. Data were charted using AI-assisted tools and analyzed thematically. We retained 70 studies from 15 countries, predominantly Brazil (n = 30) and Mexico (n = 14). Racism manifested through three interconnected forms: institutional racism (policies restricting access, absence of data reflecting ethnic identification and racialization processes, resource inequities), personally mediated racism (verbal abuse, physical mistreatment, denial of culturally appropriate care), and internalized racism (self-devaluation, acceptance of mistreatment). These forms of discrimination pervade multiple medical fields, including maternal and reproductive health, mental health services, dental care, chronic disease management, infectious disease treatment, and emergency care. Racialized populations experience delayed diagnoses, inadequate treatment protocols, and systematic exclusion from preventive care. Language barriers, cultural dismissal, and discriminatory triage decisions compound these inequities. Intersectional marginalization based on gender, class, migration status, and sexuality amplifies these effects, producing multiplicative rather than additive health impacts. Achieving health equity requires dismantling institutional racism through meaningful community participation in healthcare governance, mandatory collection of ethno-local data—population descriptors reflecting local ethnic identification and racialization processes—integration of anti-racist and decolonial frameworks in medical education, legal accountability mechanisms, and recognition of racism as a fundamental determinant of health. Introduction

A Spanish translation of this article is available in the Supplementary Material.

Racism in healthcare facilities is a critical determinant of health inequities in Latin America (Commission of the Pan American Health Organization on Equity and Health Inequalities in the Americas 2019). Racism manifests as structural discrimination that systematically segregates populations through racialization—a historical process rooted in colonialism that assigns racial meaning to individuals and groups through the interplay of ancestry, phenotypic, cultural, class-based, and gendered characteristics (Quijano 2000, Viveros Vigoya 2016, Telles 2017). This colonial tool distinguished colonizers from the colonized, generating new racialized identities (Indigenous, black, mestizo) while reconfiguring others (European) with racial significance (Reyes-Muñoz 2023), reflecting context-specific terminologies that have evolved historically and geographically into classifications more fluid in Latin America than in other world regions. Latin American populations frequently create cognitive social boundaries between themselves and others based on color, phenotype, and language, with the visible manifestation of color involving a continuum of differences rather than categorical descriptors (Telles 2017). Migrants—particularly if they are Afrodescendants or live in poverty—are racialized through skin color, physical appearance, and cultural origins, which mark them as permanent outsiders within a racial hierarchy that treats European and North American migrants as desirable, a dynamic that intersects with classism and sexism to compound their marginalization in healthcare settings (Haydu et al. 2020, Galaz et al. 2021, Obach et al. 2022).

Although the concept of race lacks biological validity as a category for human classification (American Anthropological Association 1998, National Academies of Sciences Engineering and Medicine 2023), institutional systems continue to reinforce racialization by producing dehumanizing representations and material marginalization, creating what has been termed “racism without race” (Galaz et al. 2021)—where exclusion is justified through intersecting social markers rather than explicit racist ideology. Despite constitutional guarantees throughout the region, Indigenous, Afrodescendant, migrant, and other racialized populations face systematic discrimination in the clinical encounter, compromising both healthcare access and service quality (Castro et al. 2015, Commission of the Pan American Health Organization on Equity and Health Inequalities in the Americas 2019). Gender and class hierarchies compound these effects, with racism emerging from classist ethnocentrism (Galaz et al. 2021) and operating as a structural determinant that generates material and symbolic disadvantage (Santos Silva et al. 2025).

Racism in healthcare is enacted through three interconnected pathways (Jones 2000). Institutional racism operates through policies and norms that systematically restrict access and opportunity (Werneck 2016, Goes et al. 2020, Candido et al. 2022, Tochetto et al. 2023); personally mediated racism involves discriminatory behaviors by healthcare workers, whether intentional or unconscious; and internalized racism occurs when racialized individuals accept a devalued status, reinforcing marginalization (Dörr and Dietz 2020). Intersectionality amplifies these effects: people from minoritized groups facing multiple forms of marginalization—based on socioeconomic position, migrant status, disability, age, or sex/gender conformity and non-conformity—experience multiplicative rather than merely additive impacts on health (Giacomozzi et al. 2021, Mittelbach and Albuquerque 2022, Preaux and Castro 2024). Many healthcare professionals remain unaware of how their biases and prejudices contribute to differential treatment (Falu 2019, Nandi et al. 2020). From a structural perspective, racism operates as a system that integrates the economic and political organization of society, making it a constitutive element rather than an aberration (Almeida 2019).

While acknowledging that racial categories lack biological validity, population descriptors reflecting racialization remain socially relevant and are associated with measurable impacts on health. Therefore, collecting population descriptors that reflect ethnic identification and racialization processes—what we term ethno-local data—remains essential for documenting and addressing health inequities. These data are collected through local classifications, such as census categories, self-reported color, and linguistic criteria, that vary across countries. However, how these data are collected (or fail to be collected) and measured affects the extent of this documentation (Telles 2017). The absence of such data in health records constitutes a form of institutional racism that renders discrimination invisible, preventing both measurement of inequities and accountability for addressing them (Tochetto et al. 2023, Santos Silva et al. 2025). Significant gaps persist in addressing racism in healthcare, particularly given the lack of consensus on measurement approaches across Latin American countries.

This scoping review synthesizes current evidence on racism in healthcare facilities across Latin America, examining its manifestations, mechanisms of reproduction, and impacts on racialized populations. The specific objectives are (i) to map the scope and nature of existing research on racism in healthcare facilities across Latin American countries; (ii) to identify the forms and mechanisms through which racism operates in clinical encounters; (iii) to examine the health impacts of racism on Indigenous, Afrodescendant, migrant, and other racialized populations; and (iv) to identify strategies and interventions for addressing racism in healthcare. By mapping the existing literature, we identify structural patterns of racism, clarify the mechanisms through which it is reproduced, and highlight possible strategies for transformation. Given that interventions targeting only individual provider behavior have limited impact without addressing broader structural inequalities (Castro et al. 2015), this review considers both institutional and interpersonal dimensions of racism to inform comprehensive strategies toward health equity in the region.

Methodology

This scoping review follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Statement (Page et al. 2021), the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines (Tricco et al. 2018), and an adaptation of the Arksey and O’Malley framework for conducting scoping reviews (Levac et al. 2010). The scoping review and its protocol are registered in the OSF repository (Castro 2025).

We searched for evidence of racism in healthcare facilities in Latin America. We searched PubMed, EBSCOhost (Academic Search Complete), and EMBASE online databases. In addition to Latin America and individual country names, search terms “in any field” included (racism) OR (discrimination) AND (healthcare). We included peer-reviewed studies published in any language between January 2015 and June 2025 that addressed racism in any healthcare facility where clinical encounters occur between populations and healthcare workers, including physicians, nurses, and other healthcare professionals. We excluded papers that did not meet these criteria.

We refined the research strategy in a set of steps. After conducting the searches, we uploaded them to Covidence—a web-based software platform created to facilitate the management of scoping reviews and other systematic literature search methods—and performed title and abstract screening. We removed publications that were irrelevant, not found, abstract-only, editorials, or letters. We then reviewed the full version of the remaining studies, as well as additional references found through a citation search. We assessed publications as eligible if they addressed racism in healthcare facilities and were peer-reviewed. A team of five research assistants reviewed all studies and selected those appropriate for full review. Each study was independently screened by at least two of the five reviewers. When both reviewers agreed on inclusion or exclusion, their decision was final. In cases of discrepancy, a third reviewer from the team independently assessed the study and cast the deciding vote.

For each publication, we charted the data using Notebook LM—an AI-powered research and note-taking tool developed by Google. Instead of conducting a traditional scoping review during which data charting involves extracting relevant information into a structured form, we used Notebook LM to enhance this process. We uploaded each retained publication into a single notebook and used prompts organized according to 10 themes: (i) study methodological characteristics, (ii) population characteristics, including geographic and cultural contexts, (iii) healthcare settings and services, (iv) forms and manifestations of racism, (v) experiences of discrimination, (vi) impact of racism on health outcomes, (vii) theoretical frameworks and concepts for studying racism in healthcare, (viii) root causes of racism in healthcare and contributing factors, (ix) challenges to addressing racism in healthcare, (x) interventions and strategies to addressing racism in healthcare. The prompts started with terms such as where, what, who, and which, followed by the theme. The AI tool generated structured summaries for each theme from the uploaded publications, serving as a first-pass extraction. We systematically verified these AI-generated outputs against the original publications. For each theme, we confirmed that the Notebook LM output corresponded to the information provided in the retained studies. This approach accelerated the data charting process while maintaining accuracy through human verification and analysis.

We employed thematic analysis to synthesize findings across studies. Given the heterogeneity of methodological approaches, theoretical frameworks, and definitions of racism across the included studies—which ranged from quantitative epidemiological analyses to ethnographic fieldwork and varied in whether they explicitly named racism or documented inequities linked to ethnic characteristics—we adopted an inclusive analytical strategy. We included studies regardless of whether they used the term racism explicitly, provided they documented discrimination or differential treatment in healthcare settings linked to ethnic or racialization characteristics. We acknowledge that approaches to racialization and analytical frameworks vary across the literature, evolve over time, and differ by country, which represents both a limitation and a substantive finding of this review. The thematic framework was organized around the three interconnected forms of racism (institutional, personally mediated, and internalized) as theorized by Jones (2000), which served as the primary analytical structure for the results.

There was no patient or public involvement in this study, and this scoping review does not involve human participants.

Results

The PRISMA flow chart (Fig. 1) shows the identification, screening, and inclusion process of the 70 studies retained. The studies were published in Spanish (9), Portuguese (14), and English (47).

PRISMA flow diagram.

Most publications (73%) drew on social science theories to explore racism in healthcare. They spanned 15 countries (Table 1), with Brazil (n = 30) and Mexico (n = 14) most extensively represented. Twenty-three studies focused on Indigenous communities, 20 on Afrodescendants, 10 on both Indigenous and Afrodescendants, 5 on migrants and Indigenous or Afrodescendants, 4 on migrants only, and 8 addressed racism but did not specify the population being studied. Low-income individuals who are racialized through classist ethnocentrism and xenophobia are also documented, as are women at the intersection of multiple marginalized identities who also experience layered discrimination, as well as racialized transgender and other sex/gender non-conforming individuals, who face additional discrimination through denial of gender identity recognition and provider moral judgments that further restrict access to care.

Studies on racism in healthcare in Latin America, 2015–25.

The reviewed literature shows significant variability in the terminology used to describe racialized populations across Latin America, shaped by national census categories, colonial legacies, and regional political discourses. The studies employed a range of methodological approaches, reflecting the complexity and breadth of the research questions in investigating racism in healthcare settings (see Table 1 for details). Qualitative methods predominated, alongside quantitative analyses, mixed methods, and literature reviews, reflecting the need to both measure inequities and understand lived experiences. Healthcare professionals’ discriminatory practices were specifically examined through interviews (Gouveia and Zanello 2019, Chauhan et al. 2020, Nascimento et al. 2022, Obach et al. 2022, Preaux and Castro 2023, Silva et al. 2023), ethnographic fieldwork (Vega 2017, Falu 2019, Dörr and Dietz 2020, Castro 2024), a randomized experiment with manipulated patient images (Chisini et al. 2019), quantitative analysis of health data (Santos et al. 2023, Santos Silva et al. 2025), and a pedagogical intervention (Meneses-Navarro et al. 2021).

The studies identified three interconnected forms of racism operating within healthcare settings: institutional and structural racism, personally mediated racism, and internalized racism, alongside their individual impacts and documented responses (Table 2); these three forms operate as a mutually reinforcing system, rather than as isolated phenomena.

Forms of racism in healthcare facilities in Latin America, 2015–25.

Institutional racism

Institutional racism manifests through policies, norms, and systematic practices that restrict access to and quality of healthcare for racialized populations. Language barriers emerged as a primary exclusion mechanism, with government facilities providing Spanish-only care in Indigenous territories (Castro et al. 2015, Shah et al. 2017). This linguistic discrimination had cascading effects: Indigenous women reported shame leading to home birth choices (Castro et al. 2015); Totonaco women faced doctors refusing interpreters (Dörr and Dietz 2020); Indigenous women in Southern Mexico experienced isolation preventing informed consent (El Kotni 2018); and HIV care throughout Latin America involved cultural stereotyping and segregationist assimilation (Ponce et al. 2017).

The absence of disaggregated ethno-local data in health records constituted a fundamental form of institutional racism, rendering discrimination invisible and preventing measurement of inequities (Tochetto et al. 2023, Santos Silva et al. 2025). In Brazil, Afrodescendant patients with chronic renal failure were diagnosed overwhelmingly only after hospitalization, when symptoms had already worsened, while most Eurodescendant patients received earlier diagnoses through outpatient care (Santos Ferreira et al. 2020). In sickle cell management, Afrodescendant and mixed patients were systematically undertested for stroke risk despite comprising the majority of cases (Santos et al. 2023). Quilombola communities faced compounded barriers—geographical isolation, inadequate transportation, and discriminatory treatment—explicitly linked to slavery’s historical legacy (Feitosa et al. 2021, Gomes et al. 2024). Ethnic and racist inequities explained substantial portions of the gap in maternal healthcare coverage in Mexico (Serván-Mori et al. 2025). During COVID-19, institutional racism manifested in higher mortality rates among racialized populations even after controlling for comorbidities (Santos Silva et al. 2025), and racism in access to labor support persisted even under legally protected rights (Mittelbach and Albuquerque 2022). These barriers were compounded by structural exclusions, including lack of access to information, public health policies, and appropriate medical services (Bomfim et al. 2022, Silva et al. 2023, Tochetto et al. 2023). Inequities were evident in diagnoses and outcomes across various conditions, including dental disease, chronic renal failure, hypertension, and sickle cell disease (Chisini et al. 2019, Santos Ferreira et al. 2020, Lima et al. 2021, Santos et al. 2023). Racialized patients received less preventive care, later diagnoses, and inadequate treatment, often requiring hospitalization before being identified as needing care (Santos Ferreira et al. 2020) even when socioeconomic factors were accounted for (Chisini et al. 2019). Afrodescendant homeless drug users experienced invisibility and structural racism in psychosocial care centers (Oliveira et al. 2022). Young migrants of various Latin American nationalities in Chile experienced what the researchers termed institutional racist practices intersecting with classism and sexism, resulting in a homogenized, derogatory construction of Afrodescendant migrant women (Galaz et al. 2021, Obach et al. 2022).

Evidence consistently showed Afrodescendant women in Brazil—particularly those identifying as pardas or pretas, single mothers, and lesbian women—experienced what researchers termed “profound racial and sexual prejudice” within systems treating their bodies as invisible and without dignity (Falu 2019) and systematic discrimination across the reproductive continuum—inequities that researchers explicitly linked to historical legacies of slavery and colonialism (Pereira Belfort et al. 2016, Williamson 2021). Institutional barriers to post-abortion care were significantly greater for Afrodescendant women, including longer wait times for appointments and beds compared to Eurodescendant or mixed patients, even after adjusting for clinical severity, age, and partnership status (Goes et al. 2020, Larrea et al. 2021). Even controlling for socioeconomic position, unconscious bias emerged: for example, dentists in Brazil were significantly less likely to refer Afrodescendant patients for prosthetic treatment and almost invariably recommended cheaper composite resin restorations instead, while frequently questioning their socioeconomic status—questions never posed to Eurodescendant patients (Chisini et al. 2019).

Personally mediated racism

Personally mediated racism—discriminatory practices by healthcare workers, whether intentional or unconscious—was extensively documented across the region. Healthcare professionals—including physicians, nurses, midwives, psychologists, and social workers—perpetuated discrimination through degrading stereotypes, characterizing racialized patients as “inferior, submissive, grateful and ignorant” (Sesia 2020), leading to condescending and despotic treatment (Sesia 2020, Coral Mendoza 2021). Microaggressions manifested as micro-assaults, microinsults, and corporeal violations, including sterilization efforts targeting single mothers based on assumptions about hypersexuality and hyperfertility (Smith-Oka 2015). In the Dominican Republic, researchers documented 117 specific incidents of ethnic slurs during 275 observed births (Castro 2024). Verbal abuse—encompassing patient-blaming, public humiliation, and mocking of pain—and physical abuse—including hitting, refusing pain medication, and unconsented procedures—were systematically documented across the region (Castro et al. 2015, Preaux and Castro 2023, Tijoux and Ambiado 2023, Castro 2024).

Maya women in Guatemala faced particularly severe violations—denied urgent obstetric care for administrative reasons, subjected to forced tubal ligations, and interrogated about land ownership during medical procedures (Nandi et al. 2020, Austad et al. 2021). The discrimination extended across diverse Indigenous groups: Quichua women experiencing obstetric violence (Giacomozzi et al. 2021) and delayed emergency care (Verdezoto et al. 2020); Maya women turned away based on skin color and traditional clothing (Austad et al. 2021); and Indigenous women in Mexico and Bolivia reporting shame and mistreatment (Castro et al. 2015). In Brazil, Indigenous women received fewer prenatal visits and less anesthesia during painful procedures (Tempesta et al. 2023). Epistemological dimensions of discrimination were also evident: Mapuche (Guzmán 2020) and Mapuche Tehuelche (Santisteban 2024) traditional medicine was dismissed, while Indigenous patients with diabetes in Guatemala experienced verbal abuse (Nieblas-Bedolla et al. 2019). Physical violations included intimate intrusions that disrespected cultural modesty values (El Kotni 2018). Systematic analysis showed Indigenous women experienced longer delays, substandard care, and human rights violations compared to non-Indigenous women region wide (Castro et al. 2015).

Obstetric racism emerged as a particularly acute form of personally mediated discrimination at the intersection of racist, sexual, and gender-based violence. Obstetric racism was widespread, involving critical lapses in diagnosis, neglectful and disrespectful treatment, infliction of pain, and coercive or non-consensual interventions (Castro 2019, Castro and Savage 2019, Sesia 2020, Williamson 2021, Meinerz and Dos Santos 2023, Preaux and Castro 2023, Tempesta et al. 2023). Through exit interviews and ethnographic observations, researchers documented how mistreatment was based on “racist, prejudicial treatment according to which those of higher socioeconomic position identify with the ‘white’ colonialist identity in opposition to, and above, the ‘blackness’ of the population whose rights continue to be infringed” (Castro and Savage 2019). Afrodescendant women had fewer prenatal visits, less birth companion access, more forced peregrination during labor, underwent episiotomy without anesthesia, and experienced higher post-cesarean infection rates (Lima et al. 2021); and lesbian women faced racism embedded in heteronormativity, leading to care avoidance (Falu 2019, Meinerz and Dos Santos 2023). These forms of discrimination were especially severe for single mothers, who did not receive adequate support and accompaniment during painful labor due to unspoken cues such as “the unhurried movement or inaction of health workers in the hospital, or the chaotic shuffling of patients from hospital to hospital” (Nascimento et al. 2022), and for lesbian women, who experienced dehumanized treatment in gynecological settings, where pain thresholds are defined by skin color (Meinerz and Dos Santos 2023). The combined effects of racism and abortion stigma significantly compounded these barriers (Goes et al. 2020, Larrea et al. 2021). Psychotherapists marginalized Afrodescendant women through Euro-American frameworks and therapists’ lack of training in ethno-racial issues, fostering therapeutic failure expectations and emotional distress (Gouveia and Zanello 2019).

Migrant and refugee populations emerged as increasingly vulnerable groups. According to the researchers, Haitian women in Chile experienced sexualized racialization linked to stereotypes of enslaved Afrodescendant women, preventing them from being seen as responsible mothers and leading to increased medical surveillance and economic exploitation (Reyes-Muñoz 2023, Tijoux and Ambiado 2023). In Brazil, Haitian migrants encountered communication barriers and, as identified by the researchers, color-blind structural racism “veiled in the professionals’ speech” (Silva et al. 2023). Congolese refugees in São Paulo and Bolivian immigrants in Buenos Aires and São Paulo navigated complex therapeutic itineraries that, based on in-depth interviews, the researchers interpreted as racist (Haydu et al. 2020). Health professionals perceived Haitian patients’ dwellings as “dirty, disorganized, and of scanty and shared rooms,” even without personal experience—critiques the researchers interpreted as stemming from racist stereotypes used to justify discriminatory treatment by assigning racial characteristics to living conditions and ways of life (Tijoux and Ambiado 2023). The researchers found that the migrant characteristic often predominated over the patient status, making migrants objects of punishment within healthcare facilities (Tijoux and Ambiado 2023). Nicaraguan immigrants in Costa Rica experienced, through classist ethnocentrism, purposeful neglect and denial of medical attention due, according to the researchers, to institutional racism (Castro et al. 2015). Health inequities also extended to migrant and refugee populations, who showed greater vulnerability to infectious diseases and malnutrition due to structural marginalization (Silva et al. 2023). In Indigenous populations, structural violence, racism, and gender inequity intersected to produce elevated vulnerability to HIV (Santos et al. 2023).

Transgender individuals faced compounded barriers rooted in the intersection of racism and gender-based discrimination. Despite Chile’s gender identity laws, transgender migrants lacking permanent residency often found that clinical protocols for recognizing their gender identity were ignored, with this administrative exclusion leading to severe discrimination and impeding access to necessary treatments (Obach et al. 2022). In Brazil, transgender men and women face specific barriers in gynecological care driven by healthcare professionals’ moral judgments regarding gender transition—judgments entangled with the colonial and racialized foundations of medical practice—within a cisheteronormative healthcare system that inherently prioritizes non-racialized, cisgender bodies and severely restricts access to equitable and dignified care for racialized gender and sexual minorities (Meinerz and Dos Santos 2023, Tochetto et al. 2023).

Internalized racism

Internalized racism—the acceptance by racialized individuals of a devalued status—manifested through several pathways documented in the reviewed studies. Anticipated or experienced discrimination led to fear, shame, and reduced care-seeking across multiple populations. For example, Indigenous women reported that shame about their ethnicity affected decisions to give birth at home rather than face hospital discrimination (Castro et al. 2015). Afrodescendant women in Brazil experienced what researchers described as “shattered subjectivities”—a concept developed by Gaudenzi et al. (2023) to capture the profound psychological fragmentation resulting from systemic racism in healthcare, wherein Afrodescendant women reported feeling “non-being” in a society that attributes negative representations to those perceived as “non-white.” This concept describes how the cumulative weight of repeated encounters with racism—from denial of their experiences by healthcare providers to active discrimination—erodes individuals’ sense of self and agency, producing depressive symptoms, anguish, and deeply internalized harm that fragments their identity and undermines their capacity to seek care (Tavares and Kuratani 2019, Gaudenzi et al. 2023). Leprosy patients experienced felt stigma—the expectation of discrimination leading them to hide their conditions (Shyam-Sundar et al. 2021). Among migrants in Chile, racism produced depressive symptoms, anguish, and low self-esteem (Oyarte et al. 2022). Children and adolescents encountered double discrimination from mental illness stigma and skin color, unrecognized by their therapists (Candido et al. 2022). The denial of racism by healthcare providers further compounded internalized racism by invalidating lived experiences and exacerbating trauma (Gaudenzi et al. 2023). As a result, widespread mistrust in healthcare institutions has become deep-rooted among racialized populations (Cerón et al. 2016).

Internalized racism also produced subtler but equally damaging effects. In the Dominican Republic and Mexico, women normalized mistreatment through adaptive preference—resignedly accepting poor care, harsh language, and lack of privacy as inevitable features of public healthcare, and framing obstetric violence as normal rather than as a rights violation (Castro and Savage 2019, Coral Mendoza 2021). Among Totonaco communities in Mexico, internalized racism led Indigenous midwives and patients to view their own practices as inferior to biomedicine, resulting in abandonment of cultural knowledge in favor of hospital referrals (Dörr and Dietz 2020). In Brazil, Afrodescendants sometimes interpreted structural racism through a personalist lens—attributing systemic disadvantage to personal inadequacy—generating self-hatred, guilt, and alienation (Tavares and Kuratani 2019). Afrodescendant women in psychotherapy reported a “glass wall”, self-censoring their racial trauma out of anticipatory fear that therapists would not validate their experiences (Gouveia and Zanello 2019). Finally, internalized shame compounded by intersectional stigmas caused tangible delays in care-seeking: Afrodescendant women postponed urgent post-abortion care, fearing mistreatment and judgment (Goes et al. 2020), while Afrodescendant lesbian women reported profound shame during gynecological encounters where providers dismissed their sexual identity (Falu 2019).

Intersectionality as a cross-cutting theme

Intersectionality emerged as a critical finding across the reviewed literature, demonstrating that racism in healthcare rarely operates in isolation but intersects with gender, class, migration status, sexuality, age, and disability to produce multiplicative effects (Giacomozzi et al. 2021, Mittelbach and Albuquerque 2022, Preaux and Castro 2024). Women at the intersection of multiple marginalized identities—such as Afrodescendant lesbian women (Falu 2019, Meinerz and Dos Santos 2023), Indigenous single mothers (Nascimento et al. 2022), or racialized migrant women (Galaz et al. 2021, Obach et al. 2022, Reyes-Muñoz 2023)—experienced compounded discrimination that exceeded the sum of individual forms of marginalization. Microaggressions against racialized low-income populations “reflect and reinforce class and race-based explanations of otherness” (Smith-Oka 2015), representing mechanisms of reproductive governance rooted in healthcare hierarchies. The reviewed studies demonstrated that effective analysis and intervention must account for these intersecting axes of oppression rather than treating racism as a discrete, standalone phenomenon.

Theoretical frameworks of the reviewed studies

Several studies applied theoretical frameworks that illustrate how the three forms of racism operate at the intersection of individual practice and structural forces. Preaux and Castro (2023) draw on the concept of authoritative medical habitus to explain how racialized logics become deeply internalized in healthcare providers’ dispositions and clinical reflexes, operating below conscious awareness and making discriminatory practices difficult to identify and unlearn. Smith-Oka (2015) introduces the notion of “radical otherness” and identifies “corporeal microaggressions”—subtle yet harmful practices such as non-consensual sterilizations or systematic neglect—that reproduce colonial hierarchies determining whose bodies are valued and protected. These frameworks show how racism functions not merely through explicit bias but through embodied practices that healthcare providers may not recognize as discriminatory (Smith-Oka 2015, Preaux and Castro 2023). Meinerz and Dos Santos (2023) and Williamson (2021) use the term obstetric racism to capture these racialized harms, including assumptions of higher pain tolerance and disregard for bodily autonomy. Castro (2019) and Sesia (2020) apply the concept of obstetric violence—the appropriation of women’s bodies and reproductive processes through dehumanizing, pathologizing treatment that strips bodily autonomy. Williamson (2021) demonstrates how such violence, when intersecting with racism, disproportionately affects Afrodescendant and Indigenous women.

Discussion

This scoping review demonstrates that racism in healthcare facilities across Latin America operates as a systemic, multidimensional phenomenon profoundly impacting health equity. The 70 reviewed studies show how racism manifests through interconnected institutional, interpersonal, and internalized pathways, creating multiplicative effects when intersecting with gender, social position, and migration status. A critical finding is the normalized, often invisible nature of healthcare racism. Providers frequently deny discriminatory practices through color-blind discourses claiming equal treatment for all patients, thereby invalidating racialized patients’ lived experiences (Castro et al. 2015, Smith-Oka 2015, Werneck 2016, Pacino 2017, Falu 2019, Nandi et al. 2020, Santos Ferreira et al. 2020, Galaz et al. 2021, Williamson 2021, Candido et al. 2022, Mittelbach and Albuquerque 2022, Gaudenzi et al. 2023, Silva et al. 2023, Tochetto et al. 2023, Serván-Mori et al. 2025). This denial functions as symbolic violence, perpetuating structural inequities (Williamson 2021, Gaudenzi et al. 2023). The concept of silent racism (Pacino 2017) captures how exclusions are justified through cultural or behavioral rationales rather than explicit racial references, maintaining hierarchies while avoiding overtly racialized language. In Brazil, the enduring myth of racial democracy further inhibits open discussion of racialization and health inequities (Tavares and Kuratani 2019, Williamson 2021, Candido et al. 2022, Gaudenzi et al. 2023). Discriminatory practices are often justified through essentialist cultural narratives (Sesia 2020), while stereotypes linking racialized populations to poverty, crime, or inferiority reinforce bias (Chauhan et al. 2020). In Mexico, well-intentioned concepts like interculturality could reinforce inequality when not accompanied by structural reform (Vega 2017).

These findings align with and extend global evidence on racism in healthcare. International research has documented similar patterns of provider denial and color-blind racism in healthcare across the USA (Paradies et al. 2015, Bailey et al. 2017), Europe, New Zealand, and Canada (Hamed et al. 2022, Bradby et al. 2023). However, the Latin American context presents distinctive features: the legacy of mestizaje ideologies that actively deny the salience of racism while perpetuating racialized hierarchies; the coexistence of multiple racialized groups (Indigenous, Afrodescendant, migrant) within single healthcare systems; and the intersection of racism with colonial legacies of medical authoritarianism. Our finding that silent racism operates through discourses of hygiene, poverty, and cultural deficiency rather than explicit racist language mirrors global patterns (Bonilla-Silva 2015) but takes specific forms shaped by Latin American population formations (Telles 2017, Wade 2017). The theoretical concepts applied in the reviewed studies—authoritative medical habitus, corporeal microaggressions, obstetric racism, and obstetric violence—articulate mechanisms through which structural racism is reproduced in clinical encounters, contributing to the broader literature on healthcare discrimination.

Maternal and reproductive health represents the domain where the three forms of racism converge most visibly and with the gravest consequences. The documented patterns—from denial of prenatal care and obstetric violence to differential COVID-19 mortality—show that health emergencies do not generate new inequities so much as they expose and accelerate those already structurally embedded in healthcare systems. That these inequities persist even after controlling for comorbidities and socioeconomic position (Santos Silva et al. 2025, Serván-Mori et al. 2025) challenges explanations rooted in individual risk factors and points instead to racism as an independent, constitutive determinant. This convergence also illustrates why intersectional analysis is indispensable: the compounded disadvantage facing Indigenous and Afrodescendant women cannot be addressed by interventions targeting any single axis of inequality in isolation.

Heterogeneity of ethno-local categories across Latin America

The reviewed literature showed significant variability in the terminology used to describe racialized populations across Latin America. In Brazil—the country with the most standardized regional categories—studies draw on official classifications (brancos, pretos, pardos, amarelos, indígenas) based on self-reported color (Chisini et al. 2019, Chauhan et al. 2020, Shyam-Sundar et al. 2021), alongside politicized terms central to advocacy frameworks (Werneck 2016, Goes et al. 2020, Feitosa et al. 2021, Gaudenzi et al. 2023). In Mexico, the legacy of mestizaje is evident in the use of linguistic criteria for identifying Indigenous peoples (Campos Navarro et al. 2017, Meneses-Navarro et al. 2021), while spatial and class-based terms carry racialized meanings (Vega 2017); yet this emphasis on language may exclude those who no longer speak Indigenous languages but continue to face racialization. Guatemala’s binary ladino/Indigenous classification (Carey 2022) obscures intra-Indigenous diversity and renders Afrodescendant populations—absent from the census—invisible. Ecuador’s historical preference for class-based terms like campesino (Carey 2022) over explicit ethno-local categories similarly conceals racism in health data. Chile’s evolving categories around racialized migrants (Galaz et al. 2021, Tijoux and Ambiado 2023) require constant updating of data collection instruments. This variability is further complicated by the fact that Indigenous people can adopt the lifestyles and identities of dominant groups, making ethno-local categorization fluid and context dependent.

This heterogeneity of ethno-local categories across Latin American countries presents significant challenges for implementing mandatory data collection in healthcare facilities and other sectors. Healthcare providers may resist or inconsistently apply ethno-local categories, particularly when dominant color-blind discourses claim to treat all patients equally (Galaz et al. 2021, Silva et al. 2023, Castro 2024). The political sensitivity of ethno-local classification can additionally lead to institutional reluctance to collect such data, perpetuating the invisibility of health inequities (Werneck 2016, Santos Ferreira et al. 2020). Developing context-appropriate yet comparable data collection systems that respect local self-identification while enabling regional analysis of the health impacts of racism remains a critical challenge for achieving health equity in Latin America. To be effective, interventions should occur simultaneously at structural, institutional, interpersonal, and community levels.

Implications for health equity

The findings of this review have direct implications for advancing health equity in Latin America. Racism functions as a fundamental barrier to universal access to health by creating systematic exclusions in access, quality, and outcomes that persist even when access is nominally expanded. The evidence demonstrates that expanding insurance coverage or building new facilities is insufficient when institutional racism is embedded in policies, norms, and provider practices. Specifically, the racist inequities documented in maternal healthcare coverage in Mexico (Serván-Mori et al. 2025), the differential access to timely diagnosis for chronic conditions in Brazil (Santos Ferreira et al. 2020), and the barriers to post-abortion care faced by Afrodescendant women (Goes et al. 2020) all illustrate how racism undermines the equity dimension of universal access to health. Addressing these barriers requires that health equity strategies explicitly incorporate anti-racist frameworks and that equity metrics include disaggregated data by ethno-racial categories. Without confronting racism as a constitutive element of health system functioning (Almeida 2019), health equity will remain an aspiration rather than a reality for racialized populations across the region.

Resistance and awareness are also part of the lived experience. Encounters with racism often generated counter-narratives, critical evaluations, and collective mobilization (Falu 2019, Gaudenzi et al. 2023). Yet these responses emerged against a backdrop of inadequate provider training, normalized discourses of equity that obscured structural racism, and institutional failure to enforce anti-discrimination protections (Castro et al. 2015, Falu 2019, Gouveia and Zanello 2019, Silva et al. 2023).

Finally, racism is both a barrier to equitable healthcare and a constitutive element of how many health systems currently function. Addressing it requires systemic transformation grounded in the knowledge and agency of those most affected. These findings affirm that anti-racist action in healthcare must occur simultaneously at structural, institutional, and interpersonal levels, as theorized by Jones (2000) and expanded by Almeida (2019), who emphasize that all expressions of racism are manifestations of deeper structural arrangements, that efforts to achieve health equity are inseparable from pursuits of anti-racist justice, and that racism is systematically integrated into economic and political organization.

Conclusion

This scoping review provides substantial evidence that racism constitutes a fundamental barrier to health equity in Latin America, operating not as an aberration but as a constitutive element of healthcare systems (Almeida 2019). The pervasive discrimination documented across 15 countries through diverse methodological approaches underscores the need to recognize that achieving universal access to health and the Sustainable Development Goals requires confronting racism as a prerequisite rather than a supplementary concern.

The evidence underscores the inadequacy of interventions targeting only individual bias. Cultural competency training divorced from structural transformation fails to dismantle the institutional scaffolding sustaining discrimination (Castro et al. 2015, Werneck 2016, Santos Silva et al. 2025). Meaningful change requires multilevel approaches: (i) legal recognition of racism as a health determinant; (ii) mandatory collection of ethno-local data—population descriptors reflecting ethnic identification and racialization processes (Campos Navarro et al. 2017, Bomfim et al. 2022, Santos Silva et al. 2025); (iii) integration of anti-racist and decolonial frameworks into medical education (Meneses-Navarro et al. 2021, Oliveira et al. 2022, Preaux and Castro 2023, Tijoux and Ambiado 2023); (iv) establishment of legal accountability mechanisms for discriminatory practices in healthcare; and (v) crucially, meaningful participation of racialized communities in healthcare governance.

Community-based initiatives, including Afrodescendant health collectives and Indigenous advocacy movements, offer models for reimagining care rooted in collective knowledge and healing (Falu 2019, Santos Ferreira et al. 2020, Gaudenzi et al. 2023). These perspectives must move from the margins to the center of institutional decision-making, ensuring that reforms are co-produced with, rather than imposed upon, affected communities. As Latin America pursues health justice, this review suggests that dismantling healthcare racism requires transforming the very foundations of health systems. Future research should center on racialized communities as theorists and co-designers of new paradigms of care. The path forward demands structural transformation that addresses the colonial legacies and contemporary mechanisms through which healthcare perpetuates the hierarchies it purports to heal.

Stone Endowed Chair of Public Health in Latin America and directs the Center for Health Equity in Latin America at Tulane University.
