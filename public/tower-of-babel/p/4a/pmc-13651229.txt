Women’s contraceptive knowledge, attitudes, and practices in Algeria, Morocco, and Tunisia: a scoping review
Authors: Sabrine Chengane, Roa Sabra, Angel M. Foster
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough Algeria, Morocco, and Tunisia have made significant progress in family planning and remain committed to achieving socio-economic and sustainable development goals, recent years have seen a decline in contraceptive use and a rise in unmet contraceptive need.AimOur review aimed to explore available information and identify gaps in the literature on family planning in Algeria, Morocco, and Tunisia by synthesising published literature on women’s contraceptive knowledge, attitudes, and practices in these three countries.MethodsOur review followed the framework outlined by Arksey and O’Malley [1] and revised by Levac and colleagues [2]. We conducted a comprehensive search of Medline (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), CINAHL (EBSCOhost), and Web of Science, a supplementary search in Google Scholar, and a hand search of ASJP, CAIRN.info, EMHJ, and La Tunisie Médicale. We included literature in Arabic, English, and French published between January 1990 and September 2024, focusing on women’s contraceptive knowledge, attitudes, and practices in the Maghrib.ResultsWe identified 671 articles from the databases and 70 reports from the supplementary and hand searches. After removing duplicates, conducting initial screens, and retrieving full texts we ultimately included 63 sources in our review. Existing literature revealed that, although contraceptive knowledge has increased over the past decades, gaps in knowledge and misinformation persist. Additionally, fear of side effects and rumours contribute to negative attitudes toward contraception. Through this review, we observed that specific contraceptive methods promoted by early family planning programmes, and the strategies used to promote them, may have had a lasting impact on contraceptive practices, even decades later.ConclusionOur review highlights the importance of exploring changes in knowledge and attitudes and the factors that influence them, as well as women’s lived experiences with contraceptive use and access to services. This knowledge can provide more contextually relevant evidence to inform effective family planning policies and interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12978-026-02398-7.

Background

Many nations globally have expressed their commitment to fighting maternal and infant mortality, advancing women’s status and health, and promoting socio-economic development. Three countries in the Maghrib region, Algeria, Morocco, and Tunisia, are among those nations that demonstrated these commitments by working towards the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs), Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), and International Conference on Population and Development (ICPD) agendas [3]. A key avenue to achieving these goals and agendas is ensuring access to contraception [4].

Algeria, Morocco, and Tunisia adopted family planning measures in the 1960s, with Tunisia leading the way with the first national programme in the region. While the Maghrib countries share historical, linguistic, and socio-cultural similarities rooted in Arab, Islamic, and Amazigh1 heritage and identities, as well as colonial links with France, they have adopted different approaches to family planning policies and programmes [5].

In response to a sociological survey conducted in 1962 aiming to assess population contraceptive knowledge and attitudes, in 1964, the Tunisian government collaborated with Ford Foundation and the Population Council to launch an experimental family planning programme in 12 urban and semi-urban clinics, the first family planning in both the Arab World and Africa [6]. The programme expanded nationally over the 1960s and in 1970 the Tunisian Ministry of Public Health launched the first autonomous family planning centre that evolved into a network of facilities over the ensuing decade that offered contraceptive methods free of charge. The national family planning programme helped raise awareness about contraception and birth spacing and early efforts had a strong focus on promoting the intrauterine device (IUD) and tubal ligation2 [5–7]. In 1973, the Office National du Planning Familial et de la Population (ONPFP) was established to further strengthen family planning efforts. It later became Office National de la Famille et de la Population (ONFP), expanding its services and extending outreach into rural areas throughout the 1980s [6]. Also in 1973, abortion became legally permissible, without restrictions as to reason, in the first trimester and under specified conditions after that gestational duration [8]. In 2001, Tunisia became the first country in the Arab region to register an emergency contraceptive pill (ECP), NorLevo®. Subsequent efforts to expand access led to the integration of ECPs into the public health sector through evidence-based protocols, as well as in the private sector where they are available without prescription directly from pharmacies [8].

These strategies contributed to a decline in fertility from 8 children per woman in the early 1960s to 2.05 children per woman in the late 1990s. In 2021, the fertility rate was 1.8 children per woman [9]. The contraceptive prevalence rate in Tunisia increased from 31% in 1978 to 62% in 2001 [9]. Contraceptive prevalence dropped to 54.1%, according to the 2023 Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS), while the fertility rate remained at 1.8 children per woman in 2023 [10, 11].

In preparation for its family planning programme, Morocco established the High Commission on Population in 1966. Like Tunisia, the Moroccan government collaborated with Ford Foundation and the Population Council, as well as the Swedish International Development Authority and the International Planned Parenthood Federation (IPPF) for planning and implementation [12, 13]. Two years after Tunisia, in 1966, Morocco launched the National Family Planning Programme as an experimental initiative, then expanded it by integrating it into various healthcare structures managed by the Ministry of Health [14]. Morocco also conducted a national survey on contraceptive knowledge and attitudes in 1966 and 1967 [12]. In the years following the launch of the programme, the government showcased its commitment to its family planning policy by establishing different population commissions, divisions, and central services, including the Association Marocaine de Planification Familiale (AMPF) in 1971 [13].

The programme initially focused on those in urban areas and the Lippes Loop (an IUD) was the primary method; oral contraceptive pills (OCPs) and condoms were not distributed through the public system when the national programme was launched. However, in 1969, the government shifted its strategy by introducing OCPs into the governmental programme and provided three free cycles and allowed for pharmacy dispensing; by 1974, OCPs had become the most widely used contraceptive method in Morocco [14, 15]. Attempts in the 1990s to shift back to IUD use were unsuccessful, indicating a lasting preference for OCPs [16]. In 2008, Morocco authorised the sale of NorLevo®. In part due to minimal promotion, awareness and use were initially low. Although a provider’s prescription was officially required, ECPs were often available in pharmacies without one, and AMPF-supported clinics offered them at a subsidised price [17].

These efforts contributed to the decline of the fertility rate from 5.6 children per woman in 1980 to 2.5 children per woman in 2004 [18]. The contraceptive prevalence rate increased from 19% in 1980 to 67.4% in 2011 [18]. According to data from the 2018 Enquête Nationale sur la Population et la Sante Familiale (ENPSF), the contraceptive prevalence was 70.8% [13]. Although fertility had decreased over the years, it showed fluctuations with a slight increase beginning in 2014, rising from 2.21 to 2.38 children per woman by 2018. At the same time, the use of different data sources and methodologies such as census and demographic surveys has shown varying trends, making it difficult to establish a clear long-term pattern [13, 19].

Four years after Algeria’s independence, the first national population census in 1966 revealed concerning demographic growth trends [20]. Although the government initially showed limited concern, advocacy from the Union Nationale des Femmes Algériennes (UNFA) eventually succeeded in bringing the issue to political attention, leading to the establishment of a pilot centre for birth spacing at Mustapha Bacha Hospital in Algiers in 1967 [21]. Similar to the efforts undertaken in Tunisia and Morocco, the Association Algérienne pour la Recherche Démographique et Sociale (AARDES) conducted the first contraceptive knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) survey in 1967–1968. The findings revealed broad acceptance of family planning, while also highlighting the religious influence on the public’s contraceptive attitudes. In 1968, the Algerian Higher Islamic Council issued a fatwa3 in favour of birth spacing [20, 21]. During the 1970s, the government expanded services by creating centres for Protection Maternelle et Infantile et Espacement des Naissances (PMI/EN) [20]. In the 1980s, the Algerian government launched the Programme National de Maîtrise de la Croissance Démographique (PNMCD) to expand access to birth spacing information and services, which were then integrated into health centres, polyclinics, and dispensaries; OCPs were the primary contraceptive method promoted [20]. Although Algeria has registered several ECPs, there is no available information on current availability or use in the country [17, 22].

The impact of the PNMCD has been considerable. The total fertility rate decreased from 8 children per woman in 1970 to 2.8 children per woman in 1998 [23]. Moreover, contraceptive use among women in Algeria increased from 36% in 1986 to 57% in 1995 [24]. According to the 2019 MICS for Algeria, contraceptive prevalence had dropped to 53.6% [25].The fertility rate increased to 3.1 children per woman in 2014, then began declining after 2018, reaching 2.8 children per woman again in 2023 [26].

Despite implementing policies and committing to contraceptive-focused strategies to help meet health and development goals, these countries continue to experience challenges, as reflected in contraceptive use and unmet need trends. In Morocco, unmet contraceptive need levels remained largely unchanged (11.3% in 2018), while in Algeria, they increased from 7% in 2013 to 14.1% in 2019 [13, 25, 27]. Although unmet contraceptive need increased from 7% in 2011-12 to 19.9% in 2018 in Tunisia, it decreased slightly to 16.1% in 2023 [10, 28, 29]. These patterns, alongside fluctuating contraceptive use rates, some of which have declined compared to earlier rates, suggest ongoing challenges in sustaining progress in these countries. Recognising the importance of this topic and the need for evidence to better understand women’s contraceptive knowledge, attitudes, and practices in the Maghrib, we undertook this scoping review. While contraceptive use is influenced by a range of factors beyond knowledge and attitudes, these remain crucial in shaping contraceptive literacy, practices, and access to services. Through this work, we aimed to explore the extent of available information on the topic and identify gaps in the literature.

Methods

Protocol

Our review followed the framework outlined by Arksey and O’Malley and revised by Levac and colleagues [1, 2]. In consultation with a research librarian at the University of Ottawa, we developed an a priori protocol based on this framework and accordingly completed the five required steps. We reported our findings following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist [30, 31].

Eligibility criteria

In the first step, we formulated our primary research question: What do we know about women’s contraceptive knowledge, attitudes, and practices in the Maghrib? To further structure this review, we defined our secondary research aims as: What is the state of contraceptive knowledge and attitudes among women of reproductive age in the Maghrib and what contraceptive practices do women engage in?

We defined our population of focus (women and girls of reproductive age, aged 15–49), the context (three countries of the Maghrib: Algeria, Morocco, and Tunisia), and the key concepts within our topic of contraception including knowledge, attitudes, and practices.

For the scope of our review, contraceptive knowledge refers to awareness of contraceptive methods, sources of information, and available services that enable women to make informed decisions [32, 33]. Contraceptive attitudes refer to perceptions, beliefs, and feelings about contraceptives, which are closely related to intention of use [32, 34]. Contraceptive practices refer to the use (or non-use) of one or more contraceptive methods, as well as experiences accessing them.

To widen our reach, we included materials in Arabic, English, and French that were published between January 1990 and September 2024 (inclusive), as we conducted our most recent database search in September 2024. We present our inclusion and exclusion criteria in Table 1. We did not exclude publications based on their type, study design, or quality; however, we excluded abstracts if the full text was missing or inaccessible. We did not critically appraise source material or assess their quality.

Information sources and search strategy

We searched the databases Medline (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), CINAHL (EBSCOhost), and Web of Science for published literature to identify source materials in multiple disciplines. SC also conducted a supplementary “hand search” of journals and platforms including Algerian Scientific Journal Platform (ASJP), CAIRN.info, Eastern Mediterranean Health Journal (EMHJ), and La Tunisie Médicale using the same inclusion and exclusion criteria presented in Table 1. We elected to include these platforms and journals primarily for their relevance to the geographic focus and scope of our review. Additionally, SC and RS independently conducted a supplementary search on Google Scholar in French and English. These supplementary searches yielded various types of published literature, including different types of grey literature. For each search of these sources, we included the first 100 results, as these represent the most relevant items suggested by the platform or website. By applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria to these results, we selected additional source material to include in the full-text screening phase.

We used a full range of topic-relevant keywords in English and their variations in database search strings. For the supplementary searches, we used relevant keywords in Arabic, English, and French to capture a wide range of literature from different sources. SC and AMF selected keywords based on commonly used terminology in the relevant literature on our concepts and context. SC then developed a comprehensive search strategy in consultation with a research librarian. Table 2 shows an example of search strings that we used for the database search.

Study selection

We imported the results of the database searches to Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia), a literature review management software. As a first step, the principal and second reviewer (SC and RS, respectively) independently screened all titles and abstracts of the source material identified through the database search. Items that met the inclusion criteria, even those without an abstract, were included in the full-text review to avoid missing any relevant item. The two reviewers held meetings throughout the process to discuss and resolve any inclusion or exclusion conflict. We directly added the selected source material from the supplementary searches to the full-text review after screening them for primary inclusion and exclusion criteria during retrieval. We eliminated duplicates both automatically, as detected by Covidence, and manually during screening and review.

Data charting, synthesis, and analysis

SC and AMF designed a charting form to capture source material characteristics and information relevant to our review questions, including information about contraceptive knowledge, attitudes, and practices. SC and RS independently reviewed and charted content from the included literature using Covidence and Microsoft Excel® (Microsoft® Corp., Redmond, WA) to manage the charted data. SC analysed the charted data for content and themes in Microsoft Excel® and ATLAS.ti and summarised the source material characteristics. This process allowed us to synthesise the information from the included literature to answer our review questions.

Results

PRISMA outline

We retrieved a total of 671 records from the databases: Medline (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), CINAHL (EBSCOhost), and Web of Science. Covidence automatically identified duplicates (n = 152), and we eliminated further duplicates manually as we progressed through screening (n = 16), totalling 168 eliminated duplicates. Through the first step of screening of titles and abstracts of 503 records, we excluded 266 records and sought 237 records for retrieval. We retrieved 63 records, but 174 lacked the full text.

Our supplementary search via other sources including Google Scholar, ASJP, CAIRN.info, EMHJ, and La Tunisie Médicale yielded 67 records after removing 3 duplicates. We added these to the full-text review, resulting in 130 records. At this stage, we excluded 65 records as irrelevant to our review questions and 2 due to language (other than Arabic, French, or English). After consensus meetings, we identified 63 records for inclusion in our scoping review. We outline these details in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 flow diagram [30] in Fig. 1.

Literature characteristics

We present the characteristics of the included records in our review in Table 3. Of the 63 records included in our review, 50 were published as journal articles. These appeared in a variety of journals, including disciplinary, regional, international, or university-based publications. Notably, the peer review status of some journals, especially non-commercial or locally published ones, was unclear. We categorised the included records based on the overarching methodological approach adopted in them. “Qualitative” includes both empirical studies and qualitative analyses of literature and “quantitative” includes empirical research as well as studies and analyses not involving primary data collection. Most of the included records (n = 42) adopted a quantitative approach.

While some articles focused on individual countries (Algeria (n = 16), Morocco (n = 21), and Tunisia (n = 11)), others addressed combinations of two or all three, either separately or within regional or global studies. Few records in our review were in Arabic (n = 2); most were in English (n = 32) and French (n = 29). The majority of the records included information on contraceptive practices (n = 59) and/or addressed contraceptive attitudes (n = 40). Less than half of the records included information on contraceptive knowledge (n = 28). These records included data from different time periods, with most covering the 1990s (n = 34), the 1980s (n = 21), and/or the 2000s (n = 17). Fewer records addressed recent data from 2010 to 2019 (n = 11) and only two records contained data from the 2020s.

Contraceptive knowledge

Knowledge of contraception in the three Maghrib countries has increased over the decades following the adoption of their respective family planning programmes in the 1960s. By the early 1990s, contraceptive knowledge was considered ‘practically universal’ among both urban and rural women in Algeria and Morocco [43, 44].

While the studies explored knowledge across various populations and settings, most consistently found that most married women were familiar with contraception, with OCPs being the most commonly known method [63, 80, 82, 92]. Other contraceptive methods commonly recognised by women included external condoms4, IUDs, injectables, and tubal ligation as biomedical (‘modern’) options, alongside non-biomedical (also commonly referred to as ‘traditional’ or ‘natural’) methods such as withdrawal and periodic abstinence [24, 38, 63, 72, 88]. Little literature included information on knowledge of other methods such as implants, the patch, vasectomy, diaphragms/cervical caps, spermicides, or creams [36–38, 80]. No literature included information about women’s knowledge of emergency contraception and none specifically addressed knowledge of the internal condoms. However, it is possible that some studies presented information on these methods indirectly under broader categories such as condoms, vaginal methods, or ‘other’ methods, without reporting them individually [24, 35].

Notably, the language used across various source material often categorised contraceptive methods as either ‘modern’ or ‘traditional/natural.’ The literature also highlighted common sources of contraceptive information, including relatives and friends, medical professionals and hospitals, and media [7, 63, 85].

There is limited information about contraceptive knowledge among unmarried women, as most studies primarily focused on married women of reproductive age. Masmoudi-Soussi and colleagues examined the sexuality of single Tunisian university students, finding that knowledge of contraceptive methods was poor among this group [70]. Similarly, Essaghairi highlighted the lower quality of contraceptive knowledge among unmarried compared to married couples in Tunisia [51].

Misinformation and rumours appeared to be common factors affecting contraceptive knowledge and practices. Some studies shed light on these nuances in Morocco and Tunisia [41, 43, 56]. Common beliefs that hormonal contraceptives cause infertility, IUDs would ‘fall out’ in women performing heavy chores, and tubal ligation is ineffective because the fallopian tubes would ‘grow back together’ are examples of such rumours and misinformation [41, 43].

Contraceptive attitudes

Our scoping review revealed a spectrum of attitudes towards contraceptives. Although these stances were not necessarily explicitly classified as ‘attitudes’ in the literature, we categorised and present them in this review using the binary of positive and negative. Studies suggested that contraceptive attitudes were influenced by myriad factors, including personal desire for children, societal perceptions of motherhood, and religious beliefs [42, 43, 60, 74]. Additionally, concerns related to contraceptive use, such as resistance to taking a daily pill, fear of experiencing side effects, health concerns, and the influence of rumours, played a significant role in shaping these attitudes [43, 56, 66]. The influence of religion on decision-making with respect to contraception is complex, as reflected in studies suggesting that women ‘negotiate and reinterpret’ Islamic mandates when making decisions about contraception [60, 80]. Although some women viewed contraception as religiously prohibited, they still chose to use contraceptives, often for birth spacing or by justifying their use due to economic hardship [80].

Research has highlighted different negative attitudes toward specific contraceptive methods, such as OCPs, IUDs, injectables, and tubal ligation [43, 45, 56, 81]. Moreover, societal expectations and cultural norms in these countries contribute to negative attitudes regarding contraceptive use among certain groups. Adolescents who discuss contraception, women undergoing tubal ligation, and unmarried women seeking contraceptive services are a few examples of groups facing social stigma [41, 43, 70, 73].

More generally, women held positive attitudes toward contraception due to perceived benefits such as achieving desired family size and overcoming socio-economic challenges [5, 7, 66]. While contraception can be considered a couple’s shared concern, studies highlighted women’s central role in decision-making and choice of contraceptive methods [54, 63, 65]. The literature reported on preferences for specific methods as well as instances where women switched contraceptive methods either from biomedical to non-biomedical to avoid health effects, or from non-biomedical to biomedical due to perceived higher efficacy [80, 81, 88]. Extended family also appears to influence contraceptive behaviour, as reported in Algeria and Tunisia, and shifting perceptions of contraception in Algeria has been linked to growing acceptance among the older generation [9, 64].

Contraceptive practices

Most of the literature focused on contraception among married women, with evidence indicating that contraceptive use in the three countries was more prevalent among women with children [64, 88, 90]. On the other hand, poor contraceptive practices and limited access were observed among unmarried women [51, 73]. For instance, a study on sexual debut in Morocco highlighted that unmarried women often relied on periodic abstinence and withdrawal instead of methods that required clinic visits [41].

Over the years, contraceptive use increased among women of all education levels; however, some education-level differences have remained evident [38, 44, 50, 86]. Contraceptive use also varied between rural and urban areas. Studies showed that the gap between urban and rural contraceptive use decreased between the 1980s and 2000s, but the disparity did not disappear entirely [35, 49, 50, 58].

Oral contraceptive pills were the most widely used contraceptive method in Algeria and Morocco, whereas this method was less popular in Tunisia [27, 43, 44, 59, 76, 90]. In contrast, the IUD and tubal ligation were the most commonly used methods in Tunisia [43, 51]. Studies also suggested that women more commonly used biomedical methods than non-biomedical ones [24, 69]. Breastfeeding was one of the most commonly used non-biomedical methods [24, 36, 53].

Existing research pointed to various factors influencing women’s discontinuation and non-use of contraceptives, with experienced side effects, health problems, or fear of side effects or complications being commonly cited [55, 56, 66, 84, 90]. Additional factors included husband disapproval, desire for pregnancy, and religion [36, 39, 43, 62, 84, 86].

Although the three countries made significant progress in their family planning outreach, unmet contraceptive need persisted and varied. In Algeria and Tunisia, unmet need varied by socio-economic status and urban/rural residence. In Algeria, education level and age also played a role [27, 50].

A key finding from our review was the limited literature addressing contraceptive use among women living with disabilities or chronic diseases. Although one study in Algeria did not specifically present evidence on women with disabilities, it suggested that contraceptive use in this population may be low due to potential drug interactions or side effects that could impact health [80]. Additionally, one publication included a study in Algeria that highlighted changes in methods used by women with diabetes, with tubal ligation being more common in this population [42].

Discussion

Through this scoping review, we aimed to examine the extent of existing literature focusing on women’s contraceptive knowledge, attitudes, and practices in Algeria, Morocco, and Tunisia. We observed that many studies included in our review used national surveillance data such as Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), Pan Arab Project for Child Development surveys (PAPCHILD), and MICS [50, 62, 81, 83]. Additionally, we noted that most of the data discussed in the literature originated from over two decades ago (the 1980s and 1990s). We found that a considerable portion of the literature addressed this topic through a sociology lens, often within the context of demographic growth, development, and politics, rather than through a health-oriented lens.

Our review revealed that contraceptive knowledge has significantly increased in the three countries over the years, and is often reported as universal or near-universal [43, 44]. However, evidence indicated that knowledge gaps persist and misinformation remains prevalent. Additionally, studies included in our review indicated that negative attitudes towards contraceptives stem from lack of knowledge, fear of side effects, and rumours. The literature also highlighted other factors that influence attitudes but can be overlooked when planning programmatic efforts, such as societal perceptions of motherhood, desire for children, and the role of the extended family. Therefore, continued research that explores changes in knowledge and attitudes and the factors that influence them is essential to effectively address misinformation and promote health literacy [93].

The initial focus on specific contraceptive methods in early national family planning efforts appears to have significantly influenced the acceptance and utilisation of those methods over time. For instance, early policy and programmatic choices such as Tunisia’s emphasis on tubal ligation and IUDs, compared to Algeria and Morocco’s promotion of OCPs, contributed to shaping method preferences and practices among these populations decades later [7, 43, 85].

National data from the three countries show varied contraceptive method mix among married women, reflecting both shared patterns and differences in country-specific method preferences. In Tunisia (2023 data), method mix was balanced between the IUD (47.6%) and the oral contraceptive pill (OCP) (42.5%). The use of tubal ligation (2.9%), external condoms (2.2%), implants (2%), and injectables (1.5%) was limited, while other methods including breastfeeding (0.7%), vasectomy5 (0.3%), and internal condoms (0.2%) were used to a lesser extent. No use of periodic abstinence was reported and other methods were 0.1% [94]. Two ECPs are currently available in Tunisia: NorLevo (progestin-only) ®, which does not require a prescription, and ellaOne® (ulipristal acetate), which does; however, no data are available on their use [95].

In contrast, Algeria (2019 data) and Morocco (2018 data) showed a significant reliance on OCPs, which accounted for 86.7% and 82.4% of method use, respectively. Other methods such as IUDs (5.3% in Algeria; 7.8% in Morocco), external condom (4.7% in Algeria; 4.2% in Morocco), and breastfeeding (1.6% in Algeria; 1.9% in Morocco) were used at low levels. Permanent and long-acting reversible methods’, including tubal ligation (0.9% in Algeria; 1.7% in Morocco), implants (0.7% in Algeria), injectables (1.7% in Morocco), and vasectomy (0.2% in Algeria) were limited or not reported. Periodic abstinence was negligible in Morocco (0.3%), while other methods and the internal condom were not reported [22, 96]. While Morocco had two ECPs available (NorLevo® and ellaOne®), no data were available on their use; and in Algeria, ECPs were not reported [95].

Recent years may have seen a shift in contraceptive use, with non-biomedical methods gaining increased popularity [50, 80]. This potential trend, requiring further investigation, suggests that users are prioritising what they perceive as ‘safer’ and more ‘natural’ options. This observation signals the need for studies of contraceptive knowledge, attitudes, and practices to understand behaviour change and the factors that influence this change and inform efforts to address misinformation.

Our review found a growing body of research on the topic of contraception, characterised by notable data generation during the 1980s and 1990s. These trends may have resulted from an interest in evaluating and assessing the impact of the family planning programmes launched in the 1960s. However, we also observed a decrease in data generation starting in the 2000s. Given the ongoing commitment in the three Maghrib countries to reducing maternal and infant mortality, advance women’s health, and foster socio-economic development, it is essential to return the topic of contraception to the forefront of research agendas in different disciplines, including work to address this topic through a health lens [3, 97]. Such an approach can inform policies not only grounded in demographic trends but also focused on health-related factors and dynamics, as contraception plays a key role in advancing women’s reproductive health, rights, and broader population health outcomes.

Finally, few recent studies focused on the lived experiences of women’s contraceptive practices and their access to services in the Maghrib. The literature is especially limited in addressing the specific needs of vulnerable groups, such as women living with disabilities or chronic diseases. These individuals may face unique contraceptive experiences and challenges that are not typically reflected in studies focused on healthy adults. Therefore, there is a critical need for more inclusive, dedicated research to explore these populations’ experiences and needs, in order to inform equitable and adapted policies and programmes. Furthermore, qualitative research can help capture overlooked factors and local community dynamics, offering explanations for emerging trends as populations develop and change over time [98]. Such knowledge can complement national surveys and provide deeper insights to inform evidence-based policies to improve access to contraceptive services and procurement, while more effectively addressing health behaviours and literacy [99].

Limitations and implications

Our focus on exploring the extent of existing information on women’s contraceptive knowledge, attitudes, and practices in the Maghrib reflects our recognition of their importance in understanding trends in contraceptive use and unmet need. However, we acknowledge that knowledge and attitudes should be considered alongside other factors that also shape contraceptive use and access to services. Although we took a rigorous approach to our scoping review, we note several limitations. First, we attempted to use a comprehensive search strategy to identify relevant literature by using a wide range of terms. However, we may have missed relevant records that used different terminology, especially since the topic of contraception can be addressed through different disciplinary lenses where language varies. Second, our search strategy involved searches in four major databases, hand searching of selected local journals and platforms, and supplementary searches in Google Scholar; however, we did not conduct hand searching of reference lists due to practical constraints related to time and resources to keep the review manageable [2].

While our approach was broad and reproducible in the four major databases, it is likely that we missed some relevant literature through the supplementary search, particularly those published in local journals, where many in-country researchers publish their work, which are not indexed in the selected databases. Additionally, we are aware that a significant portion of literature may exist as physical documents at research centres in the selected countries and are not accessible electronically. We also recognise that despite our efforts to include various sources, some relevant literature, such as books, dissertations, and edited volumes, may not have been fully captured by our search, even when available in full text electronically. This became evident when we noted the absence of certain known contributions on the topic, potentially due to how these types of publications are indexed or how we retrieved source material through our search strategy. Considering this and recognising the broader lack of recent data on the topic, this gap presents a barrier to advancing evidence-based policies informed by the key changes influencing contraceptive behaviour, such as evolving social norms and technologies. To help address this, we found it necessary to explore a wide range of literature by including non-peer-reviewed source material. Therefore, we reiterate the importance of continued evidence generation through research and documentation to fill this critical gap. Future scoping reviews on this topic and region may benefit from more extensive supplementary search strategies and systematic inclusion of various types of grey literature to ensure broader evidence mapping.

Finally, we adopted an independent charting approach in which each of the two reviewers extracted a single study. The principal researcher analysed all the charted data, synthesised relevant information, and translated the results into findings that answer our review questions. Therefore, we recognise the potential bias throughout the different steps of this scoping review. Nevertheless, we sought to minimize these limitations by consulting with a librarian specialised in health sciences research from the early stages of designing this scoping review protocol. We also held multiple meetings throughout the phases of the review to discuss progress and address any conflicts or emerging questions.

Conclusion

A significant body of research exists on the topic of contraception in Algeria, Morocco and Tunisia; however, much of it examines the 1980s and 1990s, through various lenses in the broader context of demographic growth and societal development. Stagnation in research on contraception in the three Maghrib countries, or perhaps its limited dissemination through peer-reviewed databases, reflects the possible relegation of contraception to a lower priority topic of inquiry. Existing literature often relied on data from periodic large-scale national studies, suggesting a missed opportunity to explore lived experiences and dynamics through locally focused, mixed-method, and qualitative studies that contribute to more nuanced understanding of the topic. Importantly, additional research that explicitly adopts a health lens could help address knowledge gaps relevant to effective policy formulation and implementation. Our findings emphasise the need to elevate the topic of contraception in research agendas in the Maghrib and point to several gaps in the literature that warrant further investigation.
