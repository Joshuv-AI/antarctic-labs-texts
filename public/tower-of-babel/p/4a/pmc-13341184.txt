Update on Glucocorticoid-Induced Osteoporosis: Emerging Therapeutic Strategies
Authors: Soo-Kyung Cho, Yoon-Kyoung Sung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glucocorticoids are widely prescribed for inflammatory diseases, but their adverse skeletal effects, particularly osteoporosis and fracture, remain clinically important. Glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis (GIOP) is characterized by rapid disruption of bone remodeling, with an early increase in bone resorption followed by suppression of bone formation. These changes substantially increase the risk of vertebral fracture. Fracture risk in GIOP cannot be explained by reductions in bone mineral density alone; it is also thought to reflect glucocorticoid-related deterioration in bone quality and microarchitecture. Current international guidelines recommend fracture risk assessment when glucocorticoid therapy is initiated and support a risk-stratified therapeutic approach. Oral bisphosphonates remain first-line therapy for individuals at low to moderate fracture risk. For patients at higher risk, more potent antiresorptive agents, including zoledronic acid and denosumab, have been shown to improve bone mineral density. In individuals at very high fracture risk, anabolic therapy followed by antiresorptive treatment is increasingly recommended. Careful attention to treatment sequencing is essential, particularly after denosumab discontinuation, for preventing rebound-associated vertebral fractures. Recent guideline updates and comparative clinical studies have placed greater emphasis on anabolic therapy and structured treatment sequencing in order to improve skeletal outcomes. This review summarizes evolving risk-based management strategies for GIOP, focusing on treatment selection by fracture risk category and planned transitions between osteoporosis therapies.

INTRODUCTION

Glucocorticoids (GCs) are widely prescribed anti-inflammatory agents and have remained central to the management of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and many autoimmune and inflammatory diseases since their introduction in the 1950s [1,2]. Population-based studies from Western countries have estimated GC use in 0.7% to 17.1% of adults [3–7], and the prescribing rate of long-term oral GC therapy has increased over the past two decades [4]. In Korea, the prevalence of chronic oral GC use (≥30 days) increased more than three-fold between 2002 and 2015 [8]. This trend has increased the number of patients at risk for GC-related skeletal complications.

Glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis (GIOP) is characterized by rapid disruption of bone remodeling, including an early increase in bone resorption followed by sustained suppression of bone formation and osteocyte activity [9]. GC exposure has been associated with reduced bone density and increased fracture risk. Fracture risk rises within the first few months after GC initiation, often before measurable reductions in bone mineral density (BMD) are detected [10,11]. Approximately 12% of patients receiving chronic GC therapy experience symptomatic fractures [12], and radiographic vertebral fractures are detected in up to 27%–51% of long-term users [13]. Importantly, fracture risk in GIOP cannot be explained by BMD alone, because age, sex, menopausal status, reduced physical activity, and underlying inflammatory disease also contribute to skeletal fragility [14].

Although early intervention is essential to reduce fracture risk, many patients receiving GCs are not adequately evaluated or treated for osteoporosis [15,16]. Updated guidelines now recommend treatment selection through an individualized, risk-stratified framework that emphasizes structured long-term care.

This review summarizes current risk-stratified treatment strategies for GIOP.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

The direct skeletal effects of GCs have traditionally been conceptualized as occurring in two distinct phases [17]. During the initial phase, GC exposure promotes osteoclastogenesis and increases bone resorption through upregulation of receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand and macrophage colony-stimulating factor, accompanied by suppression of osteoprotegerin production [18,19]. This early imbalance between bone resorption and bone formation causes rapid bone loss, particularly during the first months of therapy [20]. With continued exposure, the predominant mechanism shifts toward suppression of bone formation. GCs reduce osteoblast number and activity by inducing apoptosis in osteoblasts and osteocytes and by impairing the differentiation of mesenchymal progenitor cells. Upregulation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-gamma promotes differentiation of mesenchymal progenitor cells into adipocytes rather than osteoblasts, thereby reducing osteoblastogenesis [21]. In parallel, GCs inhibit Wnt/β-catenin signaling by increasing the expression of Wnt antagonists such as Dick-kopf-1, leading to sustained suppression of osteoblast differentiation and activity [5,22,23].

However, skeletal fragility in GIOP cannot be explained solely by reductions in BMD. Vertebral fractures frequently occur at relatively higher BMD values [24], suggesting impaired bone quality. Imaging studies have demonstrated reduced cortical thickness and increased cortical porosity despite comparable BMD [25]. These findings indicate that GIOP involves both quantitative bone loss and deterioration of bone microarchitecture.

EPIDEMIOLOGY

GIOP is the most common cause of secondary osteoporosis worldwide [9]. Fracture risk increases with both higher dose and longer duration of GC exposure, and excess risk becomes evident within the first months of therapy [10,26,27]. Even low doses of prednisone-equivalent GCs (2.5 to 7.5 mg/day) are associated with increased vertebral fracture risk, corresponding to a 1.7- to 2.5-fold increase [10,27]. Risk rises rapidly within 3 to 6 months after GC therapy is initiated and is greater among patients exposed to higher doses for prolonged periods [28,29].

Most epidemiological evidence on GIOP derives from studies of chronic oral therapy. However, non-oral routes of administration may also have systemic skeletal effects. A recent review noted that inhaled, epidural, and intra-articular GCs can result in systemic absorption, particularly with repeated exposure [30]. In addition, a large population-based study reported that repeated epidural steroid injections were associated with increased fracture incidence, with hazard ratios of approximately 1.3–1.4 for any fracture and 1.5–1.7 for vertebral fracture; risk was higher among individuals who received more frequent injections [31]. These findings suggest that cumulative GC exposure should be considered when fracture risk is assessed, regardless of administration route.

RISK STRATIFICATION IN GIOP

Fracture risk should be assessed when GC therapy is initiated and in patients who have received GCs for more than 3 months. Recent guidelines differ slightly in the populations emphasized for fracture risk assessment. The American College of Rheumatology (ACR) guideline broadly recommends initial assessment for all adults initiating or continuing GC therapy at a dose of at least 2.5 mg/day for over 3 months [32], whereas the European Calcified Tissue Society (ECTS) guideline emphasizes women and men aged 50 years or older, as well as younger adults with a prior fragility fracture or other clinical features associated with increased skeletal risk [33].

Recommended assessment extends beyond BMD and includes clinical risk factors, BMD measurement, vertebral imaging, and fracture risk assessment tool (FRAX)–based probability estimates when applicable. Earlier guidelines relied primarily on BMD thresholds, whereas contemporary approaches increasingly incorporate clinical risk factors and probability estimates derived from FRAX, often adjusted according to GC dose [34]. In the ACR guideline, vertebral fracture assessment or spine radiography is recommended together with BMD measurement, and FRAX is applied in adults aged 40 years or older [32]. Recent guidelines also emphasize clinical factors that are not fully captured by BMD or FRAX, including fall risk and comorbid conditions associated with secondary osteoporosis. The ECTS guideline recommends assessment of fragility fracture history, systematic vertebral imaging, GC-adjusted FRAX, and BMD at the lumbar spine and proximal femur [33]. Although intervention thresholds vary among guidelines, prior fragility fracture, advanced age, and higher GC exposure consistently support early initiation of pharmacologic therapy in recent international recommendations [32,33]. Fractures among GC users frequently occur despite relatively preserved BMD values [28], and FRAX may underestimate risk, particularly in individuals with vertebral fractures [35] or those receiving recent high-dose GC therapy [36]. Therefore, vertebral imaging and careful clinical judgment remain essential components of comprehensive fracture risk assessment [37]. Trabecular bone score has been proposed as an adjunctive tool to refine fracture risk assessment; however, routine use is not consistently recommended across current guidelines [36].

Routine biochemical evaluation, including serum calcium, albumin, phosphate, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, and creatinine measurement, is recommended in GC-treated patients to assess bone health and identify secondary contributors to skeletal fragility [33]. Bone turnover markers cannot distinguish GIOP from primary osteoporosis and are not diagnostic of GIOP. A characteristic biochemical pattern during GC exposure includes suppression of bone formation markers, with variable effects on markers of bone resorption. Although markers such as osteocalcin, procollagen type I N-propeptide, and C-terminal telopeptide are recommended for monitoring response to osteoporosis therapy [38], their clinical utility in GIOP remains limited by biological variability, underlying disease activity, and the absence of standardized data specific to GC-treated populations [36].

Accordingly, recent guidelines recommend an integrated risk assessment strategy that combines clinical risk factors, BMD measurement, vertebral fracture assessment, and GC-adjusted fracture probability estimates to identify patients who may benefit from early pharmacologic intervention (Table 1) [32,33,39,40].

In addition to chronic oral GC therapy, other routes of GC administration may have clinically relevant skeletal effects. Although less is known regarding the impact of non-oral GC exposure, increasing evidence suggests that repeated local administration, such as epidural or intra-articular steroid injections, may contribute to bone loss and increased fracture risk [36]. Recent observational data indicate an increased risk of osteoporotic and vertebral fractures after epidural steroid injections, highlighting the potential contribution of cumulative GC exposure even with non-systemic routes [30,31]. Similarly, intermittent high-dose GC therapy may influence bone metabolism; however, the magnitude and clinical significance of these effects remain less clearly established than those of long-term oral therapy [36]. Current international guidelines primarily base fracture risk assessment and treatment decisions on overall GC exposure, including dose and duration, and do not provide specific recommendations for local injections or pulse therapy. In clinical practice, these exposures should therefore be considered part of cumulative GC burden when fracture risk is assessed.

TREATMENT AND MANAGEMENT OF GIOP

Multiple guidelines for the management of GIOP have been developed and updated by several academic societies (Table 2). The Korean GIOP guideline was recently established through adaptation of previously published international guidelines [39]. Current international recommendations support a risk-stratified treatment framework rather than a uniform treatment strategy, with pharmacologic therapy selected according to baseline fracture risk, prior fracture status, and the extent of GC exposure (Fig 1).

Although these guidelines share common principles, they differ in the timing of treatment initiation, the definition of high-risk populations, and how anabolic therapies should be used within the treatment sequence. Overall, the ACR guideline applies explicit risk tiers—low, moderate, high, and very high—whereas European and UK guidelines focus primarily on identifying high-risk patients who warrant early pharmacologic intervention (Table 1) [32,33,39,40]. Several recent guidelines allow or favor an anabolic-first strategy for selected patients at very high fracture risk, particularly when rapid reduction of vertebral fracture risk is a clinical priority [32,33,36,40].

Non-pharmacological measures are recommended across all risk groups. Lifestyle modification and adequate calcium and vitamin D supplementation should accompany pharmacologic therapy [41]. Current guidelines recommend maintaining serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels within approximately 20 to 50 ng/ mL, with typical supplementation of 400 to 800 IU daily or equivalent dosing strategies [33]. In addition to bone-directed pharmacologic therapy, early implementation of steroid-sparing strategies is essential to reduce cumulative GC exposure and prevent long-term skeletal complications [42,43].

Evolution of treatment paradigm and risk-stratified pharmacologic treatment

Historically, pharmacologic treatment of GIOP relied primarily on antiresorptive therapies, particularly oral bisphosphonates, which were recommended as first-line treatment for most patients receiving chronic GCs. However, more recent research has shown that prolonged GC exposure suppresses osteoblast activity and impairs bone formation, resulting in a low-turnover skeletal state. These findings have increased interest in anabolic therapies that stimulate bone formation and help restore skeletal balance. Recent clinical studies support this evolving treatment paradigm. In a propensity score-matched cohort study comparing denosumab, teriparatide, and romosozumab in patients with GIOP, romosozumab produced the greatest increase in lumbar spine BMD among the three therapies [44]. Similarly, a comparative clinical study evaluating romosozumab, denosumab, and risedronate in patients newly initiating GC therapy reported superior improvements in lumbar spine BMD with romosozumab [45]. In non-GIOP osteoporosis populations, romosozumab has also been shown to improve trabecular bone score, suggesting effects on bone microarchitecture beyond increases in BMD [46]. However, concerns regarding potential cardiovascular risk have been raised [47], contributing to its cautious positioning in current guidelines. Together, these findings support a gradual shift toward greater consideration of anabolic therapies in patients with severe GIOP, particularly those at very high fracture risk.

Current guidelines use a risk-stratified approach to treatment selection (Table 2). In patients at low to moderate fracture risk, oral bisphosphonates remain the preferred first-line therapy [41]. Prolonged bisphosphonate use has been associated with rare but clinically significant adverse events, including osteonecrosis of the jaw and atypical femoral fractures [48]. Accordingly, periodic reassessment of fracture risk and treatment duration is recommended.

Selective estrogen receptor modulators may be considered in selected postmenopausal women who are unable to tolerate bisphosphonate therapy; however, their role in GIOP remains limited compared with other antiresorptive or anabolic options [49].

In patients at high fracture risk, more potent antiresorptive agents, including intravenous bisphosphonates or denosumab, may be considered [50,51]. Denosumab is widely used in clinical practice because of its potent antiresorptive efficacy. Previous studies have also reported that denosumab may reduce joint structural damage in patients with early RA [52,53]; this favorable impact of denosumab may support its use in selected patients with inflammatory rheumatic diseases requiring GC therapy. When denosumab is prescribed, clinicians should recognize that its antiresorptive effect rapidly diminishes if injections are delayed beyond 6 months [54]. Discontinuation is followed by rapid bone loss and increased risk of multiple vertebral fractures [54]. Therefore, transition to another antiresorptive agent, such as a bisphosphonate, should be planned when denosumab therapy is discontinued [55]. Sequential antiresorptive therapy should be initiated without delay after the last denosumab dose, generally within approximately 7 months, to prevent rebound-associated bone loss and reduce the risk of vertebral fractures [56].

In patients at very high fracture risk, including those with prior vertebral fractures, multiple fractures, markedly low BMD, or sustained high-dose GC exposure, an anabolic-first strategy is increasingly recommended (Table 2). Antiresorptive therapy alone may be insufficient in patients with prolonged GC exposure, particularly when bone formation is suppressed and a low-turnover skeletal state is present [41,57]. Teriparatide, a recombinant human parathyroid hormone, has demonstrated superiority over alendronate in increasing BMD and reducing vertebral fractures in GIOP, although benefits for nonvertebral fractures were not statistically significant [58,59]. Abaloparatide has shown significant BMD gains and fracture risk reduction in postmenopausal osteoporosis [57], although evidence specific to GIOP remains limited. Romosozumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting sclerostin, increases bone formation while simultaneously reducing bone resorption [60]. Although evidence in GC-treated populations remains limited, emerging clinical data in chronic GC users suggest that romosozumab can achieve greater spine BMD gains than denosumab over 12 months, with continued gains observed after transition to antiresorptive therapy [61]. Because cardiovascular risk remains a concern, romosozumab should be considered on an individual basis, with attention to each patient’s baseline cardiovascular risk.

Sequential therapy and treatment transition

Sequential treatment strategies in GIOP should be individualized according to whether GC therapy is ongoing or has been discontinued and whether osteoporosis medications, particularly denosumab or anabolic agents, have been used previously. Because direct evidence in this area remains limited, current approaches are largely informed by recent ECTS recommendations, which emphasize reassessment of fracture risk after GC discontinuation and consideration of continued therapy in patients with ongoing exposure [33]. Anabolic therapy should generally be followed by antiresorptive treatment to preserve BMD gains. This concept of sequential therapy represents an important shift in osteoporosis management, moving away from fixed single-agent treatment and toward structured long-term therapeutic strategies tailored to fracture risk.

This sequential strategy is particularly relevant in patients at very high fracture risk, for whom initial anabolic therapy may rapidly reduce vertebral fracture risk and can then be followed by antiresorptive therapy to maintain skeletal benefits. Denosumab discontinuation should be followed by transition to a bisphosphonate or another antiresorptive agent, because interruption of therapy has been associated with rebound bone loss and increased risk of vertebral fractures [54,55]. Together, these recommendations move GIOP management away from fixed single-agent therapy and toward planned treatment sequences.

CONCLUSIONS

GIOP remains a major cause of secondary osteoporosis and is associated with fracture risk that is not fully captured by BMD alone. Current management emphasizes risk-stratified treatment selection that integrates clinical risk factors, GC exposure, and prior fracture history. In patients at high or very high fracture risk, appropriate use of antiresorptive or anabolic therapy is essential for effective fracture prevention. Recent guideline updates and clinical studies have emphasized the role of anabolic therapy and structured treatment sequencing in improving long-term skeletal outcomes. Future studies are needed to clarify optimal treatment strategies and sequencing approaches in GC-treated populations. Updates to national guidelines, including the Korean guidelines, will also be important to incorporate emerging evidence and evolving therapeutic options.
