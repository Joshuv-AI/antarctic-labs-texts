Prospective cohort study on the characteristics of acute poisoning patients at a tertiary care hospital in Pakistan
Authors: Shaib Muhammad, Khalida Faryal Almani, Ubed-ur-Rehman Mughal, Muhammad Saleh Khaskheli, Razia Sultana, Rafia Tabassum, Salman Ahmed, Rabbiya Ahmad, Narendar Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to determine the characteristics of acute poisoning patients.DesignThis was a prospective cohort study.SettingThe study was conducted for 1 year (1 July 2023 to 30 June 2024) at a tertiary care hospital in Sindh, Pakistan.ParticipantsFrom the patients who arrived at the emergency department due to poisoning, 1404 were registered and included in the study.Outcome measuresThe data were collected on demographics (gender, age, residential area, education, employment) and poisoning characteristics, prehospital care, treatment, and services at the hospital, and treatment outcomes (survived and died). A χ2 test was used to find the association between independent variables and treatment outcomes. A multivariate logistic regression model was used to determine the predictors of death at a 95% CI.ResultsThe majority of patients were male (57.1%) and aged ≤30 years (77.6%). The poisoning was primarily intentional (67.5%), and pesticides (56.1%) were commonly involved in the poisoning. The patients were managed mainly by symptomatic treatment (98.1%) and gastric lavage (65.1%). Multivariate logistic regression indicated that delayed reporting (adjusted OR (AOR)=2.00; 95% CI 1.20 to 3.36; p=0.008) and non-existence of antidote (AOR=1.81; 95% CI 1.08 to 3.03; p=0.025) increased the odds of death while unintentional poisoning (AOR=0.27; 95% CI 0.14 to 0.51; p<0.001) and prolonged stay at hospital had a protective effect (AOR=0.19; 95% CI 0.10 to 0.38; p<0.001).ConclusionThe study found that the intentional pesticide poisoning within uneducated, young populations in rural areas was significantly prevalent, and early identification and management of severe cases and extended hospital stays influenced survival.Keywords: Risk Factors, Suicide & self-harm, Toxicity

Introduction

Acute poisoning is the result of exposure to a xenobiotic for a brief period (<24 hours) and is a common cause of visits to the emergency department. It may occur through any route and may be intentional or unintentional.1 The outcomes of acute poisoning range from nausea and vomiting to coma and death.2 The morbidity and mortality due to acute poisoning may vary depending on socioeconomic, cultural and healthcare infrastructure levels.3 Acute poisoning causes one million worldwide morbidities and has a 20% fatality rate.4

In developed countries, common causes of acute poisoning-related emergency admissions are therapeutic drug poisoning,5 whereas in developing countries, the primary causes of poisoning are pesticides and household cleaning substances.6 Furthermore, in developed countries, the harmful effects of poisoning are well documented, while health impacts and risks are not well assessed in developing countries. This situation arises due to the absence of an adequate surveillance system that is essential for the efficient reporting and management of such cases.7,9 These surveillance systems help in prevention and avoiding death traps through education and clinical research programmes.10 In addition to this, easy access to toxic chemicals and drugs, poor healthcare systems and ineffective regulation on the sale and purchase of poisonous substances, morbidity and mortality are even more common in low-income and middle-income countries.11

Most admissions to emergency departments due to acute poisoning are from deliberate self-harm rather than accidental events. Globally, there was a significant 26% increase in attempted suicides involving toxic substances between 2015 and 2019.12 From 2012 to 2019, the WHO also reported a drastic increase of 30.8% in suicide attempts in Pakistan.9 13 14 While Pakistan’s economy was stable in that era, the current financial and political instability has made life miserable. It is challenging for many households to make ends meet.15 The current financial crisis is also the result of the COVID-19 pandemic, which has also affected other parts of the world.16 Studies have also shown that the COVID-19 pandemic has made young individuals more prone to suicidal attempts by using poisons.17 Therefore, COVID-19 has produced a domino effect that has fuelled a significant increase in suicides and attempted suicides.16 17

It is essential to mention that the causes of acute poisoning have seen remarkable changes in recent years.18 Substances that were previously involved in the poisonings are not reported currently in emergency department visits.19 Therefore, it is necessary to examine the changes in acute poisoning in recent years to ensure adequate resource provision in the emergency departments. Moreover, establishing efficient strategies through prevention and intervention to alleviate the risk is also required.1

These evolving trends underline the importance of more investigations aimed at understanding the actual risk factors linked with poisoning.18 20 Though there are studies that have been conducted in Pakistan, these studies are more urban-centred and cover only certain geographical areas.21 22 Moreover, the earlier studies were based on limited variables, and statistical associations were not evaluated.23,25 There have been no previous detailed studies in the current geographical location where the study has been conducted. Understanding the prevalent poisoning patterns within a specific geographical area can facilitate the prompt identification and management of poisoning incidents. This can also enhance the current global understanding of the poisoning incidents. Such proactive measures are instrumental in reducing both the prevalence and severity of associated health outcomes, consequently diminishing the rates of morbidity and mortality. So, there was a dire need to carry out a study to cover the research gaps.

Methods

Study design

A prospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital in Sindh, Pakistan. The province of Sindh is the second most populous in Pakistan.26 There are a total of 102 civil, specialised and taluka public sector hospitals in 30 districts27 that serve a population of 55.7 million.26 The medical superintendent of the Peoples Medical College Hospital (PMCH) was requested through a formal letter from the University of Sindh for permission and facilitation in the execution of the study. Through a letter (Reference No. PMCHN/SBA11017/30), the medical superintendent allowed the research study and circulated it among all the relevant departments for facilitation.

The study protocol was scrutinised and approved by the Institutional Bioethics Committee of the University of Sindh, Jamshoro, Pakistan (Reference No. ORIC/SU/1484).

Study setting

A prospective cohort study was conducted at PMCH in Shaheed Benazirabad, Sindh, Pakistan. This study was carried out for 1 year, starting on 1 July 2023 and ending on 30 June 2024. The study centre is geographically located in the centre of Sindh. This hospital is a tertiary healthcare facility that serves as a referral hospital and receives many patients because of its geographical location. The population of its catchment area is 1.06 million.26 PMCH is affiliated with the Peoples University of Medical and Health Sciences for Women, Shaheed Benazirabad, Sindh, Pakistan, and imparts education and training to undergraduate and postgraduate students.

Study participants

The data was collected using a non-probability purposive sampling technique. All consecutive cases admitted to the emergency department due to poisoning during the data collection period were included in the study, regardless of their age and the cause of poisoning. The cases that had comorbidities, unclear diagnosis, chronic poisoning, did not give their consent, were dead at arrival, left against the medical advice, exposed to more than one substance and could not be followed up were not registered in the study.

Data sources

The data was collected on a pre-designed questionnaire that was developed by reviewing the literature.3 5 9 18 20 28 Three academic experts validated the study instrument. The changes were also made according to sociodemographic characteristics and local policies in the hospital, following a pretest involving data collection from 50 patients before a full-scale study. The questionnaire collected data regarding demographics and poisoning characteristics, prehospital care, treatment and services at the hospital. The demographics included gender, age group, residential area, education and employment status, while the poisoning characteristics variable included the cause of the poisoning, the poison category and the route of exposure. Variables in the prehospital care involved site of exposure, type of admission, time from exposure to the poison and reporting to the hospital, any local remedy provided at the site of exposure, and whether the ambulance service was provided to the patient. Furthermore, in the treatment, the variables included the emergency management, decontamination, elimination procedures and therapeutic interventions. Moreover, the services provided in the hospital were also determined in the study; these included antidote availability, type of indoor service (ward, intensive care unit (ICU) and observation unit), and the duration of stay at the hospital.

When the patients arrived at the hospital, the physician at the emergency department diagnosed them. If poisoning was suspected, first aid was given, and the patient was shifted to the medicine ward, where further management was carried out. If the patient became stable, the patient was discharged; however, in severe conditions, the patient was shifted to the ICU. The hospital had one emergency department, four medicine wards and two ICUs, and the data collection was carried out at all these departments. The investigator followed all the patients in these departments and personally filled out the questionnaire to avoid problems in understanding questions, anomalies and missing data. The questionnaire was filled by asking questions from the patient and/or attendant (in case the patient was unconscious or in an altered level of consciousness or a child), physicians and nursing staff, and by reviewing patient medical records. The follow-up with the patients was done after 30 days of discharge from the hospital. The medical outcomes of the study were:

Survived

Patients who may have had exposure to poison but were alive after 30 days of discharge from the hospital.

Died

Patients who could not survive during the treatment in the hospital or after 30 days of discharge from the hospital after exposure to a poison.

Statistical methods

The data was entered into IBM SPSS V.29, and statistical tools were used to analyse it. The categorical variables were represented by using descriptive statistics, such as frequencies and percentages. The χ2 test was used to determine the association of various independent variables with medical outcomes. To determine the potential predictors of treatment outcomes (survived or died), a binary logistic regression model was used at p<0.05 for statistically significant association and a CI of 95%. Multivariate analysis was done for independent variables having p<0.05 using backward stepwise multiple logistic regression. The adjusted OR (AOR) and its 95% CI were calculated for each independent variable included in the analysis. The risk factors were the variables significantly associated with the likelihood of death. The survival with reference to exposure to reporting time after poisoning and duration of stay at the hospital, and their association with the cause of poisoning and poison category, was determined through the Kaplan-Meier Survival Curve.

Ethical approval and consent from the participants

A pre-designed pro forma was used to obtain informed consent from the study participants. When the patient arrived at the emergency department, the physician diagnosed the patient, and first aid was given. After that, the patient and/or the attendant was asked to participate in the study. If the patient and/or attendant provided consent, a written informed consent was obtained. In cases of children or mentally unstable patients, the consent was provided by the parents or guardians. Those who were not willing to take part in the study were excluded. To maintain the confidentiality of data, the identity of each patient was coded on the study instrument, and the data were used only for the study purposes. The Declaration of Helsinki was followed throughout the study.29 30

Patient and public involvement

Patients were involved in the design and conduct of this research. The results of the study were not disseminated to study participants.

Results

During the data collection period, 1404 patients met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Figure 1 details the number of participants at each stage of screening. The majority of the patients were male (57.1%) and aged ≤30 years (77.6%). Predominantly, the patients belonged to rural areas (89.4%), were uneducated (68.3%), but were employed (60.8%). Poisoning was primarily intentional (67.5%), and pesticides (56.1%) were the most common type of poison. In about three-quarters (72.6%) of the patients, the route of exposure was ingestion. Additionally, the study discovered that more than three-quarters (79.9%) of poisoning incidents occurred at home, and the majority were direct admissions (57.3%). About two-thirds (65.7%) of the patients arrived at the study centre after 1 hour of exposure to the poison, and only 18.0% were transported by ambulance. In 6.6% of patients, a local remedy was administered at home. Among the study participants, 6.1% died because of the poisoning. Statistical analysis

The χ2 test was used to determine the association of various independent variables with medical outcomes. Though the demographic characteristics did not have any association with the medical outcomes, the poisoning characteristics as the cause, and prehospital care as a type of admission and exposure to reporting time were associated with the medical outcome (p<0.05). Table 1 details the association of medical outcomes with demographics, poisoning characteristics and prehospital care.

While looking for the association of treatments and services at the hospital with the medical outcome, it was found that airway support, catheterisation, gastric lavage, antidote administration, antidote availability in the hospital, ICU services and duration of stay at the hospital were significantly associated (p<0.05). Table 2 details the association of treatment and services at the hospital with medical outcomes.

Bivariate regression analysis included variables with p<0.05. These variables were entered into a multivariate logistic regression analysis to control for expected confounding variables affecting mortality. The variables were categorised into two models: (1) Demographics, poisoning characteristics and prehospital care and (2) Treatment and hospital services. Variables in each category were analysed to discover the potential factors affecting mortality.

In the first model, unintentional poisoning showed more survival (AOR=0.27; 95% CI 0.14 to 0.51; p<0.001) as compared with intentional poisoning. While reporting to the hospital after 1 hour of exposure to the poisoning, 2.0 times increased the odds of death (AOR=2.00; 95% CI 1.20 to 3.36; p=0.008).

The second model, which included variables from treatment and hospital services, indicated that patients who required airway support and ICU services had increased odds of death with AOR=7.93; 95% CI 4.23 to 14.84; p<0.001 and AOR=8.69; 95% CI 4.33 to 17.47; p<0.001, respectively. Similarly, the global non-existence of an antidote for a specific poison also had increased odds of death (AOR=1.81; 95% CI 1.08 to 3.03; p=0.025). However, increased stay at the hospital had a protective effect with AOR=0.19; 95% CI 0.10 to 0.38; p<0.001, as described in table 3.

Discussion

Acute poisoning is one of the significant causes of emergency department visits and is a global public health concern.1 This was the first prospective cohort study in Pakistan carried out for a year to determine the annual variations in poisoning. The previous studies in Pakistan were for a short period (<1 year) and estimated the number of poisoning cases in a year by extrapolation. During the study period, 1404 patients of poisoning were registered in the study, which is a much higher number than in previous studies.3 31

In the current study, poisoning was predominant in males (57.1%), which is consistent with studies carried out in Zambia32 and Iran.20 33 This may be due to the more occupation-related exposure to poison in males as well as the stress to make both ends meet.34 However, other studies in China,1 Ethiopia,28 Iran30 and Egypt35 have reported a prevalence of poisoning in females, which may be due to frustration due to family disharmony and increased societal pressures on women in those parts of the world. The poisoning incidences were more prevalent in individuals aged ≤30 years (77.6%), which is consistent with studies in other regions of the world.1 28 34 It seems that the young population cannot handle the societal, occupational and economic pressures that come as they reach adulthood.34 36 This is further confirmed through the predominance of intentional poisoning (67.4%) in this study and is also true for other parts of the world.1 28 36 37 This study also found that there was decreased survivability in the cases of intentional poisoning as compared with unintentional (AOR=0.27; 95% CI 0.14 to 0.51; p<0.001), and it was more profound with delays in arrivals at the hospital (figure 2).

In agriculture-based economies like Pakistan, pesticides are easily accessible35 38 due to poor regulation of these harmful substances.39 Additionally, the choice of substance in suicide and self-harm depends on the availability rather than the lethality.40 This is consistent with this study, as pesticides (56.1%) were the most common substances involved in the poisoning. The route of exposure primarily involved in this study was ingestion (72.6%), which is consistent with other studies.20 28 41 42 The prevalence of pesticides may also be because patients belonged to rural areas (89.4%), and because the majority of subjects were uneducated (68.3%) in this study, 60.8% were employed as farmers by profession and had easy accessibility to the pesticides. This contrasts with studies carried out in urban populations, where drugs and chemicals were predominant.1 20 31

During this study, it was found that most poisoning cases occurred at home (79.9%), which is in concordance with other studies. These studies also reported a predominance of suicidal cases.1 28 33 39 42 This may be because the majority of the poisoning cases are intentional and are influenced by family disharmony and marital and financial issues, which is why poisoning cases mainly occur at home.9 28 39 Furthermore, though this study reported that only 18.0% of patients were transported to the study centre by ambulance, the percentage is higher than a multicentre study conducted in 2010–2011 in Pakistan, which reported only 7.0% of patients.31 This recent increase in ambulance services may be due to the establishment of Sindh Emergency Service Rescue 1122 in 2022 in Sindh, Pakistan.43 Moreover, most patients arrived at the study centre after 1 hour of exposure (65.7%), consistent with another study conducted in Pakistan.35 44 The time of arrival has a profound effect on the medical outcomes. There is a decrease in survivability as the reporting time increases, as shown in figure 2. Multivariate logistic regression also indicates that the delayed arrival has two times the odds of death (AOR=2.00; 95% CI 1.20 to 3.36; p=0.008).20 35 45 This delay in arrival at the hospital is due to the unavailability of transport in rural areas, especially at night.35 This study also discovered that a local remedy was provided to the patients in 6.6% of cases to mitigate the effects of poison, which is lower than a study conducted in Ethiopia, which reported 58.8% and found its association with mortality. These remedies are intended to cause gastric emptying through vomiting and diluting the poisoning, but their effectiveness in the literature has not been proven.39 Even the literature suggests that the local remedies increase the chances of aspiration, which causes further complications.35

This study also found inconsistencies in treatment as per the international guidelines, which is in concordance with the other parts of the world as well.28 41 The guidelines suggest that gastric lavage should not be done routinely after exposure to a poison and may be associated with serious complications. Even if there is an utmost requirement of gastric lavage, it should be done by persons with training and expertise.46 However, the data in this study indicate that the procedure was performed routinely, as 65.1% of patients received gastric lavage. Furthermore, in delayed arrivals, there are more chances of systemic absorption of the poison, and elimination is a suitable method to mitigate the toxic effects.47 48 However, despite the majority of patients’ late arrival (65.7%) at the hospital, elimination was done through diuresis (2.4%) and multi-dose activated charcoal (0.1%) in very few cases, and extracorporeal techniques were not used. Furthermore, skin and eye decontamination are essential interventions to decrease the absorption of the poison. Literature suggests that in poisoning incidents, 7.9% of patients have exposure through the skin, which is concordant with this study, as 7.5% of patients had skin and eye contact and were decontaminated.48 The most common pharmacological treatment in the study was symptomatic and through circulatory support, and the most non-pharmacological treatment was gastric lavage. This may be due to the lack of recommended diagnostic tests to administer specific antidotes.28 34 39 45 However, the use of the antidotes in this study is higher (64.1%) than in other studies. This may be due to availability differences in different regions of the world.28 39 Moreover, the majority of the patients in this study stayed at the hospital for a day, which is contrary to studies conducted in other parts of the world,28 35 while another study in Egypt has consistent results.30 Studies conducted in Ethiopia and Egypt found a statistical association between the duration of stay at the hospital and survival. While this study, through Kaplan Meier Survival Curve and Multivariate logistic regression, indicated that, with an increased stay at the hospital, the chances of survival were higher (Figure 2; AOR=0.19; 95% CI 0.10 to 0.38; p<0.001).4 35

The outcomes of the poisoning seem to be promising as they were discharged within a day.34 However, the mortality rate appears to be concerning, as 6.1% of patients died, which is similar to another study conducted in Pakistan49 but higher than in Egypt35 and China.1 36 However, the truthfulness of the data may be in doubt as patients who were dead before arrival were excluded from the study.36 41 Some patients might have been declared dead due to poor prognosis, and the mortality rate may have increased if these patients were admitted. Furthermore, many of the patients may have died before reaching the hospital, and in many cases, the exposure and mortality due to intentional poisoning were not reported due to societal and cultural barriers.9 50 The studies in India and Iran, two countries bordering Pakistan, have reported much higher mortality rates (13.2% and 19.5% respectively).20 51

This study identifies various factors that are associated with mortality due to poisoning. The regulatory authorities should take concrete measures to control the easy accessibility of the poison to the public. People should be educated about the safe use and storage of poisons and prehospital care at sites of exposure. The exposure to reporting time can be decreased through easy accessibility of ambulances by emergency services. Moreover, the transport through ambulances can be made further effective by providing first aid during the transportation, as a prompt and comprehensive response decreases mortality.

This study has some limitations. The study was carried out in a single centre, and the results cannot be generalised to the other regions of Pakistan or the world. Some of the treatment consistencies may be due to factors that the patients involved in the study were of different types of poisoning and required different treatments. Separate studies should be carried out on different poisons to determine the variations in the management and outcomes of the poisoning. The poisoning cases from both children and adults were registered in the study; however, the adults and children may have different effects and outcomes of poisoning. The patients who were dead on arrival were excluded from this study, and inclusion of those patients might have increased the mortality rate. Separate studies should be carried out at primary and secondary healthcare facilities to report these deaths. The responses from the patients or the attendants may be biased due to memory recall or cultural barriers in reporting intentional poisoning in the study location.

Conclusion

This study explored the epidemiological patterns and demographic characteristics associated with acute poisoning incidents. The study found that the intentional pesticide poisoning within uneducated, young populations in rural areas was significantly prevalent, and that most deaths occurred immediately following the poisoning, highlighting the importance of early identification, prompt management of severe cases and extended hospital care in improving survival.

Through identifying vulnerable groups, delineating clinical presentations and clarifying therapeutic approaches, this research contributes significantly to the foundational knowledge necessary for developing targeted, evidence-based public health interventions to address the ongoing challenge of poisoning.
