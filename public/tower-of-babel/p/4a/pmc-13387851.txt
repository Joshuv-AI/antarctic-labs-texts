Assessing the Relationship Between Work-Related Factors and Mental Health in Public Health Doctors During National Health Emergencies
Authors: Ji-ae Yun, JinYoung Lee, Hyein Song, Sejin Choi, Sangyoon Han, Kyeong-Sook Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSouth Korean public health doctors (PHDs) serve as frontline responders in underserved regions during national health crises, such as the COVID-19 pandemic. However, the system has not adapted to evolving healthcare needs and despite the essential roles of PHDs, little is known about the unique occupational stressors they face. We examined how work-related factors, especially national guidelines and policies, impact the mental health of PHDs during public health emergency deployments, providing evidence to inform policy and support systems for this workforce.MethodsAn online cross-sectional survey of all PHDs serving between July and August 2021 was used to collect data on demographics, work-related stress, and mental health outcomes using validated questionnaires. Associations between work factors and mental health (depressive symptoms, embitterment, burnout) were analyzed using logistic regression.ResultsAmong 269 PHDs (mean age 29.7 years), 27.1% reported depressive symptoms, 9.3% embitterment, and 45.4% burnout. Dissatisfaction with financial compensation was associated with higher risks of depressive symptoms (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 2.481), embitterment (aOR, 3.695), and burnout (aOR, 2.997). Deployment of 3–6 months increased embitterment (aOR, 3.770) and burnout (aOR, 2.469). Job demands predicted all outcomes (aOR, 1.033–1.040). National guidelines/policies and concerns related to work and environment were associated with depressive symptoms (aOR, 1.138, 1.056) and burnout (aOR, 1.105, 1.042).ConclusionThis study highlights the need for adequate compensation, manageable deployment duration, and strong organizational support, including clear national guidelines and policies to mitigate mental health risks, among PHDs during public health emergency deployments.

INTRODUCTION

Medical professionals possess high levels of expertise and face significant job demands. These factors often lead to increased stress and mental health issues within the profession.1 In South Korea, public health doctors (PHDs) are young male physicians who serve mandatory military duty for 37 months in underserved regions, making their mental health a crucial concern within the unique context of the PHD system.2 PHDs hold the status of fixed-term public officials under Article 3 of the Rural Health Act; thus, they have both public servant obligations and medical professional duties.3 In practice, medical institutions in underserved rural or remote areas rely heavily on PHDs, who since the system’s establishment have played a crucial role as primary care providers in public health centers (or public health units) where access to medical services is otherwise limited.4 However, PHDs face challenging work environments characterized by unclear job scopes that often include excessive administrative duties and a lack of professional development opportunities.4 Additionally, the substitute military service framework limits long-term motivation and continuity, as many PHDs serve out of obligation rather than voluntary commitment to rural or public health practice. The fixed 37-month service term further disrupts clinical continuity, undermining both system effectiveness and public trust.5 More than half of PHDs start their public health service immediately after graduating from medical school or completing their internship, while others begin after finishing their residency training.56 Consequently, most PHDs have either no or limited clinical experience, restricted to a maximum of 4 years of residency training. This arrangement places them in a unique position within the South Korean healthcare system.

Notably, this group was pivotal at the frontlines during the early stages of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic.7 After the first confirmed case of COVID-19 appeared and cases rapidly surged, PHDs in South Korea were urgently deployed to conduct large-scale screening and diagnostic testing.3 PHDs not only responded to the initial COVID-19 outbreak but also took on additional critical roles such as working as field epidemiologists, serving as airport quarantine officers, staffing local screening centers with extended hours, and caring for patients with mild symptoms in residential treatment centers while coordinating their transport to higher-level hospitals if symptoms worsened.7

Numerous studies have investigated the mental health of healthcare professionals engaged in national emergency response deployments, including those responding to COVID-19.8 These studies consistently report high levels of occupational stress and mental health challenges among frontline health workers. However, to our knowledge, there is a lack of research focusing specifically on the mental health of South Korean PHDs deployed during public health emergencies, including the COVID-19 pandemic. Based on previous findings, it is reasonable to expect that South Korean PHDs also experienced considerable occupational stress while fulfilling their roles as experts during the acute national crisis of the pandemic. Additionally, PHDs in South Korea hold a distinctive institutional status, and their occupational stress is likely to be shaped more by organizational and systemic factors than by individual determinants. Moreover, because PHDs are deployed in times of crisis, their mental health directly affects the responsiveness and resilience of the national healthcare system. Research on their mental health must therefore address both organizational stressors and broader implications for public health preparedness. Against this backdrop, we investigate how work-related factors, including national guidelines and policies, influenced the mental health of PHDs deployed during the COVID-19 pandemic, with the aim of informing policy measures to better support their mental health and enhance the healthcare system’s responsiveness through its professional workforce in future public health emergencies.

METHODS

Recruitment

The study targeted all PHDs in South Korea serving between July and August 2021. Recruitment was conducted in collaboration with the Korean Association of Public Health Doctors (KAPHD). First, the research team approached the KAPHD to explain the study and request cooperation. Then, the KAPHD, in prior agreement with the research team, disseminated the study description and survey URL to the entire population of PHDs as of 2021, using the contact information held by the association.

Sample and data collection

The online platform automatically collected and stored responses in Excel format, ensuring efficient data collection and storage. A survey URL was distributed to 1,621 medical PHDs from the KAPHD, resulting in 324 voluntary participants (response rate 20.0%). After excluding 24 duplicate responses (identified via phone number verification as an individual participating twice for participation rewards), 6 extreme values (identified by reviewing ID lists close to the possible maximum and minimum scores of each scale and excluding respondents exhibiting extreme values in 4 or more variables, reflecting a conservative approach focused on removing cases likely to represent patterned or careless responding), and 25 participants who did not engage in public health emergency deployment, data from 269 participants were analyzed.

Measures

Independent variables

We collected data on demographic, occupational, and deployment-related factors. Demographic and socioeconomic information, including year of birth, current living arrangement, marital status, and religion, was collected as these factors may influence mental health outcomes and could serve as potential covariates in the analysis. Occupational information comprised training status, years of service, and workplace details. To assess work-related factors associated with PHDs’ deployment for COVID-19 emergency response, we measured the duration of deployment and satisfaction with financial compensation for deployment. The following scales were used to measure occupational stress.

1) Korean Occupational Stress Scale Short Form (KOSS-SF)

We used the KOSS-SF9 to measure occupational stress related to deployment duties. Specifically, we utilized 11 items from 3 subscales: job demands, lack of job autonomy, and inadequate compensation. Each item was scored on a 4-point Likert scale. The scores for each subscale were calculated using the formula: 100 × (Actual Score − Number of Items)/(Possible Maximum Score − Number of Items). The internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s alpha) was 0.76.

2) COVID-19-specific sources of distress scale

We used the scale on COVID-19-specific sources of distress, developed by Shechter et al.,10 to assess occupational stress associated with public health emergency deployment. We obtained permission from the original authors via email to translate and use the scale in Korean. The scale was translated into Korean considering sociocultural characteristics while minimizing alterations to retain the original meaning. The translation was verified by a professional translation agency through back-translation and showed minimal differences. The original scale consisted of 24 items. However, considering the sociocultural context in South Korea, an additional item was included in the “concerns outside the clinical environment (e.g., family)” category: “when returning to the original workplace, being rejected by local residents and neighbors owing to concerns about the possibility of COVID-19.” Thus, the instrument contained 25 items, each scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (no distress) to 5 (extreme distress). We categorized stressors as highly distressing if rated as either 4 (very distressing) or 5 (extremely distressing).10 Cronbach’s alpha was 0.92. Although the scale was translated into Korean and carefully adapted considering sociocultural context, the Korean version has not yet undergone formal validation. Therefore, the translated scale’s psychometric properties, such as construct and criterion validity, remain to be established. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study.

Dependent variables

Three measures were used to assess mental health outcomes.

1) Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) Korean version

The Korean version of the PHQ-9, translated and validated by Han et al.,11 was used to screen for depressive symptoms. This self-report questionnaire, originally developed by Spitzer et al.12 based on Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition criteria for major depressive disorder, consists of 9 items. Participants rate the frequency of experiencing each problem over the past 2 weeks on a 0–3 scale, resulting in a total score range of 0–27. A cutoff score of 9 was used for depressive symptom screening, based on sensitivity and specificity studies in South Korea.131415 Cronbach’s alpha was 0.91.

2) Posttraumatic Embitterment Disorder Scale Korean version

The Korean version of the Posttraumatic Embitterment Disorder Scale (PTED), translated and standardized by Shin et al.,16 was employed to evaluate embitterment reactions to negative life events. This self-report scale, originally developed by Linden,17 consisted of 19 items assessing psychological state, social functioning, emotional reactions to events, and thoughts of revenge over the past year. Each item is rated from 0 to 4, with total scores ranging from 0 to 78. A cutoff score of 48, as proposed by Shin et al.,16 was used to identify posttraumatic embitterment disorder. Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.97.

3) Single-item burnout measure

A translated version of Rohland et al.’s18 single-item burnout measure was used to measure general burnout. The translation process involved obtaining permission from the original authors, considering South Korean sociocultural characteristics while minimizing alterations, and conducting a reverse translation comparison. Following this study, the Korean version of the single-item burnout measure was validated through a separate validation study in 2023.19 The single item is rated on a 1–5 scale, with scores ≥ 3 indicating the presence of burnout symptoms.

Statistical analysis

Frequency analysis was conducted to determine prevalence rates of key variables. The χ2 and t-tests were performed to explore associations between demographic characteristics, work-related factors, and mental health assessment outcomes. To examine relationships between work-related factors and mental health outcomes among PHDs, logistic regression analyses, both univariable and multivariable, were utilized to identify factors associated with each mental health assessment item. Univariable logistic regression examined individual effects of variables, while multivariable logistic regression assessed combined effects while controlling for potential confounders. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with significance set at P < 0.05.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Eulji University Hospital, Daejeon (EMC 2021-04-013). Informed consent was submitted by all subjects when they were enrolled.

RESULTS

Table 1 presents the demographic and clinical characteristics of the 269 participants. All participants were men (100%), with a mean age of 29.66 years (standard deviation = 2.37). This demographic profile reflects the nature of the PHD system in South Korea, which serves as an alternative to mandatory military service for male physicians who have recently completed medical training. In terms of professional development, over half of the participants (56.1%) had obtained their medical license, while 23.4% had completed internship and 20.4% had board certification. The majority (84.8%) were deployed to rural administrative units (Eup/Myeon) and served primarily at public health centers (or public health units) (87.7%), consistent with the program's mission to address healthcare disparities in medically underserved areas.

Fig. 1 shows that the most common sources of distress among PHDs were systemic factors such as lack of national treatment guidelines (72.5%) and lack of national clinical guidelines about post-discharge care for patients with COVID-19 (60.2%). Concerns about family related to aspects outside the clinical environment were also prevalent, including social distancing from family (63.2%) and fear of transmitting COVID-19 to family members (60.6%). Among concerns related to work and environment, perceptions of a lack of control and/or uncertainty (64.7%) and uncertain COVID-19 status of other healthcare providers (62.8%) were most frequently reported.

Logistic regression analyses revealed several key factors associated with mental health outcomes (Table 3). The adjusted model controlled for age, current living arrangement, marital status, religion, and years of service as covariates. Dissatisfaction with financial compensation predicted all outcomes: depressive symptoms (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 2.481), embitterment (aOR, 3.695), and burnout (aOR, 2.997); the association was strongest for embitterment. Deployment of 3–6 months increased the risk of embitterment (aOR, 3.770) and burnout (aOR, 2.469). Job demands were consistently associated with depressive symptoms (aOR, 1.033), embitterment (aOR, 1.041), and burnout (aOR, 1.038). Inadequate compensation predicted depressive symptoms (aOR, 1.029) and burnout (aOR, 1.032), while lack of autonomy related only to burnout (aOR, 1.016). Public health emergency deployment-specific sources of distress from national guidelines and policies (aOR, 1.138) and concerns related to work and environment (aOR, 1.056) were significant for depressive symptoms; corresponding aORs for burnout were 1.105 and 1.042, with no associations for embitterment.

DISCUSSION

The findings demonstrate clear associations between occupational stress domains and mental health outcomes among PHDs. These findings align with established occupational stress research among healthcare professionals2021 but take on particular significance within the structural context of the PHD system. Our study, consistent with previous research,2022 confirmed that perceived lack of reward among PHDs, including dissatisfaction with financial compensation, is significantly linked to depressive symptoms, embitterment, and burnout. The “inadequate compensation” dimension measured by the KOSS-SF also reflects unfair treatment, future uncertainty, and interrupted opportunities, aligning with Siegrist’s effort–reward imbalance model23 that emphasizes both financial and non-financial rewards such as respect.24 Non-financial rewards are crucial in maintaining morale and commitment among healthcare professionals, particularly in public health settings and during national emergency responses.2526 Given their unique occupational status, establishing an effective incentive scheme that addresses the non-financial reward needs of PHDs in South Korea responding to national emergencies is critical.2527

Longer deployment duration was significantly associated with embitterment and burnout, consistent with studies showing that prolonged work hours elevate mental health risks.28293031 Notably, 64.7% of PHDs reported distress due to lack of control and uncertainty (Fig. 1), and 84% disagreed that they had control over workload and schedule (KOSS-SF item 8), indicating limited job autonomy. Setting clear deployment duration standards may enhance predictability and job autonomy, thereby reducing mental health risks during national emergency responses.32

Importantly, we also found that PHDs reported considerable concerns outside the clinical environment. However, these concerns were not associated with adverse mental health outcomes except for depressive symptoms. This finding is consistent with previous research in which the odds ratios for burnout and stress were higher for work-related factors compared with non-modifiable or non-work-related factors.3334

Lack of national guidelines and limited policies were associated with depressive symptoms and burnout. These findings illuminate significant structural vulnerabilities within South Korea’s public health emergency response system, particularly concerning the mental health burden experienced by PHDs during national disaster deployment. The most striking finding is that 72.5% of PHDs identified the lack of national clinical guidelines for COVID-19 treatment as their primary source of distress, followed by lack of national clinical guidelines about post-discharge care for patients with COVID-19 (60.2%). The absence of such guidelines places PHDs in an untenable position where they must make clinical decisions without adequate institutional support or clear procedural frameworks.

As mentioned in the Introduction, structural characteristics of the PHD system compound the challenges observed in this study. When deployed during national emergencies, these physicians face the dual burden of providing frontline medical care while navigating unfamiliar emergency protocols without adequate institutional support.3 The lack of national guidelines becomes particularly problematic for this population, as they lack the extensive clinical experience that might otherwise compensate for procedural ambiguities.3536 Similarly, we found that higher perceived job demands were associated with depressive symptoms, embitterment, and burnout. Specifically, 52% of PHDs reported significant distress when required to perform clinical work outside of their specialty (Fig. 1). Studies have shown that the presence of treatment guidelines37 and just-in-time training3839 can reduce occupational stress by helping manage uncertainty and decrease decision fatigue.4041 Furthermore, given the high job demands perceived by PHDs during national disaster emergency responses, it is essential to provide clear clinical guidelines and prioritize their mental health by recognizing these demands and providing appropriate psychological support and resources.42434445

First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal interpretations between work-related factors and mental health outcomes among PHDs, as the data represent a single time point. All measures were self-reported, which may introduce bias due to participants’ subjective perceptions or social desirability. Furthermore, the scale on COVID-19-specific sources of distress, while crucial for interpreting our results, has not been validated specifically for measuring distress in national disaster situations among PHDs or other healthcare professionals. Given the absence of validated scales for pandemic-related distress both domestically and internationally, we utilized this scale based on its prior use in similar research. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting findings based on this instrument. Although the survey was distributed to all PHDs in South Korea, the voluntary nature of participation, anonymous responses, and low response rate (20%) may have introduced selection bias and non-response bias, both of which may limit the generalizability of the results. Furthermore, because we focused solely on PHDs deployed during the COVID-19 emergency response, the results may not be generalizable to other healthcare professionals or different contexts. Finally, while several confounders were controlled for, unmeasured factors such as individual coping styles or pre-existing mental health conditions may have influenced the results, and burnout was assessed with a single-item measure, which may not fully capture its complexity.

Despite the limitations, the results have important implications for both policy and practice. Our findings indicate that policymakers should prioritize adequate financial and non-financial compensation and robust support systems for PHDs during emergency deployments. In addition, strategies to appropriately manage deployment duration and provide psychological support are essential to reduce the risk of mental health problems. Most importantly, ensuring strong organizational support and providing clear, comprehensive national guidelines and policies for emergency response are critical factors in alleviating distress among professionals. This is essential to effectively respond to national public health emergencies and to sustain the healthcare system even during crises.
