A Robust Methodology to Elucidate Kinetics of Room Temperature Electrochemical Propane Adsorption on Platinum
Authors: Ashutosh Bhadouria, Durvesh Eknath Parab, Karen Dayana Gonzalez Morales, Chunge Li, Daniel Alejandro Zea-Villa, Po-Chun Huang, Jeffrey Greeley, Brian M. Tackett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrocatalytic activation of alkanes can further decarbonize chemical manufacturing by leveraging affordable renewable electricity and readily available shale gas reserves in the United States. Earlier works have identified the unique role of Pt in adsorbing and activating alkanes, like propane, at room temperature in acidic, aqueous electrolytes, revealing spontaneous formation of deeply dehydrogenated propane-derived surface species with an intact C3- backbone. Although an adsorption mechanism was hypothesized, it has not been explicitly investigated to date, preventing the quantification of kinetic rate parameters. A robust methodology to investigate and benchmark propane adsorption kinetics on Pt is critical for the rational design of electrocatalysts that exhibit higher selectivity toward desired partially oxidized products. Herein, we analyze an oxidative current transience that appears during the adsorption of propane on Pt in aqueous electrochemical conditions and develop a methodology that elucidates the adsorption mechanism and enables quantification of rate parameters such as order dependences and apparent activation barriers. This method yields an expected first-order dependence with respect to propane concentration at low coverage and reveals a second-order dependence with respect to the concentration of surface active sites. Additionally, the apparent activation barrier for propane adsorption was calculated using an Arrhenius analysis of the current transience under temperature control. The experimentally measured activation barrier of 35 kJ mol–1 is in excellent agreement with the theoretical barrier calculated by density functional theory (DFT). The kinetic analysis was extended, via the use of transition state theory, to extract entropy and enthalpy of activation, yielding consistent results with the proposed two-step adsorption mechanism and DFT calculations. These results demonstrate reliable quantification of kinetic parameters for electrocatalytic activation of C–H bonds in alkanes that can be employed for rational catalyst development for a versatile range of electrocatalytic conditions.

Introduction

An increase in light alkane feedstock availability, due to the shale gas revolution in the past few decades, has incentivized investment in alkane valorization processes. − These processes directly utilize light paraffins, rather than naphtha, to produce higher-value chemicals, such as alkenes, alcohols, aromatics, and oligomers. Industrially, these transformations are commonly achieved via high-temperature thermal catalytic processes. However, with the growing availability of renewably sourced electricity and a pursuit to decarbonize the chemical industry, there is a push to explore alternative routes to activate and valorize light alkanes. Consequently, electrocatalytic alkane activation has seen renewed interest due to its ability to utilize electrochemical potential as the driving force to enable these chemical transformations. Recent works show that platinum (Pt) electrocatalysts enable activation and reaction of ethane, propane, and butane to various shorter-chain alkanes and, in some cases, olefins. However, rates and selectivity toward desired products remain low, and it is difficult to avoid overoxidation to CO2. ,− Regardless of the reactant or desired product, adsorption is the requisite first step for catalytic alkane activation and has often been proposed as the rate-limiting step in various thermal catalytic processes, such as propane dehydrogenation. Thus, accurate kinetic measurements of the alkane adsorption process in the electrocatalytic system should yield insights to guide performance improvement.

Kinetic parameters, such as order dependence with respect to concentration of active sites, are key metrics that can enable rational design of electrocatalysts to selectively form desired products. However, there is a lack of established parameters or kinetic insights for electrochemical alkane activation. A few works in the early 1970s explored propane adsorption kinetics on Pt at elevated temperatures of 90 °C in 30 mol % HF. , The authors employed potentiodynamic techniques to study the kinetic dependence of adsorption on Pt sites, where reductive coulometry was utilized to estimate the rate of adsorption. Propane was adsorbed at constant potential to favor coverage of propane-derived adsorbate species on the Pt surface. These adsorbates were then subsequently desorbed using a linear reductive potential sweep to 0 V vs reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE). The total cathodic charge, as a result of the reductive sweep, was recorded, and the obtained value in the presence of hydrocarbon adsorbates (Q prop) was compared to that without, i.e., under inert gas flow (Q Ar). The authors argued that the presence of propane-derived surface species hindered hydrogen underpotential deposition (HUPD) on Pt, attenuating the reduction charge in proportion to hydrocarbon coverage. Thus, eq was used to estimate the surface coverage of adsorbed hydrocarbon species (θHC). The change in surface coverage with varying adsorption time was related to a simplistic Langmuirian adsorption rate model (eq ), where k ads is the adsorption rate constant, C HC is the concentration of propane/hydrocarbon in the electrolyte, and m represents the order dependence on vacant active sites on the Pt catalyst. Their analysis yielded a vacant site order dependence of m = 3 on Pt. Our recent work, however, revealed that the cathodic charge during an identical experiment is, in fact, convoluted by multiple electrochemical processes and cannot be directly used to delineate adsorption kinetics. In addition to HUPD, some charge is consumed by the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), and reductive conditions also favor electrochemical hydrogenation of surface adsorbates to C1–C3 gas products. Therefore, the previous analysis overestimates surface coverage, and there is a need to revisit propane adsorption kinetics. Herein, we report a methodology utilizing transient coulometry to reliably delineate propane adsorption kinetics. We show how relevant parameters such as rate orders, activation energy, and enthalpy/entropy of activation can be accurately obtained to study electrocatalytic adsorption of propane on Pt metal. Moreover, we compare the rate parameters obtained to first-principles density functional theory (DFT) calculations to demonstrate the robustness and versatility of our proposed methodology. We utilize a coulometric artifact of propane adsorption on Pt (an oxidative current transience) to correlate current to the rate of propane adsorption. This transience, observed upon propane introduced to the electrochemical cell, has been previously linked to an anodic current from the oxidation of an H* adatom. We show that this oxidation process directly proceeds after the initial C–H bond activation, resulting in the formation of propane-derived adsorbate species. Therefore, transient amperometry offers a window to study adsorption kinetics via potential- and temperature-regulated experiments. Adsorption reaction orders with respect to vacant sites and propane concentration were calculated as second order and first order, respectively. Obtained rate parameters combined with a nonmonotonic dependence on potential enabled us to establish a consistent two-step microkinetic adsorption model for propane adsorption on Pt.

The methodology developed using the transient technique was further implemented to calculate crucial kinetic parameters, such as apparent activation energy (E a), and the entropy (ΔS ‡) and enthalpy (ΔH ‡) of the transition state for adsorption. The apparent activation barrier and activation enthalpy determined via temperature-controlled adsorption experiments and corroborated by DFT calculations demonstrate the feasibility of propane adsorption is accessible at 20 °C in 1 M HClO4 on a Pt electrode held at 0.3 V vs RHE. It is shown that the propane adsorption process is primarily limited by an entropic barrier, due to a low enthalpic barrier yielding a favorable free energy of activation at room temperature and the chosen adsorption potential. The current transience, therefore, provides quantitative insights into the C–H bond scission, shedding light on the kinetics and mechanism of propane activation.

Research and Discussion

Using Transient Coulometry to Study Propane Adsorption Kinetics

The extent of propane adsorption on Pt has been previously studied by utilizing gas pulse and purge techniques combined with the application of potentiodynamic sequences at the electrocatalyst. This involves holding the potential at the working electrode constant while pulsing in reactant gas for a specific time interval, followed by purging the electrolyte with an inert gas, leaving the chemisorbed species at the electrode surface. After purging, voltage-programmed oxidation or reduction of these adsorbates, combined with detection of desorbed species, reveals information regarding the surface coverage, analogous to temperature-programmed desorption experiments often employed for thermal heterogeneous catalysis studies. For the present study, this procedure was facilitated by pulsing propane through the inlet chip of a SpectroInlets electrochemical mass spectrometer (ECMS) system that enables rapid gas equilibration in a small working electrolyte volume. The working principle and setup of this operando technique have been discussed elsewhere. , Here, 100% propane at 1 atm was pulsed through the ECMS chip to a Pt electrocatalyst in 1 M HClO4 held at a constant potential of 0.3 V vs RHE, yielding a current transience, shown in Figure . The origin of this current was previously ascribed to the oxidation of H* species to protons upon dissociative adsorption of propane at potentials positive of the HUPD region. Therefore, this electrochemical signature can be considered a direct measure of propane adsorption rate on Pt electrodes.

The highlighted peak current density (black, filled circle; Figure ) should therefore be directly proportional to the initial rate of propane adsorption on Pt, that is, the rate of adsorption when the hydrocarbon coverage is zero. Throughout the experiment, the Pt electrode is held at 0.3 V vs RHE – a potential that disfavors adsorption of any water-derived species on Pt under these acidic conditions. Hence, prior to the propane pulse at 150 s, the surface is relatively devoid of specific adsorbates, such as anions, H*, or OH* species. Consequently, the total number of Pt active sites relevant to the adsorption experiment can be determined via electrochemical active surface area calculation using HUPD (Figure S1, Supporting Information). This maximum current thus represents an equivalent measure of the initial rate of adsorption, where the concentration of vacant sites is a known constant value equal to the total electrochemically active sites. As propane adsorbs over time, the current continually decays. This is consistent with propane occupying available active sites and continually decreasing the rate of adsorption until the surface is saturated with adsorbate and the current returns to zero. Thus, the time-dependent current provides the time rate of change of Pt vacant site abundance. These measurements will be used to quantify kinetic parameters for propane adsorption on Pt.

The kinetic analysis above reveals that adsorption of propane on Pt in 1 M HClO4 at room temperature at 0.3 V vs RHE is first order with respect to propane concentration and second order with respect to vacant sites. The dependence on propane concentration is expected for adsorption at low coverage, which further validates the method of using the current transience as proportional to the adsorption rate. The second-order dependence on vacant sites is less obvious and contrasts with prior work citing a third-order dependence. These results indicate that two vacant sites are involved in the adsorption process. This could suggest that propane-derived adsorbate species occupy two sites, with the dissociated hydrogen not occupying a site (e.g., by concerted proton-coupled electron transfer). Or, more likely, since the experimental data are consistent with the two-step mechanism starting with non-electrochemical dissociative adsorption, the order dependence could reflect that H* and C3H7* each require one site for adsorption to occur. Although the H* oxidizes quickly upon adsorption under conditions studied here (hence the current transience), two adjacent sites would still be required to initiate the adsorption process. However, the saturation coverage achieved when adsorbing for long times is 0.05 propane/Ptatom (see Supporting Information Section 1.2), which suggests that propane-derived adsorbates may ultimately occupy more than one Pt site. This coverage is determined using active site characterization via HUPD, combined with a potentiodynamic ECMS methodology, in which an oxidizing potential sweep is applied after initial propane adsorption, converting all adsorbates to CO2, detected via mass spectrometer. Coverage is then calculated by assuming every propane adsorbate yields three CO2 molecules, and dividing the amount of propane by the active sites – detailed methodology is shown in our prior work. The low saturation coverage could be a result of only a subset of the Pt sites, like undercoordinated edges or corners, being active for adsorption, but such a determination is beyond the scope of this work. Obtaining Kinetic Parameters from Arrhenius and Eyring Analyses

Considering the non-electrochemical nature of the proposed rate-limiting step for propane adsorption, the activation barrier for this process will be an important kinetic parameter for further catalyst development. The established methodology of measuring adsorption rate with current transience can be utilized to estimate the apparent activation energy for this process using a typical Arrhenius analysis. Temperature-controlled experiments in a jacketed, standard, three-electrode cell were used to measure the initial rate of adsorption, equivalent to the peak current. Plotting the natural logarithm of these values as a function of inverse temperature yields the expected Arrhenius dependence (Figure a). , It is important to highlight that mass transport limitations were deemed negligible as control experiments at different rotation speeds (0–2000 rpm) had the same observed maximum current under identical adsorption conditions (Figure S9, Supporting Information).

The solubility of propane is also a function of temperature, which consequently influences the initial rate of adsorption. However, this change in reactant concentration is not expected to be a strong function of temperature in the range evaluated here, according to the Ostwald relationship (Figure S10, Supporting Information). Thus, the influence of concentration on adsorption rate was considered negligible compared to the exponential dependence of the rate with respect to temperature. In order to obtain an activation energy of adsorption, an Arrhenius relationship for potentiostatic experiments was developed to relate the maximum peak current to temperature (see Supporting Information Section 2.2). Consequently, a plot of the natural logarithm of maximum transient current as a function of inverse temperature can yield the activation barrier, via eq . The slope of this modified Arrhenius plot was used to determine an apparent activation energy equal to 35 kJ/mol for propane on Pt in 1 M HClO4 at an adsorption potential of 0.3 V vs RHE, Figure a. This value is well within the bounds for accessible barriers at room temperature and is consistent with density functional theory (DFT) calculations (see below). Using transition state theory, Arrhenius analysis can be extended to calculate the change in the apparent entropy and enthalpy from reactant to an activated transition state, via eq (see Supporting Information Section 2.2). − An Eyring plot (Figure b) yielded 33 kJ/mol and −165 J/mol/K as the apparent enthalpy and entropy of activation, respectively. A positive value for the apparent activation enthalpy (ΔH ‡) is expected for this adsorption process, where the adsorption rate increases as a function of higher reaction temperatures for the accessible temperature ranges tested. , Equivalence of activation of enthalpy and the apparent barrier calculated from Arrhenius analysis is expected for condensed phase reactions, and the values obtained are consistent within experimental error. A negative apparent activation entropy (ΔS ‡) indicates a loss in entropy, due to a decrease in degrees of freedom from aqueous propane to product-like transition state species. We note that the calculated transition state entropy in this analysis depends on the number of electron transfers per propane adsorbed. However, across the range of possible electron transfers (1 ≤ n ≤ 8), there is only a 1.2% change in the calculated entropy. Density functional theory calculations on Pt(111) and Pt(211) surfaces were performed to calculate the effective activation barrier for C–H bond dissociation in propane to form adsorbed 2-propyl, which is marginally more stable than the 1-propyl isomer. First, the kinetic barrier was calculated using the Climbing Image Nudge Elastic Band method and referenced to gaseous propane (see Methods and Materials). The analysis yielded barriers of 60 and 43 kJ/mol on Pt(111) and Pt(211), respectively. Next, an adjustment was made to the barriers to reflect both zero-point energy effects and the free energy of solvation of the dissolved propane species, as determined from the measured Henry’s Law coefficient. The transition state structure, containing an intact three-carbon backbone, strongly interacts with the Pt surface. These adsorbate species are nonpolar, uncharged, and hydrophobic and thus do not have a tightly bound solvation shell, thereby suggesting that perturbations to the electrochemical double-layer water structure will be relatively minor. Although the displacement of solvent molecules near the electrode due to adsorption at an active site has been shown to influence the adsorption energies and barriers, we expect this effect to be considerably smaller for our nonpolar, hydrophobic adsorbate as compared to a polar or hydrogen bonding adsorbate that interacts strongly with the electrolyte. Therefore, no solvation correction was applied to the transition state energies. It is not known whether the measured solvation free energy corresponds primarily to enthalpic or entropic effects; hence, we evaluate the corrections in both extremes, first assuming that the solvation correction only affects enthalpy and second assuming that it affects only the entropy. On the Pt(111) surface, in the former case, the corrected enthalpy of activation is found to be ∼30 kJ/mol, and the corresponding entropy of activation is −178 J/mol/K. In the latter case, activation enthalpy is ∼44 kJ/mol, and the activation entropy is −129 J/mol/K (see Supporting Information Section 2.3). On the Pt(211) surface, the activation enthalpies are shifted downward by 17 kJ/mol. The experimentally measured activation enthalpies and entropies are 33 kJ/mol and −165 J/mol/K, respectively. The measurement of enthalpy, in particular, is at the lower end of our calculated range on Pt(111) and near the upper end of the calculated range on Pt(211), suggesting that some combination of steps and terraces could be contributing to the measured kinetics. While our analysis involves Pt(211) and Pt(111) sites that show resemblance to the thermodynamics and kinetics of adsorption, determining the exact nature of the active sites on the platinized Pt surface is nontrivial. , Efforts to delineate the structure–function relationship of facet-dependent propane adsorption will play a critical role in electrocatalyst design strategies for this reaction and should be the subject of future investigation.

To further demonstrate the versatility of this methodology, we show that the measured activation energy changes in the presence of different electrolyte anions and at different adsorption potentials. While the perchlorate anions used for analysis in Figure should most closely align with DFT calculations because of the weak perchlorate interactions on Pt electrodes, it may be expected that the presence of other anions would deviate from this adsorption behavior (Figure c). , Table S4 shows that the presence of sulfate and phosphate anions does alter the apparent activation energy in similar experiments. Interestingly, the sulfate yields a lower value and the phosphate a higher value, compared to the perchlorate base case. Recent work by Lukatsaya and co-workers has delineated the role of a weakly solvated anion on electrochemical reactions, such as the CO2 reduction reactions. The authors have shown that the free energy barrier for anions to transport and physisorb at the polarized surface is small and accessible at room temperature. This suggests that competitive adsorption could be a dominant route via which the anions influence not only the availability of sites but also the energetics of adsorption of reactant species. Specific dependence of anion identity on interactions at the electrode surface in the context of the rate of propane adsorption on Pt, however, is beyond the scope of this work. But these measurements indicate that electrolyte engineering could be a plausible route toward improving the selectivity and yield of electrocatalytic alkane reactions. Thus, the methodology here can be employed to accurately quantify kinetic parameters as a function of electrolyte and electrocatalyst composition to aid in the rational design of electrochemical systems.

Conclusions

In this work, a methodology to extract kinetic parameters from an experimental transience signature is presented. Propane adsorption on Pt electrodes has been hypothesized to occur via a homolytic C–H bond scission, leading to the formation of propane-derived adsorption species along with H* adatom. The protonation of adsorbed H* leads to an oxidative current transience that, as we demonstrate, provides detailed insight into the adsorption kinetics; namely, the peak corresponds to the initial adsorption rate, while the decay yields information about consumption of active sites. Our developed kinetic analysis using this current transience is shown to validate adsorption kinetics and establish consistency with a two-step microkinetic adsorption model, offering key kinetic insights such as the number of active sites involved in the kinetically relevant step of propane adsorption and the apparent activation barrier for adsorption. Finally, we show consistency with first-principles DFT calculations and apply the methodology to different electrochemical conditions to show robustness and versatility of the procedure.

Methods and Materials

Electrode Preparation

Platinum metal disk inserts (5.0 mm outer diameter × 4.0 mm height, mirror-polished, Pine Research Instrumentation) were mechanically polished using 3 μm polycrystalline diamond suspension (MetaDi, Buehler). The disks were sonicated in Milli-Q water (≥18.2 MΩ cm, Thermo Scientific GenPure Pro) for 10 min and dried with pressurized N2 gas (99.999%, Indiana Oxygen). The disks were flame annealed for 3 min to achieve a surface temperature exceeding 660 °C, measured with a laser temperature probe. The disks were then platinized via electrodeposition at −5 mA/cm2 for 20 min in a 70 mM H2PtCl6 and 0.13 mM Pb­(C2H3O2)2 (both 99.9% trace metals basis, Sigma-Aldrich) electrolyte, using a Pt wire counter electrode. The resulting platinized Pt electrode (Figure S11, Supporting Information), designated as the working electrode (WE), served as the electrocatalyst in this study. Electrochemical active surface area (ECSA) was determined using hydrogen underpotential deposition (HUPD) and CO-stripping. ECSA calculated via the HUPD method was chosen for data normalization. All electrochemical measurements were conducted using a BioLogic potentiostat (VSP-300).

Electrolyte Preparation

Perchloric acid (70%, ACS reagent, Sigma-Aldrich) was diluted with Milli-Q water (≥18.2 MΩ cm, Thermo Scientific GenPure Pro) to prepare a 1 M HClO4 solution. This was used as the electrolyte for all experiments unless otherwise specified. The prepared electrolyte was purged with Ar gas (99.999%, Indiana Oxygen) for 10 min before use in the electrochemical setup.

Electrochemical Mass Spectrometer Cell Setup and Description

A Kel-F electrochemical cell (4 Luer-lock port, Spectro Inlets) was cleaned with piranha solution (3:1 ratio of H2SO4/H2O2, ACS grade, Fisher Chemical). Standard aqueous microchips (Spectro Inlets) were installed at the inlet to the mass spectrometer. A detailed description of the cell and the electrochemical mass spectrometer (ECMS) instrument has been provided elsewhere. , A “leakless” Ag/AgCl electrode reference electrode (calibrated to 0.201 V vs SHE, eDAQ) was used along with a Pt wire coil (Pine Research Instrumentation) as the counter electrode. To enhance stability, a 10 Ω resistor was installed in series with the working electrode and a 33 pF capacitor in parallel circuit to the reference (REF) and counter electrode (CE). Potentials were iR-corrected using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). Helium (99.9999%, Indiana Oxygen), Argon (99.9999%, Indiana Oxygen), and propane (99.999%, Indiana Oxygen) were used as carrier and/or purge gases during the electrochemical experiments. All experiments, unless otherwise stated, were performed at room temperature (21 ± 2 °C), ambient pressure of 1 atm (1.01 bar), and referenced to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE).

Temperature-Controlled Experiments

A rotating disk electrode setup (WaveVortex, Pine Instruments) was used with a platinized Pt electrode as the working electrode in 1 M HClO4 electrolyte in a 250 mL, 5-round glass port cell. A dual inlet accessory was used to introduce either He or 100% propane as pulse/purge gas. The cell was submerged in an oil bath, which in turn was kept on a hot plate (Fisherbrand Isotemp), and the temperature was regulated using a thermocouple. Temperatures of 20, 35, 45, and 65 °C were chosen.

Density Functional Theory Calculations

Periodic density functional theory (DFT) calculations are performed with the Vienna Ab-Initio Simulation Package (VASP), where the Kohn–Sham equations are solved self-consistently using the Bayesian error estimation functional with van der Waals correlation (BEEF-vdW). The core electrons are modeled using Projector Augmented Wave (PAW) pseudopotentials, and an energy cutoff of 400 eV is chosen for the planewave basis. A 4 × 4 × 1 K-point grid is set for all surfaces sampled using the Monkhorst–Pack method. The Methfessel-Paxton scheme is used with an energy smearing of 0.2 eV to determine the partial electron occupancies. Adsorbates are relaxed until the calculated forces are less than 0.02 eV/Å. These values are confirmed to converge the adsorption energies to within 0.05 eV. To model both Pt(111) and Pt(211) surfaces, a 3 × 3 × 5 unit cell is selected. The surface slabs are separated in the z-direction with a vacuum spacing of 20 Å. Spin polarization is not included in these calculations. Crystal structures are created and visualized using the atomic simulation environment (ASE) in Python. Activation energy barriers for bond breaking are calculated using the Climbing Image Nudged Elastic Band (CINEB) method.
