Post-COVID-19 disruption of the respiratory microbiome modulates Mycoplasma pneumoniae: a multi-center retrospective investigation study
Authors: Shiyu Huang, Shengkai Li, Ruike Zhao, Huajiang Yang, Yina Liu, Xinyi Wang, Jiaqi Zhang, Chen Shen, Feina Li, Hua Xu, Changyuan Guo, Xin Ni, Heng Li, Qing Cao, Zhemin Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSince the COVID-19 pandemic, there has been a notable resurgence of Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) in children, with a concerning rise in the severity of cases. Although changes in post-pandemic respiratory infection patterns have been documented, the reasons behind the increased severity of MPP, especially concerning shifts in the respiratory microbiota, are not well understood. This study aims to explore how pandemic-associated disruptions in respiratory microbiota contribute to MPP severity. Through analysis of multiple independent cohorts, we found that the depletion of protective respiratory microbiota exacerbates MPP severity by reducing colonization resistance against M. pneumoniae. We identified two antagonistic microbiota modules with distinct metabolic features and community structures that regulate pathogen colonization and disease severity. Using a Susceptible-Infected-Recovered (SIR) model, we simulated M. pneumoniae infection dynamics across different microbiota states. Additionally, a machine-learning model based on eight key microbes effectively distinguished between cases and controls and predicted infection severity, offering insights into post-pandemic respiratory infections. This study underscores the essential role of the respiratory microbiota in influencing the severity of M. pneumoniae infections. Our findings offer a framework for managing respiratory infections in the post-pandemic era, highlighting the significance of understanding microbial community dynamics in determining disease outcomes. These insights could guide the development of therapeutic strategies focused on restoring or enhancing the protective microbiota, which may offer an effective strategy for the management of MPP and other respiratory infections.KEYWORDS: Mycoplasma pneumoniae, paediatrics, respiratory infection, severity risk model, susceptible-infected-recovered model

Introduction

The global landscape of respiratory infectious diseases has undergone significant transformation following the COVID-19 pandemic, with a notable resurgence of Mycoplasma pneumoniae infections, particularly prominent in Europe and Asia [1–3]. This resurgence, characterized by increased disease severity and treatment resistance, represents a critical challenge to the public health systems [3]. Notably, the emergence of macrolide-resistant strains, with resistance rates exceeding 90% in some Asian regions [4–6], such as Suzhou [3], Beijing [7] and Shanghai [8], underscores the urgent need for innovative therapeutic approaches beyond conventional antimicrobial strategies.

Despite its minimal genome, M. pneumoniae manifests in clinical presentations ranging from mild respiratory symptoms to severe pneumonia with systemic complications [9]. Recent evidence suggests that disease severity correlates more strongly with host immune dysregulation and microbiome perturbation than with direct pathogen-mediated damage[10]. The mechanisms underlying colonization resistance conferred by healthy microbial communities have been extensively characterized in the gut ecosystem [11]. Analogous principles may extend to respiratory microbiomes, as suggested by prior studies [12,13]. Microbial dysbiosis can lead to immune system dysfunction [14] and enhance pathogen fitness [15]. For instance, depletion of the altered Schaedler flora (ASF) consortium in murine intestines results in MyD88-dependent innate immune dysfunction [16]. Comparative studies demonstrate that individuals with higher microbial diversity and enriched populations of Dorea and Coprococcus species exhibit significantly reduced resistance to Campylobacter jejuni infection when compared to those possessing lower microbial diversity and deficient in these specific bacterial taxa [17]. Previous studies also demonstrated that there were significant differences in respiratory microbial characteristics among patients infected with COVID-19 [18] and M. pneumoniae [19] with different disease severity. The study by Wang et al. revealed differences in respiratory bacterial abundance between severe and mild MPP cases, it lacked a systematic analysis of the comprehensive characteristics of the entire microbial community[1]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the delicate balance of respiratory microbiota was significantly disrupted by viral infections, therapeutic interventions, and non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs), potentially creating an environment conducive to opportunistic pathogens like M. pneumoniae [20].

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive longitudinal analysis of M. pneumoniae infections across five Chinese megacities from 2017 to 2023, covering pre-pandemic, pandemic, and post-pandemic periods. Through systematic analysis of respiratory samples from five independent cohorts, we identified two antagonistic microbiota modules with varying resistances against M. pneumoniae colonization. The protective low severity cluster (LSC) is composed of microbes regulating immune status through enhanced chorismite synthesis and tryptophan metabolism, forming more organized community structures that might suppress pathogen growth. By integrating these findings with a modified Susceptible-Infected-Recovered (SIR) model, we successfully mapped the dynamic interactions between protective microbiota and M. pneumoniae, demonstrating how community disruption leads to enhanced pathogen fitness. Our machine learning approach, based on eight key microbes, achieves over 97% accuracy in predicting disease severity across independent cohorts. Moreover, we observed a marked reduction in the frequency of protective LSC microbiota post-pandemic, with the 2023 LSC prevalence declining to 60% of 2019 levels, which correlates with increased disease severity. These findings establish a framework for understanding and managing respiratory infections in the post-pandemic era, where altered immune landscapes and disrupted microbiota present unprecedented challenges to public health and demand innovative therapeutic strategies focused on ecosystem restoration rather than pathogen elimination alone.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study integrates genomic data from five independent cohorts encompassing patients across different geographic regions and clinical settings. The analysis incorporates two publicly available datasets (Shenzhen and Wuhan cohorts) and three newly generated datasets (Beijing, Suzhou, and Shanghai cohorts). Each cohort provides distinct clinical and methodological contributions: (a) The Shenzhen cohort (sampled in 2016) includes specimens from both healthy controls and pneumonia patients, enabling comparative analyses between infected and uninfected populations. (b) The Wuhan cohort (sampled in 2021) contains bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) specimens exclusively from patients with severe or moderate MPP. (c) The Suzhou cohort (predominantly sampled in 2023) consists entirely of BALF specimens from patients with severe MPP infections and includes comprehensive hospitalization metrics. (d) The Beijing cohort (sampled from 2017 to 2023) incorporates diverse specimen types including oropharyngeal swabs, nasopharyngeal swabs, sputum, and BALF, along with hospitalization duration data, though it lacks formal severity stratification. (e) the Shanghai cohort (sampled from 2017 to 2023) features both sputum and BALF specimens accompanied by extensive clinical diagnostics and detailed patient metadata, providing the most comprehensive clinical characterization among all cohorts.

We designated the Shanghai cohort as the primary analytical cohort due to its superior clinical granularity, while employing other cohorts for validation purposes. Both the Shenzhen and Shanghai cohorts contain longitudinal specimens collected from multiple respiratory anatomical sites within the same patients. Comprehensive patient-specific metadata cross-referenced with corresponding respiratory sample characteristics are archived in Supplementary Data S1.

Clinical information

All patients with pneumonia were enrolled in the study after confirmed infection with M. pneumoniae by PCR pathogen detection, and the following screening was performed. Inclusion criteria: (1) fever, respiratory symptoms, or abnormal lung auscultation; (2) pneumonia confirmed by chest X-ray; (3) the positive detection of M. pneumoniae DNA in nasopharyngeal aspirates or lower respiratory tract specimens by real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and the positive detection of specific MP antibody in sera using a micro-particle agglutination test. Exclusion criteria: (1) bronchopulmonary dysplasia; (2) solid tumour or hematological malignancy; (3) congenital heart disease; (4) hereditary neurological disorders; (5) immune deficiency; (6) history of recent operation (<90 days); (7) metagenomic pathogen detection was unclear for M. pneumoniae.

According to the Chinese guidelines on CAP, severe MPP (SMPP) was defined as the presence of one or more of the following manifestations: (1) poor conditions; (2) consciousness disorder; (3) hypoxemia: marked increase in respiratory rate, assisted respiration, intermittent apnea or O2 saturation less than 92%; (4) persistent high fever for more than 5 days; (5) reluctance or inability to feed; (6) chest radiography: infiltration of 2/3 of one lung, multilobar infiltration, pleural effusion, pneumothorax, atelectasis, lung necrosis or lung abscesses; (7) extrapulmonary complications. All the other subjects were considered to have general MPP (GMPP). Based on the time period of the COVID-19 outbreak and the non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPI) taken by the Chinese government, such as isolation measures and online office, our study spanning years will be divided into three time periods: before pandemic (BP, 2019), during pandemic (DP, 2020–2022), and after pandemic (AP, 2023).

Clinical information was retrospectively collected from patients’ medical records. The clinical information included age, sex, total fever duration, length of hospital stay, polyinfection, hypoxemia, wheezes or dyspnea, extrapulmonary complications and antibiotic application data. Laboratory data included white blood cell (WBC) count, neutrophil count (NE), C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-2 (IL-2), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-17 (IL-17), serum ferritin (SF), alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), D-dimer, CD4+ lymphocyte count (CD4+ T), CD8+ lymphocyte count (CD8+ T) levels and the ratio of CD4+ T and CD8+ T (CD4+ T/CD8+ T). Imaging features included pulmonary consolidation, atelectasis, parapneumonic effusion, and severe imaging findings.

Pathogen detection

A multiplex PCR-based platform was utilized to detect M. pneumoniae and ten other pathogens including influenza virus, respiratory syncytial virus, adenovirus, parainfluenza virus, rhinovirus, coronavirus, and Chlamydia pneumoniae. The M. pneumoniae specific primers are: AACTATAACGGTCCTAAGGTAGCG and GCTCCTACCTATTCTCTACATGAT, targeting the 23S rRNA region. Evidence of bacterial co-infection in our study was demonstrated using blood cultures from sterile sites or induced sputum from non-sterile sites. For positive respiratory specimens, it was considered to be a bacterial co-infection if the clinician judged it clinically relevant and provided appropriate antibiotic treatment. Persistent fever or poor chest imaging performance following treatment with macrolide antibiotics indicated the need for Bronchoscopy, which was performed under intravenous-inhalation combination anaesthesia. Bronchoalveolar Lavage Fluid (BALF) samples were collected and stored at −80℃.

Multiplex PCR

Genomic DNA extraction Kit, QIAamp® DNA Mini Kit from Qiagen, Germany, Cat No51306; The PCR reagent (Ex Taq® Hot Start Version, Code No RR006A) and DL2000 DNA Marker (Code No 3427A) were purchased from TAKARA Company in Japan. Veriti® Thermal cyclist (Applied Biosystems, USA) Gel imager (Bio-Rad Company, USA) Vitek Fully automatic Bacterial Analyzer and API Identification System (Biomerieux, France). PCR (25 μL): F (10 μmol/L), R (10 μmol/L), DNA (10 μmol/L), TaKaRa Ex Taq HS (5U/μL), 0.2 μL, 10×Ex Taq Buffer (20 mM, Mg2+)2.5 μL, dNTP Mixture (20 mM) 2 μL and sterilized deionized water 16.3 uL. The PCR reaction conditions are as follows: 95℃ for 5 min of pre-denaturation; 95℃ for 20 s of denaturation, 55℃ for 20 s of annealing, 72℃ for 30 s of extension, for a total of 35 cycles; 72°C for 5 min. The synthesis of primers and sequencing were carried out by Beijing HeLu HuaDa Genomics Technology Co., Ltd.

Sample collection

NP/OP collection: (1) Tools: nasopharyngeal swab/oropharyngeal swab, alcohol, cotton swab, hand sanitizer, etc. (2) Disinfection: The samplers use hand sanitizer to disinfect their hands, keep them clean, and wear masks to prevent contamination. (3) Sample collection: (a) NP: The patients head was slightly tilted back, and wiped the inside and around the nostrils. The wiping depth should reach the nasal mucosa. (b) OP: Assisted by a tongue depressor, the posterior pharyngeal wall is fully exposed. The swab is then sent into the mouth to wipe the secretions from the palatine arches and tonsils on both sides. (4) Sample processing: After collection is completed, place the swab in the preservation solution and send it to the laboratory for processing.

Sputum collection: The sampler should wear a mask and protect their hands with sterile gloves. The patient should first rinse their mouth with clean water to remove debris and bacteria in the mouth and reduce contamination. After taking several deep breaths, patient should forcefully cough out the deep sputum to avoid mixing it with saliva or nasopharyngeal secretions. The sputum should be directly placed in a test tube or a sealed container and sent for examination.

BALF collection method: First, through the biopsy hole of the lavage lung segment, 1–2 ml of 2% lidocaine is injected via a thin silicone tube to perform local anaesthesia on the lavage lung segment; then, the tip of the bronchoscope is tightly wedged into the opening of the target bronchial segment or subsegment, and 37°C sterilized normal saline is rapidly injected through the biopsy hole via the silicone tube. The total volume of the saline is 100–250 ml (generally not more than 300 ml), divided into 3–5 portions, with each portion being 25–60 ml for perfusion. Immediately after perfusion, use 50–100 mmHg (1 mmHg = 0.133 kPa) negative pressure suction to recover the lavage fluid. Site selection: For localized lesions, the lesion segment is selected; for diffuse lesions, the right middle lobe or the upper lingular segment of the left lung is selected. The specimens are divided and placed in different sterile test tubes or clean test tubes according to the detection purpose. Specimens for pathogen analysis need to be collected in sterile containers. The specimen volume should be no less than 3 ml. Qualified BALF specimens: (a)Recovery rate > 40%; for lavage of other lung segments or lung lobes, the recovery rate > 30%. (b)No blood mixed in, red blood cells < 10%, epithelial cells < 5%.

Nucleic acid extraction

DNA was extracted from pretreated samples (BALF, NP, OP) from CAP patients and utilized for further DNA library construction. To prepare these samples, a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube containing 500 μL of sample and 1 g of 0.5 mm glass beads was vigorously agitated on a horizontal platform of a vortex mixer at 2800–3200 rpm for 30 minutes. Subsequently, 300 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube and combined with 0.2 ng of internal DNA control. DNA extraction followed using the TIANamp Micro DNA Kit (DP316, TIANGEN BIOTECH) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The extracted DNA was quantified using Qubit, and 100 ng of DNA was allocated for the subsequent library construction. If the DNA yield was less than 100 ng, the entire DNA extract from the sample was used for library construction.

Library preparation and sequencing

The DNA library was constructed through a process involving DNA fragmentation, end-repair, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification. The constructed library was then qualified using the Agilent 2100 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) and Qubit 2.0 (Invitrogen, USA), ensuring a concentration of at least 1 ng/μL. The qualified double-stranded DNA library was converted into a single-stranded circular DNA library through DNA denaturation and circularization. DNA nanoballs (DNBs) were produced from the single-stranded circular DNA using rolling circle amplification (RCA) and qualified with Qubit 2.0. The qualified DNBs were loaded onto the flow cell and sequenced on the MGISEQ-2000 platform (MGI, China). It was required that the sequencing yielded more than 15 million reads.

Taxonomic profile of the metagenomes

The bacterial compositions in metagenomes were analyzed using SPARSE with default parameters [21] based on a pre-curated reference database selected from the RefSeq database (June. 2024), by picking only one genome from each single-linkage cluster of < 0.01 genetic distance. Subsequently, the filtered reads were aligned to the human genome (GRCh19) with bowtie2 (2.3.4.3) to eliminate human sequences with default parameters [22]. The species data were normalized to Reads Per Kilobase per Million mapped reads (RPKM) before further analysis. We implemented HUMANN3 [23] software with default parameters to predict metabolic and functional profiles from raw sequencing data. The HUMANN-provided processing scripts were systematically applied for data transformation, filtration, and consolidation. Normalization generated percentage-abundance tables of metabolic pathways or functional gene compositions, which were subsequently used for downstream analyses.

Neutral community model

We employed the neutral community model (NCM) to evaluate the influence of stochastic processes on the assembly of microbial communities [24]. This model is an adaptation of the neutral theory [25] specifically designed for large microbial populations. According to the model, taxa that are abundant within the meta-community are more likely to be widely distributed, as they have a greater probability of random dispersal among various sampling sites. In contrast, rare taxa face a higher risk of loss across sites due to ecological drift, which entails the stochastic loss and replacement of individuals. The parameter R2 in this model serves as an indicator of how well the data fit the neutral model. We calculated fitting statistics with 97.5% confidence intervals using bootstrapping. Analyses were performed for single datasets of sputum samples collected from Shanghai.

To apply the neutral community model (NCM), we classified the genera within the datasets into three partitions based on their occurrence relative to the model's predictions: taxa that occurred more frequently than the upper limit of the 97.5% confidence interval (above partition), those that occurred less frequently than the lower limit (below partition), and those that fell within the confidence interval (neutral partition).

Construction of severity risk (SR) model

Random forest analysis was performed on the Shanghai cohort using the Python module “scikit-learn.” Briefly, samples were randomly split into training (80%) or test (20%) subsets. The model was trained on the training dataset, with hyperparameters optimized using 5-fold cross-validation using the GridSearchCV function. Final model performance was evaluated on the test set.

We evaluated the contribution of each genus on the classification using the permutation approach (permutation_importance function in scikit-learn), expressing the values as mean ± SEM. Based on these results, genera were ranked by importance. We then iteratively assessed model performance using increasing numbers of top-ranked genera (Figure S2(c)). Mode l accuracy plateaued when the top eight genera were included; these were used to construct the final SR model following the same training and evaluation procedures.

An SR score was defined as the predicted probability of a sample being classified into the HSC. To evaluate its clinical relevance, we constructed a correlation network between SR scores and clinical indicators using Pearson's correlation coefficient, confirming associations between SR scores and infection severity.

External validation was performed using four independent cohorts. In the Shenzhen cohort, one-way ANOVA was used to compare SR scores between healthy and diseased individuals. In the Wuhan cohort, SR scores were compared between GMPP and SMPP patients using one-way ANOVA. For the Suzhou and Beijing cohorts, Pearson correlation was used to assess the relationship between SR scores and total hospitalization duration. These analyses collectively support the association between respiratory microbiota composition and infection severity.

Construction of susceptible-infected-recovered (SIR) model

The SIR (Susceptible-Infected-Recovered) model is a classical framework in epidemiological transmission analysis, characterizing population distributions of susceptible (S), infected (I), and recovered (R) individuals. Drawing an analogy to microbial ecology, we propose that bacterial species within a microbial community may undergo a similar transition dynamic during pathogen invasion, with “susceptible” communities being vulnerable to colonization, “infected” states reflecting successful pathogen establishment, and “recovered” states corresponding to a return to a stable, pathogen-resistant configuration.

The bimodal distribution of M. pneumoniae abundance in the respiratory microbiome (Figure 1(d)) suggests the existence of two distinct community responses to invasion: strong resistance and weak resistance. This pattern implies that the underlying microbial community structure influences resistance capacity and pathogen occupancy outcomes.

In this study, we adapted the SIR model to simulate the ecological invasion process of M. pneumoniae within respiratory microbial communities. We defined a “community health status” metric, derived from the SR score, as a proxy for the community's resistance capacity. This metric was used to parameterize the invasion dynamics under the SIR model framework.

The model incorporates two primary parameters: the transmission rate (β), which reflects the likelihood of pathogen establishment within susceptible communities, and the recovery rate (γ), which represents the rate at which communities return to a pathogen-resistant state. We estimated these parameters for both high-severity (HSC) and low-severity (LSC) community groups by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of observed SR score transitions.

Simulations using identical initial conditions revealed divergent invasion trajectories between HSC and LSC groups, consistent with empirical data. These results support the applicability of the SIR model for capturing the dynamics of M. pneumoniae invasion in microbiome contexts and highlight the critical role of community structure in determining pathogen colonization outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Quantitatively normal or nearly normal data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using the t-test. Quantitative skewness data were presented as the median (interquartile range, IQR) and analyzed using the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney rank-sum test. Categorical data are expressed as frequencies and examined using the chi-square test. Univariate analysis was performed to identify the differences between patients in the GMPP group and SMPP group, the differences between patients with older children (ages 6–18) and younger children(ages 0–6), and the differences between before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Logistic regression analysis was performed to select the variables associated with SMPP. We used the binary Logistic regression method to study the influencing factors of the severity of MPP while excluding the interference of confounding factors such as antibiotic use, and introduced other factors (such as gender, age, year, etc.) for correction. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All clinical data analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0).

Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to evaluate microbial diversity and microbial abundance between the two groups. Spearman correlation coefficient or multiple regression logic analysis was used to test the correlation between microorganisms and clinical features. Alpha-diversity indexes were calculated using the R package vegan (2.6–8). The metabolic enrichment analysis was also performed using the R package clusterProfiler (v4.12.6) [26]. Standardized mean difference (SMD) was used to evaluate the distinction in relative abundance between two groups [27]. Percentage pile bar charts and PCoA plots were conducted using the online web tool [28]. All box plots, and volcano maps were created with the ggplot2 [29] package in R v4.4.1.

Results

Clinical information of cohorts from five Chinese megacities

We collected 303 respiratory samples from 297 patients with M. pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) across three independent cohorts from Shanghai (n = 146), Beijing (n = 75), and Suzhou (n = 82) between 2019 and 2023 (Supplementary Data S1). Additionally, we included 380 samples from two publically available cohorts from Wuhan (n = 46) and Shenzhen (n = 334), resulting in a total of 683 samples from 590 individuals between 2016 and 2023 (Figure 1(a)). The final dataset comprised 307 males and 283 females, with a median age of 4.9 years, classified into 171 healthy controls and 419 MPP patients, including 182 severe MPP (SMPP), 158 general MPP (GMPP), and the rest 250 patients without clinical information.

Detail clinical information was obtained from the Shanghai cohort (Supplementary Data S1), which indicated that SMPP patients (n = 29) had significantly longer hospital stays (P < 0.001) and elevated D-dimer levels (P = 0.002) compared to GMPP (n = 111) (Table S1). SMPP patients also exhibited a higher prevalence of respiratory complications – such as hypoxemia (P < 0.001), pulmonary consolidation (P < 0.001), atelectasis (P = 0.02), parapneumonic effusion (P < 0.001), and extrapulmonary complications (P < 0.001) – alongside poorer immune status, characterized by increased WBC counts, IL-2, and IL-17 levels, and decreased CD4+ and CD8+ T cell counts (all P < 0.05). Patients older than six years also had a worse immune response, which was linked to their severe symptoms according to the clinical data documented (Table S2).

Increased M. pneumoniae levels in patient populations with severe pneumonia infections

The metagenomic analysis of the Shanghai cohort further revealed that GMPP patients exhibited significantly higher microbiome diversity compared to SMPP patients, as indicated by Simpson diversity indices of 0.20 ± 0.32 for SMPP and 0.36 ± 0.37 for GMPP (P < 0.01, Figure S1(a)). Differentiate analysis demonstrated distinct microbial compositions between the two groups (P = 0.037, Figure S1(b)). While GMPP samples were enriched in species such as Leptotrichia sp., Capnocytophaga gingivalis, and Lachnoanaerobaculum orale, SMPP samples were dominated by M. pneumoniae (Figure 1(b) and Figure S1(c)), indicating the significant association between the level of M. pneumoniae and the severity of the infections. Furthermore, higher levels of M. pneumoniae are also significantly associated with a higher prevalence of respiratory complications and lower immune status of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Figure 1(c)).

Notably, we revealed a bimodal distribution of M. pneumoniae abundance in both SMPP and GMPP samples, with 73% of samples at either low (<10%) or high (>90%) levels and only a few in between (Figure 1(d)). A similar bimodal distribution was also observed in the other four cohorts of Beijing, Shenzhen, Suzhou, and Wuhan (Figure S1(d)). Furthermore, M. pneumoniae abundance was found to be negatively correlated to the alpha diversity of the samples (P < 0.001, Figure S1(e)). However, the trend disappeared if we excluded M. pneumoniae from the calculation (P > 0.1, Figure S1(f)), suggesting the presence of diverse respiratory microbes that might interact with M. pneumoniae during the infection.

Role of protective microbiota clusters in guarding against SMPP and GMPP

Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) of functional and pathway compositions categorized the samples into two distinct clusters: the Low Severity Cluster (LSC) and the High Severity Cluster (HSC) (Figure 2(a,b)). These clusters exhibited significant differences in the incidence of SMPP (P = 0.02, Figure 2(c)) and the levels of M. pneumoniae (P < 0.001, Figure S2(a)). Notably, HSCs exhibited higher concentrations of bacterial species from Pseudomonadota (53%), including opportunistic pathogens like Moraxella A, whereas LSCs displayed greater diversity, characterized by an abundance of Streptococcus (31%) and Prevotella (5%) (Figure S2(b)). Gene family composition analysis revealed that HSCs had 314 upregulated families associated with aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, carbon metabolism, and nucleotide metabolism (Figure 2(d)). Detailed investigation of associated pathways indicated enrichment of genes involved in phosphatidic acid synthesis, which mediates lung inflammatory injury through various signalling pathways [30], as well as in the glycolysis pathway, likely associated with reduced oxygen levels during SMPP. In contrast, LSCs exhibited 2058 families related to the biosynthesis of cofactors and amino acids, as well as carbon metabolism (Figure 2(e)). An enrichment of chorismite and tryptophan synthesis was found in the LSC, both of which are associated with immune regulation [31]. Additionally, LSCs contained significantly more genes related to peptidoglycan synthesis, which is known to regulate innate immunity [32].

Severity risk (SR) prediction model identified key bacterial genera in antagonistic modules

The Neutral Community Model (NCM) evaluates the structural assemblies of the species in a community by assuming that species have equal probabilities of birth and death. We used the NCM model to assess the assembly randomness of microbial communities in LSC and HSC samples (Figure 3(a)). The HSC (R² = 0.394) demonstrated a better fit to the NCM compared to the LSC (R² = 0.121), indicating more stochastic processes in HSCs, where approximately 75% of species were randomly assembled. Conversely, fewer species in LSC were randomly assembled, suggesting selective pressures shaped the microbial structure. This finding implies that a stable respiratory microbiome in LSC patients may be crucial for developing GMPP.

Using correlation network analysis, we identified two antagonistic modules of core bacterial genera (≥10% presence & ≥ 0.1% relative abundance) associated with the sample clusters (Figure 3(b)). The LSC module was enriched in Bacteroidetes and Bacillota, particularly Streptococcus and Prevotella, while the HSC module was dominated by Proteobacteria, including opportunistic pathogens such as Moraxella and Ralstonia (Figure 3(b)). We then employed a random forest method to select eight key genera for a severity risk (SR) prediction model (Figure 3(c)), which differentiated HSC from LSC with an accuracy of 0.97 (Figure S2(c)) and an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.91 (Figure S2(d)). The most critical genus for prediction was Streptococcus (importance = 0.18), followed by Abiotrophia, Haemophilus, and others. Notably, five of the total eight genera belonged to the phylum Bacillota (Figure 3(c)), underscoring its importance to respiratory health.

We defined the SR score as the predicted probability of a sample belonging to the HSC and integrated it with clinical indicators and microbial data to construct a correlation network (Figure 3(d)). This network was divided into two major modules: one associated with severe disease factors such as C-reactive protein (CRP), D-dimer levels, and lung consolidation (left circle in Figure 3(d)), and another module linked to immune response factors, including CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, Simpson diversity, and Bacillota richness (right circle in Figure 3(d)). The SR score positively correlated with factors in the severity-associated module (red lines) and negatively with indicators in the immunity-associated module (green lines), reflecting its relationship with clinical status. Mediation analysis further revealed significant causal links, demonstrating that variations in SR scores led to decreased CD4+ T cell levels (P = 0.02) and higher hyperpyrexia (P < 0.001) through increased levels of M. pneumoniae levels (Figures 3(e) and S2(e)).

Susceptible-infected-recovered (SIR) model simulates the dynamics of infection

The bimodal distribution of M. pneumoniae in respiratory tracts indicated that specific respiratory microbiome compositions can protect against its colonization. Therefore, we utilised the Susceptible-Infected-Recovered (SIR) model to simulate M. pneumoniae invasion dynamics within the microbiome (Figure 4(a)). Assuming infection rates (β) and uniform recovery rates (γ) across microbiomes, we would expect an unimodal distribution of M. pneumoniae; however, our findings significantly deviated from this expectation. By incorporating variable recovery rates based on SR scores, we observed that HSC microbiomes exhibited slower recovery rates, allowing M. pneumoniae to dominate nearly 100% of the microbial community. In contrast, LSC microbiomes demonstrated faster recovery, effectively limiting M. pneumoniae levels (Figure 4(b)). These simulated dynamics align closely with our empirical data, suggesting that LSC-associated microbes provide a natural defense against M. pneumoniae invasion, preventing pathogen overgrowth and severe disease.

Track dynamics of M. pneumoniae infections during the COVID-19 era in Shanghai cohort

Previously, we observed a resurgence of Mycoplasma pneumoniae infections post-COVID-19 pandemic, with positive case rates rising to 50% since July 2023, a stark increase from the 10–20% incidence during the pandemic [3]. This prompted us to categorize patient data into three periods: pre-pandemic, pandemic (2020–2022), and post-pandemic (after 2023), and collect a substantial number of samples for analysis.

Post-pandemic patients exhibited significantly elevated IL-2 and IL-17 levels, indicative of a heightened inflammatory response, alongside lower LDH levels and diminished CD4+ and CD8+ T cell counts, suggesting an impaired immune system often linked with chronic infections (Table S3). Furthermore, there was an increased incidence of co-infections with respiratory viruses in post-pandemic patients, highlighting ongoing immune dysfunction. Additionally, we observed a continuous decline in LSC frequencies with time (Figure 4(c)). The relative level of LSC in 2023 was only 60% of it in 2019. This indicated an increase in susceptibility after the COVID-19 pandemic, possibly correlating with the rise of M. pneumoniae cases in China.

Model validation with respiratory metagenomic datasets from other megacities cohorts

We evaluated the performance of the SR model across all cohorts. The Shenzhen cohort included samples from both healthy controls (n = 171) and MPP patients (n = 163). Within the Shenzhen dataset, it was observed that the SR scores in healthy individuals were significantly lower in pneumonia patients (P < 0.001, Figure 4(d)). The Wuhan cohort, comprising GMPP (n = 18) and SMPP (n = 28) patients, also showed a significant lower SR scores in GMPP samples (P = 0.01, Figure 4(e)). This indicates that certain microbiome confers a protective effect in maintaining health by mitigating M. pneumoniae invasion, and respirotary dysbiosis is associated with the emergence of severe clinical infection by M. pneumoniae.

Furthermore, we found positive associations between the SR scores and the duration of hospitalization for patients in both Suzhou (R² = 0.78) and Beijing (R² = 0.79) cohorts (Figure 4(f,g)). The SR scores’ correlation with longer hospital stays supports their utility in predicting hospitalization duration, indicating that our model could help forecast the clinical prognosis of M. pneumoniae infections.

Impact of demographic factors

We consolidated data from five independent cohorts to analyze the impact of demographic factors (age, sex) on the aforementioned findings while further validating the reliability of the SR model. First, we consistently observed the bimodal distribution pattern of M. pneumoniae across different age and sex subgroups (Figure 5(a,c)). Similarly, linear correlations between M. pneumoniae relative abundance and species diversity were identified in both stratification groups (Figure 5(b,d)). These observations confirm the robustness of initial findings independent of demographic influences. Subsequently, comparing SR scores between SMPP and GMPP samples within age and sex subgroups revealed that the significantly higher SR scores in SMPP samples remained unaffected by age or sex factors (Figure 5(e,f)). This further corroborates the robustness of the SR model, demonstrating that the respiratory microbiome indeed influences disease severity in paediatric MPP infections.

Furthermore, we validated the species diversity conclusions between SMPP and GMPP samples using the integrated dataset. The results demonstrate consistent alignment with prior findings: after combining datasets from Shanghai, Suzhou, and Wuhan with clinical severity diagnostic information, SMPP samples continued to exhibit significantly lower alpha diversity compared to GMPP samples (Figure S2(f)). Additionally, integrated dataset analysis confirms significantly higher SR scores in SMPP relative to GMPP (Figure S2(g)), reinforcing the robustness of our conclusions. These provide additional confirmation of the association between microbial diversity and infection severity.

Discussion

Following the COVID-19 pandemic, consecutive outbreaks of respiratory infections were reported globally, with each linked to distinct regions and time frames [33,34]. The resurgence of MPP in 2023 in China, as part of the global re-emergence of M. pneumoniae, raised significant concern due to its association with severe cases in paediatric populations. The incidence of SMPP is closely linked to an excessive or dysregulated immune response, and emerging evidence highlights the critical role of the airway microbiome in modulating disease severity [35,36].

Our study employed a multidisciplinary approach, integrating clinical, epidemiological, and metagenomic data, to investigate the interactions between M. pneumoniae, the airway microbiome, and host immune responses over the past eight years. Notably, M. pneumoniae was significantly more abundant in samples from patients with severe respiratory symptoms, emphasizing its pathogenic role in SMPP. In line with previous studies, we observed that neutrophil activation, evidenced by elevated peripheral blood neutrophil levels, was significantly higher in the SMPP group [37,38]. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) have been shown to exacerbate tissue damage in the lungs by driving excessive inflammation [39–41]. Furthermore, cytokines such as IL-17, known to recruit and activate neutrophils, were persistently elevated in severe cases, correlating with mucus hypersecretion [42–44], airway remodelling, and lung injury [45]. These findings reinforce the notion that immune overactivation, driven by both microbial and host factors, plays a crucial role in determining the severity of MPP. The differentiated enrichment of metabolic genes in the HSCs and LSCs likely contributed to the process.

Otherwise, our findings indicate that critically ill patients exhibit elevated levels of white blood cells and inflammatory factors alongside reduced immune cell counts; however, a more detailed immune function profile remains to be explored. For instance, Lian et al. demonstrated that distinct macrophage subtypes significantly influence SARS-CoV-2 infection severity [46]. Additionally, single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealed lower neutrophil levels in severely infected tissues [47], contrasting with peripheral blood observations in this study. Thus, the immune and inflammatory mechanisms of SMPP warrant further investigation. Generally, young children possess weaker immune systems and are more susceptible to pneumonia infections [48]. However, this study observed lower inflammatory factor levels, higher immune cell counts, and milder clinical symptoms in young MPP patients. Consequently, further research is needed to validate the impact of age factors on M. pneumoniae infection and elucidate the underlying mechanisms of this effect.

A significant finding of this study is the presence of two distinct bacterial communities associated with different clinical outcomes: a protective bacterial community in the LSC and a disease-associated community in the HSC. The LSC exhibited a higher diversity of bacterial species, particularly with a notable presence of Streptococcus and Prevotella. These genera are known to play roles in maintaining mucosal immunity and modulating inflammatory responses [49]. For instance, nasopharyngeal colonization by Streptococcus pneumoniae enhances alveolar macrophage phagocytic capacity [50], while Streptococcus salivarius secretes bacteriocins to inhibit Gram-positive pathogens [51].While inflammation may not prevent colonization by specific pathogens [52], Prevotella has been shown to amplify TLR2-dependent neutrophil activation-mediated inflammatory responses and impede pulmonary colonization by Streptococcus pneumoniae [53]. Notably, pneumococcal lipoproteins also activate TLR2, contributing to early-phase inflammatory responses in pneumonia [54]. The enrichment of metabolic pathways related to amino acid biosynthesis, especially tryptophan, in the LSC is particularly intriguing. Tryptophan is a precursor for several metabolites that can influence immune regulation, such as kynurenine, which has been implicated in modulating T-cell responses and promoting anti-inflammatory effects [55]. Similarly, LSCs had significantly more genes associated with peptidoglycan synthesis. Peptidoglycan is crucial for maintaining bacterial cell wall integrity but also plays an important role in modulating host immune responses [56]. It has been associated with the regulation of innate immunity by enhancing the recognition of pathogens through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), which can lead to a more balanced immune response [57]. This suggests that the metabolic profile of the LSC could be protective against severe inflammation and tissue damage associated with MPP.

Conversely, the HSC was characterized by an overrepresentation of opportunistic pathogens from the Proteobacteria phylum, including Moraxella, which have been associated with exacerbated inflammatory responses [58]. The upregulation of gene families linked to aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis and pathways such as glycolysis in HSC indicates a shift towards a metabolic state that favours inflammation and tissue injury. Notably, the synthesis of phosphatidic acid observed in HSCs is critical for mediating lung inflammatory injury through various signalling pathways. Phosphatidic acid (PA) has been shown to enhance cytokine expression and promote neutrophil chemotaxis, thereby exacerbating lung injury during inflammatory responses [59]. This pathway's activation may contribute to increased vascular permeability and further inflammation, creating a vicious cycle that aggravates lung damage.

Our identification of the two antagonistic bacterial modules suggested that the presence of a balanced and diverse microbial community, as seen in the LSC, may serve as a protective factor against severe respiratory illness, while disruption in this balance predisposes the host to more severe disease, as seen in the HSC. Similar antagonistic bacterial modules have been found in other body sites, such as guts [60] and vagina [61], indicating the wide presence of a healthy core microbiome. We employed the SIR model which is widely applied in virus epidemiological surveillance to investigate the microbiome dynamics during MP invasion, revealing the presence of two distinct invasion patterns. In some cases, the microbiome lacked any clear protective function, allowing MP to rapidly dominate the microbial community, resulting in MP accounting for nearly all bacterial cells present after infection. This dynamic corresponds to the HSC, where reduced diversity and the presence of disease-associated microbes create an environment conducive to severe infection [62].

In contrast, other microbiomes were able to prevent MP from rapidly invading, with MP accounting for only a small proportion of the bacterial population even after infection. This pattern, observed in the LSC, suggests that a diverse microbial community can play a protective role by limiting pathogen expansion, possibly through competitive exclusion, immune modulation, or maintaining epithelial barrier integrity [63]. This concept aligns with earlier reports that protective microbial species can prevent the dominance of respiratory pathogens by promoting immune homeostasis and limiting inflammation [64]. Although traditionally used in epidemiological modelling, we adapted the SIR model heuristically to simulate the invasion of M. pneumoniae into respiratory microbiomes. While this ecological analogy helps illustrate community-level susceptibility and resilience, further validation in microbial ecology contexts is necessary before broader generalization.

The COVID-19 pandemic has had profound effects on the composition and function of the human respiratory microbiome, which may have long-lasting implications for global health [65]. Similar patterns of microbial imbalance have been reported in other post-viral respiratory infections, where the loss of beneficial commensal species creates a favourable environment for pathogen overgrowth [66]. Moreover, widespread antibiotic use during and after COVID-19 [67] and non-pharmaceutical interventions may have altered respiratory microbiome composition, potentially confounding associations between microbiota and disease severity. Early investigations reported a stable MPP severity post-pandemic, differing from our cohorts, which may reflect differences in cohort selection, healthcare practices, or regional antibiotic usage patterns [68]. Our study showed significant alterations in the respiratory microbiome post-COVID-19, with a decreased level of LSC, suggesting that the pandemic-induced disruptions in the respiratory microbiome may have facilitated the resurgence of M. pneumoniae infections, as well as other opportunistic pathogens.

Bacterial substances in the lungs are associated with cytokines and pulmonary inflammatory markers (such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β) in the activation of inflammatory vesicles. For instance, in severe COVID-19 patients, these markers are related to the development of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [69]. Based on the current research results, we speculate that post-COVID-19 the use of antibiotics and NPIs may have led to the imbalance of the microbial community, upregulating inflammatory signals or producing interfering cytokines. These factors highlight the complexity of the ecological environment in which our study is conducted and underscore the need for a more controlled setting to isolate the effects of microbiota on disease severity, but the causal relationship between its dysregulation and disease onset remains to be explored.

Our study also revealed that the post-COVID-19 immune landscape may contribute to increased susceptibility to severe infections. We observed a higher proportion of SMPP cases and viral co-infections post-pandemic, suggesting that immune dysfunction and microbiome disruption following COVID-19 may exacerbate the clinical course of M. pneumoniae infection. Future studies should explore how long-term immune alterations and microbiome shifts from viral epidemics may predispose patients to more severe bacterial infections.

These findings have broad implications for global health, particularly in the context of infection susceptibility and disease severity in the post-pandemic era. The altered microbiome could make populations more vulnerable to secondary bacterial infections and potentially lead to more severe disease outcomes, especially in high-risk groups such as children and the elderly. Additionally, the observed microbiome shifts raise concerns about the long-term effects of COVID-19 on respiratory health, which may increase the burden of respiratory diseases globally [70]. Addressing these microbiome disruptions through public health strategies, such as microbiome-targeted therapies or interventions aimed at restoring microbial balance, could be critical in mitigating the risk of severe infections and improving resilience against future pandemics.

Virus-bacterial interactions play an important role in respiratory tract infections, especially in M. pneumoniae infections. This interaction may exacerbate the severity of the disease through multiple mechanisms. Among children or patients with weakened immune systems, the co-infection rate of M. pneumoniae and respiratory viruses (such as RSV and adenovirus) is relatively high, and patients with co-infection are more prone to severe pneumonia, aerobic therapy or prolonged hospital stay. Viruses can damage ciliary epithelial cells or increase mucus secretion, reducing the clearance ability of the respiratory tract. Meanwhile, viruses can induce excessive inflammation (such as elevated IL-6 and TNF-α), causing damage to the physical barrier of the respiratory tract, thereby making it easier for M. pneumoniae to attach to the surface of the airway [71]. In addition, respiratory viruses (such as respiratory syncytial virus and rhinovirus) can inhibit the host's innate immunity (such as interferon response), weaken the bactericidal function of macrophages and neutrophils, and create conditions for the colonization of M. pneumoniae [72]. M. pneumoniae infection can induce Th17/Treg imbalance, promote the release of inflammatory factors (IL-6, TNF-α), and aggravate lung injury. The virus may enhance the pathogenicity of M. pneumoniae through similar pathways (such as NF-κB activation). In conclusion, the interaction between the virus and MP is complex. To make co-infection susceptible, many components are involved, including M. pneumoniae, the virus, and the host's immune system. However, there are still many unsolved mysteries in this process. Future research needs to combine multi-omics techniques (such as genomics, metabolomics and proteomics) to deeply analyze the complexity of virus-bacterial interactions. In addition, clinical trials should also focus on the impact of virus coexistence on the prognosis and therapeutic effect of patients with M. pneumoniae infection, in order to provide a theoretical basis for precision medicine.

This study has several limitations. The retrospective nature of the research, coupled with concurrently collected data (lacking truly longitudinal data), restricts our ability to capture the dynamic evolution of infections. Additionally, constraints in specific sample sizes (e.g. small sample sizes for viral co-infections, host demographic factors, and complex disease states) undermine the robustness of the experimental conclusions. Although five major cities were included, our findings may not fully represent the paediatric population in China. Moreover, the results still lack reliable experimental validation, necessitating caution when generalizing these outcomes. To minimize geographical specificities (such as medical practices) and batch effects, we confined metagenomic analysis and model training to the Shanghai cohort and used the other four cohorts for external validation. However, while this validation approach preserves regional specificity, it reduces the generalizability of the experimental conclusions and may limit the extrapolation of our findings to broader regions and populations. Given the high prevalence of macrolide-resistant Mycoplasma pneumoniae in China, our results may not be applicable to regions with different resistance rates and microbe-immune interactions (such as Western cohorts with low resistance rates).

Additionally, future prospective, large-scale, longitudinal, multicenter studies are essential to validate our findings and understand the causal relationships among microbial composition, immune responses, and clinical outcomes of MPP. Additionally, research on diverse populations with varying antibiotic resistance patterns is needed to assess the generalizability of these findings. The application of microbiome analysis in clinical diagnostics faces challenges such as high costs, lengthy processing times, lack of standardization, and sample handling issues. Although technological advancements are rapid, these problems still require incremental resolution through innovation and collaboration to realize the practical clinical implementation of microbiome technologies.

In conclusion, our study provides important insights into the interactions between M. pneumoniae, the respiratory microbiome, and host immune responses in paediatric patients with community-acquired pneumonia, particularly in the post-COVID-19 era. We identified distinct bacterial communities associated with different clinical outcomes, highlighting the protective role of a diverse microbiome in preventing severe infections. The application of the SIR model offered a novel approach to understanding how microbial composition influences pathogen invasion dynamics, with implications for future microbiome-targeted interventions. Additionally, the post-pandemic alteration of the respiratory microbiome and its potential to exacerbate infections raises important public health concerns regarding increased susceptibility to severe diseases. Moving forward, larger multi-center studies and microbiome-based therapeutic strategies will be essential in mitigating the impact of respiratory infections like M. pneumoniae and enhancing global respiratory health resilience.
