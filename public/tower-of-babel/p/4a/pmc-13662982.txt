Effects of cinema-based education and media representations in the psychiatry rotation of fifth-year medical students
Authors: Özgür Velioğlu Metin, Aila Gareayaghi, Mert Gürer, İren Dicle Aytaç, Aslıhan Polat, Nigar Pösteki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to examine the pedagogical effects of cinema-based learning and media representations during the psychiatry clerkship through the experiences of fifth-year medical students.MethodsDesigned as a qualitative descriptive study, the research was conducted at the Kocaeli University Faculty of Medicine during the 2024–2025 academic year. Following the screening of the film Deli Bal—which centers on bipolar disorder—as part of the clerkship curriculum, semi-structured interviews were conducted with the students. The collected qualitative data were evaluated using reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsFindings reveal that cinema-based education concretizes abstract psychopathological concepts, effectively translating theoretical knowledge into clinical visibility while enhancing students’ diagnostic reasoning and empathic capacities. Furthermore, students demonstrated stigma literacy by critically evaluating the discrepancies between cinematic dramatizations and clinical reality. They exhibited heightened sensitivity particularly toward the depiction of suicide and the inadequate representation of treatment processes. The analysis also highlighted a clear student demand for multimodal learning environments over traditional didactic instruction to combat “text fatigue.”ConclusionWhen embedded within structured guidance and debriefing sessions, cinema-based education serves as a highly effective pedagogical tool in psychiatry training. It not only strengthens clinical reasoning and combats stigma but also fosters professional identity formation among medical students.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09972-4.Keywords: Psychiatric education, Undergraduate, Psychiatry clerkship, Stigmatization, Cinema-based education

Introduction

Over the past years, cinema and television based instructional approaches in medical education have gained increasing prominence. However, the evidence base remains limited, particularly with respect to objective outcomes such as clinical reasoning and diagnostic performance among clinical-phase medical students undertaking psychiatry clerkships. Existing systematic reviews indicate that film and television are most frequently utilized in domains such as professionalism, ethics, and critical thinking. These approaches are predominantly implemented with preclinical students and are typically accompanied by structured discussion or debriefing sessions [1].

Within psychiatry, film or clip-based approaches hold the potential to concretize the observational components of psychopathology and the mental status examination (MSE), enhance student engagement and acceptability, and, in some studies, improve performance on knowledge-based assessments. However, the generalizability of these findings is limited, as a proportion of the outcome measures rely on self-reported data and non-validated instruments [2]. Media representations have been associated with stigmatizing and anti-psychiatry narratives concerning psychiatric disorders and the psychiatric profession. Such portrayals may indirectly shape students’ expectations regarding both patients and psychiatric treatments. Accordingly, educational design should prioritize explicit myth-busting strategies, the clear articulation of contemporary standards of care, and the establishment of a psychologically safe framework for discussion [3]. One of the most robust bodies of evidence concerning treatment representations pertains to the portrayal of suicide in the media (i.e., the Werther and Papageno effects) and the negative depictions of electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) in cinema. Films have been explicitly reported as a source of students’ attitudes toward ECT, and there is evidence suggesting that exposure to certain film scenes may reduce support for its use. Accordingly, the integration of film into psychiatry rotation should not follow a passive watch-and-leave format. Rather, it should be embedded within a structured sequence comprising pre-viewing framing, guided viewing, evidence-based debriefing, and formal evaluation [4]. One of the most recent systematic reviews on the use of cinema and television in medical education examined 17 studies published between 1983 and 2023. The review reported that film and television are predominantly employed as complementary educational tools; the most frequently targeted domains include professionalism, bioethics, and critical thinking; participants are largely first and second year medical students; and sessions typically center on a viewing + discussion format. While these findings support the feasibility of integrating cinema-based approaches into medical school curriculum, they also underscore the need for more targeted outcome measures in the clinical phase, particularly within psychiatry rotations, such as performance in conducting the MSE, case formulation, and risk assessment [1].

In psychiatry, clinical reasoning extends beyond the mere matching of symptoms to DSM criteria; it encompasses the contextual interpretation of symptom clusters, the generation of differential diagnoses, risk assessment, and the development of a biopsychosocial formulation. In this respect, films and selected clips offer a distinct advantage by providing rich, case-like stimuli that can approximate the complexity of clinical encounters. Nevertheless, a film character is not a real patient. Accordingly, the distinction between the represented symptoms and the heterogeneity and variability inherent in real-world clinical presentations should be made explicit within the educational process.

Among the empirical evidence, a relatively large, randomized study examining the use of film character analysis in teaching psychopathology is particularly noteworthy. Conducted among penultimate-year medical students in China, seminars structured around psychotic symptoms illustrated through film characters were associated, compared with traditional seminars, with higher levels of interest, improved scores on measures of understanding/acceptance, and superior performance on written knowledge examinations. The study’s strengths include randomization and the use of an objective written assessment. However, its limitations involve reliance on non-validated self-report measures for certain primary outcomes and the absence of long-term follow-up data [2]. Similarly, small-sample studies examining medical students’ perceptions of learning the MSE through film clips have reported that clips enhance attention, allow exposure to diverse clinical presentations within a limited time frame, and are perceived as helpful in visualizing specific components of the MSE. However, these studies also note that certain domains, such as insight, may be difficult to assess through brief clips, and that film-based materials cannot substitute for direct interaction with real patients [5].

In the domain of professional identity formation (PIF), pedagogical frameworks proposed to support medical students emphasize several key steps: facilitating lived experience, observing emotional and cognitive responses, externalizing reflection, sharing with peers, broadening perspectives, and engaging in iterative “trying on” of professional roles. These steps align closely with the nature of cinema-based sessions and may provide a particularly suitable foundation for identity work during psychiatry clerkships, especially when structured around affectively evocative clinical and ethical dilemmas [6].

One of the most detailed examinations of the learning mechanisms underlying cinema-based instruction is a mixed-methods study evaluating a structured “movie night + multi-stakeholder discussion” format. The study demonstrated that learning occurred through reflective thinking, perspective-taking, and engagement with emotionally salient narratives, and that it evolved across five temporal phases: preparation, viewing, discussion, subsequent reflection, and transfer. Such a model provides a direct framework for designing film-based activities within psychiatry clerkships, shifting the approach from ad hoc film viewing toward a phase-oriented, pedagogically grounded learning process [7].

Against this background, the present study aims, from the learners’ own perspective, to characterise how a structured cinema-based session functions as a pedagogical tool during the clinical-phase psychiatry clerkship. Three questions operationalise this aim. First, in what ways does engaging with a feature-length cinematic case support the clinical-reasoning tasks central to psychiatry—symptom recognition, differential diagnosis, and biopsychosocial formulation? Second, how do students appraise the accuracy and limitations of the cinematic representation, including its handling of stigma, treatment, and suicide? Third, what does this experience reveal about students’ emerging professional identity and their expectations of the wider curriculum? Students’ responses to media representations of stigma and to anti-psychiatry narratives are examined here not as ends in themselves, but insofar as they illuminate these educational questions (Table 1).

The role of cinema in psychiatry education: skills, empathy, and competence

Films featuring psychiatrists and mental illness have historically varied in their portrayals, ranging from negative stereotypes that perpetuate stigma to more nuanced, accurate depictions that humanize psychiatric conditions. Over recent decades, psychiatry educators have leveraged these portrayals to engage students and facilitate learning. Films can vividly illustrate psychiatric symptoms, diagnostic criteria, and therapeutic interactions, thus complementing traditional didactic teaching methods [16, 17].

A notable application of cinema in psychiatric education is seen in residency training programs, where film seminars and group discussions help bridge the gap between the art and science of psychiatry. This format has been shown to be both enjoyable and effective in promoting deeper understanding by stimulating reflection on psychiatric themes and patient experiences [17].

Beyond traditional teaching methods, there are various innovative approaches available to nurture medical students’ interest in psychiatry. Among these approaches, cinema-based education stands out and is already yielding promising results. Many Hollywood productions feature narratives centred on a main character with a mental disorder; examining these films under expert supervision and in a structured discussion environment provides a basis for questioning students’ prejudices and misconceptions about mental illness, thereby fostering a healthier understanding. Furthermore, these film screenings create a productive dialogue environment where faculty members, assistants, and medical students can come together to critically evaluate the clinical accuracy and realism of the psychiatric portrayals in the films [18].

At the undergraduate level, modules using film to explore psychiatry have also been introduced, highlighting the social, cultural, and historical contexts that shape representations of mental illness and psychiatry. Medical students exposed to such modules report increased knowledge and appreciation for psychiatric topics and recognize both the benefits and limitations of cinematic portrayals [19].

Importantly, cinema provides a culturally rich context for understanding mental health attitudes across different societies. For example, Bengali and Malayalam cinema have been examined for their treatment of mental illness, reflecting cultural influences on psychopathology, stigma, and psychiatric care. While portrayals are not always accurate, they offer valuable material for cultural competence training and discussions about stigma, myths, and attitudes toward mental health [20, 21].

Moreover, films can motivate students by making psychiatric education more engaging and relatable. Watching films is helpful for teaching skills such as mental state examination, diagnosis formulation, doctor-patient interactions, and understanding personality disorders. Educators often curate film lists tailored to specific teaching objectives to maximize relevance and impact [22, 23].

Cinema-based instruction has been used to foster empathy, critical thinking, and emotional engagement with psychiatric mental health nursing topics. Such educational methods are considered safe, economical, and effective in fostering deeper understanding [24]. Hermans [25] emphasises that current pedagogical approaches in psychiatric education are student-centred, interactive and multidisciplinary. Cinema-based education can be an effective tool for developing students’ skills in critical thinking, empathy and cultural sensitivity, in line with these approaches.

Additionally, cinema-based training enhances communication skills by exposing trainees to diverse interpersonal dynamics, including therapeutic dialogues and nonverbal behavior. It also cultivates critical thinking by encouraging reflections on the accuracy and limitations of cinematic portrayals, leading to nuanced appreciation of cultural, social, and historical factors influencing mental illness representation. Overall, cinema-based psychiatric education develops key clinical skills such as mental state examination, psychiatric diagnosis, doctor-patient interaction, clinical reasoning, empathy, and cultural competence [16, 17, 22].

Nevertheless, while film is a powerful adjunct in psychiatric education, it must be complemented with critical discussion to address inaccuracies, counter stigma, and contextualize cinematic portrayals within the broader psychiatric knowledge framework. This necessity becomes particularly evident when addressing mainstream media representations, which frequently dramatize mental disorders by foregrounding themes of dangerousness and unpredictability [26]. Because medical students derive treatment expectations not only from the curriculum but also from media exposure, educators must use classroom screenings to teach students how to critically navigate these representations. Aligning with the European Psychiatric Association guidelines, this requires explicit myth-busting strategies and proactive communication to manage the cultural image of psychiatry [3]. To effectively counter media-driven stigma, these cinematic discussions should be integrated with contact-based learning, combining post-viewing reflection with subsequent face-to-face patient encounters to safely bridge representation and clinical reality [11, 27].

Furthermore, selecting and utilizing films in educational settings requires a rigorous safety framework, particularly regarding the portrayal of suicide. Guided by the updated resources of the World Health Organization [28] and the Samaritans [29], educators must help students critically distinguish between mainstream narratives that risk the Werther effect (harmful contagion or imitation, as debated regarding series like 13 Reasons Why) and those that foster the protective Papageno effect (hope-oriented coping models) [30]. Ultimately, by framing cinematic portrayals within these evidence-based and ethical guidelines, cinema represents an untapped resource that merges scientific knowledge with humanistic insights, transforming potential media risks into a robust pedagogical exercise that actively develops students’ clinical and empathetic competence.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study adopted a qualitative descriptive design utilizing semi-structured interviews to examine medical students’ experiences with cinema-based learning in psychiatry education. The research was conducted at the Kocaeli University Faculty of Medicine, during the 2024–2025 academic year, within the mandatory fifth-year psychiatry clerkship.

Participants and sampling

A simple random sampling approach was employed to invite fifth-year students from the clerkship cohort, minimizing selection bias and capturing diverse clinical learning experiences. A total of 50 students participated. This sample size, while substantial for qualitative research, was deliberately chosen to ensure thematic saturation and accommodate variability in students’ prior exposure to psychiatry and stigma-related discussions.

Educational intervention: the Deli Bal case

The intervention centered on screening the feature film Deli Bal [31]. As a mainstream Turkish production where bipolar disorder drives the core narrative, it allows for sustained observation of symptom progression and psychosocial consequences. Consequently, the film served not as a diagnostic tool, but as a complex cinematic case for critical engagement with clinical representation, stigma, and professional responsibility.

Because the film is unlikely to be familiar to an international readership, a brief synopsis is provided. Deli Bal [31] (English title roughly “Mad Honey”; directed by Ali Bilgin) is a Turkish feature film built around Barış, a successful young architect and amateur drummer from an affluent family, whose life is reshaped by bipolar disorder, and his relationship with Füsun, a sociology student he meets while she is working as a waitress and who affectionately nicknames him “Delibal.” The film opens at its tragic end—Barış’s car is found abandoned at a cliff by the sea and the police raise the possibility of suicide—and then reconstructs the couple’s history through extended flashbacks. These trace a vividly dramatised manic course marked by sustained sleeplessness (playing the drums for hours on end), grandiosity, impulsive risk-taking and reckless behaviour (high-speed motorcycle riding, public conflict at work, physical altercations), and explosive irritability and rage. The film also depicts lower, depressive states—tearful breakdowns, exhaustion, and an inability to get out of bed (“sometimes I don’t feel like leaving the bed… I am so tired”)—and frames pharmacological treatment largely through the protagonist’s complaint that his prescribed medication had made him “a completely different person.” Füsun’s family’s reactions and the strain on the couple’s relationship foreground the social meaning of the illness. The narrative culminates in Barış’s suicide by the sea, revealed alongside a video he had recorded and concealed from Füsun. The film therefore offers a dramatically intensified portrait of mania, a comparatively brief depiction of depressive and euthymic phases, and a near-absent representation of comprehensive psychiatric care—features that themselves became objects of student analysis (see Results).

Data collection

Data were collected via face-to-face, audio-recorded, semi-structured interviews (~ 30 min) conducted by the research team post-screening. The 12-question interview guide, developed through literature reviews on cinemeducation and medical humanities, explored four interrelated domains: (1) perceived effectiveness of film in teaching psychopathology; (2) accuracy and limitations of clinical representations (e.g., diagnosis, treatment); (3) attitudes toward mainstream portrayals of mental illness; and (4) reflections on professional identity formation and stigmatizing attitudes. Interviews were transcribed verbatim.

Data analysis and trustworthiness

Data were evaluated using reflexive thematic analysis [32] an approach that acknowledges the researcher’s active role in knowledge production and theme generation. To ensure analytical rigor, a strict audit trail was established. Two researchers independently conducted line-by-line open coding, employing in-vivo coding to preserve participants’ voices (e.g., coding “you can see the clinical picture clearly” as Concretization of Theory). Coding discrepancies were resolved through iterative peer debriefing. Semantic codes were gradually collapsed into broader subthemes and overarching themes. This transparent progression—from literal excerpts to a coherent thematic structure—ensures the conceptual framework remains strictly grounded in empirical data rather than pre-existing assumptions.

Summary of themes, corresponding questions, and subthemes/codes derived from thematic analysis

To provide a cohesive and analytical narrative, the six themes and their associated subthemes emerging from the analysis have been synthesized into five main headings reflecting the students' overarching educational, clinical, and sociological engagement with the cinematic material:

What medical students learned - translating abstract psychopathology into clinical visibility

Participants consistently highlighted the pedagogical functionality of cinema-based learning in overcoming the limitations of text-heavy medical education. Psychiatry often feels conceptually distant in conventional classrooms; as P15 noted, “Psychiatry can remain a bit abstract; [through the film] you can see the clinical picture more clearly.” Students reported that the film concretized theoretical knowledge through an observable narrative, addressing their fundamental need for visualization. Emphasizing this gap, P23 stated: “When it is taught theoretically in lessons, it is very difficult to visualize it in our minds.”

The cinematic medium provided a structured "mise-en-scène" (P42) that reduced cognitive load and supported long-term retention. Students utilized the film as a simulated clinical encounter, which P5 described as a clinical rehearsal: "When your medical knowledge is high, it also serves as practice for you. There is a concrete case, and it stays in your mind." Embedding symptomatology within a narrative framework enhanced visual memory (P21, P34), effectively translating textbook criteria into recognizable behavioral patterns. As P25 emphasized, this visual encoding aids future clinical reasoning: "It is actually useful in terms of thinking, 'when I see such a person, this preliminary diagnosis should come to my mind'."

Beyond recognizing symptoms, the film facilitated access to the “backstage” of the patient’s life, compensating for time-limited psychiatric consultations. Participants contrasted the brevity of routine polyclinic visits—“Hundreds of patients come a day… five minutes each” (P45)—with the comprehensive narrative arc revealing the illness’s psychosocial context. This prolonged engagement allowed students to observe the “internal conflicts and fluctuations” (P13) of the patient, dimensions largely inaccessible since “we don’t know what the patient experiences after leaving the ward” (P45).

This profound exposure prompted a perceptual shift from a lay perspective to an informed clinical gaze. P36 noted: “Before, one thinks of it as the spoiled behaviour of a rich person. But it’s not exactly like that; it is truly a phase of the illness.” This conceptual shift demonstrates that cinema, when integrated into medical training, effectively bridges the gap between abstract psychopathology and the holistic, observable reality of clinical practice.

Responding to cinematic complexity - tension between cinematic representation and clinical reality

While acknowledging the film's pedagogical value, participants simultaneously adopted a critical stance toward its clinical accuracy, acting as "clinical auditors." Although specific manic behaviors—such as the motorcycle and drum-playing scenes—were praised for symptomatic consistency (P1, P3, P5), students quickly identified profound discrepancies regarding the cyclical nature of bipolar disorder. They observed a disproportionate focus on the manic phase and the relative invisibility of euthymic and depressive periods. Highlighting this temporal distortion, participants noted, "We never saw the normal period at all" (P23), and "The manic phase lasts too long... depressive episodes were kept very short" (P29, P42). Students warned that this selective dramatization reduces the illness to its extremes; as P46 articulated, "Because it only captures the extreme moments... it shows one aspect of the illness but not all its dimensions."

Participants also interrogated specific psychiatric inconsistencies within the protagonist's behavior, identifying an "insight paradox" during manic episodes. Noting the contradiction between clinical reality and cinematic fiction, P36 and P45 stated: "He realizes what he did was wrong and feels regret... But from what I know, patients in a manic episode are not aware [of their actions]." Others pointed out the absence of classic symptoms like the flight of ideas, observing that the character remained overly articulate and "highly stylized" (P19) rather than exhibiting expected cognitive fragmentation (P17). These dramatized choices led participants to question the narrative's primary intent: whether the goal was public awareness or simply creating a "romantic film" by portraying the illness as initially "very sweet" and exciting (P9, P12).

However, the most severe ethical criticisms targeted the representation of the treatment process. Participants unanimously noted the alarming invisibility of comprehensive psychiatric care. The absence of psychotherapy, family integration, and structured clinical follow-up was perceived as a critical flaw. Expressing this gap, P9 emphasized: "We only saw a single scene with the psychiatrist. And even then, he wasn't talking at all. He just prescribed medication... There was no professional process."

A dangerous “pharmacological fallacy” further compounded this lack of medical visibility. The narrative arc—where the protagonist discontinues medication due to side effects—was heavily criticized for reinforcing anti-psychiatry biases. Students expressed deep concern over the protagonist’s claim that the medication made him “live like a plant” (P16), arguing that such portrayals send a highly detrimental public health message: “The film gives the message that when they take these medications, they get worse” (P49). Ultimately, participants felt the film’s tragic resolution, culminating in suicide, cemented a deterministic and hopeless view of the illness, wrongfully implying that a bipolar patient “cannot reach a stable state even with treatment” (P19).

Uncovering stigma - stigma literacy and media awareness

Demonstrating profound “stigma literacy,” participants recognized the “double-edged” nature of media representation. On one hand, mainstream cinematic portrayals can facilitate public symptom recognition and empathy, particularly when a widely admired celebrity plays the patient: “Seeing [a popular actor] as bipolar increases empathy… If it were someone else, people would always remember the illness badly” (P44). This visibility also promotes normalization and symptom tolerance, serving as a “source of motivation” (P45) for actual patients while educating relatives not to “push too hard or escalate” (P26) during manic episodes.

Conversely, participants cautioned that dramatized narratives risk reinforcing societal stereotypes, cultivating what they identified as “stigmatizing awareness.” The cinematic emphasis on extreme behaviors—specifically physical violence and impulsive risk-taking—was criticized for equating mental illness with unpredictability. Warning against such depictions, P23 noted: “It can trigger prejudice and avoidance behavior against people with psychiatric illnesses.” Media’s dual potential to simultaneously inform and alienate was succinctly encapsulated by P50: “It increases awareness… but when it comes to someone close, they say, ‘Let me stay away from this’.”

Students were particularly concerned about the tangible consequences of media-driven stigma on patients’ interpersonal lives, specifically regarding relational exclusion and self-concealment. Participants argued the protagonist’s chaotic trajectory could instill fear in potential partners. Highlighting this relational anxiety, students shared hypothetical societal reactions: “You are going to marry someone. What if they commit suicide… what if they leave me halfway?” (P9), leading to the deterministic conclusion that one “cannot be a lover, cannot be a spouse” (P3, P23). Consequently, students perceptively noted that societal exclusion inevitably breeds self-stigma, with P3 confessing, “If I were a bipolar patient, I would hesitate to tell anyone [after watching this].”

Beyond relational stigma, participants critically analyzed the public health implications of the film’s tragic resolution. The protagonist’s suicide was interpreted as a “traumatic ending” (P27) that serves as a “negative role model” (P32), wrongfully implying that clinical recovery is impossible. Interestingly, students exhibited acute sociological awareness, suggesting audience reception varies significantly across socio-cultural contexts. P36 astutely hypothesized: “It might encourage a family with a high intellectual level [to seek help]. But in a family with lower social status… they might suppress the child,” driven by fear of the illness escalating. While some suggested the tragic ending might paradoxically increase “fear-based help-seeking” (P42), the consensus remained that the film predominantly risks exacerbating fear, avoidance, and fatalistic beliefs regarding psychiatric care.

Overcoming the representation - emerging shifts in professional identity formation

The cinematic experience served as a potent catalyst for Professional Identity Formation (PIF), prompting students to critically reflect on their future clinical roles and the limitations of the conventional biomedical model. Contrasting the film’s narrative depth with the time-pressured reality of actual practice, one student lamented: “Hundreds of patients come a day… five minutes each… [Physicians] might not be able to empathize much” (P15). Students realized traditional clinical encounters often obscure the illness’s psychosocial realities. The film provided crucial “backstage” access (P13), revealing “internal conflicts and fluctuations” largely invisible in brief examinations. Recognizing this gap, P45 reflected: “We don’t know what the patient experiences after leaving the ward.”

This profound “backstage” exposure facilitated a critical shift from a purely symptom-focused perspective to a holistic understanding. Engaging with the protagonist’s “lifeworld” prevented students from alienating psychiatric patients or seeing them as “aliens” (P21). The narrative structure allowed medical students to perceive the individual beyond their diagnosis. Summarizing this humanizing effect, P15 noted: “This man is not just a patient; he is a drummer and an architect. It adds the perspective that the illness can manifest itself this way in an architect’s life.”

Most notably, the film created a safe, reflective space for students to navigate anticipated clinical frustrations, particularly treatment non-adherence. Patient refusal to take medication often triggers negative emotional responses (counter-transference) in physicians. Students explicitly recognized this dynamic, transitioning from a judgment-oriented stance to a context-sensitive approach. P45 articulated this reflective shift: “‘I didn’t take my medication…’ This makes me angry. But again, we come to empathy… Why didn’t that patient take that medication?” This reflection demonstrates that cinema-based learning cultivates advanced cognitive-affective competence, transforming spontaneous frustration into clinical curiosity.

Furthermore, engaging with the narrative’s tragic elements heightened clinical vigilance. The protagonist’s deceptive claim of wellness and subsequent treatment discontinuation served as a critical warning. Viewing this from a physician’s perspective, participants described the scenario as “one of a doctor’s weakest points, the thing we don’t want and fear the most” (P50). This awareness translated into practical foresight; the film provided mental schemas (P25) helping students anticipate severe manic episodes or sudden clinical deteriorations (P9).

However, demonstrating mature media skepticism, participants maintained that while cinema enhances foresight, it cannot substitute for the experiential weight of actual patient encounters. Grounding the discussion, P16 stated: “The most important education is in medical school… I could watch it 100 times, but until [the patient] stands in front of me, it won’t change anything.” This balanced reception underscores that students process cinematic materials not as instructional dogmas, but as complementary tools continuously tested against clinical realities.

Addressing educational needs - multimodal learning expectations in contemporary psychiatry education

Analysis revealed profound frustration with conventional, text-heavy instructional methods, highlighting a phenomenon of “didactic fatigue.” Participants criticized the curriculum’s reliance on slide-based lectures, noting that sheer volume does not equate to effective learning. Expressing this cognitive overload, P6 stated: “Constant text, constant text… after a point, nothing stays in one’s mind. Our education is very monotonous.” Consequently, students demanded a transition toward a multimodal learning ecosystem integrating visual, digital, and experiential modalities.

A primary expectation centered on animation-based learning to decode complex psychopharmacological and neurobiological micro-mechanisms. Students reported significant difficulty mentally structuring abstract physiological processes: “We talk about receptors, neurotransmitters, but nothing visualizes in our minds” (P3, P12). To overcome this, students advocated for animations mapping dynamic processes like receptor blockades. Seeing these mechanisms “in sequence” (P6) makes them “much more memorable” (P3). Transforming invisible biological mechanisms into visible narratives drastically reduces the cognitive load of rote memorization.

Demonstrating an acute awareness of the “attention economy,” participants highlighted a critical need for micro-learning formats. Acknowledging that feature-length films are too time-consuming to cover every disorder, students proposed integrating ultra-short, structured video content. Reflecting digital consumption habits, they suggested brief, spot-information formats to illustrate specific clinical presentations (P8, P38). Highlighting this efficiency, P44 noted: “I came across an animation series… It tells the lives of epilepsy patients… It’s not like a movie, but a one-minute thing… One-minute videos for each [symptom] would be great.”

Finally, students emphasized that visual media requires experiential integration, strongly advocating for scenario-based and simulation-supported learning. Moving beyond passive observation, participants desired to actively manage psychiatric crises by integrating visual cases into Problem-Based Learning (PBL), utilizing Virtual Reality (VR) (P38), and employing role-play (P26). Recalling a simulated emergency, P41 and P17 shared: “There is a simulation table… we were managing it directly… managing [the patient] in that stress and keeping control… I never forgot it.” Ultimately, contemporary medical students expect cinema and digital media to function not as standalone entertainment, but as integrated components of a highly structured, interactive, and multimodal clinical training environment.

Discussion

This study explored medical students’ engagement with a mainstream cinematic portrayal of bipolar disorder during a psychiatry clerkship. Findings indicate that critically mediated cinema-based learning enhances clinical reasoning, stigma literacy, and professional identity formation (PIF).

From passive viewing to diagnostic reasoning

Aligning with Cinemeducation principles, students actively interrogated cinematic dramatizations rather than remaining passive viewers. Identifying inaccuracies—such as distorted symptom cycling and the “insight paradox”—transformed emotional engagement into diagnostic reasoning rehearsal. As Song et al. highlight distinguishing dramatized symptoms from real-world clinical heterogeneity is crucial [2]. Students’ organic recognition of these inconsistencies fulfills this pedagogical requirement, demonstrating that critical media analysis reinforces, rather than replaces, biomedical rigor.

Media representation, stigma literacy, and suicide depiction

Moving beyond simple empathy, students demonstrated high “stigma literacy” by analyzing relational stigma and societal implications. Their concerns regarding the film’s negative portrayal of psychopharmacology (the “zombification myth”) echo the European Psychiatric Association’s guidelines for countering media-driven misperceptions, aligning with Stuart’s26 warnings about entertainment emphasizing dangerousness. Furthermore, discomfort with the romanticized suicide and demands for recovery-oriented narratives reflect an intuitive grasp of media contagion theories. The dramatized risks correspond to the Werther effect (harmful contagion), while preferences for adaptive coping narratives align with the protective Papageno effect. These reflections support integrating safety frameworks [28] into curricula, proving that guided critiques cultivate students’ ability to anticipate the social consequences of psychiatric representations.

Students’ culturally situated readings of the film reflect how learners interpret cinema through their own cultural lens. This resonates with the established use of film in cultural-competence training, where cinematic narratives objectify difficult, value-laden material and create a safer entry point for group discussion of culturally sensitive topics [33]. Community-based film events that pair a screening with structured, expert-led discussion have similarly been proposed as a creative means of improving mental health awareness and treatment-seeking, reinforcing the value of pairing cinema with guided dialogue rather than passive viewing [34].

Professional identity formation in context

Reflections on treatment adherence and clinical vigilance indicate early-stage PIF. The film provided a low-risk reflective space for students to examine emotional reactions—like counter-transference towards non-adherent patients—and reframe them into a patient-centered orientation. Aligning with Rueb et al.’s model,7 this demonstrates that affectively evocative cinematic dilemmas foster perspective-taking and the iterative “trying on” of professional roles, providing a robust foundation for identity work.

Furthermore, integrating cinema within the broader medical humanities may offer a protective psychological space. Group-based debriefing fosters a sense of community and shared reflection, reducing the professional isolation that often precedes physician burnout and mitigating the psychological distress prevalent in the medical profession.

Implications for psychiatry education and multimodal curricula

To combat “didactic fatigue,” participants emphasized the necessity of guided multimodal learning. Cinema used in isolation risks reinforcing stigmatizing stereotypes; however, structured debriefing transforms it into a robust platform for clinical and ethical reflection. This supports Kinnair et al.4 and Piqué-Buisan et al.1, who caution against passive “watch-and-leave” formats. Crucially, contemporary students require an educational ecosystem extending beyond feature films. Demands for animation-based visualizations align with the Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning, demonstrating that dynamic visual channels reduce extraneous cognitive load. In today’s “attention economy,” text-heavy lectures are insufficient. Preferences for micro-learning (spot-information videos) and experiential simulations (VR, role-play) signal a necessary pedagogical shift. Ultimately, integrating these digital components with contact-based approaches [15] ensures cinema functions not as isolated entertainment, but as a critically mediated tool within an interactive multimodal curriculum.

Conclusion

This study reveals that cinema-based education transcends passive supplementation, acting as an active pedagogical component that facilitates “contextual learning.” By concretizing abstract psychopathology, visual narratives allow students to situate symptomatology within the patient’s psycho-social “lifeworld,” moving beyond rigid DSM criteria. This approach strengthens clinical observation and transforms basic empathy into a “cognitive-affective clinical competence,” enabling a holistic understanding essential for relationship-based psychiatric practice.

Conversely, the divergence of popular cinema from clinical reality underscores a critical need for “stigma literacy.” Students’ ethical sensitivity toward incomplete treatment representations, the dramatization of suicide (risking the Werther effect), and the reinforcement of relational stigma highlights the public health risks of unfiltered media. Therefore, cinema integration must be anchored by structured, expert-led debriefing sessions to guide clinical reasoning and mitigate these risks.

A fundamental outcome for medical curricula is the identification of “didactic fatigue.” The high cognitive load of traditional, text-centric methods adversely affects the retention of abstract pathophysiological concepts. Findings emphasize a generational demand for micro-learning, animation-based visualizations, and clinical simulations to structure complex mechanisms and build emotional regulation.

In conclusion, contemporary psychiatry education must evolve from single-channel information transfer toward a multisensory, interdisciplinary model. Fictional cinema, animations, and simulations are not standalone tools; they are vital pedagogical strategies that, when integrated into a multimodal ecosystem with contact-based learning, profoundly support clinical competence and professional identity formation.
