Microfluidics‐Driven Piezophotocatalysis: An Innovative Approach for Accelerated Water Remediation Using PVDF–Transition Metal Sulfide‐Based Piezocatalysts
Authors: Alisha Rohal, Vibhav Katoch, Abhishek Kumar, Ambrish Pandey, Bhanu Prakash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe growing contamination of water resources with persistent dyes and pharmaceuticals necessitates the development of rapid and energy‐efficient remediation technologies. We present a microfluidic purification reactor that synergistically integrates piezophotocatalysis with flow‐engineered microstructures to enable rapid pollutant degradation. The microreactor, fabricated from polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) via laser micromachining, was optimized with strategically positioned micropillars to induce turbulence and enhance mass transfer, with COMSOL Multiphysics simulations validating flow behavior and vorticity patterns. Catalytic functionality was introduced through an electrospun polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) nanofiber membrane embedded with MoS2 and WS2 nanoparticles forming an active piezophotocatalytic interface within the reactor bed. Structural, compositional, and functional characterizations identified PM15 (15 wt% MoS2) and PW20 (20 wt% WS2) as optimal nanocomposites, with their electromechanical response validated by a piezoelectric nanogenerator generating ≥36 V. Under optimized operating conditions, flow rate of 50 µL/min, visible‐light irradiation, and ultrasonic excitation at 60 kHz, the integrated system achieved rapid degradation efficiencies of ≥94% for RhB and ≥90% for CIP within 252 s, outperforming conventional methods. A multiple linear regression model accurately predicted degradation efficiencies from key operational parameters, demonstrating the utility of data‐driven process optimization. Overall, the integrated microfluidic–piezophotocatalyst platform establishes a rapid, high‐throughput, and energy‐efficient approach for advanced water purification.Keywords: microfluidic purification reactor, piezophotocatalysis, PVDF nanofibers, wastewater treatment, WS2/MoS2 nanocomposites

Introduction

The escalating wastewater crisis is a persistent global issue, with organic pollutants like antibiotics from pharmaceutical production and synthetic dyes from textile posing serious risks to aquatic ecosystem and human health, yet current mitigation strategies fall short of effectively addressing its far‐reaching consequences. Ciprofloxacin (CIP), a broad‐spectrum fluoroquinolone antibiotic, is widely used in human and veterinary medicine for its strong efficacy and oral absorption. However, its high stability leads to unmetabolized excretion into water bodies, posing health risks and contributing to antibiotic resistance [1, 2]. Rhodamine B (RhB), a water‐soluble dye used in textile, printing, and paper industries, is hard to eradicate from wastewater due to its complex structure. It poses serious health risks, including neurotoxicity, chronic toxicity, and carcinogenic effects in humans and animals [3, 4]. Hence, developing efficient and rapid removal strategies for these organic pollutants is imperative to safeguard water quality and environmental health.

Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) have shown exceptional wastewater degradation efficiencies by generating strong reactive oxygen species (ROS) to mineralize organic pollutants into CO2 and H2O [5]. Among emerging AOPs, piezocatalysis has garnered significant interest for its ability to transduce ubiquitous mechanical energy into electrical energy via strain‐induced potential of piezoelectric materials that concentrates the charge carriers at active edge sites, ultimately accelerating the piezocatalytic reaction [2, 6, 7]. Furthermore, it initiates oxidation/reduction reactions producing ROS, primarily superoxide (O2•−) and hydroxyl (•OH) radicals, which contribute to effective water purification [8]. Unlike photocatalysis, piezocatalysis offers distinct advantages, such as reduced dependence on light and the elimination of additional chemical agents, making it a more versatile and sustainable alternative. However, a major limitation is the frequent necessity of continuous mechanical stimulation to sustain the piezoelectric potential. Ultrasound irradiation presents a viable strategy to address this constraint by providing continuous and tunable mechanical stress which stimulates piezoelectric potential as well as sonochemical effects [9]. As ultrasound passes through an aqueous solution, it induces sonolysis, where nucleation, cavitation, and bubble interactions break chemical bonds and result in the formulation of free radicals [10]. Over the years, researchers have advanced a synergistic approach known as piezophotocatalysis, which leverages the combined benefits of these individual mechanisms, significantly accelerating the degradation process through the intensified generation of free radicals [11, 12]. In this process, photocatalytically generated radicals interact with pollutants adsorbed on the surface of the piezocatalyst and are subsequently desorbed through the mechanical forces induced by shock waves generated during the collapse of cavitation bubbles formed by ultrasonic vibrations [13]. Moreover, an internal electric field induced through piezoelectric effect enhances radical formation by facilitating charge carrier separation and migration to active sites [9].

Transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs), a notable group of two‐dimensional (2D) semiconducting materials, with structural formula MX2 (where M is the transition metal and X is the chalcogen element), have shown great potential for improving degradation performance in membrane‐driven wastewater treatment systems [14]. TMDCs like MoS2 and WS2 are extensively studied for their high surface area with abundant active sites, excellent carrier mobility, tunable bandgap, superior electrical conductivity, and photoelectrochemical stability [15]. Furthermore, their non‐centrosymmetric structures endow them with inherent piezoelectric properties, making them effective piezocatalysts for water purification applications [16]. Even though TMDCs exhibit excellent piezocatalytic performance, their tendency to aggregate and the challenges associated with recovery and separation due to their powdery nature can be mitigated by immobilization on inorganic or polymer substrates, thereby enhancing stability and enabling large‐scale applications [17].

PVDF stands out as an ideal piezoelectric polymer substrate, offering exceptional flexibility, nontoxicity, and chemical stability, making it highly compatible for integrating with semiconductor nanomaterials as a composite catalyst [18, 19]. Its intrinsic piezopotential enhances charge carrier dynamics by accelerating their transfer while effectively minimizing their recombination rates [20]. Niu's team demonstrated that incorporating 10 wt% exfoliated MoS2 into electrospun PVDF membranes significantly enhanced oxytetracycline degradation, achieving a remarkable 93.08% removal in just 24 min—21.1 times higher than pristine PVDF nanofibers [21]. Thus far, there have been numerous reports of immobilizing nanocatalysts on PVDF substrates which not only amplifies catalytic efficiency but also remarkably enhances its electroactive (EA) β‐phase, unlocking synergistic piezophotocatalytic potential. Moreover, compared to other morphologies like drop‐casted films, electrospun nanocomposites offer superior mechanical strength, enhanced hydrophobicity, larger surface area, and greater flexibility, allowing for more efficient mechanical vibration transmission and improved reactant diffusion through their interconnected nanofiber network [22].

The integration of microfluidics technology with advanced wastewater treatment processes has revolutionized wastewater treatment efficiency compared to traditional batch reactors. Microfluidic reactors enhance reaction rates with a high surface‐area‐to‐volume ratio and short diffusion length for rapid pollutant transfer [23]. Moreover, the placement of catalyst films under the fluid layer in the microreactor ensures uniform irradiation across the entire surface, resulting in higher proton efficiency and faster reaction times [24]. Additionally, they provide uniform residence time, maximizing the throughput, reducing energy consumption, and enabling miniaturized, cost‐effective treatment with precise control, enhanced mass transfer, and real‐time monitoring [25]. Our group has previously demonstrated the use of microfluidic technology for rapid dye degradation [26]. However, catalyst leaching in microreactors leads to active site loss, reduced recyclability, and microchannel clogging due to redeposition of leached species. Therefore, to address this, immobilizing catalysts on the reactor bed is a viable solution and strategies such as catalyst‐embedded membranes, including polymer nanofibers incorporated with TMDCs, enable physical separation of the catalyst from the reaction medium, enhancing stability and performance [27, 28].

In this study, we demonstrate a microfluidic device integrated with a piezocatalytic membrane that effectively degrades organic pollutants such as CIP and RhB through a synergistic combination of visible light and ultrasound stimulation. A PVDF nanofiber membrane embedded with MoS2 and WS2 nanoparticles in different ratios is fabricated and extensively characterized to identify the optimal composite with the maximum EA phase. To validate their piezoelectric response, the membrane's potential for generating output voltage is evaluated by fabricating a piezoelectric nanogenerator (PENG). The device features a strategically designed alternating micropillar architecture, validated through COMSOL Multiphysics simulations by comparing velocity profiles and vorticity magnitudes across the microreactor configuration. By controlling flow rate conditions, the impact of varying ultrasonic frequencies on pollutant degradation is also examined. The study concludes with a comparative assessment of pollutant degradation efficiency under three conditions: (1) visible light alone, (2) ultrasonic transducer alone, and (3) the combined effect of both stimuli, highlighting the enhanced performance achieved through piezophotocatalysis. Finally, a machine learning model is developed to predict degradation efficiency, while the dominant reactive species is identified and the recyclability of the piezocatalyst is also evaluated.

Materials and Method

Materials

Nanopowders of molybdenum(IV) sulfide (MoS2) and tungsten(IV) sulfide (WS2), along with polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, average M w ∼ 534 000) were procured from Sigma–Aldrich. The electrospinning process utilized N,N‐dimethylformamide (DMF; ≥99.5%, Sigma–Aldrich) and acetone (Emplura, Merck) as solvents. Rhodamine B (RhB; C28H31ClN2O3, Fisher Scientific, ≥95%) and ciprofloxacin (CIP; C17H18FN3O3, Sigma–Aldrich, ≥98%) were selected as model pollutants for catalytic experiments. For microreactor fabrication, a polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) sheet with a thickness of ∼3000 µm was obtained from Polyglass International Mfg. Corp., and silicone tubing procured from Polymax Elastomer Pvt. Ltd. was connected to the inlet and outlet ports of the microreactor. Silver nitrate (AgNO3; ACS reagent, ≥99.0%, Merck), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA; 99.995% trace metals basis, Merck), p‐benzoquinone (BQ; reagent grade, ≥98%, Merck), and isopropyl alcohol (IPA; Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used as scavengers. All chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade and employed directly without any further purification.

Preparation of Nanofiber Membrane

A spinning solution was developed using a solvent blend of DMF and acetone in an equal volume ratio (1:1, v/v). PVDF powder (16% w/v) was introduced into this solvent mixture and subjected to continuous stirring at 80°C for 3 h until a homogenous, transparent solution was obtained. Electrospinning process, illustrated schematically in Figure 1a, was employed to prepare nanofibers using an electrospinning machine (E‐Spin Nanotech Pvt Ltd., India). A 10 mL Dispo Van syringe containing the PVDF solution was mounted on a syringe pump, and electrospinning was performed at 15 kV with a 25 µL/min flow rate and a 12 cm tip‐to‐collector distance. The PVDF nanofibers were collected onto an aluminum foil‐covered drum collector rotating at ∼700 rpm and subsequently subjected to drying at 70°C for 12 h to eliminate any residual solvents before proceeding with further characterization. Additionally, to fabricate PVDF composites incorporating MoS2 and WS2 nanoparticles separately, varying weight percentages of each additive were incorporated into the previously prepared PVDF solution. Following that, the mixtures were stirred at 70°C for 6 h to ensure uniform dispersion. The PVDF–MoS2 composites, labeled as PM, were designated as PM5, PM10, PM15, and PM20, corresponding to MoS2 loadings of 5, 10, 15 and 20% w/v in PVDF, respectively. Similarly, the PVDF–WS2 composites, labeled as PW, were designated as PW5, PW10, PW15, and PW20 for WS2 concentrations of 5, 10, 15 and 20% w/v within the PVDF matrix, respectively. A similar procedure, as mentioned above, was employed to obtain PM and PW nanofibers with varying filler concentrations, which were subsequently subjected to further characterization.

Characterization of Nanofiber Membrane

X‐ray diffraction (XRD; Eco D8 Advance Bruker diffractometer) having Cu‐Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å), spanning at 2θ range of 10°–70° was used to determine the structural characteristics of nanofibers. The crystalline phases of the nanofibers were investigated through Fourier‐transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy using a PerkinElmer spectrometer, covering the 600–1600 cm−1 spectral range. Additionally, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was carried out using a DSC25 (TA Instruments), by purging N2 gas at 20 mL/min where the samples are sealed in aluminum pans and heated from 30°C to 200°C at a rate of 20°C/min. A field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM; JEOL JSMIT‐300) was utilized to examine the morphology of electrospun nanofibers, while elemental mapping was conducted using energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy integrated with the FESEM system. Thermo Scientific K‐Alpha Spectrometer was utilized with a monochromatic Al‐Kα (1486.6 eV) source for X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis, while a He‐I (21.2 eV) source was used for ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) analysis. A Raman confocal microscope (WITEC Focus innovation Alpha‐300) with a 532 nm excitation wavelength was utilized to monitor the layer count of composites, while their hydrophobicity was checked using a drop shape analyzer (DSA25E, Krüss Scientific, Germany). Digital oscilloscope (TBS1102C, Tektronix, United States) was used for piezoelectric device measurements, and the absorbance spectra of degraded products were recorded through ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy analysis using Agilent Cary 100 UV–Vis spectrophotometer spectrum across a wavelength range of 200–800 nm.

Fabrication and Integration of Nanofiber‐Embedded Microreactor

The microfluidic reactor was fabricated using a CO2 laser engraver (Trotec Speedy 100), with initial designs created in CorelDRAW (Graphics Suite 2018) and transferred to the laser system through Trotec Job Control software. During the engraving process, a PMMA sheet was positioned on a Z‐adjustable stage, where the laser was precisely focused for patterning. Two distinct microreactor configurations, one without any micropillars (i.e., only reservoir) and the other with an alternating micropillar alignment (Figure 1d), were engraved by fine‐tuning laser parameters such as speed, power, and frequency. For better understanding, the detailed dimensions of the microreactor are provided in Figure S1. Thereafter, to integrate the prepared nanofiber membrane at the reactor bed, laser micromachining was employed to create holes in the membrane that matched the micropillar diameter, ensuring proper alignment with the microreactor design. The desired nanofiber membrane is depicted in Figure 1e, while its integration within the microreactor with alternate micropillar architecture is presented in Figure 1f. Additionally, the FESEM image of the laser‐treated PM15 nanofiber (Figure S2a) reveals a membrane hole diameter of 799 µm, corresponding to micropillar diameter of 750 µm, indicating a minimal size deviation that can be considered negligible. Figure S2b confirms that the laser treatment minimally affected the nanofiber morphology, with slight aggregation near the cut‐out regions while retaining the overall structure.

Experimental Setup

The nanofiber membrane of thickness ∼44 µm (Figure S3) was embedded in the microreactor and centrally mounted on an ultrasonic transducer (NBL20602S; Digi‐Key Electronics, United States) to attain maximum acoustic energy. Moreover, to enhance its ultrasonic transmission as well as minimize the air gap between the microreactor and the transducer, a very small amount of ultrasound gel (Medprio) was applied at their interface. A sinusoidal signal, generated by a function generator (SGDA 30 M 30 MHz; VarTech, India), was amplified using a power amplifier module (SPA‐100K 100 KHz; VarTech, India), and thereafter, the amplified signal was continuously monitored via a digital oscilloscope. The model pollutants (i.e., RhB and CIP) were introduced separately into the microreactor using a syringe pump (PHD Ultra Series; Harvard Apparatus, United States) via silicone tubing, and the final product was collected in a beaker for further characterization. A digital image of the experimental setup is shown in Figure 1c. Furthermore, for experiments involving a visible light source (incandescent bulb, 100 W), the microreactor‐mounted transducer was placed inside a cardboard enclosure, with a light bulb positioned at a fixed distance of ∼14 cm from the microreactor, to ensure controlled illumination.

Degradation Studies

The degradation experiments for RhB and CIP were conducted separately to evaluate the efficiency of PM and PW nanofibers as piezocatalysts, and their synergistic effect with an ultrasonic transducer and visible light was assessed. The experimental process was systematically divided into three stages, wherein reaction conditions were optimized at each stage and subsequently applied to the following phase.

Optimization of Microreactor Design

The effect of microreactor design on the degradation of RhB and CIP was systematically evaluated through a comparative study of two distinct microreactor configurations. The first configuration (Figure S4a) featured one inlet, one outlet, and a reservoir, while the second configuration maintained the same overall geometry but incorporated an array of micropillars within the reservoir, arranged in an alternate pattern (Figure S4b). To investigate the influence of micropillars on fluid flow characteristics, both configurations were modeled using COMSOL Multiphysics and simulated using the finite element method, employing the laminar flow, stationary physics interface under incompressible flow conditions. The piezoelectric (PZT) domain was positioned directly beneath the microfluidic device in both configurations. Both geometries were simulated using the finite element method, employing the laminar flow, piezoelectricity (solid mechanics and electrostatics), and thermoviscous acoustic. Water was used as the working fluid, and a volume flow rate (Q) of 50 μL/min was applied at the inlet, while the outlet was maintained at atmospheric pressure (P 0 = 0 Pa). No slip wall condition was assigned to all channel walls. A user‐defined mesh (free tetrahedral) with a predefined finer size, calibrated for fluid dynamics applications, was employed to ensure accurate resolution of velocity and pressure gradients, particularly near the channel walls and around the micropillars. Besides this, the degradation experiments were conducted at a fixed pollutant flow rate of 50 µL/min, as established from literature, and in the absence of visible light, an external transducer, and any piezocatalyst to ensure that the observed degradation was solely attributed to the microreactor geometry. Thereafter, the selected microreactor was employed for all subsequent experimental investigations.

Optimization of Flow Rate Conditions

Following microreactor selection, the second phase focused on optimizing the pollutant solution flow rate. PM and PW nanofibers were manually embedded within the microreactor bed, and prior to experimentation, the piezocatalysts were prewetted in water for 30 min to ensure proper absorption. Degradation trials were then conducted by flowing RhB and CIP separately at varying flow rates, and the flow rate yielding the highest degradation efficiency was identified and fixed for subsequent experiments.

Optimization of Ultrasonic Frequency

With the optimized microreactor design and pollutant flow rate, the final phase involved determining the optimal ultrasonic frequency. The pollutant solution was passed through the microreactor while an ultrasonic transducer positioned below it was utilized to generate waves at different frequencies, and the samples were collected at regular intervals for further analysis. The frequency yielding the maximum degradation efficiency was selected for further analysis, and any potential temperature rise within the microreactor was not regulated or controlled.

Finally, with the optimized microreactor geometry, pollutant flow rate, and ultrasonic frequency, a comparative assessment was performed under three distinct conditions to evaluate the degradation efficiency of PM and PW nanofibers. The study investigated (i) degradation under visible‐light irradiation alone, (ii) degradation with ultrasonic transducer activation alone, and (iii) the synergistic effect of both stimuli. Antibacterial Study

The residual antibacterial activity of degraded CIP was studied using a standard disc diffusion assay against Escherichia coli (E. coli). In the experimental procedure, E. coli cultures were grown to the exponential phase and uniformly spread onto nutrient agar plates to form a confluent bacterial lawn. Sterile filter paper discs with a diameter of 6 mm were used as carriers for the test samples. One set of discs was impregnated with the parent CIP sample (CIPoriginal), while another set was loaded with the degraded ciprofloxacin sample (CIPdegradation) obtained after the degradation. The discs were aseptically placed onto the inoculated agar surface, and the plates were incubated at 37.5°C for 12 h, following standard antimicrobial susceptibility testing protocols reported in the literature [30].

Active Species Trapping

The degradation of pollutants was facilitated by the generation of ROS, which played a crucial role in the degradation mechanism. To elucidate the contribution of specific ROS, selective scavengers were employed; AgNO3 for electron (e−) capture, BQ for •O2 −, EDTA for holes (h+), and IPA for •OH. The scavenger experiments were carried out in the microreactor under optimized operating conditions, with all experimental parameters carefully controlled. A 0.5 M concentration of each scavenger was introduced, and samples were collected at 20‐min intervals to systematically identify the dominant ROS responsible for pollutant degradation. This test was performed for both pollutants (RhB and CIP), utilizing both piezocatalysts (PM and PW) separately.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of Nanofibers

Structural and Phase Analysis

The structural analysis of PVDF, MoS2, WS2, PM, and PW nanofibers was conducted using XRD, with the corresponding spectra illustrated in Figure 2a,b. The diffraction pattern for pure PVDF displays distinct peaks at 18.50° and 20.20°, correlating with (020) and (110) planes, which are characteristic of the α‐ and β‐phases of PVDF, respectively [31]. In Figure 2a, all PM samples exhibit prominent diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 14.36°, 28.7°, 32.51°, 33.71°, 35.84°, 39.46°, 44.04°, 49.90°, 58.53°, and 60.35° that are indexed to the (002), (004), (100), (101), (102), (103), (006), (105), (110), and (008) planes of MoS2, respectively, confirming its 2H‐phase structure (JCPDS card no. 37‐1492) [32, 33]. Notably, the intensity of the 20.20° peak, corresponding to the β‐phase of PVDF, increases significantly upon MoS2 incorporation, with PM15 showing the highest intensity enhancement [34]. Similarly, Figure 2b presents the XRD spectra for all PW samples, where a distinct peak at 14.4° corresponds to the (002) plane of WS2, while additional peaks at 33.5°, 40.25°, 58.4°, and 60.5° align with the (101), (103), (008), and (112) planes, respectively, confirming the hexagonal crystal structure of WS2 (JCPDS card no. 08‐0237) [35, 36, 37, 38]. Importantly, in both composites, the characteristic PVDF peaks remain prominent across varying filler concentrations, reinforcing the successful integration of MoS2 and WS2 nanoparticles into the PVDF matrix while retaining its crystalline structure.

Furthermore, to assess the influence of MoS2 and WS2 on the crystallization behavior of PVDF nanofibers, FTIR spectroscopy was employed due to PVDF's multiple crystalline phases (α, β, and γ), with β being the most EA [39]. Figure 2c,d presents a comparative FTIR spectral analysis of pure PVDF nanofibers alongside PM and PW samples, where distinct peaks at 614, 762, and 795 cm−1, characteristic of the nonpolar α‐phase, are consistently observed across all samples, while an absorption peak at 1231 cm−1 confirms the presence of the semipolar γ‐phase [40]. The β‐phase is identified by a high intensity peak at 1273 cm−1, while another peak at 839 cm−1, indicative of both β‐ and γ‐phases due to their structural similarities, is also evident [20]. The quantification of phases (Section S1) of all samples is depicted in Figure 2e,f, revealing a substantial increase in the β‐ and γ‐phase contents in composite nanofibers as compared to PVDF nanofibers [41]. Notably, the highest enhancement is observed in PM15 and PW20, indicating that incorporating optimal concentrations of MoS2 and WS2 nanoparticles into the PVDF matrix enhances the total EA phase due to the phase transition from the nonpolar α‐phase to the EA phase attributing to the reorientation of molecular chains of PVDF into all‐trans (TTTT) or trans–trans–trans–gauche (TTTG) conformations, which is further validated by the diminishing peak of the α‐phase at 762 cm−1 (Figure S5a,b) [42]. Ultimately, a significant enhancement in the EA phase is observed, rising from 33% in pure PVDF to 82% in PM15 and 77% in PW20, which underscores the role of MoS2 and WS2 nanoparticles as effective nucleating agents, driving phase transformation through electrostatic interactions with the PVDF matrix. For a deeper insight, a comparative table summarizing the quantitative phase contents of PVDF, along with all PM and PW samples, is provided in Table 1. Moreover, a FTIR analysis (Section S2) compared drop‐casted PM15 and PW20 films with their electrospun counterparts, confirming nanofibers as a suitable morphology for piezocatalytic membranes due to enhanced EA phase formation (Figure S6), attributed to electrospinning‐induced mechanical stretching and alignment of polymer chains under high electric fields promoting the formation of highly oriented crystalline phases [43, 44].

Thermal and Morphological Analysis

To assess the influence of filler incorporation on the thermal properties and crystallinity of the composite nanofibers, DSC analyses were performed, with their corresponding thermal parameters summarized in Table 1. The melting and cooling curves, depicted in Figure 3a–d, reveal that irrespective of MoS2 and WS2 content in PVDF, the endothermic and exothermic curves observed during melting and crystallization remain consistent with those of pristine PVDF. The cooling curves of PM and PW samples represented in Figure 3a,b respectively, indicate that the crystallization temperature (T x) of pure PVDF is 124.51°C, which increases to 134°C in PM15 and 133.77°C in PW20 due to the exothermic crystallization process. Similarly, the melting curves (Figure 3c,d) for all composites exhibit endothermic peaks comparable to that of PVDF, and as detailed in Table 1, pure PVDF has a melting temperature (T m) of 158.65°C, which increases to 159.14°C in PM15 and 160.13°C in PW20. This trend suggests that the incorporation of MoS2 and WS2 enhances T m in both composites, likely due to the nucleating effect of these nanofillers during crystallization [45]. The crystallinity fraction (χ c) (Equation S4) indicates an increase in crystallinity for both sets of composites compared to pure PVDF, with highest χ c values of 35.87% for PM15 and 35.21% for PW20. Based on the characterization results obtained so far, PM15 and PW20 samples demonstrated superior performance compared to their composites and showed significant improvement over pure PVDF, warranting further detailed characterization studies.

The surface morphology of the electrospun samples were analyzed using FESEM technique, where the FESEM image of the pure PVDF, depicted in Figure 1b, reveals an interconnected network of smooth, bead‐free nanofibers. Similarly, the morphological characteristics of PM15 and PW20, presented in Figure 3e,f, respectively, display a comparable arrangement of randomly oriented nanofibers. The successful incorporation of fillers is evidenced by the presence of small spherulitic structures embedded between the PVDF nanofibers. To confirm the distribution of these fillers, elemental mapping of PM15 and PW20 was conducted, elucidating the spatial distribution of key chemical elements within the composite nanofibers. As shown in Figure 3e,f, carbon (C) and fluorine (F) are uniformly dispersed across the nanofiber network, while the embedded spherulitic domains indicate the presence of molybdenum (Mo) and sulfur (S) in PM15 and tungsten (W) and sulfur (S) in PW20. Notably, the composites (PM15 and PW20) exhibit a reduced average nanofiber diameter compared to pristine PVDF, as confirmed by the diameter distribution analysis in Figure S7. This reduction can be a result of the increased charge density in the electrospinning solution upon the addition of fillers, which enhances the jet's stretching force relative to the pure PVDF [46]. Importantly, these nanofibers exhibit excellent flexibility (Figure S8), making them well‐suited for integration into microfluidic devices.

Compositional and Wettability Analysis

XPS analysis was conducted to examine the elemental composition and chemical states in PM15 and PW20. The narrow scan of C 1s, illustrated in Figure 4a,e for PM15 and PW20, respectively, exhibits characteristic peaks at binding energies of 286.6 and 291 eV, which are attributed to CH2 and CF2 functional groups. These peaks represent the dominant carbon species in PVDF and align with previous literature findings [47]. The deconvoluted F 1s spectra, shown in Figure 4b,f for PM15 and PW20, respectively, further elucidate the chemical bonding of fluorine, distinguishing semi‐ionic and covalent C–F interactions. Notably, the pronounced peak associated with semi‐ionic bonding suggests an enhanced EA phase fraction compared to the nonpolar α‐phase [48]. In PM15, the high‐resolution Mo 3d spectrum (Figure 4c) exhibits two distinct peaks at binding energies of 229.8 and 232.9 eV, corresponding to the Mo 3d5/2 and Mo 3d3/2 orbitals, respectively, which are indicative of the Mo4+ oxidation state present in MoS2 [49]. Additionally, a peak at 225.8 eV corresponds to the S 2s orbital, signifying the presence of divalent sulfur ions (S2−) in MoS2. The deconvoluted S 2p spectra for PM15 and PW20, displayed in Figure 4d,h reveal S 2p3/2 and S 2p1/2 peaks at 162.8 and 163.7 eV, respectively, further supporting the presence of sulfide species [50].

In contrast, the high‐resolution W 4f spectrum of PW20 (Figure 4g) confirms the W4+ oxidation state through deconvoluted peaks at binding energies of 33.5 and 34.1 eV, corresponding to the W 4f7/2 and W 4f5/2 states, respectively [51].

Furthermore, enhancing the hydrophobicity of nanofibers is essential not only for increasing surface roughness, which contributes to a larger surface area, but also for boosting the adsorption capacity of pollutants by providing more active adsorption sites [52]. As illustrated in Figure 4i–k, the water contact angle of pure PVDF nanofibers is ∼113°, which rises to about 141° in PM15 and 136° in PW20, indicating a significant improvement in hydrophobicity. In addition to this, Raman spectroscopy was employed to investigate the layer count of embedded nanoparticles, specifically for PM15 and PW20, as illustrated in Figure 4l. The Raman spectra of PM15 exhibited two distinct peaks at 380 and 405 cm−1, corresponding to the E2g 1 mode (associated with in‐plane vibrations) and the A1g mode (related to out‐of‐plane vibrations), confirming the formation of the MoS2 phase. The peak separation (Δ) between these vibrational modes is 26 cm−1, indicating lattice vibrations characteristic of bulk MoS2 [53]. Similarly, for PW20, the spectra exhibit two distinct peaks corresponding to E2g 1 and A1g modes, observed at 349 and 413 cm−1, respectively, with a separation (Δ = 64 cm−1) confirming the multilayered nature of WS2 [54]. Notably, bulk MoS2 and multilayered WS2 possesses an indirect bandgap, which will be analyzed in the subsequent section.

Optical Characteristics

Understanding the optical behavior of materials is essential for evaluating their light absorption capabilities, which directly influence charge carrier generation in catalytic applications. The absorbance spectra of PM and PW samples, shown in Figure 5a,b highlight prominent broad absorption features associated with pure MoS2 and pure WS2 in the 450–700 nm range. Upon incorporating nanofillers into the PVDF matrix, an increase in absorption intensity and a redshift in wavelength were observed, suggesting interfacial charge transfer between the components. The corresponding Tauc plots (Figure 5c,d) along with the calculated indirect bandgap values (Equation S5) of MoS2 decreased from 1.67 to ∼1.63 eV in PM15, while WS2 showed a reduction from ∼1.58 to 1.53 eV in PW20, with the comparative bandgap shown in Figure S9. This bandgap reduction upon composite formation aligns well with literature reports, where interfacial interactions facilitate charge transfer and tunable bandgap energies.

Piezoelectric Characteristics

A PENG was developed using electrospun nanofibers to evaluate their piezoelectric response. A 2 × 2 cm nanofiber sample was sandwiched between copper foil electrodes and tested under cyclic mechanical deformation (Figure S10). Under finger tapping, the PVDF‐based PENG showed a low open‐circuit voltage (V oc ∼ 3 V); however, the incorporation of MoS2 and WS2 significantly enhanced the output, with PM15 and PW20 achieving highest response of V oc ∼ 29 and ∼23 V, respectively (Figure 6a,b), which aligns with the FTIR results showing higher EA phase content. Foot tapping further improved the output to ∼39 V for PM15 and ∼36 V for PW20 (Figure 6c), with both devices demonstrating stable performance over 60 days under cyclic finger tapping (Figure 6d).

Degradation Studies

Effect of Microreactor Design

Two distinct microreactor configurations, as described in Section 2.6.1, were systematically investigated to assess the influence of reactor geometry on pollutant degradation efficiency. The primary objective was to recognize the optimal design for maximizing pollutant removal by enhancing mass transfer and piezocatalyst–pollutant interactions. The simulations enabled a comparative analysis of velocity distribution and vorticity magnitude in microreactors, both with and without micropillars. In both configurations, the computed cell Reynolds number was found to be less than 1 (R e ∼ 0.001), confirming that the flow operates within the creeping (Stokes) flow regime. Figure 7a,b represents the velocity magnitude distribution in the microfluidic devices without and with micropillars, respectively. The computed flow velocity remained largely uniform along the axial direction in reservoir configuration, with a volume average velocity of 3.6462 × 10−5 m/s. In contrast, the incorporation of micropillars disrupted the streamline symmetry, inducing complex flow patterns characterized by localized acceleration within constricted gaps and deceleration in recirculation zones [57, 58]. This structural change caused a slight increase in the volume average velocity to 1.1269 × 10−4 m/s. Similarly, the vorticity magnitude increased significantly in the presence of micropillars, while it was limited to 0.32 s−1 in the reservoir configuration. In contrast, the microreactor with micropillars exhibited values as high as 0.91 s−1 due to the formation of microvortices and recirculation zones generated in the presence of the micropillars, as shown in Figure 7c,d. These simulation results specify the influence of micropillars within the microfluidic device which induces localized acceleration, microvortices, and elevated vorticity magnitudes.

Velocity (a,b) and vorticity (c,d) magnitude distributions in microreactors without (a,c) and with (b,d) integrated micropillars, shown via slices and streamlines.

From the experimental studies, Figure S11a,b depicts the UV–Vis absorbance spectra corresponding to the degradation of RhB, with an absorbance peak at 552 nm, and CIP, with a peak at 271 nm, across the tested microreactor geometries. Among them, the alternating micropillar arrangement exhibited higher degradation efficiency for both pollutants. Prior studies have demonstrated that such reactor configurations not only increase the surface area‐to‐volume ratio but also improve flow dynamics, leading to more uniform mixing and enhanced degradation kinetics, through a synergistic enhancement of turbulence, mass transfer, and catalytic efficacy [59, 60]. Given these advantages and the experimental as well as computational findings obtained, we proceed with the alternating micropillar arrangement for all subsequent studies.

Therefore, the next phase of study focuses on optimizing the flow rate conditions for RhB and CIP, for which degradation experiments were conducted at varying flow rates of 25, 50, 100, 200, 400, and 800 µL/min. The highest degradation (Figure 8c,f) for both pollutants obtained at 50 µL/min, rather than 25 µL/min, stems from a balance between residence time, mass transfer, and piezocatalyst activity. Effect of Ultrasonic Transducer Frequency

At a controlled flow rate of 50 µL/min, RhB and CIP were introduced into an alternating‐micropillar configuration microreactor embedded with piezocatalyst membrane. The ultrasonic transducer positioned beneath the reactor was activated, generating frequencies of 0 (control experiment),15, 30, 45, and 60 kHz. The UV–Vis absorption spectra with their degradation efficiencies calculated from Equation (1), illustrated in Figure 8g–l, show that the highest degradation of both pollutants occurred at 60 kHz in all cases. Moreover, the degradation efficiency reveals a progressive increase in pollutant breakdown with increasing ultrasonic frequency, reaching a maximum at 60 kHz, which corresponds to the resonant frequency of the transducer. This enhanced degradation efficiency is attributed to multiple reasons. First, the ultrasonic actuation induces significant pressure fluctuations, promoting turbulence and acoustic cavitation microbubbles within the pollutant solution [61]. The rapid collapse of cavitation bubbles generates transient shock waves, intensifying molecular interactions and facilitating the breakdown of pollutants. Moreover, the continuous application of mechanical stimulation via ultrasonication sustains the piezoelectric potential of the piezocatalysts, ensuring consistent catalytic activity as the pollutant solution flows over them. These findings demonstrate that 60 kHz is the optimal operating frequency for maximizing degradation efficiency in this system.

Additionally, the total acoustic pressure distribution within the microfluidic device was analyzed through numerical simulations. From Figure S13, it can be understood that the micropillars act as obstacles that disrupt and redistribute the propagating acoustic waves, leading to the formation of localized pressure gradients around micropillars and thereby resulting in a more complex and heterogeneous pressure field.

Synergistic Effect of Piezophotocatalysis

Building on our previous findings, we have optimized the microreactor design by incorporating an alternating micropillar arrangement, with pollutant flowing at 50 µL/min and ultrasonic waves applied at 60 kHz. To facilitate pollutant adsorption, piezocatalysts were preconditioned in the dark for 30 min prior to reaction, and to evaluate the synergistic effects of piezo‐ and photocatalysis, pollutant degradation was investigated under three conditions: (i) visible light irradiation alone, (ii) ultrasonic waves alone, and (iii) their combined effect. The UV–Vis absorbance spectra (Figure 9a–d) indicate a substantial reduction in pollutant concentration (RhB and CIP) when both mechanisms were simultaneously applied, compared to individual treatments. Bar graphs (Figure 9e,f) quantify the degradation efficiencies calculated using Equation (1), for RhB and CIP, respectively, demonstrating superior performance under the synergistic effect of piezophotocatalysis. For RhB, PM15 achieved 96.73% degradation under the combined mechanism, in contrast to 53.54% with visible light alone and 73.20% with ultrasonic irradiation alone. Similarly, PW20 exhibited a degradation efficiency of 94.11%, compared to 49.36% and 66.07% under individual conditions. On the other hand, CIP followed a similar trend, with PM15 achieving 95.32% degradation under the synergistic effect, surpassing 66.04% under visible light alone and 77.81% with ultrasonic vibration alone. PW20 demonstrated 90.75% degradation, compared to 64.77% and 72.93% under individual conditions. The enhanced piezophotocatalytic performance of PM15 and PW20 can be attributed to several factors: (i) MoS2 and WS2 act as nucleating agents, increasing the EA phase of PVDF, thereby enhancing piezocatalytic activity; (ii) surface exposure of MoS2 and WS2 introduces additional catalytic active sites within the PVDF matrix; (iii) their incorporation induces local stress, amplifying nanofiber deformation and mechanical vibration transmission; and (iv) ultrasonic vibration sustains the piezoelectric potential and generates additional active sites, further enhancing catalytic performance. These properties collectively contribute to the superior degradation efficiency of water pollutants. A visual representation of RhB samples before and after degradation (Figure S14) illustrates a marked reduction in color intensity under synergistic conditions, indicative of effective degradation. In contrast, a similar approach was applied to CIP samples; however, since CIP is colorless, any potential color change in its degradation products could not be visually detected. Consequently, residual antibacterial activity assays were employed as an indirect approach to confirm effective CIP degradation (Figure S15). These findings demonstrate that the combined piezophotocatalytic mechanism significantly enhances pollutant degradation, outperforming their independent contributions. The degradation efficiencies of RhB and CIP were predicted using a machine learning approach to account for their dependence on key operational parameters such as flow rate, ultrasonic frequency, and the presence or absence of visible light. After evaluating several regression algorithms, multiple linear regression emerged as the most suitable model for estimating pollutant degradation using the piezocatalysts PM15 and PW20 individually. The model was trained using 66% of the available dataset, incorporating three input variables mentioned above, and the predictions were then compared with experimentally obtained values to assess its accuracy.

As illustrated in Figure 10a–d, the predicted degradation efficiencies closely match the experimental data for both RhB and CIP using PM15 and PW20, demonstrating strong predictive performance. The model's effectiveness is further validated by low root mean square (RMS) errors, indicating good agreement and minimal deviation. Despite the limited size of the training dataset, the model delivered reliable and consistent results, highlighting its potential as a practical tool for predicting degradation efficiencies in water treatment processes.

Reusability Studies

The stability of piezocatalysts was evaluated through piezophotocatalytic experiments conducted over five cycles in a microreactor. As illustrated in Figure 11, after five cycles, the degradation efficiency of RhB using PM15 declined marginally from 96.73% to 95.91% (a reduction of 0.82%), while PW20 exhibited a decrease from 94.11% to 93.16% (0.95% reduction). Similarly, CIP degradation with PM15 showed a minimal decline of 0.72% (from 95.32% to 94.60%), whereas PW20 exhibited a reduction from 90.75% to 89.84% (0.91% decrease). These results confirm the high stability of piezocatalysts, with minor efficiency losses likely due to residual byproducts on active sites. Additionally, FESEM and XRD analysis of the recovered catalysts after five cycles (Figures S16 and S17) revealed no significant morphological or structural alterations compared to the fresh samples, indicating that MoS2 and WS2 nanoparticles remained well‐embedded within the PVDF matrix. These findings underscore the excellent recyclability and structural integrity of PM15 and PW20 for the degradation of RhB and CIP.

Analysis of Underlying Mechanism of Piezophotocatalysis

Detection of Reactive Species

Radical trapping experiments were executed using various scavengers to detect the predominant ROS involved in pollutant degradation. Four distinct scavengers, AgNO3, EDTA, BQ, and IPA, were employed to selectively capture e−, h+,•O2 −, and •OH, respectively. As illustrated in Figure 12a,b, the introduction of these scavengers resulted in a substantial decrease in degradation efficiency, confirming their active involvement in the degradation reactions. Notably, the presence of AgNO3 led to a pronounced reduction in RhB degradation, suggesting that e− play a dominant part in degradation, when PM15 and PW20 function as piezocatalysts. Similarly, scavenger experiments conducted for CIP degradation, as revealed in Figure 12c,d, show that •O2 − primarily drive CIP degradation, followed by •OH. These findings collectively suggest that e− and •O2 − serve as the key reactive species in the degradation of RhB and CIP, respectively.

Piezophotocatalysis Mechanism

A potential mechanism for synergetic piezophotocatalytic degradation of organic pollutants, is illustrated in Figure 13a–d, derived from both literature findings and experimental results obtained in this study. This process integrates visible‐light‐driven photocatalysis, ultrasonic vibration, and piezoelectric activation, leading to enhanced degradation efficiency. Upon exposure to visible light, the piezocatalyst absorbs photons, which in turn generates e− h+ pairs within the catalyst material. Additionally, ultrasonic waves induce cavitation, leading to the formation and subsequent collapse of microbubbles. This collapse produces localized high‐temperature (>2000 K) and pressure zones, where dissolved oxygen molecules and vaporized water undergo excitation and dissociation. These processes facilitate the generation of •OH radicals that exhibit a crucial part in oxidative reactions (Equations (3) and (4)) [74, 75].

Schematic illustration of (a–d) piezocatalysis mechanism; (e) piezophotocatalytic degradation of pollutants using PM15 and PW20 as piezocatalysts.

Concurrently, ultrasonic waves also impart mechanical stress to the piezocatalyst, activating its intrinsic piezoelectric potential, thereby generating a polarized electric field with an opposite direction. This built‐in piezoelectric field (P z) induces charge carrier migration across the polarization field, driving e− and h+ toward CB and VB, respectively (Figure 13a). With increasing pressure, charge carrier redistribution reaches equilibrium, inducing simultaneous band bending in both CB and VB (Figure 13b). The extent of this bending is predominantly governed by the piezoelectric potential magnitude, which enhances e− and h+ separation, ultimately engaging in redox reactions with dissolved O2 and OH−, to generate ROS, and facilitating pollutant degradation [76]. At peak pressure, the accumulation of charge carriers on the catalyst surface disrupts the piezoelectric equilibrium, resulting in a decline in redox activity (Figure 13c). However, as pressure decreases, the system experiences a shift in piezoelectric equilibrium, reactivating the charge transfer process and promoting further redox reactions (Figure 13d) [77]. This cyclic process, governed by ultrasonic modulation of piezopotential, sustains the degradation of pollutants over repeated pressure oscillations.

Furthermore, under ultrasonic irradiation, the embedded MoS2 and WS2 nanoparticles undergo deformation within the polymer matrix because of the intense acoustic pressure generated via cavitation, contributing to improved catalytic performance. Also, the spontaneous polarization of MoS2/WS2 and PVDF induces polarization charges on opposite surfaces, thereby facilitating more efficient charge carrier transport to the catalyst surface, which, in turn, suppresses e− h+ recombination and enhances ROS generation [78]. In addition, a larger piezoelectric potential is generated in PM15 and PW20, than in pure PVDF, which not only induces more free charge carrier accumulation on the active sites of nanomaterials, but also on the nanofiber surface and their interfacial areas as well. Therefore, the observed superior degradation efficiency of PM15 and PW20 compared to PVDF stems from the optimized interplay between piezocatalysis and photocatalysis. As a result of these synergistic effects, an increased number of free charge carriers actively participate in degradation, giving rise to effective breaking of chemical bonds in target pollutants, as described by the degradation Equations (5)–(11). Consequently, the pollutants undergo progressive breakdown into intermediate species, which eventually mineralize into CO2 and H2O (Figure 13e) [79].

The present study represents a device‐level proof of concept, evaluated using single‐pollutant model solutions to elucidate the piezophotocatalytic mechanism under well‐controlled conditions, thereby providing the basis for future real wastewater degradation studies in complex water matrices and actual water bodies [80, 81, 82, 83, 84, 85].

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a microfluidic platform that integrates piezophotocatalysis with engineered microscale flow to achieve highly efficient and sustainable degradation of organic pollutants. A piezocatalyst membrane of electrospun PVDF nanofibers embedded with varying concentrations of MoS2 and WS2 nanoparticles was developed, and among the tested loadings, PM15 and PW20 were identified as optimal. These materials exhibited high EA phase content (∼82% and ∼77%), superior hydrophobicity (contact angles of ∼141° and ∼136°), enhanced crystallinity (35.87% and 35.21%), tunable bandgap (E g ∼ 1.63 and ∼1.53 eV) and excellent piezoelectric response (V oc ∼ 39 and ∼36 V). Collectively, these results establish PM15 and PW20 as highly effective piezocatalysts, justifying their selection for integration into the microreactor for degradation studies. The optimized piezocatalysts were embedded into the PMMA microreactor bed, which was fabricated via laser micromachining and featured an alternating micropillar design to enhance turbulence and mass transfer. COMSOL simulations confirmed that this geometry promoted uniform flow distribution and vortex formation, ensuring efficient contact between the pollutants and the piezocatalyst membrane. Under optimized conditions of 50 µL/min flow rate, 4.2 min residence time, and integrated 60 kHz ultrasonic vibrations, the synergistic piezophotocatalytic effect enabled high degradation efficiencies: 96.7% for RhB and 95.3% for CIP with PM15 and 94.0% for RhB and 90.7% for CIP with PW20. The synergy between the engineered microscale flow and piezocatalyst electromechanical activation enhanced reactive species formation, driving rapid and highly efficient pollutant degradation. Also, a multiple linear regression model demonstrated high predictive accuracy for pollutant degradation efficiencies, with low RMS error, highlighting its utility for data‐driven optimization in water treatment. Furthermore, the recyclability tests confirmed superior stability of piezocatalysts over five consecutive cycles, while scavenger experiments revealed that e− and •O2 − were the dominant reactive species in RhB and CIP, respectively, driving the degradation process. This study highlights the remarkable potential of piezophotocatalysis in microfluidic systems, offering a highly efficient and sustainable process for the expeditious reduction of organic pollutants. Moreover, it opens new avenues for optimizing microreactor geometries integrated with advanced piezocatalysts, driving significant improvements in water remediation technologies.
