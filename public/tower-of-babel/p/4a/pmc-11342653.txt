Musculoskeletal disorders among taxi drivers: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ehsan Rezaei, Fatemeh Shahmahmoudi, Faezeh Makki, Fatemeh Salehinejad, Hassan Marzban, Zahra Zangiabadi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTaxi drivers, as professional drivers, encounter numerous ergonomic risk factors related to musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) because of the demands of their jobs. This study conducted as a systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to explore the prevalence of MSDs among taxi drivers.Materials and methodsThe present study followed the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, and its protocol was registered in the international prospective register of systematic review (PROSPERO) under the code CRD42024509258. Searches were carried out using various databases, such as PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Science Direct, SID, ISC, and Google Scholar, with no time restrictions until February 7th, 2024. A random effects model was utilized for meta-analysis, and the I2 index was employed to assess heterogeneity among studies. Lastly, data analysis was conducted using STATA software (version 14).ResultsAfter the initial search, 1606 articles were extracted from the reviewed sources. Following screening, study selection, and quality evaluation, a total of 11 studies were chosen for meta-analysis, involving 5277 taxi drivers. Based on the results of the meta-analysis, the highest prevalence of MSDs among taxi drivers was related to the lower back region (53.87% (95% CI:40.89–66.84, I2= 98.7%, P < 0.001)). Additionally, the prevalence rates of MSDs in different body regions, such as the neck (38.15%), shoulder (34.97%), upper back (18.30%), and knee (14.10%), were also reported.ConclusionBased on the findings of this study, the prevalence of MSDs among taxi drivers is relatively high, and specific risk factors may contribute to the development of these disorders. Therefore, to prevent the occurrence of MSDs among taxi drivers, it is advisable to implement essential measures concerning the development of training programs, ergonomic interventions, and evaluation of the work environment.Keywords: Musculoskeletal disorders, Taxi drivers, Risk factors, Prevention, Ergonomics

Introduction

Musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) are a significant global public health issue that impacts quality of life, leads to injuries among the workforce, and results in economic losses due to absenteeism and medical costs [1, 2].

Professional drivers, such as truck, taxi, and bus drivers, are individuals whose occupation involves driving vehicles for business and livelihood purposes [3]. These individuals often encounter adverse conditions like traffic congestion, continuous time pressure, excessive physical demands, etc., which can impact their health and make them vulnerable to work-related musculoskeletal disorders (WMSDs) [4]. In fact, WMSDs in professional drivers are linked to various ergonomic risk factors. Long-term sitting, whole-body vibration, and the mismatch of anthropometric measurements of drivers with the physical environment of the driver cabin are among the key physical risk factors identified. In addition, concerning influential individual factors, we can include age, gender, weight, height, body mass index, and the general health status of drivers [5]. Stress and mental-psychological tensions in the work environment, including anger, anxiety, and time pressure, as psychosocial factors, can elevate the risk of WMSDs among professional drivers [6, 7].

The taxi industry, as one of the important service jobs in the transportation of people, faces numerous occupational health challenges [8]. The majority of taxi drivers work long hours, from early morning until midnight, spending extensive time on the streets and roads each day [9]. Due to the demands of their profession, they encounter various ergonomic risk factors, such as prolonged sitting, awkward postures, repetitive motions, vehicle vibrations, noise, heavy lifting, and frequent handling of luggage [6]. There is also evidence that professional taxi drivers, especially those who drive in the city, are at an increased risk of MSDs due to the relatively limited space in the taxi cab as well as the long hours spent behind the wheel [10]. Additionally, it should be considered that the health of taxi drivers is directly related to the health and safety of passengers, pedestrians, and other people [8].

The fact that professional driving is associated with MSDs has been demonstrated through epidemiological studies among drivers of various vehicle types. The study by Chen et al. revealed that the overall prevalence of MSDs among bus drivers during one year was 78.3%, with the highest prevalence observed in the neck, shoulders, and lower back regions [11]. In some studies, researchers have stated that truck drivers who spend extended periods in a seated position experience increased stress and discomfort, leading to injuries and the occurrence of MSDs [12, 13]. Another study reported that professional drivers are vulnerable to MSDs due to prolonged sitting and exposure to whole-body vibrations [14, 15].

Surveys indicate that due to the importance of MSDs among different occupational groups, including professional drivers, several studies have been conducted on the prevalence of MSDs among different groups of professional drivers. However, to our knowledge, a comprehensive study that has examined the overall prevalence of MSDs and their types among taxi drivers was not found. Hence, as it appears essential to tackle the problem of MSDs, the present study opted to carry out a systematic review and meta-analysis in this field. Additionally, by adding the available information on MSDs among taxi drivers, a valuable resource can be provided for managers of the occupational health field to enhance interventions and ergonomic training effectively, aiming to reduce the prevalence of MSDs in this occupational group. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the prevalence of MSDs among taxi drivers.

Materials and methods

In the present study, the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines were used to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis [16]. The protocol of this study was registered in the international prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO) with the code CRD42024509258. In the next step, the search strategy, screening, study selection, quality assessment, and data extraction stages were conducted in accordance with the PRISMA protocol. Two researchers independently carried out the study selection, qualitative evaluation, and data extraction steps, and any disagreements were resolved through group discussion.

Information sources and search strategy

In this study, to search for and extract relevant studies, information sources such as PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Science Direct, Iranian Scientific Information Database (SID), Islamic World Science Citation Center (ISC), Google Scholar, conference and congress articles, as well as the bibliography of selected articles and systematic review studies, were utilized. To obtain valid keywords, Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) terms, and keywords from related articles and consultation with scientific experts were employed.

For all types of databases, search strategies were developed through the following valid keywords: “Taxi drivers*”, “Professional taxi drivers*”, “Automobile driving*”, “Professional drivers*”, “Musculoskeletal disorder*”, “MSDs”, “Musculoskeletal complaint*”, “Work related Musculoskeletal disorder*”, “WRMSDs”, “Musculoskeletal problem*”, “Dysfunction*”, “Hand pain*”, “Musculoskeletal pain”, “Musculoskeletal symptom*”, “Arthritis joint*”, “Muscle strain*”, “Muscle problem*”, “Shoulder pain*”, “Musculoskeletal disease*”, “Neck pain*”, “Back pain*”, “Arthritis bone*”, and “Elbow pain*”.

In addition, search fields and operators were used to combine the keywords. The search period was considered without a time limit until February 7th, 2024. Table 1 presents three main databases along with search strategies.

Inclusion criteria

The criteria for inclusion were determined using the PECO (Population, Exposure, Comparison, Outcome) framework, which stands for population (taxi drivers), exposure (driving a vehicle), comparison (Any), and outcome (MSDs). As a result, in the present study, all research works that recorded the prevalence of MSD among taxi drivers were included without a time limit until February 7th, 2024.

Exclusion criteria

Based on the exclusion criteria, review studies, case reports, intervention studies, non-English papers, grey literature, letters to the editor, and reports on the prevalence of musculoskeletal injuries caused by accidents were excluded from the study.

Study selection

After the search was conducted, all articles were imported into EndNote X7 to manage the search results. Duplicate articles were removed, and the titles and abstracts of the remaining articles were screened according to the inclusion criteria to identify potentially related articles. Subsequently, two researchers independently examined the full text of the primary potentially relevant articles in detail. Finally, articles were selected based on the inclusion criteria.

Quality assessment and data extraction

At this step, two researchers independently assessed the chosen studies. They utilized the Appraisal tool for Cross-Sectional Studies (AXIS) [17], which provides a score ranging from 0 to 20. Based on the scale of AXIS, articles scoring 12 or higher were chosen for meta-analysis. Then, two researchers independently analyzed the data, taking into account various characteristics of each study such as the first author’s name, sample size, the mean age of the study population, tools used, number of male and female participants, and the prevalence of MSDs and their types in each study. Subsequently, the extracted features were entered into a checklist that had been prepared beforehand.

Statistical analysis

Binomial distribution was utilized to compute the variance of each study, and a weighted average was employed to combine the prevalence of MSDs from various studies. It is important to highlight that each study’s weighting was determined based on its inverse variance. A simple random effects model was applied for the meta-analysis. To assess the level of heterogeneity among studies, the I2 index was employed, and heterogeneity levels were categorized into the following ranges: less than 25% (no heterogeneity), 25–50% (medium heterogeneity), 50–75% (high heterogeneity), and above 75% (very high heterogeneity) [18]. Begg’s test was conducted to examine publication bias. Finally, the data analysis for the present study was performed using STATA software (version 14).

Results

Systematic review results

By conducting the primary search in the databases, 1606 articles were extracted. Subsequently, 1465 articles were screened by removing duplicate studies. After completing the screening stage of the primary studies, 39 studies advanced to a more detailed full-text review, from which 11 studies were chosen. Then these 11 studies were qualitatively evaluated by the researchers using the Appraisal tool for Cross-Sectional Studies (AXIS) and all of them scored above 12 and finally entered the meta-analysis stage (Fig. 1). It should be noted that the AXIS tool has a score between 0 and 20 and studies scoring 12 or higher enter the meta-analysis stage [19, 20]. The present study examined the prevalence of MSDs among 5277 taxi drivers. Out of the 11 studies, 1 reported the overall prevalence of MSDs with a study sample of 151 individuals. Meta-analysis results

Table 3 presents the results of the subgroup analysis examining the prevalence of MSDs in various body regions. Based on these findings, I2 values are very high in all five investigated regions. In addition, according to the results of Begg’s test, the publication bias in the prevalence of MSDs by different body regions among taxi drivers for all five regions was insignificant. The results of the meta-analysis also showed that the highest prevalence of MSDs was observed in the lower back at a rate of 53.87% (95% CI: 40.89–66.84, I2= 98.7%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2), while the lowest rate was associated with the knee at 14.10% (95% CI: 23.45–4.76, I2= 97.2%, P < 0.001).

Discussion

In the present research, 11 studies were chosen for meta-analysis. Based on the results of the meta-analysis, the prevalence rates of MSDs for different body regions including lower back (53.87%), neck (38.15%), shoulder (34.97%), upper back (18.30%), and knee (14.10%) were reported. Among these, the highest prevalence of MSDs was found in the lower back region.

In the context of investigating the prevalence of MSDs, numerous studies have been carried out among sedentary occupations. One such study is the review conducted by Joseph et al., which indicated that the prevalence of MSDs among professional drivers ranged from 43.1 to 93%. The study also highlighted that the most commonly affected body regions were the lower back (53%), followed by the neck, upper back, and shoulder [32]. The findings of a review study indicated a high prevalence of MSDs among bus drivers in various countries, with low back pain identified as the most prevalent musculoskeletal symptom [33]. In another review study focusing on the relationship between MSDs and sedentary activities, Guduru et al. demonstrated that the prevalence of MSDs among bank employees varies from 60 to 80%. In fact, individuals working in office settings are more prone to MSDs, particularly in the upper limbs, neck, and lower back, as a result of prolonged sedentary work [34]. Examining the results of studies conducted among similar occupational groups shows that occupations with sedentary behaviors are at risk of MSDs, consonant with the results of the present study.

In addition, the prevalence of MSDs among other occupational groups has also been examined. In a review conducted among firefighters, the overall prevalence of MSDs was 46.39%, and the highest prevalence of MSDs was related to the lower back region (34.32%) [35]. In a study focusing on the prevalence of MSDs among nurses, Sun et al. documented that the annual prevalence of MSDs was 77.2%, with the highest prevalence in the lower back (59.5%), neck (53%) and shoulders (46.8%) [36]. In another meta-analysis study carried out by researchers among employees in the automobile manufacturing industry in China, the 12-month prevalence rate of WMSDs among employees was 56.1%, with the lower back region showing the highest prevalence rate at 36.5% [37]. Based on the findings of the studies mentioned, the highest prevalence of MSDs is associated with the lower back region, aligning with the findings of this study. Although the prevalence of MSDs in the lower back region among the population examined in the present study is higher than that among firefighters and automobile manufacturing workers; however, there is not much difference compared to the prevalence of these disorders among nurses.

To reduce the occurrence of MSDs among professional drivers, it is crucial to accurately comprehend and identify the risk factors responsible for th78ese disorders. Previous studies have documented several factors in this regard. A group of researchers stated that driving itself imposes a significant physical load. Additionally, sustaining a body position while driving for a long time can result in physical injuries [38, 39], such as muscle tension in the neck, back, shoulders, and arms, leading to local muscle fatigue and pain [39]. Prolonged sitting and long working hours predispose drivers to awkward postures, which in turn can be influenced by ergonomic inconsistencies in the vehicle’s physical elements, such as seat, leading to biomechanical stresses, especially on the spine [38–43]. Body vibration is another factor that can cause MSDs among drivers [44]. Some studies have reported prolonged exposure to whole-body vibrations, rapid and repetitive movements, and prolonged sedentary lifestyles as exacerbating factors for MSDs [38, 45]. Other effective factors, such as high traffic congestion, poor quality of streets and roads, increased job demands, and time constraints, can also elevate the likelihood of occupational injuries among drivers [39, 46]. In addition to physical ergonomic risk factors, behaviors like smoking and stimulant use as individual risk factors, stress and violence as psychosocial risk factors, and sleep disorders can heighten the risk of MSDs among taxi drivers [10, 47]. The impact of psychosocial factors on the driver’s skeletal-muscular system may involve increased muscle tension during acute and chronic stress, potentially altering work performance. For instance, this heightened tension can compel the driver to exert more force on the steering wheel when driving or when maintaining an awkward posture. In addition, psychosocial factors in the workplace, like a hostile or stressful relationship between a manager and a taxi driver, can compel individuals to accelerate their work pace. This acceleration can result in more rapid and repetitive movements during work, reducing the frequency of short breaks and muscle recovery time, and potentially resulting in soft tissue damage [24].

After all, taxis play an important role in the urban transportation system and are typically accessible to the public almost anywhere, any day of the year [10]. A relatively high prevalence of MSDs can lead to early disability of taxi drivers. Therefore, to maintain the health of the musculoskeletal system and prevent musculoskeletal injuries among this occupational group, as well as to ensure sufficient ability to perform work, periodic screening programs for taxi drivers regarding MSDs and the evaluation of the ergonomic risk factors of their jobs are recommended. In addition, the design of corrective measures such as ergonomic interventions and related training programs can be useful in this regard [48]. These measures are ergonomic interventions based on teaching correct sitting behavior to drivers and corrective exercises, which have been effective in many occupational groups to reduce MSDs [49]. Therefore, the use of these two low-cost and common ergonomic interventions may also be effective in reducing MSDs among taxi drivers. Of course, for the implementation of ergonomic interventions, it is recommended that in future studies, comprehensive systematic review studies be conducted to identify the ergonomic risk factors associated with the occupation of taxi drivers. The results of the present study may have an impact by providing information on the MSDs experienced by taxi drivers. This information can enhance the understanding and awareness of managers and professionals, enabling them to develop and execute preventive programs to protect this occupational group from musculoskeletal injuries.

Limitations

The presence of heterogeneity among the studies was a limitation of this study, and factors such as sample size, tools, and varying cut-off points in the original studies could account for this heterogeneity. Also, in this study, it was not possible to report the overall prevalence of MSDs and the prevalence of MSDs separately for men and female because the original studies did not provide enough data in these fields. Another limitation of the study was the impossibility of performing subgroup analysis based on tools due to the small number of tools used to examine MSDs.

Conclusion

The findings of the present study indicate a relatively high risk of developing MSDs among taxi drivers, with the highest prevalence associated with the lower back region (53.87%). The high prevalence of MSDs may lead to significant health issues and financial damages, resulting in workforce disability, increased compensation payments, and reduced productivity. Therefore, in order to prevent the occurrence of these disorders, it is recommended to implement measures such as periodic screening for MSDs, ergonomic evaluations, design and implementing training programs and ergonomic interventions. These actions can be done in the field of correct postural behavior training while driving, appropriate corrective exercises and creating an ergonomic work environment, including improving the ergonomic level of seats, cabin space, steering wheel and other factors. In addition, policymakers, health professionals, drivers, and other stakeholders should also collaborate in combating MSDs among this population. The implementation of these measures may be effective in reducing the prevalence of MSDs among taxi drivers.
