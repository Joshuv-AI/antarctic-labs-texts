Safety of Mechanical Thrombectomy in Obese Patients: Insights From a Single-Center Retrospective Analysis
Authors: Lidiya V Sul, Akiva Rosenzveig, Pulkit Chaudhury, Ihab Haddadin, Aravinda Nanjundappa, Leben Tefera
Published: 2024

Pulmonary embolism (PE) ranks as the third leading cause of cardiovascular death in the United States.1 Obesity is an independent significant risk factor for venous thromboembolism.2 Catheter-directed therapies are increasingly used for intermediate-risk and high-risk PE management. However, concerns arise regarding vascular access in obese patients, particularly in large-bore catheter-directed thrombectomies. Femoral access in obese patients has been associated with increased vascular complications in coronary artery catheterization, including need for transfusion, surgical repair, retroperitoneal bleed, large hematoma, pseudoaneurysm, and arteriovenous fistula.3 This study aimed to investigate the safety of mechanical thrombectomy specifically in patients with obesity.

This retrospective study analyzed procedural data and outcomes of patients with a body mass index (BMI) of >35 kg/m2 who underwent mechanical thrombectomy with a large-bore aspiration device for PE between 2020 and 2024. Procedural data were extracted from medical records (Table 1). We identified outcome data about access site complications, hospital stay duration, and major bleeding events within 7 days postprocedure. We defined major bleeding as bleeding events in which bleeding was accompanied by a decrease in the hemoglobin level of at least 2 g/dL, a transfusion of at least 2 units of packed red cells was required, bleeding occurred at a critical site (intracranial, intraocular, intraspinal, intra-articular, intramuscular with compartment syndrome, pericardial, retroperitoneal), or resulted in death within 4 months.

The study included 64 patients with a mean age of 54.9 years and a BMI ranging from 35.0 to 72.0 kg/m2. Four patients were excluded as they were on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation or transitioned to extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, therefore confounding access site complications.

Most cases of PE were provoked (80.0%), with the most common provocation type being cancer (30.0%), followed by surgery (25.0%). Proximal deep vein thrombosis was observed in 64.5% of cases. Right ventricular dysfunction was present in most cases, detected by both computed tomography (90.0%) and echocardiography (80.0%). Large-bore aspiration devices were used for all thrombectomies performed. Most procedures used a 24F sheath size (95.0%), with the remainder using a 26F sheath (5.0%). The right common femoral vein was the primary access site (91.7%), and a purse-string suture closure was the most common method used (85.0%).

The average length of hospital stay was 13.6 days. Major bleeding events within 7 days postprocedure occurred in 10.0% of patients, which included vaginal bleeding, central line site bleeding, as well as a rectus sheath and epidural hematoma. One patient had a right anterior thigh hematoma that was present on admission from a mechanical fall. Adverse events related to the procedure included groin hematoma (3.3%) and access site bleed (1.7%), with an average BMI for these patients being 37.3 kg/m2 (35.5-41.4 kg/m2). In all cases, successful aspiration of pulmonary emboli was performed. Successful aspiration was defined as the removal of thrombus with subsequent improvement in hemodynamic parameters, including blood pressure, heart rate, and pulmonary artery pressures.

This study provides evidence to support the safety of large-bore catheter-directed thrombectomy in obese patients with PE. Despite concerns with achieving hemostasis from femoral access in this population, our findings demonstrate that catheter-directed thrombectomy can be performed successfully with high procedural success and low incidence of access site complications in a real-world population. There are limitations to our work. This is a single-center retrospective study, which introduces inherent bias by eliminating variations in procedural techniques and operator experience across different institutions. Furthermore, this is a relatively small sample size of obese patients and may limit our ability to detect rare or uncommon access site complications. Finally, we were unable to directly compare outcomes with those of nonobese patients to observe variance. However, previous studies have noted that vascular access complications were highest in patients who were extremely thin or morbidly obese and lowest in patients with normal BMI and in those with modest obesity.3 Overall, these results underscore the importance of considering large-bore catheter-directed thrombectomy as a safe and viable option in obese patients with PE deemed to need intervention.
