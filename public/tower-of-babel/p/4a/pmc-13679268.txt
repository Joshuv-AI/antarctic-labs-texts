Breastfeeding shapes a unique tumor-immune landscape in pregnancy-associated breast cancer from GEICAM-EMBARCAM study
Authors: Regina Peña-Enríquez, Ángel Guerrero-Zotano, Begoña Bermejo, Alejandra Díaz-Chacon, Yolanda Jerez Gilarranz, Jose J Ponce Lorenzo, Juan Miguel Cejalvo, Ana Santaballa Bertran, Elena Galve-Calvo, Antonio Fernández Aramburo, Blanca Cantos Sánchez de Ibargüen, Antonio Anton-Torres, Fernando Moreno, María Helena Lopez-Ceballos, Yolanda Fernández, Susana De La Cruz, María Eva Pérez, Isabel Blancas, María Vidal-Losada, Manuel Ruiz-Borrego, Álvaro Jiménez-Arranz, David Ponferrada, Rocío Bautista-Moreno, Ana Martínez-López, María López-Herrero, Aurora Rivas-Crespo, Raul Rincón, Rosalía Caballero, Miguel Martín, Silvia Guil-Luna, Juan de la Haba-Rodríguez

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPregnancy-associated breast cancer (PABC) is an aggressive malignancy affecting young women during gestation, lactation, or the post-weaning period. Despite its clinical significance and poor prognosis, how these physiological stages shape tumor biology remains poorly understood. Here, we integrate reproductive context with molecular and immune profiling to define shared and stage-specific features of PABC and uncover potential therapeutic vulnerabilities.MethodsWe conducted BC360 NanoString gene expression analysis and CIBERSORTx immune deconvolution in 106 breast cancer cases, including 57 PABC patients stratified by diagnosis stage (gestation, breastfeeding, or post-weaning) and 49 non-PABC controls.ResultsOur study identified a shared aggressive signature across all PABC cases, marked by increased proliferation and impaired DNA repair. Notably, breastfeeding patients (PABC-BF) emerged as a distinct subset, displaying an immune-inflamed tumor profile characterized by elevated inflammatory chemokines, enriched CD8⁺ T-cell and regulatory T-cell infiltration, and prominent TIGIT inhibitory checkpoint upregulation.ConclusionsThese findings reveal lactation as a physiological framework that shapes tumor immunity, defining a distinct tumor-immune landscape which warrants further investigation for potential therapeutic approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13058-026-02330-8.Keywords: Pregnancy-associated breast cancer, Postpartum, Breastfeeding, Immune infiltration, Tumor microenvironment

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most common malignancy among young women of reproductive age and its rising incidence underscores growing concern over pregnancy-associated breast cancer (PABC), considered a distinct aggressive clinical entity with a poor prognosis and a heightened metastasis risk [1, 2]. PABC affects a particularly vulnerable population—women who are either pregnant, breastfeeding, or within the first-year after childbirth—and poses a significant challenge due to its aggressive nature and complex management [3, 4]. Notably, PABC is frequently detected at a more advanced stage with more unfavorable tumor characteristics, including higher tumor grade, lymphovascular invasion, and hormone receptor negativity [5]. Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)—widely recognized for its worse outcomes and limited therapeutic options—is disproportionately prevalent among PABC cases [6]. In our previous work, we identified PABC as a distinct molecular and clinical entity with higher prevalence of basal-like tumors and unique gene expression patterns that reflect a more aggressive biology compared to non-PABC [7, 8]. Although PABC is considered a high-risk subset of BC among young women, no specific treatment guidelines are currently available for those diagnosed while pregnant or postpartum. Indeed, therapeutic options are limited or delayed ensuring fetal safety, and can also lead to potential complications, problems with breastfeeding and concerns about future fertility [9]. Despite its relevance, the biological mechanisms driving PABC remain poorly understood, highlighting the need for further research into their molecular basis to improve detection, treatment, and prognosis.

Currently, cancer is increasingly understood as a complex and dynamic ecosystem, where the tumor microenvironment is shaped by systemic host-related factors such as hormones, metabolism, or immune status [10]. This holistic view highlights how broader physiological contexts can influence tumor progression and immune responses. In this regard, the mammary gland undergoes drastic physiological changes during the pregnancy/lactation/involution cycle driven by hormonal fluctuations, tissue remodelling, and immune system modulation [11–13]. During gestation, a tolerogenic immune state and hormonally-stimulated expansion of the mammary epithelium may promote tumor immune evasion and proliferation. In contrast, the postpartum involution process by which the breast return to a non-lactating state—characterized by epithelial apoptosis, immune cell infiltration and extracellular matrix remodelling— has been proposed to create a transient, pro-inflammatory wound‑healing‑like microenvironment that may contribute to tumor initiation or progression [14]. However, the specific biological impact of active lactation, often overlooked in favor of studies focused on post-lactational involution, remains largely unexplored. To date, most studies have grouped PABC cases together, regardless of reproductive stage timing, thus limiting insights into potential biological distinctions among tumors diagnosed during gestation, lactation or the post-weaning period.

To address this gap, within the framework of the GEICAM-EMBARCAM population-based registry study, this translational analysis aims to provide novel insights into the complex interplay between these temporal windows and BC. We conducted a comprehensive analysis of PABC, integrating high-throughput BC360 NanoString gene expression with CIBERSORTx immune deconvolution analyses. By capturing the full spectrum of PABC and its distinct physiologically phases (gestation, breastfeeding and post-weaning), this study seeks to uncover overall and stage-specific molecular and immune features of PABC, ultimately contributing to a better understanding of the disease and potential clinical implications.

Methods

Patients and clinical samples

A total of 106 women with primary invasive BC were included in this translational analysis (Fig. 1) conducted within the ambispective, observational and multicentre Registry Study of Pregnancy and Breast Cancer (GEICAM/2017–07 EMBARCAM: NCT04603820) [7]. The PABC patient cohort comprised 57 cases from the GEICAM-EMBARCAM and GEICAM/2012–03 studies, classified as: (i) PABC-GS, if diagnosed during gestation; (ii) PABC-BF, during breastfeeding, (iii) PABC-PW, post-weaning (never breastfed or after cessation of lactation) in the first-year postpartum. The control cohort consisted of 49 non-PABC cases from the EpiGEICAM study [15, 16] comprising young women diagnosed with BC unrelated to recent pregnancy, with comparable clinical data. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumors specimens from core needle biopsies or surgically resected primary breast carcinomas were selected for subsequent analysis. Clinicopathological data were collected. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

The control cohort consisted of non-PABC cases: young women diagnosed with BC outside the context of recent pregnancy

Clinical data analysis

Statistical analysis of the clinicopathological data was performed using SPSS v.28. Normality was evaluated with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test; and continuous variables using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test. All statistical tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Tumor RNA isolation and Nanostring BC360 profiling

Gene expression profiling was performed using the nCounter® Analysis System with the BC360™ panel (NanoString), which targets 776 BC-related genes, including 18 housekeeping and 38 predefined gene signatures that reflect tumor biology, immune activity, and tumor microenvironment; ranging from single-gene proxies (e.g., TIGIT, IDO1) to composite multi-gene signatures (e.g., TIS, HRD, BRCAness). RNA was extracted from FFPE samples using the RNeasy FFPE Isolation Kit (Qiagen). RNA purity and quality were assessed with NanoDrop 1000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher) and 4200 TapeStation system (Agilent). For each sample, 150–500 ng RNA was hybridized to panel probes, and digital transcript counts were obtained. All samples passed quality control (QC), ensuring the technical reliability of the nCounter profiling assay. Data were normalized to correct cartridge-specific variability and log2-transformed.

NanoString differential expression analysis

Differential expression analyses at gene and signature levels were conducted using the limma package in R. Linear models were fitted using expression values or signature scores as the dependent variables and study groups as fixed effects. P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Yekutieli false discovery rate (FDR) method. PAM50 intrinsic subtypes (Luminal A, Luminal B, HER2 + , Basal-like) were assigned based on correlation to established centroids [17]. For analyses focus on the PABC-BF subgroup, linear models were additionally adjusted for PAM50 intrinsic subtype as a covariate (Score ~ Subgroup + PAM50 subtype). Adjusted estimated marginal means were calculated using the emmeans package in R (version 4.5.1). In addition, subtype-stratified comparisons were also performed using Student’s t-tests with FDR correction for multiple testing. Genomic risk scores were calculated based on a combination of subtype correlations and a proliferation-related signatures and normalised to a 0–100 scale, reflecting tumor aggressiveness according to genomic features.

Immune cell deconvolution analysis

The relative abundance of immune cell populations was estimated from NanoString gene expression profiles using CIBERSORT (Cell-type Identification by Estimating Relative Subsets of RNA Transcripts), a deconvolution-based algorithm for inferring immune cell composition from bulk transcriptomic data [18]. The LM22 leukocyte signature matrix was applied to quantify 22 immune cell subsets. Analyses were performed with 100 permutations, quantile normalization disabled, and a significance threshold of p ≤ 0.05.

Integrated public database analyses

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were constructed using the STRING database with confidence network edges and we set the strength of the interactions at > 0.4 as the threshold for analysis [19]. Cancer Hallmarks enrichment analysis was performed using the online tool that compares the input gene list against a predefined set of 6.763 cancer-related genes to determine overrepresentation of cancer hallmarks associated with PABC [20]. The top3 upregulated genes in PABC (CCNA2, DEPDC1 and CDC20) were further validated using public available datasets: Gene expression across normal breast tissue, primary tumors and metastatic lesions was explored using the TNMplot platform with Kruskal–Wallis statistical testing [21]. Survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan–Meier Plotter platform for overall survival (OS), recurrence-free survival (RFS), and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS). Clinical endpoints were defined according to STEEP v2.0 criteria, and survival differences between high- and low-expression groups were assessed using log-rank tests with hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) [22, 23].

Results

Patient cohort characteristics by reproductive stage

A total of 106 BC cases were included in this study, comprising 49 non-PABC controls and 57 PABC patients, with 21 diagnosed during gestation (PABC-GS), 21 breastfeeding (PABC-BF), and 15 post-weaning (PABC-PW) (Fig. 1). Clinicopathological characteristics for groups are detailed in Table S1. The median age at diagnosis was lower in PABC patients (35 years) than in non-PABC (41 years; p < 0.001), indicating earlier onset related to recent pregnancy. Notably, PABC patients showed a higher incidence of breast and/or ovarian cancer family history compared with non-PABC (46.9% vs 26.5%, p = 0.064), along with an elevated frequency of germline BRCA1/2 mutations in PABC (28% in PABC-GS, 42% in PABC-BF, and 66% in PABC-PW). Regarding tumor characteristics, the majority were invasive ductal carcinoma, predominantly poorly differentiated T1-2 tumors, with metastases being uncommon in both groups. Significantly, PAM50 classification demonstrated a clear predominance of basal-like tumors in PABC (40.4%); whereas luminal A in non-PABC (38.8%; p = 0.017).

Shared proliferative and genomic instability programs across PABC windows

As contextual background, we first compared PABC with non-PABC tumors in this extended cohort of 106 patients profiled with the Nanostring BC360 panel to establish a reference framework for subsequent subgroup analyses. The complete list of DEGs for each comparision is provided in Additional file 1.

This global comparison identified 221 DEGs, with 102 upregulated and 119 downregulated genes in PABC (p ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 2A). Consistent with our prior report, the most significantly upregulated genes were involved in cell cycle progression and mitotic regulation, including CCNA2, CDC20, and DEPDC1 (Fig. 2A), reflecting potential dysregulation of proliferative control in PABC. Whereas most significantly downregulated genes included DDB2 and MAP2K4, were implicated in DNA damage response and MAPK signaling, as well as IL4R, a mediator of type 2 immune signaling. Cancer Hallmarks enrichment analysis further supported these findings, revealing overrepresentation of pathways related to genome instability (OR = 10.6), replicative immortality (OR = 4.98), and resistance to cell death (OR = 3.43; adj p < 0.001) in PABC (Fig. 2B).

To further assess the clinical relevance of the most consistently upregulated genes in PABC, we performed in silico validation of CCNA2, DEPDC1 and CDC20 using public datasets. TNMplot analysis showed increased expression of these genes in primary breast tumors and metastatic tissues relative to normal breast tissue, and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed a significantly association between high gene expression and poorer OS, RFS and DMFS in BC patients. Particularly, CDC20 demonstrated the clearest stratification of survival curves, indicating its relevance as potential prognostic biomarker (Fig. 2C).

Pathway analysis based on NanoString B360 signatures revealed that PABC is characterized by a significant enrichment in proliferation-related pathways and genomic instability including p53 signaling, homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), and BRCAness (FC ≥ 1.5, adj p < 0.05; Fig. 3A); regardless of molecular subtype or BRCA status (Figure S1). Conversely, regulatory signatures such as apoptosis and TGF-β signaling were consistently downregulated, underscoring a shared aggressive transcriptomic core across all PABC cases (Fig. 3A, B). However, subgroup-specific analysis identified a significant enrichment of several immune-related signatures in PABC-BF (Fig. 3C). In contrast, PABC-GS tumors exhibited a marked reduction in antigenic presentation machinery (APM) and macrophage signatures, indicating an attenuated anti-tumor immunity during pregnancy. Therefore, this distinct immune-active profile in PABC-BF suggests that lactation, rather than pregnancy itself, might shape a specific tumor microenvironment that warrants further precision oncology approaches.

Distinct tumor–immune profile in breastfeeding patients

Having identified PABC-BF as a distinct subset, we focused our investigation on this subgroup to examine the influence of lactation on tumor immunity and its potential clinical implications (Fig. 4).

Gene expression analysis revealed 284 DEGs in PABC-BF tumors compared to non-PABC, with 205 genes upregulated and 79 downregulated (p ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 4A). While many of these genes are implicated in cell-cycle progression and DNA repair (molecular hallmarks broadly shared across all PABC cases), we also identified a unique cluster of genes involved in immune regulation and cytokine signaling that were specifically upregulated in PABC-BF (Fig. 4A, B). Among the top upregulated genes were CD8B, a marker of CD8 + cytotoxic T cells, along with IL22RA2 and IL1R2, both decoy receptors that suppress IL-22 and IL-1 signaling, respectively, thereby limiting downstream inflammatory signaling (Fig. 4A). Notably, significantly downregulated genes included CD276 (also known as B7-H3), an immune checkpoint that modulates T-cell responses, and SERPINH1 which plays a role in extracellular matrix organization. Besides, the STRING network constructed from the 205 upregulated DEGs (p ≤ 0.05), comprised three major clusters in PABC-BF (PPI p < 1.0e-16, Fig. 4B): cell cycle regulation (91 genes, red cluster), cytokine signaling (55 genes, green cluster) and PI3K-Akt signaling (44 genes, blue cluster); underscoring the central roles of cell-cycle and immune pathways in PABC-BF tumor biology.

To further investigate the potential influence of breastfeeding on tumor imprinting, we performed an in-depth analysis of the PABC-BF tumor profile, focusing on the immune-related signatures identified in the NanoString analysis (Fig. 4C). Specifically, PABC-BF tumors exhibited significant enrichment of immune cell abundance, including Tregs, CD8+ T-cells, and cytotoxic cells (FC > 1.5, adj p < 0.05, Fig. 4D). Actually, they were characterized by increased levels of inflammatory chemokines, which may facilitate immune cell recruitment into the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). Interestingly, the marked increase in cytotoxic activity and downregulation of the immune checkpoint B7-H3 further enhanced immune viability in these tumors; but the upregulation of TIGIT suggest a compensatory immunosuppressive mechanism within the immune-active TIME (Fig. 4D). After adjusting for PAM50 subtype, the enrichment of these immune signatures in PABC-BF remained significant. Indeed, stratification by PAM50 showed consistently higher signature scores across most subtypes (Figure S2). Taken together, the unique immunological profile of PABC-BF reflects a TIME characterized by robust immune cell infiltration and inflammatory signals, alongside the presence of selective immune inhibitory signals.

To comprehensively characterize the immune landscape of PABC-BF tumors, we employed CIBERSORTx to estimate the abundance of immune cell populations. This in silico approach revealed that PABC-BF tumors were significantly enriched with CD8+ T-cells and Tregs compared to all other subgroups (p < 0.05, Fig. 5A). These results support the presence of robust T-cell-mediated immune activation in PABC-BF, accompanied by a potential immunoregulatory counterbalance modulating this immune response. In addition, PABC-BF tumors were enriched in pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages than PABC-GS, while showing a significant depletion of immunosuppressive M2 macrophages relative to PABC-PW (p < 0.05, Fig. 5A). This M1/M2 macrophage polarization shift suggests a tumor milieu inclined toward immune stimulation. Further distinctions in the immune cell composition were observed in PABC-BF, with reduced levels of naïve B-cells and increased of activated mast cells and natural killer (NK) cells (relative to PABC-GS or non-PABC). This profile is consistent with an immune-reactive scenario, where innate immune activation may complement adaptive responses.

Finally, to further explore the heterogeneity among PABC-BF tumors, we examined their individual tumor profiles. Figure 5B showed that several PABC samples displayed strong signatures of both adaptive immune activation and immune suppression (T-cell/NK presence, antigen presenting cells, IFNγ biology and T-cell exhaustion markers). Clustering PABC-BF samples based on the 18-gene Tumor Inflammation Signature (TIS), which captures a peripherally suppressed yet T cell–inflamed microenvironment, revealed a subset of PABC-BF tumors exhibiting a transcriptomic profile that closely aligns with this immune-inflamed phenotype (Fig. 5B), suggesting a potential susceptibility to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) that warrants further clinical evidence.

Discussion

A refined understanding of the PABC biological complexity remains essential to improve clinical management and patient outcomes. Though tumors diagnosed during gestation, lactation, and the post-involution period likely share overlapping biological features; grouping them together has led to some inconsistent conclusions regarding tumor behavior and prognosis [4]. As the conventional definition of PABC becomes increasingly outdated, a temporally resolved framework becomes necessary.

Building upon our previous characterization of PABC as an aggressive entity defined by high genomic risk and a proliferative transcriptomic core [8], the present study provides a critical leap forward by dissecting the stage-specific immune dynamics of the disease. Through an expanded, temporally stratified cohort, we reinforce the key clinical molecular traits of PABC [6, 24], including its prevalence for basal-like subtype, increased BC/OC family history, or the high frequency of BRCA1/2 mutations that exceed the expected rates in young-onset BC [25], supporting consideration of systematic genetic counseling in PABC patients. Notably, CCNA2, DEPDC1, and CDC20 were consistently overexpressed in PABC and correlated with poor BC survival in TCGA datasets, in line with their reported prognostic value [26–28]. While we confirm genomic instability and DNA-repair deficiency are shared hallmarks across all PABC windows—reflecting a common aggressive biology regardless of BRCA status or molecular subtype—our findings reveal a remarkably heterogeneous tumor immune landscape.

Specifically, we identify the lactation period (PABC-BF) as a biologically unique window. Unlike the gestation or post-involution phases, PABC-BF is characterized by a distinct inflamed-but-suppressed microenvironment. This shift from a purely proliferative focus to an immune-centric characterization is essential for developing precision oncology strategies, as it identifies a subset of patients who may uniquely benefit from therapeutic interventions targeting specific checkpoints like TIGIT. Biological variability among PABC patients likely reflects maternal stage–specific immune adaptations, as pregnancy induces immunosuppression to tolerate the semi-allogeneic fetus, whereas the postpartum period represents a phase of immune recovery [29, 30]. Postpartum involution is well recognized for its role in PABC development [14, 31], yet the influence of breastfeeding on tumor biology remains unclear. Owing to the high energy demands of milk production, mammary epithelial cells undergo intense metabolic stress during lactation, which ultimately triggers an inflammatory response in the mammary gland [32, 33]. In fact, some authors suggested that lactation can be viewed as an inflammatory ‘wound’ that prompts the wound-healing response during involution, resolving inflammation and restoring tissue homeostasis [33]. Another proposed role of lactation-associated immune cells is to provide immune surveillance to protect both the breast and the infant from pathogens [34, 35].

Albeit studies on PABC have grown in recent years, most of them lack critical information about patients' lactation status at the time of diagnosis. This limits the ability to draw firm conclusions about the impact of breastfeeding on tumor biology. Emerging evidence suggests that lactation patterns may be linked to biological changes associated with abrupt involution, a process known to create a pro-tumorigenic environment [36–38]. In postpartum breast cancer (PPBC) studies, despite the absence of lactation data, tumors from ER-/ + patients have shown enrichment of inflammation-related gene signatures compared to nulliparous patients, particularly an increase in CD8+ T-cells, NK cells, and macrophages [39–41]. Remarkably, a recent study revealed that TNBC parous women who had breastfed exhibited an accumulation of CD8⁺ T-cells with a tissue-resident phenotype [42]. In this regard, our results presented herein suggest that lactation significantly shapes the TIME, through infiltration of cytotoxic T-cells and reduced B7-H3 expression. However, this immune activation appears counterbalanced by the enrichment of immunosuppressive Tregs and TIGIT inhibitory checkpoint, which may limit the enhanced cytotoxic potential during lactation. Consistently, CIBERSORTx analysis confirmed increased abundance of CD8⁺ T cells and Tregs in PABC-BF, underscoring the simultaneous presence of cytotoxic and immunoregulatory populations. At the gene level, the upregulation of CD8B corroborates CD8 + T-cell infiltration, while the decoy receptors IL22RA2 and IL1R2 point to an active suppression of IL-22 and IL-1 signaling, further supporting the coexistence of immune activation and cytokine attenuation within the lactation-associated TIME.

This landscape reflects the broader composition of the TIME, where the balance between stimulatory and suppressive T-cell subsets shapes antitumor immunity and disease progression. CD8⁺ T and NK cells serve as cytotoxic effectors, sharing inhibitory receptors such as TIGIT and PD-1/PD-L1, providing opportunities for dual checkpoint targeting [43]. On the contrary, Tregs suppress antitumor immunity by releasing immunosuppressive cytokines that promote T-cell exhaustion, marked by increased PD-1, TIM-3, TIGIT, and LAG-3 expression [44–46]. From our findings, the lactating mammary gland exhibits a unique TIME, in which immune cells infiltrate the breast to support tissue maintenance and immune surveillance, while tolerance-promoting mechanisms are also active to prevent immune reactions against milk proteins [33, 47]. This co-occurrence of immune stimulation and suppression mirrors the dual characteristics of immune-inflamed tumors and suggests that persistent exposure to tumor antigens may lead to CD8+ T-cell exhaustion, reducing their cytotoxic capacity [35, 48].

Immunotherapy has transformed the treatment of some solid tumors, but its impact in BC has been limited, as it was historically considered an “immune cold” tumor [49]. BC is now recognized as heterogeneous disease: ER + subtypes generally less immunogenic than TNBC/HER2 + tumors, which exhibit stronger immune responses and potential for immune-targeted interventions [50]. However, current PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors only benefit a subset of patients, highlighting the need for alternatives that target other immune pathways [45]. Physiological states such as lactation may further shape the immune milieu; indeed our findings suggest that systemic immune activation during breastfeeding favors an immune-inflamed microenvironment. Concurrent effector and suppressive signals may reflect partial immune exhaustion, which could inform that non-canonical checkpoints such as TIGIT merit future exploration with appropriate protein‑level and functional validation. Beyond potential therapeutic targeting, the functional composition of the immune infiltrate during lactation may also modulate response to systemic therapies. In fact, prolonged breastfeeding has been associated with higher pathological complete response (pCR) rates in the PPBC women [51], highlighting the need for prospective studies incorporating treatment response and detailed lactation history.

While this observational study provides valuable insights for exploring associations, it is important to acknowledge that it does not allow for direct causal inference and findings may be influenced by confounders. Small subgroup sizes limit statistical power, warranting validation in independent cohorts. Transcriptomic and deconvolution analyses, while informative, do not provide protein-level spatial validation or functional resolution. Future studies with larger, well-characterized cohorts and orthogonal validation approaches are needed to fully clarify lactation’s impact on the tumor immune microenvironment.

Taken together, this study maps the molecular landscape of PABC systematically dissecting the influence of pregnancy, breastfeeding and postpartum on tumor biology. We have uncovered how lactation shapes a distinct immune-inflamed tumor microenvironment marked by concurrent immune activation and regulatory signals. These findings provide a framework to understand tumor-immune interactions and generate hypotheses for future exploration.
