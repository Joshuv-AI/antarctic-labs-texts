Telomeric repeat-containing RNA G-quadruplexes trigger ZBP1-mediated cell death
Authors: Geng Qin, Chuanqi Zhao, Chengming Gao, Wenxuan Hu, Na Zhang, Jinsong Ren, Xiaogang Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractZ-DNA-binding protein 1 (ZBP1) is a nucleic acid sensor with two Z-DNA-binding domains (Zα domains) that bind Z-DNA and Z-RNA, mediating inflammatory cell death in development and disease. A recent study has demonstrated that the truncated isoform ZBP1-S senses telomeric repeat-containing RNA (TERRA), activating innate immune signaling and triggering cell death. However, the precise mechanism on how ZBP1-S senses TERRA remains unknown. G-quadruplexes (G4s) are non-canonical secondary structures of nucleic acids that can form within guanine (G)-rich strands of DNA or RNA, including TERRA. Here, we demonstrate that ZBP1-S directly senses G4 structures within TERRA, leading to ZBP1-S oligomerization and subsequent induction of inflammatory cell death. Furthermore, G4-specific binding ligands can compete with ZBP1-S for binding to TERRA G4s and inhibit ZBP1-S mediated activation of interferon signaling. Therefore, this work deciphers a hitherto uncharacterized mechanism that links TERRA G4s with ZBP1-S activity in triggering inflammatory cell death.Subject terms: DNA, Innate immunity, RNA, Telomeres

Introduction

DNA-binding protein 1 (ZBP1), initially identified as an interferon (IFN)-induced tumor-associated protein, possesses domains capable of binding to nucleic acids in the form of a left-handed helix (Z-form)1–3. ZBP1 has recently emerged as a crucial innate immune sensor that governs the activation of both programmed cell death and inflammation across various conditions, including cancer, virus infection, and embryonic development4. The ZBP1 gene encodes two Z-DNA-binding domains (Zα1 and Zα2 domains) at the N-terminal, which have been observed to bind to Z-DNA, B-DNA, and RNA5,6. Additionally, there are two homotypic interaction motifs (RHIM) domains present in the central region of ZBP1 that facilitate interactions with other proteins containing RHIM domains7,8. These RHIM domains play a crucial role in mediating cell death and inflammatory responses triggered by ZBP19,10. Furthermore, it has been reported that the conserved C-terminal domains of ZBP1 interact with TBK1 and IRF3 to induce type I interferon (IFN) responses upon exposure to immunostimulatory DNA4.

A recent study has demonstrated that dysfunctional telomeres trigger innate immune responses via mitochondrial TERRA-ZBP1 complexes to eliminate cells with a predisposition for neoplastic transformation11. TERRA interacts with ZBP1 to activate the innate immune adapter protein mitochondrial antiviral-signaling protein (MAVS), but the precise interaction mechanism of TERRA between ZBP1 is still unknown. Zα domain of ADAR1 has been shown to interact with MYC promoter G-quadruplex (G4) formed in NHEIII1 region and represses MYC transcription12. G4s are stable, non-canonical nucleic acids secondary structures adopted by guanine-rich DNAs or RNAs, which have been shown to be involved in modulating many processes of cellular biology and are viewed as potential therapeutic targets for various human disease, including cancer, neurodegenerative disorder and virus infection13–18. Given that TERRA contains abundant G4 forming sequences, therefore, we speculated that TERRA interacts with ZBP1 via RNA G4 structures.

In this study, the interactions between the Zα domains of ZBP1 and TERRA G4 have been demonstrated. The truncated ZBP1 isoform (ZBP1-S) engages with TERRA G4 via Zα2. In addition, G/A mutant of TERRA impairs its interaction with ZBP1-S and inhibits ZBP1-S mediated MAVS-dependent interferon response and inflammatory cell death. Intriguingly, TERRA G4 oligonucleotides can activate ZBP1 and induce cell death, while G4-specific ligands pyridostatin (PDS) and 5,10,15,20-tetrakis-(N-methyl-4-pyridyl)porphine (TMPyP4) inhibit ZBP1-S mediated activation of innate immune signaling by blocking the interactions between TERRA and ZBP1-S. This work deciphers the mechanism on how ZBP1-S senses TERRA, indicating the key roles of G4 structures in ZBP1 mediated activation of innate immune signaling of inflammatory cell death.

Result

The Zα domains of ZBP1 interact with TERRA G4

Recent studies have demonstrated that TERRA serves as an immunostimulatory nucleic acid species recognized by ZBP111; however, the precise mechanism underlying how ZBP1 senses TERRA remains elusive. Alternative splicing of human ZBP1 generates two predominant isoforms: the full-length ZBP1 (ZBP1-L) harbors two Z-DNA-binding domains (Zα1 and Zα2), whereas the truncated isoform (ZBP1-S) lacks Zα1 but retains Zα2 (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Zα domains of ADAR1 have been shown to recognize G4 structure12, and TERRA can form RNA G4 structures19. We first investigated whether Zα1 and Zα2 domains of ZBP1 could interact with TERRA G4. To this end, we synthesized FAM-labeled 21 nucleotide (nt) TERRA G4 wild type RNA (TERRA-G4 WT), its mutant RNA (TERRA-G4 Mut, with G/A mutations), and Flag-fused Zα1 and Zα2 domain of ZBP1 (Supplementary Fig. 1b and Supplementary Data 1). By detection of the FAM fluorescence intensity following TERRA G4 pull-down assays via Anti-Flag Magnetic Beads (Fig. 1a), we found that both Zα1 and Zα2 domains can directly bind to TERRA-G4 WT, but not TERRA-G4 Mut (Fig. 1b). To explore the interaction between RNA G-quadruplex and ZBP1, one dimensional 1H-NMR titration experiments were pursued. As a control, TERRA G-quadruplex alone showed essentially 12 characteristic guanine imino proton peaks (partially overlapped) at 11.2-11.7 ppm, likely corresponding to an intramolecular G-quadruplex structure with three stacked G-tetrads (Supplementary Fig. 1c–e). Upon increasing the concentration of ZBP1, both the chemical shift perturbation and peak broadening became more and more evident among guanine imino proton peaks of the RNA G-quadruplex, revealing their interactions between TERRA G4 and ZBP1.

It is known that G-quadruplex can form more complex higher order G4s20, such as dimer (TERRA G4-2) (Supplementary Fig. 1f–h); therefore, we further investigated the interaction between Zα domains and the higher order TERRA G4s (Fig. 1c). As expected, we found that Zα domains can bind to higher order TERRA G4s (Supplementary Fig. 1i, j). Both Zα1 and Zα2 preferred to bind to more complex higher-order G4 with higher binding affinity compared to monomer G4 (Fig. 1d). Consistently, when incubating Zα domains with the mixture of FAM-labeled 21 nt TERRA G4 and G4-2, Zα domains preferred to capture higher-order TERRA G4-2 rather than 21 nt TERRA G4, indicating its higher binding ability for higher-order TERRA G4 (Supplementary Fig. 1k). In addition, the binding affinity of Zα2 to TERRA G4 was higher compared to that of Zα1 (Fig. 1e). We also further estimated the binding constant (Kd) of Zα1 and Zα2 for TERRA G4, respectively, and found that Zα1 and Zα2 indeed prefer higher-order TERRA G4 (Fig. 1f). Taken together, our results indicate that Zα1 and Zα2 domains of ZBP1 can interact with TERRA G4.

ZBP1 engages with TERRA G4 via Zα domains

ZBP1-S has been demonstrated to sense TERRA, thereby activating innate immune signaling pathways essential for inflammatory cell death11. Given that ZBP1-S lacks Zα1 but retains Zα221, we hypothesized that ZBP1-S interacts with TERRA via Zα2 domain. First, to model the interaction of ZBP1-S with G-quadruplex, we utilized AlphaFold prediction1 on the second Z-DNA binding domain (PDB ID: 3EYI) in complex with a known human telomeric RNA G4 (PDB ID: 3IBK). The overall prediction has a high confidence (Fig. 2a), suggesting that the model is highly confident for the interaction between ZBP1 and G4, with the Zα interface supporting G4 binding. We also utilized Rosetta modeling to optimize the predicted complex, in which we observed the telomeric G4 followed a canonical RNA G4 pattern stabilized by hydrogen bonds between the guanine groups and further enhancement of the structural integrity through coordination with K+ at each layer (Fig. 2b). Moreover, the interaction between ZBP1-S and G4 was achieved through an intensive interaction network between the S8, Q9, and R52 residues (corresponding to S106, Q107 and R150 in ZBP1) that stabilized the outward-pointing residues in the G4 U7 and A8, thus stabilizing the quadruplex conformation (Fig. 2c). Other interactions also played roles in the formation of the complex such as the guanidinium group on R44 (corresponding to R142 in ZBP1) could interact with the backbone of the quadruplex on G3, thus enriching the stability of the ZBP1-G4 complex (Fig. 2d). Through the structure model, we contended that the interaction of the second domain of ZBP1 may play a crucial role in recognizing and stabilizing the G-quadruplex through the Zα interface. Then, to further substantiate this hypothesis, we did disrupt the TERRA G4-binding by point mutations (S106A, Q107A, R142A and R150A, ZBP1-SmZα2)5 (Supplementary Fig. 2a). By detection of the FAM fluorescence intensity following TERRA G4 pull-down assays, we found that ZBP1-S WT (ZBP1-SWT) can bind to TERRA-G4 WT, but not TERRA-G4 Mut (Fig. 2e–g and Supplementary Fig. 2b). Consistently, ZBP1-SWT also preferred to capture higher-order TERRA G4 (Fig. 2g), and we determined the Kd of ZBP1-SWT for TERRA G4 under our experimental conditions: Kd [TERRA G4] = 48.82 ± 0.98 nM and Kd [TERRA G4-2] = 22.30 ± 0.87 nM (Fig. 2h). As expected, electrophoresis mobility shift assay (EMSA) and TERRA G4 pull-down assays indicated that Zα2 mutant impaired the binding of ZBP1-S to TERRA G4 (Fig. 2j and Supplementary Fig. 2c, d). And a supershift assay using a validated anti-ZBP1 antibody confirmed the formation of ZBP1/TERRA G4 complexes (Fig. 2k). Consistently, a pulldown assay employing biotinylated TERRA G4 RNA verified the interaction between TERRA G4 and ZBP1 in living cells (Supplementary Fig. 2e). To further explore the interaction between ZBP1-S and TERRA G4, we synthesized FAM-labeled TERRA G4-2 (G4-2-WT) and its G/A mutant (G4-2-Mut), followed by transfection into HEK293T cells along with Flag-fused ZBP1-S. Our results demonstrated that the yellow regions in the merged image indicated a high degree of co-localization between G4-2-WT and ZBP1-S, whereas G4-2-Mut and ZBP1-S exhibited minimal co-localization (Fig. 2l and Supplementary Fig. 2f), suggesting the ZBP1-S interacted with TERRA G4 in living cells. Worth to note, in addition to the key residues (S106, Q107, R142, and R150), mutations in the canonical Z-DNA/Z-RNA binding residues (N141 and Y145), as previously reported11, similarly abolish TERRA G4 binding (Supplementary Fig. 2g–i). Collectively, our results indicate that ZBP1-S engages with TERRA G4 via Zα2 domain.

TERRA G4 promotes ZBP1 oligomerization and cell death

We next investigate whether TERRA G4 is essential for ZBP1 to activate innate immune signaling and induce inflammatory cell death. First, we investigated whether endogenous TERRA can form G4 structures in living cells. RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays showed the enrichment of TERRA in complexes precipitated by G4-specific antibody BG4 as compared with control IgG (Supplementary Fig. 3a), indicating the G4 formation within endogenous TERRA. In addition, we conducted the TERRA (TERRAWT) and its G/A mutant (TERRAMut) expression vectors for expressing TERRA and its mutant in living cells. To generate full-length TERRA transcripts, we cloned the human chromosome 15q subtelomeric regions (As a representative) containing the TERRA start sites before the TTAGGG or TTAGAG repeats (Fig. 3a, b). Upon binding to its nucleic acid substrates, ZBP1 can undergo oligomerization, thereby initiating innate immune signaling or triggering cell death3,11. Thus, we first explored whether TERRA G4 was essential for ZBP1-S oligomerization. By performing bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays in HEK293T cells, we observed a significant induction of the BiFC signal upon overexpression of TERRAWT, whereas no such effect was observed with TERRAMut (Fig. 3c, d). Consistently, by performing semi-denaturing detergent agarose gel electrophoresis (SDD-AGE), we found that TERRAWT could induce ZBP1-S oligomerization in the ZBP1-S overexpressed MCF-7 cells, but not TERRAMut (Fig. 3e). In addition, to directly visualize ZBP1-S oligomerization in living cells, we performed Airyscan super-resolution microscopy. As shown in Fig. 3f, ZBP1-S formed distinct oligomeric filaments upon induction of TERRA G4s (Fig. 3f), consistent with previous study11.

To investigate the effects of TERRA G4 on ZBP1-S mediated cell death, we constructed ZBP1-knockout MCF-7 cells for reconstituting with ZBP1-S (Supplementary Fig. 3b). We found that ZBP1-S overexpression hardly affect the cell viability (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). However, ZBP1-S significantly induced cell death in TERRAWT overexpressed MCF-7 cells, but not in TERRAMut overexpressed cells (Fig. 3g–i). In addition, ZBP1-S co-expressed with TERRAWT could activate interferon (IFN) signaling, marked by TBK1, IRF3, and STAT1 phosphorylation (Supplementary Fig. 3e). IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) (such as IFNB1, CCL5, CXCL10, and ISG15) were induced by ZBP1-S in TERRAWT overexpressed cells (Fig. 3j). Previous studies have demonstrated that ZBP1-S mediated innate immune signaling induced by TERRA is mediated by MAVS11. Indeed, we found that MAVS knockdown blocked ZBP1-S mediated up-regulation of ISGs in TERRAWT overexpressed cells (Fig. 3k and Supplementary Fig. 3f). Finally, ZBP1-knockout cells were reconstituted with mutant forms of ZBP1-S (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b) and tested for their susceptibility to induce ISGs and undergo cell death. We found that cells reconstituted with ZBP1-S containing mutations in key conserved residues involved in nucleic acid binding or RHIM1-based interaction (I206A, Q207A, I208A, G209A)7,22 did not induce ISGs and cell death in TERRAWT overexpressed cells (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d), In contrast, the cells reconstituted with ZBP1-S containing mutations in RHIM2 (V264A, Q265A, L266A, G267A) could still induce ISGs and cell death in TERRAWT overexpressed cells, indicating that RHIM2 is largely dispensable for this function (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). And the same results were obtained in MDA-MB-231, Hela and A549 cells (Supplementary Fig. 5a–g). Worth to note, TERRA with distinct subtelomere regions (Human chromosome 9p, Xp, and Xq) or TERRA G4 oligonucleotides had the ability to activate innate immune signaling and elicit cell death (Supplementary Figs. 6a–c and 7a–c), thereby emphasizing the pivotal roles of G4 structure for recognizing TERRA by ZBP1-S. Taken together, our results indicate that TERRA G4 facilitates ZBP1-S oligomerization through its Zα2 domain, thereby promoting inflammatory cell death.

G4-specific ligands inhibit ZBP1-mediated activation of innate immune signaling

G4-specific ligands have been developed and successfully used as tools for exploring the intricate biological functions and mechanisms mediated by G4, as well as potential therapeutic agents targeting human diseases23–26. Many G4-specific ligands have been demonstrated to recognize and stabilize telomeric DNA G4 and TERRA G419. G4-specific ligands PDS and TMPyP4 (Fig. 4a) have been used to target DNA and RNA G4s25. Both PDS and TMPyP4 can recognize TERRA G427,28. Thus, we next investigated the effects of PDS and TMPyP4 on the interaction between TERRA and ZBP1-S, as well as ZBP1-S-mediated activation of innate immune signaling. First, we found that both PDS and TMPyP4 were competitive with the Zα domains for binding to TERRA G4 (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 8a, b), showing that PDS and TMPyP4 could inhibit the binding of ZPB1-S to TERRA G4 (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 8c). BiFC assays showed that PDS and TMPyP4 inhibited ZBP1 oligomerization (Fig. 4d, e). Furthermore, PDS and TMPyP4 treatment significantly decreased the expression of ISGs in the ZBP1-S and TERRA overexpressed cells (Fig. 4f). However, we found that the ability to interfere with ZBP1 recognition is not a universal property of G4 ligands. The G4 ligand BMVC inhibited the interaction between ZBP1 and TERRA G4 with comparable efficacy to PDS and TMPyP4 under our experimental conditions, but PhpC or Phen-DC3 just showed slightly inhibition effect on ZBP1 binding to TERRA G4 (Supplementary Fig. 8d–g), indicating that the ability of G4 ligands to interfere with the ZBP1-TERRA G4 interaction is influenced by their binding mode, preferred G4 topology, or specific binding site within the TERRA G4. In addition, we investigated whether RNA helicases such as DHX36 could influence ZBP1-mediated activation of innate immune signaling by unwinding TERRA G4s. As expected, DHX36 knockdown significantly increases the expression of ISGs in cells co-overexpressing ZBP1-S and TERRA (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b), indicating further activation of ZBP1-mediated innate immune activation. These results suggest that DHX36 acts as a negative regulator of this pathway. We proposed a model wherein DHX36 normally functions to unwind or remodel TERRA G4s, thereby dismantling the activating ligand for ZBP1 and suppressing signaling.

Inspired by the telomeric RNA G4, we next explored the potential interaction between the Zα domains of ZBP1 and other cellular RNA G4s. We chose two well-characterized RNA G4s: NRAS and BCL-2 mRNA 5’ untranslated region (5’ UTR) G4s29,30 for subsequent study. We found that Zα domains can interact with these two RNA G4s (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). Consistently, both ZBP1-L and ZBP1-S could also bind to these two RNA G4s (Supplementary Fig. 10c, d), implying the potential of ZBP1 in recognizing other RNA G4s besides TERRA G4. Moreover, we investigated the ability of Zα domains to bind various types of nucleic acids, including G4 DNA, ssRNA, dsRNA, ssDNA, and dsDNA. Our results demonstrate that Zα domains exhibit binding affinity toward G4 DNA structures, such as MycG4 and KRAS G4, but do not bind ssRNA, dsRNA, ssDNA, or dsDNA (Supplementary Fig. 11a–d) under our experimental conditions. The biological function of ZBP1 in sensing other cellular DNAs and RNAs with G4 structures warrants further investigation in future studies.

Discussion

Innate immune sensing of pathogen-associated or danger-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs and DAMPs) initiates diverse intracellular signaling pathways and induces proinflammatory responses4. Moreover, many of the innate sensors also elicit programmed cell death in response to pathogens or other immunological stimuli, either through direct interaction with other mediators of cell death or indirectly via the downstream effects of secreted cytokines31. ZBP1 is a rising star in innate immunity4, and intriguingly, recent studies have demonstrated that dysfunctional telomeres trigger innate immune responses via mitochondrial TERRA/ZBP1-S complexes, leading to the elimination of cells predestined for neoplastic transformation11. However, the precise interaction mechanism between TERRA and ZBP1-S is still unknown. In our study, we found that G4 structure formed within TERRA transcripts is essential for ZBP1-S binding to TERRA. ZBP1-S interacts with TERRA G4 via Zα2 domain. G/A mutant of TERRA, which cannot form G4, impairs its interaction with ZBP1-S, thereby inhibiting ZBP1-S mediated MAVS-dependent interferon response and inflammatory cell death. Furthermore, TERRA G4 oligonucleotides can activate ZBP1-S mediated inflammatory cell death. These results indicate that TERRA G4 plays key roles in ZBP1 mediated activation of innate immune signaling and inflammatory cell death.

RIG-I and MDA5 are well-established sensors of double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), while ZBP1 is a distinct innate immune sensor that operates on a different principle. RIG-I is activated by short dsRNA and relies on polyubiquitin-assisted tetramerization, whereas MDA5 senses long dsRNAs by cooperatively forming helical filaments along the RNA backbone. Building on this principle, we propose a working model for ZBP1 activation wherein engagement of the TERRA G4 ligand drives its higher-order assembly. We hypothesize that the specific recognition of the TERRA G4 structure by the Zα2 domains of adjacent ZBP1-S molecules induces a conformational change that exposes oligomerization interfaces, facilitating cooperative oligomerization along or stabilized by the G4-containing TERRA. This model frames ZBP1 within the broader mechanistic context of RNA-templated oligomerization shared by RIG-I-like receptors (RLRs), even as it recognizes a distinct nucleic acid motif. In contrast, ZBP1 is activated not by the length or sequence of the RNA, but by its specific tertiary structure—TERRA G4. This interaction induces a conformational change in ZBP1, leading to its activation and subsequent signaling through MAVS. This mechanism highlights ZBP1 as a pioneer sensor that recognizes nucleic acid structural motifs, thereby defining a G4-based sensing pathway that is functionally segregated from the canonical dsRNA-sensing axes of RIG-I and MDA5.

Our study identified key residues (S106, Q107, R142, and R150) within the Zα2 domain of ZBP1-S that are critical for its specific recognition of TERRA G4 RNA. The mutations at S106, Q107, R142, and R150 significantly disrupt the interaction. Worth to note, our results indicated that mutations in the canonical Z-DNA/Z-RNA binding residues (N141/Y145), as previously reported11, similarly abrogate TERRA G4 binding. This suggests that the recognition of TERRA G4 is not mediated by a completely novel, independent interface, but likely relies on the overall structural integrity and precise conformation of the Zα domain. We speculate that TERRA G4 engagement involves an extended or adapted binding surface that cooperatively incorporates both the canonical scaffold and additional specific contact points. This composite interface may allow the Zα domain to accommodate the distinct topology of G-quadruplex, contrasting with its binding mode for left-handed Z-form helices. Future high-resolution structural studies, particularly the determination of co-crystal structures for wild-type and mutant Zα domains in complex with TERRA G4, will be essential to visualize this interaction at atomic level, validate our proposed model, and fully elucidate the molecular basis of this specific recognition event.

ZBP1 and ADAR1 are the only two mammalian proteins that contain the Zα domains32. The Zα domains are essential for ZBP1 and ADAR1 mediated immune responses, which provide host defense against infection and disease32,33. Recent studies have significantly enhanced our comprehension of ADAR1 and ZBP1 and established their pivotal roles in the regulation of innate immune responses encompassing NLRP3 inflammasome activation, inflammation, and cellular demise32,34,35. ADAR1 is a negative regulator of ZBP1 activation through the competition of their Zα domains for nucleic-acid-binding36,37. Our results have shown that ZBP1-S interacts with TERRA G4 via its Zα2 domain, while it has been reported that Zα domain of ADAR1 can also bind to G4 structure12. Whether ADAR1 compete with ZBP1-S for TERRA G4, and ADAR1 participate in the regulation of ZBP1-TERRA mediated neoplastic transformation? We have reasons to believe that ADAR1 may play important roles in ZBP1-TERRA mediated neoplastic transformation, and these issues are worthy of further clarification in subsequent studies.

In reviewing the results of this study, a potential limitation should be kept in mind. The dependence of this study on synthetic TERRA G4 oligonucleotides, transfection-based overexpression of exogenous TERRA, and G4 ligands restricts our ability to draw definitive conclusions about the native biology of endogenous TERRA G4s and their interaction with ZBP1. While we have demonstrated that endogenous TERRA can form G4 structures and that global G4-stabilizing ligands modulate the ZBP1 pathway, these approaches do not offer exclusive targeting of telomeric RNA G4s. The lack of tools to disrupt or stabilize endogenous TERRA G4s without altering total TERRA levels or sequence context prevents definitive causal attribution of the observed phenotypes exclusively to this specific RNA structure. This challenge reflects a broader methodological gap in the field of in vivo G4 biology38,39. Future developments, such as G4-targeting RNA epigenome editors or CRISPR-based systems designed to manipulate local nucleic acid topology, will be crucial for establishing direct functional causality and exploring the therapeutic potential of this pathway.

In summary, we found that G4s participate in the regulation of ZBP1-TERRA mediated neoplastic transformation and further demonstrate the functional linkage between G4s and ZBP1for innate immune signaling and induction of cell death. More importantly, G4-binding small molecules can disrupt the interaction between ZBP1 and TERRA and inhibit ZBP1 oligomerization and the activation of innate immune signaling, which presents a chemical alternative approach for modulating innate immune signaling and inflammatory cell death. To sum up, this work deciphers the mechanism on how ZBP1-S senses TERRA, indicating the key roles of G4 structures in ZBP1 mediated activation of innate immune signaling of inflammatory cell death.

Methods

Oligonucleotides and chemical compounds

All oligonucleotides used in this study were synthesized by Shanghai Sangon Biological Engineering Technology & Services (Shanghai, China). Chemical compounds were purchased from MedChemExpress (MCE, USA) and used without further purification.

TERRA G4 pull-down assays

Two μM FAM-labeled TERRA G4 RNA and flag-fused Zα1 and Zα2 domains of ZBP1 or ZBP1 proteins, as indicated concentrations, were mixed well in test tube, and then the Anti-Flag Magnetic Beads were added to pull down G4/protein complexes. After rinsing and dissolving, the pull-down FAM-G4 was detected by fluorescence spectrophotometer (JASCO FP-6500). For the biotinylated TERRA G4 pull-down assay, biotinylated TERRA G4 was transfected into MCF-7 cells, followed by cell lysis and protein extraction. Streptavidin magnetic beads were then added to isolate G4/protein complexes, which were subsequently analyzed by western blotting.

AlphaFold prediction and structural analysis

The complex prediction was achieved in AlphaFold340, using the available ZBP1 second Z-DNA binding domain (PDB ID: 3EYI) and human telomeric RNA G-quadruplex (PDB ID: 3IBK) from the available structure in the protein data bank5,41. The generated structure was optimized through PyRosetta42 under the FastRelax protocol to perform energy minimization. Further analysis was conducted in PyMol (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 3.0, Schrödinger, LLC).

Recombinant protein expression and purification

To express the recombinant protein, the pET-28a ZBP1-L and ZBP1-S vectors were introduced into the Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) strain. The bacteria were cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth supplemented with 50 μg/ml kanamycin at 37 °C until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached approximately 0.6. The cultures were then cooled to 16 °C, and expression was induced by adding 0.5 mM isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) for a duration of 16 h. Following induction, cell pellets were harvested via centrifugation and resuspended in chilled lysis buffer composed of 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 500 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 10 mM imidazole, 1 mM DTT, and 1 mM PMSF. The cells were lysed using sonication for 30 min, alternating between 5 s of pulses and 25 s of rest, all while maintaining the temperature with ice. The lysate was subsequently clarified by centrifugation at 5180 × g for 2 h. The hexahistidine-tagged fusion proteins from the supernatant were purified using a HisTrap FF prepacked column (#17531901; Cytiva). The proteins were further desalted using a HiTrap Desalting prepacked column (#29048684; Cytiva). The recombinant proteins were stored in −80 °C. For the expression of flag-fused Z-DNA-binding domains (Zα1 and Zα2 domains), we used TNT® Quick Coupled Transcription/Translation System (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy experiments

One dimensional NMR data were collected at 298 K on a 500 MHz Bruker spectrometer with a cryoprobe. The gradient-tailored excitation (WATERGATE) was used to suppress the water signal for 90% H2O/10% D2O samples. The stock solution of G-rich TERRA RNA oligonucleotide (AGGGUUAGGGUUAGGGUUAGGG) was first dissolved in 5 mM potassium phosphate buffer (K-Pi, pH 6.8). Next, this RNA sample was heated at 95 °C for 5 min and then slowly cooled to room temperature to ensure the complete formation of a G4 structure. The stock solution of protein was initially dissolved in 1 × PBS buffer. For NMR titration experiments, 0.1 mM RNA TERRA G4 sample was titrated stepwise with increasing amounts of protein, whereas the 0.1 mM RNA G4, as a reference, was only treated with corresponding amount of PBS alone without protein. All of the NMR titration spectra in Supplementary Fig. 2e, f were collected in a final buffer solution containing 3.6 mM K-Pi and 0.27 × PBS. All data were processed and analyzed with Bruker Topspin 3.6.2 software.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

ELISAs were conducted to assess the binding affinity. Briefly, for investigating the binding constant (Kd) of Zα domains and ZBP1 with G4s, biotinylated G4 oligonucleotides were immobilized onto streptavidin-coated plates. And then the plates underwent incubation with Flag-tagged Zα domains and ZBP1 and HRP-conjugated anti-Flag antibody (86861S; Cell Signaling Technology, USA). Finally, the plates were incubated with TMB (3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine, HRP substrate), and signal intensity was measured at 450 nm. The oligonucleotides are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

Cell culture

The HEK293T, Hela and A549 cell lines (obtained from the China Center for Type Culture Collection, CCTCC, Wuhan, China), as well as the MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells (obtained from CCTCC, Wuhan, China) derived from human breast cancer, were cultured in a high-glucose medium known as Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; HyClone, USA). This medium was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; HyClone, USA), along with 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. The cells were incubated at a temperature of 37 °C in a humidified environment containing 5% CO2.

Transfection of plasmids

The transfection of plasmids was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded until they reached a confluency of 70–90% prior to transfection. The plasmids and Lipofectamine 2000 reagent were combined in serum-free DMEM medium. After incubating the mixtures at room temperature for 15 min, they were added to the cells in each dish. The collected cells at specified time points corresponded to those that had undergone transfection.

Viral packaging and infection

The vectors of interest, along with viral packaging plasmids (pMD2.G and psPAX2), were introduced into HEK293T cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Briefly, cells were seeded in 60 mm culture dishes at a confluency ranging from 70% to 90% during transfection. A mixture comprising 6 µg of target plasmids, 2 µg of pMD2.G (Addgene #12259), 4 µg of psPAX2 (Addgene #12260), and 30 µL of Lipofectamine 2000 reagent was prepared in serum-free DMEM. After incubating the mixtures for a duration of 20 min at room temperature, they were added to the cells in each dish. The media was replaced after 6 h post-transfection. Virus supernatant was collected after 48 h, filtered through a filter with pore size measuring at 0.45 μM, and stored at −80 °C until further use. For infection experiments, virus supernatant was incubated with target cells for an additional period lasting 48 h at a dilution ratio equivalent to1:10 with polybrene (8 μg/mL). Subsequently, infected cells were digested and counted before being reseeded sparsely in new culture dishes supplemented with selection agents administered after 6 h.

Western blotting

To analyze the proteins in whole-cell lysates, we used RIPA buffer (CWBIO, Beijing, China) to lyse the cells. The total protein samples (10–20 μg) were then suspended in Laemmli buffer (63 mM Tris-HCl, 10% glycerol, 2% SDS, 0.0025% bromophenol blue, pH 6.8) and subjected to electrophoresis on SDS-polyacrylamide gels. Subsequently, the proteins were transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. For antibody detection of specific proteins such as Phospho-IRF3 (Ser396) (AF5838; Beyotime, Shanghai, China), Phospho-STAT1 (Tyr701) (AF5935; Beyotime, Shanghai, China), Phospho-TBK1 (Ser172) (AF595959; Beyotime, Shanghai, China), Flag (TA-05, ZSGB-BIO, China), ZBP1 (ab232977; Abcam, USA), or GAPDH (CW0098M; CWBIO Beijing, China), we performed an incubation step followed by incubation with secondary antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP). Anti-mouse antibody or anti-rabbit antibody from Beyotime (Shanghai, China) was used as secondary antibody. To visualize the immunoreactive bands on the membrane surface after incubation with HRP-conjugated antibodies, washing steps were completed using SuperSignal™ West Pico chemiluminescent substrate kit from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Finally, the Western blotting detection system from BioRad (Hercules, CA, USA) was employed for band visualization. In order to ensure accurate quantification of protein levels during western blotting assays, GAPDH was utilized as a loading control.

Semi-denaturing detergent agarose gel electrophoresis (SDD-AGE)

Cells were lysed by lysis buffer and loaded onto 1.5% agarose gel. After electrophoresis in running buffer (1× TBE, 0.1% SDS) for 2 h with a constant voltage of 50 V at 4 °C, proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes with a Wet/Tank transfer blotting system (Bio-Rad Laboratories) for western blotting analysis.

Nondenaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis

Native gel electrophoresis was carried out on acrylamide gel (15 %) and run at 25 °C, 1 × TB buffer containing 10 mM KCl, and was silver stained. To prepare the final loading sample, ZBP1-SWT (2 μM or 4 μM) or ZBP1-SMut (2 μM or 4 μM) proteins were incubated with annealed TERRA G4 WT (1 μM) or TERRA G4 Mut (1 μM), respectively, at 4 °C for 12 h. For the supershift assay, ZBP1-SWT (4 μM) or ZBP1-SMut (4 μM) proteins and ZBP1 antibody (2 μM; ab232977; Abcam, USA) were added to the annealed TERRA G4 samples (1 μM) and incubated at 4 °C for 12 h. In competition assays involving unlabeled TERRA G4, annealed FAM-labeled TERRA G4 (1 μM) was incubated with ZBP1-S (4 μM) protein in the presence of annealed unlabeled TERRA G4 (1 μM or 2 μM) at 4 °C for 12 h.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Cells were washed twice in 1 × PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100-PBS for 15 min, and then blocked for 30 min with 3% BSA-0.2% Triton X-100-PBS. Furthermore, cells were incubated with primary Mouse Flag (TA-05, ZSGB-BIO, China) for 16 h at 4 °C. Secondary antibody was Rhodamine (TRITC) Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) (AS026; ABclonal, China) used in a 1:1000 dilution for 2 h at 37 °C. DAPI (P0131; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was added for 2 min in the dark before taken images with confocal laser-scanning microscope LSM 700 (Carl Zeiss, Germany). The fluorescence value of EGPF was detected by IF assays. For image acquisition, all confocal microscopy images were acquired under identical laser power, gain, and exposure settings within the same experiment. For experiments quantifying the colocalization foci between ZBP1-S and FAM-labeled TERRA G4 was strictly defined not by simple channel overlap, but by the presence of a probe focus as defined above, that spatially coincided with a ZBP1-S punctum. A foci was defined as a discrete, punctate signal cluster meeting the following criteria: its mean pixel intensity exceeded a threshold set at three standard deviations above the mean background intensity of a cell-free region in the same image; its area, after automated particle detection, was constrained between 0.1 μm² and 1.0 μm² to exclude diffuse signals and large, non-specific aggregates. For colocalization analysis, spatial overlap was quantified by registering a colocalization event only when ≥60% of the pixels within a probe focus overlapped with the ZBP1-S mask. The results are presented as the number of colocalization events per cell. For ZBP1-S filament visualization and quantification, Zeiss Confocal LSM 980 + Airyscan 2 was used. ZBP1-S filaments in Airyscan images were defined as elongated structures meeting objective morphological criteria: a skeletonized length >0.5 µm, a length-to-width ratio >3, and a form factor <0.5. Images were processed in ImageJ: after background subtraction and thresholding, filaments were enhanced using a directional filter, skeletonized, and analyzed with the “Analyze Skeleton” plugin. The number of filaments per cell was automatically quantified.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC)

BiFC assays were performed to investigate ZBP1 oligomerization. Briefly, the gateway cloning technique was utilized to introduce the ZBP1 gene into GFP10C and GFP11C vectors, resulting in the creation of two ZBP1 constructs containing complementary fragments of GFP. These constructs were co-transfected with TERRA and ZBP1-S into cells, allowing for the assessment of ZBP1 oligomerization based on the reassembly of split GFP fragments. Subsequently, cells were fixed using 4% PFA and counterstained with DAPI in preparation for imaging.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

The cultured cells were subjected to RNA extraction using the RNApure tissue&cell kit (CW0560; CWBIO, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. To synthesize cDNAs, 500 ng of total RNAs were utilized with the PrimeScriptTM RT Master Mix (RR036A; Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The qRT-PCR assays were conducted on a qTOWER2.0 quantitative real-time PCR system (Analytik Jena, Germany) using BeyoFastTM SYBR Green qPCR Mix (D7260; Beyotime, China). The comparative Ct method was employed to calculate the relative expression levels of RNAs. Additionally, melting curve analyses were performed for all PCRs in order to eliminate any non-specific amplification concerns. The primers were described in Supplementary Data 1.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

RIP assays were performed using the Magna RIP RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation kit (Millipore, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. G4 specific antibody BG4 was used for the RIP. Briefly, Cells cultured in 10-cm plates were washed twice with ice-cold PBS, scraped, and pelleted by centrifugation. The cell pellet was resuspended in complete RIP lysis buffer. Lysate was incubated with antibody-conjugated beads overnight at 4 °C in immunoprecipitation buffer. Beads were collected magnetically and washed five times with RIP wash buffer. Immunoprecipitated RNA was eluted by incubation with proteinase K buffer at 55 °C for 30 min, followed by phenol:chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation. Purified RNA was resuspended in RNase-free water and analyzed by qRT-PCR.

Cell viability and cell growth assays

Cell viability and cell growth of MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells with Bio-PDC treatment were assessed using Cell Counting Kit-8 (BA00208; Bioss, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Statistical analysis

The data presented in the figures represent a minimum of three independent experiments. Each corresponding figure and its legend provide information on the number of samples per experiment, as well as details regarding statistical methods and reproducibility. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 13.0 software (Chicago, IL, USA) and GraphPad Prism software (La Jolla, CA, USA). A Student’s t-test was employed to compare quantitative data between two groups. Significance was determined at P < 0.05.
