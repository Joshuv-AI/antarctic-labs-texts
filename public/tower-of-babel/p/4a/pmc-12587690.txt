Clinical evaluation of a functional diet containing Siraitia grosvenorii residual extract for atopic dermatitis in dogs: a self-controlled study
Authors: Mi-Kyung Park, Jihee Hong, Woo Jae Cho, Kun-Ho Song
Published: 2025

Introduction

Canine atopic dermatitis (CAD) is a chronic, pruritic, allergic skin condition affecting genetically predisposed dogs. It typically presents with characteristic clinical signs and is often linked to IgE-mediated hypersensitivity to environmental allergens [1, 2]. CAD is among the most common immunologically mediated dermatoses in dogs, with a reported prevalence of approximately 10–15% [3]. Although its exact pathogenesis remains unclear, CAD is widely believed to arise from a combination of genetic and environmental factors, ultimately resulting in impaired skin barrier function, immune dysregulation, and alterations in the cutaneous microbiota [4]. As a common condition in small animal practice, CAD significantly impairs the quality of life of both affected dogs and their owners and often necessitates long-term, if not lifelong, therapeutic management [5]. Moderate to severe pruritus is typically the earliest and most prominent clinical sign followed by erythema, papules, excoriation, and chronic skin changes [6–9].

Effective management of CAD typically combines environmental control, allergen-specific immunotherapy, and pharmacological agents targeting inflammation and pruritus. Additionally, secondary skin infections caused by bacteria or yeasts must also be addressed to achieve long-term disease control [7, 10, 11].

The fruit of Siraitia grosvenorii (Swingle), commonly referred to as monk fruit or nahangwa in Korean (NHG), belongs to the Cucurbitaceae family. It has traditionally been used in herbal medicine for treating respiratory conditions such as bronchitis, asthma, cough, and sore throat, in addition to being widely used as a natural sweetener [12]. Several studies have demonstrated that the extracts and active constituents of Siraitia grosvenorii exhibit a broad range of pharmacological activities, including antitussive, anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, hypoglycemic, anti-cancer, and antioxidant effects [13]. In vitro, NHGR restored filaggrin expression and reduced TARC, RANTES, and p38 MAPK activation in TNF-α/IFN-γ stimulated HaCaT keratinocytes [14]. In vivo, oral NHGR attenuated allergic skin inflammation and increased filaggrin, claudin-1, and SIRT1 expression in the Dermatophagoides farinae mite antigen extract (DfE)-induced NC/Nga atopic dermatitis model [14]. Based on these findings, we conducted a self-controlled clinical study in dogs with CAD to evaluate the practical efficacy of NHGR-enriched diets.

This study investigated the clinical efficacy of an NHGR containing diet in dogs with CAD using a within-subject (self-controlled) crossover design to compare outcomes before and after dietary intervention.

Materials and methods

Study design and animals

This investigation was conducted as a 24-week, self-controlled clinical trial across multiple Veterinary clinics in South Korea. A total of 32 privately owned dogs clinically diagnosed with atopic dermatitis were enrolled following eligibility screening. Each dog underwent two consecutive 12-week feeding phases. The study was conducted from January to June 2024 in the Republic of Korea; the control phase corresponded to January–March (weeks 0–12), and the treatment phase to March–June (weeks 13–24).:

Study population and baseline characteristics

Thirty-two privately owned dogs with clinician-diagnosed canine atopic dermatitis (CAD) were enrolled after screening at participating clinics. Inclusion criteria were chronic pruritus with compatible signs for ≥ 6 months and fulfillment of Favrot’s criteria; dogs were current on ectoparasite prevention and free of flea infestation. Exclusion criteria included uncontrolled systemic disease, primary infectious dermatoses, or recent systemic immunomodulatory therapy.

At baseline, the cohort included 17 males (1 intact, 16 neutered) and 15 females (2 intact, 13 spayed), with a median age of 5.3 years (IQR 3.5–7.4) and body weight 4.7 kg (IQR 3.7–7.5). Breeds were diverse, with Poodles (n = 7, 21.9%) and Pomeranians (n = 7, 21.9%) most common, followed by Maltese (n = 6, 18.8%), Bichon Frise (n = 5, 15.6%), and other breeds. Baseline CADESI-04, PVAS, and TEWL scores reflected moderate clinical severity. Enrollment occurred between January and June 2024 across multiple clinics in Korea, spanning winter to late spring/early summer.

Diet composition

The control and functional diets were formulated to meet or exceed the Association of American Feed Control Officials (AAFCO) Dog Food Nutrient Profiles for adult maintenance and were isocaloric [15]. Both diets contained similar base ingredients including chicken meal, corn flour, corn gluten meal, beet pulp, and chicken fat. The functional diet was additionally supplemented with 6.5 g/kg diet of Siraitia grosvenorii residual extract (NHGR), which was absent from the control diet. The detailed composition of both diets is shown in Table 1. The NHGR used in this study (Siraitia grosvenorii residual extract; JEIL FEED Co., Daejeon, Republic of Korea; lot no. 20231218) was standardized by the manufacturer to mogroside V at 5.0 mg/g ± 20% (w/w), as documented in the Certificate of Analysis (CoA) (Supplementary Table S1). A single lot was used throughout. Clinical assessments

Clinical evaluations were performed at baseline (week 0) and at weeks 6 and 12 of each feeding phase (control and treatment). The primary clinical parameter was. the Canine Atopic Dermatitis Extent and Severity Index (CADESI-04), a validated scoring. system assessing lesion severity (erythema, excoriation, and lichenification) across multiple anatomical regions [16]. Each lesion was scored from 0 (normal) to 3 (severe), and the total score was used to monitor clinical progression or improvement. Dermatitis severity was graded with the veterinarianassessed CADESI-04. For descriptive categorization, dogs were grouped as mild (10–34), moderate (35–59), or severe (≥ 60) [16].

In addition to CADESI-04, owners completed the Pruritus Visual Analog Scale (PVAS) rating pruritus severity at each visit using a scale of 0–10 (0 = no itch, 10 = worst imaginable itch) [17]. During the treatment phase, the owners rated their subjective satisfaction with diet efficacy using the Owner’s Global Assessment of Treatment Efficacy (OGATE), graded from 0 (no improvement) to 4 (very satisfactory) [18]. Transepidermal water loss (TEWL) was measured using a GPSkin Barrier® device (GPower Inc., Seoul, Republic of Korea) after a 15-min acclimation in a controlled room (22 ± 2℃; 45–55% relative humidity); three consecutive readings per prespecified site were averaged to assess skin barrier function, following Marsella et al. [19]. Higher TEWL values indicated greater skin barrier dysfunction. At each visit, peripheral blood samples were collected for serum total Ig E analyses to evaluate systemic immunologic and inflammatory responses.

All clinical examinations were performed by the same investigator to reduce inter-observer. variability. Changes in medication, deviation from the feeding protocol, or observed. adverse events were recorded. Dogs that required prohibited medications, exhibited. significant distress, or whose owners failed to comply with study instructions were. withdrawn from the study. Owners retained the right to voluntarily withdraw their dogs at any point during the study. No safety concerns were reported during the follow-up period.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of the functional diet.

in improving clinical parameters of CAD. Prior to hypothesis testing, the normality of each continuous variable was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Variables that were normally distributed, such as TEWL, were analyzed using independent t-tests to compare the differences between the control and treatment phases. By contrast, the variables did not follow a normal distribution, CADESI-04, PVAS, and serum IgE, were evaluated using the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to evaluate potential interaction effects between treatment group and time. Where appropriate, post hoc pairwise comparisons were carried out to identify specific changes within and between groups at different time points. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the clinical efficacy of a functional diet containing NHGR in dogs diagnosed with CAD. A self-controlled study design was employed, wherein each subject served as its own control, thereby reducing inter-individual variability and enhancing the statistical power of the analysis. The results revealed that the functional diet significantly improved multiple dermatological and symptomatic parameters associated with CAD, including TEWL, CADESI-04, and PVAS. These findings suggest that dietary supplementation with NHGR may offer therapeutic benefits for managing allergic skin inflammation in dogs.

A marked reduction in TEWL during the test diet period (p < 0.0001) indicated restoration of the epidermal barrier, which is often compromised in CAD due to chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation. The skin barrier is critical for preventing allergen penetration and microbial invasion; thus, its recovery is considered a central therapeutic goal in both human and veterinary dermatology [20, 21]. Furthermore, CADESI-04 scores, which objectively reflect lesion severity and distribution, significantly improved after consumption of the NHGR-containing diet (p < 0.0001), supporting the anti-inflammatory potential of the extract. Similarly, PVAS scores, based on owner-perceived pruritus severity, showed a significant improvement (p < 0.0001), which is clinically important given that pruritus is the most common and distressing symptom for both dogs and their owners. Additionally, the OGATE survey showed that approximately 80.65% of owners observed moderate to marked improvements, highlighting the perceived real-world effectiveness and value of this dietary approach.

These results align with those of previous in vitro murine studies, which demonstrated that S. grosvenorii extracts exert anti-inflammatory and anti-pruritic effects by downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines and restoring barrier-related function [22, 23]. Specifically, modulation of IL-4, IL-13, and thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), which are key mediators of Th2-mediated immune responses, has been observed under atopic conditions [14]. Several studies supported these findings, showing reduced allergic inflammation, histological improvement, and cytokine suppression following administration of NHGR or its bioactive components such as mogroside V [24]. These mechanistic insights complement our clinical findings and suggest that the symptomatic improvements observed in dogs may be linked to similar immunological and epidermal pathways.

However, inconsistencies in the literature remain. A few experimental models did not observe significant dermatological or systemic effects of S. grosvenorii, possibly due to differences in extract composition, dosing, treatment duration, or species-specific factors [25]. These discrepancies emphasize the need for standardized extract formulations and optimized dosing regimens, particularly for translational veterinary applications. To enhance reproducibility, we used a single lot of NHGR standardized to mogroside V; details and the CoA are available in Supplementary Table S1.

This study offers several novel contributions to the field of veterinary dermatology. To our knowledge, it is the first clinical trial to evaluate the therapeutic potential of NHGR in dogs with naturally occurring atopic dermatitis. Unlike previous studies conducted in controlled laboratory settings or in non-clinical species, this investigation was carried out in real-world veterinary setting and employed clinically validated outcome measures. The self-controlled design minimized confounding variables, thereby enhancing internal validity. Importantly, the inclusion of both objective measures (TEWL and CADESI-04) and subjective indices (PVAS and OGATE) allowed for a more holistic assessment of treatment efficacy.

Despite these promising results, several Limitations of this study must be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size and lack of randomization Limit the generalizability of the findings. Moreover, serum IgE concentrations did not differ significantly between the two dietary phases, suggesting that the observed clinical benefits may be localized to the cutaneous immune responses rather than reflecting systemic immunomodulation. It should be noted that the baseline values at the start of the treatment phase did not precisely correspond to the final values of the control phase. This discrepancy reflects intra-individual variability and averaging across subjects, as each phase was analyzed independently rather than in a crossover design. Future studies incorporating, broad immunological profiling, including cytokine assays and skin biopsy-based histopathology, may provide a deeper understanding of the mechanistic pathways involved. The study was conducted from January to June 2024 (winter to late spring/early summer in Korea). Because aeroallergen and meteorological data were not collected, potential seasonal effects cannot be ruled out.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides preliminary evidence suggesting that dietary supplementation with S. grosvenorii residual extract may improve the clinical signs of atopic dermatitis in dogs. These findings support its potential role as a well-tolerated, non-pharmacological, adjunctive treatment strategy for managing CAD. As the present trial did not include a formal safety evaluation, dedicated toxicology and longer-term safety studies in dogs are warranted. Additionally, the self-controlled clinical trial provides evidence that functional pet food supplemented with Siraitia grosvenorii residual extract can effectively alleviate the clinical signs of atopic dermatitis in dogs. The intervention was associated with significant improvements in both objective dermatological parameters and subjective owner-reported outcomes without inducing systemic adverse effects. Further randomized, placebo-controlled trials with larger populations and longer follow-up durations are warranted to confirm these results and explore the broader immunological mechanisms underlying its effects. However, given the current paucity of research on plant-derived antipruritic agents in veterinary medicine, this study may serve as a foundation for future research in this emerging area of integrative dermatological care. Given its favorable safety and tolerability profiles, this nutritional approach may serve as a valuable adjunct to conventional therapeutic strategies for managing chronic canine atopic dermatitis.
