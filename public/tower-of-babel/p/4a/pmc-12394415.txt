Fused RGB and IR image based deep learning detection of dried laver bugak for robotic automation systems
Authors: Tae Hyong Kim, Ah-Na Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In Kimbugak manufacturing system, sorting and loading before frying are still performed manually, which imposes a heavy workload on workers and limits scalability. This study focuses on detecting and classifying the physical characteristics of dried laver bugak to enable robotic pick-and-place operations by developing a You Only Look Once (YOLO) and Real-time Detection Transformer (RT-DETR) deep learning detection model based on fused RGB and infrared (IR) images for integration into a robotic automation system. Through experiments, it was found that at least five physical classes are needed for effective robotic handling. A novel approach combining RGB and IR image fusion using the Visual Geometry Group 19-layer (VGG19) network is introduced to enhance the input quality for detection. Experimental results show that the YOLOv11l model significantly outperforms the YOLOv8s model, achieving an F1 score of 0.94 and a mean Average Precision at 0.5 (mAP@0.5) of 0.95. These results demonstrate that VGG-based image fusion with YOLOv11l is an optimal solution for classifying and locating dried laver bugak. This research highlights the importance of physical class definition, multimodal image fusion, and detector selection in developing an effective automated sorting and loading system for Kimbugak production.

Introduction

Lavers are red seaweed which belong to the genera Porphyra and Pyropia in the Bangiaceae family. They were cultivated in northeastern Asia countries, with South Korea, China, and Japan due to regional environmental conditions favorable for aquaculture1. Recently, many researches have been reported that laver and its product are health food that is rich in health-promoting substances such as essential amino acids, essential fatty acids, minerals, and vitamins2. The awareness of their health benefits led to globalization of processed food products and dramatic increases in consumption across the world3. Generally, most of the raw laver consumed as laver products. For the most products, it is essential to dry raw laver for improving self-life as well as palatability, and the dried laver may be further re-dried, roasted and/or seasoned4. Dried laver is used in the form of paper-like thin-layered traditional side dishes known as ‘Kim’ in South Korea, ‘Nori’ in Japan, and ‘Zicai’ in China5,6.

The majority of the products are sold and exported in the form of seasoned laver and snacks7. In particular, the demand for Kimbugak which is the most representative Korea’s traditional laver snack is continue to be increased8. However, in the laver product industry, the seasoned laver is the only product that is manufactured on a massive scale with machines. It is difficult to meet the demand of Kimbugak throughout mass production because it is produced manually9. Furthermore, manual production is attributed to non-standardized and/or defective snack products. Mechanization of the manufacturing process is necessary to produce Kimbugak in large quantities and standardize the products for industrialization9.

Kimbugak is traditionally produced through a manual process involving the uniform pasting of dried laver with glutinous rice paste, hot-air drying, sorting, and deep-fat frying9–11. However, structural defects such as holes and cracks frequently occur in intermediate or final products. These defects are influenced by the initial quality of raw laver, drying methods, and processing conditions12. Due to its thin and delicate structure, laver is particularly vulnerable to texture changes during thermal processing, which can affect both edibility and commercial value. During drying, moisture migration and stress imbalance may lead to cell rupture, shrinkage, hardening, and cracking13–16.

Building on our previous work, which developed a data-driven simulation system for robotic automation of the Kimbugak frying process17, this study aims to improve product quality through better defect detection. In the current manufacturing process, most defects happen during the drying of laver coated with glutinous rice paste. Despite this, sorting defective semi-products (dried laver bugak) still relies on manual visual inspection, leading to inconsistent results and lower productivity. Therefore, this study proposes a systematic and automated method for defect detection in the pre-frying stage, with the goal of enhancing final product quality and supporting large-scale production.

Most significant contribution of this research is presented as follows: (1) This research collected RGB and Infrared (IR) image of dried laver bugak. (2) Increase performance of detecting physical characteristic of bugak using image alignment and fusion technique. (3) Determination of bugak criteria at least 5 classes using robot automation experiment. (4) The Image-fused deep learning model using You Only Look Once (YOLO) model to evaluate how the model well detect the physical characteristic of bugak. (5) Research demonstrate that Fusion with YOLOv11l has the potential to implement in real-manufacturing process. The following section describe the process of this research. Section 2 provides the background of research purpose and methodology. Section 3 provide the detail methodology of our model. Section 4 provides the results and discussion.

Materials and methods

Dried laver bugak

Dried laver bugak (half-product) was purchased from Namwon Chunhyang Kimbugak (Namwon, Korea) and it was composed of Pyropis yezoensis and Porphyra tenera which is produced by Jangheungmusangin Co., Ltd. (Jangheung-gun, Korea) from November to April 2023. The weight of dried laver bugak was 7.79 ± 1.68 g. The moisture content of the samples was 5.27 ± 1.46% as determined by AOAC method no 934.06. The samples were stored in airtight opaque plastic containers with desiccant at 0 ± 1℃ until use.

Gripping test system

Developed vacuum gripping system to allow pick and place operation of dried laver bugak is composed of a SCARA robot (EPSON, T30) along with custom designed end-effector equipped with vacuum suction cup. The pick and place operation of SCARA robot is done using Epson RC + 7.0 software (Fig. 1). First, the SCARA robot moves to home position to be ready. Next, the SCARA robot end effector moves to coordinates where the sample is located, and then vacuum is engaged by actuation signal of pneumatic controller. The vacuum gripper picked and placed the sample when pneumatic pressure was fully applied.

Fig. 1SCARA robot (EPSON, T30) along with custom designed end-effector equipped with vacuum suction cup to pick and place operation using Epson RC + 7.0 software.

SCARA robot (EPSON, T30) along with custom designed end-effector equipped with vacuum suction cup to pick and place operation using Epson RC + 7.0 software.

Robot gripping test is performed to assess the performance of robotic grippers or end effectors, ensuring they can securely and effectively grasp objects under various conditions with depending on the physical characteristic of dried laver bugak. In this study, vacuum-gripping of the front and back surfaces for one hundred dried laver bugak was conducted using two suction cups such as TB2.5/20-P27-W5 and TB2.5/20-U30-W5 (VMECA Co., Ltd, Seoul, Korea). Top-bellows, top-bellows size, suction lip, and lip material of TB2.5/20-P27-W5 were 2.5 folds, 20 mm diameter, P-type sealing lip (27 diameter), and white silicone (30º hardness), respectively. In the same manner, them of TB2.5/20-U30-W5 were 2.5 folds, 20 mm diameter, U-type sealing lip (30 diameter), and white silicone (50º hardness), respectively. Results such as success rates (%) of gripping and depth (mm) caused by length contraction of suction cup and deformation of sample were obtained and compared.

Image acquisition system

Based on the gripping test, we developed vision-based image acquisition system to acquire dried laver bugak RGB and IR image data for detection of physical characteristic (Fig. 2). Developed vision system composed of camera, light and jig with can fix camera and light system. The light system (LV-ILA-162ST, Light for Vision System, Seoul, South Korea) is connected to main vision system through Ethernet environment to turn on/off light. The developed vision system is mounted on the conveyor belt with distance of 40 cm for automatic image acquisition process (Fig. 2). In this study, we used Intel Realsense D435i camera, which allow to acquire RGB image and IR image at the same time. Based on Intel Realsense SDK, we developed image acquisition software using Python 3.8 and spyder version 6.0.0. Initial setting of image acquisition software is as follow. The size of RGB image is 1,280 by 720 with frame rate of 30 and size of IR image is 640 by 480. The auto exposure with set ROI, decimation filter, threshold filter, spatial filter, temporal filter is applied.

Fig. 2Specially designed vision-based image acquisition system to acquire dried laver bugak RGB and IR image data for detection of physical characteristic of dry laver bugak.

Specially designed vision-based image acquisition system to acquire dried laver bugak RGB and IR image data for detection of physical characteristic of dry laver bugak.

In this study, we developed RGB-IR fused image-based YOLO model for detecting dried laver bugak. We acquired the RGB and IR image of the samples using image acquisition system. Total of 3,067 RGB and IR image is collected for training. These images were divided into training and test dataset with ratio of 0.8 and 0.2, respectively (Fig. 3). The physical characteristic of dried bugaks can be categorized into 5 classes where the backside with no ingradient (Back), frontside with ingradient (Front), the region with hole or crack (Hole&Crack), the region where high deformation during drying dried laver bugak which cannot grip with robot system (NoGripZone), and a completely damaged and fragmented defective sample (Defect). Classifying the back or front side is also important, as incorrect classification may lead to poor gripping performance by the robot system. Next, each class of image is labeled, and the regions are annotated in the ABB bounding boxes format. The bounding boxes are rectangular frame with four coordinates (x_min, y_min, x_max, y_max). Figure 4 represents the different class of dried laver bugak for image labeling process where multiple class or bounding box are annotated in single image. The LabelImage label tool is applied to generate a bounding box for each images18.

Dried laver bugakRGB and IR image fusion

In this study, the initial image processing of image contrast enhancement, sharpening is applied. Next, image alignment process is applied since the size of RGB and IR image is different. A SIFT feature detection algorithm which create scale space and detect extrema, keypoint localization, orientation assignment, and keypoint descriptor is applied with maximum number of feature 500 and matching feature using norm hamming algorithm is applied. After extracting the location of good match point, a homography is estimated by calculating the transformation matrix using the RANSAC algorithm, which is then applied to warp the image. The threshold of 5.0 is applied for RANSAC algorithm. The performance of image alignment is determined by calculating minimum loss for half of extracted feature points. Lastly, there are several image fusion deep learning model such as vision-transformer or generative adversarial network. However, this study requires two deep learning model for dried laver detection for image fusion and object detector, therefore, the RGB image and aligned IR image is fused using VGG-19 deep learning model by consideration of low FLOPs and number of parameters for future model quantization19. Applied VGG-19 deep learning based fusion model is based on image decomposition of source images to obtain the fusion base part and detail content. Next, weight averaging fusion strategy to fuse base parts and detail content is obtained by the multi-layer fusion technique and reconstruction of final fused image using based part and detail contents20. The performance of image fusion is determined by calculate structural similarity where it represents the structural similarity operation between source images.

Dried laver bugakdetection deep learning model

The YOLO deep learning model is a typical end to end network. In 2020, YOLOv5 is developed by Ultralytics which is built based on EfficientDet object detection framework along with single-shot detector architecture. It significantly enhanced detection speed and accuracy compared to previous versions, even with a small dataset21. Next, YOLOv8 is developed with minor change by applying communicating sequential processes (CSP). The C2f module is creased by integrating Extended Efficient Layer Aggregation Network (ELAN) with C3. In addition, the dominant SPPF module is applied in the final layer of the back bone architecture and the output of each layer was concatenated to maintain lightweight architecture. The most recent version of YOLO is version 11 which is published in 202422 where performance of extracting feature and high accuracy with low parameter by applying C2k2, SPPF and C2PSA (Cross Stage Partial with Spatial Attention) instead of SPPF. C3k2 module is composed of C3 block with light convolution layer with kernel of 2 and C2PSA module is implemented after SPPF block by specific region of interest to feature map pooling. In head, C3k2 block is applied to process multi-scale feature in various depth for faster processing and parameter efficiency. In addition, convolution-BatchNorm-SiLU layer is contained for relevant features extraction, batch normalization and Sigmoid Linear Unit activation function for model performance. Therefore, in this study, we applied YOLOv8 with variant size of small and large and v11 with variant size of small and large use input image of visible and IR fused image for detecting laver. Figure 5 represents overall flow of RGB-IR fused image based dried laver bugak YOLO detection model. The gathered RGB and IR image is aligned and fused using VGG-19 model. Fused Model used as input of YOLO detector. Detail structure of YOLOv11 is shown in Fig. 5. In addition, additional deep learning detection model Real-time Detection Transformer (RT-DETR) which is an advanced object detector built on vision transformer architecture. It efficiently handles multi-scale features by separating intra-scale interaction through a hybrid encoder. This model uses IoU-aware query selection to focus on the most relavant objects to improve detection accuracy. Therefore, this RT-DETR is applied for comparison between YOLO-deep learning models. The training and testing YOLO and RT-DETR model is done using PyTorch version 2.4.1 with python version of 3.9.

Fig. 5Overall representative flow of RGB and IR image fused with YOLO detector.

Overall representative flow of RGB and IR image fused with YOLO detector.

Our research applied data augmentation during model training to increase the number of dataset. The flipping image channel, translation, mosaic with 0.5, mixup, flip up and down, flip left and right and scale with 0.2 technique is applied. In addition, our model uses initial learning rate of 0.001, learning rate decay of 0.1, momentum of 0.95, batch size of 0.8, adam optimizer and maximum epochs of 1000 which can enhance the performance of deep learning model. Cross-validation is applied to ensure an early training stop criterion.

In addition, the workstation specifications are: Window 10, Intel core i9-13900HX, GPU with NVIDIA A4500 (24GB), and 128GB of DDR4 RAM. For the experiment result, mean average precision (mAP) based on 50% Intersection over Union (IoU), which calculates the overlap ratio between the predicted bounding box and the ground truth, was computed. Also, the output classification performance is evaluated by recall and F1-score and number of layer, parameters and GFLOPs for each deep learning model is calculated for model efficiency.

Results

Vacuum-gripping the front and back surfaces using different suction cups

The success rates for vacuum-gripping the front and back surfaces of dried laver bugak were investigated. In addition, the two suction cups such as 20-P27-W5 and 20-U30-W5 of the gripper were applied to vacuum gripping the samples (Fig. 6). The success rates for vacuum-gripping the back surface using TB2.5/20-P27-W5 and TB2.5/20-U30-W5 were 96% and 90%, but them for the front surface were 8% and 2%, respectively (Fig. 7a). In comparison to the two suction cups, TB2.5/20-P27-W5 was found to have a better success rate than TB2.5/20-U30-W5. The depth was measured to analyze the distance from contact start position of the cup and sample surface to vacuum gripping position (Fig. 7b). Jaiswal and Kumar23 reported that the depth of suction cup can be used for vacuum suction cup areas that are very irregular and curved. In this study, the average depth of TB2.5/20-P27-W5 and TB2.5/20-U30-W5 were 4.17 and 6.20 mm, respectively. It can be found that vacuum gripping using TB2.5/20-P27-W5 had not only the shorter travel distance and the higher vacuum gripping speed but also the lower sample deformation rate, compared to TB2.5/20-U30-W5. As a result, gripping the back using a vacuum gripper equipped with TB2.5/20-P27-W5 suction pad had superior performance on successful pick and place.

Discussion

For the purpose of automating the production process of Kimbugak, stable conveyance of the half-product, dried laver bugak, prior to frying is essential. Due to its extremely fragile nature, a pick-and-place operation using a SCARA robot equipped with a vacuum gripping system was selected. In this study, the gripping success rate of the front and back sides was preliminarily tested using a vacuum gripper equipped with two suction cups, TB2.5/20-P27-W5 and TB2.5/20-U30-W5. The vacuum gripping equipped with TB2.5/20-P27-W5 of the back sides showed the highest gripping success rate, achieving 96%. In vacuum gripping system, there are three categories of leakage occurs within the suction surface at an opening in the workpiece, between the contact surface of the suction cup and the workpiece, and air permeability of the workpiece24. The vacuum requires is more prone to mishaps due to misaligned suction cups that fail to achieve an airtight seal23. The front is dried after applying various ingredients including glutinous rice paste and garnish (i.e. sesame), while nothing is applied to the back of the sample. An uneven and coarse front surface of the sample may cause significant leakage, resulting in very low success rate of vacuum gripping25. The result indicated that a screening operation which can detect front and back is necessary to increase the success rates by placing the back face upward and gripping the back surface.

However, research is necessary to further improve the gripping success rate of dried laver bugak. The failure of vacuum gripping has been shown to be attributed to three major factors: holes, cracks, and severe deformation. The vacuum-gripping can be conducted when the internal pressure of the enclosed space between the suction pad and the item lower than atmospheric pressure with a vacuum pump, but the holes and cracks of the object lead to vacuum leakages26. Drying causes irreversible structural damage to the cellular structure of foods because it is a complex process which coupled heat and mass transfer processes attended by physical, chemical, and phase change transformations27. The moisture gradient will drive water transfer within the cracker in order to reach equilibrium. The sections of the crackers that will uptake water will expand whereas the sections that will lose water will shrink, resulting in thermo and/or hydro mechanical stress that may cause cracks and eventually breakage, particularly if the product is in a glassy state28. Among the drying methods, the hot air drying is the traditional and the most widely used method to produce the half-products of Kimbugak, but the long drying times and high surface temperature may cause negative effects on physical qualities such as appearance, microstructure, and texture29. The structural damages have an effect to on mass diffusion coefficient, thermal conductivity, and thermal diffusivity during drying or frying. Then, the three failure factors can be caused by the excessive shrinkage and deformation of the size and shape, and eventually breakage, resulting in defective snack products. This study developed the deep learning model to detect five classes- ‘Back’, ‘Front’, ‘Hole&Crack’, ‘NoGripZone’, and ‘Defect’-to achieve perfect success rate in vacuum-gripping of the dried laver bugak.

This study applied a VGG-19 based fusion model to create a new RGB-IR fused image, leveraging the network’s powerful multi-scale and hierarchical feature extraction capabilities. Infrared and visible image fusion techniques leverage the unique features captured by different sensors, preserving complementary information from each source while enhancing the overall reliability of the resulting fused image30. The proposed fusion method based on the VGG-19 convolutional neural network represents a significant advancement over traditional pixel-level and transform-domain fusion techniques, offering a deep feature-level approach that effectively extracts and combines high-level semantic information from both modalities31. One of the main strengths of using the VGG-19 network lies in its ability to extract multi-scale and hierarchical features from input images32,33. By leveraging both shallow and deep layers of the network, the fusion algorithm captures fine-grained textures from visible images and high-contrast thermal signatures from infrared images, resulting in a fused output that preserves critical information from both sources. This multi-level feature representation enables the fused image to maintain both clarity and semantic richness34. This approach makes the method particularly adaptable and efficient, especially for domains where collecting large datasets is impractical. The ability to utilize transfer learning also makes the model scalable to real-time systems and broad applications such as surveillance, autonomous vehicles, search-and-rescue operations, and then food manufacturing35. By combining these complementary data sources, the VGG-19-based fusion method can effectively monitor food products during production, ensuring uniformity36,37. In summary, the VGG-19-based infrared and visible image fusion method demonstrates notable advantages in terms of feature representation, robustness, and generalization. It opens up promising avenues for further research, including integration with self-supervised learning, multi-modal transformers, or real-time hardware optimization for deployment in embedded systems38.

Then, we developed RGB-IR fused images based deep learning detection model to detect five classes of dried laver bugak: ‘Back’, ‘Front’, ‘Hole & Crack’, ‘NoGripZone’, and ‘Defect’. The ‘Back’ and ‘Front’ classes showed consistently high mAP@.5 above 0.99, regardless of the model used. For ‘Defect’ class, only the YOLOv11l model achieved an mAP@.5 above 0.99. In contrast to the other classes, the ‘Hole&Crack’ and ‘NoGripZone’ classes exhibited markedly higher mAP values with YOLOv11 models compared to RT-DETR models. Consequently, the YOLOv11l model utilizing RGB-IR fused images achieved the highest detection performance across all classes. The observed superiority of the YOLOv11 model over YOLOv8 and RT-DETR in the task of detecting the bugak across multiple defect classes can be attributed to a combination of architectural enhancements and methodological innovations. YOLOv11 likely incorporates a deeper and more efficient backbone architecture, which allows it to extract more discriminative and semantically rich features from the RGB-IR fused images22. This is particularly important in multimodal image analysis, where the model must effectively integrate complementary visual cues from both thermal (infrared) and visible spectra. Additionally, YOLOv11 benefits from improved training strategies such as adaptive label assignment, advanced loss functions (e.g., SIoU or focal loss), and regularization techniques including label smoothing and dropout39. These enhancements collectively contribute to better generalization and robustness, particularly for fine-grained classification tasks. Notably, classes such as ‘Hole&Crack’ and ‘Defect’—which often involve small, low-contrast regions—were detected with higher precision using YOLOv11, suggesting that the model’s multi-scale feature representation is more effective than that of YOLOv840. In addition, the computational efficiency was analyzed in order for our developed model’s real-time robot automation expandability. The RT-DETR model showed highest number of layers and parameters along with GFLOPs which means the computational cost is expensive and require longer detection time. The lowest GFLOPs was YOLOv11s model where highest GFLOPs was RT-DETRx model with almost 10-fold. Furthermore, the architecture of YOLOv11 appears to be more compatible with RGB-IR fused imagery, enabling the model to fully exploit the added depth information and thermal contrast that are not available in visible-light images alone41. As a result, the YOLOv11 model demonstrated consistently higher mAP values across most classes and outperformed rest of deep learning model implemented in this study in overall detection accuracy. This makes it a promising candidate for deployment in quality control systems within food manufacturing environments, where detecting subtle defects with high reliability is critical21.

Conclusions

A deep learning-based detection model was successfully developed to classify five different classes of dried laver bugak, aiming to enhance the success rate of vacuum gripping during the automated pick-and-place process. The YOLOv11l model demonstrated superior performance in distinguishing between Front, Back, Hole&Crack, NoGripZone, and Defect classes, which are critical factors affecting gripping stability. It is expected to contribute to reducing gripping failures caused by severe deformation or surface defects. Future research will focus on improving the model’s generalization capability by incorporating larger and more diverse datasets, including multi-spectral imaging to better capture defect characteristics. Additionally, while the current model effectively detects predominant defect types, real-world manufacturing environments present a broader spectrum of irregular and rare defects. Addressing this challenge requires expanding the defect classes and mitigating data imbalance through advanced augmentation strategies, including synthetic defect generation and GAN-based data augmentation. To enhance the practical deployment of the YOLOv11 model in industrial settings, further optimization for real-time processing is imperative. This includes reducing computational complexity through model compression techniques such as pruning, quantization, and hardware-specific acceleration frameworks like TensorRT. Such efforts will significantly improve the feasibility of on-site defect detection with low latency. Moreover, integration with real-time feedback control systems and advanced robotic gripping strategies will be explored to further minimize failure rates. Overall, this study lays the groundwork for fully automated dried laver bugak manufacturing, and ongoing efforts will continue to optimize detection accuracy and robotic handling for enhanced production efficiency.
