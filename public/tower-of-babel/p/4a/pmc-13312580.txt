Non-destructive diagnosis of ambrosia beetle attacks via detection of the associated tree pathogens
Authors: Hayato Masuya, Akira Ishikawa, Yukako Hattori, Yukiko S. Takahashi, Shota Jikumaru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ambrosia beetles have become major threats to forest ecosystems, often vectoring pathogenic fungi and causing severe tree mortality. Early infestations produce only a few frass-filled pinholes, making species identification difficult without destructive sampling, which may further attract beetles. We developed a non-destructive detection method based on the specific fungal symbionts of Platypus quercivorus (Dryadomyces quercivorus) and Euwallacea interjectus (Fusarium kuroshium). Plastic inoculating needles inserted into beetle pinholes allowed sampling without injuring trees. Fungal detection was achieved through either fungal isolation, or direct DNA analysis using species-specific primers for both symbionts. This approach enables reliable, non-destructive identification of ambrosia beetles through their associated fungi.•We developed a non-destructive method that detects species-specific fungal tree pathogens of wood‑boring ambrosia beetles using plastic inoculating needles, enabling indirect identification of the associated beetle species.•For fungal detection, we confirmed the utility of species-specific primers for Fusarium kuroshium and developed a species-specific probe for Dryadomyces quercivorus.•This study provides the first report of non-destructive detection and identification of ambrosia beetles through their associated tree pathogens.

Background

In recent years, widespread tree mortality caused by ambrosia beetles has been reported in many countries [1]. Ambrosia beetles maintain an obligate symbiotic relationship with fungi that provide essential nutrition for their larvae [2]. These fungal symbionts can also act as pathogens of host trees [3], and ambrosia beetles can serve as vectors of additional pathogenic fungi, thereby playing a central role in forest decline [3]. A well-documented example is Japanese oak wilt, associated with Platypus quercivorus. Since the 1990s, infestations have spread throughout Japan, causing extensive tree mortality in multiple regions [4]. In addition, Euwallacea interjectus has been recorded infesting fig trees in Japan, but it is known to have a broad host range and is therefore considered a potential threat to forest ecosystems [5]. In North America, this species is recognized as a major invasive pest [6], while in China it has been reported attacking poplars and numerous other tree species [7].

Various management strategies have been proposed for ambrosia beetles [8]. Among these, early detection in areas not yet affected and rapid diagnosis of infested trees are particularly critical. However, in regions where beetle populations are still sparse, the use of pheromone traps may inadvertently attract beetles and exacerbate infestations. Consequently, early-stage diagnosis still relies primarily on visual inspection during field surveys.

Infestations are generally assessed by detecting the beetles themselves. Adult beetles can be reliably identified based on morphology; however, when only entrance holes or frass are present, species-level identification becomes difficult. In heavily infested stands, large accumulations of frass (Fig. 1a) may contain adult beetles, enabling straightforward identification. In contrast, during the early stages of an outbreak, entrance holes may be few, increasing the likelihood of misidentification with other ambrosia beetle species. For example, holes produced by E. validus may closely resemble those of E. interjectus or P. quercivorus (Fig. 1b–d). Furthermore, E. validus is morphologically similar to E. interjectus (Fig. 1f, g), although it is readily distinguishable from P. quercivorus (Fig. 1e). Traditionally, accurate identification under such conditions has required destructive approaches such as tree felling or log splitting. While these methods are suitable for dead trees, destructive sampling of living trees may attract additional beetles and promote further spread. In recent study, molecular identification was achieved from frass of an ambrosia beetle Xyleborinus saxesenii [9] but DNA extraction from frass is labor-intensive, and the extracted DNA often contains PCR inhibitors that hinder amplification. Because ambrosia beetles obligatorily carry and depend on specific fungal symbionts for their development [e.g., 2], the identity of the associated fungi, especially with problematic ambrosia beetles, is typically well established. Consequently, beetle species can often be identified indirectly through their associated fungi. Like other members of the genus Euwallacea, E. interjectus is associated with fungi of the genus Fusarium, which includes several important plant pathogens. Fusarium floridanum has been reported from E. interjectus in North America, whereas F. kuroshium has been detected in Japan [7]. In contrast, P. quercivorus consistently carries Dryadomyces quercivorus, which contributes to the rapid mortality of oak trees [10]. These associations are well supported in previous studies and provide a biologically grounded basis for indirect beetle identification.

Building on these beetle–symbiont relationships—specifically, P. quercivorus with D. quercivorus and E. interjectus with F. kuroshium—we developed a non-destructive diagnostic method that identifies the ambrosia beetle species through detection of their specific fungal symbionts. This study presents a reproducible, non-destructive molecular diagnostic approach that eliminates the need for destructive sampling or direct beetle collection. By addressing the limitations of existing methods, this approach enables early-stage diagnosis while minimizing damage to living host trees and reducing the risk of inadvertently promoting further beetle attraction.

Method details

The method consists of two core steps: (i) non-destructive sampling from beetle entrance holes, and (ii) detection of associated fungal symbionts. The detection step can be implemented using two alternative strategies: (ii-a) fungal isolation followed by species identification, or (ii-b) molecular detection, which includes optimized DNA extraction targeting fungal symbionts and species identification using symbiont-specific molecular markers.(i)Non-destructive sampling from beetle entrance holes.

(i)Non-destructive sampling from beetle entrance holes.

Non-destructive sampling from beetle entrance holes.

For sample collection, a plastic rod approximately 1 mm in diameter (Inoculating Needle NEST WNB716001) (Fig. 2a & b) was used. This size is suitable for insertion into the entry holes of Platypus quercivorus and Euwallacea interjectus, but not for the smaller-sized P. calumus and E. fornicates, which sometimes co-occur with them. The needle was inserted into the entry hole until resistance was felt. The insertion depth varies depending on the hole but was typically 2–3 cm from the bark surface for both target species. The inserted needle was then withdrawn, and the tip portion (2 cm) was placed into a 2 ml sterilized microtube(Fig. 2c). The sample was then transported to the laboratory for detection analysis. Samples could be kept at room temperature (20- 25 °C) within two days but for DNA integrity and culture success, these should be stored at 4 °C and processed within 48 h.(i)Detection of associated fungal symbionts.Fig. 2Tools for non-destructive sampling from beetle entrance holes. a: a plastic needle 1 mm in diameter, b: inserted needle, c: microtube contains a tip of a plastic needle. Fungal symbionts associated with the inoculating needle were detected using two complementary approaches.(a)Culture-based isolation.

(a)Culture-based isolation.

Culture-based isolation.

The distal 2 cm of the needle tip was excised and placed onto selective culture media. For samples collected from P. quercivorus entrance holes, 1% malt extract agar supplemented with 200 ppm cycloheximide (200 mg cycloheximide, 10 g Difco malt extract, and 15 g agar per liter) was used. For samples from E. interjectus entrance holes, 0.1% potato dextrose agar (0.29 g Difco potato dextrose broth and 15 g agar per liter of distilled water) was used.

Plates were incubated at room temperature, and emerging hyphae were observed after one week. Hyphal tips were transferred to 2% malt agar to establish pure cultures. Isolates were initially screened based on colony morphology, hyphal characteristics, and conidial morphology. Representative isolates exhibiting traits consistent with members of the families Ophiostomataceae and Nectriaceae were selected for species identification using a combination of morphological examination and DNA-based analyses. Bacterial contamination has often occurred but target associates with P. quercivorus and E. interjectus can grow rapidly and easily overgrow on the contaminants. If growth of the target fungi is prohibited against contaminants, isolation medium can be amended with 200 ppm streptomycin or other antibiotics.(a)DNA-Based Detection.

(a)DNA-Based Detection.

DNA-Based Detection.

The 2 cm needle tip was further divided into four segments of approximately 0.5 cm each and placed into a 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tube. A volume of 100 µl of PrepMan™ Ultra Sample Preparation Reagent (ThermoFisher Inc.) was added, followed by vortexing for 1 min. The samples were then incubated at 98 °C for 10 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 2 min to extract DNA. The resulting supernatant could be used as template for PCR without any treatment.

We relied on curated reference sequences from previous taxonomic and ecological studies (e. g. Kubono & Ito 2002; Jiang et al. 2023). For the detection of Fusarium kuroshium, the primers developed and validated by Vázquez-Rosas-Landa et al. [11] were employed. Among the three primer pairs they proposed, the following two pairs demonstrated superior specificity and sensitivity for F. kuroshium and were used in this study: FuKu01F (GGACAAATGCCTTGGATGCG) and FuKu01R (CCTTTGCCACATTGGATCGC), and FuKu03F (AAGACGAGTGCAGAGATGGC) and FuKu03R (CGCCTTCTGTTGCTGTATGC). For the rapid detection, we used thermal cycler T100 (Applied Biosystems) and DNA polymerase, KOD One (TOYOBO Co., Ltd.) with the following PCR conditions: 35 cycles of 98 C for 5 s, 56C for 5 s, and 68C for 5 s. The resulting amplicons were checked with agarose gel electrophoresis and determined the positive or negative for each sample.

Both the cultured isolates and the DNA extracts were analyzed using specific primers and probe targeting partial LSU ribosomal DNA for the detection of D. quercivorus. The primer pair, Amb-LSU-F (TATCAATAAGCGGAGGAAAAGAAACC), Amb-LSU-R (GAGTCGAGT(A/T)GTTTGGGAATGC), and a D. quercivorus-specific probe, Dq-P (FAM-CCCGGCGAGTTCCCTGGAA-[BHQ1]) were newly designed on the sequence data of LSU (AB552937) on the ex-type isolate of D. quercivorus. In this study, we used Quantum studio 5, a real time PCR system, and TaqPath™ qPCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Inc.) with following PCR condition: initial denaturation at 95C for 2 min with 40 cycles of 95C for 10 s and 60C for 30 s.

To verify the effectiveness of this method, both non-blind and blind experiments were conducted. For non-blind test, target beetles were confirmed and identified prior to examine the associated fungi. Also, the fungi were isolated from the beetles and their galleies and identified by using morphology, DNA sequence,and PCR. For blind test, unknown entrance holes were examined by needle detection. In the non-blind test, beetle specimens were collected from wood and the exterior of tunnels in Schefflera heptaphylla (Kagoshima Prefecture), Quercus crispula, and Q. serrata, and identified to the beetle species. Euwallacea interjectus was confirmed in S. heptaphylla, E. validus in Q. crispula, and Platypus quercivorus in Q. serrata. These beetles produce entry holes of nearly identical sizes, making it difficult to distinguish species based solely on frass or hole characteristics (see Figs. 1b-d). Fungal isolation was performed for each beetle species, confirming the associations of Fusarium kuroshium with E. interjectus, but not with E. validus, and Dryadomyces quercivorus with P. quercivorus. Blind test: design, sampling, and results.

In the blind test, sampling was conducted on both living and dead trees bearing beetle entrance holes in Ficus carica (Hiroshima Prefecture), Wisteria floribunda (Tochigi Prefecture), S. heptaphylla (Kagoshima Prefecture), Q. crispula (Hokkaido and Ibaraki Prefectures), and Q. serrata (Ibaraki Prefecture).

To detect early-stage beetle attacks, a monitoring site for P. quercivorus was also established in the Ogawa Academic Reference Forest in northern Ibaraki Prefecture, where surveys were conducted from 2022 to 2023. Although P. quercivorus was first confirmed at this site in 2020, no tree mortality had been observed until recently. Beetle entrance holes were observed on ten trees of Q. serrata, one of Q. crispula, one of Fagus crenata, one of Carpinus tschonoskii, and one of Prunus grayana. For each tree, three to five entrance holes were sampled using a plastic needle approximately 1 mm in diameter. The needle was inserted as deeply as possible into each hole and then withdrawn. The tip was placed into a sterilized 2 mL microtube and transported to the laboratory for fungal detection. If any of the three to five holes per tree tested positive, the tree was classified as positive.

As a result, F. kuroshium was detected in all samples from F. carica and W. floribunda (Table 1). Based on these findings, we predicted that E. interjectus was present in both regions, which was consistent with prior captures of this species at the same locations. Furthermore, F. kuroshium was detected from the entrance holes of S. heptaphylla, suggesting that these holes were also caused by E. interjectus. To confirm this, wood pieces were cut from the logs, and E. interjectus was successfully detected. In the E. Interjectus-F. kuroshium system, there were no difference in detection between culture-based and DNA-based detection. F. kuroshium could be successfully detected from all entry hole of Ficus by both isolation and PCR methods. In the P. quercivorus-D. quercivorus ststem, notably, the selective media method yielded positive results for most sampled entry holes, whereas DNA-based detection exhibited lower sensitivity, with several samples falling below the detection threshold. It remains unclear whether this discrepancy was due to inefficient DNA extraction or low fungal loads. The fungal load on the needle may be extremely low in early infestations, making direct PCR less reliable. In other possibility, PCR inhibitors orignating from the woods may be adhering to the needles. While, the isolation method allows amplification of even very small fungal inocula, effectively increasing detection sensitivity. Also, selective media enrich the target fungi while suppressing competitors, improving detection.

Regarding detection time, the selective media method typically required about two weeks to obtain pure fungal cultures. However, performing species-specific PCR directly on hyphae from primary isolation reduced the detection period to approximately one week. In contrast, direct DNA extraction enabled completion of the detection process within a single day, offering a substantial time advantage. The combined use of both methods may therefore allow more rapid, reliable, and practical diagnosis of Japanese oak wilt and E. interjectus infestations.

Limitations

The method developed here enables detection of D. quercivorus and F. kuroshium in Japan, allowing identification of the associated ambrosia beetle species without causing injury to host trees. However, beetle–fungus associations may vary among regions, which could limit the applicability of this approach outside Japan. For example, in North America, E. interjectus is associated with F. floridanum rather than F. kuroshium [12], and in China it associates with multiple Fusarium species [4], making species-level identification more challenging. Nevertheless, if the specific fungal associates are known, our method could be adapted to each region.

In Japan, F. kuroshium is also carried by E. fornicatus [13]; therefore, detection of this fungus does not necessarily indicate E. interjectus activity. Because E. fornicatus has a smaller body size [14], the needle used in our sampling procedure did not fit its gallery entrance holes. With appropriate modifications to needle dimensions and the use of primers targeting each fungal associate, this method could be extended to detect additional ambrosia beetle species non-destructively.

Sampling for P. quercivorus was conducted from October to December, and no seasonal variation in detection success was observed during this period. However, early-stage entry holes formed in May–June were not examined, and further studies are needed to evaluate potential seasonal effects on detection rates. Future research should also focus on optimizing DNA extraction protocols and improving molecular detection sensitivity.

Isothermal DNA amplification techniques such as LAMP and RPA show promise for rapid, on-site diagnosis [15]. Although development and reagent costs remain challenges, integrating these approaches with our method could substantially enhance diagnostic efficiency and enable real-time monitoring in the field.
