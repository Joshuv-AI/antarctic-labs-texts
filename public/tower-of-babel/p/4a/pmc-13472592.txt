A Longitudinal Analysis of Frontotemporal Dementia Patients in Spain throughout the COVID-19 Pandemic: A Descriptive Study
Authors: Verónica Puertas Martín, Sara Llamas-Velasco, Marta González-Sánchez, Víctor Blanco Palmero, Alberto García Redondo, David Andrés Pérez Martínez, Alberto Villarejo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFrontotemporal dementia (FTD) comprises a heterogeneous group of neurodegenerative syndromes characterized by diverse behavioural, cognitive, and language profiles. Longitudinal data describing its clinical course progression using FTD-specific instruments remain limited. This study aimed to characterize the functional, cognitive, and behavioural trajectories of FTD clinical syndromes in a Spanish cohort, including assessments performed remotely during the COVID-19 pandemic.MethodsSixty-seven patients diagnosed with behavioural variant FTD (bvFTD), non-fluent variant primary progressive aphasia, or semantic variant PPA (svPPA) were prospectively followed at Hospital Universitario 12 de Octubre (Madrid, Spain). Functional and neuropsychiatric status were assessed with the Frontotemporal Dementia Rating Scale (FTD-FRS), the Interview for Deterioration in Daily Activities in Dementia (IDDD), the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI), and the telephone Mini-Mental State Examination (t-MMSE). Thirty-three assessments were conducted remotely during pandemic restrictions.ResultsOver a mean follow-up of 3.1 years, significant declines were observed in FTD-FRS, IDDD, and t-MMSE (p < 0.001), whereas NPI scores remained stable. The svPPA group exhibited the greatest cognitive and functional decline, while bvFTD showed more heterogeneous progression. Twelve participants (18%) died during follow-up, predominantly older men with poorer baseline performance.ConclusionFunctional and cognitive decline in FTD varies across clinical variants, with the semantic subtype showing the most rapid progression in our cohort. Remote assessments are feasible and represent a valid approach for longitudinal monitoring.

Introduction

The term frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD) was first introduced in 1998 by Swedish and British researchers to describe the neuropathological process characterized by frontal and anterior temporal atrophy underlying progressive behavioural changes [1]. Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) comprises the clinical syndromes resulting from FTLD pathology [2]. It is one of the most frequent causes of young-onset dementia and accounts for approximately 5% of neuropathologically confirmed dementia cases (incidence 9.4 per 100,000 person-years under the age of 65), ranking second only to Alzheimer’s disease among presenile dementias [2]. The main clinical presentations include the behavioural variant of FTD (bvFTD), the semantic variant of primary progressive aphasia (svPPA), and the non-fluent variant of primary progressive aphasia (nfvPPA), each associated with distinct underlying pathologies (FTLD-tau, FTLD-TDP, and FTLD-FUS) [3–7].

FTLD can occur in both familial and sporadic forms. Approximately one-third of cases are familial, most commonly associated with mutations in GRN, MAPT, and C9orf72. Structural MRI typically demonstrates progressive frontotemporal grey-matter atrophy across genotypes, whereas prominent white-matter hyperintensities are a characteristic feature in GRN mutation carriers [8–10].

Functional impairment represents a key criterion for the diagnosis of clinically probable FTD [3]. However, the early predominance of behavioural and executive deficits, alongside relative preservation of memory, limits the usefulness of general dementia severity scales such as the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) and the Global Deterioration Scale (GDS), which were primarily developed to track Alzheimer’s disease progression [3]. Moreover, patients with FTD frequently experience diagnostic delays and a higher use of psychoactive medication [11–14].

To overcome these limitations, FTD-specific instruments, such as the Frontotemporal Dementia Rating Scale (FTD-FRS) and the Deterioration in Daily Activities in Dementia (IDDD), have been developed. These tools focus on caregiver-reported changes in daily functioning, loss of initiative and decline relative to premorbid functioning, and are better suited to capture the functional impact of FTD [15, 16].

During the COVID-19 pandemic [17], many patients were confined to their homes, had limited access to hospital services, and could not be monitored in person. Consequently, the assessment of cognitive and functional progression relied increasingly on alternative approaches, including telephone- and Internet-based evaluations [18]. Nevertheless, global cognitive screening instruments such as the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and its telephone adaptations have limited sensitivity to executive and behavioural disturbances and may underestimate or mischaracterize impairment in frontal phenotypes. Therefore, greater emphasis should be placed on functional and caregiver-based instruments specifically designed or validated for FTD, such as the FTD-FRS and related functional scales.

The aim of the present study was to characterize the clinical trajectory of patients with different FTD variants in a clinical cohort from a hospital in Madrid. To this end, we employed a validated instrument specifically developed for the assessment of FTD. Additionally, in response to the restrictions imposed by the COVID-19 pandemic, telephone-based assessments were incorporated to allow continuous and comprehensive monitoring of patient progression.

Methods

Participants

A total of 67 patients were assessed in this study. Participants were consecutively recruited through convenience sampling from the Dementia Unit of Hospital 12 de Octubre in Madrid, Spain. Assessments were conducted in the clinic, at the participants’ homes, or within care facilities. The recruitment period spanned from November 2015 to November 2017, with the second visit period ranging from June 2019 to April 2020. Notably, 33 patients were evaluated during quarantine periods, wherein assessments were conducted via telephone interviews. Participants were included at baseline if they met the following criteria:1.Fulfillment of current diagnostic criteria for the behavioural variant of FTD, as outlined by Rascovsky et al. [3], or for svPPA or nfvPPA, according to the criteria of Gorno-Tempini et al. [19]2.Availability of a reliable proxy informant capable of reporting on the patient’s everyday functioning and behaviour. Informants were considered reliable if they either cohabited with the patient or had regular interactions and were able to provide clear, accurate information regarding functional and behavioural status3.Absence of major physical impairments necessitating mobility aids or influencing activities of daily living4.Absence of major depression

Diagnosis process: a multidisciplinary consensus was reached based on a complete medical history, neurological examination, a comprehensive neuropsychological battery assessing memory, attention, executive function, language, and visuospatial abilities, cranial magnetic resonance imaging, and brain fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography. Estimated disease duration was calculated at the time of diagnosis, considering the reported onset of symptoms provided by the informant. It is important to note that the measure included in this study was not utilized in the diagnostic process.

Prior to participation, all patients and their caregivers received comprehensive explanations regarding the objectives of the study, and written informed consent was obtained. The study protocol underwent review and approval by the local hospital committee, in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Instruments

The Frontotemporal Dementia Rating Scale (FTD-FRS) was developed based on items from the Cambridge Behavioural Inventory and the Disability Assessment for Dementia [15]. It is a 30-item questionnaire assessing domains such as Behaviour, Outing and Shopping, Household Chores, Telephone, Finances and Correspondence, Medications, Meal Preparation, Eating, Self-care, and Mobility. The scale was designed to evaluate disease severity and progression in FTD. Response options for each item are “all the time = 0,” “sometimes = 0,” and “never = 1.” The examiner sums the number of items marked as “never” and divides by the total number of items answered, generating a percentage that represents functional preservation while accounting for the patient’s premorbid abilities (tasks never performed are excluded from the score). After calculating the percentage of preservation, the score should be converted to a logarithm, and the severity of the disease is established (very mild, mild, moderate, severe, very severe, and profound). The FTD-FRS demonstrates strong psychometric properties, with internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) of 0.93 and test-retest reliability of 0.994. A Spanish validation is available [16], and a conversion guide is freely accessible via the Frontier Frontotemporal Dementia Research Group Website (www.ftdrg.org).

The FTD-FRS was scored according to the original procedure: the number of “never” responses was divided by the number of applicable items to calculate a percentage of functional preservation. This percentage was used as a continuous measure in the longitudinal analyses; however, the corresponding log-transformed values were not used to allocate patients to the established severity stages (very mild to profound) because the variable was not categorized.

The telephone version of the Mini-Mental State Examination (t-MMSE) was developed by Reference [20] and based on items from the original Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) (21 is a brief global cognitive screening instrument). It comprises seven items assessing the following cognitive domains: orientation, language comprehension and expression, attention, and memory. Total scores range from 0 to 22, with higher scores indicating better cognitive status. In a Spanish validation study, the t-MMSE demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.86; 95% CI, 0.80–0.90) and a moderate to strong correlation with the standard MMSE (Spearman’s r = 0.76; p < 0.001) [21].

In the present study, the t-MMSE was used to provide a brief, longitudinal overview of global cognitive status, rather than as a disease-specific staging instrument. Given its limited sensitivity to executive dysfunction and to language-dominant presentations, results were interpreted with caution, particularly in behavioural variant FTD and primary progressive aphasia subtypes.

Although telephone-based assessments were valuable for serial monitoring under pandemic-related restrictions, they do not allow direct observation of behaviour, motor performance, or non-verbal executive tasks. Consequently, impairments in these domains may be underestimated.

The Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI), short version, is a 10-item questionnaire used to assess the presence, frequency, and severity of neuropsychiatric and behavioural symptoms [22]. Each domain yields a maximum score of 12 points, calculated by multiplying symptom frequency by severity. The domains assessed include delusions, hallucinations, agitation and aggression, dysphoria, anxiety, euphoria, apathy, disinhibition, irritability/lability, aberrant motor activity, night-time behaviours, and changes in appetite. Higher scores indicate greater symptom severity and frequency, with total scores ranging from 0 to 144, where 0 indicates the absence of behavioural disturbances. In the Spanish validation study, the test-retest reliability of the NPI using Pearson correlation index was r = 0.89 for total symptom scale and r = 0.90 for distress scale [23].

Interview for Deterioration in Daily Activities in Dementia (IDDD): the scale consists of concretely worded items reflecting both the initiative to perform activities and the actual performance of self-care and more complex daily activities [24]. The adapted version comprises 33 items divided into 2 sections: 16 items assessing basic activities of daily living and 17 items assessing instrumental activities of daily living. All items are rated on a three-point scale and summed to obtain a global score. Two additional response options are available for each item: 8 = “never performed this activity” and 9 = “the caregiver cannot judge the initiative or performance of the activity.” These responses are excluded from the final score calculation. The IDDD has demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.985) and high reproducibility (intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.94) [25].

The IDDD provides caregiver-based ratings that capture both the initiative to begin activities and the actual performance of basic and instrumental activities of daily living. This distinction is particularly relevant in FTD, in which executive and motivational deficits may lead to reduced initiation despite relatively preserved basic motor abilities [26]. The FTD-FRS and IDDD were used as the primary measures of functional progression, whereas the t-MMSE served as a secondary, descriptive index of global cognitive status over time.

Procedures

The patients attended the neurology clinic at Hospital 12, where they underwent thorough examination by neurologists from the Dementia Unit (S.L.V., M.G.S., V.B.P., D.P.M., and A.V.G.). The neurologists determined whether patients met the study criteria, gathered clinical data encompassing cardiovascular risk factors, emotional symptoms, and disease duration and established the specific FTD subtype. For eligible patients, blood samples were obtained for genetic analyses, and they were scheduled for a cognitive evaluation via telephone interview 1 week later. The data collection process typically lasted around 20 min and was administered by a trained examiner (V.P.M.).

Subsequent follow-up visits were initially planned annually and synchronized with the patients’ routine neurologist appointments, which occur every 6 months. However, the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic led to substantial disruptions in the visit schedule, resulting in follow-up intervals ranging from 3 to 51 months (median [IQR]: 29 [18–42] months). During these follow-up visits, participant deaths were documented based on information provided by caregivers. This heterogeneity in follow-up intervals was accounted for by calculating the annualized rate of change (ARC) for each measure, as described below. The instruments were consistently administered in a standardized sequence. Initially, the t-MMSE was conducted with the patient, followed by the involvement of a designated family member, who remained consistent across both visits. Subsequently, the FTD-FRS, NPI, and IDDD were administered in succession.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive statistics were employed to characterize the patient sample. Following tests of normality (Kolmogorov-Smirnoff), several variables of interest were found to deviate from a normal distribution. Therefore, a non-parametric approach was adopted, utilizing Mann-Whitney tests for group comparisons and Chi-square tests for comparing proportions. In instances involving more than two groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied, while Wilcoxon tests were utilized to assess significant differences in test-retest scores. To evaluate changes between test-retest assessments, the ARC for each patient was computed using the formula outlined by Rascovsky et al. [28]:ARC=scoreatevaluation2–scoreatevaluation1monthsbetweenevaluation×12.

The ARC assumes a linear rate of decline over time, which may represent a simplification given the potential nonlinear progression in FTD. Sensitivity analyses (e.g., restricting to a narrower interval range) were not feasible due to sample size limitations [29].

Cohen’s d effect size was calculated based on differences between group means and weighted standard deviation, where values below 0.20 indicate no effect, values between 0.21 and 0.49 suggest a small effect, 0.50 and 0.70 imply a moderate effect, and values 0.80 indicate a large effect [30]. Cohen’s d was computed solely for statistically significant findings. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0, with the alpha level set a priori at 0.05 (two tailed).

Ethics Approval

Participants provided written informed consent for data collection and genetic analyses. All patient information was de-identified, and data collection and processing were approved by the Ethics Committee for Biomedical Research of the 12 de Octubre Hospital Research Institute (Madrid). The study was conducted according to the principles expressed in the Declaration of Helsinki. This genetic study was approved by the local CEIm at Hospital 12 de Octubre Research Institute. All patients received a patient information sheet, and an informed consent was obtained from all subjects involved in the study.

Comparing FTD Clinical Syndromes

When comparing FTD variants (Table 1), mean age was broadly similar across the three groups, with only minor differences. The nfvPPA group tended to be slightly older than the other groups. In addition, significant differences in sex distribution were observed, with the bvFTD group including a higher proportion of women.

With respect to prior medical history, differences were observed in the prevalence of depression: 43% of patients with svPPA had depression, compared with 34% of those with bvFTD and 5% of those with nfvPPA (p = 0.38). Furthermore, although the bvFTD group showed a higher prevalence of familial dementia, meaningful comparisons with the other groups were limited by the small sample sizes in those subgroups.

When assessing the magnitude of Cohen’s d effect size, a substantial effect was observed for the t-MMSE (Cohen’s d = 1.16), indicating a large impact. In the follow-up analysis across different FTD variants (see Fig. 2), noteworthy differences emerged in the t-MMSE retest. Specifically, the svPPA group experienced the most substantial decline, with losses exceeding 10 points, followed by the nfvPPA group, which exhibited a loss of six points. These differences were statistically significant. Additionally, the FTD-FRS test also demonstrated significant disparities in retest performance, with the svPPA group displaying greater losses.

Assessing the magnitude of Cohen’s d effect further elucidated these findings. In the t-MMSE retest, the svPPA group exhibited the highest effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.28), indicating a greater impact compared to the nfvPPA (Cohen’s d = 0.76) and bvFTD (Cohen’s d = 1.17) groups. Discussion

This longitudinal study demonstrates that functional decline in FTD differs across clinical variants, with svPPA showing the most rapid deterioration. Although impairments in functional capacity are well established in dementia, longitudinal studies examining their progression over the course of disease remain relatively scarce, particularly in FTD. Given the marked clinical heterogeneity of FTD syndromes, such as bvFTD, svPPA, and nfvPPA, understanding variant-specific trajectories of functional deterioration is essential for accurate prognostic counselling, care planning, and the development of targeted interventions [31].

In the follow-up analyses, patients with svPPA showed the largest mean decline in FTD-FRS scores, suggesting a relatively rapid functional deterioration in this subgroup (eliminated). However, these findings should be interpreted with substantial caution because of the very small number of svPPA cases and should therefore be considered exploratory and hypothesis generating rather than definitive. Importantly, this pattern diverges from the broader literature, which has generally characterized svPPA as the slowest-progressing PPA variant [32]. Consequently, our findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a uniformly rapid or aggressive disease course in svPPA. Several factors may account for this apparent discrepancy. First, the small subgroup size increases the likelihood of sampling variability and limits generalizability. Moreover, svPPA comprises both left- and right-predominant temporal variants, which differ in their language, semantic, and behavioural profiles. Left-predominant svPPA is typically associated with more severe verbal semantic deficits, whereas right-predominant svPPA is more strongly associated with non-verbal semantic impairment and socio-emotional changes. Because patients were not stratified according to lateralization, heterogeneity in underlying atrophy patterns may have influenced the observed trajectories and contributed to the apparent severity observed in the svPPA group [33]. This finding aligns with previous reports describing a more aggressive disease course in this variant [34, 35]. Interpretation of decline in svPPA may also be influenced by the use of language-weighted cognitive measures such as the MMSE/t-MMSE. These instruments may disproportionately capture semantic-language impairment rather than global disease progression and may therefore overestimate decline in patients with prominent language dysfunction [36]. Accordingly, functional and caregiver-based measures may provide a more clinically meaningful reflection of disease burden in svPPA than cognitive scores alone.

In svPPA, severe language and semantic deficits may also influence caregiver perceptions of functional status. Difficulties in understanding instructions, processing written information, or expressing needs can increase care demands and may be interpreted as more pervasive functional dependence. Studies in primary progressive aphasia indicate that poorer language abilities are associated with greater proxy bias in caregiver-reported outcomes, including quality of life and daily functioning. Therefore, part of the pronounced decline observed in svPPA on caregiver-based scales may reflect the impact of communication difficulties on care requirements rather than exclusively true loss of practical skills [37].

This longitudinal study provides a detailed characterization of the clinical and functional progression of different FTD variants in the unique context of the COVID-19 pandemic. The most frequent subtype in our sample was bvFTD (61%), in line with previous reports [2, 3, 12, 13, 38]. In contrast to some prior studies [31], which reported poorer outcomes in bvFTD across assessments, our findings suggest a smaller longitudinal decline. This discrepancy may be partly explained by greater baseline impairment in our bvFTD group, which may have limited the magnitude of observable longitudinal decline, as previously noted by Cosseddu et al. [39].

Patients with nfvPPA demonstrated an intermediate trajectory, whereas patients with bvFTD showed a more heterogeneous course, likely reflecting the broad clinical and pathological diversity of this subtype [36]. Consistent with previous studies [2, 3], significant longitudinal changes were observed in cognitive and functional measures, particularly in t-MMSE, FTD-FRS, and IDDD scores. Although scales originally developed for Alzheimer’s disease can detect longitudinal change in FTD, many disease-specific features remain insufficiently captured, underscoring the need for instruments specifically designed for FTD [40]. While a marked decline in t-MMSE scores was observed, this measure should be interpreted as a nonspecific index of global deterioration, given the well-documented limitations of t-MMSE-based instruments in detecting executive and language deficits in FTD and primary progressive aphasia [36].

Importantly, the functional scales used in this study reflect not only loss of practical abilities but also reduced initiative and executive control in everyday activities. In bvFTD, apathy, disorganization, and impaired goal-directed behaviour often manifest as failures to initiate or sustain activities despite relatively preserved basic motor or practical skills [15]. Consequently, the observed decline in FTD-FRS and IDDD scores likely reflects a combination of increasing dependence, loss of initiative, and broader executive dysfunction rather than a pure loss of functional capacity [15].

NPI total scores remained relatively stable across follow-up, suggesting that behavioural and neuropsychiatric symptoms may persist at relatively constant levels even as cognitive and functional abilities decline. This pattern is consistent with previous findings indicating that behavioural disturbances tend to emerge early and subsequently stabilize [10]. Accordingly, our primary conclusions regarding disease progression are based on FTD-FRS and IDDD trajectories, which more directly capture everyday functioning and caregiver-reported changes.

In our sample, NPI total scores showed minimal change between baseline and follow-up. However, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence of genuine behavioural stability. Behavioural symptoms in FTD often follow nonlinear trajectories, with phases of escalation, plateau, and partial attenuation. Two assessment time points with variable inter-assessment intervals are unlikely to fully capture these dynamics. Moreover, the NPI is an informant-based retrospective measure, and perceived stability may reflect caregiver adaptation, changes in tolerance, recall bias, or ceiling effects when symptoms are already prominent at baseline. Thus, the apparent stability of NPI scores should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as reflecting relatively stable caregiver-reported burden within the limits of the instrument rather than absence of behavioural progression [41, 42].

Emerging studies have reported dysphoria, worry, and somatic complaints in svPPA, possibly related to right temporal involvement or preserved insight into semantic deficits. In addition, the NPI dysphoria subscale may capture social withdrawal, reduced initiative, or apathy-like presentations rather than major depressive disorder, particularly when semantic impairment manifests as emotional and social disconnection [43]. Apathy and depression represent dissociable constructs with overlapping but distinct neural substrates. Whereas depression is characterized by negative affect and rumination, apathy primarily reflects diminished goal-directed motivation. In svPPA, anterior temporal degeneration may disrupt emotional semantics and reward processing, producing a phenotype intermediate between the disinhibited presentation of bvFTD and depressive-like withdrawal. Although our cross-sectional design and reliance on caregiver report do not allow firm differentiation, these observations merit further investigation in larger cohorts using apathy-specific instruments and longitudinal mood assessments [44].

Mortality analyses indicated an average survival of approximately 8 years from symptom onset, in line with previous estimates of life expectancy in FTD [13]. No significant differences in mortality were observed across the three FTD variants. However, the number of deaths in our sample was small (12 subjects), and mortality data by variant type are decisive. In recent reviews, the mean survival from symptom onset was 7.6 years for bvFTD, 7.1 years for nfvPPA, and 12 years for svPPA [45]. Higher mortality among older individuals and men highlights the influence of demographic and comorbidity factors on prognosis. Patients who died during follow-up showed poorer baseline performance in activities of daily living.

The COVID-19 pandemic required substantial adaptations in longitudinal monitoring. Telephone-based assessments enabled continued follow-up when in-person visits were not feasible and demonstrated practical utility during periods of restricted hospital access. However, remote assessments have well-recognized limitations, including the inability to observe behaviour, facial expression, or motor abnormalities and the inability to administer performance-based executive or visuospatial tasks. Although the t-MMSE provided a feasible indicator of global cognitive change, equivalence with in-person assessment cannot be assumed, particularly in bvFTD, where direct behavioural observation is central to clinical characterization [15]. In this study, 33 patients (49%) were assessed remotely during COVID-19 quarantine periods. No formal comparison between in-person and telephone assessments was performed, and assessment modality was not included as a covariate because of design constraints [46, 47]. Consequently, potential modality effects on observed rates of change, particularly for behavioural and executive measures, cannot be excluded.

The annualized rate of change (ARC) revealed the largest changes in IDDD scores, followed by t-MMSE, indicating substantial decline in functional and global cognitive domains. However, these estimates should be interpreted cautiously given the heterogeneity of follow-up intervals and the assumption of linear change inherent in the ARC approach. Future studies with larger samples and systematic documentation of hemispheric predominance are needed to determine whether svPPA truly follows a more aggressive clinical course or whether the pattern observed here reflects sampling variability and measurement bias related to language-based screening instruments.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size and convenience sampling limit generalizability. In particular, the svPPA subgroup was very small, and findings related to this variant should therefore be interpreted as exploratory. Follow-up intervals were highly heterogeneous (3–51 months), largely due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Although ARC was used to standardize change across intervals, this method assumes a constant rate of decline and may not reflect the nonlinear progression typical of FTD. Longer intervals may dilute sensitivity to early change, whereas shorter intervals may underestimate cumulative decline. Future studies should adopt more standardized follow-up schedules to better characterize disease trajectories [48]. Remote assessment represents an additional limitation. Nearly half of follow-up evaluations were conducted by telephone, and we did not assess measurement equivalence, test-retest reliability, or internal consistency across modalities. Telephone-based assessments are known to be less sensitive to executive dysfunction and behavioural change than in-person evaluations, and this constitutes an important methodological limitation and a missed opportunity for formal validation of remote tools in FTD cohorts. Despite these limitations, the principal strengths of this study include its longitudinal design, the use of validated FTD-specific instruments, and the inclusion of a well-characterized clinical cohort.

In conclusion, this study supports the utility of the FTD-FRS and functional measures for tracking disease progression in FTD and demonstrates the practical value of remote follow-up during public health emergencies when in-person assessment is not feasible. Although patients with svPPA showed greater decline on some measures, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the very small subgroup size, potential language-related assessment bias, and divergence from the broader literature. Future research should further clarify variant-specific trajectories, integrate genetic and neuroimaging biomarkers, and evaluate targeted interventions aimed at mitigating functional decline and improving quality of life for both patients and caregivers.
