Sebaceous gland necrosis induced by Demodex mites: a case report
Authors: Carmine N. Palladino, Maia K. Keller, Gerilyn M. Olsen, Kassandra E. Holzem, Kara E. Young
Published: 2026

Introduction

Demodex mites, including Demodex folliculorum and Demodex brevis, are obligate ectoparasites residing in the pilosebaceous units of human skin.1 Although typically asymptomatic, these mites can provoke inflammatory dermatoses in immunocompromised or predisposed individuals. Conditions, such as rosacea, blepharitis, and acne vulgaris, have been associated with increased mite density and subsequent inflammatory responses.1, 2, 3 Histopathological findings in such cases often reveal a dense perivascular and perifollicular lymphohistiocytic and neutrophilic infiltrate, with numerous Demodex mites in the follicular infundibula.4 Although Demodex mites are common commensals, their presence alone is insufficient for diagnosis, as they may be identified on normal-appearing skin. Demodicosis represents a spectrum of cutaneous disorders resulting from abnormal or increased Demodex proliferation and requires careful clinicopathologic correlation. Diagnostic criteria include compatible clinical morphology, histopathologic evidence of increased or aberrantly localized mites, and clinical improvement following acaricidal therapy with normalization of mite density. While Demodex-associated inflammatory dermatoses are well described, sebaceous gland necrosis attributable to Demodex infestation is rare, with few reports documenting this finding histopathologically.5

Case presentation

Dermatology was consulted in the emergency department for a 78-year-old male with a 3-week history of several heme- and honey-crusted vesicles scattered on the head and trunk (Fig 1, A-C). The patient had a history of prostate cancer and genital herpes but was otherwise healthy. The lesions were mildly pruritic, although not painful. He was previously seen by his primary care physician and diagnosed clinically with varicella zoster but had not improved with oral valacyclovir. Biopsy performed by an outside dermatologist showed intraepidermal vesicles and adjacent necrosis concerning for a viral process; however, additional testing, including immunohistochemical stain of lesional tissue for herpes simplex virus and polymerase chain reaction testing for mpox virus, herpes simplex virus, and varicella zoster, was negative. The patient was evaluated by an infectious disease specialist and treated with oral cephalexin without improvement, prompting evaluation in the emergency department. Deep tissue culture for bacteria, mycobacteria, and fungus were negative. He was treated empirically with doxycycline for suspected bullous impetigo. The patient was evaluated in the dermatology clinic 3 weeks later. In the interim, he continued to be seen by an infectious disease specialist and did not improve despite trials of topical mupirocin, oral trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, and intravenous daptomycin and ceftriaxone. Punch biopsy was obtained that showed superficial and deep inflammation with abundant eosinophils, necrosis of the pilosebaceous unit with surrounding lymphohistiocytic infiltrate, and associated Demodex particles (Fig 2, A and B). Direct and indirect immunofluorescence were also obtained and were negative. The differential diagnosis included bullous impetigo, arthropod bite reaction, eosinophilic folliculitis, autoimmune bullous disease, and drug reaction. Bullous impetigo was considered, given the heme– and honey–crusted morphology but was excluded because of negative bacterial cultures and lack of response to antibiotics. Arthropod bite reaction and eosinophilic folliculitis were considered in the setting of eosinophil-rich inflammation but were less favored given the presence of necrotic pilosebaceous units and associated Demodex organisms. Treatment and Clinical Course

The patient was treated with 2 doses of oral ivermectin (200 mcg/kg) spaced 1 week apart.6 At subsequent follow-up, he demonstrated significant clinical improvement, although a few new lesions continued to develop. A third dose of oral ivermectin was administered, followed by initiation of topical ivermectin applied twice daily. This combined systemic and topical antiparasitic therapy resulted in marked and sustained clinical improvement, with near-complete resolution of lesions at follow-up several weeks later, with no evidence of recurrence at last evaluation (Fig 3, A-C).Fig 3Clinical photographs demonstrating significant resolution of vesiculobullous lesions observed on the following areas: (A) bilateral chest, (B) left face and neck, and (C) bilateral frontal facial regions.

Discussion

Demodex mites are common commensal organisms residing in human pilosebaceous units. While generally asymptomatic, their unchecked proliferation can result in inflammatory dermatoses. This case represents a rare and clinically significant presentation of sebaceous gland necrosis attributed to Demodex mites, an entity that underscores their potential pathogenicity in specific conditions. The mechanism appears to involve direct follicular invasion, destruction of sebaceous glands, and an ensuing robust inflammatory response.

Demodex–associated eosinophilic follicular reactions have been previously described and are considered distinct from classic eosinophilic folliculitis. Sabater-Marco et al7 reported an eosinophil-rich follicular inflammatory reaction induced by D. folliculorum, emphasizing its uncommon clinicopathologic features. In contrast to prior reports, the present case demonstrates frank necrosis of sebaceous glands in association with Demodex infestation, expanding the known histopathologic spectrum of demodicosis. The presence of vesiculobullous lesions and extensive eosinophilic inflammation further distinguishes this case from previously described entities.

Histopathology played a pivotal role in identifying the underlying etiology in this case. Several biopsies were required to determine the diagnosis, as initial samples revealed nonspecific inflammation with eosinophils but lacked definitive evidence of an underlying cause. The persistence of symptoms despite a wide variety of antimicrobial therapy prompted additional tissue sampling, which ultimately identified necrotic sebaceous glands with associated Demodex mites. The biopsy findings, coupled with an eosinophil-rich inflammatory infiltrate, strongly suggest an immunologic reaction to the mites, which ultimately guided therapeutic choice. Treatment with ivermectin, a well–established antiparasitic agent, demonstrated rapid efficacy in reducing both the parasitic burden and the associated inflammatory response, resulting in sustained clinical improvement.

This case highlights the importance of considering demodicosis in the differential diagnosis of refractory or atypical dermatoses in seborrheic areas, particularly when histopathology reveals an eosinophilic infiltrate. Other eosinophil-related findings, such as flame figures and eosinophil granule accumulation on collagen bundles, have also been reported in association with Demodex.7 While follicular necrosis is not a commonly associated feature, this case suggests its presence may also be a helpful indicator. Demodex mites have a predilection for the head and neck, likely due to the high density of sebaceous glands in these areas. Recognizing a sebaceous-rich distribution can aid in clinical suspicion. Such cases necessitate a comprehensive diagnostic approach that includes both clinical and histopathological evaluations. While antiparasitic therapies like oral and topical ivermectin have proven effective, the lack of standardized protocols for diagnosing and managing severe Demodex infestations remains a challenge.

The broader implications of this case extend beyond individual management. It underscores the need for further research to elucidate the pathogenic mechanisms of Demodex mites, particularly their triggers for overproliferation and their interactions with the host immune systems.2,4,8 Establishing standardized diagnostic criteria and treatment guidelines could significantly enhance dermatological practice and improve patient outcomes. As our understanding of Demodex–related dermatoses evolves, this case serves as a critical reminder of the diagnostic value of histopathology and the therapeutic potential of antiparasitic interventions.
