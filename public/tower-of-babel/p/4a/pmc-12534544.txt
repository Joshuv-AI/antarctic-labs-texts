Weather drivers of reproductive variability in perennial plants and their implications for climate change risks
Authors: Valentin Journé, Dave Kelly, Andrew Hacket-Pain, Ian S. Pearse, Jakub Szymkowiak, Jessie J. Foest, Katarzyna Kondrat, Iris Oberklammer, Mario B. Pesendorfer, Akiko Satake, Michał Bogdziewicz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Seed production in perennial plants often shows strong year-to-year variation, a phenomenon known as masting. Masting is typically adaptive and driven by weather cues that synchronize reproduction by promoting or suppressing flowering and seed set. These cues, involving temperature, precipitation, and drought, differ across species and regions, yet a global synthesis is needed. Understanding such patterns is crucial, as reproductive responses to climate interact with resource constraints to shape plant fitness under climate change. Here, we analyze climate-reproduction relationships in 746 populations of 331 species worldwide using moving-window methods. We find that temperature is the dominant cue for reproductive investment, though its influence is weaker in the wettest and driest climates. In temperate and boreal regions, reproduction is most often linked to warmer conditions, whereas in the tropics, reproduction is disproportionately linked to cooler temperatures. Therefore, we expect rising temperatures to increase reproductive failures in the tropics, but to reduce interannual variability in temperate and boreal zones as favorable cues become more frequent. These results confirm long-standing theoretical predictions and provide a global framework for evaluating climate change risks to mast seeding.

Introduction

Many perennial plant populations show mast seeding or masting, the variable and synchronous reproduction across years1,2. Notably, interannual variation in seedfall exceeds that expected by weather-driven resource matching; it represents the result of selection for exaggerated variation across years3. At the ultimate level, masting provides fitness benefits when synchronous reproduction increases reproductive efficiency through Economies of Scale (EoS) (EoS, enhanced pollination efficiency, and predator satiation)4,5. Those fitness benefits require synchrony, so on a proximate level, there is selection for seed production to be responsive to weather conditions, termed weather cues6,7. For example, flower bud initiation may be higher after hot summers, or reproduction may largely fail when spring weather conditions impede effective pollen transfer8–10. The responsiveness of individual plants to shared weather cues creates synchrony within populations in both high- and low-seed years11,12.

Improving our understanding of the weather cues that synchronize masting is important for several reasons, including clarifying underlying selective processes7, improving masting forecasts for management13–15, and predicting plant responses to climate change16,17. Weather cues are diverse; depending on species and location, masting correlates with variations in temperature, precipitation, or drought at various stages before and during the reproductive process2,18–22. Although theory allows predictions about which types of cues should be most common, synthesis has been hindered by limited data availability. Hypothesized relationships between weather cues and mast seeding

These hypotheses reflect theoretical expectations previously proposed in the literature but supported by limited empirical evidence. Our study aims to systematically test these relationships across a global dataset, using a compilation of annual records of seed production from 746 populations of 331 plant species extracted from MASTREE+. Prediction 7 is excluded from testing, as it cannot be reliably assessed using observational data.

Because reproductive efficiency is maximized by high synchrony among individuals7,23, temperature should be the most common type of weather cue for masting (Prediction 1)4,24. High synchrony requires cues that are well correlated in space24. Temperature is generally better correlated spatially than precipitation or drought, even though precipitation can provide local-scale synchronization25. Generally, plants measure precipitation indirectly via soil moisture, which can vary within a site due to microtopography. Temperature cues are often reported for masting species6,12,26,27. However, the most common cues plants use can vary with differences in climate, even among closely related species28. For instance, precipitation or drought-related cues are frequently observed in species in dry or very wet areas (tropics)22,29–31. This also interacts with leaf habit: drought appears to play a more important role in seed production in evergreen compared to deciduous species28,32. However, a formal comparison of the relative frequency of temperature vs. precipitation/drought cues across a wide range of masting species is needed.

Selection should favor cues that correlate positively with resource availability or acquisition (Prediction 2), as this would reduce costs of resource storage24. Warm growing-season temperatures are often associated with higher photosynthesis and increased growth33–35. Warm temperatures are usually associated with increased seed crop sizes, but sometimes cool temperatures promote reproduction36–38.

Weather cues should act in different seasons and at varying times before seedfall to match physiological processes and reproductive phenology (Prediction 3)2. These cues can be broadly grouped into two categories: those that induce flowering (the ‘flower masting’ of2) and those that influence the transition from flowers to mature fruits (‘fruit-maturation masting’). In the first case, gene regulatory networks integrate signals, such as temperature and photoperiod, determining flowering intensity10,39. In temperate, alpine, and boreal zones, where tissues are dormant in winter40, these cues are expected to act in summer or autumn, 6–9 months before spring flowering, and thus one or more years before seedfall depending on seed maturation time10,41. Predictions are less certain in tropical regions, where flowering may occur year-round and fruit development may follow quickly. In such cases, the main constraint may be the rarity of weather signals that recur at multi-year intervals42. However, in tropical areas with a dry season, cues are likely to act near the start of the wet season. In fruit-maturation masting, weather affects seed crop size via pollination success or fruit abortion27,28,43, with immediate effects. In temperate species, such cues should occur in spring, when most flowering happens44–46, or in summer, during fruit development29,47.

Selection should lead species to differ in their sensitivity to weather variation (Prediction 4)42. Strong masting (high CVp—standard deviation of population-level seed production divided by its mean) is favored when large EoS exist1,3.42 proposed that this is achieved through selection for hypersensitivity to weather. Supporting this, New Zealand species with high CVp show flowering highly responsive to temperature fluctuations6. Similarly, individual Fagus sylvatica trees with seed production closely tied to temperature exhibit higher variability and synchrony, along with lower seed predation and greater pollination efficiency7. This higher sensitivity in strongly masting species has implications for climate change. If cues are based on absolute temperature, hypersensitivity may increase vulnerability to warming3,48. In contrast, if plants respond to relative cues, climate change is expected to have little effect6 (see Discussion).

Masting species may use a single dominant weather cue, or multiple sequential cues. Prediction 5 is that selection should favor single cues if a cue exists with suitable properties (low frequency, wide spatial extent), because plants using a single cue should have higher synchrony37,49. Some studies find support for single dominant cues, but there is wide variation among papers in how many different combinations of cues are tested. For example, Nothofagus seed crops were predicted from a single temperature variable by ref. 6, but by a sequence of two different temperatures, soil moisture, and plant resources by ref. 50. Inconsistency among studies constrains conclusions from the literature about how often plants rely on single vs. multiple cues.

Weather cues driving masting are predicted to show phylogenetic conservatism (Prediction 6). This prediction is based on descendants inheriting a common molecular mechanism for responding to particular cues, and those traits could be maintained if ecological factors (selective benefits and broad climate types) have not changed much as species diverged and colonized different geographic regions. This hypothesis is supported by analyses across 15 oak species28. Furthermore, masting itself demonstrates a phylogenetic signal51–54. Therefore, to the extent that masting persists as species diverge, their weather cues may follow from their common ancestry.

Finally, it is hard to test whether the weather cue is an absolute or relative value (Prediction 7). Some plants flower heavily the year after a summer of warm absolute temperatures55, while others respond to a summer that is relatively warmer than the summer before (the ΔT cue:6). Relative temperature cues have potential benefits for plants, including automatic adjustment to local microclimates across different altitudes and aspects, but they require a complex mechanism for comparing temperatures. There is an ongoing debate about whether ΔT is the actual mechanism, or just a proxy for the combined effects of absolute temperature and internal plant resources56–58. There is support for ΔT in the stability of masting patterns in North American conifers through five decades of warming temperatures59. However, distinguishing between ΔT and the proxy hypothesis is currently difficult, and may even be impossible with observational data unless there is a substantial rise in site mean temperatures during the study period.

In this paper, we seek to test Predictions 1–6 on the nature of weather cues. There is a large literature on correlations between weather variation and annual reproduction60. However, a review of this literature is limited by the diverse a priori selection of cues believed to be significant in focal species61. Also, recent research has shown there can be within-species variation in the major weather effects on seed production27,62,63. For instance, in sessile oaks (Quercus petraea), seed production in semi-continental climates is mainly determined by pollination success driven by spring temperatures, whereas in oceanic climates, spring temperatures have less impact as seed production is more determined by flower abundance27.

We avoid pre-selection of weather variables and instead search consistently for the best seeding-weather correlation for each population, often across multiple populations per species. We then use these data to test predictions. Specifically, we use moving windows analysis to identify a period (or ‘window’) during which reproduction (flower, seed, or cone production, collectively referred to as seed production for clarity throughout the text) is most responsive to weather variation. We consider three types of weather variables: temperature, precipitation, and moisture deficit. Moisture deficit is included alongside precipitation as the mechanisms behind their effects on seed production vary and involve disruptions in pollination64, drought-related seed abortion29, or drought-related activation of floral transition65. Once we determine the weather variables and their time windows for each population, we explore several general patterns to assess which species may be more at risk of changes in masting3. Since the cue predictions are based on assumptions of the selective benefit of masting, support for them suggests that the observed weather cues are primarily shaped by selection for synchrony and variability, rather than a non-selective plant response to a variable environment.

Results

Prediction 1: type of weather cue

Temperature cues had the largest relative contribution to explaining the variation in annual seed production (Fig. 1), consistent with Prediction 1 (Table 1). The contributions of temperature as the predictor of annual seed production were greater than that of precipitation and moisture deficit across the latitudes with extensive forest cover, especially near the equator and between 30° and 60° latitude, with an average contribution of 50%, compared to 25% in the case of precipitation and moisture deficit (Fig. 1b, c). The observed difference between relative contributions was weaker between 10° and 25∘ latitude, and above 65°. Predictions 2 and 3: season and direction of the cue effect

A breakdown of the weather cue effects by their timing (year and season) and the direction of their effect on seed production (i.e., whether cues were positively or negatively correlated with reproduction) revealed important differences between temperate-boreal and tropical species. To facilitate interpretation, we refer to the year of seed fall as Y0, and use Y1, Y2, and Y3 to indicate one, two, or three years prior to seed fall, respectively.

Weather cues in temperate-boreal species showed a strong effect of autumn and spring temperatures in the year of seed fall (Y0), but almost no seasonal pattern in precipitation or moisture deficit (Fig. 3). The most common association with high seed crops was with the previous summer (Y1) (77% of species). There was also a significant association with warm temperatures in the winter in Y3 (Fig. 3). In contrast, in tropical species, there was a consistent association across all years (Y0 to Y3) of cool temperatures with subsequent higher reproduction, with over ~75% of responses to temperatures being negative across all years and seasons (Fig. 4). Prediction 4: hypersensitivity to cues

Temperate and boreal populations that show strong masting (high CVp) were more sensitive to temperature variation (Fig. 5). The CVp was positively correlated with sensitivity to temperature variation, both in the case of positive and negative correlations between seed production and temperature. Temperature sensitivity for species that had CVp = 3 was ~ 2-fold larger than for species that had CVp = 1 in case of positive responses (which Fig. 3 showed were typically in the summer before seeding Y1), and 1.1-fold larger in case of negative responses (typically two summers before seeding, Y2). Sensitivity to variation in precipitation and moisture deficit was not related to CVp (Fig. 5). Prediction 5: multiple vs. single cues

When considering single vs. multiple cues, temperate and tropical species showed different patterns, but single cues were rare across all biomes. In Venn diagrams (Fig. 6) the percentage of species affected by all three cues was higher in the temperate zone than in the tropics (36 vs. 6% respectively). Correspondingly, fewer temperate than tropical species responded to only a single cue (10 vs. 36%). That relative pattern was unchanged by using a higher cutoff (0.2 relative contribution) for including a cue (Fig. S3). A GLM model indicated that the mean number of cues per species increased significantly with the number of sites per species (presumably by including environmentally diverse sites for the same species), but was unaffected by the sampling method (seed traps, which were the most common method used in the temperate zone, vs. other methods). The proportion of multiple cues would have been higher if it had included cases that had several significant cues of the same type (e.g., independent spring and summer temperature cues), but that is not possible within the analytical framework used.

Prediction 6: phylogenetic conservation

A clustering analysis was used to discriminate species based on their dominant cue. The clustering analysis based on the species-level associations of seed production and weather variation identified ten clusters in temperate-boreal species (Fig. S4), and 14 in tropical ones (Fig. S5). However, none of these clusters were associated with phylogenetic groups or leaf habits.

Discussion

Analysis across 746 populations of 331 perennial plant species provided a systematic test of predictions for the weather cues associated with high-seed years. Most of the predictions were supported for temperate-boreal species, but the picture was more complex for tropical species. Our results provide a template informing which masting plant species are likely to be vulnerable to disruption under climate change.

Do weather cues match predictions based on selective benefits?

There was strong support for Prediction 1 about the weather cues used by masting species (Table 1): temperature was the most common weather cue. This provides a consistent large-scale test of the early prediction that temperature should be the dominant cue for masting, from the diverse array of possible weather cues reported in the literature24. Similarly, the greater importance of moisture effects in dry and wet habitats aligns with predictions that species in water-stressed climates should exhibit sensitivity to such cues32,66. The greater importance of moisture in dry areas (Fig. 2a) is plausibly related to the direct role of precipitation in allowing resource gain, which enables both growth and reproduction3,4. In wet areas, moisture may act through water deficit either directly as a rare constraint on reproduction47, or indirectly as a rare synchronizing cue in a normally well-watered environment65.

In contrast, Prediction 6 (phylogenetic conservation) found no support. The responses of seed production to weather variation did not exhibit clear phylogenetic structuring. Previous studies exploring cueing variation within the Quercus genus hinted at the possibility of such structuring. Weather cues were linked to phylogeny and leaf type across 15 oak species from three geographic regions28. In a synthesis of 22 case studies, evergreen oaks, but not deciduous ones, were found to be sensitive to moisture deficit in summer32. However, such patterns were not evident in our analysis, which encompassed a larger sample of species spanning 74 families. Accumulating evidence now suggests that significant variability exists within species in their responses to weather27,62,63, although exceptions exist10,67. This intraspecific variation underscores the crucial role of local conditions in determining the primary factors limiting reproduction, which may override any phylogenetic signal.

The results for the other predictions varied between the temperate and tropical datasets. For temperate-boreal species, most of the testable predictions in Table 1 were supported. Temperate species generally showed the expected association between more favorable conditions and higher reproduction (Prediction 2), and in the predicted seasons (Prediction 3): temperatures were usually warmer in the summer before seeding (Y1), and the autumn and spring of seedfall (Y0). Boreal-temperate species displaying high interannual variation in seed production did achieve this through hypersensitivity to temperature variation (Prediction 4), providing broad support for the hypersensitivity to weather cues as a major masting mechanism6,42. The unsupported prediction is the prevalence of multiple vs. single cues (Prediction 5). While temperature is the generally dominant cue, it usually operates together with other factors, highlighting the sensitivity of the seed maturation process to precipitation and moisture deficit.

For tropical species, there was much less support for the predictions in Table 1. For Prediction 2 (masting being associated with favorable conditions), tropical species exhibited mainly correlations with colder temperatures and drought in initiating flowering. The dominant association with colder temperatures is inconsistent with the prediction but agrees with the detailed case studies that linked fruit production to cool temperatures in SE Asia30,65,68. In turn, the temporal pattern for drought supports the prediction of favorable conditions, as high reproduction followed after wet years.

Sensitivity to climate change

The possible responses of masting species to climate change vary depending on the weather cues to which they respond3. Notably, relative vs. absolute cues link with Prediction 2 (direction of cues) to affect the species’ response to climate change3,16. Relative temperature cues are inherently insensitive to gradual warming6. In contrast, absolute cues make plants sensitive in ways that vary with the cue direction. When warm absolute temperatures promote reproduction, cues may occur more frequently than plants can replenish resources after the previous reproductive event58,69–71. This leads to decreased synchrony, which weakens masting (lower CVp). For instance, in European beech (Fagus sylvatica), a 1 °C increase in mean summer temperatures resulted in a five-fold increase in cue occurrence, leading to decreased synchrony and CVp, higher seed predation, and higher pollen limitation, which together caused an up to 80% decline in viable seed production55,72. Conversely, in species where cold absolute temperatures promote reproduction, warming reduces the frequency of heavy-flowering cues, thereby reducing the frequency of reproduction21,66. For example, in a New Zealand tree Beilschmiedia tawa, seeding is stimulated by low winter and summer temperatures, which now occur less frequently, resulting in widespread reproductive failure37. Our study sheds light on the prevalence of positive and negative responses of species to weather, helping predict masting responses to climate change.

The widespread requirement of cooling for flowering initiation across the 229 tropical species studied here suggests that the tropics are at risk of experiencing widespread declines in seed production. The reduced frequency of low-temperature cues has already been reported as a threat to the fruiting of Dipterocarps in SE Asia68, and our work suggests that such a process can be widespread. Recently reported trends support that. For instance, the longest monitoring of tropical tree reproduction at Lope National Park in Gabon reported over an 80% decline in fruit availability between 1986 and 201873. Consistent with our findings, this decline was observed across all but one of the 73 species monitored and had cascading effects on the lowered body condition of elephants73. Furthermore, a recent synthesis across 17 tropical sites found that negative trends in fecundity are present across 22% of species, compared to just 5% showing an increase74.

In species hypersensitive to weather variation, even minor climate trends may result in substantial changes to their reproductive patterns75. Our study indicates that cue hypersensitivity is an important masting mechanism in temperate and boreal species, hinting that disruptions in masting mechanisms are more likely in these biomes, especially in strongly masting species (high CVp). This assertion finds support in a previous study examining temporal trends in CVp across 363 species, which observed that the most significant temporal changes in CVp, likely linked to shifting climates, occur in species with the largest CVp values48. Depending on the direction of response to cues, disruptions could come from more frequent cueing and subsequent more regular reproduction (lower CVp)55 in species where warm temperatures promote flowering, or from less frequent cueing and a decline in reproduction37 in species where cool temperatures promote flowering. A slight dominance of the positive effects of temperature on seed production in temperate-boreal biomes indicates that lowered CVp is likely to be more widespread. Crucially, the differences in the prevalence of positive and negative responses to weather cues in temperate-boreal vs. tropical species suggest that species from these biomes may diverge in their responses to climate change.

Study limitations

The mechanisms underlying the impact of weather on flowering and seed maturation are diverse, as summarized in our synthesis, and these effects can manifest throughout all seasons and involve lags over multiple years. Typically, the physiological mechanisms behind these correlations are inferred from relevant physiological processes occurring during specific periods22,60,76. For instance, the effects of summer temperature are commonly associated with their impact on flowering initiation77. Winter weather effects are often linked with resource accumulation favoring later reproductive investment78. Spring weather may influence pollination or disrupt reproduction if late spring frost damages flowers8,9,43,44. Lagged effects frequently stem from the extended reproductive cycle, which in certain species, like pines, may span several years59,79. However, our understanding of the physiology behind these correlations remains incomplete60,80. Future studies that identify the particular process involved and establish the cause-and-effect relationship are thus essential and may provide a step-change in understanding the proximate mechanisms of masting and could be incorporated into climate change models81,82, and seed production forecasting14.

Despite the large size of our dataset, the inevitably uneven sampling across regions, phylogenetic groups, and species requires some caution when interpreting the results. In tropical regions, methodological challenges arise due to complex seasonality and uncertain effects from previous years’ weather conditions on seed production. Some correlations observed—such as the association with cooler weather and weather cues extending back to three years (Y3) without a clear causal mechanism—suggest that our understanding of masting cues remains incomplete. Additionally, how to treat seasons appropriately, especially in the tropics, is not always clear, and this may affect the interpretation of results. Data collection methods like seed trap studies and sampling efforts can introduce uncertainties, especially in diverse tropical forests where many species are zoochorous. If individual species are rare, the number of reproductive individuals contributing to measured seedfall will be low, and additional uncertainty results from the high dispersal of fruits and seeds in and out of the study plot. This makes it challenging to determine parentage and the actual number of individuals contributing to the seed pool. However, it is reassuring that the sampling method (which often varied between tropical and boreal-temperate studies) had no significant effect on the number of cues per species. These challenges may limit the generalization of our findings and highlight the need for a comprehensive data-collection effort.

In conclusion, our analysis provides support for several long-standing, but so far not comprehensively tested, predictions, offering valuable insights into how these dynamics may shift under climate change. The support for temperature as the dominant cue in temperate-boreal species aligns with long-held predictions about selective benefits. The patterns observed in tropical species, particularly their reliance on cooling temperatures to initiate flowering, raise concerns about reduced seed production in warming climates. The absence of phylogenetic structuring in weather responses highlights the pivotal role of local environmental conditions over evolutionary lineage in determining reproductive strategies. Given the hypersensitivity of many temperate and boreal species to weather variation, even minor climatic shifts could substantially disrupt masting patterns, with cascading effects on ecosystem function and biodiversity83,84. Our findings stress the need for targeted research into the physiological mechanisms underpinning masting and for the development of predictive models to anticipate and mitigate the impacts of climate change on perennial plants’ reproduction.

Methods

Seed production data

The seed production data were extracted from MASTREE+, a database of annual records of population-level reproductive effort from all vegetated continents70,85. We restricted our analysis to the continuous time series of seed and flower production that covered more than 14 years, observed after 1961. This cutoff was associated with limited weather data availability before this date. We also excluded observations that lacked precise geolocation. The datasets included temperate, boreal and tropical species, with tropics defined as regions between the Tropics of Cancer (23.44°N) and Capricorn (23.44°S). In total, our analysis included 746 populations from 331 species (averaged length time series, 26 years, maximum 58 years: Fig. S6, Table S3). The temperate-boreal zone had more sites than the tropics (110 vs. 7) and more populations (517 vs. 229) but fewer species (109 vs. 222). For each population, we standardized seed production between 0 and 1 by dividing all values by the highest recorded value in the time series. This was done to accommodate among-population variation in methods of seed production monitoring among datasets included in MASTREE+10,14.

Weather data and climate data

We extracted monthly weather data from TerraClimate, a high-resolution (~4 km) climate reanalysis dataset covering the period 1958–201986. TerraClimate is based on interpolated weather station observations and combines data from CRU and JRA-55 with empirical downscaling and topographic correction, making it well-suited for capturing spatial variation in observed historical climate. For each population, we extracted mean temperature (T, ° C), precipitation (P, mm), and potential evapotranspiration (PET, mm). We determined moisture deficit (D, mm) as P − PET.

Identification of weather cues with moving climatic windows

We used a moving time window approach to detect a period or ‘window’ over which annual seed production is sensitive to the weather. Moving windows analysis was implemented via climwin (v1.2.3), following the workflow described in refs. 87 and88. We used logit link beta regression fitted with the glmmTMB package89 (v1.1.8). Relative contribution of weather cues

To assess the importance of particular weather cues for predicting seed production, we determined the relative contribution of each included weather predictor for each population. To do so, we ran separate models, excluding one predictor at a time, and calculated the relative decline of root-mean-square error (RMSE). Thus, a model with one significant predictor (T or P or D) would produce a contribution of that predictor equal to 100%. For a model with more than one predictor, the contribution of the focal predictor is the relative reduction in the model RMSE once that predictor is removed.

To compare the number of cues affecting species, we tallied species separately in temperate and tropical biomes (for species with multiple sites, we averaged cue contributions across sites). We counted which cues affected each species, defined either as a cue with a relative contribution of >0 or >0.2. To determine what factors affected the number of cues per species, we ran a quasipoisson Generalized Linear Model (GLM) testing the number of cues (1, 2 or 3 cues with >0 influence), against the number of sites for that species (average 4.74 sites in temperate zone but 1.03 in tropics), sampling method (seed traps vs. all other types), and biome (Temperate-Boreal vs. Tropical). The Method by Biome interaction was also initially included but was not significant and was dropped.

Hypersensitivity to weather cues and CVp

To test the relationship between weather cue sensitivity and the coefficient of variation of seed production (CVp) we used a generalized additive mixed model (GAMM). We included the slopes between seed production and weather calculated during the moving windows analysis for each population as a response, while the coefficient of variation (CVp), a factor characterizing biome (temperate-boreal/tropics), weather cue direction (positive/negative), and their interaction were used as predictors. We fitted the GAMM with a Gaussian distribution family by using mgcv R package (v1.8-42,95). A genus-family factor was included as a random intercept (see above). Observations (slopes) in the model were weighted by the inverse of their associated standard errors.

Cluster analysis of weather cues

To assess whether weather cues are more similar in species that are closely related phylogenetically, we performed hierarchical clustering separately for temperate and tropical species. For these two groups, we determined the season of the weather cues (for temperate-boreal species using autumn, winter, spring, and summer seasons; for tropical species, wet and dry seasons) for each year (from current year Y0 to three years before Y3). In the Northern hemisphere, winter included Dec–Feb, spring Mar–May, summer Jun–Aug, and autumn Sep–Nov, with Southern hemisphere seasons shifted 6 months later. Dry/wet seasons were identified based on monthly precipitation patterns (Fig. S7). For each season, we determined if each species was responding (=1) or not (=0) to the cue in the particular season. We then used hierarchical clustering by the Ward method and the Euclidian distance using the hclust function. We determined the best number of clusters by using NbClust (v3.0.1,96) and visualized the clusters with pheatmap package (v1.0.12,97). This analysis was performed on data aggregated at a species level. The analytical steps are summarized in a flowchart at Fig. S8.
