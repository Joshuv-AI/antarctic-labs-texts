The relationship between sedentary time, sleep quality, glycemic control, and mild cognitive impairment in older adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Jingzheng Yan, Zhichao Wei, Ruiqun Chen, Yingjuan Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis cross-sectional study examined associations among sedentary behavior, sleep quality, glycemic control, and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) in older adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and explored latent sleep profiles and their associations with MCI.MethodsFrom October 2021 to October 2022, 257 patients with T2DM aged ≥ 60 years were recruited from a tertiary hospital in Jinan, China. Cognitive status was operationalized using a structured study framework that combined MoCA screening with education adjustment, assessment of daily functioning, clinical exclusion criteria, and exclusion of dementia. Participants were classified as having T2DM with MCI (T2DM-MCI; n = 146) or T2DM with normal cognitive function (T2DM-NCF; n = 111). Analyses included descriptive statistics, group comparisons, complete-case logistic regression, latent profile analysis (LPA), a PSQI cutoff plus hypnotic-medication classification, sensitivity analyses, correlation analysis, and cross-sectional indirect-effect models, including exploratory parallel and serial models.ResultsAmong the 257 participants, 146 were classified as T2DM-MCI and 111 as T2DM-NCF. LPA identified three sleep profiles: good sleep, poor sleep with low hypnotic-medication use, and poor sleep with high hypnotic-medication use. A simpler PSQI cutoff plus hypnotic-medication classification showed a broadly similar pattern. In adjusted logistic regression, the poor sleep with low hypnotic-medication use profile was associated with higher odds of MCI than the good sleep profile (OR = 2.50, p = 0.025), whereas the poor sleep with high hypnotic-medication use profile was not statistically significant. Exploratory indirect-effect models suggested significant total indirect effects through HbA1c and PSQI score, but the order-specific serial pathways were not statistically significant. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence of temporal or causal mediation.ConclusionSedentary time, PSQI score, and HbA1c were associated with MCI in older adults with T2DM.These findings support further longitudinal research on multidimensional interventions addressing sedentary behavior, sleep, and glycemic management; however, causal conclusions cannot be drawn from this cross-sectional study.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07956-z.

Introduction

The prevalence of diabetes has increased steadily worldwide and is projected to affect 642 million people by 2040 [1]. Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) accounts for more than 90% of diabetes cases and poses a substantial threat to the health and quality of life of older adults.

Increasing attention has been paid to diabetes-related central nervous system injury, and cognitive impairment and dementia are increasingly recognized as common complications and comorbidities of T2DM [2]. Cognitive dysfunction in patients with diabetes is often manifested as MCI, a transitional state between age-related cognitive decline and dementia. MCI is characterized by deficits in learning, memory retention, and complex information processing [3], and some individuals with MCI may progress to dementia. Because effective treatments for dementia remain limited, lifestyle interventions targeting individuals with MCI have become an important research focus. Identifying modifiable factors associated with MCI in patients with diabetes is therefore clinically important for delaying further cognitive deterioration.

Suboptimal glycemic control is associated with a higher prevalence of MCI in patients with T2DM [4–6]. HbA1c, a key indicator in T2DM management, reflects average glycemic control over approximately 2–3 months and serves as an important marker of disease status.

Previous studies have associated elevated HbA1c with oxidative stress and neuronal or vascular damage, and higher HbA1c concentrations have been correlated with poorer cognitive performance. Glycemic control is therefore an important clinical factor when studying cognitive impairment in individuals with T2DM.

A growing body of evidence indicates that prolonged sedentary behavior, defined as any waking behavior with an energy expenditure of ≤ 1.5 metabolic equivalents (METs) while sitting, reclining, or lying [7], is associated with cognitive decline. Sedentary behavior is highly prevalent among individuals with T2DM [8] and is correlated with glycated hemoglobin levels in this population [9–10]. Emerging evidence also links greater sedentary time with cognitive impairment in community-dwelling older adults with T2DM. Although prior studies have separately examined glycemic control and cognitive impairment, sedentary behavior and cognitive impairment, and sedentary behavior and glycemic control, the extent to which glycemic control statistically accounts for the association between sedentary time and MCI in older adults with T2DM remains unclear.

Sleep disturbances in older adults with T2DM are complex and show substantial inter-individual heterogeneity [11]. Older adults with T2DM may exhibit sleep-related differences that require clinically transparent assessment. A conventional PSQI cutoff is easy to apply in practice, whereas latent profile analysis (LPA) can descriptively summarize patterns across the seven PSQI components [12]. Because mixture models may be less stable in modest samples, we treated LPA as an exploratory descriptive approach and also evaluated a simpler PSQI cutoff plus hypnotic-medication classification. Given evidence linking sedentary behavior with insomnia and sleep disturbances [13–16], we examined whether sleep quality statistically accounted for part of the association between sedentary time and MCI.

The health ecological model [17] emphasizes that health outcomes are shaped by factors operating at multiple levels. Because the variables available in this dataset were primarily individual-level behavioral, psychological, and physiological indicators, we used this framework only as a broad organizing perspective rather than as a full multilevel ecological model. The present analysis focused on intrapersonal factors, including sedentary time, physical activity, sleep quality, depressive symptoms, glycemic control, comorbid chronic diseases, and physical function. We therefore examined: (1) factors associated with operationally defined MCI in older adults with T2DM; (2) associations between sleep-quality classifications and MCI; and (3) whether glycemic control and sleep quality statistically accounted for indirect pathways linking sedentary time with MCI. The cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about temporal ordering or causality.

Methods

Study population

This cross-sectional study was conducted at Qilu Hospital of Shandong University from October 2021 to October 2022. Participants were recruited from the Department of Endocrinology and included patients aged ≥ 60 years with a diagnosis of T2DM. Cognitive status was classified using a prespecified operational framework rather than a full specialist diagnostic work-up. The framework included MoCA screening with the recommended education adjustment, an item-level ADL screen to assess preserved basic daily functioning, review of exclusion criteria, and medical-record review to exclude previously documented dementia. Participants were assigned to the T2DM-NCF group if they had confirmed T2DM, age ≥ 60 years, written informed consent, no previously documented dementia according to medical-record review, preserved basic daily functioning according to the ADL item-level screen described below, and MoCA score ≥ 26 after education adjustment. Participants were assigned to the T2DM-MCI group if they had confirmed T2DM, age ≥ 60 years, written informed consent, no previously documented dementia according to medical-record review, preserved basic daily functioning according to the same ADL item-level screen, and MoCA score < 26 after education adjustment. For the ADL screen, preserved basic daily functioning was operationally defined as complete independence on all 14 ADL items. Participants who reported difficulty in at least one ADL item, defined as any item score ≥ 2, were excluded from both cognitive-status groups because they did not meet the preserved-functioning criterion. This ADL rule was a prespecified study-specific functional screening rule rather than a published ADL total-score cutoff or a stand-alone diagnostic criterion for dementia.

The protocol did not include a comprehensive domain-specific neuropsychological battery or a structured clinical adjudication panel; therefore, MCI classification should be interpreted as an operational study classification based primarily on MoCA screening plus functional and exclusion criteria. Exclusion criteria for both groups were: (1) severe visual or auditory impairment, psychiatric disorders, or communication difficulties; (2) malignancy, severe hepatic or renal dysfunction, or other major comorbidities; and (3) dementia. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Nursing and Rehabilitation at Shandong University, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

Measures

General data questionnaire

Data were collected on gender, age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), education level, marital status, monthly personal income, health insurance coverage, occupation, residential location, smoking status, alcohol consumption, duration of diabetes, glycated hemoglobin levels, number of chronic comorbidities, and handgrip strength.

International physical activity questionnaire

Physical activity and sedentary time were assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short Form (IPAQ-S) [18]. The first six items were used to calculate weekly physical activity in MET-min/week, with MET values of 8.0 for vigorous-intensity activity, 4.0 for moderate-intensity activity, and 3.3 for walking. Participants were categorized as having low (< 600 MET-min/week), moderate (600–3000 MET-min/week), or high (> 3000 MET-min/week) physical activity. Item 7 asks participants to report total daily sitting time over the previous 7 days and was used as the study indicator of sedentary time because the primary sedentary behavior variable was total sitting duration. Because this measure is self-reported, it may be affected by recall and reporting error, particularly in older adults; therefore, sedentary time was interpreted as an approximate indicator rather than an objective device-based measure.

Pittsburgh sleep quality index

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [19] was used to assess sleep quality in older adults with T2DM across seven domains. Each domain is scored from 0 to 3, and the total PSQI score ranges from 0 to 21. Higher scores indicate poorer sleep quality. A global PSQI score < 7 was defined as good sleep quality, whereas a score ≥ 7 indicated poor sleep quality.

Montreal cognitive assessment

The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) was used as the primary cognitive screening instrument for the operational MCI classification [20]. It evaluates multiple cognitive domains, including visuospatial ability, executive function, naming, attention, language, abstraction, delayed recall, and orientation. Total scores range from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating better cognitive performance. A cutoff score of ≥ 26 was considered normal, and an additional point was added for individuals with ≤ 12 years of formal education to reduce educational bias. Because MoCA specificity may be lower among participants with very low educational attainment, education-related misclassification remains possible and was considered when interpreting the findings.

Activities of daily living scale

The Activities of Daily Living (ADL) scale includes 14 items, each scored on a 4-point scale ranging from 1 (“completely independent”) to 4 (“unable to perform”). Total scores range from 14 to 56, with higher scores indicating greater impairment in daily living activities. For the operational cognitive-status classification, the ADL scale was used as an item-level functional screen rather than as a summed-score cutoff. Participants were considered to have preserved basic daily functioning only when all 14 ADL items were scored 1. Any item score ≥ 2, indicating difficulty in at least one daily living activity, was treated as evidence that the participant did not meet the preserved-functioning criterion and led to exclusion from both cognitive-status groups. Medical records were reviewed to exclude participants with a previously documented diagnosis of dementia. This decision rule was prespecified as a study-specific functional screening convention and was not used as a stand-alone diagnostic criterion for dementia.

Five-item geriatric depression scale

The Five-item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-5) [21] was used to assess depressive symptoms in older adults with T2DM. This abbreviated scale was derived from the 15-item GDS and includes five yes/no items, with total scores ranging from 0 to 5. Higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms, and a cutoff score of ≥ 2 is considered indicative of clinically significant depressive symptoms. Its brevity and validated psychometric properties make it a practical screening tool in geriatric populations.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized as means ± standard deviations (M ± SD) for continuous variables and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Between-group comparisons used chi-square tests for categorical variables and independent-samples t-tests or Welch t-tests for continuous variables, as appropriate. Covariates in multivariable logistic regression were selected based on clinical relevance, prior literature, and univariate associations. Collinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with particular attention to sedentary time and physical activity. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test was used to assess logistic model calibration.

Because the study was cross-sectional, indirect-effect analyses were interpreted only as decompositions of statistical associations rather than as causal mediation. In addition to the two single-mediator models, we fitted an exploratory cross-sectional parallel indirect-effect model that included HbA1c and PSQI score simultaneously and estimated the covariance between these variables. We also fitted two exploratory serial indirect-effect models with alternative mediator ordering: sedentary time -> HbA1c -> PSQI score -> MCI and sedentary time -> PSQI score -> HbA1c -> MCI. These indirect-effect models were unadjusted exploratory path models; covariate-adjusted associations were reported separately in logistic regression analyses. For models involving binary MCI status, MCI was coded as 0/1 in observed-variable path models, standardized coefficients were reported, and 95% bootstrap confidence intervals were estimated using 10,000 resamples for the exploratory parallel and serial indirect-effect estimates. These serial models were used only to examine whether the observed associations supported either ordering; they were not interpreted as temporal evidence. We also performed a clinically interpretable sleep classification based on PSQI score (PSQI < 7 versus ≥ 7) and hypnotic-medication use, defined as a PSQI medication component score ≥ 1. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0, Mplus version 8.4, and Python version 3.7.7.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis

With MCI status as the dependent variable, complete-case multivariable binary logistic regression (n = 238) showed that higher HbA1c (OR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.14–1.82, p = 0.002), longer sedentary time (OR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.20–1.48, p < 0.001), and higher PSQI score (OR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.01–1.22, p = 0.030) were associated with higher odds of MCI in older adults with T2DM. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test did not indicate poor calibration (chi-square = 6.798, df = 8, p = 0.559). VIFs for sedentary time, moderate physical activity, and high physical activity were 1.37, 1.59, and 1.64, respectively, indicating no material collinearity between sedentary time and physical activity in the final model. Health insurance type was associated with MCI in univariate analysis, but it was not included in the primary multivariable model because the Other category was sparse and overlapped conceptually with socioeconomic indicators such as monthly income. A sensitivity model adding health insurance type yielded materially unchanged estimates for HbA1c, sedentary time, and PSQI score (Supplementary Table 10) (Table 2).

Latent sleep profiles and MCI

Model selection for latent profile analysis

LPA was conducted as an exploratory descriptive analysis of sleep profiles among older adults with T2DM based on the seven PSQI component scores. Models with 1–4 latent classes were fitted sequentially, and model fit indices are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

The optimal model was selected using information indices (AIC, BIC, and adjusted BIC), comparative indices (LMR and BLRT p values), entropy/classification accuracy, class size, and interpretability. The three-profile solution was retained because it showed acceptable fit, produced interpretable classes, and avoided the small and less stable class generated by the four-profile solution. However, because profile separation largely reflected overall PSQI severity and hypnotic-medication use, the LPA findings were interpreted cautiously and considered alongside the simpler PSQI cutoff plus hypnotic-medication classification.

Naming and classification of latent profiles

Figure 1 presents the conditional means across the seven PSQI components for the three latent sleep profiles. Supplementary Tables 8 and 9 provide posterior classification diagnostics and conditional means ± standard errors. The good sleep profile accounted for 65.0% (n = 167) of participants and showed lower scores across all PSQI components. The poor sleep with low hypnotic-medication use profile accounted for 22.2% (n = 57) and showed elevated scores for subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, and daytime dysfunction, with low but nonzero hypnotic medication use. The poor sleep with high hypnotic-medication use profile accounted for 12.8% (n = 33) and was characterized by poor sleep quality together with high hypnotic medication use. Separation between the two poor-sleep profiles was strongest for hypnotic-medication use and was more limited across other PSQI domains, supporting cautious interpretation of the incremental value of LPA.

Associations between sleep profiles and MCI in older adults with T2DM

In the unadjusted model, both the poor sleep with low hypnotic-medication use profile (OR = 3.34, p < 0.001) and the poor sleep with high hypnotic-medication use profile (OR = 2.50, p = 0.025) had higher odds of MCI than the good sleep profile. After adjustment for the covariates listed in Table 3 Model 2, excluding health insurance type because of sparse cells and overlap with socioeconomic indicators, the poor sleep with low hypnotic-medication use profile remained associated with higher odds of MCI (OR = 2.50, p = 0.025), whereas the poor sleep with high hypnotic-medication use profile was no longer statistically significant (OR = 1.80, p = 0.260). A clinically interpretable cross-classification using the conventional PSQI cutoff and hypnotic-medication use showed a similar pattern: PSQI ≥ 7 without hypnotic-medication use remained associated with MCI after adjustment (OR = 2.36, 95% CI: 1.20–4.62, p = 0.013), whereas PSQI ≥ 7 with hypnotic-medication use was attenuated and not statistically significant (OR = 1.79, 95% CI: 0.70–4.59, p = 0.222; Supplementary Table 13). This consistency suggests that the main sleep-related association was driven primarily by poorer overall sleep quality rather than by a qualitatively distinct latent phenotype.

Correlation analysis

Based on the multivariable logistic regression results, sedentary time, HbA1c, and PSQI score were associated with MCI in older adults with T2DM. Pearson correlation analysis showed positive correlations between sedentary time and HbA1c (r = 0.159, p = 0.011; Supplementary Table 3) and between sedentary time and PSQI score (r = 0.274, p < 0.001; Supplementary Table 4). The correlation between HbA1c and PSQI score was small and not statistically significant (r = 0.110, p = 0.078); after accounting for sedentary time, their residual correlation was also small (r = 0.070, p = 0.264). These associations were interpreted as cross-sectional statistical relationships.

Cross-sectional indirect pathway through HbA1c

Supplementary Table 5 shows the cross-sectional indirect-effect model for sedentary time, HbA1c, and MCI. Sedentary time was associated with MCI (standardized coefficient = 0.420, p < 0.001) and with HbA1c (standardized coefficient = 0.159, p = 0.004). HbA1c was also associated with MCI (standardized coefficient = 0.249, p < 0.001). The indirect pathway from sedentary time to MCI through HbA1c was statistically significant (standardized coefficient = 0.040, p = 0.004), accounting for approximately 8.6% of the total cross-sectional association (Fig. 2).

Exploratory cross-sectional parallel indirect-effect model

An exploratory parallel indirect-effect model that included HbA1c and PSQI score simultaneously was fitted, with the covariance between the two variables estimated (Supplementary Table 12). The total standardized association between sedentary time and MCI was 0.460 (95% CI: 0.358–0.556, p < 0.001), and the direct association was 0.378 (95% CI: 0.270–0.484, p < 0.001). The indirect pathway through HbA1c was 0.038 (95% CI: 0.011–0.070, p = 0.004; proportion = 8.2%), and the indirect pathway through PSQI score was 0.044 (95% CI: 0.014–0.084, p < 0.001; proportion = 9.7%). The total indirect effect was 0.082 (95% CI: 0.041–0.129, p < 0.001; proportion = 17.9%). Because temporal ordering cannot be established in this cross-sectional study, this model was treated as a statistical decomposition of associations rather than evidence of causal mediation.

Exploratory cross-sectional serial indirect-effect models

Two exploratory serial indirect-effect models were fitted to examine alternative ordering of HbA1c and PSQI score (Supplementary Tables 15 and 16). In the model specifying sedentary time -> HbA1c -> PSQI score -> MCI, the total indirect effect was statistically significant (standardized effect = 0.082, 95% CI: 0.041–0.128, p < 0.001), but the serial indirect pathway was small and not statistically significant (standardized effect = 0.002, 95% CI: -0.001 to 0.007, p = 0.274). In the alternative model specifying sedentary time -> PSQI score -> HbA1c -> MCI, the total indirect effect was also statistically significant (standardized effect = 0.082, 95% CI: 0.041–0.128, p < 0.001), whereas the serial indirect pathway was not statistically significant (standardized effect = 0.005, 95% CI: -0.003 to 0.015, p = 0.269). These exploratory findings did not support a clear order-specific serial pathway in the cross-sectional data.

Discussion

We observed a significant association between sedentary time and prevalent MCI among older adults with T2DM; longer sitting time was associated with higher odds of MCI. This finding is consistent with prior evidence linking sedentary behavior with MCI in community-dwelling older adults [22]. Fritschi et al. reported that objectively measured sedentary time was associated with prolonged hyperglycemic states in patients with T2DM [23]. In the present study, higher HbA1c was also associated with MCI. Prior systematic reviews and observational studies have linked poorer glycemic control, fasting glucose, HbA1c, and glucose variability with poorer cognitive outcomes [24–25]. Potential biological explanations include glucose-induced oxidative stress, advanced glycation end-products, and microvascular or macrovascular injury [26–27]. Because our study was cross-sectional, these mechanisms should be interpreted as biologically plausible explanations from previous literature rather than as causal pathways established by the current data.

Poorer sleep quality was associated with higher odds of MCI in older adults with T2DM, consistent with previous studies linking sleep disturbance with cognitive deterioration [28–30]. LPA identified three sleep profiles: good sleep, poor sleep with low hypnotic-medication use, and poor sleep with high hypnotic-medication use. After covariate adjustment, only the poor sleep with low hypnotic-medication use profile remained significantly associated with MCI compared with the good sleep profile, whereas the poor sleep with high hypnotic-medication use profile was not statistically significant. However, the adjusted confidence intervals for the two poor-sleep profiles overlapped considerably (low hypnotic-medication use profile: 1.12–5.56; high hypnotic-medication use profile: 0.65–5.02).

Therefore, the apparent numerical difference between these two profiles should not be interpreted as evidence that their associations with MCI were statistically distinguishable. The simpler PSQI cutoff plus hypnotic-medication classification produced a similar pattern, indicating that the principal distinction was poorer sleep quality combined with medication-use status rather than a clearly separate latent phenotype across all PSQI domains.

This finding should be interpreted cautiously. The dataset did not include detailed information on hypnotic medication class, dose, duration, frequency, indication, or duration of sleep disorder. Therefore, the high hypnotic-medication use profile may reflect clinically recognized sleep problems, closer medical follow-up, short-term symptom relief, or other unmeasured differences rather than a direct protective or harmful medication effect. Given known concerns about hypnotic use in older adults, including falls, delirium, residual daytime sedation, and potential cognitive effects, the present findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and should not be interpreted as evidence supporting hypnotic medication use for cognitive protection. Future longitudinal studies with granular medication data are needed.

The indirect-effect model suggested that HbA1c statistically accounted for part of the cross-sectional association between sedentary time and MCI. Longer sedentary time was associated with higher HbA1c, and higher HbA1c was associated with MCI. These findings are compatible with prior evidence linking sedentary behavior with poorer glucose metabolism [9] and glycemic dysregulation with cognitive impairment [31–32]. However, because exposure, mediator, and outcome were measured simultaneously, the model should be interpreted as a decomposition of associations rather than as evidence that sedentary behavior causes MCI through impaired glycemic control. Longitudinal studies with repeated measures of sedentary time, glycemic variability, and cognitive function are needed to clarify temporal relationships.

The indirect-effect model also suggested that sleep quality statistically accounted for part of the cross-sectional association between sedentary time and MCI. Longer sedentary time was associated with poorer sleep quality, consistent with previous studies [33–34], and poorer sleep quality was associated with MCI. Potential mechanisms discussed in previous literature include circadian disruption, psychological distress, lower physical activity, and impaired slow-wave sleep [13, 35]. In the two exploratory serial models, the total indirect effect remained statistically significant, but the order-specific serial pathways were small and not statistically significant. These results do not support a clear temporal sequence between HbA1c and sleep quality in the present cross-sectional data. These explanations remain speculative and should be tested in longitudinal or interventional studies.

Several limitations should be considered. First, this was a cross-sectional study; temporal ordering among sedentary time, glycemic control, sleep quality, hypnotic medication use, and MCI cannot be established, and the indirect-effect models cannot be interpreted as causal mediation. Second, MCI was operationalized primarily using MoCA screening with education adjustment, functional assessment, and dementia exclusion rather than a full structured, multi-domain neuropsychological diagnostic protocol. In addition, the ADL criterion was an item-level study-specific functional screening rule rather than a validated dementia-stage ADL total-score threshold, and medical-record review was limited to identifying previously documented dementia. Therefore, residual functional-status or dementia-related misclassification cannot be fully excluded. Given the strong association between education and MCI status in this sample, residual education-related misclassification may have occurred, especially among participants who were illiterate or had only elementary-level education.

Third, all participants were recruited from a single tertiary hospital in Jinan, which may limit generalizability to broader community-dwelling populations. Fourth, sedentary time was measured using the self-reported sitting-time item of the IPAQ-S. Self-report may introduce recall and reporting bias, and device-based measures such as accelerometry would provide more precise estimates of sedentary behavior. The large between-group difference in sedentary time should therefore be interpreted cautiously and warrants confirmation in future studies. Fifth, the smallest latent sleep profile contained only 33 participants, which may reduce estimation precision in regression analyses; moreover, the simpler PSQI cutoff plus hypnotic-medication classification produced a broadly similar pattern, suggesting limited incremental clinical value of LPA in this modest sample. Sixth, hypnotic medication use was measured only through the PSQI component score; medication class, dose, duration, indication, and adherence were not available. Seventh, depressive symptoms were assessed using the GDS-5 screening scale rather than a diagnostic interview. Eighth, handgrip strength had missing values; therefore, complete-case multivariable models included 238 participants. Finally, the dataset included overall chronic-disease status and chronic-disease count but did not include detailed cardiometabolic diagnoses such as hypertension or dyslipidemia; residual confounding by these unmeasured conditions cannot be excluded. Future multicenter longitudinal studies with objective sedentary behavior measures, detailed medication information, full cognitive diagnostic assessment, and more granular comorbidity measures are needed.

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional study of older adults with T2DM, sedentary time, PSQI score, and HbA1c were associated with operationally defined MCI. LPA identified sleep profiles, but the simpler PSQI cutoff plus hypnotic-medication classification produced a broadly similar pattern, and LPA should be interpreted as exploratory. Glycemic control and sleep quality statistically accounted for indirect pathways between sedentary time and MCI, whereas exploratory serial models did not support a clear order-specific pathway between HbA1c and sleep quality. These findings do not establish temporal or causal mediation. The results support future longitudinal and intervention studies targeting sedentary behavior, sleep, and glycemic management in older adults with T2DM.
