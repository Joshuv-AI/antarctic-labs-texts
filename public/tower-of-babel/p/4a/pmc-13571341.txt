History of Presentation

A 48-year-old male laborer, with no documented past medical history, presented to an outpatient clinic with a 2-day history of crushing, retrosternal chest pain (8 of 10 intensity) radiating to the left arm.

Take-Home Messages

•In young patients presenting with unexplained coronary ostial occlusion and ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, the diagnostic workup should systematically include aortic sinus thrombus: coronary angiography may show contrast retention at the sinus of Valsalva, transthoracic echocardiography can identify a mobile aortic root mass, and contrast-enhanced CT is essential to exclude dissection before proceeding to surgical exploration.

In young patients presenting with unexplained coronary ostial occlusion and ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, the diagnostic workup should systematically include aortic sinus thrombus: coronary angiography may show contrast retention at the sinus of Valsalva, transthoracic echocardiography can identify a mobile aortic root mass, and contrast-enhanced CT is essential to exclude dissection before proceeding to surgical exploration.

•When aortic root pathology remains diagnostically ambiguous in the setting of active coronary ischemia, emergent surgical exploration is both justified and potentially life-saving, enabling simultaneous definitive diagnosis, thrombectomy, and revascularization.

Past Medical History

No prior cardiovascular, metabolic, or hematological diagnoses. No history of thrombotic events, or autoimmune disease. No current medications. No tobacco, alcohol, or substance use. Remote surgical history: open reduction of femur fracture. No family history of premature cardiovascular disease or coagulation disorders.

Investigations

At the outpatient clinic, coronary angiography revealed 30% stenosis in the left anterior descending artery, nonobstructive circumflex disease, and near-total occlusion of the RCA ostium with contrast retention at the right sinus of Valsalva. An apparent supracoronary dissection flap was identified and the procedure was aborted. Aortic computed tomography (CT) angiography suggested a contained discontinuity in the right supracoronary region.

On arrival at the tertiary center, emergency transthoracic echocardiography performed by cardiology (parasternal long-axis view with aortic valve zoom, Figure 1A) identified an irregular, mobile, hyperechogenic mass attached to the right coronary sinus, with inferior and inferoseptal akinesia, mild left ventricular systolic dysfunction (left ventricular ejection fraction ∼42%), and tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion 13 mm, without valvular disease. Angiography (Figure 1B) confirmed persistent contrast retention at the right sinus of Valsalva. CT angiography (Figure 1C) demonstrated a hyperdense right coronary sinus lesion without a classic dissection flap, membrane, or double lumen. Key laboratory values: white blood cell count 11,910/μL (neutrophilia 79%); hemoglobin 14.6 g/dL; creatinine 1.12 mg/dL; prothrombin time 12.4 seconds; partial thromboplastin time 21.4 seconds.

Intraoperative assessment (Figure 1D) identified a 4 × 2-cm mass occupying the right coronary sinus and completely occluding the RCA ostium, with no evidence of aortic dissection, intramural hematoma, or penetrating ulcer. Moderate turbid pericardial effusion was noted. Severe right ventricular dysfunction with hemorrhagic infiltration of the epicardial fat and extensive biventricular inferior wall scar consistent with prior undiagnosed chronic RCA territory ischemia were additional intraoperative findings.

Final results: Histopathology confirmed an organized thrombus with no malignant cells or microorganisms. Cultures of the mass and pericardial fluid were negative. The complete thrombophilia panel—antiphospholipid antibodies (anticardiolipin immunoglobulin [Ig]G/IgM, antiβ2GP1 IgG/IgM, lupus anticoagulant), protein C, protein S, homocysteine, and antinuclear antibodies—was unremarkable.

Management

Given the diagnostic and imaging-based presumption of Type A aortic dissection with active RCA ischemia, emergency open surgery was performed. Preoperatively, dual-antiplatelet therapy (ticagrelor + aspirin) had been initiated at the outpatient clinic before transfer, and a labetalol infusion (0.5 mg/kg/h, targeting systolic blood pressure <120 mm Hg and heart rate <60 beats/min) and morphine 3 mg intravenously were administered. Median sternotomy was performed and pericardial effusion was aspirated for culture and cytochemistry. Cardiopulmonary bypass was established via distal ascending aorta and bicaval cannulation. Under aortic cross-clamping (59 minutes; total cardiopulmonary bypass time 81 minutes), a transverse aortotomy revealed the 4 × 2-cm mass adherent to the right coronary sinus with complete RCA ostial occlusion. No dissection was identified.

Complete en bloc excision of the mass was performed and sent for histopathology, culture, and cytochemistry. RCA revascularization was achieved with a saphenous vein graft. Aortorrhaphy was performed using the Carrel technique with 4/0 polypropylene. An epicardial pacemaker lead was placed in the right ventricle. Right pleural and mediastinal drains were inserted.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was extubated 12 hours post surgery. Vasoactive and inotropic support was fully weaned by postoperative day 2. Intensive care unit stay was 4 days. The patient was transferred to the general ward hemodynamically stable, ambulatory, and pain-free. As detailed previously, histopathology confirmed an organized thrombus and the complete thrombophilia workup was unremarkable, establishing a final diagnosis of idiopathic ascending aortic thrombus. He was discharged on postoperative day 5 on aspirin 100 mg and clopidogrel 75 mg daily, with antiplatelet therapy transitioned from ticagrelor to clopidogrel after surgery, and with outpatient cardiology and cardiovascular surgery follow-up.

Discussion

Native aortic sinus thrombosis causing acute myocardial infarction (MI) through coronary ostial occlusion is an exceptionally rare entity. The JACC systematic review by Al-Ogaili et al1 identified only 74 cases in the global literature, with MI as the most common presentation (36%) and antiphospholipid syndrome as the leading identifiable thrombophilic etiology (27%). Our case represents an idiopathic variant—defined after a complete thrombophilia workup—localized specifically to the right coronary sinus, one of the least frequently involved sites.

The primary diagnostic challenge was the imaging appearance of the thrombus, which was indistinguishable from an acute aortic syndrome on 3 sequential modalities.2 The coronary angiogram showed contrast retention at the sinus of Valsalva and apparent RCA ostial occlusion. CT angiography demonstrated a contained hyperdense lesion without a definitive double lumen. Emergency transthoracic echocardiography identified the mass but could not exclude a dissection flap, consistent with the reported sensitivity of approximately 59% for transthoracic echocardiography in this location vs 100% for transesophageal echocardiography.1 This triple imaging convergence on the same presumptive diagnosis highlights the inherent limitation of current noninvasive modalities in distinguishing aortic sinus thrombus from dissection in this anatomical region.

The decision to proceed with emergent surgery based on the diagnostic and imaging-based presumption of a Type A aortic dissection was clinically appropriate and likely life-saving.3,4 With the RCA completely occluded, the right ventricle acutely ischemic, and an uncharacterized aortic root mass carrying high embolic potential,5 the risk of further delay vastly outweighed the risk of exploration. Intraoperative discovery of the mass as a discrete, resectable lesion—with no dissection flap present—redefined the surgical strategy from a planned aortic reconstruction to a combined thrombectomy and single-vessel revascularization, a considerably more favorable operative profile.

The extensive biventricular inferior wall scar found at surgery in a patient with no known coronary artery disease raises the compelling hypothesis that the aortic sinus thrombus was not a de novo event but a chronically evolving process that had silently compromised RCA perfusion for months to years before this acute presentation. This shifts the diagnostic interpretation from acute aortic thrombus causing ST-elevation MI to chronic aortic sinus thrombus causing insidious RCA ischemia with acute decompensation—a distinction with important implications for understanding the natural history of this entity.

The predilection for thrombus formation in the right coronary sinus warrants mechanistic consideration. The sinuses of Valsalva generate characteristic vortical flow patterns that facilitate aortic valve closure and coronary perfusion; however, this same anatomy creates zones of relative flow deceleration and stasis during diastole. The right coronary sinus is particularly susceptible because coronary flow into the RCA is intermittent and of lower volume compared with the left system, resulting in greater diastolic stasis within the sinus.6,7 Computational fluid dynamics studies have demonstrated that altered vortex dynamics in the aortic sinus, particularly with increased leaflet stiffness or reduced coronary runoff, can prolong the residence time of blood within the sinus and promote thrombogenic conditions.8

From a surgical standpoint, the management of aortic sinus thrombus with a mobile component requires careful operative planning.9,10 Cannulation strategy is critical: peripheral cannulation via the femoral or axillary artery should be considered when the ascending aorta harbors an uncharacterized mass to avoid dislodgement and systemic embolization. In this case, distal ascending aortic cannulation was performed safely given the mass was localized to the right coronary sinus without involvement of the cannulation site. Cardiopulmonary bypass with aortic cross-clamping allowed a controlled bloodless field for complete en bloc resection. In cases with a freely mobile or more proximally located thrombus, peripheral cannulation with de-airing maneuvers under direct vision would be the preferred approach.4,11

Conclusions

Idiopathic ascending aortic thrombus is an extremely rare but potentially fatal cause of acute MI through coronary ostial occlusion. Its imaging appearance can be indistinguishable from acute aortic dissection, creating a compounded diagnostic challenge that may only be resolved at surgical exploration. Emergent surgery combining thrombectomy and coronary revascularization is definitive and can be life-saving. A complete thrombophilia workup is mandatory in young patients without conventional risk factors, even when results are ultimately unremarkable.12
