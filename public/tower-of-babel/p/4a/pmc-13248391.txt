Real-world evaluation of adherence to single-inhaler triple therapy in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: the LIFE Study
Authors: Hiroaki Ogata, Kazuya Tsubouchi, Tomotsugu Takano, Megumi Maeda, Futoshi Oda, Isamu Okamoto, Haruhisa Fukuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdherence and persistence for inhaled maintenance therapy markedly influence outcomes in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). However, real-world data for single-inhaler triple therapy (SITT), including the effects of prior controller use and the selected regimen, have been limited. We here aimed to evaluate real-world adherence and persistence for SITT in COPD and to examine differences according to prior controller use and inhaler regimen.MethodsUsing the population-based LIFE Study administrative claims database in Japan, we conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients with COPD who newly initiated budesonide-glycopyrronium-formoterol (BGF) or fluticasone furoate–umeclidinium–vilanterol (FUV) between April 2020 and March 2022. Medication adherence over 12 months was assessed according to the proportion of days covered (PDC), with good adherence defined as a PDC of ≥ 0.80. Multivariable-adjusted risk ratios (RRs) for good adherence were estimated by modified Poisson regression. Treatment persistence was evaluated as time to discontinuation determined by Kaplan-Meier analysis with inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), and differences were quantified as restricted mean survival time (RMST) over 365 days.ResultsAmong 4077 individuals initiating SITT, only 36.4% achieved good adherence during the 12-month follow-up period. Controller-naïve patients were substantially less likely to achieve good adherence than patients with prior controller use (multivariable-adjusted RR, 0.37; 95% CI, 0.34–0.41). Treatment persistence was also worse for controller-naïve patients, with an IPTW-adjusted RMST that was 3.42 months shorter than that for patients with prior controller use (95% CI, − 3.67 to − 3.15 months). With respect to regimen, FUV was associated with a higher likelihood of good adherence compared with BGF (multivariable-adjusted RR, 1.40; 95% CI, 1.28–1.52) and showed longer treatment persistence (IPTW-adjusted RMST difference of 0.49 months; 95% CI, 0.22–0.76 months).ConclusionsAdherence and persistence for SITT were suboptimal among patients with COPD in real-world clinical practice, particularly among controller-naïve individuals. Although the once-daily FUV regimen was associated with better adherence and persistence than BGF, substantial gaps in treatment continuity persisted with both regimens. These findings highlight the need for enhanced support at treatment initiation and during early follow-up to optimize long-term SITT use in routine COPD care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-026-03658-7.

Background

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [1, 2]. Inhaled maintenance therapies constitute the cornerstone of pharmacological management for COPD [1, 3, 4]. Adequate medication adherence and treatment persistence are essential for achieving optimal clinical outcomes [1, 5–7], with poor adherence having been associated with an increased risk of exacerbations, hospitalizations, and mortality as well as greater health care utilization and an increased economic burden [5, 7–10]. In routine clinical practice, however, adherence to inhaled therapies and treatment continuity remain suboptimal despite their well-established benefits [11, 12].

Multiple-inhaler triple therapy (MITT), which requires the use of two or more inhaler devices, may impose a considerable treatment burden on individuals with COPD. Single-inhaler triple therapy (SITT) simplifies treatment delivery by combining all components into a single device and has therefore been increasingly adopted in clinical practice [13, 14]. Although SITT has been found to be associated with better medication adherence and treatment persistence compared with MITT [15–19], real-world evidence regarding such adherence and persistence for SITT has remained limited, particularly with respect to differences across individual SITT regimens [20]. Moreover, factors beyond the inhaler regimen itself, such as treatment history before SITT initiation, may also influence SITT adherence and persistence [15]. However, the potential impact of prior controller therapy on treatment continuity after SITT initiation has not been well characterized.

To address these knowledge gaps, we undertook the present study with the aim of evaluating real-world adherence and persistence for SITT among COPD patients with the use of a large, population-based administrative claims database in Japan. We further examined differences in adherence and persistence for SITT according to inhaler regimen and prior controller use.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study relied on medical and pharmacy claims data from the LIFE Study database to identify individuals with COPD who newly initiated budesonide-glycopyrronium-formoterol (BGF) or fluticasone furoate–umeclidinium–vilanterol (FUV) treatment between 1 April 2020 and 31 March 2022. BGF was defined as the fixed-dose combination delivering 320 µg of budesonide, 14.4 µg of glycopyrronium, and 9.6 µg of formoterol fumarate over two inhalations, and FUV was defined as the fixed-dose combination containing 100 µg of fluticasone furoate, 62.5 µg of umeclidinium, and 25 µg of vilanterol per inhalation. The higher-dose formulation containing 200 µg of fluticasone furoate was excluded because of the absence of an approved indication for COPD in Japan. The index date was defined as the date of the first pharmacy claim for BGF or FUV on or after 1 April 2020. The 12 months preceding the index date were designated as the baseline period, for which baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were assessed. The follow-up period extended for 365 days from the index date (see Figure S1 in the online data supplement).

Data source

The analysis in this study was conducted with the LIFE Study database containing information from 19 municipalities in Japan. The LIFE Study is a community-based study initiated in 2019 with the aim of increasing healthy life expectancy and reducing health disparities [21]. The database includes codes for diseases diagnosed on the basis of medical claims data, codes for medications prescribed, the numbers of beds in medical facilities, and information on the municipality of patient residence. Diagnoses are coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), and drug details are coded according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemistry classification.

Study population

Among 3,279,845 individuals in Japan who were covered by the National Health Insurance system, the Latter-Stage Elderly Healthcare System, or public assistance medical coverage and had at least one claim recorded in the LIFE Study database between 1 April 2020 and 31 March 2022, 14,512 patients were prescribed BGF or FUV during the study period. Of these latter patients, we excluded 2935 individuals who had at least one dispensing of BGF or FUV during the baseline period, 1757 individuals who had received at least one dispensing of another triple therapy (including MITT) during the baseline period, 1832 individuals without a diagnosis code for COPD during the baseline period or the index month, 1037 individuals who had at least one dispensing of another controller medication in the index month, 666 individuals whose claims initiated during the baseline period, 122 individuals who were younger than 40 years at the index date, 615 individuals whose claim records ended during the follow-up period, and 1471 individuals who switched to another controller medication during the follow-up period. The remaining 4077 patients were included in the final analysis (Figure S2).

Study outcomes

Medication adherence was assessed on the basis of the proportion of days covered (PDC), which was calculated for each patient as the number of days covered by the prescribed medication divided by the duration of the follow-up period [22]. Good adherence was defined as a PDC of ≥0.80 [22–25]. Treatment persistence was evaluated as the time to discontinuation of the index therapy, with discontinuation being defined as a gap in therapy of > 30 days between the end of the days of supply of a dispensing and the start date of the subsequent prescription fill, or between the end of the days of supply of the last dispensing and the end of the follow-up period [19, 20, 26].

Clinical evaluation

For each patient, information including sex, age group (categorized in 5-year intervals), municipality size, and facility size was collected during the index month. Data on prior controller medications, reliever prescriptions during the 12 months preceding the index date, and medical histories of bronchial asthma, ischemic heart disease, atrial fibrillation, chronic heart failure, sleep apnea syndrome, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, malignant neoplasms, and cerebrovascular disease were collected for the baseline period. Differences in access to medical care among residents were taken into account by defining municipality size according to the classification of local governments by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications as follows: small towns (< 50,000 residents), medium-sized cities (≥ 50,000 to < 200,000 residents), large cities (≥ 200,000 to < 500,000 residents), and metropolitan areas (≥ 500,000 residents) [27–29]. Facility size was categorized as follows: clinics (0–19 beds), small hospitals (20–200 beds), medium-sized hospitals (201–400 beds), and large hospitals (≥ 401 beds) [28, 30]. Definitions of comorbidities on the basis of ICD-10 codes are provided in Table S1. Patients were categorized according to baseline controller use status as either baseline controller users or controller-naïve individuals. Controller-naïve status was defined as the absence of controller prescriptions during the 12-month baseline period, regardless of whether a COPD diagnosis code had been recorded before the index date. A preindex COPD diagnosis was defined as the presence of at least one COPD diagnosis code recorded during the baseline period prior to the index month. Controller medications were defined as inhaled corticosteroids, long-acting β2-agonists, or long-acting muscarinic antagonists, whereas reliever medications were defined as short-acting β2-agonists or short-acting muscarinic antagonists.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was performed with R software version 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For baseline characteristics, heterogeneity according to controller-naïve status and SITT regimen (BGF versus FUV) was assessed with the chi-square test. The distribution of PDC was also compared according to controller-naïve status and SITT regimen with the chi-square test. Median PDC values were summarized with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and compared between individuals with prior controller use and controller-naïve patients, as well as between the BGF and FUV groups, with the use of the Mann-Whitney U test. For evaluation of the associations of prior controller use or SITT regimen with good adherence (PDC of ≥ 0.8), modified Poisson regression analysis was applied to estimate risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Models were adjusted for sex, age group, municipality size, health care facility size, SITT regimen, baseline controller use, baseline reliever use, preindex COPD diagnosis, bronchial asthma, and comorbidities including ischemic heart disease, atrial fibrillation, chronic heart failure, sleep apnea syndrome, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, malignant neoplasms, and cerebrovascular disease. Subgroup analysis was conducted on the basis of SITT regimen, baseline controller use, and asthma status, with corresponding multivariable adjustment excluding the stratification variable in each analysis. Treatment persistence was evaluated with the Kaplan-Meier method. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on propensity scores was applied to balance baseline covariates across groups defined by baseline controller use or SITT regimen. Propensity scores were estimated by logistic regression incorporating the same covariates as in the multivariable adherence models. Covariate balance before and after IPTW was evaluated on the basis of standardized mean differences (SMDs), with values of < 0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance. Restricted mean survival time (RMST) over the 365-day follow-up period was estimated from IPTW-adjusted Kaplan-Meier curves in order to compare treatment persistence between patients with prior controller use and controller-naïve patients, and between patients initiating BGF and those initiating FUV. Differences in RMST according to prior controller use and SITT regimen were estimated with 95% CIs. IPTW-adjusted RMST analysis was further performed for subgroups defined by SITT regimen, baseline controller use, and asthma status.

A landmark analysis was performed to evaluate adherence beyond the early treatment phase. Patients who received at least one additional prescription between days 31 and 90 after SITT initiation were included, and PDC during days 91 to 365 was assessed according to the same definitions and analytic approach as those adopted for the principal analysis. Modified Poisson regression was again applied to estimate adjusted RRs for the landmark cohort. As an additional sensitivity assessment, the adherence and persistence analyses were repeated with the inclusion of treatment switchers in the study population. Treatment switchers were defined as patients who changed from their initial SITT regimen to another controller medication during the follow-up period (n = 1471), and switching was treated as discontinuation of the initial regimen. The same covariates adopted in the primary analysis were applied for adjustment.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 4077 individuals with COPD who newly initiated SITT was identified, of whom 2019 were controller-naïve and 2058 had prior controller use, and 1704 received BGF and 2373 received FUV. Baseline characteristics of the study population stratified by controller-naïve status and SITT regimen are summarized in Table 1. Compared with patients with prior controller use, controller-naïve patients were more likely to initiate BGF, and were less likely to be aged ≥ 75 years or to have a preindex COPD diagnosis, a prior reliever prescription, or a history of bronchial asthma, sleep apnea syndrome, or malignant neoplasms. The proportion of patients with a preindex COPD diagnosis was low in both groups. Facility size also tended to be smaller for controller-naïve patients, whereas most cardiovascular and metabolic comorbidities as well as municipality size were similarly distributed between the two groups. With regard to SITT regimen, male patients, individuals with prior controller medication use, those with a preindex COPD diagnosis, and those using reliever medications were more common in the FUV group than in the BGF group, and individuals receiving FUV were more likely to reside in smaller municipalities. No meaningful differences were observed between the BGF and FUV groups with respect to age, major comorbidities, or facility size.

Baseline characteristics of treatment switchers are shown in Table S2. Compared with patients included in the primary analysis, treatment switchers were more likely to have prior controller use and a history of asthma. The distribution of SITT regimens also differed moderately between the two groups, with FUV being more common among switchers. Other baseline characteristics—including age, sex, preindex COPD diagnosis, and most comorbidities—were broadly similar between the two groups.

Adherence and persistence for SITT according to baseline controller use

In the overall cohort, only 36.4% of patients achieved good medication adherence (Figure S3). The proportion of individuals with good adherence was 53.3% among subjects with baseline controller use and 19.2% among controller-naïve patients (P < 0.01) (Fig. 1A). The median PDC was 0.82 (IQR, 0.46–0.94) for patients with prior controller use and 0.19 (IQR, 0.08–0.69) for controller-naïve patients (P < 0.01, Mann-Whitney U test). Adjusted analysis revealed that controller-naïve patients remained less likely to achieve good adherence than patients with prior controller use (multivariable-adjusted RR of 0.37, with a 95% CI of 0.34–0.41; P < 0.01). This association was consistently observed regardless of SITT regimen or the presence of bronchial asthma (P < 0.01 in each case) (Fig. 2).

In a landmark analysis restricted to individuals who received at least one additional prescription between days 31 and 90 after initiating SITT (n = 2702), the proportion of patients achieving good adherence increased to 52.1% during days 91 to 365. This proportion was 61.9% among participants with prior controller use and 36.1% among controller-naïve patients (P < 0.01). Concurrently, the number of controller-naïve patients was reduced by approximately half (Figure S4A). The association of prior controller prescription with good adherence after adjustment for potential confounders remained consistent with the results of the main analysis (Figure S5).

IPTW-adjusted proportions of patients remaining on treatment according to prior controller use are shown in Fig. 3A. Whereas some baseline covariates differed between controller-naïve patients and prior controller users before weighting (SMD of > 0.1), IPTW reduced all SMDs to < 0.1, indicative of adequate covariate balance (Figure S6). Treatment persistence was substantially worse among controller-naïve patients than among individuals with prior controller use, paralleling the differences observed in good adherence. The IPTW-adjusted RMST was significantly shorter in controller-naïve patients than in individuals with prior controller use (difference of − 3.42 months, with a 95% CI of − 3.67 to − 3.15 months; P < 0.01). This finding was consistent across all subgroups defined by SITT regimen (P < 0.01 for both) and asthma status (P < 0.01 for both) (Fig. 4).

Sensitivity analysis including treatment switchers revealed that controller-naïve patients remained significantly less likely to achieve good adherence than patients with prior controller use (multivariable-adjusted RR of 0.42, with a 95% CI of 0.39–0.46; P < 0.01). Consistently, treatment persistence remained significantly shorter in controller-naïve patients (IPTW-adjusted RMST difference of − 3.27 months, with a 95% CI of − 3.54 to − 3.05 months; P < 0.01) (Figure S7A).

Adherence and persistence for SITT according to regimen

Stratification by SITT regimen showed that the proportion of participants with good adherence was 27.8% in the BGF group and 42.6% in the FUV group (Fig. 1B). The median PDC was 0.45 (IQR, 0.10–0.83) in the BGF group and 0.69 (IQR, 0.16–0.92) in the FUV group (P < 0.01, Mann-Whitney U test). After multivariable adjustment, FUV was associated with a significantly higher likelihood of good adherence compared with BGF (multivariable-adjusted RR of 1.40, with a 95% CI of 1.28–1.52; P < 0.01). Similar patterns were observed irrespective of baseline controller use or asthma status (P < 0.01 in each case) (Fig. 5).

For the landmark analysis (n = 2702), good adherence was observed in 40.5% of patients receiving BGF and 60.1% of those receiving FUV (P < 0.01) (Figure S4B). This regimen-specific difference remained robust after multivariable adjustment (Figure S8).

Consistent with the adherence findings, treatment persistence was also better for FUV than for BGF (Fig. 3B). Baseline imbalance between the BGF and FUV groups was moderate (all SMD values were < 0.1 before weighting), and IPTW further improved covariate balance (Figure S9). The IPTW-adjusted RMST was 0.49 months longer in the FUV group than in the BGF group (95% CI of 0.22–0.76 months, P < 0.01). Similar results were obtained in subgroup analysis according to baseline controller use (P < 0.01 for both) and asthma status (P < 0.01 for patients with asthma and P = 0.01 for those without asthma) (Figure S10).

When treatment switchers were included in the analysis, FUV remained associated with a higher likelihood of good adherence compared with BGF (multivariable-adjusted RR of 1.34, with a 95% CI of 1.26–1.43; P < 0.01). Treatment persistence was also longer in the FUV group (IPTW-adjusted RMST difference of 0.49 months, with a 95% CI of 0.25–0.72 months; P < 0.01) (Figure S7B).

Discussion

Our study has revealed degraded medication adherence among individuals with COPD initiating SITT, with only 36.4% achieving good adherence. The adherence for controller-naïve patients was only about one-third of that for participants previously treated with controller medications, and this association remained consistent across subgroups defined by SITT regimen and asthma status. Between the two SITT regimens, adherence was significantly better for FUV than for BGF, again with consistent findings across subgroups stratified by baseline controller use and asthma status. IPTW-adjusted Kaplan-Meier curves revealed longer treatment persistence among patients with baseline controller use than among controller-naïve individuals, as well as in the FUV group compared with the BGF group, mirroring the directional patterns observed for good adherence. As far as we are aware, our study is the first to evaluate SITT adherence and persistence in individuals with COPD while simultaneously examining differences not only between SITT regimens but also according to baseline controller use.

To date, five real-world claims database studies have evaluated the 12-month proportion of COPD patients achieving good adherence (PDC of ≥ 0.8) to SITT [16, 17, 19, 20, 24]. Young et al. analyzed a large U.S. claims database and found that the proportion of patients achieving good adherence was 35.1% in the FUV group and 24.8% in the BGF group [20], indicating that more than half of patients had suboptimal adherence—findings broadly consistent with those of the present study. Other real-world analyses, including a U.S. claims-based study and an analysis based on linked primary and secondary care data in the United Kingdom, similarly reported low proportions of patients with good adherence (25.9% and 34.4%, respectively) [16, 24]. The slightly higher frequency of good adherence observed in our population compared with these U.S. cohorts may be attributable in part to the universal insurance and public assistance systems in Japan, which facilitate regular follow-up and continuous prescription refills, given that high treatment cost has been recognized as a risk factor for poor adherence [31]. In a separate U.S. claims-based study, Mannino et al. found a good adherence rate of 43.2% among individuals receiving FUV [19], which is slightly higher than that observed in the present study. This difference may be explained in part by differences in the study populations, given that > 80% of patients in the previous cohort had received controller therapy prior to SITT initiation—a factor that we also identified as being associated with better adherence. By contrast, a Japanese claims database study based on the Medical Data Vision database reported a markedly lower proportion of patients achieving good adherence to FUV (5.6%) [17], which is likely attributable to differences in the underlying study population and data source characteristics compared with the present analysis. Although SITT has been found to be associated with better adherence relative to MITT, for which reported rates of good adherence are as low as 3.7% to 17.4% [16, 17, 19, 24, 26], our finding that fewer than half of patients achieved good adherence even with SITT underscores a pressing unmet need in the management of COPD.

In the present study, treatment adherence and persistence were particularly poor among controller-naïve patients relative to individuals with baseline controller use, with about half of the controller-naïve patients having already discontinued SITT between 31 and 90 days after initiation. These observations indicate that controller-naïve patients are at particularly high risk for early discontinuation and sustained poor adherence after SITT initiation, consistent with findings of a previous real-world study [15]. Given that the associations between controller-naïve status and both adherence and persistence remained robust after adjustment including preindex COPD diagnosis, early discontinuation in such patients may not necessarily indicate poor adherence behavior but may reflect in part treatment initiation dynamics. After exclusion of individuals who discontinued SITT within 90 days after initiation, the proportion of controller-naïve patients who achieved good adherence nearly doubled from 19.2% to 36.1%, even though this still represented only about one-third of such patients. Accordingly, targeted strategies to support treatment initiation in this population—such as reinforcement of inhaler education and close early follow-up—appear warranted. In the absence of such interventions, the potential benefits of SITT may not be fully realized in routine clinical practice [32–34].

Direct comparison of treatment adherence and persistence between different SITT regimens has remained limited to a single real-world study [20]. This previous analysis, based on a U.S. claims database, showed that FUV was associated with better adherence and persistence relative to BGF [20], consistent with the present study. The more favorable adherence and persistence observed with FUV in both studies may be attributable to regimen complexity. FUV thus requires only one inhalation once daily, whereas BGF requires two inhalations twice daily, with the higher inhaler burden of the latter regimen possibly contributing to poorer adherence [35]. However, given that our study was based on claims data, we were unable to assess behavioral or prescribing factors that may also influence adherence, with the underlying causes therefore remaining uncertain.

The inverse associations between controller-naïve status and both medication adherence and treatment persistence, as well as the positive associations between FUV therapy and these outcomes, that were observed in the present study remained consistent irrespective of asthma status. Individuals with asthma or COPD who newly initiate controller therapy have been shown to be at a considerably higher risk of low adherence and early discontinuation [15, 36, 37]. Previous studies have consistently shown that simpler dosing regimens with fewer daily inhalations are associated with improved medication adherence [6, 12, 38]. Our present results do not contradict such evidence. The consistency of the observed associations irrespective of asthma status suggests that controller-naïve status and regimen simplicity may play a more dominant role in treatment continuity than the underlying disease phenotype.

A major strength of the present study is its reliance on a large, population-based administrative claims database covering > 3 million individuals across Japan, with the detailed information available on inhaler medications, comorbidities, municipality size, and health care facility size allowing comprehensive multivariable adjustment. Several limitations of our study should also be acknowledged, however. First, it was based on administrative claims data only from Japan, and differences in health care systems and insurance structures may limit the generalizability of our findings to other countries. Second, detailed clinical information—such as pulmonary function parameters, symptom burden, and disease severity—was not available in the claims database. We were therefore unable to directly assess COPD severity or to adjust for these clinical factors, which may influence both treatment selection and medication adherence. Although we adjusted for several proxy indicators of disease severity and confirmed adequate covariate balance after weighting, residual confounding due to unmeasured clinical factors cannot be completely excluded. Third, the use of municipal-level claims data limited the ability to fully capture patient movement between health care facilities, potentially resulting in misclassification of treatment discontinuation or persistence. However, patients whose claims records ended within 365 days after the index date were excluded from the analysis, suggesting that this limitation is unlikely to have substantially affected the main findings. Fourth, the study population was skewed toward older individuals. Given that COPD predominantly affects older adults, this age distribution is unlikely to have materially influenced interpretation of the results. Fifth, patients who switched SITT regimens during follow-up were excluded from the primary analysis so as to allow a clearer evaluation of adherence and persistence for the initially prescribed regimen. Although sensitivity analysis including treatment switchers yielded similar results, exclusion of such patients may limit generalizability to clinical settings in which treatment modification is common. Finally, medication adherence was assessed on the basis of prescription refill data, which serve as a proxy for actual medication use. This approach does not provide direct confirmation that patients used their inhalers as prescribed or with the correct inhalation technique and may therefore overestimate true adherence.

Conclusions

Our study provides real-world evidence on treatment adherence and persistence for SITT and shows that controller-naïve patients are at particularly high risk for early discontinuation. Although FUV therapy showed better adherence and persistence than did BGF, treatment continuity remained suboptimal regardless of the inhaler regimen. These findings suggest that improvement of real-world SITT use will require enhanced support at treatment initiation and during early follow-up so as to optimize long-term treatment continuity in everyday clinical care.
