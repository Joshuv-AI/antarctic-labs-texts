The ratio of serum uric acid to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol as a predictor for frailty prevalence and mortality in the elderly U.S. adults
Authors: Yongwei Huang, Xiaoyi Wang, Xiaoshuang Yin, Zongping Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrailty represents a common condition among older individuals, linked to heightened vulnerabilities for negative health effects and death. The ratio of uric acid (UA) to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) (UHR) serves as a novel indicator for inflammatory and oxidative processes, although its connections to frailty rates and death risks in frail elderly populations are still unexamined. Our research explored the relationships between UHR, frailty occurrence, and death results in frail older adults in the United States, along with evaluating the intermediary effects of eating habits.MethodsData from 11,751 participants (weighted n = 364.6 million individuals) across 12 NHANES cycles (1999–2023) were analyzed. UHR was calculated as [serum UA (mg/dL) / HDL-C (mg/dL)] × 100%. Frailty was assessed using a 49-item deficit accumulation index and participants needed to respond to a minimum of 39 out of 49 items. A frailty index ≥ 0.25 indicates frailty. Mortality data were linked from the National Death Index up to December 31, 2019. Dietary patterns were evaluated via the Mediterranean diet score, the Healthy Eating Index-2020, the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension Index, the Planetary Health Diet Index-US, and the Dietary Inflammation Index (DII). Weighted multivariable logistic/Cox regressions, restricted cubic splines, threshold analyses, subgroup interactions, and mediation models were employed, adjusting for demographics, lifestyle, clinical covariates, and diverse diet scores.ResultsHigher UHR was associated with increased frailty risk (fully adjusted OR per unit: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.06–1.08) in a linear pattern (Pnonlinearity = 0.640). In frail participants (n = 2,498; median follow-up 5.92 years), UHR predicted higher all-cause mortality (ACM; HR: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.03) and cardiovascular mortality (CVM; HR: 1.04, 95% CI: 1.02–1.05), with nonlinear U-shaped relationships (both Pnonlinearity < 0.001) and thresholds at 9.07 (ACM) and 9.54 (CVM). Tertile analyses showed dose-dependent effects, with T3 vs. T1 yielding OR = 2.05 (frailty), HR = 1.28 (ACM), and HR = 1.51 (CVM). Associations of UHR and frailty were stronger in females (Pinteraction = 0.016), living alone (Pinteraction = 0.008), and alcohol consumers (Pinteraction = 0.048). No interaction effects of the subgroups were observed in the associations of UHR and mortality outcomes in frailty participants. Sensitive analysis revealed the robustness and generalizability of our findings. Exploratory analysis of dietary patterns in the UHR-frailty association demonstrated that dietary patterns mediated 1.35–5.06% of the UHR-frailty link, with DII exerting the largest effect (5.06%).ConclusionsElevated UHR independently predicts frailty and mortality in elderly U.S. adults. As an accessible biomarker, UHR offers potential for early risk assessment and intervention in aging populations. Future trials should validate these findings and explore therapeutic targeting of UHR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02756-7.

Introduction

The global population is currently confronting an increasingly severe challenge posed by aging, accompanied by a annual increase in the prevalence of frailty [1, 2]. Frailty is characterized as a multifaceted syndrome encompassing diminished physical function, impaired mobility, and depleted physiological reserves, which predisposes individuals to various adverse health outcomes, including falls, hospitalizations, cognitive impairment, and elevated mortality risk [3]. The ramifications of frailty extend beyond the affected individuals, substantially compromising their quality of life and imposing considerable economic burdens on healthcare systems [4]. This underscores the imperative to identify and manage the contributing factors to this condition. Frailty is not an irreversible state and can be prevented or ameliorated through appropriate interventions [5]. Consequently, elucidating and recognizing the determinants associated with frailty is essential for improving the health outcomes of older adults.

Frailty arises from a variety of factors, including poor nutrition, sedentary lifestyles, hormonal changes, persistent conditions like cancer and heart disease, as well as hereditary factors [6]. The underlying mechanisms of frailty are complex, where chronic inflammation and oxidative damage play central roles [7]. As the final metabolite in purine breakdown, uric acid (UA) shows notable connections to inflammatory processes and oxidative imbalances [8]. Meanwhile, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) demonstrates protective effects against inflammation and oxidation, although it can also contribute to the development of insulin resistance (IR) and metabolic disorders [9–11]. The ratio of uric acid to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (UHR) emerges as a novel indicator for inflammation and metabolism, associated with increased risk of metabolic syndrome, abdominal aortic aneurysms, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and heart-related ailments [12–17]. Serving as a composite index, UHR enables a comprehensive assessment of overall oxidative burden in the body. Commonly measured in routine clinical practice, this marker provides valuable advantages for practical use, such as simplicity in testing, affordability, and high reproducibility. Importantly, the connection between UHR and frailty occurrence, along with mortality rates, in elderly U.S. populations remains unexplored. Therefore, exploring UHR’s predictive potential holds substantial importance for both theoretical insights and medical applications. This work aims to explore the link between UHR and frailty prevalence, explore how dietary patterns mediate the UHR-frailty connection, and determine UHR’s association with mortality risks in frail older adults in the United States.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This investigation utilized data sourced from twelve consecutive cycles of NHANES, spanning from 1999 to 2023. NHANES, employed by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) under the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), employs a complex, multistage, probability sampling design to ensure robust representation of the non-institutionalized U.S. population. This approach incorporates stratification and clustering techniques to capture a diverse cross-section of demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related characteristics, thereby enhancing the generalizability of findings to the broader U.S. population.

To focus the analysis on older adults and ensure data integrity, specific inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied. Participants younger than 60 years were excluded to target the elderly population, where frailty is more prevalent. Additionally, individuals with incomplete or missing data on critical variables—namely, the UHR, frailty diagnosis, relevant covariates (e.g., age, sex, comorbidities), or mediating factors (e.g., dietary patterns)—were excluded to maintain the reliability and validity of the statistical analyses. Measurement of UHR

The UHR was computed using the formula: UHR = [serum UA (mg/dL)/HDL-C (mg/dL)] × 100% [17]. Trained staff collected blood samples at the Mobile Examination Center (MEC). Serum UA concentrations were measured through the timed endpoint method, while HDL-C levels were evaluated using the enzymatic assay [18]. Detailed descriptions of the experimental protocols can be found on the NHANES website.

Assessment of frailty

The evaluation of frailty employed a model based on the accumulation of deficits. Participants needed to respond to a minimum of 39 out of 49 items on the frailty index (FI). The FI score, which varies from 0 to 1, was determined by summing the individual deficit factors and dividing that total by the overall number of assessed deficits. A score of 0 signifies the absence of deficits, while a score of 1 reflects the maximum degree of deficit accumulation. This index includes 49 distinct criteria categorized into seven areas: cognitive ability, functional dependence, depressive signs, comorbid conditions, healthcare usage, physical measurements, and laboratory results (Supplemental Table S1). To facilitate analysis, the continuous frailty score was divided into two categories based on a threshold of 0.25. Scores that surpassed this value were considered indicative of frailty, consistent with earlier studies [19].

Assessment of mortality outcomes

This research assessed two mortality outcomes: all-cause mortality (ACM) and cardiovascular mortality (CVM). ACM encompasses deaths from any cause, whereas CVM specifically refers to deaths resulting from cardiovascular diseases. Mortality data were obtained from the National Death Index (NDI) and linked to NHANES data up to December 31, 2019.

Assessment of diet patterns

In the NHANES study, dietary pattern assessment primarily utilized 24-hour dietary recall, during which participants document the types, quantities, and timing of all foods and beverages consumed the previous day [20]. This intake information was standardized by referencing global food composition databases to normalize data for each food or nutrient. The standardized data were then converted into percentile scores to address the impact of skewed dietary distributions. Adherence to a Mediterranean‑style pattern was captured with the 0–9 point Mediterranean diet score (MED), which awards 1 point when consumption of vegetables, legumes, fruits and nuts, whole grains, fish, or the monounsaturated‑to‑saturated‑fat ratio is at or above the sex‑specific median, 1 point when dairy or red/processed meat intake is below the median, and 1 point for moderate alcohol intake (5–25 g per day); higher totals denote closer conformity to the traditional Mediterranean diet [21]. The dietary approaches to stop hypertension index (DASHI) quantifies eight food components (fruit, vegetables, nuts/legumes, whole grains, low‑fat dairy, red/processed meat, sweetened beverages, sodium) by assigning sex‑specific quintile scores (1–5 in favorable order for healthy foods, reverse order for unhealthy foods) and summing them to 8–40, which was rescaled to 0–9 for comparability, with higher values indicating greater adherence to the DASH diet [22]. Overall diet quality was further evaluated by the healthy eating index 2020 (HEI‑2020): thirteen components aligned with the 2020–2025 Dietary Guidelines for Americans were expressed per 1,000 kcal, individually scored (0–5 or 0–10) and summed to a total of 0–100, where higher scores signify better compliance with guideline recommendations [23]. The United States planetary health diet index (PHDI-US) was formulated and scored according to the principles of the original PHDI framework [24]. The index, comprising 16 components with a highest score of 150 points, reflects better compliance with the PHD dietary guidelines as the PHDI-US score increases [25]. Finally, the diet inflammation index (DII) was calculated for up to 45 energy‑adjusted nutrients and bioactive compounds; each parameter was centered to a global reference mean, multiplied by its literature‑derived inflammatory effect score and summed such that more positive values reflect a more pro‑inflammatory dietary profile [26].

Extraction of covariates

To effectively manage the confounding variables that could potentially skew the results of the study, a comprehensive approach was adopted that focused on a diverse array of known covariates. These covariates include demographic factors, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, educational attainment, marital status, and the family poverty income ratio (PIR). Additionally, lifestyle covariates (alcohol consumption and smoking behavior) and laboratory parameters (UA and HDL-C) were taken into account. The study further incorporated specific dietary patterns and hyperlipidemia. Dietary patterns were quantified by MED, DII, HEI-2020, DASHI, and PHDI-US. Diagnosis of hyperlipidemia: (1) total cholesterol ≥ 200 mg/dL (5.18 mmol/L); triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL; LDL: LDL ≥ 130 mg/dl (3.37 mmol/L); (2) high density lipoprotein < 40 mg/dL (1.04 mmol/L) for males, 50 mg/dL(1.3 mmol/L) for females; (3) individuals reporting use of cholesterol-lowering medications. Income levels were classified into categories: low (< 1.3), moderate (1.3–3.5), or high (> 3.5). Smoking status was categorized as never, former, or current smokers. Alcohol consumption was grouped according to drinking patterns into five categories: never, former, heavy, moderate, and light drinkers. A comprehensive description of these covariates is provided in Supplemental Table S2.

Statistical analysis

The NHANES employed an advanced multi-stage probability sampling method to guarantee a wide representation of the sample. Following the analysis guidelines set by NHANES, we included sampling weights and took into account primary sampling units alongside stratification factors. Continuous variables were shown as medians accompanied by interquartile ranges (IQR) or means accompanied by standard deviation (SD) based on distribution of skewness or normality of data, while categorical variables are depicted as number and percentages. To assess the significance of differences among groups, we utilized t-tests, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, and chi-square tests. All statistical evaluations were carried out using R (v 4.4.2) and EmpowerStats, with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05.

The relationship between UHR and frailty risk was analyzed through weighted multivariable logistic regression, and the relationship between UHR and mortality outcomes in frail participants was analyzed through weighted multivariable Cox regression analyses. Several models were established. Specifically, unadjusted Model 1, partially adjusted Model 2 (age, sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, educational attainment, and family PIR), partially adjusted Model 3 (Covariates in Model 2 + hyperlipidemia, alcohol consumption, and smoking status), and fully adjusted Model 4 (Covariates in Model 3 + MED, DASHI, HEI-2020, DII, and PHDI-US). The findings were expressed through weighted odds ratios (ORs) or hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs), which offered a statistical measure of the precision of the estimates. To further investigate the dose–response relationship between UHR and frailty risk and mortality, restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression models were applied. This approach aimed to identify potential threshold values and the non-linear relationship. To reaffirm the robustness of our results, stratified analysis and interaction tests were performed. Moreover, we executed a mediation analysis using the R package “mediation,” which involved 1,000 bootstrap resampling iterations. This analysis was designed to investigate the mediating contributions of diverse diet patterns in the UHR-frailty association. In this context, direct effects were defined as the influence of UHR on frailty, while indirect effects represented the contributions of diverse diet patterns as mediators. The mediation effect ratio was computed using the formula: indirect effect/(indirect effect + direct effect) × 100%.

Results

Baseline characteristics of all participants stratified by UHR tertiles

Table 1 summarizes the baseline sociodemographic, clinical, and biochemical characteristics of 11,751 participants from the NHANES (1999–2023), representing a weighted population of approximately 364.6 million U.S. adults aged 60 years or older. Participants were stratified into tertiles based on UHR values: Tertile 1 (T1: ≤ 8.95, n = 3,917), Tertile 2 (T2: 8.96–13.07, n = 3,920), and Tertile 3 (T3: ≥ 13.08, n = 3,914). Significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed across most variables, except race/ethnicity (P = 0.082). Higher UHR tertiles were characterized by a greater proportion of males, married individuals, former smokers, and those with hyperlipidemia. Frailty prevalence escalated dose-dependently from 16.9% in T1 to 27.4% in T3 (P < 0.001). Biochemical markers aligned with the UHR definition: mean UA increased from 4.6 ± 1.0 mg/dL in T1 to 6.9 ± 1.3 mg/dL in T3, while HDL-C decreased from 69.5 ± 15.7 mg/dL to 40.5 ± 8.1 mg/dL. Dietary indices indicated poorer dietary quality in higher tertiles, with declines in MED, DASHI, HEI-2020, and PHDI-US. The DII showed a modest pro-inflammatory shift (median 1.8 [IQR: 0.2–2.9] in T1 to 1.9 [0.4–3.0] in T3; P < 0.001).

Baseline characteristics of frail participants stratified by UHR tertiles

Table 2 focuses on the frailty subgroup (n = 2,498; mean age, 72.1 ± 7.5 years), revealing similar patterns but with amplified disparities. Higher UHR tertiles (T1: ≤ 9.78; T2: 9.79–14.56; T3: ≥14.57) correlated with increased male representation, hyperlipidemia, and former drinkers. Mortality outcomes were notably higher in T3: ACM prevalence increased from 53.3% in T1 to 63.3% (P < 0.001), and CVM from 18.9% to 25.0% (P = 0.005). Biochemical trends mirrored Table 1, with UA rising from 4.7 ± 1.1 to 7.4 ± 1.5 mg/dL and HDL-C falling from 65.4 ± 15.8 to 38.8 ± 8.3 mg/dL. Dietary scores again declined in higher tertiles (MED, HEI-2020, and PHDI-US). These baseline profiles highlight UHR as a marker intertwined with male sex, metabolic dysregulation, unhealthy lifestyles, and suboptimal diets, setting the stage for adjusted associations.

Dose-response and threshold effects

Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a significant positive association between the UHR and the risk of frailty in a linear pattern (Pnonlinearity = 0.640, Fig. 3A), with the risk increasing steadily above a reference value of 10.59. In contrast, non-linear U-shaped relationships were observed between UHR and both ACM and CVM (both Pnonlinearity < 0.001,Fig. 3 [B, C]), with the lowest risk observed at a UHR of approximately 11.85. Subgroup and interaction analyses

Stratified analyses confirmed the robustness of UHR-frailty associations across subgroups, with consistent positive ORs (1.06–1.08; all P < 0.05, Table 5). Significant interactions were noted for sex (P = 0.016; stronger in females: OR = 1.08 vs. males: 1.06), marital status (P = 0.008, stronger in separated/divorced/widowed: OR = 1.08 vs. married/living with partner: 1.07), and alcohol consumption (P = 0.048; stronger in drinkers: OR = 1.08 vs. non-drinkers: 1.06). No interactions emerged for age, race/ethnicity, education, PIR, hyperlipidemia, or smoking (Pinteraction >0.05).

Exploratory analysis of dietary patterns in the UHR-frailty association

An exploratory mediation analysis was conducted to explore how dietary patterns mediate the UHR-frailty association. Diverse dietary patterns were found to be significant mediators in the association between UHR and frailty (Supplemental Table S3 and Fig. 1). In particular, the total effect of UHR on frailty risk was determined to be 0.0057 (95% CI: 0.0051–0.0062). For DII, MED, DASHI, HEI-2020, and PHDI-US, the indirect effects were 0.0003 (95% CI: 0.0002–0.0004), 0.0002 (95% CI: 0.0001–0.0004), 0.0001 (95% CI: 0.0000–0.0002), 0.0002 (95% CI: 0.0000–0.0003), and 0.0001 (95% CI: 0.0000–0.0002), respectively, accounting for 5.06%, 4.14%, 1.35%, 2.85%, and 1.74%, respectively, of the total effect. Importantly, while these models suggest potential indirect associations via dietary inflammation (e.g., DII mediating 5.06% of the total effect), they should be interpreted descriptively, as reverse causation or confounding is more plausible than true mediation.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness and generalizability of our findings within the NHANES dataset, we conducted a split-sample validation. The full cohort was divided into two subsets based on survey cycles: a derivation set (1999–2008 cycles; n = 6,312) and a validation set (2010–2023 cycles; n = 5,430). The UHR-frailty association remained consistent (derivation OR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.06–1.09; validation OR: 1.06, 95% CI: 1.05–1.08, P < 0.001), as did mortality outcomes (ACM: derivation HR: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.03; validation HR: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.04, P < 0.001 and CVM: derivation HR: 1.03, 95% CI: 1.01–1.05; validation HR: 1.05, 95% CI: 1.02–1.08, P < 0.001) in frail participants. More detailed results are provided in Supplemental Table S6.

Discussion

Frailty, which impacts more than 10% of people aged 60 years and older, is associated with heightened risks of disability, hospitalization, and death, making it a significant public health concern for aging populations [27, 28]. This scenario highlights the pressing necessity for creating effective intervention strategies [29]. Research shows that frailty is strongly related to the emergence of multiple chronic illnesses, such as cardiovascular conditions, diabetes, and cognitive impairments [30–32]. Furthermore, frailty is an independent risk factor, contributing to elevated mortality rates among older individuals. In this nationally representative cohort study utilizing NHANES data from 1999 to 2023, we demonstrated that elevated serum UHR independently predicts higher frailty prevalence and mortality risks among U.S. adults aged 60 years or older, with dietary patterns partially mediating this association. These findings underscore UHR as a novel, accessible biomarker integrating oxidative stress and metabolic dysregulation, offering potential for early frailty risk stratification and intervention in aging populations.

Serum UA, the end-product of purine metabolism, has been implicated in frailty through its dual role in oxidative stress and inflammation. On the one hand, serum UA exhibits antioxidant properties, particularly in extracellular environments, where it scavenges free radicals and protects against oxidative damage, a key contributor to aging and frailty [33]. Studies have shown that higher SUA levels are associated with increased handgrip strength, a critical marker of muscle function and sarcopenia, particularly in older women [34]. This protective effect is thought to be mediated by serum UA’s ability to counteract oxidative stress, which is a major driver of muscle wasting and functional decline in aging [35]. Conversely, elevated Serum UA levels have also been linked to chronic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, which are hallmarks of frailty. For instance, higher UA levels have been associated with increased levels of inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), both of which are elevated in frail individuals [36]. Additionally, serum UA has been shown to inhibit vitamin D production, leading to secondary hyperparathyroidism and exacerbating bone loss, a common feature of frailty [37]. Thus, the relationship between UA and frailty appears to be context-dependent, with both protective and detrimental effects observed.

HDL-C, often referred to as “good cholesterol,” plays a critical role in lipid metabolism and cardiovascular health. Lower HDL-C levels have been consistently associated with frailty, likely due to their role in modulating inflammation and oxidative stress [38]. HDL-C exerts anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and promoting the efflux of cholesterol from macrophages, thereby reducing the formation of foam cells in atherosclerotic plaques [39]. In older adults, low HDL-C levels have been linked to increased frailty risk, as evidenced by studies showing that frail individuals exhibit significantly lower HDL-C levels compared to their non-frail counterparts [40]. Furthermore, HDL-C has been shown to protect against endothelial dysfunction, a key contributor to arterial stiffness and reduced physical function in frailty [41]. The protective role of HDL-C is further supported by its association with better physical performance, including grip strength and walking speed, both of which are critical components of the frailty phenotype [42].

The interplay between serum UA and HDL-C in frailty is complex and likely mediated by their shared involvement in metabolic and inflammatory pathways. Elevated serum UA levels have been associated with reduced HDL-C levels, possibly due to serum UA’s pro-inflammatory effects on lipid metabolism [43]. For example, studies have shown that individuals with higher serum UA levels exhibit lower HDL-C levels, which may exacerbate frailty risk by promoting systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction [44]. Conversely, higher HDL-C levels may mitigate the detrimental effects of serum UA by enhancing antioxidant capacity and reducing inflammation. This bidirectional relationship suggests that interventions targeting both serum UA and HDL-C levels could be effective in preventing or mitigating frailty. For instance, lifestyle modifications such as dietary changes and physical activity have been shown to improve HDL-C levels and reduce serum UA levels, thereby potentially reducing frailty risk [45]. This is consistent with our mediation analysis results, which showed that different dietary patterns affected the relationship between UHR and frailty to varying degrees.

As an integrated index of integrating UA and HDL-C, the UHR has emerged as a promising biomarker reflecting the interplay of inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic dysregulation across various health conditions. Prior studies on UHR provide a robust foundation for clinical application. For instance, Bao et al. conducted a cross-sectional analysis of NHANES data from 2011 to 2018, linking elevated UHR to increased COPD risk, characterized by linear associations and CRP mediation (18.36%), akin to our linear UHR-frailty relationship yet divergent from our U-shaped mortality pattern [46]; their observation of stronger effects in females parallels our findings, although CRP mediation differs from our emphasis on dietary influences. Similarly, Ao et al. identified nonlinear UHR-osteoarthritis (OA) associations, with more pronounced effects in females and older adults, consistent with our female frailty bias but exhibiting an inverse directionality—protective at low UHR in OA versus detrimental at high UHR in frailty—likely attributable to OA’s joint-specific inflammatory processes contrasting frailty’s systemic vulnerabilities [47]. Wang et al. reported J-shaped nonlinear UHR-obesity relationships in elderly populations, modulated by body mass index, yet differing from our linear UHR-frailty association, indicating that obesity’s metabolic convergence with frailty may potentiate UHR effects [48]. Nian et al., in a 12-week randomized controlled trial, demonstrated that fish oil supplementation reduced UHR and insulin resistance, suggesting potential interventional modulation not explored in our observational design and underscoring opportunities for frailty prevention through n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids targeting UHR [49]. Chen et al. established a linear UHR-chronic kidney disease (CKD) association in Chinese patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), modified by BMI, aligning with our linear UHR-frailty linkage [50]. Yan et al. observed UHR as a predictor of peripheral arterial disease in hypertensive individuals, with sex-specific differences comparable to ours [51]. Kong et al. associated UHR with infertility, exhibiting stronger effects in individuals younger than 35 years, diverging from our focus on elderly cohorts but sharing metabolic mediators such as oxidative stress, and further revealing that elevated UHR correlates with heightened infertility risk, thereby offering insights beneficial for developing non-pharmacological interventions to enhance fertility outcomes [52]. Lu et al. and Li et al. documented nonlinear UHR-abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) associations, with inflammatory mechanisms that overlap frailty’s oxidative stress pathways [53, 54]. Li et al. identified J-shaped UHR-eGFR relationships in hypertensives, correlated with CRP [55]. Wang et al. established UHR as an independent risk factor for diabetic retinopathy (DR) in T2DM, serving as a readily accessible indicator integrating metabolic and inflammatory status for early DR detection [56]. Liu et al. linked UHR to atrial fibrillation in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease patients, sharing metabolic connections but lacking nonlinearity [57]. Xu et al. defined a UHR-AAA cutoff of 7.78 for the first time in Chinese subjects [14]. Zhou et al. reported nonlinear UHR-IR associations, akin to our sex- and age-specific effects despite differing populations [58]. Yu et al. associated UHR with metabolic syndrome in non-diabetic men, exhibiting male-specific patterns that contrast our female frailty predominance [59].

Additionally, as a composite metric, UHR facilitates a holistic evaluation of systemic oxidative stress. This biomarker, routinely assessed in clinical settings, offers practical benefits for translational applications, including ease of measurement, cost-effectiveness, and high reproducibility. Our results reveal a robust, dose-dependent positive association between UHR and frailty risk, with each unit increase correlating to a 7% higher odds in fully adjusted models, persisting with a linear relationship. This aligns with UHR’s role as a composite indicator of systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, mechanisms central to frailty pathogenesis [7, 60]. Elevated UA promotes pro-oxidant effects via xanthine oxidase activation, exacerbating endothelial dysfunction and muscle wasting [46, 61–63], while diminished HDL-C impairs antioxidant defenses, amplifying vulnerability to physiological deficits [64, 65]. The observed threshold effects for ACM—negative associations below 9.07 and positive above—suggest a U-shaped risk profile, consistent with prior studies in diabetic populations [17]. This biphasic pattern likely arises from distinct pathophysiological mechanisms at the low and high ends of the UHR spectrum. At low UHR values, reduced serum UA levels may diminish extracellular antioxidant capacity, as UA serves as a major scavenger of reactive oxygen species, protecting against oxidative damage in aging tissues [33, 66]. Concurrently, elevated HDL-C, while generally protective, may become dysfunctional in frail elderly individuals due to oxidative modifications (e.g., oxidation of its apoA-I component), impairing its anti-inflammatory and cholesterol efflux functions and paradoxically promoting endothelial dysfunction and atherosclerosis [38, 67]. This “dysfunctional HDL” phenotype, prevalent in chronic inflammatory states like frailty, could unmask underlying vulnerabilities, contributing to heightened mortality risk below the threshold. In contrast, at high UHR levels (>9.07 for ACM; >9.54 for CVM), excessive UA overwhelms antioxidant systems, shifting to a pro-oxidant role via xanthine oxidase activation, which generates ROS and amplifies endothelial dysfunction, muscle wasting, and cardiorenal toxicity [8, 61–63]. Low HDL-C further exacerbates this by reducing reverse cholesterol transport and anti-inflammatory signaling, culminating in a synergistic inflammatory cascade that drives all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [64, 65]. These opposing dynamics underscore UHR’s value as a nuanced biomarker, where optimal ranges may confer resilience, while deviations signal targeted therapeutic opportunities (e.g., UA-lowering agents for high UHR or HDL-functionalizing interventions for low UHR). Nevertheless, a significant positive association between UHR and CVM was observed only above 9.49, highlighting a potentially higher threshold for cardiovascular-specific endpoints. Previous studies have demonstrated that serum UHR is associated with ACM and CVM in patients with COVID-19, diabetes/prediabetes, acute myocardial infarction, and peritoneal dialysis, exhibiting a J-shaped nonlinear relationship in diabetes/prediabetes and good predictive value [46, 68–70]. Subgroup analyses further indicate sex-specific modulation, with stronger frailty links in females, possibly due to estrogen-mediated UA metabolism differences post-menopause and lifestyle interactions like alcohol enhancing associations through additive oxidative burden. Mediation analysis highlights dietary patterns as partial conduits, with pro-inflammatory diets (higher DII) mediating about 5.06% of the effect, implying that nutrient imbalances exacerbate UHR-frailty links via amplified inflammation, though direct effects predominate.

UHR’s role as a composite biomarker of inflammation and oxidative stress suggests potential as an intervention target. UA-lowering therapies like allopurinol, which inhibits xanthine oxidase, have reduced UA levels and cardiovascular risk in trials [71]. Lipid-modulating therapies, including statins or dietary interventions (e.g., Mediterranean diet, fish oil), have been shown to elevate HDL-C and mitigate inflammation [72]. Our mediation analysis indicates dietary patterns influence UHR-frailty links (1.35–5.06% mediation), supporting anti-inflammatory diets as adjunctive strategies. Randomized trials targeting UHR (e.g., allopurinol in high-UHR frail elders) are warranted to test causality and therapeutic efficacy.

This study is the first to establish UHR as a robust predictor of frailty prevalence and mortality in elderly U.S. adults, offering a unique contribution to frailty research by integrating oxidative stress and lipid metabolism into a single, accessible biomarker. Compared to established frailty biomarkers like CRP and IL-6, which primarily reflect systemic inflammation and are elevated in frail individuals [34, 40], UHR provides a composite metric capturing both inflammatory and metabolic dysregulation, potentially offering incremental predictive value. For instance, while CRP and IL-6 are sensitive to acute inflammatory states, UHR’s inclusion of HDL-C reflects chronic lipid-related vulnerabilities, which are critical in frailty’s multifactorial pathogenesis [38]. Our findings suggest UHR’s particular utility in subgroups with metabolic syndrome, where elevated UA and low HDL-C converge to amplify risk (OR = 2.05 for frailty in T3 vs. T1), aligning with prior studies linking UHR to metabolic disorders [16, 59]. Unlike CRP or IL-6, which require specialized assays, UHR leverages routinely measured serum UA and HDL-C, enhancing its feasibility for clinical risk stratification. Future studies should validate UHR’s incremental value over CRP/IL-6 in predictive models and explore its specificity in metabolic syndrome or other high-risk subgroups to refine its role in personalized geriatric care.

Our findings advance theoretical understanding by positioning UHR as a modifiable nexus of metabolic-inflammation pathways in aging, with clinical implications for routine screening in geriatric care to guide antioxidant or lipid-modulating therapies. Potential applications include integrating UHR into frailty prediction models, enhancing precision over isolated markers, and informing dietary interventions targeting anti-inflammatory patterns to attenuate 1–5% of risks. Future research should validate causality via randomized trials (e.g., UA-lowering agents like allopurinol in high-UHR frail elders) and explore genetic modifiers (e.g., SLC2A9 polymorphisms) in multi-omics frameworks to refine thresholds across diverse populations.

Strengths and limitations

This study leverages a large, nationally representative sample from the NHANES database spanning 1999–2023, encompassing 11,751 elderly U.S. adults, which enhances generalizability to the U.S. population and provides robust statistical power for detecting associations between UHR and frailty/mortality outcomes. The incorporation of weighted multivariable logistic and Cox regression models, restricted cubic spline analyses, threshold effect evaluations, stratified subgroup analyses, and mediation analyses offered a comprehensive methodological framework, allowing for nuanced exploration of linear and nonlinear relationships while adjusting for a wide array of confounders, including demographics, lifestyle factors, and metabolic markers. Notably, this is the first investigation to examine UHR’s association with frailty prevalence and mortality in an elderly cohort. The inclusion of diverse dietary patterns (e.g., MED, DASHI, HEI-2020, PHDI-US, DII) as mediators represents a novel extension, revealing partial mediation effects (1.35–5.06%) and highlighting modifiable lifestyle factors. Long-term follow-up (median 5.92 years) strengthens the assessment of mortality endpoints, while sensitivity analyses (e.g., interaction tests) affirm the robustness of findings across subgroups, positioning UHR as a practical, cost-effective biomarker for risk stratification in geriatric care.

As a retrospective cohort study relying on NHANES data, causality between UHR and frailty/mortality cannot be definitively established, and residual confounding from unmeasured variables (e.g., genetic factors like SLC2A9 polymorphisms or detailed medication histories) may persist despite extensive adjustments. Dietary patterns were assessed via 24-hour recalls, which are susceptible to recall bias and may not fully capture long-term habits, potentially underestimating mediation effects. Our analysis did not adjust for kidney function (e.g., eGFR), lipid-lowering drugs (statins), diuretics, urate-lowering therapy (allopurinol), or systemic inflammation (CRP) in the main models due to significant missing data in NHANES. This could introduce residual confounding, as these factors influence UA and HDL-C levels. Future studies with more complete data are needed to fully account for these confounders. Frailty was defined using a deficit accumulation index with a threshold of 0.25, which, while consistent with prior studies, may differ from alternative assessments like the Fried phenotype, limiting direct comparability. While the 49-item FI offers a comprehensive assessment suited to NHANES data and our systemic biomarker focus, alternative definitions like the Fried phenotype may yield lower prevalence estimates and emphasize physical aspects. Future studies could explore UHR associations across frailty tools, though our findings align with the FI’s established prognostic value in similar cohorts. Our findings are based on NHANES, limiting generalizability to non-U.S. populations with different ethnic or lifestyle profiles. While split-sample validation within NHANES confirmed consistency, external validation in real-world cohorts (e.g., European or Asian populations) is needed to enhance generalizability. Finally, the observational design precludes evaluation of UHR-targeted interventions, necessitating future randomized trials to validate causality and therapeutic implications.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this comprehensive analysis of NHANES data from 1999 to 2023 demonstrates that elevated serum UHR serves as a robust, independent predictor of frailty prevalence and mortality risks among elderly U.S. adults, with dietary patterns, particularly pro-inflammatory diets, partially mediating the UHR-frailty link. As a simple, cost-effective, and routinely measurable biomarker, UHR holds significant promise for early risk stratification and targeted interventions in geriatric populations. Our insights emphasize the importance of monitoring UHR in clinical practice to inform preventive strategies, such as anti-inflammatory dietary modifications or lipid-modulating therapies. Future prospective studies and randomized controlled trials are warranted to establish causality, explore genetic modifiers, and validate UHR’s utility in diverse global cohorts for frailty prevention and improved longevity.
