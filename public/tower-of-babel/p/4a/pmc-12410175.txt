Deep learning-based prediction of axillary pathological complete response in patients with breast cancer using longitudinal multiregional ultrasound
Authors: Yu Liu, Ying Wang, Jiaxin Huang, Shufang Pei, Yuxiang Wang, Yanfen Cui, Lifen Yan, Mengxia Yao, Yumeng Wang, Zejun Zhu, Chunwang Huang, Zaiyi Liu, Changhong Liang, Jiayao Shi, Zhenhui Li, Xiaoqing Pei, Lei Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundNoninvasive biomarkers that capture the longitudinal multiregional tumour burden in patients with breast cancer may improve the assessment of residual nodal disease and guide axillary surgery. Additionally, a significant barrier to the clinical translation of the current data-driven deep learning model is the lack of interpretability. This study aims to develop and validate an information shared-private (iShape) model to predict axillary pathological complete response in patients with axillary lymph node (ALN)-positive breast cancer receiving neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) by learning common and specific image representations from longitudinal primary tumour and ALN ultrasound images.MethodsA total of 1135 patients with biopsy-proven ALN-positive breast cancer who received NAT were included in this multicentre, retrospective study. The iShape was trained on a dataset of 371 patients and validated on three external validation sets (EVS1-3), with 295, 244, and 225 patients, respectively. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). The false-negative rates (FNRs) of iShape alone and in combination with sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) were also evaluated. Imaging feature visualisation and RNA sequencing analysis were performed to explore the underlying basis of iShape.FindingsThe iShape achieved AUCs of 0.950–0.971 for EVS 1–3, which were better than those of the clinical model and the image signatures derived from the primary tumour, longitudinal primary tumour, or ALN (P < 0.05, as per the DeLong test). The performance of iShape remained satisfactory in subgroup analyses stratified by age, menstrual status, T stage, molecular subtype, treatment regimens, and machine type (AUCs of 0.812–1.000). More importantly, the FNR of iShape was 7.7%–8.1% in the EVSs, and the FNR of SLNB decreased from 13.4% to 3.6% with the aid of iShape in patients receiving SLNB and ALN dissection. The decision-making process of iShape was explained by feature visualisation. Additionally, RNA sequencing analysis revealed that a lower deep learning score was associated with immune infiltration and tumour proliferation pathways.InterpretationThe iShape model demonstrated good performance for the precise quantification of ALN status in patients with ALN-positive breast cancer receiving NAT, potentially benefiting individualised decision-making, and avoiding unnecessary axillary lymph node dissection.FundingThis study was supported by (1) Noncommunicable Chronic Diseases-10.13039/501100018537National Science and Technology Major Project (No. 2024ZD0531100); (2) 10.13039/501100015956Key-Area Research and Development Program of Guangdong Province (No. 2021B0101420006); (3) 10.13039/501100001809National Natural Science Foundation of China (No. 82472051, 82471947, 82271941, 82272088); (4) National Science Foundation for Young Scientists of China (No. 82402270, 82202095, 82302190); (5) 10.13039/501100010256Guangzhou Municipal Science and Technology Planning Project (No. 2025A04J4773, 2025A04J4774); (6) the 10.13039/501100003453Natural Science Foundation of Guangdong Province of China (No. 2025A1515011607); (7) 10.13039/501100003785Medical Scientific Research Foundation of Guangdong Province of China (No. A2024403); (8) Guangdong Provincial Key Laboratory of Artificial Intelligence in Medical Image Analysis and Application (No. 2022B1212010011); (9) Outstanding Youth Science Foundation of Yunnan Basic Research Project (No. 202401AY070001-316); (10) Innovative Research Team of Yunnan Province (No. 202505AS350013).

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed for publications without date or language restrictions, with the terms (“deep learning” OR “radiomics” OR “artificial intelligence” OR “AI”) AND (“breast cancer” OR “breast tumour” OR “axillary lymph node” OR “ALN”) AND (“neoadjuvant chemotherapy”) AND (“axillary pathological complete response” OR “axillary pCR” OR “axillary response”). Only four artificial intelligence-based studies utilising ultrasound images for predicting axillary response have been published. However, these studies did not consider the multiregional tumour burden from the primary tumour or axillary lymph nodes and lacked interpretability. Thus, the clinical application of these models remains limited.

Added value of this study

In this multicentre study, we developed and validated an information shared-private (iShape) model to predict ALN pCR in patients with ALN-positive breast cancer undergoing NAT, by learning common and specific image representations from longitudinal primary tumour and ALN ultrasound images. The performance of iShape was superior to that of the clinical model and the image signature derived from the primary tumour, longitudinal primary tumour or axillary lymph node. The decision-making process of iShape was explained by imaging feature visualisation. Additionally, RNA-sequencing analysis revealed that a lower deep learning score was associated with immune infiltration and the tumour proliferation pathway.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings showed that the proposed iShape model has potential value for predicting ALN pCR and guiding axillary surgery, thereby avoiding unnecessary ALND. Furthermore, the decision-making process of iShape was explained through imaging visualisation and RNA sequencing analysis, which enhanced the reliability of our model's decision.

Introduction

Axillary lymph node (ALN) metastasis occurs in 34%–50.3% of patients with breast cancer.1, 2, 3 Neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) can eradicate nodal metastasis in 37%–75% of patients with breast cancer.4, 5, 6 Patients who achieve ALN pathological complete response (pCR) can be exempt from axillary lymph node dissection (ALND)7 and have improved long-term survival.8 However, ALND has remained a routine surgical procedure for clinical ALN-positive patients who have undergone NAT in recent years.6 Patients with ALN pCR are exposed to unnecessary sequelae of ALND, such as lymphoedema, numbness, axillary web syndrome, and upper-extremity range of motion loss.9 In this context, accurate prediction of ALN pCR and identification of patients who can be exempt from ALND are critically important.

Recently, sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) has emerged as the preferred method for the evaluation of ALN status in patients with breast cancer receiving NAT. However, large prospective trials have shown that the overall false-negative rates (FNRs) of SLNB are 12.6%–14.2%,10, 11, 12 exceeding the clinically accepted cut-off of 10%.5,13In addition, the frozen sectioning of SLNBs takes a long time, leading to an unavoidable extension of the surgical procedure. Therefore, a reliable and efficient diagnostic approach is urgently needed for the assessment of ALN status.

Image-based biomarkers, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT), and ultrasound, have been widely used in clinical decision-making.6 Compared with MRI and PET/CT, ultrasound has outstanding advantages in terms of reproducibility, convenience, and easy accessibility.14,15 Unfortunately, its predictive performance is limited by high false-negative rates.16

Deep learning models have been proposed for predicting ALN response in patients with breast cancer.17,18 However, there are several barriers to the clinical application of the ALN pCR prediction model. First, positive ALNs are caused by metastatic cancer cells from the primary tumour, and these involved ALNs may share both common and specific phenotypic traits with the primary tumour.19 For example, the levels of oestrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) in metastatic ALNs may differ from those in primary tumours.20,21 Additionally, breast tumour and ALN images contain vast amounts of information, but only a small portion is related to treatment response, which can be evident from heatmap visualisation.22,23 Previous studies have simply fused information from breast tumours and ALNs, ignoring their common and specific discriminative features, resulting in data redundancy and thus decreasing generalisation performance. Second, interpretability is crucial for the clinical application of artificial intelligence. Currently, deep learning models are predominantly data-driven, and lack the underlying biological basis for analysing the prediction mechanism, presenting a significant hurdle for clinical translation. Third, most previous models have been developed solely on the basis of the characteristics of the primary tumour at a single time point. NAT is a dynamic process, and taking the longitudinal changes in tumours induced by NAT into account may improve the prediction performance.22,24

In this study, we developed and validated an information shared-private (iShape) model to predict ALN pCR in patients with ALN-positive breast cancer undergoing NAT by learning common and specific image representations from longitudinal primary tumour and ALN ultrasound images. In addition, we provide an explanation for iShape by visualising key areas in the heatmap and investigating the biological basis of the model's prediction. This noninvasive approach may facilitate individualised axillary surgery decision-making and even eliminate surgery for ALN pCR patients.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This multicentre, retrospective study was approved by the ethics committee of each participating hospital (KY2023-956-03, ZE2023-350-01, KYLX2024-262, 2022-373-X01, and IIT-2023-163L) and was in compliance with the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) standard guidelines specific to artificial intelligence.25 All patient-related information was anonymised, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

On the basis of 95% statistical power, a two-sided significance level of 0.05, an alternative hypothesis AUC of 0.800 (vs. null AUC of 0.500), and a 1:1 allocation ratio between the pCR and non-pCR groups, a minimum of 42 patients (21 pCR and 21 non-pCR) was required for each cohort.

From May 27, 2014 to October 25, 2023, a total of 1135 patients with biopsy-proven ALN-positive breast cancer treated with NATs from five tertiary academic Chinese hospitals in different regions of China were enrolled. Patients from Guangdong Provincial People's Hospital (GPPH, 306 patients) and Shanxi Cancer Hospital (SCH, 65 patients) were used as the training set (TS, 371 patients). Patients from Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Centre (SYSUCC, 295 patients), Guangdong Province Traditional Chinese Medical Hospital (GPTCM, 244 patients), and Yunnan Cancer Hospital (YNCH, 225 patients) were used as independent external validation sets (EVSs, named EVS 1–3, respectively) (Supplementary Figure S1). Most patients received six or more cycles of taxane-based, anthracycline-based, or anthracycline and taxane-based neoadjuvant therapy protocols according to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN)26 and China Anti-Cancer Association breast cancer guidelines.27 For HER2+ patients, an additional prescription of trastuzumab (8 mg/kg loading dose, 6 mg/kg maintenance dose) was given. Some of the hormone receptor (HR)+/HER2− patients received exclusive neoadjuvant endocrine therapy at the same time according to the recommendation.

Another 24 ALN-positive patients from GPPH underwent RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to explore the potential biological mechanisms of iShape. Fresh tumour samples were obtained using ultrasound-guided core-needle biopsy. RNA extraction, mRNA purification, ctDNA library qualification, and sequencing were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions using commercial kits and equipment from reputable suppliers. After sequencing, the quality of the generated cDNA libraries was assessed using a Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit and a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer. The Illumina platform is generally utilised for generating 150 bp paired-end reads. After sequencing, considerable raw data are obtained and require processing and interpretation through bioinformatics analysis. Initially, FastQC software was employed to perform quality control on the sequencing data, evaluating the quality and distribution of bases within the reads. The HISAT2 software was subsequently used to align the reads to a reference genome to determine the genomic location of each read. The feature counts tool was then utilised to quantify gene expression levels within the aligned reads, aiding in the construction of a read-count matrix. This matrix encompasses the gene expression levels in the samples, providing essential data for subsequent differential expression analysis and functional annotation.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) had biopsy-proven ALN-positive breast cancer treated with NAT; (2) underwent surgical procedures within 1 month after NAT; and (3) had complete clinicopathological data. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) lack of pre- and post-NAT primary tumour or pre-NAT axillary lymph node ultrasound images; (2) prior breast cancer; (3) poor quality of the ultrasound images (e.g., motion, speed propagation or refraction artefacts or low image resolution); and (4) a unilateral multifocal primary tumour or a tumour in the ultrasound image that was inconsistent with the surgical pathology.

Ultrasound images and clinicopathological data acquisition

The ultrasound examinations were performed by sonographers with over 10 years of experience in breast ultrasound diagnosis. Ultrasound examinations were performed before and after NAT using fourteen different devices (Supplementary Table S1). Longitudinal multiregional ultrasound images, including the largest cross-sections of the pre- and posttreatment primary tumour and pretreatment malignant axillary lymph node proven by biopsy (denoted as pre-pt, post-pt, and pre-aln, respectively) were acquired (Fig. 1). Clinicopathological information, including age at the initial diagnosis, initial clinical T stage, the status of ER, PR, HER2, Ki-67, and treatment regimens, was collected from the electronic medical records system for further analysis. The definitions of ER, PR, HER2, and Ki-67 were determined by immunohistochemistry (IHC) or fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH). Patients with a Ki-67 proliferation index less than 30% were classified as having low proliferation, and high proliferation otherwise.26 The ER and PR statuses were regarded as positive if the tumour showed at least 1% positive cells via nuclear staining. For HER2 status, IHC results scored as 3+ were defined as HER2+ and IHC results scored as 0 or 1+ were defined as HER2−. In the case of a 2+ IHC score, fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) was used to confirm HER2 amplification. Image processing

Custom software (https://github.com/WULei-MedIA/Axillary-pCR-Prediction) was used to remove text, icons, and other nonbreast tumour tissue information from the images. In this study, images were normalised separately using Z-scores to obtain a standard normal distribution of image intensities. During deep learning training, images were augmented using contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalisation, horizontal flipping, rotation, random contrast, and brightness adjustments to help the model adapt to varying image appearances from different machines (Supplementary Data). Finally, the images were resized to 224 × 224 and normalised to meet the model's input requirements. If no visible tumour was present after NAT, the region was cropped on the basis of the corresponding tumour bed fibrosis. The region of interest included the tumour and its surrounding tissues.

iShape model development and training

We proposed an information shared-private (iShape) model to learn image-common and image-specific representations from longitudinal multiregional ultrasound images. The pre-pt, post-pt, and pre-aln images were used as inputs for iShape, and the outputs were axillary treatment response predictions. The iShape includes the following components: 1) feature extraction, which uses transformers for feature extraction; 2) feature projection, which projects features from different images into the same space to reduce interimage differences; 3) feature encoding, which identifies image-common and image-specific features across different images; and 4) feature fusion and treatment response prediction, which implement feature fusion through multihead attention mechanisms and predict treatment response via fully connected layers (Supplementary Figure S2).

We designed four loss functions for model training. The first is the central moment discrepancy (CMD) loss, which reduces the differences between the common representations of different images. The second is cosine distance loss, which ensures that the model captures the private information from each image. The third is the feature reconstruction loss, implemented using the mean squared error, which ensures effective representation after passing through the image-common and image-specific modules. The final loss function is binary cross-entropy loss for classification. Additionally, we employed data augmentation (including horizontal flipping, brightness adjustment within the range of 0.4–0.8, rotation, scaling, and cropping), dropout, and regularisation techniques to minimise the risk of overfitting. We conducted training on the TensorFlow platform using the Adam optimiser with a learning rate of 0.0001 on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. Detailed descriptions of the model are provided in the Supplementary Data.

Image- and biological pathway-level interpretability of iShape

To better understand the decision-making basis of the model, we employed feature visualisation and RNA-seq analysis to enhance the interpretability of iShape at both the images and biological pathway levels. First, the image-common and image-specific representations from longitudinal multiregional ultrasound of all patients were embedded into a two-dimensional feature space and then plotted using the t-Stochastic Neighbour Embedding (t-SNE) algorithm to understand the different distributions of features. Then, gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) was used to visualise the key region of iShape for ALN prediction. Finally, RNA-seq analysis was conducted on 24 patients with ALN-positive breast cancer to explore the underlying biological mechanisms of iShape. We processed longitudinal multiregional ultrasound images to calculate the score for each patient and divided them into high- and low-score groups on the basis of the cutoff value of ALN pCR predicted by iShape in the training set. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between the groups with an absolute log2 fold change (log2FC) > 1 and a false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P value < 0.05 using the R package DESeq2. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (“clusterProfiler” package in R) was employed to reveal the heterogeneity in genetic pathways. We further utilised CIBERSORTx (cibersortx.stanford.edu) to analyse the differences in the infiltration of immune cells in microenvironments between the two groups. Additionally, drug sensitivity (cgp2014 database) analysis was also performed using the “pRRophetic” R package, and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of each drug was calculated. Differences in anthracyclines-based, taxane-based, lapatinib, and pazopanib use between the two groups were compared.

Integration with clinicopathological characteristics

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to construct a clinical model. We then combined iShape and clinical model to generate a fusion model to evaluate the efficiency of the ultrasound imaging marker comprehensively.

Ablation analysis for iShape

To demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach, we performed ablation experiments and compared the performance of the models trained with pre-pt, post-pt or pre-aln images (named Single-regionNet; Supplementary Figure S3), longitudinal primary tumours (pre-pt and post-pt images; named LongitudinalNet; Supplementary Figure S4), and pre-pt, post-pt and pre-aln images (named Multi-regionNet; Supplementary Figure S5). To illustrate the significance of capturing the common and specific information from the primary tumour and ALN, we trained a model (called NocsNet; Supplementary Figure S6), which removed the image-common and image-specific feature encodings, using longitudinal multiregional ultrasound images. Furthermore, we conducted several comparative studies by benchmarking it against other previously published artificial intelligence methods, including ResNet5027 (Supplementary Figure S7) and DenseNet12128 (Supplementary Figure S7), for ALN pCR prediction.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed with R software (version 3.6.4 Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and SPSS 23.0 for MacOS (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics are summarised as the means ± SDs or medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Comparisons between groups were performed using Fisher's exact test or the chi-square test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whiney U test or t test for continuous variables, as appropriate. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were used to assess the model's performance in predicting ALN pCR. Differences between various AUCs were compared using the DeLong test. The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for these performance metrics were calculated by bootstrapping. The FNR was calculated as the number of false-negative events divided by the total number of patients with residual disease (SLNB, ALND, or both). For SLNB alone, a false-negative event was defined as patients with negative sentinel nodes who had residual disease in ALND. For iShape alone, a false-negative event was defined as a patient with a negative iShape result who had residual disease in either SLNB or ALND, or both. For the combined iShape and SLNB, a false-negative event was defined as a patient with a negative iShape and SLNB who had residual disease in ALND. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to identify the difference between the iShape score and immune infiltration. All the statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value of less than 0.05 indicated significance.

Clinical variables associated with ALN pCR

Univariable analysis revealed that ALN pCR was correlated with ER, PR, HER2, molecular subtypes, and NAT regimens in the TS (Table 1). Multivariable regression analysis demonstrated that PR, HER2, and NAT regimens were independent predictors of ALN pCR. These factors were used to construct a clinical model as a baseline to compare with the performance of iShape.

Performance of the models

The iShape model achieved high classification performance across the three EVSs, with AUCs of 0.971 (95% CI: 0.955, 0.987), 0.950 (95% CI: 0.923, 0.976), and 0.953 (95% CI: 0.928, 0.978) for EVS 1–3, respectively (Table 2, Fig. 2a, Supplementary Table S2). Other classification metrics (accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, NPV, and PPV) are presented in Supplementary Table S2. Across all sets, the iShape score was significantly greater in the pCR group than in the non-pCR group (Fig. 2b, Student's t test). The confusion matrix revealed strong agreement between the model predictions and actual treatment responses (Fig. 2c). The performance of iShape was significantly better than that of Single-regionNet (AUC of 0.759–0.871), Longitudinal-Net (AUC of 0.826–0.860), Multi-regionNet (AUC of 0.806–0.899), and the clinical model (AUC of 0.577–0.719) in the TS and EVSs (Table 2, Supplementary Table S3, Supplementary Figure S9). There was no significant improvement in the prediction of ALN pCR when iShape was combined with the clinical model in the EVSs (P > 0.05, DeLong test), except in EVS 2 (P = 0.004, DeLong test) (Table 2).

To illustrate the significance of capturing the common and specific private information from a primary tumour and ALN, we trained the NocsNet model with longitudinal multiregional ultrasound images. Unsurprisingly, iShape outperformed NocsNet (AUC: 0.954 vs. 0.868 in the TS, 0.971 vs. 0.890, 0.950 vs. 0.853, and 0.953 vs. 0.868 in EVS 1–3, respectively, all P < 0.001, DeLong test, Table 2, Supplementary Tables S2 and S3). Moreover, the performance of using the transformer as the backbone network for feature extraction in iShape is better than that of ResNet50 (AUCs of 0.801, 0.834, 0.812, and 0.856 in the TS and EVS 1–3, respectively, all P < 0.001, DeLong test, Table 2, Supplementary Table S3) and DenseNet121 (AUCs of 0.829, 0.830, 0.797 and 0.842 in the TS and EVS 1–3, respectively, all P < 0.001, DeLong test, Table 2, Supplementary Table S3).

Furthermore, we evaluated the clinical usefulness of iShape compared with that of Single-regionNet, LongitudinalNet, Multi-regionNet, NocsNet, ResNet50, and DenseNet121 for ALN pCR prediction via decision curve analysis, which revealed that iShape achieved greater net benefits (Supplementary Figure S10).

Surgical decision support of iShape

The proposed iShape successfully identified 93.1% (525 of 564) of ALN pCR patients and 89.5% (511 of 571) of ALN non-pCR patients. Thirty-nine patients with ALN pCR were subjected to unnecessary ALND. Sixty patients who were predicted as ALN pCR had residual ALN disease (Fig. 2c). The false-negative rates for iShape were 7.8%, 8.1%, and 7.7% for EVSs 1–3, respectively (Supplementary Table S2), which were less than the clinically required 10%.

To explore whether iShape could reduce the FNR for SLNB, we further analysed the FNR for SLNB alone and iShape combined with SLNB in patients receiving both SLNB and ALND. The overall false-negative rate for SLNB alone was 13.4%. In patients with fewer than three sentinel lymph nodes removed, the FNR for SLNB alone was 26.9%, whereas it decreased to 7.1% in patients with at least three sentinel lymph nodes removed. When iShape was combined with SLNB, the FNR decreased to 3.6% (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figure S11).Fig. 3The false-negative rates of SLNB with the assistance of iShape. Subgroup analysis

To evaluate model stability, we performed a subgroup analysis on the basis of clinicopathological characteristics. The performance of iShape within ER, PR, HER2, Ki67, and the four molecular subgroups in the TS and EVSs is shown in Supplementary Figures S12 and S13, with AUCs ranging from 0.939 to 0.985, 0.932 to 0.976, 0.946 to 0.974, 0.909 to 0.998, and 0.917 to 1.000, respectively. In the subgroup analysis of treatment regimens, patient age group, menstrual status, and T stage, iShape also achieved satisfactory performance, with AUCs ranging from 0.812 to 1.000, 0.881 to 0.992, 0.931 to 0.986, and 0.943 to 0.983 in the TS and EVSs, respectively (Supplementary Figures S13 and S14). Moreover, the machine type did not affect iShape effectiveness, with AUCSs ranging from 0.950 to 0.971 (Supplementary Figure S15).

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated an iShape model to predict ALN pCR in patients with breast cancer via longitudinal multiregional ultrasound (pre- and post-NAT primary tumour and pre-NAT axillary lymph node) via real-world data from five tertiary academic hospitals in China. The iShape model demonstrated satisfactory discrimination for ALN pCR prediction in three independent EVSs (AUCs: 0.950–0.971). The proposed model also showed robust classification in clinically meaningful subgroups as defined by age, menstrual status, T stage, molecular subtype, treatment regimens, and machine type. We further demonstrated that iShape had a FNR ranging between 7.7% and 8.1% in the EVSs, which was below the clinically accepted cut-off of 10%. More importantly, the iShape-assisted surgical strategy could further reduce the FNR for SLNB alone, thus serving as a potentially efficient tool to guide axillary surgical decisions in patients with breast cancer after NAT.

Given that the metastatic ALN is from the primary tumour, the ALN may have common and specific phenotypic traits with the primary tumour.20,21 A strength of this study is that we proposed an iShape model by learning shared and specific image representations from longitudinal primary tumours and ALN ultrasound to predict ALN pCR. The evidence can be obtained from Fig. 4a and b, and t-SNE visualisation clearly revealed overlapping common information among longitudinal primary tumours and ALNs, whereas specific information remained distinct. As expected, iShape outperforms NocsNet, a network that removes the common and specific information capture branches (AUCs: 0.971 vs. 0.890, 0.950 vs. 0.853, 0.953 vs. 0.868 in EVS 1–3, respectively). Previous studies have also predicted ALN response using artificial intelligence techniques based on ultrasound images, with AUCs ranging from 0.806 to 0.899,23,29,30 which had lower performance than our method. The reasons for this discrepancy are as follows. First, the involved ALNs may have distinct phenotypic characteristics and responses to NATs from primary tumours,31,32 and previous studies did not consider the biological phenotypes of ALNs. The combination of primary tumours and ALNs in the predictive model is quite significant.4,33 Second, NAT is a dynamic process, and some previous studies did not perform longitudinal analyses of treatment response. Our ablation experiments verified the above theories. The iShape model achieved an increased performance than the pre-pt, post-pt, pre-pt & post-pt, pre-aln, pre-pt or post-pt and pre-aln (AUC: 0.971 vs. 0.808 vs. 0.864 vs. 0.826 vs. 0.850 vs. 0.806 vs. 0.896 for EVS 1, 0.950 vs. 0.784 vs. 0.853 vs. 0.862 vs. 0.871 vs. 0.837 vs. 0.863 for EVS 2, 0.953 vs. 0.759 vs. 0.806 vs. 0.860 vs. 0.856 vs. 0.811 vs. 0.899 for EVS 3, respectively). Our approach could therefore potentially provide a new paradigm for processing longitudinal multiregion data in medical images. Wang et al. developed a serial radiomic model based on multitime and multiregional MRI analyses to predict ALN pCR, with an AUC of 0.88 in the validation set.19 However, their radiomic model extracted hand-crafted features that were poorly reproducible and time-consuming. In addition, the patients in their study were from a province in China, which may have led to a lack of sample diversity. Mao et al. proposed a multiregional MRI-based integrated system to predict ALN pCR with slightly lower efficiency than our study.34 This may be because they did not consider longitudinal treatment response. In addition, compared with MRI, ultrasound is more cost effective and easier to access.

The aim of our study was to identify more patients with ALN pCR to avoid unnecessary ALND and to ensure essential ALND for patients with non-ALN pCR. The proposed iShape model successfully identified 93.1% (525 of 564) of patients with ALN pCR and 89.5% (511 of 571) of patients with ALN non-pCR. A total of 585 patients were identified ALN pCR, and 60 patients experienced ALN metastasis. The false-negative rates for iShape were 7.8%, 8.1%, and 7.7% for EVSs 1–3, respectively, which are below the clinically required 10%.35 Thus, our proposed model could be an accurate and noninvasive tool for ALN pCR prediction.

Recently, SLNB has been used as an alternative surgical method for axillary staging after NAT in patients with clinically node-positive breast cancer.36 However, several large prospective studies have shown that the overall false-negative rate (FNR) of SLNB are 12.6%–14.2%,10, 11, 12 which exceeds the clinically accepted cut-off of 10%.5,13 Clinicians have often sought techniques to reduce the FNR for SLNBs, such as dual tracer staining, removing of more than three nodes, and placing a clip with I125 radioactive seeds and wires. However, the implementation of these invasive methods faced several challenges. First, the use of radioisotopes in double staining was limited because of the preparation and regulations of reagents for nuclear medicine and the lack of prospective studies with large samples. Second, the removal of more than three sentinel lymph nodes and identification of the clipped nodes often failed.5,11,37 We then assessed the efficacy of iShape-assisted axillary surgery in patients who underwent both SLNB and ALND. The FNR for SLNB alone was 13.4%. When iShape and SLNB are combined, the false-negative rate decreases to 3.6%, which strengthens our confidence in exempting ALND.

The main barrier to the clinical translation of the deep learning model is its inability to be interpreted. Understanding the predictive basis of a deep learning model and its potential biological mechanism can help clinicians better assist in clinical decision-making. Our image visualisation analysis revealed that the peri-tumour region and the peri-cortex of the ALN were associated with ALN non-pCR, and the intratumour region and the cortex of the ALN were associated with ALN pCR. This may be because changes in tumour echogenicity38 and ALN cortex features38 are correlated with ALN pCR. In contrast, the microenvironment in the peri-tumour region (lymphovascular invasion)39 and features in the peri-cortex of the ALN (extranodal extension)40 are associated with a poor response.

To move forwards, we performed a gene analysis of the RNA-seq data to reveal the underlying biological basis of the proposed model. We found that the deep learning scores were associated with immune infiltration and the tumour proliferation pathway, which in turn supported the finding that a low iShape score was associated with a greater likelihood of ALN non-pCR. This finding aligns with the results of the immune cell infiltration analysis: the low-score group presented increased numbers of M0 macrophages, activated mast cells, and neutrophils. These cells may promote tumour progression, growth, angiogenesis, and immune evasion,41, 42, 43 potentially hindering the achievement of pCR with NAT. Additionally, drug sensitivity highlights its potential in facilitating the development of selective antitumour drugs in the field of precision medicine, although further studies are needed.

Our study had several limitations. First, we did not perform longitudinal node-to-node evaluation in this study, because it is difficult to perform a one-to-one analysis of the ALN. Second, some of the patients in this study did not undergo ALND, and 4.8% (54/1135) underwent SLNB only. This could leave a possibility of undetected ALN metastasis in unexcised ALNs. However, all the patients had at least three sentinel lymph nodes removed. Third, this was a retrospective study, and large prospective validations of iShape are needed in the future.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that the iShape model, which incorporates longitudinal multiregion primary tumours and ALN ultrasound, has the potential to predict ALN pCR and guide axillary surgery, thus avoiding unnecessary ALND. Furthermore, the decision-making process of iShape was explained through imaging visualisation and RNA sequencing analysis, which bolstered the reliability of our model's decision. To enhance the predictive performance and generalisability ability for further clinical application, future studies should incorporate extensive data from various nations and ethnicities.
