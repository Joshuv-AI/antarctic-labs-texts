Arthroscopic Medial Meniscus Replacement Using a Peroneus Longus Autograft: Surgical Technique
Authors: Erdinç Genç, Emin Can Balcı, Okan Tezgel, Mehmet Kürşat Yılmaz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Medial meniscus deficiency is associated with progressive joint degeneration and impaired load distribution in the knee. While meniscus allograft transplantation is an established treatment for young and active patients, its accessibility is limited in many regions. This technical note describes a reproducible arthroscopic technique for medial meniscus replacement using an autograft harvested from the peroneus longus tendon. After subtotal meniscectomy, tibial tunnels are created at the native root attachment sites, and the graft is shaped and passed through the posterior tunnel. The graft is secured with an all-inside suture device and tensioned anteriorly. This method provides a biologically compatible and cost-effective alternative for meniscal reconstruction in patients without access to allograft tissue.

In recent years, preserving the native meniscus has become the cornerstone of treatment strategies for meniscal injuries, as it plays a critical role in maintaining normal knee biomechanics and reducing the likelihood of osteoarthritis. However, in cases involving complex, irreparable tears, chronic meniscal deficiency, or previous subtotal or total meniscectomy, replacement becomes necessary.1

Various replacement techniques have been developed for such scenarios, including meniscal allograft transplantation (MAT), collagen-based scaffolds, and synthetic implants. Yet, these solutions often face limitations—especially in low-resource settings—due to high costs, limited accessibility, and regulatory challenges.2 As an alternative, autologous tendon grafts have recently gained traction for meniscal substitution. Their natural biocompatibility, affordability, and potential to biologically integrate with host tissue make them particularly attractive. Tendons, such as the quadriceps, patellar, semitendinosus, and peroneus longus have all been studied, with the peroneus longus tendon showing promising biomechanical properties and favorable handling during surgery.3

Although prior research has largely focused on partial lateral meniscus reconstruction using autografts, there is still a lack of evidence regarding their use for medial meniscus replacement. This case report introduces a surgical technique in which a peroneus longus autograft is used to reconstruct the medial meniscus in a patient with a near-total medial meniscectomy.4 When traditional replacement options are unavailable or unsuitable, this method presents a viable and biologically integrative solution for restoring meniscal function—especially in environments where access to advanced technologies is limited.

Surgical Technique

Arthroscopic Preparation

The procedure is performed under general or regional anesthesia with the patient in a supine position. A standard arthroscopic assessment of the left knee is conducted to evaluate the remaining meniscal tissue and ensure the appropriateness of reconstruction. Medial meniscus loss is confirmed. If necessary, a subtotal meniscectomy is performed to remove residual fragments (Fig 1).Fig. 1Arthroscopic view of the left knee from the anterolateral portal showing a subtotal meniscectomy of the medial meniscus body in the supine position. The approximate length of the native meniscus is assessed using a ruler or calibrated probe. Following the diagnostic assessment, an extra anteromedial (far medial) portal is opened. Pie-crust of the medial collateral ligament is performed.

Graft Harvesting and Preparation

The graft preparation is done from the same leg, if there is no contraindication. A 2-3 cm longitudinal incision is made ∼2 cm above and posterior the lateral malleolus. Tenodesis is performed on the distal part of the peroneus longus, peroneus brevis. After the tenodesis, the peroneus longus tendon is identified, isolated, and harvested using a tendon stripper (Arthrex, Naples, FL).5

After harvesting, the tendon is placed on the preparation table, where each end is reinforced with nonabsorbable sutures, securing 2 to 3 cm of the graft to facilitate handling and fixation. The midpoint of the intra-articular portion of the graft is marked with 1 nonabsorbable suture. This suture will be used as a centralizing and traction suture. The graft diameter is measured with a sizer for tunnel placement.

Tunnel Preparation for Graft Roots

The root tunnels are created in a retrograde manner using the meniscus root guide, aiming to replicate the anatomic position as closely as possible. Guide pins are inserted through the aimers to establish the correct trajectory. The bone tunnels are then prepared using a standard drill and retrodrilling device (Smith and Nephew, Andover, MA), with the drill size corresponding to the graft diameter. Initially, a transtibial tunnel is created from the medial aspect of the tibia to the posterior root. Following this, the anterior root tunnel is formed using the same technique (Figs 2 and 3).Fig. 2Arthroscopic view of the left knee in the supine position through the anterolateral portal, demonstrating posterior root tunnel preparation on the tibial side. Graft Passage

The PL tendon graft is inserted through an accessory portal, and the folded end of the graft is pulled into the posterior root tunnel (Fig 4).Fig. 4Arthroscopic view of the left knee in the supine position through the anterolateral portal, showing the peroneus longus graft passed through the posterior root tunnel. This step demonstrates posterior fixation of the graft during medial meniscus replacement.

Arthroscopic view of the left knee in the supine position through the anterolateral portal, showing the peroneus longus graft passed through the posterior root tunnel. This step demonstrates posterior fixation of the graft during medial meniscus replacement.

The graft is then pushed in place along the capsular border using a blunt instrument. The sutures from the other graft end are retrieved and pulled down the anterior root tunnel. The traction suture is fixed. In order to adjust the tension appropriately, sutures are placed starting from the posterior meniscus and moving anterior; all inside (Smith and Nephew, Andover, MA), outside-in and inside-out (Fig 5).Fig. 5Arthroscopic view of the left knee in the supine position through the anterolateral portal, showing graft fixation to the capsule using an all-inside suture device. This step illustrates stabilization of the peroneus longus graft within the medial compartment.

Arthroscopic view of the left knee in the supine position through the anterolateral portal, showing graft fixation to the capsule using an all-inside suture device. This step illustrates stabilization of the peroneus longus graft within the medial compartment.

Fixation of the Posterolateral and Hamstring Grafts

After the meniscus is placed with the posterolateral (PL) graft, the fixation on the tibia is started. The anterior and posterior roots of the PL graft are fixed on the same or different suture buttons (Smith and Nephew, Andover, MA), according to the surgeon’s preference. Discussion

It is well established that medial meniscectomy, especially in young and active patients, significantly increases the risk of early-onset osteoarthritis due to the critical load-distributing function of the medial meniscus. Even after partial resection, biomechanical imbalance and progressive cartilage degeneration can be observed. To prevent post-meniscectomy syndrome and subsequent joint deterioration, various meniscus replacement techniques have been introduced. Among these, MAT is considered the gold standard. However, MAT has several limitations, including donor–recipient size mismatch, the need for bone block fixation, high cost, and limited accessibility in many regions. Furthermore, allografts can provoke immunologic reactions and carry potential legal and regulatory barriers in certain countries.2,6,7

Meniscal scaffold implantation has also gained traction, particularly for partial meniscal defects. Although some long-term studies have shown favorable outcomes, there remains concern regarding incomplete integration and scaffold resorption over time. Moreover, collagen-based implants are not designed for total meniscus replacement and are restricted by availability and cost.8,9

Tendon autografts, widely used in labral reconstructions in both shoulder and hip surgeries, have emerged as a viable alternative for meniscal substitution. Their advantages include the absence of immunologic response, faster rehabilitation, cost effectiveness, and universal graft availability. Autografts are especially valuable in settings where allograft use is not feasible. However, commonly used tendons, such as semitendinosus or gracilis often lack the volume and strength required for complete meniscus replacement, particularly on the medial side.9,10

The peroneus longus tendon, by contrast, possesses favorable anatomical and biomechanical characteristics that closely mimic the native meniscus. Its adequate diameter, tensile strength, and ease of harvest make it a suitable candidate for full medial meniscus substitution. While prior literature has described its use in lateral meniscus reconstruction, reports on its application for the medial compartment are sparse.3, 4, 5,11 The peroneus longus tendon has been effectively used in posterolateral corner reconstruction and lateral meniscus transplantation, showing favorable clinical and biomechanical outcomes. Its proven strength, compatibility, and integration make it a promising graft, supporting its application in medial meniscus reconstruction as presented in our study.4,11,12

In this report, we describe a surgical technique for medial meniscus reconstruction using a peroneus longus tendon autograft. The procedure is technically feasible using standard orthopedic instrumentation, and the graft can be customized intraoperatively without the need for precise size matching, as is required in allograft procedures. The tendon’s mechanical strength is adequate to withstand compressive loads within the knee joint, and any excess tissue can be easily trimmed or managed extra-articularly.

The main indications for this procedure mirror those of MAT and scaffold implantation: medial joint line pain following subtotal or total meniscectomy in young, active individuals without advanced osteoarthritis or significant malalignment. Contraindications include obesity, inflammatory arthritis, synovial pathologies, and a history of intra-articular infection.

Potential drawbacks include donor site morbidity and theoretical risks, such as peroneal nerve irritation, lateral ankle instability, or arch collapse. However, previous studies have reported low complication rates with peroneus longus tendon harvest.3,13 Another limitation is the current lack of long-term follow-up data regarding graft remodeling, functional outcomes, and chondroprotective efficacy. Additionally, differences in collagen fiber orientation between tendon and meniscus tissue raise questions about the long-term biomechanical integrity of the graft.

Despite these limitations, our initial experience with the technique has been positive. The procedure is reproducible, cost-effective, and provides a promising biologic alternative for medial meniscal reconstruction in appropriately selected patients. Further clinical studies and longer-term follow-up are necessary to validate the outcomes and determine the role of this technique in the broader landscape of meniscus-preserving surgery.
