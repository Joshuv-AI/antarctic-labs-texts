Parallel expansion of trehalose transporter genes integrates intestinal energy metabolism with developmental growth in lepidopteran insects
Authors: Qingyu Lu, Yanyan Zhou, Yanting Liang, Wenhui Jing, Yinghui Li, Jingbo Zhao, Huabing Wang, Koichiro Tamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTrehalose is the principal circulating sugar in insects, yet how diversification of its transport system contributes to metabolic adaptability is unclear. Here, comparative genomics across 194 insect species reveals recurrent expansion of the trehalose transporter Tret1, with Lepidoptera retaining a conserved tandem duplication that generated two paralogs, Tret1-1 and Tret1-2. Functional analyses suggest a differentiated functional relationship between them: BmTret1-1 is indispensable for systemic trehalose flux, midgut integrity, and larval growth, whereas BmTret1-2 is highly expressed in the brain and may serve as a compensatory paralog of BmTret1-1. By integrating multiomics, single-nucleus transcriptomics, and functional assays, we further show that loss of BmTret1-1 is associated with altered intestinal stem cell to enterocyte (EC) differentiation dynamics, accompanied by reduced EC abundance, thereby linking nutrient transport capacity to epithelial renewal and organismal growth. Together with the parallel expansion of trehalase genes, our findings reveal a general framework in which coordinated expansion and functional cooperation of the trehalose transport–catabolism axis contribute to metabolic homeostasis in Lepidoptera. This study offers novel insights into the evolution of insect trehalose metabolism.Keywords: trehalose, parallel evolution, gene expansion, metabolic adaptation

Introduction

Living organisms rely on robust metabolic systems as buffers against environmental variability and for the maintenance of growth, survival, and reproduction (Thaiss et al. 2014; Koyama et al. 2020; Lin et al. 2024). Among metabolic substrates, sugars serve as universal energy sources and carbon providers, supporting ATP generation and biosynthetic processes (Chandel 2021; Kukurugya et al. 2024). Trehalose, a nonreducing disaccharide composed of two glucose units, is widely distributed across bacteria, fungi, plants, and invertebrates (Elbein et al. 2003; Iturriaga et al. 2009). In insects, trehalose rather than glucose dominates the hemolymph, functioning as a major energy reservoir and as a stabilizing agent that protects proteins and membranes during desiccation and cold stress (Wyatt and Kale 1957; Horie 1960; Matsuda et al. 2015; Wen et al. 2016; Yoshida et al. 2016; Nishimura 2020; Mizutani et al. 2024). Tight regulation of trehalose homeostasis thus underpins insect growth, developmental progression, and resilience to ecological stressors (Liu et al. 2013; Volkenhoff et al. 2015; Nishimura 2020; Yamada et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2022). Most studies have focused on trehalose synthesis and hydrolysis, whereas much less attention has been paid to the evolutionary history and functional specialization of trehalose transport system.

Insects synthesize trehalose predominantly in the fat body, an organ analogous to the mammalian liver and adipose tissue, and distribute it through the hemolymph to fuel peripheral tissues (Matsushita and Nishimura 2020; Nishimura 2020). Because trehalose cannot passively diffuse across membranes, its transport relies on specific transmembrane carriers, similar to glucose transporters in mammals such as the insulin-regulated GLUT4 (Huang and Czech 2007; Nishimura 2020; Yuan et al. 2022). The trehalose transporter Tret1, a member of the major facilitator superfamily, serves as the principal conduit linking trehalose production sites with energy-consuming organs (Kikawada et al. 2007; Kanamori et al. 2010; Nishimura 2020). Tret1 homologs have been identified in diverse insects including Drosophila melanogaster, Bombyx mori, and Apis mellifera, but they differ markedly in transport capacity (Kanamori et al. 2010), suggesting that they have undergone substantial evolutionary divergence. In the desiccation-tolerant midge Polypedilum vanderplanki, Tret1 functions as a bidirectional, high-capacity transporter supporting extreme anhydrobiosis (Kikawada et al. 2007; Kanamori et al. 2010; Mizutani et al. 2024). Conversely, lineage-specific duplications—such as the emergence of Drosophila Tret1-2—illustrate functional diversification, including the loss of transport activity (Kanamori et al. 2010). Such patterns are consistent with the broader view that metabolic gene families frequently evolve through gene duplication, dosage modulation, and functional divergence to meet novel ecological challenges (Klure et al. 2025). Indeed, parallel evolutionary solutions involving gene duplication and functional expansion have been documented across diverse taxa facing strong dietary or environmental pressures, underscoring the predictability of adaptive trajectories in metabolic systems (Zhen et al. 2012). In the case of trehalose transporters, these contrasts suggest that trehalose transporters have undergone recurrent, context-dependent evolutionary adaptation to meet distinct ecological and physiological demands. Despite the central role of Tret1 in metabolic homeostasis, its evolutionary origins, diversification, and functional consequences remain poorly understood.

Here, we reconstruct the evolutionary history of insect trehalose transporter Tret1 and uncover unexpected, lineage-specific expansions—most prominently in Lepidoptera—that reveal a dynamic evolutionary trajectory for this central metabolic gateway. By integrating single-nucleus transcriptomics, targeted metabolomics, and functional experiments, we dissect the regulatory architecture of the paralog Tret1-1 and uncover how trehalose uptake is coupled to intestinal epithelial dynamics and organismal metabolic state in vivo. The expansion of the trehalose transport–catabolism axis in Lepidoptera represents an important strategy that enhances the metabolic plasticity of this group.

Results

Lineage-specific tandem duplications of Tret1 in Lepidoptera

Trehalose transport in insects is mediated by Tret1, a conserved transmembrane carrier that is essential for systemic sugar mobilization (Nishimura 2020). To trace its evolutionary history, we conducted a phylogenomic survey across 206 insect genomes representing 11 major orders (Figure S1). From 5,989 candidate sequences, 268 clustered into a well-supported monophyletic Tret1 clade that includes D. melanogaster Tret1-1 and Tret1-2, of which 245 high-confidence homologs were retained after manual curation. Copy-number reconstruction across 194 species revealed a striking pattern: whereas most insects retain a single Tret1 gene, all surveyed lepidopterans harbor a conserved pair of paralogs (Fig. 1 and Figure S2). Synteny analyses further showed that these lepidopteran paralogs originated from a shared tandem duplication, in contrast to the sporadic, lineage-restricted duplications found in other orders (Figure S3). These results show that Tret1 is mainly maintained as a single-copy gene across insects, yet it underwent a stable duplication in Lepidoptera.

Lepidopteran Tret1-2 retains trehalose transport activity

We then compared exon organization and domain composition across insect lineages to assess how gene duplication shaped the evolution of Tret1. Lepidopteran Tret1-1 and Tret1-2 do not share conserved exon structures and have diverged markedly in terms of exon number and N-terminal architecture, whereas there is partial conservation of exons in dipteran paralogs (Figures S4 to S6). Despite pronounced structural divergence, key transmembrane domains and functional motifs remain conserved, implying the selective retention of transporter functionality (Figures S5 and S6). Functional assays in Xenopus laevis oocytes showed that both B. mori and Papilio xuthus Tret1 paralogs localize to the plasma membrane and mediate trehalose uptake (Fig. 2a and b and Figure S7). BmTret1-2 exhibited significantly lower trehalose transport activity than BmTret1-1, indicating reduced but retained transport activity (Fig. 2b).

Both Tret1 genes were found to be upregulated during starvation in BmN cells (Fig. 2c and d), and exhibited changes in expression associated with egg diapause and its termination (Figures S8 and S9). These results indicate that duplication of Tret1 in Lepidoptera produced structurally divergent yet functionally conserved transporters. This evolutionary pattern suggests that gene duplication enabled structural diversification while preserving transport capacity.

BmTret1-2 partially compensates for larval sugar homeostasis disrupted by BmTret1-1 loss

We examined the tissue-specific expression patterns of the two paralogs using publicly available SilkDB 3.0 datasets. BmTret1-1 is broadly expressed across multiple tissues, including the head, fat body, and midgut, whereas BmTret1-2 shows lower expression but is more enriched in the head than in most other tissues (Figure S10). Day 3 of the fifth instar (L5D3) brain transcriptome data and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) showed that BmTret1-2 was highly expressed in the brain, at levels comparable to those of BmTret1-1 at this stage (Fig. 3j and k). In pupal samples, qRT-PCR showed that BmTret1-2 expression was lower than that of BmTret1-1 in the head, but was significantly higher than that of BmTret1-1 in the brain at pupal stages P2 and P6 (Figure S11c and d).

To investigate the physiological functions of the two lepidopteran Tret1 paralogs, we generated CRISPR/Cas9-mediated mutations in BmTret1-1 and BmTret1-2 in the lepidopteran model insect B. mori. For each gene, one of two initially identified mutant alleles was isolated as a homozygous line for functional analyses. The BmTret1-1 homozygous mutant line carried a 2-bp deletion, whereas the BmTret1-2 homozygous mutant line carried a 2-bp insertion, both resulting in frameshift mutations (Fig. 3a and Figure S12). BmTret1-1 mutants displayed pronounced developmental defects, including reduced body size and weight, slower weight gain, decreased food intake, and lower approximate digestibility of mulberry leaves (Fig. 3b to e and Figure S13). Although BmTret1-2 mutants exhibited no obvious developmental defects, the increased expression of BmTret1-2 in BmTret1-1 mutants suggests that it may play a compensatory role (Fig. 3b to f).

Hemolymph metabolite profiling revealed a profound collapse of trehalose homeostasis. Both mutants showed reduced trehalose, but only BmTret1-1 mutants exhibited a concomitant rise in glucose (Fig. 3g and h), suggesting increased glucose availability when trehalose import is compromised. In the fat body, trehalose levels were significantly increased in BmTret1-1 mutants, whereas BmTret1-2 mutants showed only a slight but nonsignificant increase (Figure S14). These findings establish BmTret1-1 as the critical trehalose transporter sustaining organismal sugar balance and larval energy supply.

Given the tight coupling between larval energy supply and gut physiology, we further examined intestinal expression patterns and found that both paralogs localize to the midgut and hindgut, with BmTret1-1 showing consistently higher midgut expression (Fig. 3i and Figure S11a and b). This difference highlights the intestine as the primary interface where loss of BmTret1-1 disrupts nutrient uptake, epithelial function, and whole-organism metabolism.

BmTret1-1 modulates midgut energy metabolism and digestive homeostasis

Given the pronounced phenotypic defects of BmTret1-1 mutants and the lack of an overt phenotype in BmTret1-2 mutants, we focused subsequent analyses on BmTret1-1. We next performed metabolomic profiling of BmTret1-1 mutant larvae at day 2 of the third instar (L3D2) to examine the physiological consequences of impaired trehalose transport. Trehalose levels were markedly reduced, accompanied by elevated isocitrate, indicating a shift toward tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle-driven energy compensation (Figure S15). Perturbations extended to nucleotide metabolism, with increased phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate and reduced deoxyribose (Figure S15), suggesting imbalanced flux through the pentose phosphate pathway. Among metabolites identified as significantly differentially abundant (variable importance in projection [VIP] > 1 and P < 0.05), downregulated metabolites outnumbered upregulated ones and were enriched across lipid, nucleotide, and amino acid metabolic pathways (Figure S16), highlighting broad metabolic depression.

Because BmTret1-1 mutants exhibited severe growth defects at L5D3, we next examined midgut transcriptional and metabolic states during this critical stage. Gut transcriptomes revealed the widespread repression of genes involved in carbohydrate, lipid, and amino acid metabolism, together with the strong depletion of digestion-related pathways (Fig. 4a and Figures S17 and S18). Targeted metabolomics further confirmed reduced levels of D-glucose, L-methionine, and other central metabolites, while there was an accumulation of L-serine, deoxyadenosine monophosphate, and related metabolites (Fig. 4b). These shifts were particularly associated with pathways spanning carbohydrate, lipid, nucleotide, and amino acid metabolism (Figure S19). Loss of BmTret1-1 triggered systemic energy deficits and impaired nutrient assimilation, underscoring the central role of trehalose flux in maintaining intestinal homeostasis. Together, these findings reveal that BmTret1-1 is indispensable for sustaining midgut energy metabolic output and digestive capacity during larval growth.

BmTret1-1 maintains enterocyte abundance and shapes cellular composition of larval midgut

We next performed single-nucleus RNA sequencing on L3D2 guts (midguts and hindguts) from WT and BmTret1-1 mutants. After quality filtering, we obtained high-resolution transcriptomes from 10,570 WT and 11,257 mutant cells. By integrating marker expression, gene ontology (GO) enrichment, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) embeddings, and transcriptomic correlation, we identified 17 transcriptionally distinct gut cell clusters, encompassing intestinal stem cell (ISC), EC, endocrine cells (EE), goblet cells (GC), circular muscle cells (CM), and posterior-gut cells (PG) (Fig. 5a to d and Figures S20 and S21).

The 17 clusters identified above could be grouped into six cell types: ISC, EC, EE, GC, CM, and PG (b). In addition, five clusters (Cluster 4, Cluster 5, Cluster 7, Cluster 11, and Cluster 19) could not be confidently assigned to any known cell type.

Comparative mapping of WT and mutant cell atlases revealed striking alterations in epithelial composition in the absence of BmTret1-1 (Fig. 5e). Mutants exhibited a marked reduction in EC abundance, accompanied by a near absence of the EC-like3 subtype and substantial depletion of EC-like1 and EC-like5 (Fig. 5f and Figure S22). Conversely, several non-EC populations—including ISC, EE, GC, CM, and PG cells—showed proportional increases, potentially reflecting shifts in epithelial homeostasis. Taken together, these results suggest that loss of BmTret1-1 is associated with a reorganization of epithelial cell composition, and support a link between trehalose transport capacity and midgut tissue homeostasis. These findings are consistent with a role for BmTret1-1 in maintaining the integrity and abundance of EC populations in the midgut.

BmTret1-1 is required for EC energy metabolism and digestive function

We further investigated how BmTret1-1 deficiency impacts cellular metabolism. Transcriptional profiling across the six major gut cell types revealed widespread perturbations in core energy metabolism and digestive pathways (Figure S23a and b). Sucrase genes, including beta-fructofuranosidase (Suc1) and maltase A1 (SUH), were consistently upregulated across five cell types, highlighting a compensatory response to impaired trehalose transport (Figure S24). These results indicate that the loss of BmTret1-1 disrupts intestinal energy metabolic homeostasis and compromises digestive efficiency.

Upon focusing on individual EC subtypes, we found that upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in EC-like1 and EC-like2 were enriched in glycolysis, TCA cycle, or nutrient assimilation pathways (Figure S23c and d). Notably, the top 100 marker genes from the EC-like3 cluster—absent in BmTret1-1 mutants—were similarly associated with energy metabolism and digestion (Figure S23e), suggesting that the loss of BmTret1-1 contributes to systemic metabolic dysfunction. Many important enzymes mediating carbohydrate flux, including HK2, Eno, Ald, Tpi, Nc73EF, Idh3a, and mAcon1, were differentially expressed across EC clusters (Figures S25 to 27), underscoring the broad rewiring of glycolytic and TCA cycle activity. The loss of BmTret1-1 triggered transcriptional reprogramming that both reflected and compensated for metabolic stress, revealing a direct mechanistic link between trehalose transport, nutrient metabolism, and the functional integrity of the midgut. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that BmTret1-1 is essential for maintaining EC energy balance and digestive competence.

BmTret1-1 deficiency alters ISC-to-EC differentiation dynamics and activates hippo signaling in the midgut

Given the concomitant reduction in EC number and expansion of ISC population in the midgut of BmTret1-1 mutant larvae, we hypothesized that lineage output from ISC to EC is disrupted. BmTret1-1 was detected in both ISC and EC, with modestly higher expression in the former (Figure S28). To directly assess alterations in the differentiation process, we applied Slingshot trajectory analysis focusing on ISC and EC populations. The inferred trajectory recapitulated the established ISC-to-EC lineage relationship (Fig. 6a).

Notably, EC subpopulations in the BmTret1-1 mutant midgut exhibited a pronounced redistribution along the differentiation trajectory (Fig. 6a). In particular, the proportion of EC-like1 cells located at the terminal end of the trajectory was markedly reduced (Fig. 6a), suggesting changes in ISC-to-EC differentiation continuity and reduced progression toward terminal maturation. Consistent with this observation, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed significant positive enrichment of cell differentiation pathway in ISC from BmTret1-1 mutant midguts, whereas no significant changes were detected in EC (Fig. 6b and Figure S29a). Given the central role of Notch signaling in regulating ISC differentiation (Ohlstein and Spradling 2007), we further examined its activity in this context. Notably, the Notch signaling pathway was significantly positively enriched in ISC from BmTret1-1 mutant midguts, whereas no significant enrichment was observed in EC (Fig. 6c and Figure S29b). Importantly, the apoptosis pathway was not significantly enriched in EC (Figure S29c), which does not support widespread apoptosis as the main explanation for EC reduction. Collectively, these data suggest that loss of BmTret1-1 is associated with a systemic remodeling of the ISC-to-EC differentiation trajectory, characterized by altered distribution of intermediate differentiation states and reduced representation of mature EC populations.

While the analyses above suggest that loss of BmTret1-1 primarily perturbs ISC-to-EC lineage progression, these differentiation defects alone may not fully account for the pronounced growth phenotype observed at the tissue level. We therefore asked whether additional growth-regulatory signaling pathways are altered in the BmTret1-1 mutant midgut. Given its central role in controlling epithelial growth and organ size in insects, Hippo signaling was prioritized for further analysis. Notably, KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs revealed that this pathway was significantly enriched only among upregulated genes in ISC, but not in other midgut cell types (Fig. 6d and Tables S1 and S2), suggesting a cell type-specific involvement of Hippo signaling at the stem cell level. Moreover, GSEA showed a significant positive enrichment of the Hippo signaling pathway in ISC of BmTret1-1 mutant midguts (Figure S29d). Consistent with this transcriptional signature, immunoblotting revealed increased phosphorylation of Hpo and Wts in the midgut of BmTret1-1 mutants (Fig. 6e and Figure S30), indicating activation of the Hippo pathway. These results suggest that loss of BmTret1-1 reduced proliferative activity in the midgut, potentially through activated Hippo signaling. Collectively, our findings suggest that BmTret1-1 supports ISC-to-EC differentiation and epithelial growth by maintaining metabolic homeostasis.

Discussion

Trehalose transport is central to insect energy homeostasis (Nishimura 2020; Chatterjee and Perrimon 2021). By integrating phylogenomic, single-nucleus, and functional analyses, we show that Tret1 evolution in Lepidoptera involves gene duplication, functional divergence, and differential regulation of paralogs, features that may support trehalose transport and energy homeostasis. Our findings suggest that evolutionary remodeling of a core metabolic interface contributes to adaptive metabolism, providing a conceptual framework for understanding the evolution of carbohydrate and energy metabolism in insects.

Our phylogenomic survey of 194 insect genomes reveals recurrent, lineage-specific expansions of Tret1, most prominently in Lepidoptera. Whereas most insects retain a single copy, lepidopterans consistently harbor two paralogs arising from tandem duplication, reflecting both evolutionary conservation and lineage-specific diversification (Lynch and Conery 2000; Innan and Kondrashov 2010). Sporadic duplications in Diptera, Coleoptera, and Hymenoptera underscore a dynamic interplay between gene stability and adaptive diversification in sugar transport systems (Assis and Bachtrog 2013; Hoile et al. 2025; Zhong et al. 2025). In addition, our previous work identified three distinct trehalase forms in Lepidoptera, including the tandemly duplicated Treh1a and Treh1b, which differ in expression, enzymatic properties, and subcellular localization, suggesting sub- and/or neofunctionalization (Zhou et al. 2019). The specific co-expansion of trehalase and trehalose transporter genes in lepidopteran insects represents an evolutionary innovation in trehalose metabolism. By augmenting the flux and regulatory precision of the trehalose “transport-breakdown” pathway, this genetic feature contributes to the physiological demands of lepidopterans. These expansions therefore represent not merely increases in copy number, but an integrated metabolic feature contributing to metabolic capacity and physiological performance in lepidopteran insects.

Functional analyses indicate that these duplications are not merely structural. In B. mori, BmTret1-2 retains the capacity to transport trehalose, albeit at significantly lower levels than the primary BmTret1-1. In P. xuthus, PxTret1-2 also showed detectable trehalose uptake activity, whereas in D. melanogaster, DmTret1-2 has completely lost this function (Kanamori et al. 2010). Loss-of-function assays show that disruption of BmTret1-1, but not BmTret1-2, causes severe metabolic collapse, depleting hemolymph trehalose, impairing growth and disrupting midgut energetic balance. These findings indicate functional asymmetry between the paralogs (Kanamori et al. 2010; Volkenhoff et al. 2015), suggesting partial functional differentiation, in which BmTret1-1 plays a dominant role in maintaining systemic trehalose flux, whereas BmTret1-2 may act as a compensatory paralog of BmTret1-1. Although BmTret1-2 shows lower overall expression than BmTret1-1, its expression is relatively higher in the head than in most other tissues. Further expression analyses showed that BmTret1-2 is highly expressed in the brain, with levels comparable to or higher than those of BmTret1-1 at specific developmental stages. Previous studies in Drosophila have demonstrated that trehalose transport is essential for glial cells and plays a critical role in maintaining neural metabolic homeostasis (Volkenhoff et al. 2015). These expression patterns suggest that BmTret1-2 contributes to trehalose uptake in neural tissues, thereby potentially supporting neural metabolic demands; however, this hypothesis requires further experimental validation. The low viability of BmTret1-1 homozygous mutants makes the long-term maintenance of homozygous mutant lines technically challenging. Future functional characterization of BmTret1-1 could therefore be strengthened by additional rescue experiments or overexpression analyses.

At the tissue level, loss of BmTret1-1 triggers profound intestinal remodeling. In insects, midgut EC performs essential digestive functions analogous to mammalian epithelial cells (Hung et al. 2020; Awais et al. 2024). Single-nucleus transcriptomic analysis shows a sharp decline in EC populations, including a near absence of the EC-like3 subtype, accompanied by dysregulated expression of energy metabolic and digestive genes. This reduction is consistent with disrupted continuity of ISC-to-EC differentiation. These results establish a functional link between nutrient transport, cell differentiation, and epithelial homeostasis, suggesting that BmTret1-1 integrates metabolic state with tissue renewal to sustain larval development. BmTret1-1 plays a dominant role in sustaining systemic trehalose transport and intestinal homeostasis, whereas BmTret1-2 is highly expressed in the brain and may act as a compensatory paralog of BmTret1-1. The functional divergence of BmTret1-1 and BmTret1-2 contributes to maintaining trehalose homeostasis. Notably, the conserved and survival-essential Tret1-1 represents a promising target for the chemical control of pest insects (Redhai et al. 2020).

Tret1 exemplifies how gene duplication and divergence can reshape conserved metabolic pathways through structural diversification and regulatory variation. Together with the coordinated expansion of trehalase genes in Lepidoptera (Zhou et al. 2019), our findings suggest that the trehalose transport–catabolism module represents a coordinated evolutionary feature associated with the regulation of metabolic flux and energy homeostasis (Fig. 7). This coupled expansion and functional cooperation provide a metabolic framework potentially linked to the diversification and ecological success of lepidopteran insects. More broadly, our results suggest that gene family evolution links molecular innovation to organismal performance, highlighting duplication and divergence as potential drivers of metabolic adaptability (Conant and Wolfe 2008; Innan and Kondrashov 2010). Taken together, these findings advance our understanding of the genomics and evolution of insect trehalose metabolic systems, and suggest that evolutionary reconfiguration of trehalose transport–catabolism axes contributes to metabolic plasticity in lepidopteran insects.

Evolutionary diversification of Tret1 contributes to metabolic plasticity in lepidoptera. Tret1 mediates trehalose transport in insects. Lepidoptera uniquely retain a conserved tandem duplication that generated the paralogs Tret1-1 and Tret1-2. These paralogs retain transport capacity but have undergone regulatory and functional divergence. BmTret1-1 is indispensable for larval growth, epithelial integrity, and intestinal metabolic homeostasis, whereas BmTret1-2 is highly expressed in the brain and may act as a compensatory paralog of BmTret1-1. The expansion and diversification of trehalose transport and trehalase genes may contribute to metabolic plasticity in lepidopteran insects.

Materials and methods

Phylogenetic analysis of insect Tret1 homologs

A BLASTp search was performed using DIAMOND v2.1.8 against a custom protein database comprising genomes from 206 insect species, with an E-value cutoff of 1e−10, a minimum sequence identity of 30%, and a minimum alignment coverage of 60% (Buchfink et al. 2021). After removing redundant protein IDs, a total of 5,989 putative Tret1 homologs were obtained. Full-length amino acid sequences of these homologs were retrieved using SeqKit v2.6.1, aligned using MAFFT v7.520, and ambiguously aligned regions were trimmed with trimAl v1.4.1 (Capella-Gutiérrez et al. 2009; Katoh and Standley 2013; Shen et al. 2024). A maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was then constructed based on the aligned sequences using IQ-TREE v2.2.6 with 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates (Nguyen et al. 2015). Based on the resulting phylogeny, we identified a well-supported clade (bootstrap = 95) containing previously characterized insect Tret1 proteins from D. melanogaster, B. mori, A. mellifera, and other species. This clade initially comprised 268 sequences. After manual curation to remove obsolete database records and a small number of ambiguously annotated sequences, 245 high-confidence Tret1 homologs were retained for further analysis.

A final maximum-likelihood tree of these 245 homologs was constructed to infer their evolutionary relationships. To explore the distribution of these 245 homologs across insects, a separate maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed using representative 194 species, annotated with the number of Tret1 homologs present in each species. Conserved protein domains within Tret1 homologs were identified using pfam_scan against the Pfam-A database. Conserved motifs were predicted using MEME v5.5.8 (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme). Both domain and motif architectures were mapped and categorized according to the phylogenetic tree. All phylogenetic trees and associated annotations were visualized using iTOL v7.2 (https://itol.embl.de/).

Conserved synteny analysis

Tret1 was used as an anchor to extract upstream and downstream genes from each species. The corresponding protein sequences of neighboring genes were retrieved and orthologous gene clusters were inferred using OrthoFinder v2.5.4 (Emms and Kelly 2019). Genomic synteny of Tret1 and its neighboring genes was visualized using the R package gggenes, with gene clusters categorized and annotated according to the phylogenetic relationships among insect species.

Exon structure analysis

The exon–intron structures of Tret1 genes, including exon number and lengths, were calculated based on genomic coordinates.

Heterologous expression of Tret1 in X. laevis oocytes and trehalose uptake assay

The open reading frame (ORF) of Tret1, lacking the stop codon, was amplified from cDNA templates. For B. mori and P. xuthus, Tret1-1 was amplified from fat body cDNA, whereas Tret1-2 was amplified from brain cDNA. The PCR product was cloned into the pcs-EGFP vector digested with the same enzymes. The resulting plasmid was linearized with NotI, and capped RNA (cRNA) was synthesized in vitro using the mMESSAGE mMACHINE SP6 Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), followed by polyadenylation using the Poly(A) Tailing Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Purified cRNA was used for microinjection.

Fresh oocytes were isolated from X. laevis and injected with 8 nL of cRNA (300 ng/μL). Oocytes were cultured at 22 °C for 3 d in calcium-containing MBS buffer. EGFP fluorescence was examined under a fluorescence stereomicroscope (SMZ18), and fluorescent oocytes were selected for further analysis. Selected oocytes were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, incubated in PBS, embedded in OCT compound, cryosectioned, mounted in 80% glycerol, sealed with nail polish, and imaged using confocal microscopy (Zeiss LSM 780) to determine the subcellular localization of the Tret1-EGFP fusion protein.

For trehalose uptake assays, fluorescent oocytes were incubated in MBS medium supplemented with 105 mM trehalose at 22 °C for 12 h, then washed thoroughly with trehalose-free MBS buffer. Oocytes were collected and homogenized in 90% ethanol containing sorbitol. After centrifugation, the supernatant containing intracellular trehalose was evaporated to dryness under nitrogen and reconstituted in water to concentrate the sample. Trehalose content was quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Agilent 1,200) using an amino column (NH2, 4.6 × 150 mm, 5 μm), with quantification based on external calibration curves. Oocytes injected with cRNA encoding the empty vector were used as negative controls.

CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing

Single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting BmTret1-1 and BmTret1-2 were designed using the online tool CRISPOR (https://crispor.gi.ucsc.edu/) (Concordet and Haeussler 2018). Candidate target sites with minimal predicted off-target effects were identified based on a genome-wide search against the B. mori genome using CRISPOR. sgRNA for BmTret1-1: TCTTCTGCCAACCGCGTTTGG. sgRNA for BmTret1-2: TCAAATGTCAGGAAGTTCTGTGG. The two sgRNAs were synthesized in vitro using the MEGAscript T7 Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The Cas9 protein (New England Biolabs, USA) was complexed with sgRNAs (1 µg sgRNA + 3 µg Cas9) in a total volume of 10 µL. The sgRNA/Cas9 solution was microinjected into B. mori (N4 strain) embryos within 6 h postoviposition. Mutant lines for BmTret1-1 and BmTret1-2 were established through PCR amplification of the target regions followed by Sanger sequencing to confirm CRISPR-induced mutations, and were subsequently maintained by selective breeding.

Transcript-level validation of CRISPR/Cas9-induced indels

To validate CRISPR/Cas9-induced indels at the transcript level, total RNA was extracted from head tissues of L5D3 WT, BmTret1-1 mutant, and BmTret1-2 mutant larvae using RNAiso Plus (Takara, China). cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, China). Regions spanning the CRISPR/Cas9 target sites were amplified from WT and the corresponding mutant cDNA using gene-specific primers for BmTret1-1 or BmTret1-2. The RT-PCR products were directly subjected to Sanger sequencing. Primers used for RT-PCR amplification are listed in Table S4.

Phenotypic analysis

Larvae at the L5D3 stage from WT, BmTret1-1, and BmTret1-2 mutants were used for analysis. For each genotype, 13 independent biological replicate groups were established, with 10 larvae per group. Measurements were averaged within each group, and these group means were used for statistical analysis (n = 13 per genotype). Considering the low survival rate of BmTret1-1 mutants during the larval stage, phenotypic measurements were conducted over a 24-h period. Body weights were recorded at L5D3 and L5D4, and the difference was defined as the weight gain after 24 h of feeding on mulberry leaves. The weights of supplied leaves, remaining leaves after feeding, and residual feces were recorded. Food intake was calculated as the difference between the supplied and remaining leaf weights, and approximate digestibility was calculated as

All leaf and feces weights refer to dry weight. The fresh weight of supplied leaves was converted to dry weight using a dry-weight coefficient, while the remaining leaves and feces were dried at 60 °C for 48 h before weighing. The coefficient was determined from the ratio of dry to fresh weights of 35 leaf samples (Table S3).

Quantification of trehalose and glucose by HPLC

Hemolymph from L5D3 larvae was immediately heated at 80 °C for 10 min to inactivate endogenous enzymes, including trehalase and sucrase. The samples were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min, and the resulting supernatant was diluted at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio with 80% (v/v) acetonitrile prior to analysis.

For fat body trehalose measurement, fat bodies from L5D3 larvae were dissected and homogenized in ice-cold PBS. The homogenates were heated at 80 °C for 10 min to inactivate endogenous enzymes and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min. The resulting supernatant was collected for both trehalose quantification and protein concentration measurement. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay. Before HPLC analysis, the supernatant was diluted at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio with 80% (v/v) acetonitrile. Fat body trehalose content was normalized to the protein concentration of each sample and expressed as milligram trehalose per milligram protein.

All samples were analyzed using a HPLC system (Waters Alliance 2,695, Waters, Milford, MA, USA) equipped with an evaporative light scattering detector (ELSD, Waters 2,424). Chromatographic separation was performed on a BEH Amide analytical column (Waters XBridge, 130 Å, 4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) coupled with a guard column (Waters XBridge, 130 Å, 4.6 × 20 mm, 5 μm), maintained at 35 °C. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (ultrapure water containing 0.2% triethylamine) and solvent B (acetonitrile). The gradient elution program was as follows: initial composition of 15% A and 85% B; a linear increase to 40% A and 60% B over 20 min; maintained at 40% A and 60% B for 5 min; then returned to 15% A and 85% B within 2 min and held for an additional 8 min. The flow rate was set at 0.75 mL/min. ELSD settings were as follows: nebulizer pressure, 40 psi; drift tube temperature, 50 °C; and gain, 200.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Midguts and hindguts were collected from WT silkworms at the L3D2 and L5D3 stages, respectively. Brain tissues were collected from WT larvae at the L5D3 stage. Head and brain tissues were collected from WT pupae at the P2, P4, and P6 stages. Eggs during diapause and diapause termination were collected from day 1 to day 8 after oviposition. BmN cells were collected after 3, 6, and 24 h of serum starvation. Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus (Takara, China), and reverse transcription was performed with the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, China) to generate cDNA. qRT-PCR was carried out on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA) using Hieff UNICON Universal Blue qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, China). Gene-specific primers are listed in Table S4. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Transcript levels were normalized to ribosomal protein 49, used as an internal reference. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔCt or 2−ΔΔCt method.

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was extracted from L5D3 midgut tissues using RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara, China). Three independent biological replicates were prepared, each consisting of pooled midgut tissues from three larvae. A total of six RNA-seq libraries were constructed (three per genotype). Paired-end RNA sequencing was performed on the Illumina platform by Novogene Co., Ltd., producing raw reads. Read quality was assessed using FastQC, and adapter sequences and low-quality bases were trimmed using Trimmomatic v0.39 (Bolger et al. 2014). Clean reads were aligned to the B. mori reference genome using STAR (Dobin et al. 2013), and gene expression levels were quantified using RSEM (Li and Dewey 2011). DEGs were identified using DESeq2 (Love et al. 2014), with a cutoff of adjusted P-value < 0.05 and |log2 (fold change) | > 1. GO enrichment analysis of DEGs was conducted using TBtools (Chen et al. 2020).

For L5D3 brain transcriptome sequencing, total RNA was extracted from brain tissues of WT larvae. Three independent biological replicates were prepared, each consisting of pooled brain tissues from ten larvae. A total of three RNA-seq libraries were constructed. RNA quality and quantity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and an Agilent 2,100 Bioanalyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). RNA-seq library construction and single-end 50-bp sequencing were performed on the BGISEQ-500 platform by BGI-Shenzhen Co., Ltd. Raw reads were filtered using SOAPnuke v1.5.2 (Chen et al. 2018). Clean reads were mapped to the B. mori reference genome using HISAT2 v2.0.4 (Kim et al. 2015), and gene expression levels were quantified as TPM values after alignment to the reference coding gene set using Bowtie2 v2.2.5 (Langmead and Salzberg 2012). Public tissue expression data for BmTret1-1 and BmTret1-2 were obtained from SilkDB 3.0 and used to compare their tissue expression patterns.

Nontargeted metabolomics of midgut

Metabolites were extracted from L3D2 midgut tissues. Six independent biological replicates were analyzed for each genotype (WT and BmTret1-1 mutant), with each biological replicate consisting of pooled samples from eight larvae. A pooled quality control (QC) sample was generated by mixing equal volumes of metabolites from all individual samples. Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) analysis was performed on a UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system equipped with an ACQUITY HSS T3 column (Waters, USA) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Raw LC–MS data were processed using Progenesis QI software (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA). Metabolite identification was achieved by searching HMDB, Metlin, and a self-compiled database provided by Majorbio. After database matching, the resulting data matrix was filtered by removing features with excessive missing values across samples, followed by data normalization. Metabolites with a relative standard deviation (RSD) > 30% in QC samples were excluded to ensure data consistency. The filtered dataset was log10-transformed prior to statistical analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were performed using the R package “ropls” (version 1.6.2). Model robustness and predictive performance were assessed via 7-fold cross-validation. Metabolites with a VIP score > 1 and a Student's t-test P-value < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially abundant. Functional annotation of these differential metabolites was conducted using the KEGG database.

Targeted metabolomics of midgut

Targeted profiling of energy-related metabolites in L5D3 midgut tissues was conducted using LC–MS at Shanghai Meiji Biological Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. Three independent biological replicates were analyzed for each genotype (WT and BmTret1-1 mutant), with each biological replicate consisting of pooled samples from eight larvae. For standard preparation, defined amounts of energy metabolism reference standards were accurately weighed, dissolved in methanol, and diluted to a final volume of 1 mL to generate stock solutions. These were further diluted in 50% (v/v) methanol–water to prepare working standard solutions at appropriate concentrations. For sample extraction, 20 mg of tissue was accurately weighed and mixed with 500 μL of extraction buffer (80% methanol in water). Samples were homogenized using a cryogenic grinder for 6 min (−10 °C, 50 Hz), followed by low-temperature ultrasonication for 10 min (5 °C, 40 kHz). After centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, 60 μL of supernatant was mixed with 15 μL of internal standard mixture and analyzed by LC–MS. Another aliquot of 300 μL of the supernatant was evaporated to dryness under nitrogen stream and reconstituted in 75 μL of 50% (v/v) acetonitrile–water solution containing 20% internal standard mixture, followed by LC–MS analysis.

Quantification and identification of energy metabolites were performed using LC–ESI–MS/MS (UHPLC-Qtrap). QC samples were analyzed, and all targeted metabolites exhibited RSDs < 15%, confirming good measurement reproducibility. Raw data were processed with Analyst software (AB Sciex) using default parameters for automatic peak detection and integration, with manual verification as needed. Metabolite concentrations were calculated based on calibration curves. Multivariate statistical analysis, including PCA and OPLS-DA, was performed using the ropls package (v1.6.2) in R. Model robustness was assessed via 7-fold cross-validation. Metabolites with statistical significance were identified by Student's t-test (P < 0.05), and those with VIP > 1 were defined as differential metabolites. Functional annotation and pathway enrichment of differential metabolites were performed based on the KEGG database.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing

For each genotype (WT and BmTret1-1 mutant), a single pooled biological sample was prepared by combining L3D2 guts (midguts and hindguts) collected from 12 larvae. Samples were then flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. All subsequent procedures for nuclei isolation were performed on ice. The tissues were minced in 1 mL of ice-cold nuclei lysis buffer (NST) containing 0.1% NP-40, 10 mM Tris–HCl, 146 mM NaCl, 1 mM CaCl2, 21 mM MgCl2, and 40 U/mL RNase inhibitor. Lysis was carried out for 7 min. The efficiency of nuclear release was assessed by trypan blue staining and microscopic examination. Following lysis, 1 mL of ST Wash buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, 146 mM NaCl, 1 mM CaCl2, 21 mM MgCl2, 0.01% BSA [NEB B9000S], and 40 U/mL RNase inhibitor) was added. The suspension was filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer (BD), and the filter was rinsed with additional ST Wash buffer. The flow-through and wash fractions were combined and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C using a horizontal rotor. The nuclei pellet was resuspended in 5 mL of PBS containing 1% BSA, washed once, and finally suspended in 100 μL of the same buffer. Nuclei were counted using trypan blue staining and microscopy.

The nuclei suspension was diluted to a final concentration of 700 to 1,200 nuclei/μL using PBS with 1% BSA. Single-nucleus RNA-seq was performed using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3ʹ Reagent Kits v3.1 (10× Genomics, 1,000,268) following the manufacturer's instructions. cDNA libraries were amplified and constructed using the Chromium Single Cell 3′/5′ Library & Gel Bead Kit (10× Genomics, 1,000,020). Paired-end sequencing (PE150) was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6,000 platform. In total, 10,570 and 11,257 high-quality nuclei were obtained from WT and BmTret1-1 mutant samples, respectively. Sequencing yielded an average of 38,004 and 29,618 reads per nucleus, with median detected genes of 877 and 1,254, respectively.

Single-nucleus RNA data analysis

Initial quality control of the snRNA-seq data was performed using Cell Ranger (10× Genomics) to align raw reads to the B. mori reference genome and calculate standard metrics, including total UMI counts, gene counts, and mapping rates. Subsequent filtering and downstream analyses were conducted using the Seurat R package (Butler et al. 2018). Low-quality nuclei were excluded based on the following thresholds: <200 detected genes, <1,000 UMIs, log10(genes/UMIs) ratio < 0.7, or >5% mitochondrial-derived UMIs. Putative doublets were detected and removed using the DoubletFinder algorithm (McGinnis et al. 2019).

Batch effects were corrected using Harmony for integration. The corrected dataset was projected into 2D space via UMAP for clustering visualization. Inter-cluster transcriptional similarity was assessed by calculating Pearson correlation coefficients based on average gene expression levels per cluster.

Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the presto framework with default Wilcoxon rank-sum testing. Marker genes were identified for each cluster by applying a log fold change (logFC) > 0 and minimum expression fraction (min.pct) > 0.25. DEGs between biological conditions were identified using FindMarkers in Seurat, applying a |log2(fold change)| > 1.2 and q-value < 0.05. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on selected marker genes or DEGs to explore functional enrichment.

Gene set enrichment analysis

GSEA was performed using the C5 (GO gene sets) and C2 (KEGG gene sets) from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB; http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb) to assess the enrichment of the target GO and KEGG pathways between the WT and BmTret1-1 mutants (Subramanian et al. 2005). Gene sets were defined as significantly enriched based on the following criteria: normalized enrichment score (|NES| > 1), P < 0.05, and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25.

Slingshot analysis

Slingshot was used to infer lineage structures and pseudotime trajectories of cell types (Street et al. 2018). This analysis was performed using the slingshot R package (version 1.8.0). The dimensionally reduced Seurat object was first converted to a SingleCellExperiment object using the as.SingleCellExperiment function. ISC was designated as the starting cluster for trajectory inference.

Western blot analysis

Midgut extracts from WT and BmTret1-1 mutant larvae at the L5D3 and L5D5 stages were prepared by mechanical homogenization in RIPA buffer (Fude Biotechnology, FD008) containing PMSF (Boster, AR0162) and phosphatase inhibitor (Beyotime, P1082). Homogenates (three larvae per replicate) were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA kit (Fude Biotechnology, FD2001). Samples were boiled in SDS loading buffer, separated by SDS–PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and detected using FDbio-Femto ECL reagent (Fude Biotechnology, FD8030).

Primary antibodies: rabbit monoclonal anti-p-MST1 (Thr183)/MST2 (Thr180) (p-Hpo; 1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, #49332) and rabbit polyclonal anti-p-LATS1 (Ser909) (p-Wts; 1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, #9157). Secondary antibody: HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:4000; Dingguo Changsheng, IH-0011). Tubulin was used as an internal control, prepared in our laboratory. Experiments were independently repeated at least three times.
