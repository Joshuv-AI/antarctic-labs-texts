Transcriptional regulation of phospholipid transport in cotton fiber elongation by GhMYB30D04–GhHD1 interaction complex
Authors: Qingwei Song, Chuanhui Du, Yiyang Xu, Jin Wang, Min Lin, Kaijing Zuo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCotton fiber length is basically determined by well‐coordinated gene expression and phosphatidylinositol phosphates (PIPs) accumulation during fiber elongation but the regulatory mechanism governing PIPs transport remains unknown. Here, we report a MYB transcription factor GhMYB30D04 in Gossypium hirsutum that promotes fiber elongation through modulating the expression of PIP transporter gene GhLTPG1. Knockout of GhMYB30D04 gene in cotton (KO) results in a reduction of GhLTPG1 transcripts with lower accumulation of PIPs, leading to shorter fibers and lower fiber yield. Conversely, GhMYB30D04 overexpression (GhMYB30D04‐OE) causes richer PIPs and longer cotton fibers, mimicking the effects of exogenously applying PIPs on the ovules of GhMYB30D04‐KO and wild type. Furthermore, GhMYB30D04 interacts with GhHD1, the crucial transcription factor of fiber initiation, to form an activation complex stabilized by PIPs, both of which upregulate GhLTPG1 expression. Comparative omics‐analysis revealed that higher and extended expressions of LTPG1 in fiber elongation mainly correlate with the variations of the GhMYB30D04 gene between two cotton allotetraploids, contributing to longer fiber in G. babardense. Our work clarifies a mechanism by which GhHD1–GhMYB30D04 form a regulatory module of fiber elongation to tightly control PIP accumulation. Our work still has an implication that GhMYB30D04–GhHD1 associates with development transition from fiber initiation to elongation.Keywords: cotton fiber, fiber cell elongation, MYB–HD‐ZIP transcription complex, phospholipid transport, transcriptional regulation

INTRODUCTION

Cotton fibers are the most important source of natural fibers widely used in the textile industry (Li et al., 2015; Kim and Triplett, 2021). Fiber length is an important trait which chiefly influences cotton fiber quality and economic value of cotton textiles (Ma et al., 2018; He et al., 2021). Cotton fiber cell elongates from the day of anthesis, and then goes to quick‐elongating stage along with dramatic cell membrane expansion and cytoskeleton rearrangement (Ruan et al., 2001; Ji et al., 2003; Qin and Zhu, 2011; Zhang et al., 2011). At the end of fiber elongation (about 20 d post‐anthesis (DPA)), cotton fibers reach almost 90% length of mature fibers (Sun et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2020). Therefore, cotton fiber length is largely determined by the synergetic expressions of 1,000s of genes during fiber elongation (Huang et al., 2021; Tian and Zhang, 2021), and that has inspired scientists to elucidate the regulatory mechanism for improving fiber quality of cotton varieties (Xiao et al., 2019).

Compelling evidence now indicates phosphatidylinositol (PI) and phospholipid metabolism pathways are indispensable for cell membrane expansion, cytoskeleton establishment and polar growth of cotton fibers (Liu et al., 2015). Transcriptomics combined with mutant analyses revealed that the metabolism pathways of PI and phospholipids rank as the top three significant pathways closely linked to fiber elongation (Wanjie et al., 2005; Gou et al., 2007; Zhu et al., 2021). Accordingly, phosphatidylinositol (mono)‐phosphates are remarkably enriched in fast‐elongating fibers, which contain about fourfold higher amounts of PI phosphates (PIPs) than cotton ovules (Long et al., 2018). PIPs showed unique function of stimulating fiber growth when they were applied on in vitro cultured ovules, whereas another five classes of glycerophospholipids (GPLs) based on head group identities lacked this ability (Liu et al., 2015). PIPs are synthesized by the PIP kinases using PI as the immediate precursor, in which PI results from the reaction between cytidine diphosphate‐diacylglycerol and inositol‐phosphate catalyzed by PI synthase (PIS) (Munnik and Nielsen, 2011; Champeyroux et al., 2020; Colin and Jaillais, 2020). Therefore, the mutants of β‐ketoacyl‐CoA synthase gene (GhKCS), fatty acid desaturase gene (GhFAD), PIS and PI kinase (PIK) in the PIP synthesis pathway displayed similar defects in PIPs production and the failure of fiber elongation when they were silenced in cotton (Liu et al., 2015; Xiao et al., 2016; Long et al., 2018). These results together highlight that the accumulation of PIPs in elongating fibers is essential for fiber cell growth.

PIPs are imported into fiber cells from the underlying seed coat cells by PIP transporters such as GhLTPG1 (glycosylphosphatidylinositol‐anchored lipid transfer protein 1) across the plasma membrane (Deng et al., 2016). GhLTPG1 belongs to a small subclass of the lipid transfer protein (LTP) family with a glycophosphatidylinositol (GPI)‐anchored domain in cotton. The GhLTPG1 homologs in Arabidopsis, LTPG1 and LTPG2, which are specifically expressed in the developing lateral root primordium or the epidermal cells of young shoots and leaves, are involved in transporting very long chain fatty acids and their derivatives onto the cell surface (Kim et al., 2012; Uemura et al., 2023). The Arabidopsis ltpg mutants exhibit the phenotypes of retarded cell expansion including delayed lateral root growth or enlarged pollen tube (Lee et al., 2009). In cotton, GhLTPG1 possesses the ability of binding and transporting PI monophosphates (PI3P, PI4P) and phosphatidic acid (PA) into fiber cells (Deng et al., 2016). Knockdown of the GhLTPG1 gene in cotton significantly reduced the fiber length, decreased the content of PIPs, and repressed the expression levels of fiber elongation‐related genes. GhLTPG1 is highly and specifically expressed in seed epidermal cells and fiber cells, keeping pace with the requirement of increasing PIPs in elongating cotton fibers. The activities of LTPGs in transporting PIPs are totally different from PI transfer proteins (PITPs), another kind of LTPs, which are responsible for distributing phospholipids to intracellular organelles in plant cells (Routt and Bankaitis, 2004; Huang et al., 2016). In Arabidopsis, PITPs are spatially and strictly controlled by the regulatory proteins to achieve cell polarity, organelle functions and cell development in response to developmental cues and external stimuli (Routt and Bankaitis, 2004; Huang et al., 2016). Considering the high relevancies of their protein structures, we thus want to explore the transcriptional regulatory mechanism of GhLTPG1 in fiber development (Montag et al., 2020).

Here, we report the identification of GhMYB30D04 which specifically regulates GhLTPG1 expression, and promotes PIP accumulation in elongating fiber. More importantly, we show that GhMYB30D04 can directly interact with the fiber initiation transcription factor, GhHD1, to increase GhLTPG1 transcripts. Since GhHD1 has a START domain with phospholipid binding ability, PIPs binding to GhHD1 can consolidate the localization of GhHD1–GhMYB30D04 in the nucleus. These regulatory processes compose a delicate regulatory loop from the GhLTPG1 expression, then PIPs transportation, finally to stabilize the transactivation complex, which probably facilitates the transition from fiber initiation to fiber elongation.

RESULTS

GhMYB30D04 transcriptionally activates GhLTPG1 gene

Given that GhLTPG1 is essential for PIPs accumulation and quick fiber elongating (Deng et al., 2016) (Figure S1), we employed published RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) datasets to comprehensively capture the underlying co‐expression networks to mine the transcriptional regulators of GhLTPG1 gene. Analysis of differentially expressed genes in fiber elongation gave us a total of 214 co‐expressed genes (Pearson correlation coefficient >0.8) (Figure S2; Table S1). We further performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis to identify the functional relevance of these genes. These co‐expressed genes were enriched in the pathways of fatty acid biosynthesis and PIP signaling, reflecting the importance of PIPs in the fiber elongating process (Figures S3, S4).

Among all the genes co‐expressed with the GhLTPG1 gene, 16 transcription factors (TFs) had high Pearson correlation coefficients (Table S1). We thus employed a luciferase reporter system to systemically check which TF could activate the GhLTPG1 expression, and found that a MYB gene Ghi_D04G02406, named as GhMYB30D04 (Figure S5A–C), markedly activated GhLTPG1 expression, which most likely serves as the transcriptional regulator of GhLTPG1 (Figure 1A). We then investigated the activation effect of GhMYB30D04 using a dual‐luciferase (dual‐LUC) system. The promoter region of GhLTPG1 was fused upstream of the LUC gene to construct the reporters. The Renilla (REN) gene in the reporter construct was used as the internal control. The LUC/REN ratio was significantly higher when reporters and effector (GhMYB30D04) were co‐transformed into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves (Figure 1B). To explore the precise binding site of GhMYB30D04, we searched the conserved cis‐elements in the GhLTPG1 promoter online by PlantCARE software (Figure S6), and demonstrated them by yeast one‐hybridization assay (Y1H). The analysis showed that GhMYB30D04 bound with two MYB cis‐elements (M2 and M5 regions) in the GhLTPG1 promoter (Figure 1C). Reassuringly, we applied chromatin immunoprecipitation – quantitative polymerase chain reaction (ChIP‐qPCR) to verify that the p2 and p5 fragments are the direct binding targets of GhMYB30D04 (Figure 1D, E). Collectively, these results demonstrate that GhMYB30D04 regulates GhLTPG1 expression via directly binding its MYB cis‐elements.

Subcellular localization assay revealed that GhMYB30D04 protein localized to the nucleus (Figure S5D). Transcriptional activity by yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) assay indicated that GhMYB30D04 is a transcriptional activator (Figure S5E). We also determined that GhMYB30D04 is highly expressed throughout fiber elongation with the peak level in cotton fibers at 3 DPA (Figure 1G), sharing similar expression profiles with GhLTPG1 (Figure S1). RNA in situ hybridization showed that GhMYB30D04 is strongly expressed in fiber cells and the outer epidermis of cotton ovules (Figure 1F), in agreement with the spatial positions of GhLTPG1 expression. Taken together, GhMYB30D04 directly binds to the GhLTPG1 promoter and activates its expression.

Knockout of GhMYB30D04 gene in cotton inhibits fiber elongation and the accumulation of PIPs

To examine the regulatory function of GhMYB30D04 in fiber development, we successfully generated GhMYB30D04 knockout plants by clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR‐associated nuclease 9 (Cas9) gene editing after PCR analysis and genomic sequencing (Figure S7). All the T0 transgenic plants were continuously self‐crossed until obtaining the corresponding homozygous T3 lines. Therefore, we selected two lines (KO‐2 and KO‐5) for later analysis. Null plants were the sergeant plants without gene editing.

Compared with the null plants, GhMYB30D04 mutants (KO‐2 and KO‐5) showed indistinguishable alteration in plant height and flowering date (Table S2). In contrast, GhMYB30D04 mutants (KO‐2, and KO‐5) had smaller cotton balls and shortened fiber lengths, which had 33.3%–39.1%, 43.5%–45.2%, 40.8%–41.5%, 40.0%–43.8%, and 27.1%–31.3% reduction of fiber lengths during fiber elongation (3, 6, 9, 12 DPA and mature) (Figure 2D–F), respectively, compared with null plants. Electron microscopy scanning indicated no detectable difference in the number of initiated fiber cells (0 DPA) in GhMYB30D04 mutants (KO‐2) in comparison with the null control (Figure S8, S9), while fiber elongation was notably suspended since 3 DPA (Figure 2B, C).

We further tested the effect of GhMYB30D04 knockout on the yield and quality of mature cotton fiber for 2 consecutive years, 2021 and 2022, in the field. Compared to the null plants, the lint indexes of GhMYB30D04 mutants (KO‐2 and KO‐5) were decreased 13.0%–21.0% and 10.3%–15.3% in 2021 and 2022, respectively, and the lint percentages were correspondingly reduced 10.6%–16.3% and 7.3%–8.8% (Table 1). Similarity, other important traits of cotton mature fiber such as fiber strength, fiber elongation rate, micronaire and uniformity index showed obvious reductions in the GhMYB30D04 mutants (Table 2). More importantly, the striking decreases in fiber upper half mean length (4.8%–5.0% in 2021; 9.5%–10.9% in 2022) and elongation rates (29.8%–30.1% in 2021; 19.6%–20.3% in 2022) were observed in GhMYB30D04 mutants (KO‐2, and KO‐5) respectively compared with the null plants (Table 2). These results indicate that knockout of GhMYB30D04 in cotton could inhibit fiber cell elongation, especially during rapid fiber elongation, resulting in reduced fiber yield and quality.

Reverse transcription (RT)‐qPCR showed that, compared to the null plants, GhMYB30D04 knockout caused a significant reduction in GhLTPG1 abundance (Figure 2A), and fewer transcripts of genes related to fiber cytoskeleton development (ACT1, FLA1, and FLA2), cell expansion (EXP1, and RDL1) and cell wall synthesis (CESA1) (Figure 2H). In addition, the PI and PIP (PI3P and PI4P) contents were noticeably lower in the elongating fibers (6 and 9 DPA) of GhMYB30D04 mutants (Figure 2G). To test the effect of GhLTPG1 silencing on PIP transport, we applied PIPs (PI3P and PI4P) in liquid Beasley and Ting (BT) medium of ovule culture in vitro. After 7 d growth, the fiber lengths of GhMYB30D04 mutants were recovered by 22.8% compared to the control without PIP addition, but remained shorter than the null plants (Figure S10A, B). In conclusion, these results indicate that repressed GhMYB30D04 expression decreases GhLTPG1 transcription and consequently reduces the PI and PIP accumulations in cotton fibers.

Overexpression of GhMYB30D04 in cotton promotes fiber elongation and PIP contents in cotton fiber

To further determine whether the alteration in fiber length is due to the transcriptional changes of GhLTPG1, we generated stably transformed gain‐of‐function cotton plants. The homozygous lines overexpressing GhMYB30D04 gene were obtained using the same pipelines of constructing GhMYB30D04 mutants. In short, two GhMYB30D04 overexpressed lines (OE‐7 and OE‐8) were used for further analysis, while the homozygotes of negative segregates were used as the null controls (Figure S11).

Compared with the null control, GhLTPG1 expression and the transcriptions of genes related to fiber cytoskeleton development (ACT1, FLA1, and FLA2), cell expansion (EXP1 and RDL1) and cell wall development (CESA1) were significantly upregulated in GhMYB30D04 overexpressed plants (OE‐7 and OE‐8) in whole stages of fiber elongation (0, 3, 6, 9 and 12 DPA) (Figure 3A, H). Accordingly, the contents of PI and PIPs (PI3P and PI4P) were obviously increased in the quick‐elongating fibers (6, 9 DPA) of GhMYB30D04‐OE lines compared with the null plants (Figure 3G). We thus added the PI inhibitor 5‐hydroxytryptamine (5‐HT) in the liquid BT medium of ovule culture in vitro. After 7 d cultivation, the fiber lengths were noticeably reduced 52.5% in the GhMYB30D04‐OE line (OE‐7) compared to the untreated ovules, but longer than the null control (Figure S10C, D). The phenotypes of GhMYB30D04 overexpressed plants in GhLTPG1 transcripts and phospholipid accumulations are opposite from those in gbmyb30d04 mutants.

To test the effect of GhMYB30D04 overexpression on fiber development, we used electron microscopy scanning to observe fiber initials and elongating fibers. GhMYB30D04‐OE lines (OE‐7) had no difference in the number of fiber initials at 0 DPA in comparison with null plants (Figures S12, S13), indicating that GhMYB30D04 does not regulate fiber initiation. In contrast, fiber lengths of GhMYB30D04‐OE lines were quickly raised since 3 DPA, showing 35.8% increments than the null plants (Figure 3B, C).

We further tested mature fiber yield and quality of GhMYB30D04‐OE lines (OE‐7 and OE‐8) and the null controls in 2021 and 2022. Compared with the null plants, the GhMYB30D04‐OE lines (OE‐7 and OE‐8) showed a 29.1%–43.9% and 38.7%–63.8% increase in lint index in 2021 and 2022, respectively, as well as a 12.9%–14.7% and 14.6%–25.4% increase in lint percentage, respectively (Table 3). In addition, fiber strength and fiber upper half mean length of the GhMYB30D04‐OE lines (OE‐7 and OE‐8) also showed obvious elevation compared to the null plants, with significant increase in fiber elongation rate by 38.6%–39.2% in 2021 and 7.3%–10.4% in 2022 (Table 4). Taken together, these results indicate that GhMYB30D04 positively regulates GhLTPG1 expression and phospholipid accumulation in fiber cells and thus promotes fiber quality.

GhMYB30D04 interacts with GhHD1

Homeodomain‐leucine zipper (HD‐ZIP) IV protein GhHOX3 is a core regulator of fiber elongation (Shan et al., 2014), while GhHD1 functions as the crucial TF of the fiber initiation (Walford et al., 2012). Given the importance of HD‐Zip Ⅳ TFs in cotton fiber development, we chose the representatives of HD‐Zip Ⅳ family (GhHOX3, GhANL2, GhGL2, and GhHD1) to analyze their interactions with GhMYB30D04 (Figure S14). After testing protein–protein interaction by pairs, we identified that only GhHD1 interacted with GhMYB30D04 (Figures 4A, S15). We further divided GhHD1 protein into different segments (HD, ZLZ, START, SAD and START–SAD) to search the key domain of protein interaction (Figure S16). The Y2H results showed that the protein interaction occurred between the START–SAD domain of GhHD1 and the transcriptional activation domain of GhMYB30D04 (Figure 4A, B). Meanwhile, GhHD1 could form homodimers by itself through the ZLZ domain (Figure S17).

After confirming their interaction in vitro, we further tested whether GhHD1 and GhMYB30D04 can interact in plants. As shown in Figure 4C, strong yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) signals were observed in tobacco leaves co‐expressing GhHD1‐nYFP and GhMYB30D04‐cYFP, but were invisible in the control group. More importantly, the pull‐down assay demonstrated that glutathione S‐transferase (GST)‐labeled GhMYB30D04 protein could bind with GhHD1–MBP (maltose‐binding protein) fusion protein (Figure 4D). Taking these observations together, we concluded that GhMYB30D04 directly interacts with GhHD1, forming a transcriptional complex in cotton.

GhHD1 upregulates GhLTPG1 transcription driven by GhMYB30D04

To explore the biological function of GhHD1–GhMYB30D04 complex, we first analyzed whether GhHD1 can bind to the promoter of GhLTPG1 and activate it alone using Y1H and dual‐LUC assays. The results indicated that GhHD1 could bind to the L1‐box in the GhLTPG1 promoter (Figure 4E) but could not activate its expression (Figure 4F). Given that GhLTPG1 expression was greatly reduced in the cotton fibers of GhHD1‐KO lines (Figures S18, S19), we deduced that GhHD1 upregulates GhLTPG1 transcription requiring GhMYB30D04. We then examined this hypothesis by the luciferase reporter assays. Tobacco leaf cells co‐expressing GhMYB30D04‐GFP (green fluorescent protein), GhHD1‐GFP and pLTPG1‐LUC showed significantly higher fluorescence intensity than the combination of GhMYB30D04‐GFP and pLTPG1‐LUC (Figure 4F–H). These results indicate that GhHD1 enhances the activity of GhMYB30D04 activating GhLTPG1.

To analyze whether enhanced expression of GhLTPG1 is due to the reciprocal regulation between GhHD1 and GhMYB30D04, we conducted Y1H, luciferase reporter, and dual‐LUC assays. The results indicated that GhHD1 was unable to bind and activate the GhMYB30D04 promoter even though it contains a L1‐box (Figure S20A, C, E). For GhMYB30D04, it could not bind to the GhHD1 promoter and transcriptionally activate GhHD1 either (Figure S20B, D, F). These results totally suggest that GhHD1 and GhMYB30D04 have no mutual transcriptional regulation, and the upregulated transcription of GhLTPG1 mainly results from their protein interaction.

To further validate this result, we overexpressed GhMYB30D04 gene in GhHD1‐KO plant (OE‐1, OE‐2, OE‐3) (Figure 4K), and GhLTPG1 expressions as well as their fiber lengths were thus significantly increased in fiber elongation (6, 9, and 12 DPA) compared to GhHD1‐KO plants (Figure 4L–N). These results totally indicate that GhHD1–GhMYB30D04 cooperatively regulates fiber elongation by activating GhLTPG1 expression.

START–SAD domain of GhHD1 is crucial for transactivation of GhHD1–GhMYB30D04 complex

Given that the START–SAD domain is indispensable for the interaction of GhHD1–GhMYB30D04, we subsequently analyzed the effects of domain deletion on GhLTPG1 expression. As shown in Figure 4F–H, the GhHD1 variant without START–SAD domain abolished the ability of enhancing GhLTPG1 expression when it co‐expressed with GhMYB30D04‐GFP and pGhLTPG1‐LUC in both luciferase reporter and dual‐LUC assays. Likewise, the GhHD1 variant without HD domain lost the ability of binding the GhLTPG1 promoter and enhancing the transcriptional activation of GhLTPG1 (Figures 4F–H, S21). These results suggest the both HD and START–SAD domains are required for the transactivation of the GhHD1–GhMYB30D04 complex.

Since both GhHD1 and GhMYB30D04 can bind to the GhLTPG1 promoter, we analyzed their binding sites for the space accessibility of protein interaction. We truncated GhLTPG1 promoter (1 Kb) based on the distribution of MYB motif and L1‐box to drive the LUC reporter (Figure S22A). Dual‐LUC assays showed that the region from −1,000 to −550 bp containing a L1‐box and a MYB cis‐element (M2 and M5) is crucial for sequential binding with GhHD1 and GhMYB30D04 (Figure S22B, C). Next, we verified this result by yeast three‐hybrid assay (Y3H). We mutated the L1‐box in the GhLTPG1 promoter and found that GhMYB30D04 was still able to bind the promoter of GhLTPG1, but the addition of GhHD1 did not enhance binding (Figure 4I). When we introduced GhHD1, GhMYB30D04 and GhLTPG1 promoter together in the Y3H system, the β‐galactosidase activity was the most among all the tested combinations (Figure 4J). Collectively, these results showed that the GhMYB30D04–GhHD1 complex co‐localized in the GhLTPG1 promoter to regulate its expression.

The phospholipid binding capability of GhHD1 influences subcellular localization and the transactivation function of the GhHD1–GhMYB30D04 complex

Given that phospholipid binding ability affects the subcellular localization and functions of HD protein (Munnik and Nielsen, 2011; Yao et al., 2013; Heilmann and Heilmann, 2015; Nakamura, 2017), we applied protein–lipid overlay membranes to analysis the phospholipid binding activity of GhHD1 in vitro. Incubation of GhHD1–GST fusion protein with phospholipid and PIP membranes respectively showed that GhHD1 protein bound to PI monophosphate PI3P, PI4P and PI5P, and PI (4,5) P2 and PI (3,5) P2 (Figure 5A). In addition, GhHD1 had the ability of binding PA and PS, and cardiolipin (Figure 5A). The GhHD1 binding specificity of phospholipids is consisted with that of GhLTPG1 (Deng et al., 2016).

We further point‐mutated the conserved amino acid for binding phospholipids in GhHD1 (Figure S23) and analyzed its subcellular localization (GhHD1W456L). Confocal microscopy observation showed that the GhHD1 protein was localized in the nucleus, whereas the signals of GhHD1W456L‐GFP were distributed in cell membrane and cytoplasm (Figure 5B). Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) further showed that GhHD1W456L lost the interaction with GhMYB30D04 (Figure 5C). Since W456 locates in the START–SAD domain that is essential for GhHD1–GhMYB30D04 interaction and the transcriptional activation of GhLTPG1, we further analyzed the effect of point mutation on their functions. As we expected, GhHD1W456L abolished the ability of improving GhLTPG1 expression driven by GhMYB30D04, and the LUC/REN value in tobacco leaves expressing GhMYB30D04‐62SK and Pro GhLTPG1 ::LUC was the same as that expressing GhHD1W456L‐62SK, GhMYB30D04‐62SK, and Pro GhLTPG1 ::LUC together (Figure 5D). In conclusion, these results demonstrate that phospholipid binding activity affects the subcellular localization of GhHD1 and its interaction with GhMYB30D04 in enhancing the transcriptional activation of GhLTPG1.

The variation of MYB30D04 gene across cotton cultivated species contributes to the differences in LTPG1 expression and fiber length

Gossypium babardense and G. hirsutum are two cultivated species with distinctly different fiber lengths. We hypothesized that their fiber length difference may be linked to LTPG1 gene expression discrepancies in fiber development. Transcriptome‐wide association studies (TWAS) of CottonMD database was applied to determine whether the expressions of GhMYB30D04 and GhLTPG1 are associated with fiber traits in G. hirsutum. The results showed that the expressions of GhMYB30D04 and GhLTPG1 genes are significantly correlated with fiber length in G. hirsutum (Table S4), supporting the phenotype analysis of GhMYB30D04 mutant and its overexpressed plants (Figures 2, 3).

Comparative transcriptomic analysis showed that GbLTPG1, the GhLTPG1 allele in G. babardense, expressed higher and longer in the fibers of G. babardense variety Hai7124 than GhLTPG1 in those of G. hirsutum variety TM‐1 (Figures 6A, S24). Similarly, GbMYB30D04 expressed significantly higher and longer in Hai7124 than GhMYB30D04 in TM‐1 (Figures 6A, S24). In contrast to MYB30 and LTPG1 genes, the expression difference of HD1 between two species mainly appeared before 3 DPA (Figure S24). Given that GhLTPG1 expression is directly driven by GhMYB30D04, these results indicate that LTPG1 expression differences between two cotton species are primarily attributed to the regulation of MYB30.

We then investigated the variations of MYB30‐Dt/At gene sequences including their promoters and encoding regions between two allotetraploid genomes. Five single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and an insertion‐deletion (INDEL) were found in the MYB30 promoter (Figure S25). Specifically, two SNPs including a C–T and a G–C substitution resulted in novel cis‐elements (CArG, ARFAT) that are potentially recognized by MADS and ARF TF respectively to modulate GbMYB30D04 expression in G. babardense (Figure 6B). To evaluate the effects of these SNPs on fiber quality, we comprehensively analyzed the distributions of allele frequency across diverse cotton accessions. The allele frequencies of MYB30D04 were mostly different between two cotton species (Figure 6C, D). Two alleles displayed significant dominant effect and remarkable correlation with fiber length in G. hirsutum (Figure 6E, F). These results totally indicate that MYB30D04 gene variations have a noticeable effect on LTPG1 expression, and then influencing fiber length in cotton.

DISCUSSION

GhMYB30D04–GhHD1 protein complex controls phospholipid accumulation in elongating fibers

The accumulation of PIPs specifically and highly in elongating fibers is fundamental for cell membrane expansion and the polar growth of cotton fibers (Wanjie et al., 2005; Liu et al., 2015). The transcriptional regulator that shapes the expression of the PIP transporter gene, GhLTPG1, in response to the PIPs' requirements during fiber elongation remains unknown (Deng et al., 2016). Here we discovered that GhMYB30D04 functions in controlling GhLTPG1 expression by the interaction with GhHD1.

We carefully chose RNA‐seq datasets and co‐expression approach to search the regulator of GhLTPG1 (Table S1). Although many MYB TFs co‐expressed closely with GhLTPG1 (coefficient > 0.80), only GhMYB30D04 was characterized as the regulator of GhLTPG1 instead of GaMYB2, GhMYB109 and GhMYB25‐like protein, which are known to modulate fiber development (Figure 1) (Pu et al., 2008; Walford et al., 2011). The function of GhMYB30D04 was experimentally validated by luciferase reporter, dual‐LUC and Y1H assays, and we found that GhMYB30D04 binds to two MYB cis‐elements in the GhLTPG1 promoter (Figure 1A–E). Additionally, the GhMYB30D04 mutant and GhMYB30D04‐OE plants showed opposite phenotypes in GhLTPG1 expression, PIPs accumulation and fiber length (Figure 2, 3). Applying PIPs on the GhMYB30D04‐KO ovules in vitro restored their fiber elongation, while applying PI synthesis inhibitor 5‐HT to GhMYB30D04‐OE ovules significantly inhibited fiber elongation (Figure S10), illustrating that GhMYB30D04 positively regulates PIPs content and fiber growth by enhancing GhLTPG1 expression.

MIXTA or MIXTA‐like proteins, a clade of MYB family members, generally interact with the HD‐Zip Ⅳ TF to form a protein complex mediating the growth of glandular secretory trichomes growth in plants (Yan et al., 2018; Xiong et al., 2020; Xie et al., 2021). The similar regulatory mechanism between cotton fiber and glandular secretory trichome led us to screen the GhMYB30D04 partner, and we confirmed that GhMYB30D04 interacts with GhHD1 by pull‐down, Y2H and BiFC assays (Figure 4A–D). GhHD1 can bind the L1‐box element of GhLTPG1 promoter, the locus of which is 50 and 150 bp from the MYB cis‐elements recognized by GhMYB30D04, providing suitable physical space for two proteins binding jointly on the GhLTPG1 promoter (Figures 4E, S22). The START domain of GhHD1 has phospholipid binding capability, whereas the domain‐mutated GhHD1 lost nucleus‐localization capability (Figure 5A, B). These results suggest that PIP accumulation in fiber cells beneficially facilitates GhHD1 targeting to form a transactivation complex in the nucleus of the elongating fiber cell.

GhHD1 cannot directly activate GhLTPG1 expression, so the function of GhHD1 in enhancing GhLTPG1 expression depends on GhMYB30D04 (Figure 4F–H). Moreover, GhHD1 and GhMYB30D04 have no mutual transcriptional regulation activity (Figure S20). Therefore, GhMYB30D04 is the main regulator of GhLTPG1 expression in the GhHD1–GhMYB30D04 complex, while GhHD1 is the accessory protein. TWAS analysis revealed that persistently higher expression of LTPG1 in elongating fiber is mostly due to the variations of GhMYB30D04 between two cotton culturing species, which to some extent affects their fiber lengths (Table S4).

Based on our investigations, we propose a model of transcriptionally regulating phospholipid transport in fiber elongation (Figure 7). In this model, the low expression of GhMYB30D04 at the earliest stage of fiber elongation produces relatively low transcripts of GhLTPG1. With the promotion of PIP content in the fast‐growing fibers, GhHD1 protein promptly targets to the nucleus to form the complex GhMYB30D04–GhHD1. PIP accumulation reinforces this regulatory complex and then accelerates the expression of GhLTPG1, which in turn expedites the transport of PIPs and the lengthening of cotton fibers.

GhHD1 promotes the developmental transition from fiber initiation to elongation by interacting with different regulators

As demonstrated by the aforesaid investigations, GhMYB30D04 is the main regulator of the GhHD1–GhMYB30D04 complex in transcriptionally controlling GhLTPG1 expression, while GhHD1 acts as the enhancer of this regulation complex. GhMYB30D04 expresses from 1 DPA from 20 DPA, whereas GhHD1 highly expresses from −1 to 5 DPA in cotton (Figure S24). The overlapped expressing duration of both GhMYB30D04 and GhHD1 covers the onset stage of fiber elongation from 1 to 5 DPA. More importantly, GhHD1 is incapable of activating the expression of GhLTPG1 (Figure 4F–H). Overexpressed GhMYB30D04 in the GhHD1‐KO mutant could significantly enhance the fiber length (Figure 4M, N). All of these findings point to the importance of GhHD1 in establishing a signal converter from fiber initiation to fiber elongation.

In addition to GhMYB30D04, GhHD1 can interact with GhHOX3 and GhMYB25‐like proteins (Zhang et al., 2010; Walford et al., 2012; Shan et al., 2014). It is widely accepted that the module GhHD1–GhMYB25‐like controls the number of fiber initials by regulating the expressions of genes related to fiber initiation (Walford et al., 2011; Walford et al., 2012); the interactor GhHD1–GhHOX3 drives the expressions of target genes related to cell expansion such as EXPA1 and RDL1 in fiber elongation (Shan et al., 2014). These results prompted us to test whether GhMYB30D04 interacts with GhHOX3 to form a complex to drive fiber elongation. Contrary to our deduction, GhHOX3 does not interact with GhMYB30D04, suggesting that GhHD1–GhHOX3 and GhHD1–GhMYB30D04 may operate independently in fiber elongation (Figure S15).

Different from the signaling pathway activated by GhHD1–GhHOX3, GhMYB30D04–GhHD1 participate in the regulation of phospholipid transport through transcriptionally activating GhLTPG1 expression. This regulation mode was confirmed by the target region of GhLTPG1 promoter recognized by GhMYB30D04, and the exactly opposite phenotypes of PIPs accumulation and fiber length between GhMYB30D04‐OE and GhMYB30D04‐KO plants (Figures 2, 3). Given that the genes related to cell expansion like EXPA1 were also upregulated in GhMYB30D04‐OE plants (Figure 3H), whether GhHD1–GhHOX3 interactor responses to PIP change in cotton fibers is unclear. Hence, further characterization of the regulatory network integrated the signals of hormones and PIPs could help us to clearly solve this problem. In summary, our results signify a step toward unraveling the mechanism of transcriptional regulation of PIP transport in fiber development. The natural variations of MYB30D04 promoter between cotton species will be used to generate the cultivars with longer fiber through the directed‐mutation strategy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials and growth conditions

Cotton plants (G. hirsutum variety Jin668, Z37, Xuzhou142 (Xu142) and its fiber‐less mutant (Xufl)) were grown in the greenhouse at Shanghai Jiaotong University, Shanghai, China. Flowers on the day of blooming were tagged as 0 DPA, and the bolls were harvested every 3 d after 0 DPA. Cotton ovules were carefully separated from the bolls and the fibers were then gently collected from above ovules. All the samples were immediately stored in liquid nitrogen and later ground to powder in liquid nitrogen with a Tissuelyser‐192 (Jingxin, Shanghai) at 55.0 Hz for 60 s. The samples used in the analysis of ChIP‐qPCR and in situ hybridization were freshly fixed according to the described methods (Wang and Ruan, 2012; Zhang et al., 2014). N. benthamiana plants were cultivated in a growth chamber (16 h light and 8 h dark at 28°C) for protein subcellular localization and interaction assays.

Generation of transgenic cotton plants

To generate overexpression cotton plants, the full‐length GhMYB30D04 and GhLTPG1 genes were respectively cloned into the pWMV062 vector (WIMI, Jiangsu) under the control of CaMV35S promoter (pWMV062‐Pro35S:GhMYB30D04 or GhLTPG1). CRISPR technology was employed to create the GhMYB30D04 and GhHD1 knockout plants. The primers listed in Table S6 were used to amplify transfer RNA (tRNA) and guide RNA (gRNA) from the template pGTR vector. Finally, the PCR products were purified and inserted into the BsaI‐digested pRGEB32‐GhU6.9 vector using ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme) (Qin et al., 2022). The positive plasmids were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain LBA4404 for genetic transformation of cotton.

Gossypium hirsutum cv. Z37 and Jin668 seeds were respectively used as the transgenic receptor (Li et al., 2019). Cotton seeds were sterilized and cultured in a chamber without light for 6 d at 30°C (Song et al., 2023). The cotton transformation was performed using hypocotyl segments as explants according to the described method (Sun et al., 2019). The explants were immersed in an A. tumefaciens suspension after they were cut into 5‐mm segments. After callus induction, proliferation, embryogenic callus induction, embryo differentiation, and plantlet regeneration, the putative transgenic plants were grown in the greenhouse. The successful transformation and integrity of the transgene were confirmed by PCR analysis (Table S6). RT‐qPCR was used to detect GhMYB30D04 and GhLTPG1 expression levels in transgenic lines, and cotton ubiquitin was used as an internal control. Positive transgenic plants were self‐crossed for three generations to obtain homozygous T3 lines. To confirm the knockout plant, the targeted regions were amplified by PCR using site‐specific primers. We screened for the homozygotes by sequencing the sergeants from the same plants.

RNA extraction and RT‐qPCR analysis

Cotton RNA was isolated from samples using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (TIANGEN Biotech, Beijing, China). Total RNAs were reverse‐transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the HiScript III RT SuperMix and gDNA wiper (Vazyme Inc., Nanjing, China). Gene‐specific primers for RT‐qPCR analysis were designed using the online Primer‐BLAST tool (National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/). RT‐qPCR was performed on a Roche 480 PCR System using Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). The cotton ubiquitin gene was used as the reference for normalization (Walford et al., 2011). Transcriptional changes in gene expression were calculated based on the comparative ΔCT method (Pfaffl, 2001). Three biological replicates were used for each sample, with three technical replicates. Primer sequence information is shown in Table S6.

Fiber development investigation

Cotton ovules (−1, 0 and 3 DPA) were isolated and directly observed by scanning electron microscope under cold vacuum (SEM; TCS SP8 STED 3X; Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Cotton ovules at different developmental stages (3, 6, 9 or 12 DPA, and mature) were collected to analyze fiber length. The ovules were immersed in 95°C water for 5 min to relax the fiber. Fibers were imaged and fiber length was calculated using the ImageJ program (http://rsb.info.nih.gov/ij/) (Xu et al., 2012).

Fiber quality analysis

Healthy mature bolls were harvested during the 2021 and 2022 growing seasons. The collected bolls were dried at 25°C with low humidity for 1 week and used for fiber quality analysis. Fiber yield and quality index analysis were performed according to the principles reported by Zhang et al. (2011). Lint index (fiber weight per 100 seeds in grams), seed index (seed weight per 100 seeds in grams) and lint percentage were calculated based on 50 bolls. Fiber quality parameters were calculated using a high‐capacity fiber testing system (Premier HFT 9000; Premier Evolvics Pvt. Ltd, Coimbatore, India) at the Cotton Fiber Quality Inspection and Testing Center, Ministry of Agriculture, China (Anyang City, Henan Province, China). All experiments were replicated at least three times.

In situ hybridization

Sense and anti‐sense probes were prepared using the DIG RNA Labeling Kit (SP6/T7) (Roche, Mannheim, Germany). Cotton ovules (3 DPA) were fixed with formaldehyde‐acetic acid‐ethanol fixative, dehydrated (50%–100% ethanol) and embedded in paraffin. Hybridization assays were performed according to the enhanced sensitive In situ Hybridization detection kit (AP) (BOSTER, Wuhan, China). Signals were observed with an Olympus BX51 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Subcellular localization

The coding region of GhMYB30D04, GhHD1 and GhHD1W456L (mutations in phospholipid binding sites) were cloned into the pEarlyGate101 vector to generate CaMV35S::GhMYB30D04, GhHD1 or GhHD1W456L YFP expression cassettes. Cultures of A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 carrying constructs expressing different YFP fusion proteins and P19 were mixed in a 1:1 ratio and introduced into N. benthamiana leaves by infiltration. After 2 d of infiltration, the subcellular localization of the proteins was analyzed by confocal laser microscopy (TCP SP5; Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). YFP was excited with a 488‐nm laser (emission, 500–535 nm).

Yeast one‐hybrid assay

For the Y1H assay, the open reading frames (ORFs) of GhMYB30D04 and GhHD1 were amplified using KOD‐plus DNA polymerase (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) and then cloned into pB42AD (EcoRI/XhoI) using ClonExpress II (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The full‐length promoter of the GhLTPG1 gene and fragments of different MYB cis‐elements were inserted into pLacZ2u. The analyses were promoted using the yeast EGY48a strain as previously described and empty vectors were used as negative controls. The synthetic dropout (SD)/‐Thr/‐Ura medium without and with X‐gal were used in the assay (Chen et al., 2017). All sequences of the promoter segments are listed in Table S6.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation‐qPCR assay

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assays were performed according to the instructions of the EpiQuik Plant ChIP Kit (Epigentek, Farmingdale, NY, USA). The GhMYB30D04 bound DNA was used as the template for qPCR; IP/input (%) was calculated by comparing Ct values between immune‐precipitation and input. The negative control NC1 fragment in the 5′ untranslated region and NC2 fragment in the coding region of the candidate gene were used as negative controls.

Dual‐luciferase assay

The 1 kb promoters of GhLTPG1, GhMYB30D04 and GhHD1 genes were amplified from cotton genomic DNA using the primers listed in Table S6. The reporter constructs were generated by inserting the promoters into pGreen II 0800‐LUC vector respectively, while the REN luciferase driven by CaMV35S promoter on the same vector was used as the reference to calibrate transfection efficiency (Hellens et al., 2005). The coding sequences of GhMYB30D04 and GhHD1 were respectively cloned into the 62SK vector downstream of the CaMV35S promoter to produce the effector. The constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium GV3101 with the helper plasmid pSoup‐P19 to avoid gene silencing. Four‐week‐old N. benthamiana leaves were inoculated with Agrobacterium. After 2–3 d, the promoter activity was analyzed by the previously described method (Sun et al., 2019). For each dual‐LUC activity assay with a combination of promoter‐protein, more than six leaves from three different tobacco plants were used for each experiment and three independent experiments were performed.

In vitro pull‐down assay

The GhMYB30D04 and GhHD1 genes were ligated to expression vectors pDEST‐565 and pDEST‐566, respectively, and the positive vectors were introduced into the Escherichia coli strain BL21 (WeiDi, Shanghai, China). GhMYB30D04‐GST and GhHD1‐MBP proteins were induced with the addition of 0.5 mmol/L IPTG (isopropyl β‐D‐1‐thiogalactopyranoside) when the bacterium grew at 37°C. The fusion proteins were purified using GSTSep Glutathione Agarose Resin and MBPSep Dextrin Agarose Resin according to the kit protocol (YEASEN, Shanghai, China). The purified GhHD1‐MBP protein was incubated with glutathione sepharose (GE Healthcare, Marlborough, MA, USA) and GhMYB30D04‐GST fusion proteins, or the control GST protein for 2–3 h at 4°C, respectively. After that, the beads were washed for three times with 1×phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS). Proteins were then eluted from the beads with 100 μL elution buffer (1×PBS, 10 mmol/L glutathione, 50 mmol/L Tris‐HCl, pH 8.0). The eluted proteins were then loaded onto sodium dodecyl sulfide ‐ polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis for western blotting. Gel blots were analyzed using an anti‐GST antibody (working dilution 1:2,000; Abcam, UK) and anti‐MBP antibody (working dilution 1:2,000; Abcam, UK), and the empty GST tag was used as the control.

Yeast two‐hybrid assay

The Y2H Gateway™ adapted vectors pGBKT7 and pGADT7 were used. Full‐length cDNA and truncated fragments of the GhMYB30D04 gene (GhMYB30D04‐N/DNA‐binding domain, GhMYB30D04‐C/activation domain) were cloned into the pGADT7 vector to generate constructs expressing prey proteins. The full‐length GhHD1 and truncated fragments (HD, ZLZ, START, SAD, START–SAD) were cloned into the pGBKT7 vector to generate binding domain vectors. The bait and prey constructs were co‐transformed into yeast AH109 cells via the PEG/LiAc transformation method (Gietz, 2006). After incubation for 3–4 d at 30°C, single positive yeast cells were picked and incubated in a liquid SD medium lacking Leu and Trp until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was 0.6. Yeast cells were screened on SD‐Trp/‐Leu/‐His/‐Ade plates and incubated at 30°C for 3–5 d for the test of protein interaction. The primer sequences used for the plasmid construction are listed in Table S6.

Bimolecular fluorescent complementation analysis

For the BiFC assay, the coding sequences of GhMYB30D04, GhHD1 and GhHD1 W456L without stop codon were cloned into pEarlyGate201 or pEarlyGate202 vectors, respectively, and fused to the N‐ or C‐terminal region of YFP. Expression of the target gene alone was used as a negative control. Agrobacterium cells carrying the indicated vector were resuspended in infiltration buffer containing 10 mmol/L MgCl2, 10 mmol/L 2‐[N‐morpholino]ethanesulfonic acid and 150 μmol/L acetosyringone to a final concentration of OD600 = 0.5. Agrobacterium cultures were then mixed 1:1 and infiltrated into 2‐week‐old N. benthamiana leaves. Fluorescence of the recombinant YFP was observed on tobacco leaves after 2 d of infiltration using a TCS SP5 confocal laser scanning microscope.

In vitro cotton ovule culture

Cotton bolls at 1 DPA were collected from plants and sterilized in 30% H2O2 (v/v) for 15 min, followed by washing twice with sterilized distilled water. The bolls were dried in the air for 5 min, and then the ovules were gently removed from the sterilized bolls under bacterium‐free conditions. The separated unwound ovules were cultured in BT medium at 28°C in darkness, as described by Beasley and Ting (1973). For PIP, 5‐HT (PI synthesis inhibitor) treatment assays, the ovules were cultured at 28°C in BT medium supplemented with single or combinations of PIP (0.5 mg/L) and 5‐HT (0.5 mg/L). Ovules cultured in BT medium without treatment were used as a control. The average fiber length was calculated using 15 ovules of each line.

Fatty acid and PI content analysis

A total of 100 mg freeze‐dried cotton fibers was immersed in 2 mL isopropyl alcohol at 85°C for 30 min, and then 3 mL n‐hexane was added (Liu et al., 2015). The fatty acids were extracted in 2 mL methanol after 1.5 h incubation at 90°C. C17:0 fatty acid (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) used as an internal standard was added before fatty acid extraction. All the samples were analyzed by Thermo Scientific TRACE GC Ultra (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). A standard curve was drawn using external fatty acid and PI standards (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) to test the stability of the gas chromatography (GC) system and qualitatively analyze the contents of different fatty acids and PIPs. Relative content of each fatty acid and PIP was carried out by comparing peak areas with internal chemical standards. All samples were analyzed for three biological repeats.

Protein–lipid overlay assay

The GhHD1 gene was ligated to the expression vector pDEST‐565, and the recombinant plasmids were introduced into the Escherichia coli strain BL21 (WeiDi, Shanghai, China). GhHD1‐GST protein was induced with the addition of 0.5 mmol/L IPTG when the bacterium grew at 37°C. The GhHD1‐GST fusion proteins were purified using GST‐Sep Glutathione Agarose Resin (YEASEN, Shanghai, China) according to the kit protocol. Lipids were dissolved in chloroform, and 10 μg each of the lipids was then immobilized and air‐dried on Nitrocellulose Membrane (Pall Corporation, New York, NY, USA). Lipid membranes were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (w/v) in PBST solution (4 mmol/L NaH2PO4, 16 mmol/L Na2HPO4, 150 mmol/L NaCl, 0.1% Tween‐20) at room temperature for 1 h, and then incubated with GhHD1‐GST protein in blocking buffer at 4°C for 12 h. The protein–lipid overlay membrane was washed three times, for more than 10 min in PBST solution (Dowler et al., 2002; Han et al., 2020). After that, the bound proteins were immunoblotted with anti‐GST antibody, and then detected with horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated anti‐mouse secondary antibodies.

Transcriptome analysis

Gene expression data of ovules and fibers of Hai7124 (G. babardense) and TM‐1 (G. hirsutum) at different developmental periods (−3, 0, 1, 3, 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25 DPA) were downloaded from the NCBI database (Sequence Read Archive (SRA): PRJNA490626) (Hu et al., 2019). The SRA data were converted to the Fastq format using the SRA toolkit with the ‐split‐3 parameter. Data quality control was checked by FastQC likewise trimmed for linked adaptors and bad quality reads by Trimmomatic 0.33 (Bolger et al., 2014). The RNA‐seq clean data from accessions were mapped to the TM‐1 reference genome (https://www.cottongen.org/) using Hisat2 (v 2.1.2) with default parameters. Gene expression level was normalized using the number of fragments per kilo‐base of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) by StringTie software (v 1.3.5) with default settings (Pertea et al., 2016). The co‐expression network was obtained by calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient of pairwise gene expression levels, and the gene modules including the gene pairs with a Pearson coefficient of larger than 0.8 retained as a co‐expression network.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or a two‐tailed Student's t‐test with software Microsoft Excel. Error bars represent the SD from three biological replicates of three parallel experiments.
