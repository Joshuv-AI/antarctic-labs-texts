Development of Brain Penetrant Pyridazine Pantothenate Kinase Activators
Authors: Rajendra Tangallapally, Chitra Subramanian, Mi-Kyung Yun, Anne Edwards, Lalit Kumar Sharma, Lei Yang, Katie Creed, Jina Wang, Suzanne Jackowski, Charles O Rock, Stephen W White, Richard E Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractConversion of pantothenate to phosphopantothenate in humans is the first dedicated step in the coenzyme A (CoA) biosynthesis pathway and is mediated by four isoforms of pantothenate kinase. These enzymes are allosterically regulated by acyl-CoA levels, which control the rate of CoA biosynthesis. Small molecule activators of the PANK enzymes that overcome feedback suppression increase CoA levels in cultured cells and animals and have shown great potential for the treatment of pantothenate kinase-associated neurodegeneration and propionic acidemias. In this study, we detail the further optimization of PANK pyridazine activators using structure-guided design and focus on the cellular CoA activation potential, metabolic stability, and solubility as the primary drivers of the structure–activity relationship. These studies led to the prioritization of three late-stage preclinical lead PANK modulators with improved pharmacokinetic profiles and the ability to substantially increase brain CoA levels. Compound 22 (BBP-671) eventually advanced into clinical testing for the treatment of PKAN and propionic acidemia.

Introduction

Coenzyme A (CoA) is an essential cellular cofactor that carries carboxylic acid substrates in key metabolic pathways, including the citric acid cycle, sterol biosynthesis, amino acid metabolism, and fatty acid biosynthesis and oxidation.1 Acetyl-CoA is the most abundant CoA thioester and is a central metabolic intermediate that also acts as a second messenger.2 The pantothenate kinases (PANK, EC 2.7.1.33) phosphorylate pantothenate (vitamin B5) in the first and rate-limiting step of the CoA biosynthetic pathway.3,4 Three genes encode four PANK isoforms in humans, PANK1α, -1β, -2, and -3, and the isoform expression levels in different tissues, together with potent feedback inhibition by CoA thioesters, control the intracellular CoA concentrations.5,6 Unsurprisingly, CoA deficiencies have serious negative consequences in humans. Most notably, mutations in PANK2 directly cause pantothenate kinase-associated neurodegeneration (PKAN), and mutations in propionyl-CoA carboxylase (PCCA) indirectly cause propionic acidemia.7,8

PKAN, formerly called Hallervorden-Spatz syndrome, is an autosomal recessive movement disorder with onset in childhood and characterized by neurodegeneration, progressive parkinsonism, and brain iron accumulation (NBIA).9 PKAN accounts for ∼50–70% of NBIA cases,10 and recent clinical trials using iron chelation11 or phosphopantothenate replacement therapies did not significantly reverse or attenuate disease progression.12 PKAN patients have few therapeutic options, although other approaches are currently under consideration (https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT04182763). We previously reported compound 1 [PZ-2891 (Figure 1A)] as an allosteric activator of PANK3 that counteracts allosteric inhibition by acetyl-CoA.13,14 Significantly, 1 increases the level of CoA in cells, crosses the blood–brain barrier, and increases the level of CoA in the brains of mice. PANK3 is the primary target as it is expressed ubiquitously in all tissues, including the brain.6 Most importantly, 1 rescues a conditional PKAN mouse model with CoA deficiency in neurons and corrects the growth defect, improves the movement disorder, and extends survival substantially. The development of 1 was limited by its rapid metabolism, which led to the generation of cyclopropyl analogue 2 (Figure 1A) with increased metabolic stability. We recently demonstrated that 2 is a potential therapeutic for the treatment of acidemias by increasing CoA levels and relieving the sequestration of CoA caused by mutations in PCCA that lead to the accumulation of branched acyl-CoA esters.8

Despite their proven efficacies, 1 and 2 both suffer from metabolic liabilities, including the potential for glutathione conjugation,15 and low solubility. In this study, we explore the further optimization of these initial leads using structure-guided design that focuses on cellular CoA activation potential, increased metabolic stability, and solubility as the primary drivers of the structure–activity relationship (SAR), rather than biochemical inhibition (PANK3 IC50) and LipE that were used to identify 1.14 Within the series, the compounds typically function as orthosteric inhibitors at high potencies (PANK3 IC50s of <2 nM) and, as desired, as allosteric activators at intermediate potencies (PANK3 IC50s of 2–20 nM). In this study, SARs are generated around four primary functional group modifications (FG1–FG4) as outlined in Figure 1B, while the key pyridazine and amide functional groups that we have shown to be pivotal to the activity and binding of this compound class are maintained.14 FG1 modifications introduce changes to the alkyl side chain. FG2 modifications target the phenyl ring, and FG3 modifications allow examination of the methylation of the piperazine linker. FG4 is either a cyano or a chloro. A representative compound from each modification class was evaluated by X-ray crystallography to understand the differences in target engagement and aid further design. The pharmacokinetics of the emerging leads was tested by oral gavage, and their ability to effectively increase the concentrations of CoA in the liver and brain was determined. These studies led to the discovery of potent PANK activators with improved pharmacokinetic properties and in vivo efficacy and significantly enhanced therapeutic potential.

Results and Discussion

FG1 SAR: Small Alkyl Side Chains

Variations of small alkyl side chain FG1, which metabolic identification studies had indicated as the major site of metabolism for 1, were analyzed. First, hydroxy metabolite 3 was generated. It was found to weakly increase CoA levels in C3A cells and was less potent in our biochemical PANK3 assay (Table 1). Compound 3 showed improved metabolic stability and increased solubility. Next, the FG4 chloro versions of 1 and 2, compounds 4 and 5, respectively, were examined and exhibited increased biochemical potency but decreased microsomal stability probably due to increased lipophilicity and consequent potential for FG1 oxidation. 4 is extremely potent (IC50 of 0.22 nM) and functions as an orthosteric inhibitor at higher concentrations, a property that is not desired in this study; it was also noted to be very rapidly metabolized in liver microsomes presumably at the isopropyl motif given the greater stability of 5.

Next, a set of FG1 fluorinated analogues 6–11 designed to block oxidative metabolism at this position were generated (Table 1). Within this subset, analogues with branched isopropyl-like motifs were the most potent binders (6, 7, and 12), as these functional groups engage a region of the PANK binding site that has evolved to bind the gem–dimethyl motif of pantothenate. Trifluoro-isopropyl analogues 6 and 7 were highly potent, functioned as orthosteric inhibitors, had poor solubilities, and did not offer an improvement in microsomal stability. Unbranched analogues 8–11 had higher IC50 values, particularly trifluoromethoxy analogues 10 and 11, which were too weak as binders to act as activators. Trifluoroethyl analogues 8 and 9 were good activators but had metabolic stabilities and solubilities similar to those of 1 and 2, disfavoring their advancement. Monofluorination of 1 resulted in 12, which was a comparatively weaker PANK3 inhibitor but a stronger activator with increased metabolic stability, suggesting it may be a suitable alternative motif. Next, the isopropyl group of 1 was exchanged with N,N-dimethyl amine analogue 13. However, 13 had weak biochemical potency but good solubility and metabolic stability. To improve the biochemical potency, FG4 chloropyridazine analogue 14 was generated, which improved the IC50 value into a desirable range and acted as a potent CoA activator improved solubility and microsomal stability compared to those of 1.

Structural studies were then used to rationalize the observed SAR (Figure 2). The PANK3·AMPPNP·Mg2+·1 dimer complex structure shows that the isopropyl group of 1 packs into a hydrophobic pocket created by I253′, A269′, V250′, Y254′, and Y258′ (the prime indicates residues in the monomer across the dimer interface), notably in the same location as the dimethyl group of pantothenate (Figure 2A).13 Similar to 1, the cyclopropyl group of 2 packs into a hydrophobic pocket as described above and adopts the preferred conformation.162 is accompanied by a water molecule that bridges G193 and the γ-phosphate of AMPPNP I253′ (Wat1) (Figure 2B). The R-enantiomer of 3 binds in a fashion very similar to that of 1 (Figure 2C and Table S1a), and the hydroxyl group occupies the Wat1 location observed in 2. The structure of compound 6 (Figure 2D) shows that both enantiomers can bind (36% R and 64% S). The trifluoromethyl group of the S-enantiomer interacts with the γ-phosphate of AMPPNP in the “up” conformation and packs with I253′, A269′, V250′, and Y254′. The trifluoromethyl group of the R-enantiomer binds in the “down” conformation and interacts with E138 and S195. Wat1 is present in both binding modes. The structure of compound 14 shows N-dimethyl FG1 is planar to the aromatic ring. This suboptimal conformation likely accounts for its weaker IC50 value (Figure 2E and Table S1b).

FG2 SAR: Phenyl Ring Modifications

The FG2 ring system is another potential site for the oxidative metabolism of 1 and 2. We synthesized compound 15, which represents a putative hydroxyphenyl metabolite. 15 exhibited a major loss of IC50 activity, encouraging our efforts to fortify this ring to block oxidative metabolism (Table 2).

Table 2

The potential of introducing a pyridyl nitrogen as a metabolic blocking group adjacent to FG1 was explored in 16 and 17. The introduction of the pyridyl motif increased both the solubility and the microsomal stability compared to those of direct isosteres 1 and 4, although the PANK3 IC50 values were higher than those of their phenyl counterparts. 17 was a strong CoA activator but suffered from faster metabolism. 18 was designed for investigation of the introduction of a fluorine on the pyridyl ring to reduce the basicity of 16, and this modification increased the CoA activation potency and maintained the good metabolic stability of 16. Various ring phenyl fluorinations of 1 and 2 and their FG4 chloro derivatives in compounds were investigated [19–26 (Table 2)]. The ring phenyl fluorination of 1 and 2 in compounds 19–26 increased the lipophilicity of the compounds, and the solubilities of these analogues were lower than those of their corresponding isosteres. Introduction of a single fluorine adjacent to the FG1 isopropyl or cyclopropyl motif did not improve the metabolic stability in most cases. The biochemical potency of isopropyl analogs 19 and 20 was higher, while 21–25 cyclopropyl analogues were potent CoA activators. The introduction of a fluorine meta to FG1 in compounds 23 and 24 engendered notably improved microsomal stabilities with high PANK3 affinity. Difluoro analogue 25 suffered from reduced PANK3 affinity and rapid metabolism. Overall, the data for this subseries confirmed our prior SAR trends that showed that the FG1 cyclopropyl analogues are better activators but less potent binders compared to those with the isopropyl substitution.

We determined the crystal structures of four PANK3 complexes from this FG2 series, 18 with a singly fluorinated pyridyl ring and 21–23 with single fluorine atoms on the original phenyl ring (Figure 4A–D, Table S1b,c, and Figures S1 and S2). The structure of the PANK3·AMPPNP·Mg2+·18 complex (Figure 3A and Table S1b) shows that 18 bound in the same manner as 1 (Figure 3A). The pyridine nitrogen forms a hydrogen bond network with Wat1, I253′, and G193, an interaction that does not exist in the PANK3·AMPPNP·Mg2+·1 complex. Compounds 21–23 also bound in a fashion very similar to that of parent compound 2, with the fluorine atoms engaging available pockets created by V250′, L263′, and A267′ in the protein and thereby increasing the number of van der Waals interactions. These additional interactions explain the slightly higher IC50 values for these fluorinated compounds relative to those of 2 (Table 1). Complex 21 revealed that the phenyl ring adopted two orientations that were 180° apart; in the “up” position, the fluorine had the same interactions as 22, and in the “down” position, it occupied a tight pocket comprising Val250′, Tyr254′, Tyr258′, and Leu263′. It is unclear why 21 adopted the two conformations because it appears to negatively impact the IC50 value compared to that of 22. In complex 23, the fluorine packed into a hydrophobic cavity created by V250′, L263′, and A269′ (Figure 3 B,C and Table S1c). These additional interactions would explain the superior IC50 values for these two compounds relative to those of 2 (Table 2).

FG3 SAR

The piperazine ring acts as a spacer between the aryl and pyridazine motifs and does not have direct interactions with PANK3, suggesting that space is available for small modifications (Figure 2A). In the FG3 series, the introduction of a methyl group into the piperazine ring was explored at this common motif that is often optimized in medicinal chemistry programs,17,18 resulting in a chiral center. The chirality would potentially have two advantages. It should improve solubility by disrupting the tight crystal packing nature of these flat molecules,19 and the separate enantiomers might have very different activities in CYP-mediated metabolism.20 Apart from these ADME reasons, the effect of introducing a methyl group to “magically” improve potency has been of interest in the drug development community and led to the term “magic methyl effect” to describe this phenomenon.21 Overall, of the properties that were targeted for improvement by these modifications, only the solubility was positively impacted by the addition of the methyl groups on the piperazine ring (Table 3, compounds 27–34). The methyl position on the piperazine ring did not dictate any specific trends, and all of the compounds maintained their PANK3 potencies in the single-digit nanomolar range and were potent CoA activators. The structure of the PANK3·AMPPNP·Mg2+·29 complex (Figure 3D and Table S1c) demonstrates 29 binds in a manner identical to that of 1 (Figure 2A) with the additional methyl group interacting with L263′ and W341′ accounting for its slightly increased potency. The microsomal stabilities of the FG3 series varied. 27, 28, and 30 were more stable than their corresponding parents 2 and 5, but FG2-fluorinated analogues 31, 33, and 34 were less stable.

Pharmacokinetics Studies

On the basis of their ability to increase the level of cellular CoA, to improve solubility and microsomal stability, and to maintain structural diversity, compounds 5, 14, 21, 22, 24, 29, and 33 were advanced to pharmacokinetic profiling experiments in mice in which their properties were compared to the results of prior studies on our initial lead 2 (Table 4). Chloropyridazine 5 was less bioavailable, was more rapidly cleared than was 2, but had a larger volume of distribution. Dimethyl aniline analogue 14 was most rapidly cleared and had low exposure. 21, a fluorinated analogue of 2, demonstrated a profile similar to that of 2 with excellent oral bioavailability, slightly higher clearance, and a greater volume of distribution. 22, a fluorinated analogue of 5, demonstrated a profile similar to that of 5 with excellent oral bioavailability, lower clearance, and higher exposure. 24 had the best profile of the compounds tested with high oral bioavailability, high exposure, and low clearance. Methyl piperazine analogues 29 and 33 both suffered from fast clearance, suggesting that the methyl piperazine substitution introduced a further site of metabolism. Overall, many of the compounds were judged to have suitable pharmacokinetic exposures and CoA cellular elevations to advance into in vivo efficacy testing.

Drug-Induced CoA Elevation In Vivo

We advanced the most promising compounds into mouse efficacy experiments. The compounds were administered to mice by oral gavage, and the CoA concentrations in the liver and brains of the animals were determined 4 h after the dose. This regimen was established from the pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics experiments performed with 1 and 2 that demonstrate that 4 h after the dose was the optimal time to measure tissue CoA levels and rank the in vivo efficacy of the compounds.8 Oral administration of our benchmark compounds 2 and 5 efficiently increased CoA levels in the liver and brain (Figure 4A–C). Treatment of the animals with 3 increased the liver CoA level, but there was no impact on brain CoA levels, illustrating that 3 cannot pass the blood–brain barrier (BBB) (Figure 4B). Hydrogen bond donors are known to interfere with the ability of drugs to penetrate the BBB,22 and the properties of 3 indicate that the presence of a hydroxyl group eliminates brain CoA elevation, ruling out the use of this substituent to increase solubility in this series. The dosage of compounds in mice was altered between 10 and 15 mg/kg depending on the solubility in 30% Captisol. The CF3 substituent in 8 and 9 led to compounds that were not as effective as 2 in either liver or brain (Figure 4A–C).

The FG2 modification in 17 and 18 increased the liver CoA level and less so the brain level, but they were clearly not as potent as 2 (Figure 5A–C). Fluorinated FG2 derivatives 21, 22, and 24 were excellent CoA activators in liver and brain and were comparable to 2, with 21 and 22 being superior to 24 in CoA elevation in the brain (Figure 5B,C). These fluorinated FG2 derivatives were metabolically stable and had CoA activation potential and BBB penetration that made them suitable alternatives to 2. FG3 derivatives 27 and 29 performed well in increasing the liver CoA level, and 29 was equivalent to 2 in increasing the brain CoA level (Figure 5D–F). Combining the FG1 fluorine substitution with the R-methyl FG3 substituent in 31 and 33 led to reduced potency in both the liver and brain compared to that of 2 (Figure 5D–F). These animal studies showed that 21, 22, 24, and 29 matched or exceeded the brain CoA elevation benchmark of 2.

The pyridazine-3-nitrile motif is known to be liable to glutathione (GSH) trapping with direct thiol addition to the electron-poor nitrile.15,23 Thus, to aid prioritization, key analogues were profiled for GSH trapping (Table S3). As anticipated, compounds with chlorine at the FG4 position overcame the liability and demonstrated excellent stability due to the low reactivity of 3-chloro-6-piperazinyl pyridazines to nucleophilic aromatic displacement. In contrast, 2 demonstrated high levels of GSH trapping in this assay.

Conclusions

In this study, the optimization of 2 focused on increasing the CoA activation potential, metabolic stability, and solubility rather than biochemical inhibition. The SAR of CoA activation remained consistent within the series. The choice of a chloro or cyano pyrazine FG4 was used to balance the biochemical potency within the desirable range of IC50 values of 2–20 nM for optimal CoA elevation. A similar trend was seen between the cyclopropyl (increased activation, decreased biochemical potency, and increased metabolic stability) and isopropyl (increased potency) FG1 motifs across all of the series that were examined. Introduction of methyl substitutions into the piperazine core was not overall beneficial despite increasing the solubility as designed, as such analogues were subject to more rapid metabolism. The introduction of a fluorine into the phenyl motif was a useful tool for increasing potency and metabolic stability. Eight strongly activating compounds representative of structural subclasses were advanced into PK studies. The PK profiles were in line with microsomal stability testing results, suggesting that phase I metabolism is the major route of elimination for this series. Subsequently, several of the analogues were advanced into efficacy testing, and most compounds demonstrated large increases in the CoA levels in liver, hindbrain, and forebrain. The increase in the CoA levels in the brain was consistent with the design of these molecules that incorporates physiochemical properties suitable for crossing the BBB.24 On the basis of these results, compounds 21, 22, and 33 were designated as late preclinical leads for the treatment of PKAN and propionic acidemia, and subsequently after further preclinical testing, including extensive safety profiling, 22 (BBP-671) has advanced into clinical testing (NCT04836494).25 It is tempting to speculate that the ability of this series to increase CoA levels and enhance mitochondrial function in the brain may suggest that such analogues may have other therapeutic uses for other chronic mitochondrially associated diseases.26

Materials

d-[1-14C]Pantothenate (specific activity, 55 mCi/mmol) was from American Radiolabeled Chemicals. d-Pantothenic acid, hemicalcium salt, and AMP-PNP were from Sigma-Aldrich. EMEM was from ATCC. Human C3A (HepG2/C3A, derivative of HepG2) cells (ATCC #CRL-10741) and fetal bovine serum were from Atlanta Biologicals. Monobromobimane (mBBr) was from Life Technologies, Fisher Scientific. All other materials were reagent grade or better.

PANK Activity Assays

The PANK3 activity assay was performed as described above (PMID 3035299). Briefly, the reaction mixture contained 100 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 10 mM MgCl2, 2.5 mM ATP, 45 mM d-[1-14C]pantothenate (specific activity, 22.5 mCi/mmol), and 5 nM PANK3 in the presence of 0–0.1 mM compound. PANK3 concentrations were calculated using an extinction coefficient at 280 nm of 39 225 M–1 cm–1. The assay was linear with time, and after 10 min at 37 °C, the reaction was stopped by the addition of 4 mL of 10% (v/v) acetic acid. The mixture was spotted onto a DE81 disk and analyzed as described previously (PMID 20797618). All of the experiments were performed in duplicate, and the data were averages ± SEM.

Efficacy in C3A Cells

Human C3A cells were maintained in Eagle’s minimum essential medium (ATCC) supplemented with 2 mM glutamine, 10% fetal bovine serum, 50 units/mL penicillin, and 50 mg/mL streptomycin. The cell line was confirmed to be free of mycoplasma. The cells were seeded, grown to a density of 2 × 106, and then treated with either DMSO or 10 μM compound for 24 h. Total CoA was extracted and estimated as described below.

Determining Solubility

This assay was performed on a Biomek FX ADME-TOX workstation. Drugs and/or compounds were presented in triplicate on a 96-well microtiter plate as previously reported.27 After dilution with phosphate buffer, incubation at room temperature for 18 h, and filtration, samples were transferred to the UV module for reading, and concentrations and solubilities were determined. The whole procedure and data analysis were performed automatically using μSol Evolution Command Software.

Microsomal Stability

Mouse microsomal degradation was assessed using multiple time points up to 4 h to monitor the rate of disappearance of the parent compound during incubation monitored by LC-MS analysis as previously reported.27 The metabolic stability was evaluated via the half-life from the least-squares fit of the multiple time points based on first-order kinetics. All compounds and time points were tested in triplicate.

Crystallization and Structure Determination

PANK3 with two amino acids (DD) added to the carboxy terminus was expressed, purified, and crystallized as previously described.4 The crystals of the PANK3·AMPPNP·Mg2+·pantothenate complex were soaked in mother liquor {0.2 M ammonium acetate, 0.1 M citrate (pH 5.6), 50 mM MgCl2, 32% polyethylene glycol 4000, 4% DMSO, and 10 mM AMPPNP [adenosine 5′-(β,γ-imido)triphosphate]} and 1 mM small molecules for 1–2 days. Crystals were cryoprotected with 29% ethylene glycol, and diffraction data were collected at SER-CAT beamlines 22-ID and 22-BM at the Advanced Photon Source and processed using HKL2000.28 The structures were determined by molecular replacement using the PANK3 structure (PDB entry 6B3 V) and PHASER.29 The structures were refined and optimized using PHENIX and COOT, respectively.30 The atomic coordinates and structure factors have been deposited in the Protein Data Bank. The PDB codes and the data collection and refinement statistics are listed in Table S1a–c. The electron density maps are shown in Figure S1. All structures were rendered with PyMOL version 2.5 (Schrödinger, LLC).

Animal Ethics Statement

All procedures were performed according to protocols 323 and 556 that were approved by the St. Jude Children’s Research Hospital Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Mouse Efficacy Testing

The 6–8-week-old C57Bl6/J mice from Jackson Laboratory were fed an isocaloric purified diet (Envigo TD.170178) containing 17.9% protein, 62.3% carbohydrate, 6% fat, and pantothenic acid-supplemented diet (1000 ppm) for 2 weeks. The mice were maintained at room temperature (72 ± 2 °F), 50 ± 10% humidity, and a 14 h light/10 h dark cycle with the dark cycle starting at 18:00 hours. Water was supplied ad libitum. The mice were randomized into treatment arms to achieve a normal weight distribution. Compounds were formulated in 30% Captisol and administered by oral gavage at 12 h intervals for five doses or 24 h intervals for three doses. Control mice were administered 30% Captisol. The mice were euthanized, and tissues were harvested 4 h after the last dose. Organs, including liver, forebrain, and hindbrain, were quickly excised from the euthanized animals and immediately flash-frozen in liquid N2. The tissue samples were used for the total CoA measurements.

Total CoA Measurements

Total CoA from cells and frozen tissues was measured by derivatizing CoA with mBBr. Briefly, the cells were resuspended, or the frozen tissues were homogenized in 2 mL of cold water to which 500 μL of 0.25 M KOH was added, derivatized with monobromobimane (mBBr), and quantified by HPLC. The top layer was loaded on a 2-(2-pyridyl) ethyl solid phase extraction column that was equilibrated with 1 mL of a 50% methanol/2% acetic acid mixture. The column was washed twice with 1 mL of a 50% methanol/2% acetic acid mixture and 1 mL of water. The sample was fractionated by reversed phase HPLC and detected using UV–vis and fluorescence. A standard curve of a known amount of derivatized CoA was generated. The integrated sample peaks were quantified using the standard curve as described previously.31

Pharmacokinetics

The plasma pharmacokinetics of the test compounds in male Balb/c mice following a 2 mg/kg single intravenous administration were determined at SAI LifeSciences Ltd., India. The samples were formulated in 30% Captisol. Briefly, blood samples were collected before treatment and after 0.08, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, and 24 h (iv) in EDTA from retro orbital plexus under light isoflurane anesthesia. A 25 μL sample was spiked with 500 ng/mL lansoprazole as an internal standard. All samples were processed for analysis by protein precipitation using acetonitrile (ACN) and analyzed with a fit-for-purpose LC/MS/MS method (LLOQ, 1.26 ng/mL). Pharmacokinetic parameters were calculated using the noncompartmental analysis tool of Phoenix WinNonlin version 6.3.
