PARK19 truncation mutant Dnajc6 causes lysosomal deficiency-induced upregulation of pathologic α-synuclein and neurodegeneration of substantia nigra dopaminergic cells in PARK19 knockin mice
Authors: Hung-Li Wang, Ying-Ling Chen, Tai-Ju Chiu, Ching-Chi Chiu, Yi-Hsin Weng, Shu-Yu Liu, Allen Hon-Lun Li, Tu-Hsueh Yeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHomozygous (Q789X) DNAJC6 mutation causes PARK19. Q787 of Dnajc6 corresponds to Q789 of DNAJC6. Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mouse was utilized to elucidate pathomechanisms underlying (Q789X) DNAJC6-induced PARK19. Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice displayed PARK19 motor deficits and degeneration of substantia nigra (SN) dopaminergic neurons. (Q787X) Dnajc6 decreased clathrin heavy chain and lysosomal number, leading to downregulation of lysosomal cathepsin D and upregulation of α-synuclein or α-synuclein oligomers in SN dopaminergic neurons. Lysosomal biogenesis activator rapamycin precluded (Q787X) Dnajc6-induced downregulation of cathepsin D, upregulation of α-synuclein, and PARK19 phenotypes. (Q787X) Dnajc6-induced elevation of ER and mitochondrial α-synuclein excited ER stress and mitochondrial pro-apoptotic cascades. (Q787X) Dnajc6-evoked α-synuclein oligomer overexpression activated SN microglia and NLRP3 inflammasome and upregulated IL-1β, IL-18, and TNF-α, which stimulated MKK4-JNK -c-Jun/ATF-2 and RIPK1-RIPK3-MLKL death cascades. Our results suggest that PARK19 (Q789X) DNAJC6 mutation causes lysosomal deficiency and impairs cathepsin D-mediated degradation of α-synuclein, resulting in upregulated α-synuclein-induced neurodegeneration of SN dopaminergic cells.Subject terms: Cell biology, Genetics, Molecular biology, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Most patients afflicted with Parkinson’s disease (PD), which results from demise of substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc) dopaminergic neurons and is the most prevalent neuronal death-induced motor disorder, are sporadic cases1,2. Approximately 5–10% of PD patients belong to autosomal dominant or recessive cases as a result of monogenetic mutation-induced neurodegeneration of SNpc dopaminergic cells2,3. Autosomal recessive PARK19 (familial type 19 of Parkinson’s disease) cases exhibit early-onset PD symptoms2,3 and are caused by homozygous intronic, missense, or truncating mutation of the DNAJC6 gene2–8. A homozygous (c.801-2A>G) mutation located in intron 6 of the DNAJC6 gene is observed in PARK19 patients4. Homozygous missense (R927G) and C-terminal truncating (R256X), (Q734X), or (Q789X) mutation of DNAJC6 are the genetic cause of PARK195–8.

Within CNS neurons, DNAJC6 functions as a co-chaperone of HSP70 chaperone-like heat shock cognate 70 (HSC70)9,10. C-terminal J-domain of DNAJC6 containing (R927) residue interacts with HSC70 and is indispensable for HSC70-DNAJC6 complex-mediated physiological effects9–11. PARK19 truncating (R256X), (Q734X), or (Q789X) and (R927G) mutations5–8 are expected to debilitate the function of the C-terminal J-domain of DNAJC6 and cause loss of DNAJC6-mediated physiological effects. Our in vitro study showed that WT DNAJC6 exerts a neuroprotective effect on human SH-SY5Y dopaminergic neurons and that PARK19 mutant (Q789X) or (R927G) DNAJC6 loses the ability to protect dopaminergic neurons12. Thus, homozygous C-terminal truncating or (R927G) mutation-induced loss of DNAJC6 function and resulting degeneration of SNpc dopaminergic neurons is likely to cause PARK19.

Primary neuropathological manifestation of sporadic and familial PD cases is the existence of Lewy bodies with aggregated α-synuclein and phospho-α-synuclein1,13. SN of mouse or rat-targeted overexpression of WT human α-synuclein leads to demise of SNpc dopaminergic cells14. Upregulated mRNA and protein of α-synuclein are found in postmortem SN of sporadic PD cases15,16. Upregulation of α-synuclein resulting from duplication or triplication of the α-synuclein gene causes autosomal dominant PD2,3. Elevation of α-synuclein is implicated in the etiology of autosomal dominant PD caused by mutation of CHCHD2, DNAJC13, or LRRK217–19 and autosomal recessive PD stemming from mutation of ATP13A2, DJ-1, or PINK120–22. Thus, upregulation of α-synuclein is one of the principal pathomechanisms that trigger demise of SNpc dopaminergic cells found in idiopathic or genetic PD patients1,23,24.

Macroautophagy and chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) primarily catabolize intraneuronal α-synuclein25,26. Impaired CMA or macroautophagy increases α-synuclein within CNS neurons and could induce demise of SNpc dopaminergic cells25,26. A normal number of lysosomes is necessary for macroautophagy- or CMA-mediated clearance of α-synuclein27,28. Autophagic lysosome reformation (ALR) plays a pivotal role in sustaining the number of lysosomes within CNS neurons27–29, and dysfunctional ALR results in depletion of lysosomes and autophagy inhibition28,29. Clathrin heavy chain initiates ALR by causing budding of tubular structures from autolysosomes28,29. Knockdown of clathrin heavy chain impairs ALR and causes lysosomal dyshomeostasis30. During clathrin-mediated endocytosis within CNS neurons, HSC70 is recruited to clathrin-coated vesicles by the C-terminal J-domain of DNAJC6, which augments ATPase activity of HSC70 and promotes HSC70-induced uncoating and disassembly of clathrin coats31. Thus, DNAJC6 participates in controlling the intraneuronal level of free clathrin heavy chain. PARK19 C-terminal truncating or (R927G) mutation-induced generation of DNAJC6 lacking functional J-domain could impair HSC70-DNAJC6 complex-mediated clathrin-uncoating and downregulate intraneuronal clathrin heavy chain, leading to defective ALR and diminished number of lysosomes in SNpc dopaminergic neurons. Cathepsin D is a major lysosomal hydrolase of CNS neurons, including SNpc dopaminergic cells, and is mainly responsible for CMA- or macroautophagy-mediated proteolysis of α-synuclein32. Elevation of α-synuclein is observed in the CNS of cathepsin D knockout mice32–34. It is reasonable to hypothesize that PARK19 loss-of-function (R256X), (Q734X), or (Q789X), or (R927G) DNAJC6 reduces lysosomal number and cathepsin D of SNpc dopaminergic cells via downregulating clathrin heavy chain and impairs cathepsin D-mediated degradation of neurotoxic α-synuclein, leading to upregulated α-synuclein-triggered degeneration of SNpc dopaminergic neurons.

Accumulation of disease-causing proteins within the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) of CNS neurons activates ER stress and unfolded protein response (UPR)35, and resulting excitation of the ER stress apoptotic cascade plays a critical role in the etiology of several neurodegenerative disorders, including PD35,36. Upregulated protein markers of ER stress, UPR, and ER stress apoptotic signaling are detected in SN of idiopathic PD cases37. α-Synuclein is located within the ER of SNpc dopaminergic cells36, and upregulation of ER α-synuclein initiates ER stress and UPR36. Overexpression of WT α-synuclein within the ER of mouse SNpc dopaminergic cells causes degeneration of SNpc dopaminergic neurons38. It is possible that PARK19 truncating or missense mutant DNAJC6s-induced elevation of α-synuclein causes demise of SNpc dopaminergic cells via increasing ER α-synuclein and exciting ER stress apoptotic signaling.

Disease-causing protein-triggered impairment and oxidative damage of mitochondria are implicated in the etiology of numerous neurodegenerative diseases, including PD39,40. Defective mitochondrial complex 1-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS)-promoted mitochondrial oxidative stress are detected in SN of idiopathic PD cases41,42. α-Synuclein is found in mitochondria of SNpc dopaminergic neurons43, and accumulation of mitochondrial α-synuclein could inhibit mitochondrial complex 1 activity and produce mitochondrial oxidative damage via upregulating ROS44, leading to activation of mitochondrial pro-apoptotic signaling39,40,45. PARK19 loss-of-function mutant DNAJC6s-induced upregulation of α-synuclein could increase mitochondrial α-synuclein and cause mitochondrial apoptotic cascade-mediated degeneration of SNpc dopaminergic neurons.

Pathological activation of microglia and NLRP3 inflammasome-triggered neuroinflammation plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis and progression of several neurodegenerative disorders, including PD46,47. Neurotoxic oligomeric α-synuclein acts like a prion and is released from degenerating CNS neurons48. Then, α-synuclein oligomer could activate surrounding microglia and stimulate microglial NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to upregulation and secretion of neurotoxic cytokines, such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-18 (IL-8), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)47–49. It is very likely that PARK19 truncating or (R927G) mutation of DNAJC6-evoked elevation of pathologic α-synuclein causes neurodegeneration of SNpc dopaminergic cells by activating microglia-NLRP3 inflammasome-neurotoxic cytokines cascade.

Mouse model of (Q789X) DNAJC6, which simulates truncating and (R927G) mutations-induced production of DNAJC6 without a functional J-domain, can be utilized to elucidate pathomechanisms of PARK19. Q787, located in exon 16 of the mouse Dnajc6 gene, corresponds to Q789 of human DNAJC6. We prepared homozygous Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mouse as an animal model of (Q789X) DNAJC6-induced PARK19. Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice were used to test the hypothesis that (Q789X) DNAJC6 impairs lysosome-mediated degradation of α-synuclein by decreasing lysosomal number and cathepsin D, and triggers demise of SNpc dopaminergic neurons via elevated α-synuclein-induced activation of ER stress and mitochondrial apoptotic cascades and microglia-NLRP3 inflammasome-pro-inflammatory cytokines pathway.

Results

Preparation of heterozygous Dnajc6Q787X/+ and homozygous Dnajc6Q787X/Q787Xknockin mice

CRISPR/Cas9 technology50 was used to introduce a mutated [(CAG)Q787 → (TAG)Stop codon787] sequence at exon 16 of the Dnajc6 gene and the creation of an AccI site. Heterozygous Dnajc6Q787X/+ mice were selected via digesting the tail PCR product with AccI and bred to generate Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice (Fig. 1A). cDNA encoding exon 16 of the Dnajc6 gene was amplified by conducting RT-PCR using SN total RNA of WT, Dnajc6Q787X/+, or Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X mouse and subcloned for DNA sequencing. As opposed to (CAG)Q787 mRNA sequence of the WT mouse, the substituted (TAG)Stop Codon787 mRNA sequence was detected in all of the cDNA clones obtained from homozygous Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X mouse (Fig. 1B). Mutated (TAG)Stop codon787 mRNA sequence was found in half of the cDNA clones acquired from heterozygous Dnajc6Q787X/+ mouse. Western blot assay using anti-Dnajc6 antiserum, which recognizes both WT and truncated (Q787X) Dnajc6, revealed that only truncated (Q787X) Dnajc6 was expressed in SN of homozygous Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X mouse aged 5 months and that both WT and truncated (Q787X) Dnajc6 were detected in SN of Dnajc6Q787X/+ mouse (Fig. 1C). DNAJC6 is believed to be specifically expressed in CNS neurons4. Confocal double immunofluorescence imaging using antiserum of microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2), which is neuronal marker, and anti-Dnajc6 antibody, which interacts with WT and (Q787X) Dnajc6, showed that WT and (Q787X) Dnajc6 were localized in SN MAP2-positive neurons of WT, Dnajc6Q787X/+ or Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X mouse (Fig. 1D). Confocal double staining with anti-tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) antiserum and anti-Dnajc6 antibody also revealed that WT and (Q787X) Dnajc6 were expressed in SNpc TH-expressing dopaminergic neurons of WT, Dnajc6Q787X/+ or Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X mouse (Fig. 1E). WT and (Q787X) Dnajc6 were not localized in SN GFAP (glial fibrillary acidic protein)-expressing astrocytes of WT, Dnajc6Q787X/+ or Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X mouse (data not shown).

Homozygous Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X knockin mice display neurological and neuropathological phenotypes of PD

As an animal model of homozygous (Q789X) DNAJC6 mutation-induced early-onset autosomal recessive PARK198, homozygous Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X mice but not heterozygous Dnajc6Q787X/+ mice are expected to manifest progressive and young age-onset PD neurological symptoms. Similar locomotion velocity or distance and performing time of the pole test were observed in WT, Dnajc6Q787X/+, or Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X mice aged 5 months (Fig. 2A, B). Homozygous Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X mice at the age of 9 months exhibited a significant reduction in locomotion velocity or distance (Fig. 2A), which was a PD hypokinesia phenotype, and a pronounced longer time needed to execute the pole test (Fig. 2B), which was a PD bradykinesia symptom. Heterozygous Dnajc6Q787X/+ mice aged 9 months did not display hypokinesia and bradykinesia phenotypes of PD (Fig. 2A, B).

PD motor impairment observed in 9-month-old Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice (Fig. 2A, B) is likely to result from neurodegeneration of SNpc dopaminergic cells. TH-immunohistochemical staining indicated that homozygous DNAJC6Q787X/Q787X mice at the age of 9 months exhibited a significant decrease in the number of TH-positive SNpc dopaminergic neurons (Fig. 2C). As a consequence of cell death of SNpc dopaminergic neurons, the density of TH-positive nigrostriatal dopaminergic terminals was also markedly reduced in striatal sections of DNAJC6Q787X/Q787X knockin mice aged 9 months (Fig. 2D). Heterozygous Dnajc6Q787X/+ mice did not manifest degeneration of SNpc dopaminergic neurons (Fig. 2C, D). Phospho-α-synuclein-accumulated Lewy bodies found in SN are a prominent neuropathological hallmark of PD patients1,13. Immunohistochemical staining with anti-phospho-α-synucleinSer129 antibody showed that aggregation of phospho-α-synuclein was observed in SN of 9-month-old homozygous Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice (Fig. 2E; n = 5 animals).

Homozygous expression of (Q787X) Dnajc6 downregulates the protein level of clathrin heavy chain and the number of lysosomes in SNpc dopaminergic neurons of Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X knockin mice

Autophagic lysosome reformation (ALR) plays an indispensable role in maintaining a normal number of lysosomes within CNS neurons27–29, and defective ALR leads to depletion of lysosomes and impairment of autophagy28,29. Clathrin heavy chain-induced budding of tubular structures from autolysosomes is an imperative initial step of ALR28–30, and downregulated expression of clathrin heavy chain suppresses ALR function30. Amid the process of clathrin-mediated endocytosis in CNS neurons, the C-terminal J-domain of DNAJC6 recruits HSC70 to clathrin-coated vesicles and fosters HSC70-mediated uncoating and disassembly of clathrin coats, which is involved in controlling the intraneuronal level of clathrin heavy chain, by stimulating ATPase activity of HSC7031. Homozygous expression of PARK19 C-terminal truncating (Q789X) DNAJC6, which fails to interact with HSC70, could impair HSC70-facilitated clathrin-uncoating and reduce the intracellular level of free clathrin heavy chain in CNS neurons, including SNpc dopaminergic cells of PARK19 patients. In agreement with this hypothesis, the protein level of clathrin heavy chain was decreased in SN cellular extracts of 9-month-old homozygous Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X mice (Fig. 3A). Confocal double immunofluorescence imaging further revealed reduced levels of clathrin heavy chain within TH-positive SNpc dopaminergic neurons of Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X knockin mice aged 9 months (Fig. 3B). Altered protein expression of clathrin heavy chain was not observed in SNpc dopaminergic cells of heterozygous Dnajc6Q787X/+ mice (Fig. 3).

(Q787X) Dnajc6-induced downregulation of clathrin heavy chain (Fig. 3) is predicted to impede ALR initiation and cause ALR impairment-evoked reduction in the number of lysosomes within SNpc dopaminergic neurons of Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X mice. Western blot analysis was utilized to visualize SN expression of Lamp1 and Lamp2, which are lysosome-associated membrane glycoproteins and lysosomal protein markers. Decreased protein levels of Lamp1 and Lamp2, which resulted from reduced lysosomal number, were found in SN cells of Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X mice at the age of 9 months (Fig. 4A). Confocal double immunofluorescence imaging was conducted to quantitate density and number of Lamp1- or Lamp2-positive lysosomes in TH-positive SNpc dopaminergic cells. Fluorescence intensity, density, and number of Lamp1- or Lamp2-expressing lysosomes were reduced in SNpc dopaminergic neurons of Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X mice aged 9 months (Fig. 4B). Decreased density of Lamp1- or Lamp2-positive lysosomes was not found in SNpc dopaminergic cells of Dnajc6Q787X/+ mice (Fig. 4B).

p62/SQSTM1 (sequestosome-1) acts as a selective autophagy receptor and is catabolized by macroautophagy51. Macroautophagy malfunction leads to defective degradation and overexpression of p62/SQSTM1. Thus, elevated protein level of p62/SQSTM1 is an indicator of macroautophagy defect51. (Q787X) Dnajc6-induced reduction in lysosomal number of SNpc dopaminergic neurons (Fig. 4B) is expected to cause macroautophagy impairment. As a result, the upregulated protein level of p62/SQSTM1 was observed in SN of Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X knockin mice at the age of 9 months (Fig. 4A). Autophagosome marker LC3-II is degraded following the fusion of autophagosome and lysosome, a macroautophagy defect resulting from (Q787X) Dnajc6-evoked decrease of lysosomal number is expected to cause accumulation of LC3-II. In line with this hypothesis, the protein level of LC3-II was significantly elevated in SN of Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X mice (Fig. 4A). In addition to impairment of macroautophagy-mediated LC3-II degradation, accumulation of LC3-II could also result from increased autophagosome formation. Therefore, further measurement of autophagic flux is required to exclude the involvement of elevated autophagosome formation.

(Q787X) Dnajc6 downregulates lysosomal hydrolase cathepsin D and upregulates pathologic α-synuclein or phospho-α-synucleinSer129 in SNpc dopaminergic neurons of Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X mice

Cathepsin D is a prominent lysosomal aspartyl protease of SNpc dopaminergic neurons and required for autophagy-mediated clearance of α-synuclein32–34. (Q787X) Dnajc6-induced decrease in lysosomal number (Fig. 4) is predicted to reduce the protein level of lysosomal hydrolase cathepsin D within SNpc dopaminergic cells. Protein level of mature lysosomal cathepsin D was decreased in SN cellular extracts of homozygous Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice aged 9 months (Fig. 5A). Confocal double immunofluorescence imaging revealed reduced level of lysosomal hydrolase cathepsin D in TH-positive SNpc dopaminergic neurons of Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice at the age of 9 months (Fig. 5D). Reduction of cathepsin D was not found in SNpc dopaminergic cells of Dnajc6Q787X/+ mice (Fig. 5D).

Deficiency of lysosomal cathepsin D upregulates the protein level of pathological α-synuclein within brain neurons32–34. Thus, downregulation of lysosomal protease cathepsin D within SNpc dopaminergic neurons of Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice (Fig. 5A, D) could upregulate pathologic α-synuclein. Protein levels of α-synuclein and phospho-α-synucleinSer129, a dominant modified form of pathological α-synuclein14,15, were markedly elevated in SN cellular extracts of homozygous Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice aged 9 months (Fig. 5A). Double confocal immunofluorescence imaging also demonstrated increased α-synuclein or phospho-α-synucleinSer129 within TH-positive SNpc dopaminergic cells of Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice (Fig. 5D). Elevation of intraneuronal α-synuclein (Fig. 5A) could promote the generation of α-synuclein oligomers, which exert more pronounced neurotoxicity14,15. In accordance with previous study reporting that major in vitro oligomeric form of endogenous α-synuclein isolated from mouse brain is α-synuclein pentamer52, Western blot assays using native gels revealed presence of α-synuclein pentamers in SN extracts of WT or Dnajc6Q787X/+ mice at the age of 9 months (Fig. 5B). Increased protein level of pathological α-synuclein pentamers was detected in SN cellular extracts of Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X knockin mice (Fig. 5B). Upregulated expression of pathological α-synuclein, phospho-α-synucleinSer129 or α-synuclein oligomer was not found in Dnajc6Q787X/+ mice (Fig. 5).

Rapamycin, an activator of TFEB-mediated lysosomal biogenesis and macroautophagy, precludes (Q787X) Dnajc6-induced reduction of lysosomal number, downregulation of cathepsin D, upregulation of α-synuclein, PD neuropathology, and motor disability in Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice

Transcription factor EB (TFEB) acts as a master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis by enhancing transcription of genes encoding vital lysosomal proteins, including LAMP1 and lysosomal hydrolases, including cathepsin D, leading to an increased number and hydrolase activity of lysosomes54,55. Under basal conditions, mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) kinase phosphorylates TFEB at Ser211 and promotes interaction with 14-3-3 proteins, resulting in retention of phospho-TFEBSer211 within the cytosol and avoidance of nuclear TFEB-induced activation of target genes54–56. The mTORC1 inhibitor rapamycin suppresses Ser211 phosphorylation of TFEB and causes translocation of dephosphorylated TFEB into the nucleus, leading to TFEB-activated transcription of mRNAs encoding lysosomal proteins, such as cathepsin D and LAMP1, and generation of new lysosomes54,56. Rapamycin also directly activates macroautophagy that is largely in charge of catabolizing α-synuclein57. Thus, rapamycin activation of TFEB-mediated lysosomal biogenesis and macroautophagy is expected to reverse (Q787X) Dnajc6-induced decrement of lysosomal number, downregulation of lysosomal cathepsin D, elevation of neurotoxic α-synuclein, degeneration of SNpc dopaminergic neurons, and motor deficits observed in Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice. Rapamycin was intraperitoneally applied to 5-month-old Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice, which did not display PD motor deficits (Fig. 2A, B), for 4 months. Ser210 residue of mouse Tfeb corresponds to Ser211 residue of human TFEB. Four-month application of rapamycin inhibited mTORC1 activity and significantly decreased protein level of phospho-TfebSer210 in SN of WT or Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice (Fig. 7A). Confocal double staining using anti-TH and anti-phospho-TFEBSer211 antiserums also revealed that rapamycin pretreatment significantly reduced fluorescence intensity of cytosolic phospho-TfebSer210 in SNpc dopaminergic cells of WT or Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X mice aged 9 months (Fig. 7B). As consequence of elevated dephosphorylated nuclear Tfeb-promoted lysosomal biogenesis and augmented macroautophagy activity, rapamycin administration markedly averted (Q787X) Dnajc6-induced reduction of Lamp1, Lamp2 and cathepsin D, which resulted from diminished lysosomal number, and elevation of pathological α-synuclein in SN of homozygous Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice aged 9 months (Fig. 7C). As result of preventing upregulation of neurotoxic α-synuclein, rapamycin significantly alleviated (Q787X) Dnajc6-triggered demise of SNpc dopaminergic neurons and PD hypokinesia or bradykinesia symptom observed in Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice at age of 9 months (Fig. 7D, E). Overexpression of neurotoxic α-synuclein also causes cell death of ventral tegmental area (VTA) dopaminergic neurons58, and loss of VTA dopaminergic neurons is also found in PD patients58. Compared with SNpc dopaminergic neurons, a lesser but significant reduction in the number of VTA dopaminergic neurons was observed in Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice aged 9 months (Fig. 7D; WT mice = 4650 ± 195 neurons; Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice = 3720 ± 160 neurons; n = 5 mice). Discussion

Homozygous (R927G) as well as C-terminal truncating (R256X), (Q734X), and (Q789X) DNAJC6 mutations are the genetic cause of autosomal recessive PARK195–8. DNAJC6 serves as a co-chaperone of HSC709,10, and the interaction between the J-domain containing amino acid R927 at the C-terminus of DNAJC6 and HSC70 is of vital importance for HSC70-DNAJC6 complex-mediated physiological functions in the CNS9–11. PARK19 (R927G) mutation of DNAJC6 impairs the essential function of J-domain12. PARK19 (R256X), (Q734X), or (Q789X) mutation leads to the generation of truncated DNAJC6 without the C-terminal J-domain. We hypothesized that homozygous C-terminal truncation and (R927G) mutations-induced loss-of-DNAJC6 function causes neurodegeneration of SNpc dopaminergic cells and PARK19. Q787 of mouse Dnajc6 corresponds to Q789 of human DNAJC6. In this study, a homozygous Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mouse, which replicates homozygous (R256X), (Q734X), (Q789X), or (R927G) mutation of DNAJC6-induced production of PARK19 DNAJC6 lacking the functional C-terminal J-domain, was utilized as a PARK19 animal model to elucidate molecular pathomechanisms of PARK19. Similar to PARK19 patients, homozygous Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice aged 9 months exhibited early-onset PD hypokinesia and bradykinesia symptoms resulting from degeneration of SNpc dopaminergic neurons. In accordance with autosomal recessive inheritance of PARK19, heterozygous Dnajc6Q787X/+ mice did not display PD neurological and neuropathological phenotypes.

Upregulation of pathological α-synuclein is one of the primary pathomechanisms that trigger degeneration of SNpc dopaminergic cells detected in sporadic and hereditary PD cases1,13–15. Phospho-α-synuclein-accumulated aggregates were found in SN of homozygous Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice, suggesting that upregulation of neurotoxic α-synuclein is the pathomechanism underlying homozygous expression of (Q787X) Dnajc6-induced degeneration of SNpc dopaminergic neurons. In line with our hypothesis, elevated protein levels of pathologic α-synuclein, phospho-α-synucleinSer129, and α-synuclein oligomers were observed in SNpc dopaminergic neurons and SN cellular extracts of Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice. Two lysosome-dependent degradation pathways, macroautophagy and chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA), are primarily in charge of catabolizing aggregation-prone and misfolded disease-causing proteins, including α-synuclein, and play an indispensable role in regulating intraneuronal homeostasis of α-synuclein25,26. The proteolytic function of macroautophagy and CMA necessitates normal function and number of lysosomes27,28. Lysosomal deficiency, which results from defective function of lysosomes or diminished number of lysosomes, could undermine CMA- or macroautophagy-mediated degradation of pathological proteins such as α-synuclein, leading to accumulation of neurotoxic protein aggregates and neuronal death25.

Autophagic lysosome reformation (ALR), which occurs at the late phase of autophagy and is initiated by clathrin heavy chain-induced proto-lysosome budding, plays a crucial role in preserving the number of lysosomes within CNS neurons27–29. Debilitated ALR leads to a diminution of lysosomal number and autophagy impairment28,29. C-terminal J-domain of DNAJC6 plays a vital role in maintaining intraneuronal level of clathrin heavy chain in the CNS via stimulating HSC70-mediated uncoating and disassembly of clathrin coats during clathrin-mediated endocytosis31. Homozygous expression of PARK19 (R256X), (Q734X), (Q789X), or (R927G) DNAJC6 lacking functional C-terminal J-domain is expected to downregulate free clathrin heavy chain in CNS neurons, including SNpc dopaminergic cells of PARK19 patients. Consistent with this hypothesis, the protein level of clathrin heavy chain was decreased in SNpc dopaminergic cells of Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mouse, which is the PARK19 animal model. (Q787X) Dnajc6-induced decrement of clathrin heavy chain could debilitate initiation and function of ALR, leading to a reduction in lysosomal number within SNpc dopaminergic neurons of Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice. Thus, decreased protein level of lysosomal marker Lamp1 or Lamp2 in SN cellular extracts and diminished density of Lamp1- or Lamp2-expressing lysosomes within SNpc dopaminergic cells, which result from reduced number of lysosomes, were observed in Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X knockin mice. (Q787X) Dnajc6-induced decrease in lysosomal number also led to impaired macroautophagy, which is indicated by elevation of p62/SQSTM151, in SN of Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X mice. Our results suggest that PARK19 C-terminal truncating mutations-induced loss-of-DNAJC6 function causes lysosomal deficiency, autophagy impairment, and upregulation of neurotoxic α-synuclein in SNpc dopaminergic neurons by downregulating clathrin heavy chain and diminishing the number of lysosomes.

(N855S) mutation of DNAJC13 causes autosomal dominant PD with Lewy body pathology and accumulation of intraneuronal α-synuclein71. Dnajc13 deficiency debilitates ALR by impairing scission of autolysosome tubules in CNS neurons72, indicating that PD mutant DNAJC13-induced ALR malfunction could reduce lysosomal number and diminish lysosome-mediated clearance of α-synuclein within SNpc dopaminergic cells. Together with this reported study, our results propose that different PD-causing genetic mutations could reduce lysosomal number by impairing distinct steps of ALR and produce lysosomal deficiency-prompted upregulation of neurotoxic α-synuclein and ensuing demise of SNpc dopaminergic neurons. ALR impairment is also involved in the etiology of other genetic neurodegenerative diseases. Homozygous mutations of CLN3 cause neurodegenerative lysosomal storage disorder Batten disease73. CLN3 is needed for lysosomal tubule formation during ALR, and CLN3 insufficiency causes ALR dysfunction and lysosomal dyshomeostasis73. Genetic mutations of LYST lead to autosomal recessive Chediak-Higashi neurodegenerative syndrome74. LYST promotes fission of protolysosomes from autolysosome tubules during ALR, and a lack of LYST-induced ALR defect leads to lysosomal depletion in human glutamatergic neurons74. In addition to a decreased number of lysosomes, mutations of PD-associated gene-induced impairment of lysosomal function can also cause lysosomal deficiency-evoked upregulation of neurotoxic α-synuclein. PARK9 mutations of lysosomal H+/K+-ATPase ATP13A2 upregulate α-synuclein by inducing lysosomal alkalinization and resulting impaired lysosomal function20. Mutation of lysosomal K+/H+ ion channel TMEM175 is a risk factor of PD and could increase α-synuclein by impairing lysosomal pH homeostasis and function75.

Aspartic protease cathepsin D is the most abundant lysosomal protease of CNS neurons, including SNpc dopaminergic cells, and plays an indispensable role in catabolizing intraneuronal misfolded or aggregated disease-causing proteins, such as amyloid β-protein (Aβ), α-synuclein, huntingtin, and Tau32–34. Downregulated expression or proteolytic activity of cathepsin D leads to accumulation of neurotoxic protein aggregates within CNS neurons and demise of CNS neurons32. We hypothesized that homozygous expression of (Q787X) Dnajc6-induced diminution of lysosomal number upregulates neurotoxic α-synuclein in SNpc dopaminergic neurons of Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice by downregulating lysosomal cathepsin D and impairing cathepsin D-mediated degradation of α-synuclein. Consistent with our hypothesis, the protein level of mature lysosomal cathepsin D was reduced in SNpc dopaminergic cells of Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice. Therefore, PARK19 C-terminal truncation and (R927G) mutations-induced loss-of-DNAJC6 function could upregulate neurotoxic α-synuclein and induces degeneration of SNpc dopaminergic neurons by downregulating lysosomal number and aspartyl protease cathepsin D. To prove our hypothesis, we tested the possibility that rapamycin, which stimulates TFEB-mediated lysosomal biogenesis and macroautophagy55,57,58, prevents (Q787X) Dnajc6-induced reduction of lysosomal number, downregulation of cathepsin D, upregulation of α-synuclein, PD neuropathology and motor impairment observed in Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice. Rapamycin pretreatment pronouncedly precluded downregulation of Lamp1, Lamp2, and cathepsin D, which resulted from diminution of lysosomal number, and elevation of pathological α-synuclein in SN of Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X knockin mice. As a consequence of averting upregulation of α-synuclein, rapamycin also significantly ameliorated degeneration of SNpc dopaminergic cells and PD motor deficits observed in Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice.

Interestingly, decreased plasma level of cathepsin D and CSF activity of cathepsin D were also observed in idiopathic PD patients76,77. In addition to PARK19 (Q789X) DNAJC6-induced degeneration of SNpc dopaminergic cells reported in this study, the downregulated level of lysosomal cathepsin D is also likely to be implicated in the pathogenesis of sporadic PD. Besides PD, previous studies suggest that decreased expression or activity of lysosomal cathepsin D promotes neurodegeneration of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) or frontotemporal dementia (FTD). Knockout of the cathepsin D gene increased neurotoxic Aβ42 and Aβ40 in the mouse cerebral cortex78. Cathepsin D level was reduced in plasma and fibroblasts of sporadic and familial AD patients79,80, and plasma cathepsin D level was negatively correlated with the clinical dementia rating scale79. Thus, downregulation of cathepsin D-induced elevation of neurotoxic Aβ42 and Aβ40 could be involved in the etiology of AD. Heterozygous loss-of-function mutations of progranulin cause autosomal dominant FTD81,82. Progranulin functions as chaperon and interacts with cathepsin D within lysosomes, resulting in enhancement of cathepsin D activity81,82. Heterozygous FTD mutations of progranulin induce haploinsufficiency of lysosomal progranulin and impair activity of lysosomal cathepsin D, leading to FTD neurodegeneration.

α-Synuclein or phospho-α-synucleinSer129 is found in the ER and mitochondria of SNpc dopaminergic cells36,43. In this study, homozygous mutation of (Q787X) Dnajc6-induced upregulation of α-synuclein or phospho-α-synucleinSer129 within SNpc dopaminergic neurons increased ER and mitochondrial levels of α-synuclein or phospho-α-synucleinSer129 in SN of Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice. Stimulation of the ER stress apoptotic cascade and defect or oxidative damage of mitochondria-excited mitochondrial pro-apoptotic signaling play a pivotal role in etiopathogenesis of sporadic or hereditary PD cases35,36,39,40,45,83. (Q787X) Dnajc6-induced accretion of ER and mitochondrial α-synuclein or phospho-α-synucleinSer129 could cause demise of SNpc dopaminergic neurons via activating ER stress and mitochondrial apoptotic pathways36,39,40,45,83. Consistent with this hypothesis, our results suggest that (Q787X) Dnajc6-evoked elevation of α-synuclein or phospho-α-synucleinSer129 within the ER could induce activation of ER stress and UPR, leading to upregulation of pro-apoptotic Noxa, Bim, or Puma and excitation of ER stress apoptotic signaling in SN of Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X knockin mice. Our data also propose that (Q787X) Dnajc6-induced increment of mitochondrial α-synuclein or phospho-α-synucleinSer129 could cause mitochondrial impairment by reducing mitochondrial complex 1 activity and oxidative stress via increasing ROS in SN of Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice. Homozygous mutation of (Q787X) Dnajc6-evoked elevation of Noxa, Bim, or Puma and mitochondrial defect or oxidative damage is likely to stimulate the mitochondrial pro-apoptotic cascade in SN of Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X knockin mice. Our results suggest that excitation of ER stress and mitochondrial apoptotic pathways is involved in the pathogenesis of (Q789X) DNAJC6 mutation-induced PARK19.

Upregulated prion-like α-synuclein oligomers could be released from degenerating SNpc dopaminergic neurons and trigger activation of neighboring microglia and microglial NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to upregulation and release of pro-inflammatory and neurotoxic IL-1β, IL-8, or TNF-α and subsequent PD-causing degeneration of SNpc dopaminergic neurons48. Homozygous expression of (Q787X) Dnajc6-induced elevation of pathological α-synuclein oligomers caused selective activation of pro-inflammatory M1-like microglia and excitation of microglial NLRP3 inflammasome-induced upregulation of neurotoxic IL-1β, IL-8, or TNF-α in SN of Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X knockin mice. (Q787X) Djanc6-induced elevation of IL-1β or IL-18 and TNF-α excited MKK4-JNK-c-Jun/ATF-2 and RIPK1-RIPK3-MLKL necroptotic neuronal death pathways in SN of Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice. Our data suggest that PARK19 truncating and (R927G) mutations of DNAJC6 promote degeneration of SNpc dopaminergic neurons by exciting microglia-NLRP3 inflammasome-neurotoxic cytokines cascade.

In conclusion, our results using homozygous Dnajc6Q787X/ Q787X mice, which replicate PARK19 mutations-induced generation of mutant DNAJC6 lacking functional C-terminal J-domain, suggest that PARK19 C-terminal truncating and (R927G) DNAJC6 mutations, including (Q789X) substitution, downregulate lysosomal number via decreasing clathrin heavy chain and lysosomal hydrolase cathepsin D, leading to impairment of cathepsin D-mediated clearance of neurotoxic α-synuclein and upregulated α-synuclein-triggered neurodegeneration of SNpc dopaminergic cells.

Methods

Generation of heterozygous Dnajc6Q787X/+ and homozygous Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X knockin mice

CRISPR/Cas9 method50 was utilized to prepare the Dnajc6Q787X/+ knockin mouse. Q(CAG)787 of Dnajc6 is located in exon 16 of the mouse Dnajc6 gene. Replacing single-strand DNA (ssDNA; 1.5 kb) contained mutated [(CAG)Q787 → (TAG)Stop codon787] sequence. Cas9 nuclease, ssDNA, and two single guide RNAs (sgRNA1 targeting intron 15:5′AGTAGGGCAAGGGCACTACCTGG3′ and sgRNA2 targeting intron 16: 5′GCCCTGC TAGATTAG ATTCTTGG3′) were microinjected into pro-nuclei of zygotes, and injected embryos were then transferred to pseudo-pregnant female mice. Tail genomic DNA of pups was used to amplify the intron 15 fragment, exon 16, and the intron 16 fragment of the Dnajc6 gene using primers (Dnajc6F:5′GCTTAGGAG GGAGCAGTCTGGTCAGG G3′; Dnajc6R: 5′ GAACATGTCTACACTATGCCTGCATGT3′). The CAG to TAG mutation resulted in the creation of the AccI site. Heterozygous Dnajc6Q787X/+ mice were identified by digesting the PCR product with AccI and bred to prepare homozygous Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice. PCR assays followed by AccI digestion were conducted for routine genotyping of WT, Dnajc6Q787X/+, and Dnacj6Q787X/Q787X mice. An altered nucleotide sequence was not found in two predicted off-target sequences of sgRNAs identified by the Cas-OFFinder Program. Methods of animal experiments were inspected and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chang Gung University (IACUC No. CGU110-084).

RT-PCR confirmation of (Q787X) Dnajc6 knockin mutation

SN tissue was dissected out from WT, Dnajc6Q787X/+ or Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mouse as described previously84, and total RNA was purified from SN using Trizol Reagent (Thermo Fisher). Reaction mixture containing total RNA (30 μg), oligodT primer (10 ng/μl), 1 mM of each dNTP, RNasin (20 U), and SuperScript III RT (100 U; Thermo Fisher) was incubated at 50 °C for 1 h and used to synthesize the first-strand cDNA. PCR using cDNA was conducted to amplify exon 16 of the mouse Dnajc6 gene. The PCR product was then purified, subcloned, and used for DNA sequencing.

Western blot assays of cathepsin D, clathrin heavy chain, Dnajc6, Lamp1, Lamp2, LC3, or p62/SQSTM1

SN or striatal tissues were sonicated with ice-cold lysis buffer A containing NaCl 150 mM, CHAPS 1%, NP40 1%, sodium deoxycholate 0.5%, EGTA 1 mM, EDTA 1 mM, Tris-HCl 50 mM, and a cocktail of protease inhibitors. Supernatant obtained after 13,000 × g centrifugation was used for immunoblotting assays. Protein samples resolved on SDS-PAGE (10%) were transferred to PVDF membrane, which was then interacted with the following primary antibodies: (1) Rabbit anti-cathepsin D polyclonal antiserum (Cell Signaling Technology; CST). (2) Rabbit anti-clathrin heavy chain monoclonal antibody (CST). (3) Rabbit polyclonal anti-Dnajc6 antiserum (Abcam). (4) Rabbit anti-lysosomal-associated membrane protein 1 (Lamp1) monoclonal antibody (CST). (5) Rabbit anti-lysosomal-associated membrane protein 2 (Lamp2) monoclonal antiserum (CST). (6) Rabbit monoclonal anti-LC3A/B antibody (CST). (7) Rabbit monoclonal anti-p62/SQSTM1 antiserum (CST). After incubating with HRP-linked secondary antibody, immunoreactive proteins were visualized with the ECL Kit (Merck). Density of the protein band was quantified and normalized to the actin band.

Examination of motor function

To analyze velocity and distance of locomotor activity, WT, Dnajc6Q787X/+, or Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice at different ages were placed in an open field of an acrylic box (45 × 45 × 45 cm) for one hour. Movement of the animal was videotaped with a camera installed above and examined with tracking software TopScan (Clever Sys.). To assess motor performance with the pole test, the animal was positioned at the top of the pole and trained to come down along the pole to the base of the pole put in the cage. After two-day training, the average time required for carrying out five trials of descending along the pole was measured.

Immunohistochemical staining of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) or phospho-α-synucleinSer129

Following perfusing an anesthetized animal with paraformaldehyde, cryosections (20 μm) of the brain were prepared from WT, Dnajc6Q787X/+, or Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice aged 9 months. Permeabilized SN or striatum cryosections were incubated with mouse monoclonal anti-TH antibody (Chemicon) or rabbit monoclonal anti-phospho-α-synucleinSer129 antiserum (Abcam). After treatment of horse anti-mouse or goat anti-rabbit biotinylated IgG, SN or striatal sections were interacted with VECTASTAIN ABC kit (Vector Laboratories) and visualized with diaminobenzidine. TH-positive SNpc dopaminergic neurons were viewed with a Leica DM2500 upright microscope connected with a RET 2000R CCD camera (QImaging) and numbered with StereoInvestigator software (MBF Bioscience). Image J software was utilized to analyze the density of TH-immunoreactive dopaminergic nerve terminals in striatal sections.

Confocal double immunofluorescence imaging

To observe the presence of Dnajc6 within MAP2-positive neurons, TH-expressing dopaminergic neurons, or GFAP-positive astrocytes, SN cryosections of WT, Dnajc6Q787X/+, or Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice were interacted with mouse anti-MAP2 monoclonal antiserum (Proteintech), mouse anti-TH monoclonal antibody (Chemicon), or mouse anti-GFAP monoclonal antiserum (Santa Cruz) and rabbit polyclonal anti-Dnajc6 antiserum (Abcam). To visualize expression of clathrin heavy chain, Lamp1, Lamp2, cathepsin D, α-synuclein, phospho-α-synucleinSer129 or phospho-TfebSer210 within SNpc dopaminergic neurons or SNpr GABAergic neurons, SN sections of WT, Dnajc6Q787X/+ or Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice were incubated with mouse anti-TH monoclonal antibody (Chemicon) or anti-glutamate decarboxylase 1 (GAD1) mouse monoclonal antiserum (Proteintech) and rabbit anti-clathrin heavy chain monoclonal antibody (CST), rabbit monoclonal anti-Lamp1 antiserum (CST), rabbit anti-Lamp2 monoclonal antibody (CST), rabbit anti-cathepsin D monoclonal antiserum (CST), rabbit anti-α-synuclein polyclonal antiserum (Proteintech), rabbit anti-phospho-α-synucleinSer129 monoclonal antibody (Abcam) or rabbit polyclonal anti-phospho-TFEBSer211 antibody (Thermo Fisher). SN cryosections were then interacted with Alexa Fluor 488- and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibodies. The Leica STELLARIS 8 Confocal Microscope was used to collect confocal immunofluorescence images, which were analyzed with Leica LAS X software.

Western blot analysis of α-synuclein or phospho-α-synucleinSer129

SN or striatal tissues of WT, Dnajc6Q787X/+ or Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice were sonicated using lysis buffer consisting of NaCl 150 mM, Triton X-100 2%, NP40 0.5%, sodium deoxycholate 0.5%, EGTA 1 mM, EDTA 1 mM, Tris-HCl 50 mM, protease inhibitor, and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. Following 13,000 × g centrifugation, the supernatant containing the cellular lysate was used for immunoblotting assays. Mitochondrial fraction and endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-containing microsomal fraction of SN were purified as described previously85. SN tissues were homogenized with buffer containing 250 mM sucrose, 5 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 2 mM EDTA, 20 mM HEPES, cocktails of protease inhibitors, and phosphatase inhibitors. Homogenate was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant obtained was centrifuged at 9000 × g for 10 min to isolate the mitochondrial pellet. The resulting supernatant was centrifuged at 100,000 × g for 60 min to collect a pellet comprising the ER-containing microsomal fraction and supernatant containing the cytosolic fraction. Protein samples of cellular extract, ER-containing microsomal fraction, or mitochondrial fraction were resolved on 15% SDS-PAGE or native gel, and membranes were incubated with rabbit polyclonal anti-α-synuclein antiserum (iREAL), rabbit monoclonal anti-α-synuclein antibody (Abcam), or rabbit monoclonal anti-phospho-α-synucleinSer129 antibody (Abcam). After treatment with HRP-linked secondary antiserum, immunoreactive proteins were detected with the ECL kit. Band density of protein was normalized with that of actin and quantified.

Intraperitoneal application of rapamycin, an activator of transcription factor EB (TFEB)-induced lysosomal biogenesis and macroautophagy

Rapamycin (MedChemExpress), which activates TFEB-mediated lysosomal biogenesis and macroautophagy57,58, was intraperitoneally injected (three days per week; daily dosage = 25 mg/Kg body weight) to 5-month-old WT mice or homozygous Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice. After four-month intraperitoneal administration, rapamycin-injected 9-month-old WT or Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice were used for appraisal of motor function as described above, Western blot assays of lysosomal proteins or α-synuclein, and TH-immunohistochemical staining of SNpc dopaminergic cells.

Immunoblotting analysis of phospho-TfebSer210

SN tissues of WT or Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice and rapamycin-treated WT or Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice were sonicated with ice-cold lysis buffer A supplemented with a cocktail of phosphatase inhibitors. Protein samples of cellular extracts obtained following 13,000 × g centrifugation were resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE. PVDF membranes were then incubated with rabbit anti-phospho-TFEBSer211 polyclonal antibody (Thermo Fisher) or rabbit anti-TFEB monoclonal antiserum (CST). After interacting with the HRP-linked secondary antibody, immunoreactive proteins were visualized using the ECL Kit.

Quantitative real-time RT-PCR measurement

SN total RNA of 9-month-old WT, Dnajc6Q787X/+ or Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mouse was isolated with Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher) and used for production of the first-strand cDNA as described above. Then, quantitative real-time PCR was carried out using the StepOne Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems) with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems). Internal control was determined by measuring GAPDH mRNA level, and the change in mRNA expression was computed with the equation: Fold change = 2−(ΔΔCt). ΔΔCt = (Ctgene - CtGAPDH)Dnajc6Q787X/+ mouse or Dnajc6Q787X/Q787Xmouse - (Ctgene - CtGAPDH)WT mouse.

Immunoblotting assays with protein markers of ER stress, UPR, and ER stress pro-apoptotic signaling

SN cellular extracts of WT, Dnajc6Q787X/+ or Dnajc6Q787X/Q787X mice were acquired with lysis buffer A supplemented with a cocktail of phosphatase inhibitors and separated on 10% or 15% SDS-PAGE. Membranes were interacted with the following antibodies: (1) Anti-calnexin rabbit monoclonal antiserum (CST). (2) Anti-Grp78 rabbit monoclonal antibody (CST). (3) Anti-PERK rabbit monoclonal antiserum (CST). (4) Anti-phospho-PERKThr980 rabbit monoclonal antibody (Thermo Fisher). (5) Anti-phospho-IRE1αSer724 rabbit polyclonal antiserum (Thermo Fisher). (6) Anti-IRE1α rabbit monoclonal antibody (CST). (7) Anti-ATF4 rabbit polyclonal antiserum (Proteintech). (8) Anti-Bim rabbit monoclonal antibody (CST). (9) Anti-CHOP rabbit monoclonal antiserum (CST). (10) Anti-caspase-12 rabbit polyclonal antibody (CST). (11) Anti-Noxa rabbit polyclonal antiserum (Invitrogen). (12) Anti-Puma rabbit monoclonal antibody (CST). After treatment of the HRP-linked secondary antibody, immunoreactive proteins were detected by the ECL solution.

Measurement of mitochondrial ROS (reactive oxygen species)

SN mitochondrial level of ROS was quantified with OxiSelect In Vitro ROS/RNS assay kit (Cell Biolabs). SN mitochondrial extract (50 µg) purified as described above was applied to a 96-well plate and incubated with oxidation-promoting catalyst agent for 5 min. ROS probe DCFH-DiOxyQ was added into the mixture and interacted with the mitochondrial ROS sample for 30 min to generate the DCF fluorescence signal, which was measured with fluorescence (480 nm excitation/530 nm emission) microplate Reader.

Quantifying mitochondrial complex I activity

Mitochondrial complex 1 activity of mouse SN tissues was determined using the Complex I enzyme activity colorimetric assay kit (Abcam). Briefly, SN mitochondrial protein extract (50 µg) was applied to a microplate layered with anti-mitochondrial complex I antibody and incubated for three hours at room temperature. After washing, the assay reactant containing NADH and dye was added to the reaction mixture with the attached mitochondrial complex 1 of the mitochondrial sample. Subsequently, an increased OD450 nm value as a result of NADH oxidation to NAD+ was obtained to quantify mitochondrial complex I activity.

Immunoblotting analysis with proteins mediating the mitochondrial pro-apoptotic cascade

As described above, mitochondrial and cytosolic protein extracts were purified from mouse SN tissues and resolved on 15% SDS-PAGE. Then, the membrane was treated with the following antibodies: (1) Anti-cytochrome c mouse monoclonal antibody (Abcam). (2) Anti-active caspase-9 rabbit polyclonal antiserum (CST). (3) Anti-active caspase-3 rabbit polyclonal antibody (CST). After incubating with HRP-linked secondary antiserum, immunoreactive proteins were visualized with ECL solution.

Western blot analysis of Iba-1, CD86, CD206, GFAP, NLRP3 inflammasome complex or pro-inflammatory cytokines

SN immunofluorescence imaging of Iba-1

Statistics

One-way ANOVA analysis with the following Tukey’s comparison test was carried out to evaluate the statistical difference among multiple experimental groups.
