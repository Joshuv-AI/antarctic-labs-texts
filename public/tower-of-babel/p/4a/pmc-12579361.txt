Characterizing detectable milk protein allergens β‐casein and β‐lactoglobulin in milk ladder foods
Authors: Ruth Simmons, Thomas R. Vennard, Zafir Gaygadzhiev, Nelson Cano, Michael A. Gray, Rosan Meyer, Carina Venter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCow's milk allergy (CMA) is the most common and complex presentation of food allergies in early childhood. The “ladder” approach has gained popularity to reintroduce cow's milk into a child's diet depending on the type of CMA, though there are very few studies quantifying the level of allergen within the steps of food ladders. This paper aimed to assess detectable levels of milk protein allergens in milk ladder foods.MethodsCookies, muffins, and pancakes were prepared according to the recipes of the International Milk Allergy in Primary (iMAP) care ladder. The effect of the food matrix, heating, and industrial milk processing was assessed by measuring levels of bos d5 (β‐lactoglobulin) and bos d11 (β‐casein) by ELISA. The other steps of the milk ladder, cheese, yogurt and milk, were also tested and compared.ResultsThe food matrix, baking conditions, and milk processing all influenced the detected levels of bos d5 and bos d11 in the milk ladder foods. The order of the foods by increasing antigen levels depends on the parameter used: calculated levels of total milk protein, detected bos d5, or detected bos d11.ConclusionsThis study shows that the order of milk ladder foods is different depending on whether it is based on calculated protein amounts, measured levels of bos d5 or measured levels of bos d11. HCPs need to be aware that depending on which protein is tested, ladder foods have a different order. This paper provides novel data on detectable proteins bos d5 (β‐lactoglobulin) and bos d11 (β‐casein) in milk ladder foods, which contribute to our current understanding of milk ladders, critical in the management of CMA.

INTRODUCTION

Cow's milk allergy (CMA) is the most common and complex presentation of food allergies in early childhood. 1 Based on a systematic review, European data shows that the point prevalence of CMA was 1.8% (2012–2021) 2 and 1.6% (2000–2011), indicating no significant changes in CMA in Europe. 2 Similar findings are reported by comparing two cohorts born in the same geographic location in the United Kingdom. 3

CMA is divided into IgE‐mediated and non‐IgE mediated CMA. 4 , 5 Management of CMA includes individualized avoidance of cow's milk and cow's milk‐containing foods. Breastfeeding should always be supported, and maternal avoidance of cow's milk should be considered on an individual basis, though not often required. 6 Specialized formula for infants or plant‐based substitutes for older children and adults is recommended to replace cow's milk in the diet. 7 , 8

Up to 70%–80% of children with IgE‐mediated CMA can tolerate baked forms of the food, while still demonstrating symptoms to unbaked forms. 9 , 10 Studies about tolerance to baked milk for non‐IgE mediated CMA, such as FPIES and EoE, are insufficient to reach any conclusion. 11 , 12 , 13 For milder forms of non‐IgE mediated CMA, an at‐home food “ladder” approach has been suggested to reintroduce cow's milk into a child's diet. 5 , 11 , 14 A milk ladder is a stepwise progression from extensively heated to less heated food. As the ladder advances, the amount of allergenic protein consumed also increases. 11

Cow's milk contains 80% casein proteins and 20% whey proteins. A S1‐casein, A S2‐casein, β‐casein, κ‐casein make up the casein proteins, and β‐lactoglobulin, α‐lactalbumin, serum albumin, immunoglobulins (Ig) A, M, E, and G, and lactoferrin make up the whey proteins. 15

The allergenicity of these proteins is affected differently by heating, which led to the use of milk ladders in the management of CMA. The detection of bos d5 (β‐lactoglobulin) can be reduced by 99% in baked products, whereas bos d11 (β‐casein) detection is reduced by 30%. 11

The first published ladder was created in 2013 for milder forms of non‐IgE mediated CMA in the United Kingdom by Venter et al. 14 This 12‐step ladder initially contained British foods and was shortened to 6 steps based on international foods in 2017. 5

Although initially created for non‐IgE‐mediated allergies, many providers also use ladders for progressive induction of tolerance at home for IgE‐mediated CMA. 16 , 17 Milk ladders should be cautiously used in children with IgE‐mediated cow's milk allergy and are best suited for children with prior mild, non‐anaphylactic reactions, non‐asthmatic or well‐controlled asthma, in families who are willing, well‐prepared, and able to access emergency care if needed, children with a high previous reaction threshold, low or decreasing SPT/sIgE levels, and younger children (e.g., preschool). 18 Yet, multiple studies support the safety and effectiveness of milk ladders for children with IgE‐mediated cow's milk allergy from the United Kingdom, 19 Ireland, 16 , 17 Spain 16 and Canada. 20 Taken together, these findings indicate that milk ladders are generally safe, effective, and may accelerate tolerance development, though patient‐specific factors (e.g., SPT size, sIgE levels) should guide an individualized approach.

This expanded use of the ladder outside of its initial target population highlighted the need for safer ladders where the heat treatment is standardized to prevent pockets of uncooked milk, 21 and a known quantity of milk protein, ideally verified by scientific assays. A ladder must offer for safe use a stepwise progression of cow's milk protein content, with decreased denaturing as the ladder progresses. 18

We aimed to quantify the levels of bos d5 (β‐lactoglobulin) and bos d11 (β‐casein) in the foods from the international milk ladder (iMAP) 14 and the Mount Sinai muffin 22 based on an ELISA detection assay and compare this to the calculated total milk protein content of foods in the iMAP milk ladder.

METHODS

Samples and sample extraction

Cookies, muffins, and pancakes were prepared according to the recipes and methods in the iMAP ladder published in 2017, 14 and the Mount Sinai muffin was prepared according to the School of Medicine muffin recipe 22 (Table S1). The Mount Sinai muffin was tested to assess the impact of recipe composition on the detection of bos d5 and bos d11 (Figure S1 and milk processing Figure S2 and matrix effect considering). Three batches of each food were made by the same person, using the same kitchen equipment and cleaned between preparations to avoid any contamination.

Three lots of commonly eaten brands of yogurt, cheese, and milk were bought at a local store in the USA and were: Stonyfield Whole Milk Plain Yogurt, Tillamook Medium Cheddar Cheese, PET Pasteurized Whole Milk, and UHT milk Horizon Organic UHT Whole Milk. Raw, unprocessed milk was acquired from BioChemed Services (Virginia, USA).

To extract protein from the samples, each food sample was manually reduced to breadcrumb size (if needed) by a food processor, grater, or mortar and pestle to create a homogeneous sample, and then 1 gram of the sample was weighed out. Appropriate volume of MARIA® for Foods extraction buffer (1x phosphate buffered saline, 1M NaCl, 2% Tween‐20, pH 7.4) was added to achieve a 1/10 dilution. The extract was vortexed until uniformly mixed and then sonicated for 30 s. The extract was then incubated for 15 min at 60°C in a shaking water bath. After incubation, the extract was immediately spun down using a centrifuge at 250×g rcf for 20 min. The extract supernatant was transferred to a new 2 mL tube and stored at 4°C until analysis or at −20°C for long‐term storage.

ELISA

Two proteins were chosen to test by ELISA, bos d5 and bos d11 being two of the four major allergens in cow's milk and based on previous studies. 21 , 23 , 24 Polyclonal IgE testing kits are not commercially available as they require serum from allergic individuals and hence IgG kits were used. Additionally, commercially available kits with known allergen specificity are limited. Bos d5 is also the most abundant protein in whey, is absent from human milk, and individuals with cow's milk allergy have a high prevalence of IgE to bos d5. 23 , 25 , 26

Extracted samples were analyzed using the native bos d5 ELISA 2.0 kit (EPC‐NBD‐5‐X, InBio, VA, USA) and the bos d11 ELISA 2.0 kit (EPC‐BD11‐X, InBio, VA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions and as previously described. 27 , 28

Spiking

Cookies, muffins, and pancakes all have very different matrices. The spiking experiments were designed to understand how the matrix may impact the ELISA measurement. To assess the suitability of comparing levels of bos d5 and bos d11 across different foods, equivalent foods where the milk was replaced with water were prepared, and these were spiked with pure protein (bovine‐sourced bos d5 (L3908) and bos d11 (C6905) reference standard materials, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), and recovery rates were calculated. To assess the accuracy of the method, this study utilized both pre‐ and post‐extraction spiking of bos d5 and bos d11 in all tested matrices. A preliminary screening analysis was performed to ascertain the intrinsic bos d5 and bos d11 content in each matrix, which was then used to calculate the spike solution concentrations and volumes to approximate the recipe levels. An overview of the extraction procedure for samples and spikes is shown in Figure S4, and ELISA results from the spiked samples are shown in Figures S5 and S6.

Calculated levels of bos d5 and bos d11

The levels of total milk protein indicated in each food of the milk ladder by Venter et al. 5 were used to estimate the equivalent levels of bos d5 and bos d11 in the milk ladder foods. For milk, based on Monaci et al., 29 bos d5 represents 50% of whey proteins, which represent 20% of milk proteins (i.e., 10% of total milk protein is bos d5). Similarly, bos d11 represents 35% of casein proteins, which represent 80% of milk proteins (i.e., 28% of total milk protein). 29 These were plotted against ELISA‐measured bos d5 and bos d11 (Figures S7 and S8).

Statistical analysis

For bos d5, baked milk foods (cookies, muffins, and pancakes) were analyzed using a one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post‐hoc test for multiple comparisons. Given the potential for unequal variances among fresh dairy foods (cheese, yogurt, and milk), a Welch's one‐way ANOVA was used, followed by Dunnett's T3 post‐hoc test. For bos d11, a one‐way ANOVA was used for both baked milk foods and fresh dairy foods, followed by Tukey's post‐hoc testing. All statistics were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, version 10.1.0 (316), San Diego, CA, USA).

To address the risk of inflated Type I error due to analyzing bos d5 and bos d11 separately, all post‐hoc p‐values were combined within each dataset and corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini‐Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure with q = 0.05. Both p‐values and FDR‐adjusted q‐values are reported, with the latter used to determine statistical significance. Post‐hoc p‐values were exported from GraphPad Prism and adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini‐Hochberg FDR procedure (q = 0.05) implemented in Microsoft® Excel® for Microsoft 365 MSO (Version 2507 Build 16.0.19029.20136) 64 bit. Full statistical values are included in Tables S2–S5.

RESULTS

Levels of bos d5 (β‐lactoglobulin) and bos d11 (β‐casein) in the international milk ladder (iMAP)

iMAP cookies, muffins, pancakes, cheese, yogurt, and milk were all tested for levels of bos d5 and bos d11 by ELISA (Figure 1). The levels of bos d5 in cookies, muffins, cheese, and milk followed a gradual increase in milk protein as expected in a ladder approach, but the pancake did not follow the same sequence as the published iMAP ladder, with a lower bos d5 content than the muffin. The levels of bos d5 were statistically different between 1 cookie and 1 muffin (p = <.0001, q = 0.0054) and between 1 muffin and 1 pancake (p = .0001, q = 0.0054), though for the latter, levels decreased rather than increased. Though levels in yogurt were lower than in cheese, this was not statistically significant (p = .0593, q = 0.1104), whereas the final three foods tested showed an incremental increase in bos d5.

The levels of bos d11 measured in the 100 mL portion of milk (last step of the milk ladder) were lower than those detected in cheese and yogurt. The 2017 iMAP ladder was designed using theoretical quantities of allergens based on the quantity of milk added to the food and the heating process. Figures S7 and S8 show the relative detection of milk allergens compared to the theoretical quantities expected. To understand some of these differences, the levels of bos d5 and bos d11 were measured in foods in different conditions.

Adjusted levels of bos d5 and bos d11 based on matrix effect

The matrix within which the milk protein is contained can affect the level of food proteins/allergens that are detected. After baking, the levels of bos d5 and bos d11 significantly dropped for most foods (Figure S3). Additionally, even within the same food type (muffin) with different recipes (iMAP vs. Mt. Sinai), the levels of detectable allergens differed before and after baking (Figure S1). Finally, the levels of bos d5 and bos d11 were also found to be similar between raw milk and pasteurized milk; however, bos d5 was significantly reduced in UHT milk (Figure S2). To validate the method used, the recovery of pure allergens was also assessed in spiking experiments (Figures S4–S6). The recovery of milk allergen bos d5 in cookies appeared higher than 100% in some cases, and the recovery of milk allergen in iMAP muffins and pancakes appeared lower than 100% (supplement Figure S5). These recovery rates were applied to the measured values from Figures 1 and 2 and adjusted as illustrated in Figures 3 and 4. These values are only indicative due to the limited and variable recovery data. Importantly, all the observations of order of magnitude still hold true after adjustment.

DISCUSSION

CMA is a complex presentation of food allergy, often encountered during infancy and early childhood. Recent data indicate that milk ladders may be a suitable strategy for the individualized management of CMA. To improve the safety and standardization of milk ladders, an understanding of the individualized milk proteins in the ladder foods is required. There are limitations to testing the milk proteins in foods as the results can be influenced by the validity of the assay used, ingredients used, the type of food used, the baking conditions, and how milk is processed industrially. In this study, we tested the bos d5 (β‐lactoglobulin) and bos d11 (β‐casein) composition of the iMAP milk ladder foods.

Choosing the most appropriate assay

Several analytical methods have been used to assess the protein content of foods containing milk. ELISA was chosen as it can quantify the level of a specific protein, while relying on the conformation of the protein which may change with heating. Additionally, the nature of the ELISA is a similar molecular mechanism of action to some types of allergies, where antibodies bind to protein. If processing alters the protein conformation, it can alter its detection by antibodies. An important difference is that the ELISA test uses IgG‐binding, whereas allergic reactions are mediated by IgE binding to the food allergen; thus, conformation changes in proteins may affect binding to these two antibodies differently. Although the thorough analysis presented here shows that detection by ELISA is influenced by several factors, this was still considered the most appropriate way to quantify milk protein in various foods given the inaccuracy of other methods such as the Kjeldahl method. 30 During human ingestion, it is likely that the effect of different matrices, baking, and processing will also influence how the immune system detects the allergen.

Levels of bos d5 and bos d11 in the International Milk Ladder

Previous studies have shown that the matrix and baking can impact milk proteins, 21 yet these proteins are often not measured in food ladders. In our analysis, the levels of bos d5 measured in pancakes per serving were lower than iMAP muffins. Muffins are currently the step before pancakes in the iMAP ladder based on total milk protein calculations. There was also the assumption that a shorter cooking time would imply a higher allergenicity. Levels of bos d5 in yogurt were also lower than cheese. During the cheese‐making process, casein is precipitated in the curd to form cheese, and most of the whey protein in solution is removed. The bos d5 detected in the cheese sample is likely because manufacturing conditions can be adjusted to increase the yield, and in some cases, this leads to the retention of some of the whey proteins. Interestingly, these findings are in line with the outcomes reported by Alesandri et al. 31 where the most abundant proteins found in Parmigiano‐Reggiano cheese were α‐lactalbumin and β‐lactoglobulin. Cheese is currently indicated as the step in the iMAP ladder before yogurt.

In the cookie, quantities of milk per serving were the lowest, with the density of the food matrix potentially having an impact. The heat levels measured with a thermocouple within the cookie were higher, as expected, than within the iMAP muffin (data not shown). In the muffin, where the quantities of milk per portion were higher, the temperature in the core was lower than that of the cookie, which was consistent with a previous study where different areas of the muffin were assessed (data not shown). 21 Interestingly, the pancake had the highest percentage of milk per portion but the lowest levels of bos d5 detected. It was also the most variable in results, possibly due to being the thinnest and the highest temperature achieved at the core. This step of the ladder was inconsistent with the previously proposed order based on calculated total milk protein content. The levels of bos d5 increased significantly from the baked foods to the non‐baked samples, which may necessitate an intermediate step. Further testing of baked cheese, for example, may provide information to include a step between the muffin and unbaked cheese.

The levels in yogurt were also not consistent with the previous order proposed by Venter 5 as the levels of bos d5 per portion were lower than cheddar. The fermentation process decreases levels of immunoreactivity of bos d5. In cheese manufacturing, most of the whey is drained off, explaining lower levels of bos d5 compared to milk, and the fermentation also decreases levels of immunoreactivity of bos d5, 32 , 33 , 34 though it is expected that the diversity in cheese manufacturing processes will also impact these levels in cheeses other than cheddar. This is an important consideration given the milk ladder is used internationally. Clinical tolerance of cheese and yogurt in cow's milk allergic children has also been reported. 31 , 35 The levels of bos d5 detected in pasteurized milk were consistent with the order of the iMAP ladder, and interestingly, there was no significant difference seen between pasteurized milk and raw milk. Here, pasteurization does not have a significant effect on the detection of bos d5 though the trend is a decrease. Mixed evidence in the literature probably reflects the lack of standardization of the pasteurization technique. 36 , 37 , 38 , 39

In contrast, levels of bos d11 detected did not follow a general increase across the ladder. It is known that bos d11 is not as temperature labile. 38 , 40 Similar to bos d5, the levels of bos d11 were much higher in cheddar, likely due to the high proportion being casein. The cheese aging process also decreases levels of β‐casein, which may explain variation seen between foods. 41 Levels in yogurt were very similar to cheese (statistically non‐significant). Fermentation is known to decrease casein allergenicity. 35 , 41 , 42 , 43 We found that the levels of bos d11 measured in the recommended serving size of milk were lower than those detected in cheese and yogurt. This is likely due to the levels in cheese being very high, and though the levels in yogurt are much lower, the quantity in the portion size (125 mL yogurt vs. 15 g of cheese) means the final levels are much higher, whereas the portion size of milk advised is 100 mL. A significant increase in milk protein levels, especially in bos d5, was observed between foods and uncooked milk. To understand the clinical relevance, this would need to be tested in a clinical study. However, in the meantime, physicians may choose to add in an additional step of 50 mL milk between the yogurt and 100 mL milk steps.

Adjusted levels of bos d5 and bos d11 based on matrix effect

Based on the spiking experiments, we plotted adjusted values against measured values of the two milk allergens. The order of the foods did not change based on the matrix effect. It is important to note that the spiking experiments were only based on a small number of experiments, and hence we would recommend only using the data measured. Further testing on larger sample sizes would be required to determine a more accurate effect of the matrix.

Several food ladders have been published in the last few years; however, only a few have quantified the levels of allergen in the foods, and this was limited either to bos d5 or the allergen was non‐specific. 24 , 44 The strength of our study is that this is the first study to take into account the effect of ingredients, baking, and processing on the ability to detect two major milk allergens, bos d5 and bos d11.

There are limitations to this study. First, a limited sample size of foods was tested, and given the variability observed, a larger sample size would be required to further qualify the matrix effect. Secondly, only two milk proteins were assessed. Many ELISA kits commercially available do not test further milk proteins. Several kits claim to test total milk proteins; however, they do not detail the specificity of the assays, and even a polyclonal antibody mix would need to be validated to ensure all milk proteins could be accurately detected. The ELISA kits in this study were chosen due to the extensive validation provided and the spiking results. Developing further methodologies to understand the full picture of all the proteins in the foods will be critical to understanding how the international milk ladder works clinically, but also to design a food ladder based on the totality of the sample rather than just two proteins. Nonetheless, we believe this study provides a significant advancement on the understanding of the current practices.

We have shown that the order of milk ladder foods can vary depending on the metric used, for example, whether based on measured levels of bos d5 (β‐lactoglobulin) or measured levels of bos d11 (β‐casein). This means that the allergenicity of a particular step may appear higher or lower depending on which protein is being quantified. For example, a food with low bos d5 content may still have relatively high β‐casein content, leading to a different relative position on the ladder depending on the protein marker selected.

From a clinical perspective, this observation raises an interesting and potentially important question: should a child's specific sensitization profile (e.g., bos d5 vs. bos d11) influence the order in which ladder foods are introduced? While this is a compelling hypothesis, our findings should not be taken as a call for immediate changes in clinical practice. Rather, they underscore the need for further research to determine whether protein‐specific sensitization is predictive of clinical reactivity and whether tailoring the ladder accordingly improves safety or outcomes. Given the variability found in this small sample of foods and previous studies, 21 it is evident that there is a need for standardization of ladders, especially given the burden on carers and HCPs to do these reintroductions. 18 , 45

Future steps will include linking the quantified allergen levels to actual clinical outcomes in patients. Establishing this correlation will enable us to move beyond a hypothesis‐generating exercise and provide an evidence‐based foundation for reordering the ladder as a clinical recommendation. We will also account for the fact that conformational changes to proteins during heating can affect antibody binding in an assay differently than they affect IgE binding and subsequent clinical reactivity. In addition, methodologies covering a more comprehensive range of milk proteins would be required to further understand the totality of allergens across foods in the milk ladder.

This study highlights how multiple components can influence the detection of milk allergens by ELISA: the chosen assay, ingredients used, the type of food used, the baking conditions, and how milk is processed industrially when reintroducing dairy products to children with CMA. This paper provides novel data on detectable proteins bos d5 and bos d11 in milk ladder foods, which contribute to our current understanding of milk ladders, critical in the management of CMA.
