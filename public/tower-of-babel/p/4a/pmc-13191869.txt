A retrospective study on isolated acetabular edge blunting in infants: clinical significance and proposed follow-up protocol
Authors: Nuri Koray Ülgen, Mehmet Tiftik, Ali Said Nazlıgül, Nihat Yiğit, Mehmet Orçun Akkurt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGraf-based ultrasound (US) is widely used to screen for developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH), a common orthopedic disorder, in infants. However, isolated acetabular edge blunting is occasionally reported, despite normal alpha and beta angles and adequate femoral head coverage. The clinical significance and follow-up strategies for this isolated finding remain unclear. In this study, we aimed to evaluate acetabular development in infants with isolated acetabular edge blunting and propose a rational follow-up protocol.MethodsThis retrospective study reviewed 5,190 reports on hip USs performed between June 2023 and July 2024. A total of 211 infants with isolated acetabular edge blunting and normal alpha-beta angles were included in this study. Demographic data, follow-up US results, and pelvic radiographs taken after 6 months were analyzed. Acetabular index (AI) angles and femoral head ossification center areas were measured and statistically compared.ResultsOf 211 infants, 144 (68.2%) were female. Spontaneous normalization to Graf type 1 A was observed in 58.7% of patients on the second US and 11.4% on the third US. At 6 months, pelvic radiographs were available for 37 infants, none of whom showed DDH according to the Tönnis classification. However, 54.0% had significantly higher AI angles on the blunting side than on the contralateral side (mean difference, 3.5°; p = 0.012). No significant difference was found in the femoral head ossification center area (p = 0.712).ConclusionsIsolated acetabular edge blunting does not progress to DDH but is associated with slightly higher AI values, suggesting subtle variations in acetabular development. In follow-up protocols, a second US and follow-up with a pelvic radiograph at 6 months is sufficient instead of unnecessary repeated ultrasonography checks. Larger prospective studies are required to clarify the long-term clinical significance of these findings.

Background

Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH), one of the most common pediatric orthopedic diseases, can lead to permanent disability, gait disturbance, and early degenerative arthritis if left untreated [1, 2]. Therefore, early diagnosis and treatment are extremely important. In Turkey and many other countries, hip ultrasonography (US) is routinely performed to screen for DDH [3]. US evaluation generally uses the alpha and beta angles according to the Graf method, and also takes into account femoral head coverage [4]. However, additional findings such as “blunt acetabular edge” are sometimes reported in clinical practice. This appearance is characterized by the loss of prominence of the lateral edge of the acetabulum and is sometimes reported even when the alpha-beta angles are normal and femoral head coverage exceeds 50% [5].

However, the clinical significance of these findings remain unclear, and there is insufficient evidence in the literature to determine whether it is a potential risk factor or an insignificant variant. To date, no studies have examined the relationship between an isolated acetabular edge blunting and the potential of developing DDH in normal hips. This uncertainty may lead to unnecessary, repeated US and orthopedic consultations.

This study evaluated the acetabular index (AI) angles in infants with only acetabular edge blunting, normal alpha-beta angles, and femoral head coverage after 6 months of age. The acetabular index (AI) measured on plain pelvic radiographs is considered an objective indicator of acetabular development and plays a complementary role in the evaluation and follow-up of DDH, particularly after the age of six months [6, 7]. No studies in the literature have investigated the clinical significance of acetabular edge blunting. This study is the first to systematically examine the AI angles in patients with acetabular edge blunting. New data is presented to determine whether this finding was an actual structural change affecting future acetabular development, or merely an imaging artifact. Our results may contribute to the prevention of unnecessary follow-ups and repeat US, leading to a more accurate identification of at-risk cases.

Methods

This retrospective study was conducted at SBU Sincan Training and Research Hospital. We reviewed hip ultrasound (US) reports performed between June 2023 and July 2024. The local ethics committee approved the study protocol, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent revisions. We retrospectively analyzed data from infants who underwent hip US as part of DDH screening found to have only a blunted acetabular edge. The US measurements were performed by a pediatric radiologist. Representative hip ultrasonography images illustrating normal acetabular morphology and isolated acetabular edge blunting were obtained using standard Graf planes and are presented in Fig. 1.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: normal alpha and beta angles according to Graf’s US classification, femoral head coverage greater than 50%, and at least one pelvic radiograph taken at or after the sixth month. The exclusion criteria included abnormal alpha or beta angles, femoral head coverage less than 50%, history of co-existing medical conditions with the potential to affect hip development, and incomplete follow-up data.

A total of 5,190 hip US reports performed within the specified date range were reviewed to identify patients with isolated acetabular edge blunting. Demographic data (age at presentation, sex, and affected side), number of US scans, and findings at each follow-up were recorded for the 211 patients included in the study. AI measurements were performed in patients with pelvic radiographs at 6 months and later.

Measurements were performed by two independent orthopedic surgeons using a standard technique: the Hilgenreiner line was drawn, and the angle between this line and the line extending from the triradiate cartilage to the lateral border of the acetabulum was recorded as the AI. Measurements were repeated 1 month later, and the average values ​​were calculated for each patient. Both hips were compared in patients with bilateral acetabular blunting and the blunt side was compared with the contralateral side in patients with unilateral acetabular blunting.

In addition, the area of ​​the ossified nucleus of the femoral head was measured planimetrically in both hips using anteroposterior pelvic radiographs. Measurements were performed by two observers using the Picture Archiving and Communication System freehand drawing tool to trace the contour, and the area (mm²) was recorded. The data from the two observers were averaged and the differences between the two sides were assessed using paired t-tests.

Data were analyzed using SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics, Version XX, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (min–max), and categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages. Normal distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The paired t-test was used for normally distributed continuous variables and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for non-normally distributed variables. The chi-squared test was used for categorical variables. In unilateral cases, the AI ​​angles of the blunt and contralateral sides were compared, and bilateral cases were evaluated using normal reference values. Spearman’s correlation was used for the correlation analyses. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Of 211 patients included in this study, 144 were female (68.2%) and 67 were male (31.8%), with a significantly higher proportion of females (p < 0.001). The lesion side was recorded as left in 102 patients (48.3%), right in 62 (29.4%), and bilateral in 47 (22.3%). No significant difference was found between sexes in terms of side distribution (p = 0.41).

The mean age at the first presentation was 71 days (range, 60–90 days), and the mean age at the second presentation was 102 days (range, 90–124 days). Of the patients with acetabular edge blunting detected at the first presentation, 124 (58.7%) had normal type 1 A hips on the second US examination and 24 (11.4%) were considered normal at the third follow-up visit. Most infants with acetabular edge blunting detected at first presentation returned to normal during follow-up, which was statistically significant (p < 0.001). A final evaluation could not be performed because 26 of the 63 patients with persistent acetabular edge blunting at 6 months were not referred to our clinic.

The AI angles were measured in 37 patients with available pelvic radiographs starting at six months. The mean AI angle was 24.27°. Acetabular blunting was detected on the left side in 25 patients, right side in 7 patients, and bilaterally in 5 patients. DDH according to the Tönnis staging was not detected in any patient. The AI ​angle of the blunt side was significantly higher than that of the contralateral side in 20 patients (54.0%) (mean difference, 3.5°; p = 0.012). The contralateral AI angle was higher, although not significantly, in eight patients (21.6%) (p = 0.27). The AI ​​angles of the two sides were similar in nine patients (24.3%) (p = 0.88) and the AI ​​angles of the patients with bilateral blunting were symmetrical (p = 0.72). To further investigate the intra-patient difference, we performed a paired analysis on the 32 infants with unilateral involvement. As illustrated in Fig. 3, which details the individual changes between the normal and affected sides, the AI was significantly higher on the side with acetabular edge blunting compared to the contralateral normal hip (p = 0.012). These findings suggest that while not progressing to frank dysplasia, acetabular edge blunting is associated with a subtle but consistent increase in the acetabular index.

No significant relationship was found between sex and AI (p = 0.46). Furthermore, no correlation was observed between the side distribution and AI difference (Spearman’s rho = 0.12; p = 0.39). The ossific nucleus was observed in the radiographs of 25 patients, and there was no statistically significant difference between the measured areas of the right and left sides (p = 0.712).

Discussion

DDH, one of the most important diseases encountered in pediatric orthopedics, can be diagnosed early using neonatal US and radiographic screening, thus preventing long-term complications with appropriate treatment. However, the clinical significance of certain US parameters, such as acetabular edge blunting, has not been clearly established. When observed in conjunction with a distorted alpha angle and decreased femoral head coverage, acetabular edge blunting is considered a significant finding in DDH. However, the presence of acetabular edge blunting alone, despite the angles being within normal limits and with adequate femoral head coverage, leads to uncertainties during patient follow-up. Although the literature suggests that this finding may be a temporary condition of the developing acetabulum, no clear consensus currently exists on the follow-up protocol. Studies generally recommend frequent ultrasonographic examinations [7, 8]. However, many physicians, especially pediatricians, are unsure of how to manage these findings. In our study, we propose a more rational follow-up protocol based on follow-up data of patients diagnosed with isolated acetabular edge blunting. We believe that the AI angles and ossific nucleus area measurements obtained in this study will guide future research.

In our study, a female predominance (68.2%) was identified in children with isolated acetabular edge blunting. This is similar to the significant female predominance in all reported studies on DDH [1, 7, 9]. Further, the rate found in our study is similar to the sex distribution reported in a series examining type 2 A immature hips [5, 8, 10]. Given this similarity, isolated acetabular edge blunting has been suggested as a sign of immaturity.

When examining data on which hip was affected, the left side was affected in 48.3% of patients, the right side in 29%, and both sides in 22.3%. Left dominance in patients with DDH has been demonstrated in many previous studies [1, 3, 8]. Furthermore, the bilaterality rate we observed was similar to that reported in a large series of DDH studies, with published bilaterality rates between 25% and 30% [3]. Isolated acetabular edge blunting alone did not cause hip dysplasia. Although its clinical significance is unknown, its similarity to the epidemiology of DDH in immature hips is striking.

Of the cases with acetabular edge blunting detected at initial presentation, 58.7% were identified as normal type 1 A hips on the second US, and 11.4% of the patients were identified as having normal hips on the third US. Although no studies have reported on isolated acetabular edge blunting, studies examining immature type 2 A hips have found that more than 95% of them are converted to normal hips within three months [3, 11].

In our study, although some patients were lost to follow-up at 6 months, 17.5% of the infants with acetabular edge blunting were still diagnosed with acetabular edge blunting on previous US at 6 months. None of the infants had DDH on hip radiographs. No significant difference was found between the sides of the bilateral ossific nuclei. It is well known from clinical experience that isolated acetabular edge blunting does not progress to hip dysplasia, and this finding was objectively demonstrated in a small sample in our study. However, noteworthy results were obtained from the AI angle measurements. In 54% of infants with radiographs, the AI was significantly higher on the side with acetabular edge blunting than on the contralateral side (p = 0.012). An increase in the AI ​​angle is reportedly associated with acetabular dysplasia [12]. These results suggest that isolated acetabular edge blunting alone does not cause hip dysplasia. However, prospective studies with larger samples and longer durations are needed to determine the clinical significance of this difference in the AI ​​angle. Isolated acetabular edge blunting may be an early indicator of a limited clinically significant acetabular developmental disorder or dysplasia that has not yet become apparent.

Although pelvic radiography becomes informative after 4–6 months of age due to the appearance of the femoral head ossification center, radiographic evaluation before this period may be limited by ongoing physiological acetabular maturation and incomplete ossification. In addition, unnecessary radiation exposure should be avoided in early infancy. Therefore, consistent with conventional clinical practice, we recommend performing a pelvic radiograph at 6 months of age for infants with isolated acetabular edge blunting. The sixth month represents a well-established and reliable time point for radiographic assessment of acetabular development [13, 14].

Previous studies have not described the follow-up of patients with isolated acetabular edge blunting. Current general recommendation is for repeated US checks at 3–4-week intervals and hip X-ray evaluations after 6 months [13, 14]. However, frequent US checks can cause unnecessary anxiety among parents of infants. Based on our study findings, we propose a streamlined follow-up protocol to minimize parental anxiety and unnecessary healthcare utilization. Infants diagnosed with isolated acetabular edge blunting on initial screening should undergo a single control ultrasound after an interval of 4 weeks to rule out missed dysplasia or progression. If the finding persists on this second ultrasound, no further ultrasonic follow-up is required; instead, a definitive evaluation with a pelvic radiograph should be scheduled at 6 months of age. This approach ensures that pathology is not missed while avoiding the burden of repetitive monthly ultrasound examinations (Fig. 4).

This study has some limitations. First, it was conducted retrospectively at a single center, which may have limited the generalizability of the results. Furthermore, the study population consisted of a relatively small sample, and some patients were lost to follow-up, especially after 6 months. This may have negatively affected the evaluation of long-term outcomes and statistical power. While the use of a single pediatric radiologist provides an advantage in terms of intra-observer reliability, another limitation is the inability to assess inter-observer differences. Although AI angle measurements and ossific nucleus area assessments were performed in our study, more advanced imaging methods or three-dimensional analyses were not used. Finally, the absence of comparable studies on isolated acetabular edge blunting limited the interpretation of the clinical significance of the findings. Confirmation of these results with larger sample sizes and multicenter prospective studies is necessary.

Conclusion

This is the first study to systematically evaluate the acetabular development in infants with isolated acetabular edge blunting. Our findings indicate that isolated acetabular edge blunting does not progress to DDH; however, it may be associated with a significant increase in the AI. This suggests that acetabular edge blunting may represent a subtle acetabular developmental anomaly, or an early indicator of dysplasia that is not yet clinically apparent. Furthermore, the study demonstrated that instead of repeated and unnecessary US examinations, a follow-up protocol consisting of a single repeat US and pelvic radiograph at 6 months may be sufficient for infants with isolated acetabular edge blunting. This approach not only reduces parental anxiety but also provides a more rational follow-up strategy for clinicians. Large-scale, long-term prospective studies are required to validate these findings and clarify the true clinical significance of acetabular edge blunting.
