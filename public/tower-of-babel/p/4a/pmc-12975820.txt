Lipopolysaccharide-Induced Nasal Inflammation Swiftly Spreads Into the Olfactory Bulb, Accelerating its Network Activity and Uncoupling it from Breathing
Authors: Armando Ortega-Xique, Benito Ordaz, Jonathan-Julio Lorea-Hernández, Fernando Peña-Ortega
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNasal inflammation-induced olfactory dysfunction is caused by sensory processing alterations in and beyond the olfactory epithelium, likely through the spreading of peripheral inflammation into the olfactory bulb (OB), damaging its neuronal computations. Although the consequences of chronic nasal inflammation have extensively described in animal models, their presence upon acute inflammation has not been evaluated. Thus, we tested whether acute nasal inflammation induced by a single lipopolysaccharide (LPS) intranasal application swiftly spreads into the OB, focusing on its microglia, interleukin 1 beta (IL-1β) levels, neuronal network activity, and olfactory function. We found that a single intranasal LPS application leads to early microglial morphological changes at OB granule cell-layer, along with increased IL-1β staining, the following day. Intranasal LPS application decreases slow OB network activity and increases fast OB network activity, while uncoupling its slow activity from breathing. Moreover, acute LPS-induced nasal inflammation dampens odor-induced OB network activation, correlating with deficient olfactory detection and discrimination. We conclude that acute LPS-induced nasal inflammation quickly spreads into the OB, mildly activating its microglia and disrupting its network activity and odor response, which likely contribute to olfactory impairment. These acute central nervous system alterations, whose mechanism remain to be determined, should be considered when diagnosing and treating inflammation-induced olfactory dysfunctions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10753-026-02467-w.Keywords: Microglia, Olfactory bulb, Inflammation, Network activity

Introduction

Chronic nasal inflammation associated with autoimmune/immune diseases, rhinitis, and pathogens leads to olfactory dysfunction in humans [11, 31, 50], and its severity is directly associated with peripheral inflammation levels [50]. In rodents, chronic intranasal administration of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) induces olfactory impairment [31, 44, 68], which is correlated with the level of neuroinflammation [31, 44, 68]. LPS is a component of the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria that activates the innate immune system by binding to a variety of receptors, including Toll-like receptor 4, triggering signaling pathways leading to the production of pro-inflammatory mediators, including interleukin 1 beta (IL-1β), that recruit immune cells and cause inflammation [48]. Thus, LPS-induced effects can be reversed by anti-inflammatory drugs [10].

Olfactory dysfunction has classically been linked to chronic inflammation of the olfactory epithelium [19–21, 31, 44], mainly induced by the local deleterious effects of infiltrating cells and proinflammatory mediators [19–21, 31, 44]. However, growing evidence indicates that chronic inflammation spreads into the olfactory bulb (OB) [6, 19–21, 31], resulting in chronic neuroinflammation [6, 19–21, 31, 33], that ultimately contributes to olfactory dysfunction [31, 68].

Nonetheless, long-term inflammation may not be necessary for inducing hyposmia [4, 35], since acute nasal bacterial inflammation (e.g., acute bacterial rhinosinusitis) [4, 35], can also rapidly produce olfactory deficits in humans [4, 35]. Similarly, in rodents and humans, peripheral or intranasal LPS administration can elicit signs of peripheral and central inflammation within hours [6, 10, 11, 19–21, 32, 59], with peak effects observed the following day [11, 32]. These acute signs of inflammation can include microglial activation in the OB and throughout the brain [32] and damage to olfactory sensory neurons (OSN; [10], the later acutely reversed by anti-inflammatory treatment [10]. Although acute systemic administration of a small amount of LPS did not induce olfactory dysfunction in humans [59], this study examined whether acute nasal LPS-induced inflammation triggers microglial activation, disrupts OB function and affects olfactory processing in mice. We found that a single intranasal LPS application leads to rapid and mild microglial activation in the OB granule cell-layer, as evidenced by some early morphological changes, increases IL-1β staining, while simultaneously decreasing slow OB network activity and increasing fast OB network activity (i.e., neuronal network acceleration). Slow OB network activity was decoupled from breathing (which remained unaltered). Moreover, acute LPS-induced nasal inflammation dampens odor-induced OB network activation and causes deterioration in olfactory detection and discrimination. These acute alterations, whose mechanisms remain to be determined, should be considered when diagnosing and treating bacterial inflammation-induced olfactory dysfunctions.

Materials and Methods

Animals

All experimental procedures were approved by the Bioethics Committee at the Institute of Neurobiology, UNAM, and were performed according to the Official Mexican Standard for the Use and Care of Laboratory Animals (Norma Oficial Mexicana NOM-062-ZOO-1999).

Adult male (n = 71) and female (n = 22) C57BL/6 mice (3–5 months old) were obtained from the Institute of Neurobiology, UNAM Vivarium, and housed in collective cages (max. 5 animals). Animals were maintained at a controlled room temperature (22 ± 1 °C) in a normal 12 h light/12 h dark cycle (lights on at 7:00 a.m.), with water and food ad libitum.

Intranasal LPS administration

Before any experimental manipulation, animals were habituated for five consecutive days to the testing room, the experimental instruments as well as to the experimenter. In a group of animals, body surface temperature was measured using a non-contact infrared thermometer (Yvwell YT-1; range 32–43 °C, accuracy ± 0.2 °C and working distance 0–5 cm). To do so, mice were removed from their home cage and gently restrained against the lid and the sensor was aimed at their back a distance < 2 cm, without contact [38]. Temperature was recorded before and 24 h after the intranasal administration described next. Nasal inflammation was induced by bilateral intranasal administration of 10 µl LPS solution (1 mg/ml, E. coli O111:B4; Sigma, St. MO, USA, in sterile saline) or the equivalent volume of sterile saline [19–21]. This procedure was performed under anesthesia with Sevoflurane 3% (Svofast, Baxter, Mexico). Once anesthetized, animals were held by their shoulders with their head positioned vertically. A micropipette tip approached at 45º one nostril to apply drops of the desired solution. Once a drop was inspired by ongoing breathing, subsequent ones were applied until the totality of the 10 µl was administered. Then, animals were held in the described position for 20 s to ensure complete administration. The same procedure was repeated with the contralateral nostril. After recovery, animals were videorecorded for 5 min to evaluate their nasal irritation by quantifying instances of sneezing and nasal rubbing [33]. Twenty-four hours post-intranasal inoculation, mice underwent some of the following experimental protocols:

Open Field Test

Locomotion was evaluated using the open field test [39, 40, 53, 55]. For this test, animals were placed in one of the four corners of an open field acrylic arena (48 cm × 26 cm × 20 cm,covered with sawdust), and allowed to explore it for 5 min, while videotaped for offline analysis [39, 40, 53, 55]. Total distance and mean speed were quantified using the SMART v.3 software (Panlab, Harvard Apparatus; [39, 40]).

Social Interaction

The three-chamber test for social interaction was used to assess sociability [39, 40]. The apparatus consists of an acrylic rectangular box divided into three identical compartments (40 cm × 20 cm × 15 cm) connected by entrances. Each lateral compartment contained a transparent acrylic box with perforations on the walls. The floor was covered with new sawdust before every test. The experimental subject was habituated to the apparatus 5 min before the beginning of tests. For sociability testing, a conspecific (sample subject), matching age and sex of the tested subject, was placed inside a perforated acrylic box located in one lateral chamber (randomly chosen). In the contralateral chamber, the acrylic box was left empty. The test subject was placed in the central chamber and allowed to explore all compartments freely for 10 min. Social choice was determined as the time spent exploring the box containing the conspecific against the time exploring the contralateral empty box (i.e. none; [39, 40]).

Buried Food Test

Olfactory detection was evaluated with the buried food test [7, 23]. In brief, animals were habituated for 5 min to the experimental cage (42 × 20 × 20 cm) with 3 cm thick, clean sawdust covering the floor. Then, animals were removed from the experimental cage, and a small piece of chocolate cookie ( ∼100 mg) was hidden in a random corner. Mice were returned to the experimental cage and their latency to reach the hidden food was quantified [7, 23, 33]. We considered that the animals successfully found the cookie when they held it with both their forepaws. A limit of 600 s was set to perform this test. To evaluate the animals’ locomotion and motivation, we repeated the same test but with a visible piece of cookie [7, 23]). This test was performed in two sets of animals: 1.- normally fed (Fig. 2) and 2.- food deprived for 24 h (food deprivation starting right after intranasal treatment; Supplementary Fig. 1; [7]).

Consumption of Palatable Food

To assess the animals’ ability to enjoy palatable food, each animal was provided with a small piece of chocolate cookie ( ∼100 mg), and intake (% of initial weight) was quantified during a session that ended when the cookie was fully consumed or when the animal ceased interacting with it [61]. The procedure was repeated 5–10 min later.

Habituation/dishabituation Test

To evaluate olfactory desensitization and discrimination, animals underwent the habituation/dishabituation test [9, 23]. In brief, animals were habituated to the experimental cage (27 × 17 × 12 cm) for 30 min before the presentation of any stimuli. The stimuli consisted of 5 µl of either distilled water, vanilla or vinegar (in this order) applied on a 2 × 2 cm filter paper square, which was adhered to a metallic plate and placed over the lid [23]. Distilled water was presented twice, followed by three consecutive presentations of vanilla and three consecutive presentations of vinegar. Each presentation lasted 3 min and was separated by a 1-min interval. Animals were videotaped for offline analysis. In addition, the exploration time was recorded manually when the animal approached the filter paper at a < 1 cm distance [23]. The exploration of each presentation was quantified as the percentage of net exploration. The discrimination index was calculated by subtracting the exploration during the final vanilla stimulation to the exploration during the first vinegar stimulation [66].

OB Network Activity Recording

To evaluate OB local field potential (LFP), animals were anesthetized with urethane (1.6 g/kg, intraperitoneal) and affixed to a stereotaxic frame. A craniotomy was performed over the OB to introduce a stainless-steel 2 MΩ bipolar electrode. The electrode tip was aimed at the OB granule cell layer using the following coordinates from Bregma: AP: 4.8 mm and ML: 0.8 mm. The electrodes were inserted −1.5 mm from the dura at a speed of 100 µm/min [46]. Baseline activity (without any odor stimulation) was recorded for 30 min, followed by four ethyl butyrate stimulations delivered via an olfactometer for 10 s each, with a 170-s interstimulus interval [23, 27]. At the end of the experiment, animals were euthanized with a sodium pentobarbital overdose while recordings continued to verify the level of electronic noise [1, 27]. Electrophysiological signals were filtered (0.1–300 Hz) and amplified (100 x), with a wide-band amplifier (Grass Instruments), and digitized at 1 kHz, with a MiniDigi2A and Axoscope (Molecular Devices), for offline analysis.

The OB LFP power spectrum (0–52 Hz, before the 60 Hz filter, with a 0.6 Hz resolution) was obtained using the fast Fourier transform (FFT) with a Hamming window (50% overlap) in Clampfit (Molecular Devices). Random 10-s windows in the absence of odors were analyzed, as well as all 10-s odor presentations. The relative power of each frequency under basal conditions was quantified as its contribution to the total spectrum power, whereas the odor-induced power change was calculated by setting the power at any given frequency during basal conditions at 100% [3, 24, 27, 49, 64]. An exploratory paired statistical comparison between the values at each frequency between groups was performed before multiple comparison correction. Then, in those frequency ranges where uncorrected statistical differences were detected, a group comparison of the average values within these ranges was made. Cluster-based permutation analysis [37], using the permutest function for Matlab [17], was performed to evaluate the presence of changes in the frequency ranges just described. Although some of the empirically defined frequency rages were detected in the cluster-based permutation analysis, their clustering did not reach significance (p ≈ 0.2). Finally, we applied a False Discovery Rate correction to evaluate if a given isolated data point exhibited a significant difference independently of the number of comparisons performed (Pinedo-Vargas et al., 2025). An identical approach was used for the cross- and auto-correlograms obtained from the analysis described next. The cross-correlation of OB activity and breathing was evaluated, for the entirety of the basal recording period (30 min), for OB LFP filtered to the range of respiratory frequencies observed in this study (2–5 Hz), using the xcorr function with a lag of ± 1.5 s in Matlab (Version 2023b) [41]. Breathing was recorded with a piezoelectric device attached to the thorax, whose signal was filtered (0.1–300 Hz) and amplified (10x) with a wide-band amplifier (Grass Instruments), and digitized at 1 kHz, with a MiniDigi2A and Axoscope (Molecular Devices) [23, 25] for offline analysis. The autocorrelation of breathing was evaluated using the autocorr function within a lag of ± 1 s in Matlab (Version 2023b) [18, 47]. To evaluate breathing periodicity using the autocorrelogram we measured the ratio of its second peak over the maximal autocorrelation (always observed at lag zero-first peak) [18, 47].

Immunofluorescence

To evaluate the morphology of microglia and the levels of IL-1β in the OB granule cell-layer, as well as the terminals from OSN at the glomerular cell-layer, mice that received intranasal administration of LPS or saline but not undergone any other experimental procedure, were deeply anesthetized with pentobarbital (Pisabental®) and transcardially perfused with 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) dissolved in PBS. Brains were removed and left in 4% PFA for 48 h. Afterwards, they were immersed daily in sucrose solutions at increasing concentrations (10%, 20% and 30% in PBS) for cryoprotection. The OBs were sliced (30 µm thick) using a cryostat (Leica CM1850, Leica Biosystems). Free-floating slices were repeatedly washed with PBS and sequentially exposed to 30% hydrogen peroxide, 1% sodium borohydride and 10% Triton X, with PBS washes between each treatment. Then, sections were incubated for 48 h with one of the following primary antibodies: anti-Iba1 (ionizing calcium-binding adapter molecule 1; dilution 1:2000; Wako Chemicals, Richmond, VA), anti-IL-1β (dilution 1:2000; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) or anti-OMP (olfactory marker protein; dilution 1:500; Abcam, UK), in PBS containing 0.5% Triton at 4 °C. Next, the sections were washed with PBS and incubated at room temperature for 2 h with an Alexa Fluor 568 or Alexa Fluor 488 anti-rabbit secondary antibody (dilution 1:500; Wako Chemicals, Richmond, VA) in PBS containing 0.5% Triton. Finally, slices were incubated with DAPI (1:5000; Thermo Fischer Scientific) in PBS and subsequently mounted on glass slides and covered with Entellan and glass coverslips. Fluorescence microphotographs were obtained using an Axio Imager 2 (ZEISS) fluorescence microscope equipped with an Axiocam 305 (ZEISS) camera and a 40 × objective. In slices processed simultaneously and microphotographed with identical settings (obtained for 3 animals per group) Iba1 intensity, as its Signal to Noise Ratio (SNR), was measured in an area of 697.57 × 526.06 µm located in the granule cell-layer. In slices processed simultaneously and microphotographed with identical settings (obtained for 3 animals per group) IL-1β average fluorescence intensity was measured in an area of 697.57 × 526.06 µm located in the granule cell-layer. In slices processed simultaneously and microphotographed with identical settings (obtained for 5 animals per group) the area covered with OMP signal and the average fluorescence intensity were measured in an area of 697.57 × 526.06 µm located in the glomerular-layer. A group of slices from the same animals described in this paragraph were stained with Nissl (0.1% cresyl violet, Sigma, USA) [40]

Microglial Morphometric Analysis

Digital processing and morphometric analysis of stained granule cell-layer microglia was performed with ImageJ [54]. Sections were chosen aiming to match the granule cell layer region where electrophysiological recordings were obtained in other animals. For the morphometric analysis, fourteen slices for each group (obtained from four saline-treated animals group and from five LPS-treated animals) were used to select microglial cells clearly differentiated from background noise signal and with intact cell processes. Images were converted to 8-bit grayscale format, convoluted (5 × 5 kernel), and denoised with the Despeckle and Remove Outliers functions (radius: 2-pixel, threshold: 50). Binary masks were generated by adjusting the image threshold using the maximum entropy method, aiming to isolate and highlight labeled cells. Microglial morphometric values were obtained with the Measure function (ImageJ), rendering the following outputs: cell area, cell perimeter, aspect ratio, circularity, roundness and solidity [13, 54]. Branching parameters (number of primary branches, number of branches, average branch length, maximum branch length, number of junctions, and number of triple and quadruple points) were obtained from binarized images using the Skeletonize function (2D/3D). Sholl analysis was performed with the following parameters: start radius = 6.125 µm; step size = 2.522 µm; end radius = 50.180 µm.

Statistical Analysis

Normal data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Outliers were identified and removed with the Grubbs’ method for groups comprised of ≤ 6 samples or with the Iterative Grubbs’ method for groups comprised of ≥ 7 samples. Relative and normalized powers, as well as comparisons involving more than 2 groups, were compared through an analysis of variance (ANOVA) or its corresponding non-parametric test (Kruskal–Wallis). To compare between paired quantifications, or for comparisons involving only 2 groups, a Student’s t-test or its corresponding non-parametric test (Mann–Whitney) was used. Other statistical comparisons are described in the Results section. Data are shown in graphs as the mean ± the standard error of the mean (SEM), and differences were considered significant with a p value ≤ 0.05. Unless otherwise indicated, the n value represents the number of animals used for each quantification.

Results

Intranasal LPS Triggers Acute Local Inflammation and Sickness Behavior

As indicators of acute local nasal inflammation, nasal rubbing and sneezing were quantified immediately after intranasal LPS injection [33], revealing that LPS-treated animals exhibited more sneezes (15.63 ± 1.18 in 5 min, n = 24; Fig. 1A) and nasal rubbings (10.38 ± 1.12 in 5 min, n = 24; Fig. 1B) than saline-treated animals (8.35 ± 0.90 and 7.90 ± 1.13 in 5 min, respectively, n = 20 and 22, respectively, Mann–Whitney, p < 0.05 for both cases; Fig. 1A and B). Then, to determine whether the single intranasal LPS administration would induce acute sickness [58], we initially measured body temperature and found no change in this parameter when compared before and 24 h after intranasal application of LPS (Δ0.33 ± 0.31 ºC, n = 7, Mann–Whitney, p > 0.05, Fig. 1C) or saline (Δ0.21 ± 0.46 ºC, n = 7, Mann–Whitney, p > 0.05; Fig. 1C). Additionally, water and food consumption during 24 h was quantified [58], Fig. 1D and E). Our findings indicated that LPS-treated animals consumed less food (2.78 ± 0.56 g, n = 22, Fig. 1E) and water (1.83 ± 0.26 ml, n = 23; Fig. 1D) than saline-treated animals (3.59 ± 0.31 g and 2.77 ± 0.33 ml, respectively, n = 22 and 21, respectively, Mann–Whitney, p < 0.02 in both cases; Fig. 1D and E). As another sign of sickness behavior, we evaluated animals’ consumption of a small piece of chocolate cookie (a palatable food) and found that despite that LPS-treated animals seem to consumed less chocolate cookie the second time it was provided to them (43.19 ± 12.91%, n = 6, Fig. 1F), compared to saline-treated animals (67.05 ± 14.08%, n = 6, Fig. 1F), this difference was not significant (Kruskal–Wallis, p = 0.28; Dunn, p > 0.99). No difference in consumption was found during the first chocolate cookie presentation either (Saline: 72.70 ± 17.27%, n = 6 vs LPS: 84.45 ± 15.55%, n = 6, Kruskal–Wallis, p = 0.28; Dunn, p > 0.99; Fig. 1F). Further assessing sickness behavior, sociability was evaluated as the difference in exploration of boxes containing either a conspecific or an empty one (Fig. 1F; [39, 40]. While saline-treated animals spend more time exploring a box containing a conspecific (167.57 ± 9.76 s, n = 6, Fig. 1G) than an empty one (93.82 ± 7.82 s, n = 6, Mann–Whitney, p = 0.002; Fig. 1G), LPS-treated animals only exhibited a trend towards spending more time exploring a box containing a conspecific (186.98 ± 20.65 s, n = 6; Fig. 1G) than an empty one (119.12 ± 15.08 s, n = 6, Mann–Whitney, p = 0.06; Fig. 1G). Our final assessment of sickness behavior was locomotion, which showed that LPS-treated animals traveled less distance in an open field (1804.81 ± 139.58 cm in 5 min, n = 19; Fig. 1G), than saline-treated animals (2156.62 ± 92.74 cm in 5 min, n = 17, t-student, p = 0.048; Fig. 1H). Despite differences in traveled distance, we found no difference in movement speed between groups (Saline: 8.18 ± 0.46 cm/s, n = 17 vs LPS: 7.09 ± 0.57 cm/s, n = 19, t-student, p = 0.15; Fig. 1I).

Intranasal LPS Acutely Reduces Olfactory Detection and Discrimination

To evaluate whether intranasal LPS acutely affects olfaction (detection, desensitization and discrimination), we performed the buried food and habituation-dishabituation tests, in normally fed or in food deprived animals (only in the former test), 24 h after the intranasal injection of LPS or saline. In the buried food test, fed LPS-treated animals took longer to reach the buried food than fed saline-treated animals (Saline: 59.61 ± 8.16 s, n = 18 vs LPS: 101.10 ± 16.36 s, n = 21; t-student, p = 0.038; Fig. 2A). We found no differences in the time taken to reach visible food between groups (Saline: 50.24 ± 17.55 s, n = 18 vs LPS: 41.41 ± 13.14 s, n = 21, t-student, p = 0.98). Considering a hyposmia threshold of 93.23 s (set at control group mean + standard deviation), 8 LPS-treated animals (out of 21; 38%) exhibited olfactory detection impairment while only 2 control animals (out of 18; 11%) did so. When the buried food test was performed in food-deprived animals, despite similar performance as fed animals, no significant differences in the time taken to reach buried food were found (Saline: 44.13 ± 11.73 s, n = 8 vs LPS: 76.89 ± 24.95 s, n = 9; p = 0.72; Supplementary Fig. 1). However, considering a hyposmia threshold of 77.33 s (set at control group mean + standard deviation), up to 4 LPS-treated animals (out of 9; 44%) exhibited olfactory detection impairment, while only 1 control animals (out of 8; 12%) did so. These proportions are very similar to those found in the normally fed groups. We found no differences in the time taken to reach visible food between food-deprived groups (Saline: 62.83 ± 23.22 s, n = 6 vs LPS: 23.50 ± 2.51 s, n = 6, Mann–Whitney, p = 0.68).

Although LPS-treated animals showed normal odor habituation (i.e., reduced exploration of the repeated odor), they exhibited reduced discrimination between odors (Fig. 2B), as evidenced by the limited exploration time during the first vinegar presentation (preceded by repeated and habituated vanilla) (15.08 ± 3.41% of net exploration, n = 19; Mann–Whitney, p = 0.01; Fig. 2B) compared to saline-treated animals (30.43 ± 3.04% of net exploration, n = 19; Fig. 2B). Thus, LPS-treated animals showed a lower odor discrimination index (10.16 ± 4.47% of net exploration, n = 19; Fig. 2C) compared to saline-treated animals (26.25 ± 3.79% of net exploration, n = 19 in both groups; Mann–Whitney, p = 0.009; Fig. 2C). It is worth noting the deficit in odor discrimination just described is not observed when vanilla was presented after habituated water (Fig. 2B), likely because the high novelty of vanilla contrasting to the familiarity of animals with water could increase their discriminability [5, 29, 51]. It is also worth noting that vinegar strongly activates the trigeminal system [60], which is highly impaired during inflammation [22, 31], likely contributing to a reduction in discriminability between vinegar and vanilla in LPS-treated animals.

A Single Intranasal LPS Application Does Not Affect OB Dimensions, Qualitative Cellular Organization or its Innervation from the Olfactory Epithelium

Considering that chronic nasal inflammation has been related to changes in OB dimensions [19–21, 31], cellular organization [15, 31, 56] or its innervation from the olfactory epithelium [10, 28, 31, 67], we evaluated these parameters after acute local nasal inflammation (Fig. 3). We found no apparent macroscopic differences in OB shape between groups (Fig. 3A), which is corroborated with the absence of significant differences between groups when comparing their OB length (Saline: 25.52 ± 0.78 mm, n = 15 vs LPS: 26.21 ± 1.40 mm, n = 15, Mann–Whitney, p = 0.69) or width (Saline: 23.26 ± 5.27 mm, n = 15 vs LPS: 23.80 ± 1.06 mm, n = 14, Mann–Whitney, p = 0.89). Qualitative evaluation of OB layering in Nissl-stained coronal sections (Fig. 3D), or similar sections co-immunolabeled for IL-1β and Iba1 (Fig. 3E), revealed the typical OB layering and cellular integrity was intact in both groups. Finally, considering that LPS-induced nasal inflammation can damage OSN in the olfactory epithelium [10, 28, 31, 67], we evaluated their synaptic terminals at the OB glomerular layer, by means of OMP labelling (Fig. 3F), and found a trend towards a reduction in the area labelled by OMP in LPS-treated animals (133453.29 ± 14080.12 µm2, n = 5, Mann–Whitney, p = 0.095; Fig. 3G) compared to saline-treated animals (166406.68 ± 9532.13 µm2, n = 5; Fig. 3G), in an area of 697.57 × 526.06 µm. Quantification of OMP-related fluorescence intensity, at the OB glomerular layer, showed no difference between LPS-treated animals (37.84 ± 18.69, n = 5, t-student, p = 0.84) compared with saline-treated animals (20.42 ± 7.73, n = 5; Supplementary Fig. 2).

Intranasal LPS Leads to Acute Morphological Signs of Mild Bulbar Microglia Activation and IL-1β Increment

The characterization of OB granule cell layer microglia in animals fixed the day after intranasal administration revealed no difference between groups neither in microglial density (Saline: 45.63 ± 5.32 cells in an area of 697.57 × 526.06 µm, n = 4 vs LPS: 48.96 ± 4.57 cells in an area of 697.57 × 526.06 µm, n = 4, Mann–Whitney, p = 0.97; Fig. 4A, D), nor in Iba1 intensity (Saline: 25.86 ± 5.36 AU, n = 3 vs LPS: 24.02 ± 2.58 AU, n = 3, Mann–Whitney, p = 0.99; Fig. 4A, E). Most descriptors of microglial shape such as cell area, cell perimeter, circularity, solidity, roundness and aspect ratio were not significantly different between groups (Mann–Whitney, p > 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 3A-F). In contrast, analysis of skeletonized OB microglia of animals fixed the day after intranasal administration revealed that OB microglia in the LPS-treated animals exhibit a shorter maximum branch length (14.78 ± 0.41 µm, n = 5) than OB microglia in saline-treated animals (17.15 ± 0.4 µm, respectively, n = 4, Mann–Whitney, p = 0.016; Fig. 4F). Other descriptors of branch complexity, such as the number of branches, number of primary branches, mean branch length, number of junctions, number of triple or quadruple branching points, were not significantly different between groups (Mann–Whitney, p > 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 3G-L). We did not observe differences between groups neither in maximum number of processes (Saline: 11.02 ± 0.19, n = 4 vs LPS: 11.20 ± 0.27, n = 5, Mann–Whitney, p = 0.55; Fig. 4G), nor in the Schoenen Ramification index (Saline: 2.57 ± 0.10, n = 4 vs LPS: 2.89 ± 0.17, n = 5, Mann–Whitney, p = 0.41; Fig. 4H). Finally, the Sholl analysis revealed that OB microglial cells in LPS-treated animals have more branch intersections at Sholl rings near their cell soma (at 6–11 µm from soma; 24.88 ± 0.92, n = 5) than OB microglia in saline-treated animals (21.84 ± 0.59, n = 4, Mann–Whitney, p = 0.03; Fig. 4I, J). In contrast, OB microglia in LPS-treated animals have fewer branch intersections at Sholl rings far from their cell soma (at 23–36 µm from soma, 12.94 ± 0.93, n = 5) than OB microglia in saline-treated animals (18.84 ± 0.56, n = 4, Mann–Whitney, p = 0.016; Fig. 4I, K). The characterization of IL-1β staining in the OB granule cell layer of animals fixed the day after intranasal administration revealed sparce staining that rarely colocalized with Iba1 staining (Fig. 4L, insets). In fact, when cells could be clearly identified by IL-1β staining, they did not exhibit Iba1 positivity (Fig. 4L, insets). Regardless of its cellular origin, the quantification of field average IL-1β-related fluorescence intensity revealed a significant increase in IL-1β labelling in LPS-treated animals (55.45 ± 4.07, n = 3, t-student, p = 0.017; Fig. 4M), compared with saline-treated animals (39.05 ± 0.79, n = 3, Fig. 4M).

Since LPS-treated animals exhibit a reduction in the slow frequency band of the OB activity, which is closely coupled to breathing [23, 49] Fig. 5A, we assessed respiratory frequency (Fig. 6A) and found that LPS-treated animals (3.07 ± 0.18 Hz, n = 9; Fig. 6C) and saline-treated animals (3.41 ± 0.26, n = 15, Mann–Whitney, p = 0.25; Fig. 6C) had identical respiratory frequencies. Discussion

In the present study, we demonstrated that acute LPS-induced nasal inflammation can induce olfactory impairment, correlating with spreading of mild inflammation into the OB, acceleration of OB network activity (along with a depressed responsivity to odors), and its uncoupling from breathing. This is correlative and descriptive study that prompts the search for the cellular and network underlying mechanisms.

The finding that intranasal LPS induces odor detection impairment in 38% and 44% of fed or starved animals, respectively (compared to only 11% and 12% of saline-treated animals, respectively), indicates that there is a subset of highly LPS sensitive animals (often referred as high-responders), as identified in mice [16] and humans [12],which has been associated to subtle variations in age, gender, genetics or epigenetics, that predispose them to inflammatory challenges [12, 16]. Despite that the proportion of LPS high-responders does not vary depending on animals’ satiety, the measurement of olfactory detection in the whole population only showed a LPS-induced deficit in satiated animals, indicating that the increased motivation to seek food and the increased sensory function associated with fasting [36, 57], is powerful enough to overcome what we can consider a mild olfactory deficit, acutely induced by intranasal LPS.

The mild LPS-induced olfactory deficit described in the previous paragraph, as well as the deficit in olfactory discrimination also found in this study, should be taken with additional caution; mainly because they were detected 24 h after LPS intranasal administration, which certainly was the purpose of the study, but that occurred when scarce signs of sickness behavior were still present [58], Fig. 1), including a reduction in baseline food intake and a slight motor impairment (reflected in reduced traveled distance; Fig. 1H, I), which could negatively influence animal performance during the olfactory tests. However, it is worth noting that this acute sickness behavior did not reduce movement speed (which would affect food reaching latency, Fig. 1I) and did not affect the motivation or locomotion required for the animals to reach visible palatable food or to hold and eat chocolate (Fig. 1H). Thus, the normal ability to reach a more palatable and less familiar food exhibited by LPS-treated animals indicates that immediate motivation and basic motor function remain intact, suggesting that inflammation could affect baseline feeding during sickness behavior (Fig. 1E) but, in our experimental conditions, is not enough to reduce acute responses to salient rewards (Figs. 1H, 2A and Suppl. Figure 1). It has been proposed that inflammation reorganizes animal’s priorities towards less demanding and adapting behaviors, including seeking palatable food (a positive valence stimulus) when it does not represent major efforts [61, 62], which seems to be the case in our experimental conditions.

Considering that in this study we have not revealed the cellular and network mechanisms behind the described phenomenon, we will provide hypothetical cellular and network processes that might be involved in it, and which we believe are worth evaluating for future contributions. Systemic or intranasal LPS administration leads to the fast release of proinflammatory mediators peripherally and within the central nervous system [6, 11, 19–21, 31], including TNFα, IL-6 and IL-1β [6, 19–21, 26, 31, 52]. This release peaks at around 6 to 9 h post-administration [11] and may last longer [11, 19–21, 52]. As previously mentioned, we hypothesize that it is likely that the acute pathological actions of these mediators [6, 19–21, 31], can rapidly cause both acute sickness behavior and the olfactory dysfunction observed in this study by swiftly acting in the olfactory epithelium, the OB and likely beyond them [6, 32]. Thus, we also hypothesize that long-term peripheral and/or central inflammation is not needed to induce animal discomfort and hyposmia [19–21, 26, 31, 68], which explains why bacterial infection can also lead to acute hyposmia in humans [4, 35]. Moreover, our study shows that acute LPS-induced nasal inflammation is enough to elicit cytological and functional alterations in the OB [31, 33], perhaps secondarily to the LPS-induced damage of OSN [10], although not reflected in their innervation into the OB (Fig. 3F, G), through mechanisms yet to be determined.

There is overwhelming evidence that nasal or systemic LPS administration leads to microglial activation [6, 19–21, 30, 31, 48] which can be observed in a matter of hours [32]. This phenomenon can also be reflected in morphological signs of microglial activation (i.e., an increase in soma size and a decrease in the extension of microglial processes) several hours after LPS injection [6, 32] and which can be long lasting [19–21, 30, 31]. Consistent with previous research [6], we found that the day after intranasal LPS administration, OB granule cell-layer microglia reduced their branch length, which is a sign of microglial activation leading to a proinflammatory phenotype [13, 30, 54]. Therefore, we can conclude that nasal inflammation quickly spreads into the OB, inducing mild microglial morphological changes, that correlate with the functional alterations in this circuit. The LPS-induced OB inflammation, and particularly the LPS-induced early microglial morphological changes, was so mild in our experimental conditions that we were not able to detect a major change in expression of IL-1β co-localizing with Iba1, indicating that the significant expression observed in the LPS-treated group could be occurring in other cell types such as neurons, astrocytes or infiltrating cells [6]. It is worth noting that our evaluation of microglial cells and IL-1β labelling was restricted to the granule cell-layer, mainly because this was the area targeted by our electrophysiological evaluations, but also because the highly ramified and more amoeboid-like microglia are unevenly distributed across the OB layers [69].

In this study, we present the first evidence that acute LPS-induced nasal inflammation swiftly induces an acceleration of OB activity characterized by a decrease in a narrow segment of the slow oscillations and an increase in a narrower segment of fast ones (although this frequency band were not completely confirmed by cluster-based permutation analysis). This finding aligns with previous reports of electroencephalographic alterations induced by LPS [2, 26, 34], which are associated with signs of sickness behavior [2, 26, 34] and the emergence of epileptiform activity [34]. This last observation is relevant because we have previously found that other inflammatory conditions impacting olfaction [23, 24], also lead to heightened fast network oscillations [63], rendering circuits prone to hyperexcitability [3, 63].

The bulbar oscillatory activity increased in our experimental conditions after a single LPS nasal administration, and which we insist was significant only in a narrow frequency range and was only partially confirmed by cluster-based permutation analysis, falls into the broader and classical “beta oscillations” [42, 43, 56]. As for the other classical frequency bands [8], beta oscillations constitute a family of different oscillatory patterns [42] associated with different brain computations, relying on different cellular and network mechanisms, and whose narrow identification offers great potential to understand brain function and dysfunction in more detail [8, 42]. Interestingly, the increase in the bulbar narrow frequency beta activity observed in this study 24 h after intranasal administration of LPS closely resembles the mitral cells hyperexcitability after chronic inflammation [56]. The broad and classical bulbar beta rhythms are governed by afferent centrifugal influences from the basal forebrain, including the piriform cortex [43], and by dendrodendritic interactions between bulbar principal neurons and granule cells [43, 56]. Thus, it is possible that the spread of nasal inflammation into the basal forebrain, as already suggested by Lalancette-Hébert et al. [32], would affect the centrifugal influences on the OB, disrupting the proper regulation of bulbar beta rhythms [43]. On the other hand, the increase in beta activity discussed here, closely resembles the one observed in other networks upon sensory deprivation [45] which, in our case, we hypothesize is a very likely possibility since LPS consistently damage OSN [10, 28, 31, 67], even shortly after its acute application [10]. However, it is worth noting that we were unable to detect alterations in OMP signal in the OB glomerular layer, which indicates that if any OSN damage is being induced in our experimental conditions and is contributing to the alterations reported here, it is not enough to swiftly alter its projection into the OB (Fig. 3F and G).

We also hypothesized that the spread of nasal inflammation into the OB could alter, through OSN-independent mechanisms, intrabulbar dendrodendritic interactions by influencing the intrinsic excitability principal neurons or granule cells [14, 31, 56, 65] or its modulation by inhibitory influences [31, 56, 65]. We based this hypothesis on two pieces of evidence: the first one is that a reduction of GABAergic control over bulbar principal neurons is enough to increase principal cell excitability [14] and enhance bulbar beta activity [14, 65]. The other one is the well know deleterious effect of LPS-induced inflammation on GABAergic terminals, GABAergic transmission [30] and GABAergic interneurons [31, 52].

Slow bulbar oscillatory activity is tightly coupled to the respiratory rhythm (mechanotransduced at the olfactory epithelium; [15, 28] but relies on local glomerular circuitry [15, 28]. As observed in our experimental conditions, the reduction in bulbar respiratory coupling impairs odor coding precision and stability, thereby affecting olfaction [28]. Considering that breathing is not affected in LPS-treated animals (Fig. 6), we hypothesize that it is likely that the alterations in the slow bulbar oscillatory activity and its coupling to respiratory function could involve changes in the olfactory epithelium, especially in OSN, that alter the transmission of sensory information to the OB [10, 28, 31, 67] and pathological alterations in the outer layers of the OB [19]. Both OSN, in the olfactory epithelium, and glial cells and neurons in the outer layers of the OB are highly sensitive to inflammation [19, 31, 44]. Thus, we hypothesize that inflammation-induced alterations in OSN could be a major factor contributing to LPS-induced OB dysfunction [10, 28, 31, 67], particularly the reduction in OB slow activity. However, our evaluation of OMP expression at the glomerular layer, which reports the input of OSN into the OB [19], show no significant change 24 h after intranasal LPS, indicating that no major OSN degeneration is being induced in our experimental conditions. Alternatively, in the OB, diverse interneuron populations interact with principal bulbar cells to amplify slow oscillatory activity and its coupling to breathing [15]. Interneurons in the outer layers of the OB are particularly damaged after chronic administration of LPS [19, 31], which reduces their lateral control of principal bulbar cells [31]. We hypothesize that alterations in this circuitry could also contribute to the reduction of slow bulbar activity, its coupling to breathing and its response to odors, through mechanisms yet to be determined.

Aside from the alterations in background bulbar network activity, we also observed that a single intranasal LPS administration swiftly decreases odor-evoked bulbar activity, similar to the reductions in odor-evoked activity in animal models of chronic rhinitis [44]. As previously hypothesized, this functional alteration may arise from inflammation-induced changes in the olfactory epithelium [19–21, 31, 44], anddamage of OSN [10, 28, 31, 67], of bulbar principal neurons and interneurons in the glomerular layers [15, 31, 56] and throughout the OB [15, 31, 56], as previously documented in other pathological conditions [31, 44, 56]. We hypothesized that, as reported for chronic inflammation [31, 56], acute olfactory bulb inflammation triggered by intranasal injection of LPS could rapidly induce changes in neuronal excitability and/or neuronal interactions in the olfactory epithelium [10, 28, 31, 67] and in the OB [31, 56]. These changes could hinder the OB’s capacity to produce proper neuronal network activity and respond to odor information input, resulting in olfactory impairment, through mechanisms yet to be determined. This scenario must be considered when evaluating both the origins of acute hyposmia induced by nasal bacterial infections and potential treatments [4, 35].
