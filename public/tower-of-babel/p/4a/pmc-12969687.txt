XN@CuO/HA nanobipyramids trigger FOXO1-GADD45G-mediated cuproptosis and synergy with anti-PD-1 for lung cancer therapy
Authors: Ruiyang Jiang, Xiangming Huang, Yiran Ouyang, Bichong Luo, HongYang Wan, Fangyang Shou, Xiahui Wu, Junting Fan, Jingyuan Zhang, Dongdong Sun, Changliang Xu, Zhijun Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lung cancer is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, with current therapies limited by drug resistance and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). Cuproptosis offers new therapeutic prospects, but effective induction and targeted delivery remain challenging. Herein, we developed xanthohumol (XN)-coordinated copper oxide/hyaluronic acid (XN@CuO/HA) nanobipyramids as a novel cuproptosis inducer for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) therapy. XN@CuO/HA exhibited good colloidal stability, and potent In vitro antitumor activity by inducing mitochondrial dysfunction, reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, and cuproptosis. Mechanistically, transcriptomic and functional analyses identified the FOXO1-GADD45G axis as a critical mediator of XN@CuO/HA-induced cuproptosis. In vivo, XN@CuO/HA significantly suppressed LLC xenograft growth in C57BL/6 mice with excellent biosafety. Moreover, it remodeled the immunosuppressive TME by enhancing CD8+ T/NK1.1+ cell infiltration and reducing MDSCs, synergizing with anti-PD-1 antibody to achieve superior antitumor efficacy. Collectively, our findings highlight XN@CuO/HA as a safe and effective nano-therapeutic that induces FOXO1-GADD45G-mediated cuproptosis and potentiates immunotherapy, providing a promising combinatorial strategy for NSCLC treatment.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 18% of all cancer deaths annually, with an overall 5-year survival rate of less than 20% [1,2]. Despite advances in therapeutic strategies—including surgical resection, platinum-based chemotherapy, and targeted therapy—most patients with advanced or metastatic lung cancer still face treatment failure due to drug resistance, tumor recurrence, and systemic toxicity [3,4]. Only 20–30% of lung cancer patients derives durable clinical benefit from anti-PD-1 monotherapy [[5], [6], [7]]. This limited responsiveness is largely attributable to the highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), characterized by inadequate T cell infiltration, T cell exhaustion, and the accumulation of immunosuppressive cells [8,9].

Emerging regulated cell death (RCD) mechanisms have opened new avenues for cancer therapy, with “cuproptosis” standing out as a copper-dependent form of RCD that differs fundamentally from apoptosis, ferroptosis, or pyroptosis. Cuproptosis is triggered by the accumulation of intracellular copper ions (Cu2+/Cu+), which bind to lipoylated mitochondrial proteins (e.g., dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase, DLAT) in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, inducing abnormal protein oligomerization. Concurrently, excess copper downregulates the expression of iron-sulfur (Fe-S) cluster proteins (e.g., ferredoxin 1, FDX1; lipoyl synthase, LIAS), disrupting mitochondrial metabolism and triggering protein toxic stress [10,11]. A key advantage of cuproptosis is its ability to elicit immunogenic cell death (ICD): during cuproptosis, tumor cells release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs, e.g., ATP, HMGB1) and tumor-associated antigens, which recruit and activate dendritic cells (DCs) and effector T cells—directly addressing the “low immunogenicity” bottleneck of lung cancer [12,13]. Moreover, recent studies have shown that intracellular copper levels regulate PD-L1 expression in tumor cells, and manipulating copper homeostasis can sensitize tumors to anti-PD-1 therapy [14,15]. However, effective cuproptosis induction in lung cancer remains challenging: tumor cells employ robust copper detoxification mechanisms (e.g., glutathione-mediated copper chelation, copper transporter-mediated efflux) to limit intracellular copper accumulatio [16]. While copper-based nanomaterials have been explored to deliver copper to tumors, their clinical translation is hindered by poor targeting, insufficient copper release in the TME, and an incomplete understanding of the downstream signaling pathways that mediate cuproptosis—leaving a critical gap in optimizing cuproptosis-based therapies.

Natural bioactive compounds have gained attention for their synergistic effects with nanomaterials in cancer therapy. Xanthohumol (XN), a prenylated chalcone derived from hops, exhibits anti-tumor activity by inhibiting cell proliferation [17,18], inducing oxidative stress [19,20], and modulating immune responses [21]—with low systemic toxicity. Notably, its chemical structure allows it to chelate metal ions like Cu2+, facilitating the formation of a stable nanocomplex and potentially enhancing copper-induced cytotoxicity. We postulated that this coordination could not only promote intracellular copper delivery but also disrupt copper homeostasis more effectively than either agent alone.

In recent years, nanomaterials have emerged as powerful platforms for precision cancer therapy due to their tunable physicochemical properties, controllable biodegradability, and ability to co-deliver therapeutic agents [22]. To overcome the barriers of the immunosuppressive TME, innovative nanotechnology-based strategies have been extensively explored to remodel the tumor microenvironment and trigger immunogenic cell death (ICD). For instance, recent pioneering works have utilized functionalized carbon dots and defect-engineered sonozymes to achieve precision tumor therapy and augmented immunotherapy [23]. Furthermore, advanced single-atom catalysts have been developed to relieve hypoxia and deplete glutathione (GSH) in the TME, thereby reversing immunosuppression [24]. Beyond these modalities, the integration of cuproptosis with other therapeutic strategies (e.g., sonodynamic therapy) has also shown potent synergy in potentiating anti-tumor immunity [25,26]. Metal-based nanostructures, such as copper, iron, and manganese oxides, are particularly attractive because of their redox activity and catalytic responsiveness to the TME. Copper-based nanomaterials, in particular, can not only serve as reservoirs for controlled Cu2+ release but also participate in redox cycling (Cu2+/Cu+) to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to mitochondrial stress and cell death [27]. The combination of such inorganic nanoplatforms with bioactive small molecules offers an opportunity to design multifunctional systems that integrate metal-triggered cytotoxicity, oxidative stress amplification, and immune activation [[28], [29], [30]]. Moreover, surface modification with biopolymers such as hyaluronic acid (HA) can enhance tumor targeting via CD44 receptor-mediated endocytosis and improve colloidal stability under physiological conditions [31]. This rational integration of nanochemistry and bioactivity provides a promising framework for developing nanotherapeutics capable of inducing cuproptosis while reshaping the TME to potentiate immunotherapy.

Our developed XN@CuO/HA demonstrated efficient cellular internalization and provoked severe mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and marked anti-proliferative effects in lung cancer cells. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that the FOXO signaling pathway was significantly engaged by XN@CuO/HA treatment. Subsequent investigation identified the FOXO1-GADD45G axis as a critical downstream signaling module. We demonstrated that XN@CuO/HA activates FOXO1, which in turn upregulates GADD45G, thereby promoting the key molecular events of cuproptosis. Furthermore, we posited that the robust cuproptosis triggered by XN@CuO/HA could remodel the immunosuppressive TME through ICD. Indeed, In vivo experiments showed that combining XN@CuO/HA with an anti-PD-1 antibody elicited a powerful anti-tumor immune response, characterized by enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration and activation, and a reduction in myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs).

In summary, this study introduces XN@CuO/HA NPs as a novel nano-inducer of cuproptosis, in which the choice of XN is rationalized by its intrinsic bioactivity and copper-chelating properties. Our work not only elucidates the crucial role of the FOXO1-GADD45G pathway in this process but also demonstrates the synergistic potential of combining this nanotherapeutic with anti-PD-1 immunotherapy, providing a compelling combinatorial strategy for the treatment of lung cancer.

Results and discussion

Result1: synthesis and characterization of XN@CuO/HA nanobipyramids

The XN@CuO/HA nanobipyramids were successfully fabricated through a coordination–precipitation process using Xanthohumol (XN) as both a chelating and reducing agent to coordinate with Cu2+ ions, followed by hyaluronic acid (HA) modification to improve colloidal stability and biocompatibility (Fig. 1A). As shown in Fig. 1B, the obtained nanoparticles exhibited a uniform and well-defined bipyramidal structure with good dispersity. This specific morphology is anticipated to facilitate cellular internalization. STEM-EDS elemental mapping (Fig. 1C) displayed a homogeneous distribution of Copper (Cu) and Nitrogen (N) elements throughout the nanostructure, confirming the successful integration of XN and the copper-based framework. The surface modification process was monitored by Zeta potential analysis (Fig. 1E), which revealed a reversal in surface charge from positive to negative, confirming the successful coating of HA. The UV-Vis absorption spectra (Fig. 1F) showed that compared to free XN, the XN@CuO/HA formulation exhibited a distinct bathochromic shift (red shift) and peak broadening, indicative of the strong coordination interactions between Cu ions and the functional groups of XN. Furthermore, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to analyze the valence states. The analysis (Fig. 1H–J) confirmed the chemical composition, wherein the presence of characteristic satellite peaks in the Cu 2p spectrum indicated that copper predominantly exists in the Cu(II) oxidation state.

Finally, the drug release capability of XN@CuO/HA was evaluated under simulated physiological and tumor microenvironment (TME) conditions (Fig. 1G). In a neutral environment (PBS, pH 7.4), the cumulative release of XN was relatively low (<30% after 48 h), suggesting excellent stability during blood circulation, which is crucial for minimizing systemic toxicity. In sharp contrast, under TME-mimicking conditions (pH 6.5 with 0.1 mM H2O2), a burst release profile was observed, with the cumulative release reaching approximately 75% within the same period.

Taken together, the morphological and spectroscopic analyses demonstrate that XN@CuO/HA nanobipyramids were successfully constructed with a uniform bipyramidal shape, high colloidal stability, and characteristic Cu(II) redox features, establishing a robust foundation for subsequent tumor therapy and mechanistic studies.

Result2: antitumor activity of XN@CuO/HA nanoparticles In vitro

We first evaluated the cellular internalization of FITC-labeled XN@CuO/HA in lung cancer cells. After co-incubation with A549 and H1975 cells, strong green fluorescence was observed, and the fluorescence intensity increased in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. S1A), indicating effective uptake of XN@CuO/HA by both cell lines. To further investigate the functional effects of XN@CuO/HA, a series of In vitro assays were performed. In a 3D tumor spheroid formation assay, XN@CuO/HA treatment significantly suppressed spheroid growth compared to the PBS control (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. S1B). The anti-proliferative effect was assessed by MTT and colony formation assays. The results demonstrated that XN@CuO/HA treatment significantly reduced cell viability (Fig. 2C and D), decreased the proportion of EdU-positive cells (Fig. 2E), and markedly inhibited clonogenic survival (Fig. 2F and G) relative to Cu2+ or XN treatment alone. Cell viability was further visualized using live/dead staining, which showed the highest proportion of dead cells in the XN@CuO/HA-treated group (Fig. 2H–J). Intracellular ROS levels were detected using the DCFH-DA probe, and the strongest green fluorescence was observed in the XN@CuO/HA group (Fig. 2M and N), suggesting substantial ROS accumulation. As excessive ROS can induce mitochondrial damage, we assessed the mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) using JC-1 staining. The results indicated that Cu2+, XN, and XN@CuO/HA all reduced ΔΨm, with the XN@CuO/HA group showing the most pronounced green fluorescence, reflecting the greatest degree of mitochondrial impairment (Fig. 2O and P).

Collectively, these In vitro results demonstrate that XN@CuO/HA exhibits superior antitumor efficacy compared to XN or Cu2+ alone. This enhanced performance is likely attributable to XN's ability to induce oxidative stress and modulate membrane properties [[32], [33], [34]], which promotes the cellular uptake of CuO NPs and subsequent copper release. The resulting copper overload, in turn, exacerbates ROS production and mitochondrial damage [35,36]. This synergistic effect aligns with prior research, demonstrating that natural bioactive compounds can boost the efficacy of metal-based nanomaterials by enhancing biocompatibility and targeting capacity. For example, Dong et al. reported a multifunctional DNA nanosponge system that enables targeted sensitization of chemotherapy for ovarian cancer through metabolic reprogramming and ferroptosis induction [37]. Yu et al. investigated aloe-emodin (AE), a natural compound derived from medicinal plants, which self-assembles into nanoparticles (NPs) following copper ion (Cu) loading and modification with PEG-DSPE-FA 2k. These NPs can target, accumulate, and persist in tumor sites, exerting dual functions: inducing tumor cell death and enhancing the photodynamic therapy (PDT) effect [38].

Result3: XN@CuO/HA induces cuproptosis in lung cancer cells

Cuproptosis is triggered by excessive copper, which promotes abnormal oligomerization of copper-dependent lipoylated proteins in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and downregulates iron-sulfur (Fe–S) cluster proteins. A key regulator of this process, ferredoxin 1 (FDX1), reduces Cu2+ to Cu+, facilitating the lipoylation of dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT) and decreasing Fe–S cluster protein levels, ultimately leading to cell death [16,39]. As shown in Fig. 3A, a marked increase in green fluorescence intensity was observed in H1975 cells treated with Cu2+, XN, or XN@CuO/HA compared to the control, with the highest intensity seen in the XN@CuO/HA group, indicating effective accumulation of labile Cu(II). We further examined the expression and oligomeric state of DLAT, a key protein in cuproptosis. Immunofluorescence staining revealed pronounced DLAT aggregation in all treatment groups, most prominently in XN@CuO/HA-treated cells (Fig. 3B and C). Further supporting cuproptosis induction, we observed downregulation of ETFDH and ACO2—Fe–S cluster proteins essential for mitochondrial respiration and TCA cycle function. Depletion of Fe–S clusters is known to disrupt mitochondrial metabolism and trigger proteotoxic stress [40,41], aligning with the mechanisms of cuproptosis. Western blot analysis validated these changes: treatment with Cu2+, XN, or XN@CuO/HA downregulated the expression of mitochondrial Fe–S cluster proteins (including FDX1, ETFDH, and ACO2) while upregulating levels of the copper importer SLC31A1 and heat shock protein HSP70. Among all groups, XN@CuO/HA treatment resulted in the lowest expression of ACO2, ETFDH, and FDX1, and the highest expression of HSP70 (Fig. 3H and I).

Since the TCA cycle is involved in cuproptosis [42], we also measured its key metabolites. Metabolite analysis showed that α-ketoglutarate and pyruvate levels increased, whereas succinate levels decreased in cells treated with Cu2+, XN, or XN@CuO/HA (Fig. 3J–L). Taken together, these results demonstrate that XN@CuO/HA effectively induces cuproptosis in H1975 cells.

Result 4: mechanism of cell death mediated by XN@CuO/HA

Given the remarkable In vitro antitumor properties of XN@CuO/HA, we performed transcriptomic sequencing analysis on H1975 cells treated with Cu2+, XN, or XN@CuO/HA, along with untreated controls, to elucidate the underlying antitumor mechanisms. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct transcriptomic profiles among the four groups, which formed separate clusters upon dimensionality reduction (Fig. 4A). Violin plots illustrated relatively consistent gene expression patterns across the different samples (Fig. 4B). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using thresholds of p < 0.05 and |Log2FC| > 1.5. Comparative analysis of DEGs between each treatment group and the control identified 41 overlapping DEGs common to the XN@CuO/HA, Cu2+, and XN groups (Fig. 4C). Further intersection of these 41 DEGs with the overlapping DEGs from the Cu2+ vs XN@CuO/HA and XN vs XN@CuO/HA comparisons yielded a final set of 27 core DEGs (Fig. 4D). Volcano plots displayed the types and numbers of DEGs in each comparison (Fig. 4E–G). Specifically, the control vs Cu2+ comparison yielded 419 upregulated and 148 downregulated DEGs; control vs XN identified 77 upregulated and 155 downregulated DEGs; and control vs XN@CuO/HA revealed 2115 upregulated and 1025 downregulated DEGs. Heatmap analysis of these 27 DEGs confirmed that XN@CuO/HA induced the most pronounced expression changes compared to the control, with clearer distinctions than those observed in the Cu2+ or XN groups (Fig. 4H). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was employed to investigate the functional implications of the altered gene expression. As shown in Fig. 4I, intervention with XN@CuO/HA primarily affected pathways involved in tumorigenesis, including the MAPK signaling pathway, FOXO signaling pathway, and HIF-1α signaling pathway. qPCR validation of key genes within the MAPK, FOXO, and HIF-1 signaling pathways demonstrated that XN@CuO/HA had the most significant impact on the FOXO signaling pathway (Fig. 4L). Using the cistarget database, we predicted that the transcription factor FOXO1 possesses potential DNA binding sites in the promoter region of the differentially expressed gene GADD45G (Fig. 4M). GADD45G is a novel tumor suppressor molecule that exerts regulatory functions in various tumors, including lung cancer [43], myeloproliferative neoplasms [44], esophageal cancer [45], and leukemia [46], etc. Reportedly, nuclear FOXO1 plays a key role in cell cycle arrest, cell death, and DNA damage repair by upregulating the expression of p27 (Kip1), Bim, and GADD45 genes, respectively [47]. Based on these findings, we propose the hypothesis that XN@CuO/HA exerts its anti-lung cancer effects via the FOXO1–GADD45G axis.

Result5: XN@CuO/HA promotes cuproptosis via the FOXO1–GADD45G axis

Transcriptional dysregulation is frequently observed in lung cancer, with Forkhead box O1 (FOXO1) serving as a key tumor suppressor that regulates cell fate [48]. Given the marked involvement of the FOXO signaling pathway in XN@CuO/HA activity, and since TCGA data support FOXO1-mediated transcriptional upregulation of GADD45G, we utilized the FOXO1 inhibitor AS1842856 to examine whether XN@CuO/HA-induced cuproptosis depends on the FOXO1–GADD45 signaling axis. We first evaluated the expression of FOXO1 and its downstream cuproptosis-related protein DLAT using immunofluorescence. Compared with the control, AS1842856 alone markedly reduced nuclear FOXO1 and DLAT fluorescence intensity, whereas XN@CuO/HA alone enhanced both nuclear FOXO1 accumulation and DLAT expression. Using a Cu2+-specific fluorescent probe, we measured intracellular free Cu2+ levels. AS1842856 alone significantly lowered Cu2+, while XN@CuO/HA induced a strong fluorescent signal, confirming copper overload. This XN@CuO/HA-driven Cu2+ elevation was suppressed when combined with AS1842856 (Fig. 5C). Cell proliferation was assessed via EdU staining and colony formation assays. XN@CuO/HA significantly reduced the percentage of EdU-positive cells, an effect partially restored by AS1842856 (Fig. 5D). Similarly, XN@CuO/HA potently suppressed clonogenicity in H1975 cells, and this suppression was markedly alleviated in the combination group (Fig. 5E). Western blot analysis of the FOXO1–GADD45G pathway and cuproptosis-related proteins revealed that XN@CuO/HA reduced phosphorylation of FOXO1 at Ser256 (p-FOXO1), indicating FOXO1 activation. Consequently, downstream GADD45G, along with cuproptosis-related proteins LIAS and HSP70 —changes that were reversed by AS1842856 (Fig. 5F–G).

These findings identify the FOXO1-GADD45G axis as a novel regulator of cuproptosis. While previous studies have primarily focused on FDX1 and DLAT as core mediators of cuproptosis [49,50], our work demonstrates that XN@CuO activates the FOXO pathway by inhibiting FOXO1 phosphorylation, leading to GADD45G upregulation. GADD45G, in turn, may promote cuproptosis potentially by modulating mitochondrial function or Fe-S cluster protein stability, consistent with its role in DNA damage response [51]. The attenuation of XN@CuO/HA-induced cuproptosis by AS1842856 confirms that the FOXO1-GADD45G axis is not merely associated with, but critically involved in, cuproptosis execution. This discovery expands our understanding of copper metabolism regulation by linking a well-established tumor suppressor pathway to copper-dependent cell death.

However, we acknowledge that the direct molecular bridge between GADD45G and core cuproptosis regulators (e.g., FDX1 or DLAT) remains to be fully characterized. Whether GADD45G directly interacts with these proteins or acts via mitochondrial proteotoxic stress is an important question. Future studies employing Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) are warranted to resolve these precise biochemical interactions.

Histological analysis via H&E staining revealed nuclear shrinkage, fragmentation, and localized necrosis in tumor tissues from the treatment groups (Fig. 6G). Immunohistochemistry further demonstrated that both XN and XN@CuO/HA reduced the proportion of Ki-67-positive cells, indicating suppression of tumor proliferation. Immunofluorescence analysis showed decreased expression of the cuproptosis-related protein ACO2 in treated tumors, and both XN and XN@CuO/HA downregulated ETFDH expression (Fig. 6J–M). Western blot results corroborated these findings, showing that XN and XN@CuO/HA reduced protein levels of ACO2 and FDX1, with the most pronounced reductions in the XN@CuO/HA group. Conversely, HSP70 expression was upregulated, highest in the XN@CuO/HA-treated mice. In terms of safety, no significant changes in body weight were observed during the treatment period (Fig. 6E). H&E staining of major organs revealed no obvious abnormalities, and serum levels of ALT, AST, and CRE remained within normal ranges, indicating good hemocompatibility (Supplementary Fig. S3A–D). Biodistribution and metabolic studies revealed that XN@CuO/HA exhibited a transient accumulation in the liver and spleen, followed by effective clearance of 83.7% of copper ions within 64 h, indicating its excellent biodegradability. Furthermore, the lack of significant elevation in serum pro-inflammatory cytokines (IFN-γ, IL-6, and TNF-α) confirmed that the nanoplatform is highly biocompatible and does not trigger a systemic inflammatory response (Supplementary Fig. S3E–F).

In conclusion, XN@CuO/HA demonstrates robust antitumor efficacy In vivo through suppression of tumor growth and induction of cuproptosis-related molecular alterations, while maintaining an exceptional biosafety profile. The absence of organ toxicity and normal serum biochemistry support XN@CuO's potential for clinical translation, while the increased spleen index hints at immune modulation—laying the groundwork for subsequent studies of XN@CuO's interaction with the immune system.

Result8:XN@CuO/HA induce an immune response In vivo

Recent advances in immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), particularly targeting the PD-L1/PD-1 axis, have revolutionized cancer treatment. However, the efficacy of anti–PD-1 monotherapy is often limited by a highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), which significantly restricts its effectiveness [52]. Cuproptosis, as a novel form of immunogenic cell death, has been reported to reverse immunosuppressive TME and enhance immunotherapy by activating immune responses [53]. Based on this, we further investigated whether XN@CuO/HA could synergize with anti–PD-1 therapy to achieve superior antitumor efficacy.

A lung cancer model was established in C57 mice, which were treated with XN@CuO/HA and/or anti–PD-1. Compared with the model group, both monotherapies reduced tumor volume and weight, whereas the combination of XN@CuO/HA and anti–PD-1 resulted in the most pronounced suppression (Fig. 8A–C). H&E staining further confirmed the enhanced therapeutic effect of the combination, showing the largest area of necrotic tumor tissue in the XN@CuO/HA + anti–PD-1 group. Quantitative immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis of Ki67 and PTEN also supported the superior tumor-suppressive outcome of the combination treatment (Fig. 8D). As the spleen serves as a key immune organ, changes in immune cell proportions reflect systemic antitumor immunity. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that XN@CuO/HA, anti–PD-1, and their combination all increased the proportions of CD8+ T cells and NK1.1+ cells in the spleen (Fig. 8E–G). In addition, anti–PD-1 and the combination treatment reduced the proportion of Ly6G+ myeloid-derived suppressor cells (Fig. 8H and I). We further assessed IFN-γ secretion by splenic CD8+ T cells and found that all treatment groups promoted IFN-γ production (Fig. 8J), indicating that the combination therapy not only expanded CD8+ T cells but also enhanced their cytotoxic function. Finally, ELISA measurements of serum immune-related factors showed that the XN@CuO/HA + anti–PD-1 group significantly increased the levels of IFN-γ and granzyme B, while decreasing the level of Arg-1, compared with either monotherapy (Fig. 8K–M). IFN-γ not only enhances the cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells and NK cells but also upregulates PD-L1 expression on tumor cells, creating a “feed-forward” loop that sensitizes tumors to anti–PD-1 therapy [56]. In contrast, Arg-1, a key immunosuppressive factor secreted by MDSCs, depletes extracellular arginine to impair T cell proliferation and function; its downregulation by the combination treatment further validates the reversal of TME immunosuppression.

To elucidate the translational significance of XN@CuO/HA, it is valuable to benchmark it against emerging copper-based therapeutic strategies. Current approaches largely fall into three categories: copper depletion, inorganic overload, and natural product-mediated modulation [[57], [58], [59]]. While copper-depletion prodrugs effectively limit tumor metabolism, they fail to exploit the potent cytotoxicity of copper overload. Conversely, recent studies have utilized external triggers, such as Aloe-emodin-copper nanoparticles, which combine photodynamic therapy (PDT) with cuproptosis to achieve high tumor inhibition (93.12% with α-PD-L1) [38]. While highly effective, such strategies often depend on light penetration depth. Recent advances have also highlighted the potential of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) monomers in regulating intracellular copper homeostasis. For instance, Celastrol-Cu nanoparticles amplify cuproptosis by scavenging glutathione (GSH) via the NF-κB pathway, and Shikonin-Cu nanoregulators enhance stress by inhibiting protective autophagy (ATG5 knockdown) [54,60].

Taken together, these results demonstrate that XN@CuO/HA synergizes with anti–PD-1 antibody to enhance antitumor efficacy In vivo. This combination promotes a robust T cell–mediated immune response by increasing the proportions of antitumor effector cells (CD8+ T and NK1.1+ cells), elevating effector molecules (IFN-γ and granzyme B), and reducing immunosuppressive factors (Arg-1) and inhibitory cells (Ly6G+ MDSCs). We acknowledge the species mismatch between human In vitro lines and the murine LLC model; however, the syngeneic LLC model was indispensable for evaluating immunomodulation and anti-PD-1 synergy in an immunocompetent host. Despite genetic variations (e.g., KRAS vs. EGFR status), the cuproptosis machinery and FOXO1 pathway are highly conserved. Notably, our In vivo analysis (Fig. 7) confirmed that XN@CuO/HA effectively modulates the FOXO1-GADD45G axis in LLC tumors, verifying mechanistic consistency across species.

Conclusion

This study highlights the FOXO1-GADD45G signaling axis as a previously unrecognized master regulator of nanomedicine-induced cuproptosis, providing a novel mechanistic link between TCM-derived components and mitochondrial metabolic stress. Beyond direct cytotoxicity, we demonstrate that triggering this axis effectively remodels the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, offering a robust strategy to sensitize NSCLC tumors to anti-PD-1 checkpoint blockade. Ultimately, this work presents a promising, TCM-inspired translational paradigm to overcome therapeutic resistance and improve clinical outcomes in lung cancer treatment.
