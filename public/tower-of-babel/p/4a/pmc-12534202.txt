Intraocular Pressure Trends in Children with Myopia Receiving Atropine Therapy
Authors: Yun Hsia, Pao-Ju Chen, I-Hsin Ma, Yi-Chieh Lee, Tzu-Hsun Tsai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTopical atropine is widely used for myopia control in children, with proven efficacy in slowing myopia progression. However, concerns remain regarding its potential effects on intraocular pressure (IOP), particularly with long-term use. This study aimed to evaluate the longitudinal effects of topical atropine on IOP in children with myopia.MethodsThis retrospective, longitudinal study enrolled children using 0.125% atropine to control myopia progression and atropine non-users. IOP was measured repeatedly before and during treatment. RTVue optical coherence tomography (OCT) measured retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) and ganglion cell complex (GCC) thickness. Multilevel models assessed IOP changes by adjusting age, sex, spherical equivalent, central corneal thickness, and baseline IOP.ResultsA total of 188 patients (376 eyes) in the atropine group and 188 patients (376 eyes) in the control group were included. There were 86 boys (46%) aged 8.0 ± 2.5 years. Patients in the atropine group were more myopic (−1.17 ± 1.40 versus 0.73 ± 1.83 D, P < 0.001). The central corneal thickness and baseline IOP (atropine: 17.6 ± 3.0 mmHg; control: 17.2 ± 3.6 mmHg) were similar. Over a follow-up of 18.6 months (atropine, 19.3 months; control, 18.0 months), the final IOP was higher in the atropine group (18.3 ± 3.6 versus 16.7 ± 3.2 mmHg, P < 0.001). In the multivariable multilevel models, atropine was associated with an additional 0.51-mmHg increase (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.36–0.67, P = 0.001) in IOP per year when adjusted for sex, age, central corneal thickness, spherical equivalent, and baseline IOP. RNFL (104.0 ± 8.8 versus 102.6 ± 7.5 µm, P = 0.475) and GCC thickness (98.1 ± 5.6 versus 96.7 ± 5.8 µm, P = 0.270) showed no significant differences between groups.ConclusionsAtropine use for myopia control in children was associated with a modest IOP increase without apparent impact on RNFL. Regular IOP monitoring is advisable.

Introduction

The prevalence of myopia and high myopia is increasing, with an anticipated number of affected individuals worldwide reaching approximately 930 million by 2050 [1]. This surge raises substantial concerns, given the association of high myopia with potentially blinding complications, including macular degeneration, glaucoma, retinal detachment, and cataract [2, 3]. Owing to the irreversible nature of axial elongation in myopia progression, preventative measures are crucial, especially among the younger population. Optical, pharmaceutical, and environmental strategies are employed to control myopia. Mainstay optical measures involve reducing peripheral hyperopic defocus and inducing myopic defocus through methods such as multifocal or peripheral defocus soft contact lenses, specially designed spectacles, and orthokeratology [4, 5].

Although the mechanism of atropine in retarding globe elongation is under investigation, it has demonstrated successful control of myopia progression [6–10]. Given prevalent use of atropine to control myopia in Asian countries [11–13], there are concerns about safety associated with its long-term use. Its cycloplegic and mydriatic effects may theoretically increase the resistance of trabecular meshwork outflow [14, 15]. This influence may be negligible in normal eyes [14]; however, it could predispose individuals with narrow angles [16] or open-angle glaucoma to elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) [15]. Although some studies in children using atropine for myopia control showed no significant IOP difference compared with the control group [12, 17–21], these studies were limited by factors such as small cohorts, cross-sectional designs, short follow-up periods, lack of controls, or absence of IOP measurements before the initiation of atropine treatment [12, 17–21]. Some studies assessed IOP only once during treatment, while prospective studies were limited by brief follow-up periods and modest cohort sizes. To address these gaps, we aimed to investigate the longitudinal changes in IOP and their associated factors in children undergoing atropine therapy for myopia management, incorporating repeated IOP measurements throughout follow-up.

Methods

Patients and Study Design

This retrospective, longitudinal, observational study enrolled individuals aged < 18 years who attended the ophthalmology clinics at the National Taiwan University Hospital and National Taiwan University Hospital Hsin-Chu Branch from January 2016 to June 2024. Patients were divided into atropine and control groups. In the atropine group, all patients used 0.125% atropine sulfate eyedrops (Kingdom, Taiwan) once daily at night during the follow-up period. All patients underwent clinical follow-up for refractive error, with atropine prescribed to manage myopia progression. Only those with a documented pre-treatment IOP record were included in the atropine group. The control group comprised pediatric patients who had never received atropine treatment and who presented for refractive assessment or routine vision screening. To ensure suitability for longitudinal analysis, only patients with two or more IOP records separated by at least 6 months were included. Patients were followed at 6-month intervals, with slight variations in follow-up timing. Patients with macular disease, retinopathy, glaucoma, congenital cataract, optic neuropathy, corneal disease, amblyopia, strabismus, a history of ocular surgery, or the use of glaucoma medications or topical/systemic steroids were excluded. Since other myopia control methods, such as orthokeratology, defocus lenses, or contact lenses, may influence axial length and corneal thickness, children who received these treatments concurrently were also excluded [22, 23].

Procedure

All participants underwent comprehensive examinations, including autorefraction using the auto kerato-refractometer KR-8800 (Topcon Inc., Tokyo, Japan), slit lamp biomicroscopy, and visual acuity measurement. Cycloplegia was induced using three drops of Mydrin-P (tropicamide 0.5%/phenylephrine hydrochloride 0.5%, Santen) administered at 5-min intervals, with cycloplegic autorefraction obtained at least 30 min after the first drop. Spherical equivalents were calculated for analysis. Noncontact tonometry was performed to measure IOP and central corneal thickness before cycloplegia using Canon TX-20P (Canon, Tokyo, Japan). IOP was measured three times before cycloplegia, either during the morning clinic session between 9 and 11 AM or during the afternoon clinic session between 1 and 3 PM. Most children attended follow-up appointments consistently in either the morning or afternoon sessions. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) scans of the optic nerve head and macula were obtained once using an RTVue XR Avanti (Optovue Inc, Fremont, CA, USA) during follow-up. The average of the retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) was measured along a circle with a diameter of 3.45 mm in the optic disc scan. Furthermore, ganglion cell complex (GCC) thickness was measured from the RNFL layer to the external boundary of the inner plexiform layer in the macular scan.

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles embodied in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board/Ethics Committee of National Taiwan University Hospital (no. 202311067RINA). The need for the acquisition of informed consent from the participants was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study.

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using R, version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Descriptive statistics included calculating the mean and standard deviation for parametric data; percentages were computed for categorical data. To compare clinical characteristics and IOP at different time points between patients in the atropine and control groups, a linear mixed model was employed, accounting for correlated data from both eyes of each patient. A multilevel model was used to assess longitudinal changes in IOP, incorporating a random intercept model. The normality of residuals and random effects was assessed using Q–Q plots. Potential confounding factors influencing longitudinal IOP changes were included in the multivariable multilevel model. The final model was selected on the basis of the Akaike information criterion value. The marginal R2, conditional R2, and root mean square error (RMSE) were reported.[24] A threshold of P < 0.05 was applied for statistical significance.

Results

In this retrospective study, 188 patients (376 eyes) were included in each of the atropine and control groups. Table 1 presents a summary of the clinical characteristics of the enrolled patients. Comparable distributions were observed between the two groups in terms of age, sex, central corneal thickness, baseline IOP, and follow-up time. Patients in the atropine group manifested greater myopia than those in control group (−1.17 ± 1.40 diopters [D] versus 0.73 ± 1.83 D). During the follow-up period, the changes in spherical equivalent were more pronounced in the atropine group than in control group (−0.42 ± 0.69 D versus −0.19 ± 0.70 D, P < 0.001). By the final visit of each patient, the IOP was significantly higher in the atropine group than the control group (18.3 ± 3.6 mmHg versus 16.7 ± 3.2 mmHg, P < 0.001). In addition, the increment in IOP during the follow-up period was significantly higher (P < 0.001) in the atropine group (0.7 ± 3.5 mmHg) compared with the control group, which showed a decrease in IOP (−0.5 ± 3.2 mmHg). Furthermore, data from OCT scans were available for 178 eyes (24%). The average thicknesses for the RNFL and for the GCC were similar between the two groups. We also compared the characteristics of patients with and without OCT data. Patients with OCT data were older (P = 0.015, 8.6 ± 2.5 years versus 7.8 ± 2.5 years), more myopic (−0.91 ± 1.96 D versus −0.01 ± 1.81 D, P < 0.001), and had a higher IOP at baseline (18.5 ± 3.3 mmHg versus 17.1 ± 3.2 mmHg) compared with those without OCT data. To examine the longitudinal changes in IOP, we constructed a multilevel model with a random intercept for each group. Table 2 shows the influence of time and other confounding factors and their interaction term on the longitudinal alterations in IOP. Within the atropine group, each additional 3 months of atropine use was associated with a significant increase in IOP by 0.096 mmHg (P < 0.001). In contrast, the control group did not show significant changes in IOP over time (β = −0.025, P = 0.279). In both groups, older age was significantly correlated with higher IOP levels. However, age influenced the rate of IOP change over time differently between the two groups. In the control group, older age was associated with a smaller increase in IOP over time (β = −0.018, P = 0.010), suggesting that the IOP elevation may plateau in older individuals. Conversely, in the atropine group, age did not significantly affect the rate of IOP change over time (β = −0.004, P = 0.585), indicating a consistent trend of IOP increase regardless of age. Thicker central corneas were associated with elevated IOP in both groups (atropine group, β = 0.026, P < 0.001; control group, β = 0.019, P = 0.001). In both the atropine and control groups, children with lower baseline IOP experienced a greater increase in IOP over time compared with those with higher baseline IOP (atropine group, β = −0.029, P < 0.001; control group, β = −0.056, P < 0.001). Discussion

In the present study, we compared the changes in IOP between 188 children undergoing atropine treatment for myopia management and 188 control subjects over an average follow-up period of 18.6 ± 9.8 months, encompassing a total of 376 eyes in each group. Throughout the follow-up period, which included multiple IOP measurements, and we found that the IOP in the atropine group was increased significantly by 0.4 mmHg per year. Consequently, the final IOP was significantly higher in the atropine group than in control group but without any apparent impact on RNFL.

Atropine is commonly recognized as an efficient approach to slow down myopia progression in children. However, potential side effects, including photophobia, blurred vision, difficulties in near work, decreased amplitude of accommodation, and allergic responses, are dose-dependent [5, 18, 25, 26]. The long-term safety of atropine use remains an area of active research. A recent study investigating potential complications 10–20 years after atropine treatment found no significant differences in the incidence of cataracts, glaucoma, or macular degeneration between those treated with atropine and those who were not [27]. Nevertheless, the increase in IOP and its impact on the RNFL is a concern associated with atropine use [12, 26]. Our study demonstrated a slight increase in IOP among patients using atropine to control myopia. Given the 3-month measurement interval, the estimated IOP increment per year after starting atropine treatment was 0.4 mmHg. However, there were no significant changes in IOP observed in the control group. This observation is reinforced by the significant interaction term between atropine use and time when analyzing the combined atropine and control groups. Specifically, patients treated with atropine experienced an additional annual increase of 0.5 mmHg in IOP compared with the control group.

We adjusted the changes in IOP with confounding factors reported to be associated with IOP in children, such as age, sex, central corneal thickness, and spherical equivalent [28–30], which was not addressed in previous studies [12, 20, 21]. The average IOP value in our study was comparable to that of previous studies that measured IOP in healthy children [29, 31]. Previous studies reported that IOP may increase with age in children younger than 10–12 years owing to the eyeball growth [28, 29, 32]. Subsequently, IOP may reach adult levels around the age of 12 years [33, 34]. Given that the mean age of our cohort is approximately 8 years, age acted as a confounding factor in IOP changes. Our data align with literature, showing that older children had higher IOP. In the control group, the interaction term of age and time had a significant negative effect on IOP, indicating that the IOP increase associated with ocular growth may decelerate over time. Conversely, this plateau effect was not observed in the atropine group, suggesting a continuous increment in IOP. Since the control group did not show significant IOP changes during the follow-up, the increment of IOP in the atropine group cannot be simply attributed to aging.

The relationship between axial length, refractive error, and IOP in children remains controversial. While some studies have identified an association between higher IOP and longer axial length [29, 34], others have reported an inverse relationship [30] or no association at all [34]. In addition, elevated IOP has been observed following the onset of myopia [35]. In our cohort, baseline spherical equivalent and the extent of myopia progression were included in the model to account for their influence on IOP change. After adjustment, IOP continued to increase over time in the atropine group, whereas no significant temporal change in IOP was observed in the control group. Moreover, the change in spherical equivalent in the atropine group during follow-up was −0.42 D, which is less likely to significantly affect IOP [28–30, 36]. Therefore, the observed increase in IOP in the atropine group cannot be attributed to myopia progression and may instead be related to atropine use. It should be noted that axial length data were not available owing to the retrospective nature of this study. Although spherical equivalent may serve as a surrogate marker for axial length [37, 38], the association between axial length and IOP remains debated. Therefore, incorporating axial length data would allow for a more prudent interpretation of the findings.

The blockade of parasympathetic innervation of the ciliary muscle, causing mydriasis and cycloplegia, may lead to impaired trabecular meshwork outflow, resulting in elevated IOP [14, 15]. Although this effect may be negligible in normal eyes, it could be significant in individuals with compromised outflow facilities and a narrow angle [15, 16, 26]. Following pharmacologic dilation, IOP may demonstrate considerable inter-individual variability in children, ranging from substantial elevation to notable reduction [39]. Possible contributors to this variability include individual variations in anterior chamber configuration, angle anatomy, baseline outflow facility, and responsiveness of the trabecular meshwork to ciliary muscle relaxation. A persistent rise in IOP potentially deteriorates the RNFL. Another speculated mechanism that causes RNFL damage involves the antimuscarinic effect of atropine, causing vasoconstriction and compromising the perfusion of the optic nerve [12]. However, the magnitude of IOP increment was modest in our study; thus, its clinical significance requires further investigation. Our observations, based on an average 1.5-year follow-up, may not be extrapolated to long-term changes in IOP. It is unknown whether the IOP may become more stable or whether the elevation of IOP may become exacerbated under long-term use of atropine. Additionally, Chan et al. reported no significant changes in RNFL thickness and IOP in patients receiving atropine during a 1–1.5-year follow-up [12]. Data from OCT scans were only available for some of the patients in our cohort. The average thicknesses of the RNFL and GCC were comparable between the atropine-treated and control groups, and no RNFL defect was noted in these patients. Considering that patients with early-onset myopia may require continuous atropine treatment until late adolescence [40], regular IOP monitoring or even OCT scans in patients showing a trend for IOP elevation should be considered.

Several studies have investigated IOP changes in children using topical atropine, with none finding evidence of IOP elevation during follow-up [12, 17–21]. Notably, a randomized controlled trial by Bukhari et al. using 0.01% atropine found no significant difference in IOP between the atropine-treated and the control groups at 6 and 12 months [17]. Lee et al., in a prospective study of patients treated with 0.25 and 0.125% atropine, observed comparable IOP between atropine-treated and control groups during a 1-year follow-up, although the study size was relatively small [18]. In a retrospective study of 489 patients with myopia treated with atropine and 132 controls, Wu et al. found no difference in IOP between the two groups, with no association between cumulative atropine dosage and IOP. Older patients and those with a higher degree of myopia had higher IOP. However, this study only measured IOP once, and central corneal thickness was not recorded. Therefore, the authors recommended repeated measurement of IOP in the future study [21]. In a subsequent study, the same group of researchers enrolled 121 patients with myopia and 65 controls. Two IOP measurements were performed during a 10.6-month follow-up, revealing a statistically nonsignificant IOP increase in both control and atropine groups, and no association with cumulative atropine dosage [20]. However, the authors did not specify whether the initial IOP was recorded before or after the application of atropine. Chan et al. conducted a cohort study of 35 patients using 0.25% atropine to control myopia, with a 1–1.5-year follow-up, and found no changes in IOP or thickness of the peripapillary RNFL during follow-up [12]. Furthermore, Wu et al. utilized retrospective data from 1545 myopic eyes to build machine-learning models, identifying baseline IOP as the most predictive factor for endpoint IOP in patients on topical atropine treatment. The authors recommended considering baseline IOP when prescribing atropine, although the machine-learning model did not demonstrate the specific positive or negative associations between final IOP and the predictors [19]. IOP demonstrates both short- and long-term fluctuations [41]. Consequently, determining the trend of IOP changes through repeated measurements during follow-up is considered more ideal than relying on a single data point. Additionally, obtaining baseline IOP values before the initiation of atropine treatment is crucial. Including IOP measurements during atropine treatment can answer the question of stability; however, it may not detect IOP elevation after the commencement of atropine. Hence, our study enrolled patients with IOP measurements before and after atropine treatment, with repeated IOP measurements during follow-up. Furthermore, we used a multilevel model, treating the time point of measurement as an independent variable, to illustrate the longitudinal trend of IOP and adjust for other confounding factors [42]. This approach is considered superior to comparing IOP between atropine-treated and control groups using analysis of variance or independent t-tests. In addition, it is important to recognize that postdilation IOP changes can vary among individuals. Some people may experience an increase in IOP, while others may experience a decrease, resulting in no significant change when averaged across the entire population. Therefore, it is recommended to monitor IOP during atropine treatment, as some patients may be at risk of elevated IOP [39].

This study had a few limitations. First, there was a significant difference in spherical equivalent and myopic progression between the atropine and control groups, which could potentially act as confounding factors in the analysis of IOP changes. This limits the ability to distinguish atropine-specific effects from myopia-related IOP changes. The ideal study design would be a randomized controlled trial to allocate patients with myopia requiring myopia control into either an atropine-treated group or a placebo-controlled group. However, given that atropine is a well-established and effective method for myopia control, and considering the high prevalence of high myopia in Taiwan, it is ethically challenging to expose patients to the risk of myopia progression [12]. To address this, we employed a regression model to adjust for potential confounding factors. Moreover, enrolling a large control group with consistent follow-up is challenging. Therefore, we retrospectively included children with multiple IOP measurements who visited clinics for refraction assessment, excluding those with ocular pathologies that might affect IOP. Including a control group was essential, as IOP may naturally increase with age in children. This comparison strengthened the interpretation of IOP elevation in the atropine group. In the control group, the follow-up interval is less regular than the atropine group owing to their stable condition. To account for varying follow-up intervals, a multilevel model was employed, and the mean follow-up duration was comparable between groups. Furthermore, we conducted a sensitivity analysis including patients with a baseline spherical equivalent ≤ 0.5 D, comprising 165 eyes in the control group and 376 eyes in the atropine group. The results of the multivariable multilevel model remained consistent with our original findings: children in the 0.125% atropine group exhibited an additional IOP increase of 0.76 mmHg per year compared with the control group. Second, we only included patients who received 0.125% atropine. Given that different concentrations of atropine can lead to varying side effects, further studies with diverse concentrations of atropine are warranted to provide a comprehensive understanding of its effects. Third, non-contact tonometry was used instead of Goldmann applanation tonometry to measure IOP, which may overestimate IOP values. A previous study demonstrated that 36% of children under 10 years of age may be unable to undergo successful IOP measurement using Goldmann applanation tonometry [43]. To ensure a consistent measurement method throughout follow-up, we adopted noncontact tonometry for this pediatric population. Additionally, there is evidence supporting noncontact tonometry as a reliable method for measuring IOP in children [43]. As each IOP measurement method has its strengths and limitations, combining multiple modalities may be beneficial and could be considered in future studies. Lastly, the observed trend in IOP change was derived from a relatively short follow-up period. Longer-term studies are warranted to confirm the long-term IOP trajectory in children treated with topical atropine. Further investigation is needed to clarify the roles of corneal biomechanical properties, trabecular meshwork function, and aqueous humor dynamics in the observed trend of IOP elevation.

Conclusions

Compared with the control group, the atropine group exhibited a modest yet significant increase in IOP over time, while showing no detectable short-term impact on RNFL thickness. Consequently, baseline IOP evaluation and regular IOP monitoring is recommended for patients undergoing atropine treatment, and the consideration of OCT disc scans is advised for individuals with high IOP. Future research should aim to collect longitudinal data with extended follow-up, including IOP and serial measurements of RNFL thickness, to further assess the safety of long-term atropine use.
