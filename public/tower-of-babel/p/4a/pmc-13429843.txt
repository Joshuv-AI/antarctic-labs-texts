Effect of Home-Based Exercise Training on Anthropometric Indices and Health-Related Quality of Life in Children and Adolescents with Overweight/Obesity: A Randomized Clinical Trial
Authors: Alireza Ahmadi, Zohreh Sadat Navabi, Mohammad Reza Maracy, Fahimeh Moheb-Mohammadi, Mehdi Dehghani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOverweight and obesity are health challenges in children and adolescents and have increased in prevalence in years. This study evaluated the effects of home-based high-intensity interval training (HIIT), resistance training (RT), or combined HIIT+RT on anthropometric indices and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in children and adolescents with overweight or obesity.MethodsA randomized, single-blinded clinical trial was conducted in Isfahan, Iran. A total of 120 patients (aged 10–18 years) with overweight or obesity participated. Eligible participants were recruited using a simple sampling method and randomly assigned to one of four groups: three intervention groups (HIIT, RT, and combined HIIT+RT) or one non-exercising control group with dietary advice. The intervention groups received 24 home-based training sessions over 2 months. Anthropometric indices and the KIDSCREEN-27 questionnaire were assessed both pre- and post-intervention.ResultsAfter 2 months, body weight decreased significantly in the RT group compared with baseline measurements (P=0.010). Waist circumference (WC) also decreased significantly in the RT group (P=0.001), and hip circumference (HC) decreased significantly across the three intervention groups. Adjusted analyses revealed significant reductions in body weight (P=0.028), WC (P=0.024), and HC (P=0.006) across interventions. No significant changes were observed in waist-to-hip ratio or body mass index (BMI). In addition, an analysis of covariance showed no significant differences in the mean changes in HRQoL among the three intervention and control groups pre- and post-intervention.ConclusionHome exercise was associated with improvements in anthropometric indices excluding BMI, whereas HRQoL remained unchanged in children and adolescents with overweight or obesity.Keywords: High-intensity interval training, Resistance training, Quality of life, Overweight, Obesity

INTRODUCTION

The prevalence of childhood and adolescent overweight and obesity has risen markedly, and now presents a significant public health challenge. In 2008, 170 million children and adolescents were overweight or obese, and projections estimate that approximately 30% will be affected by 2030.1 In developing countries, obesity prevalence rates among children and adolescents in Iran and Saudi Arabia are approximately 30%.2 Childhood obesity has multifactorial causes, including metabolic, genetic, and behavioral factors,2 and is associated with dyslipidemia, type 2 diabetes mellitus, glucose metabolism disorders, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, autoimmune diseases, and psychosocial consequences.3,4 Dietary changes, increased consumption of junk food, reduced physical activity, and mobile-phone addiction have contributed to rising rates.5,6 Studies in the Middle East show that overweight and obesity are linked to breakfast omission, low vegetable intake, consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages, and insufficient vigorous physical activity.7 World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines recommend that children and adolescents aged 5 to 17 years perform moderate-to-vigorous physical activity for an average of 60 minutes daily, at least 3 days weekly,8 yet only 15% to 24% of youths in Europe and Canada engage in such activities.9 Home-based exercise programs can improve physical performance and health-related quality of life (HRQoL)10,11 and offer the advantages of parental supervision, cost reduction, and time efficiency.12 Supportive family environments, particularly maternal involvement, positively influence activity levels.13 To evaluate the effectiveness of such lifestyle and exercise interventions, anthropometric indices such as the body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), and waist-to-hip (WHR) ratio are essential for monitoring fitness progression, evaluating health status, and estimating the risk of future disease.14,15 Lifestyle changes, including reduced energy intake, increased activity, and decreased sedentary time, are critical for weight control.4 Obesity in youth is associated with low self-esteem, poor body image, and reduced HRQoL,16,17 defined by the WHO as the impact of health on physical, psychological, and social well being.18 While weight management interventions improve obesity indicators (e.g., BMI and weight),19 few studies have examined the effects of home-based combined exercise regimes on anthropometric indices and HRQoL in overweight and obese youth. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of home-based exercise high-intensity interval training (HIIT), resistance training (RT), or combined HIIT+RT regimes on anthropometric indices and HRQoL in children and adolescents with overweight or obesity.

METHODS

Study design

This randomized, single-blinded clinical trial was conducted in Isfahan, Iran, between August 2017 and December 2018. It was registered with the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (registration number IRCT20150428021987N4) and adhered to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials guidelines. An ideal sample size for the present study was calculated for the purpose of comparing BMI based on similar studies.16 The confidence interval was set at 95%, while the power of the study (1–β) was set at 80%. Assuming a variance of the desired attribute across groups of 2.26 and an effect size of 1.63, a sample size of at least 120 subjects (n=30 per group; three intervention groups and one control group) was determined to be sufficient. The research team consisted of a physician and a trained research assistant who conducted assessments, physical education and nutrition professionals who delivered the training sessions, and researchers who analyzed the results. Data were collected at pre-test and post-test. A post-intervention assessment was conducted 2 months after the last training session.

Randomization and blinding

The current investigation was a randomized controlled trial comprising four groups: a control group (n=30), an HIIT group (n=30), an RT group (n=30), and a combined HIIT+RT group (n=30). In this study, a randomization code was generated using randomized and permuted blocks of size 4, based on four random-sequence orders of assignment. Randomization was performed by an external assistant, and participants were not aware of their group allocation (single-blinded design).

Participants

Participants in this study were children and adolescents with overweight or obesity who attended the Clinic of the Pediatric Cardiovascular Research Center in Isfahan, Iran. Inclusion criteria were children and adolescents aged 10 to 18 years with overweight or obesity, as defined by the WHO Growth Reference as having a BMI‑for‑age greater than 1 standard deviation (SD) above the median (overweight) or greater than 2 SDs above the median (obesity).20 The exclusion criteria included overweight or obesity resulting from a known syndrome or organic cause; intellectual or physical disability; injury or illness that prevented participation in exercise sessions; use of medications affecting weight loss; or voluntary withdrawal from the study. In the HIIT group, a single participant was excluded due to fear of blood sampling; in the RT group, two participants were excluded due to personal reasons and fear of blood sampling; in the combined HIIT+RT group, one participant was excluded due to unwillingness to continue in the study; and in the control group, one participant was excluded due to lymphoma and two participants due to migration. Ultimately, 113 participants remained in the study: 29 in the HIIT group, 28 in the RT group, 29 in the HIIT+RT group, and 27 in the control group. Fig. 1 illustrates the participants’ flowchart.

Intervention

The physical exercise intervention comprised aerobic training, RT, and combined training. Training protocols for HIIT and RT are outlined in Table 1. Participants in the combined group (HIIT+RT) alternated between RT and HIIT every 3 minutes during 30-minute sessions. Each session commenced with a 10-minute warm-up involving on the spot running and stretching, progressed to the main exercise phase, and concluded with 10 minutes of cool-down stretching.

Group 1 participants received dietary advice alongside HIIT. The HIIT sequence included a burpee, squat with ground touch, lateral burpee, high knees, jump squat, lunge, squat jack, controlled burpee, and scissor squat. Sessions lasted 30 minutes with rest intervals of 20 seconds between movements.

Group 2 consisted of participants undertaking RT in combination with dietary advice. The RT program comprised a squat, push-up, sit-up, bridge, pull-up, bicycle crunch, shoulder press, dip, and prone back extension. Exercises were performed in three sets, each set containing three movements, for a total duration of 30 minutes. Rest intervals were 30 seconds between movements and 60 seconds between sets.

Group 3 consisted of participants following the alternating HIIT+RT regimen described above, combined with dietary advice. Group 4 served as the control group, receiving dietary advice only without exercise intervention.

Prior to implementation, all participants attended three instructional sessions lasting 60 minutes each at the Pediatric Cardiovascular Research Center under the supervision of a sports physiologist. These sessions provided training on correct exercise techniques, scheduling, frequency, and intensity through lectures, demonstrations, role-play, and interactive discussions. A printed manual containing illustrations and detailed guidelines was distributed after the third session. Members of the intervention groups completed 21 monitored home-based exercise sessions under parental supervision. A trained research assistant conducted weekly follow-up calls to assess adherence and address any challenges related to diet or exercise. Dietary recommendations, which were identical across all groups, emphasized fruit, vegetables, low-fat or fat-free milk, whole grains, poultry, lean meat, fish, eggs, legumes, and nuts, while reducing intake of fats, cholesterol, trans-fats, sugars, and added salt. Dietary advice was delivered by a nutritionist in a dedicated 60-minute session comprising a lecture, a question-and-answer segment, video, and visual materials.

Measurement anthropometric indices

Participant weight was measured with minimal clothing and without shoes using a calibrated Seca 786 mechanical column scale (Seca) with an accuracy of 0.1 kg. Height was recorded to the nearest 0.5 cm. WC was measured at the midpoint between the last rib and the iliac crest after exhalation, and hip circumference (HC) was assessed in the standing position at the widest part of the pelvis. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m²). All measurements were objectively obtained by trained research staff using calibrated instruments.

Data collection

A checklist was used to collect demographic information on participants, including age, sex, educational level, and parents’ education, occupation, and family income. Household income was classified into three categories: low (<Iranian Rials [IRR] 10,000,000/US$ 323 per month), moderate (IRR 10,000,000–IRR 20,000,000/US$ 323–645 per month), and high (>IRR 20,000,000/US$ 645 per month) using data provided by the Statistical Center of Iran. Primary data, including weight, HC, WC, and BMI, were collected before and 2 months after the intervention using a checklist separate from the main questionnaire. Quality of life (QoL) was assessed with the KIDSCREEN-27 (Child Public Health), a standardized instrument for evaluating QoL in children and adolescents.21 The Persian version of the KIDSCREEN-27 had previously been translated and validated in studies conducted in Iran, with Cronbach’s alpha reliability exceeding 0.70.22 This questionnaire comprises five domains: physical well-being (five items), psychological well-being (seven items), parent relations and autonomy (seven items), social support and peers (four items), and school environment (four items). Each item is rated on a Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always).

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp.). A Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to assess the normality of the data prior to analysis. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare baseline characteristics among the four groups. Paired t-tests were conducted to compare pre- and post-intervention values. Normally distributed data were further analyzed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), with baseline values entered as the covariate. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

Ethics statement

This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the ethics committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUI.REC.1396.3.365) approved the protocol. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participating children and adolescents, who were fully informed about the study’s objectives before providing consent.

RESULTS

Ultimately, 113 participants were included in the study. The intervention and control groups were comparable at baseline. ANOVA and chi-square tests found no statistically significant differences among the groups in terms of age, sex, education level, parents’ education and occupation, or family income. The mean ages in the HIIT, RT, and combined HIIT+RT groups were 14.5±2.4, 14.0±2.6, and 14.6±2.2 years, respectively, while the mean age in the control group was 13.0±2.4 years (Table 2). As shown in Table 3, the results of paired t-tests comparing baseline and post-intervention values within each group revealed significant differences in mean height in all groups, mean weight in the RT group (P=0.010), mean WC in the RT group (P=0.001), and mean HC in the HIIT (P=0.034), RT (P=0.004), and combined HIIT+RT groups (P<0.001). No significant between-group difference was observed for BMI. For WHR, the unadjusted between-group difference after the intervention was significant; however, it was no longer significant after adjustment for age, sex, and baseline values. Moreover, ANOVA revealed significant between-group differences in the post-intervention values for height (P=0.032) and WHR (P=0.049). However, these differences were no longer significant after adjustment for age, sex, and baseline values. WC, HC, and WHR decreased during the 2-month training period in all intervention groups.

DISCUSSION

The present study evaluated the effect of home-based exercise training on anthropometric indices and HRQoL in children and adolescents with overweight or obesity. The paired t-test revealed a significant increase in height in both intervention and control groups, along with significant reductions in weight and WC in the RT group and in HC across all intervention groups after 2 months. These findings align with those of García-Hermoso et al.,23 who reported improvements in weight, WC, and HC following RT, and with Jalali-Kahnooj et al.,24 who observed significant reductions in WC and HC after 8 weeks of combined aerobic-RT, corroborating the effectiveness of combined protocols in improving body composition and reducing these anthropometric indices.25 Several physiological and behavioral mechanisms may explain the superior effect of RT over HIIT and combined protocols observed in the present study. RT increases lean body mass, which elevates resting energy expenditure and promotes long-term fat oxidation; induces prolonged excess post-exercise oxygen consumption; and imposes lower mechanical stress on joints, thereby reducing discomfort and injury risk in overweight youth and improving adherence to exercise programs. These mechanisms are consistent with those described in previous reports demonstrating that structured RT significantly enhances body composition in overweight children,26 improves physical fitness with high compliance due to reduced mechanical strain,27 and is recognized as one of the most effective school-based physical activity interventions for adolescent boys.28,29 After adjusting for confounding variables, the present study showed favorable changes in WC and HC in the intervention groups; however, no significant improvement was observed in the control group, consistent with the findings of Andreato et al.,30 who reported that short-term interval training enhances aerobic fitness without necessarily reducing BMI. Moreover, evidence from a systematic review indicates that engaging in at least three sessions exceeding 60 minutes per week for 12 weeks or longer improves physical health in youths with overweight or obesity.31 Regarding HRQoL, ANOVA revealed post-intervention improvement solely in the parent relations and autonomy domain, consistent with findings reported by Kvalø and Natlandsmyr32 who observed benefits in these aspects of adolescent autonomy and relationships, variables positively associated with HRQoL.33 However, ANCOVA showed no significant differences among groups in mean changes of physical well‑being, psychological well‑being, social support and peers, school environment, and total QoL after 2 months. In contrast, Goldfield et al.34 reported that 6 months of combined aerobic and RT was associated with significantly improved physical and psychosocial indicators, with any discrepancies likely attributable to intervention duration and participant age ranges. Similar long‑term benefits were also reported by Berge et al.35 in adults following a 24‑week HIIT+RT program, and by García-Hermoso et al.23 in a meta‑analysis showing that interventions lasting 10 to 48 weeks improved metabolic profiles and HRQoL in children with obesity. Furthermore, systematic reviews confirm that home‑based interventions reduce BMI and facilitate management of childhood obesity.36 A notable strength of the present study is that it is the first in Iran to evaluate the effects of home-based exercise on anthropometric indices and HRQoL in children and adolescents with overweight or obesity. The intervention was feasible, low-cost, and required minimal equipment, and participants were supported by continuous parental supervision and structured weekly follow-ups.

The present study has some limitations, including a relatively short duration and the absence of assessments of pubertal status, which may have influenced BMI, skinfold thickness, and height-for-age measurements. Additionally, the reliance on a self-reported QoL questionnaire may have affected the precision of the collected data. Future studies should incorporate longer intervention and follow-up periods, include pubertal status evaluations, recruit participants representing wider socioeconomic diversity, and adopt video- or app-based exercise supervision to enhance adherence, ensure proper exercise technique, and improve the accuracy of outcome assessments. Home-based aerobic, resistance, and combined exercise programs, when accompanied by dietary counseling, may contribute to meaningful improvements in anthropometric indices, particularly WC and HC, and can be recommended as supportive components within educational and public health strategies aimed at preventing and managing overweight and obesity in children and adolescents.
