Isolation and molecular characterization of bacteriophages isolated from lake water and their application in foods against Bacillus cereus
Authors: Denny Rizkinata, Vania Clarissa Kusnadi, Diana Elizabeth Waturangi, Adi Yulandi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesBacteriophages are viruses that specifically target and kill bacteria. Bacteriophages are considered safe for humans, making them suitable for use in food applications Therefore, this study was conducted to isolate, characterize, and apply bacteriophages from the environment to control food spoilage bacteria.ResultsBacteriophages were isolated by using Bacillus cereus (B. cereus) as reference host. We found bacteriophage BC-VP titers (1.16 ± 0.18 × 109 PFU/mL) and BC-AJ (1.72 ± 0.19 × 108 PFU/mL). Both bacteriophages were polyvalent, targeting not only B. cereus, but also lysing enteropathogenic Escherichia coli (EPEC) and enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC) with different efficiencies. Bacteriophage BC-VP and BC-AJ miMOI were determined to be 0.01 and 1, respectively. Bacteriophage BC-VP morphology was analyzed using Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) and categorized as Myoviridae family from the order of Caudovirales. Both bacteriophages showed significant bacterial reduction when applied to artificially contaminated cooked rice and pasteurized milk. The reduction number was higher in pasteurized milk at 28℃ for BC-VP (93%) and BC-AJ (90%). Molecular characterizations were performed and showed that BC-VP was 96.88% a virulent bacteriophage. Also, no sign of antibiotic resistance gene in BC-VP and genes related to lytic cycle such as putative tail lysin and tail fiber, were annotated.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-025-07436-4.

Introduction

Food spoilage is a metabolic process that causes changes in food sensory characteristics, including smell, texture, taste, and appearance, making it undesirable or unacceptable for consumption [1]. Food spoilage can be caused by physical, chemical, or microbial actions. Microbial spoilage is one of the most common sources of food spoilage. Since food products consist of carbohydrates, protein, water, fat, and organic compounds, it is often used by microbes as carbon and energy sources to promote their growth. Bacteria that are commonly involved in food spoilage are from Bacillus sp. genus, such as B. cereus. These organisms are Gram positive, facultative anaerobic, spore-forming bacteria which are commonly found in food products, such as dairy products, bread, canned food, dough and rice, causing off odor, slime formation, ropiness, and sour spoilage. Spore-forming bacteria are a major problem in the food industry due to their high heat resistance which allows them to survive some food preservation methods [1–2].

Food preservation is a technique used to increase shelf life while also retaining original color, texture, flavor, and nutritional values of food products. These techniques can be classified into chemical and physical processing and have been proven to increase shelf life. However, there are some disadvantages, such as significant loss of flavor and nutrition, health effects, and bacteria resistance. Thus, an alternative preservation method is needed to prolong shelf life while also maintaining food quality [1].

Bacteriophages are viruses that have strong antimicrobial properties. Lytic bacteriophages can kill bacteria by causing them to lyse and have high specificity towards bacteria. These viruses are naturally occurring and capable of self-replication upon reaching their target host bacteria. Bacteriophages are not harmful to humans and have obtained generally regarded as safe (GRAS) status, making them safe for use as bio-preservatives in food products and can be used as an alternative to conventional preservation methods [3]. This study aimed to isolate and characterize bacteriophages from the environment and apply them against B. cereus. Additionally, molecular characterization of isolated bacteriophage was performed to obtain detailed information about their genome sequences, gene compositions, and life cycles.

Methods

Inoculum Preparation

B. cereus ATCC 10,876 was used as a reference host for bacteriophage isolation. Bacterial culture was inoculated onto Luria Bertani (LB) agar plate and incubated at 37℃ overnight, the bacteria was kept at 4℃ as the working culture.

Bacteriophage isolation

Lake water samples were collected from three different lakes in Tangerang, Indonesia and then transported to the laboratory and processed for bacteriophage isolation. B. cereus was inoculated in 10 mL of LB broth and grown to reach OD600 = 0.132 by incubation at 37℃, 120 rpm, overnight. Then, 5 mL of the water sample was added to 30 mL of LB broth with 300 µL of host bacteria and supplemented with 150 µL of 10 mM of CaCl2 and 0.5 mM of MgSO4 to enhance bacteriophage adsorption. The culture was then incubated at 37℃, 120 rpm, overnight and centrifuged at 6000 xg for 15 min [13, 30]. The supernatant was filtered using a 0.22 μm syringe filter, then re-centrifuge at 6000 xg for 10 min. The bacteriophage sample filtrate was then tested for bacteriophage presence by mixing 150 µL of the bacteriophage filtrate alongside with 150 µL of reference host bacteria, 50 µL of each with 10 mM of CaCl2 and 0.5 mM of MgSO4 with 4 mL molten soft agar (0.4% LB agar). The mixture was then poured on the bottom agar plate (2% LB agar) and incubated at 37℃ overnight. The visible plaques formed were observed [28, 29].

Bacteriophage purification and enrichment

The isolated bacteriophage was purified and enriched by gently stabbing a single visible plaque using a sterile micropipette tip. The tip was then resuspended in 10 mL of LB broth to release the bacteriophage and mixed with 150 µL of the reference host bacteria and 50 µL of each with 10 mM of CaCl2 and 0.5 mM of MgSO4. The mixture was incubated at 37℃ overnight at 120 rpm. After incubation, the mixture was centrifuged at 6000 xg for 15 min. The supernatant was filtered using 0.22 μm syringe filter then mixed with Ringer solution with a 1:1 (v/v) ratio [13, 30].

Bacteriophage titer determination

Bacteriophage titer was determined using the double agar overlay assay method. The purified bacteriophage was diluted in a tenfold series dilution using SM buffer and supplemented with 50 µL of 10 mM of CaCl2 and 0.5 mM of MgSO4. The mixture was then plated onto the agar overlay assay and incubated at 37℃ overnight. The visible plaques formed between 30 and 300 plaques were then converted into plaque forming units per milliliter (PFU/mL) [8, 30].

Host range determination

Bacteriophage host range determination was done using the double agar overlay assay method with different species of bacteria, which include B. cereus ATCC 10,876 (BC), B. subtilis ATCC 6633 (BS), Vibrio cholerae ATCC 14,033 (VC), Aeromonas hydrophila (AH), enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli US-Namru 1 (ETEC), enterohemorrhagic Escherichia coli US-Namru 1 (EHEC), enteropathogenic Escherichia coli US -Namru 2 (EPEC). 150 µL of the bacteriophage solution will be mixed with 150 µL of host bacteria and 4 mL of molten top agar and supplemented with 50 µL of each 10 mM of CaCl2 and 0.5 mM of MgSO4. The mixture was then poured on the top agar and incubated at 37℃ overnight [31].

Efficiency of plating (EOP)

The bacteriophage yielding positive result in the host range determination test was diluted in four dilutions and tested using the agar overlay assay. EOP was calculated by dividing the average PFU of targeted bacteria yielding positive result in the host range determination by the average PFU of the host bacteria [32].

Minimum inhibitory multiplicity of infection (miMOI)

The reference host bacterium was grown in 10 mL of LB broth and incubated at 37℃, 120 rpm, overnight. The bacterium was measured at OD600 = 0.132 to reach 108 CFU/mL. The bacterium and bacteriophage were diluted to obtain MOI from 0.00001 to 100, then 100 µL of each bacterium and bacteriophage were placed into a 96-well microplate. The microplate was incubated at 37℃ and the bacteriophage concentration is determined every 1 h for a duration of 10 h [13].

Morphology analysis

The bacteriophage morphology was analyzed with Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) using the negative staining method. 10 µL of the bacteriophage stock solution was dropped onto the grid. After 30 s, the grid was dried using filter paper then 5 µL of 2% uranyl acetate solution was dropped to the grid and left for 1 min. After 1 min, the 4 grid was later observed using TEM at a magnification of 30000x [33].

Bacteriophage application on food samples

The samples, 1 gram of cooked rice and 1 mL of pasteurized milk samples, were each sterilized using autoclave for 15 min at 121℃ in 50 mL and 15 mL of conical tubes. Each sample was contaminated with 100 µL of each B. cereus and bacteriophage suspension based on the MOI from the miMOI test result. The negative control was made by substituting the bacteriophage suspension with SM Buffer while the positive control was made by inoculating B. cereus without the bacteriophage suspension. The samples were incubated at 4℃ and 28℃, overnight. After incubation, the sample was suspended in 9 mL of SM Buffer and vortexed for 3 min. Then, the samples were diluted in a tenfold series dilution and spread on Luria-Bertani Agar and incubated at 37℃ overnight. The colonies formed were counted between 30 and 300 and determined as CFU/mL [13, 30].

Bacteriophage DNA extraction and next generation sequencing (NGS) analysis

Bacteriophage DNA isolation was performed and purified by using bacteriophage DNA isolation kit protocol from Norgen [34]. The purified DNA bacteriophage was tested and sequenced for NGS using MGI platform [35]. DNA sequences were obtained by UseGalaxy Australia (usegalaxy.org.au) an open, web-based platform for computational research. UGene was used to perform multiple sequence alignment. FastQC was performed to check the quality of the data sequence. Shovill was used to assemble the bacteriophage genome using default parameters, and Quast was performed to visualize the quality of assembly genome result [36].

Bacteriophage annotation and analysis of tail fiber protein

Bacteriophage annotation was figured out with Phaster [37]. A gene and protein analysis were also identified by using UniprotKB [38]. CARD was used to identify antibiotic resistance gene existence in the bacteriophage genome [39]. Likewise, PhageAI, the artificial intelligence to classify the lifecycle of bacteriophages and bacteriophage taxonomy [40].

Statistical analysis

The data obtained from the application test was analyzed with SPSS Inc. IBM corporation using the one-way ANOVA test which was followed by the Tukey’s-B test. The level of difference in this test was defined at P ≤ 0.05. The different letters presented in each column indicated significant differences between other samples within that column. The reduction significance between control and treatment was determined using the paired-samples T-test, with the level of difference defined at P ≤ 0.05 [13].

Results

Bacteriophage isolation

Two bacteriophages against B. cereus, named BC-VP and BC-AJ, were successfully isolated from lake water samples collected from two different locations. The presence of bacteriophages was indicated by clear plaque formed in the overlay agar assay, which showed the ability of bacteriophages to lyse bacteria (Supplementary Fig. S1) [4].

Bacteriophage titer determination, host range determination & efficiency of plating (EOP)

The bacteriophages concentration was measured using the titer determination test and expressed as plaque forming units per milliliter (PFU/mL). Bacteriophage BC-VP showed a higher titer value of 1.16 ± 0.18 × 109 PFU/mL. (Supplementary Table 1). The host range determination result was shown below, showing that both bacteriophages had lytic activity towards EPEC and ETEC.

Bacteriophage genomic isolation and next generation sequencing

The BC-VP genome was isolated with a bacteriophage DNA isolation kit from Norgen and subjected to next generation sequencing (NGS). 156,840 bp in length yield of DNA was obtained with 39.95% of GC-content. Bacteriophage genome of BC-VP has been submitted and officially published to Genbank with accession number (OR725122).

Bacteriophage annotation, antibiotic resistance gene and life cycle determination

BC-VP raw genome sequence was processed and aligned by using UseGalaxy. The assembled genome was annotated by PHASTER and various genes (Supplementary Table 3) that related with cell lysis, bacteriophage structure, assembly, and DNA package were detected.

CARD analysis revealed zero results of perfect, strict, and loose parameters that resulting antibiotic resistance genes were not found in BC-VP genome. In addition, PhageAI also performed to determine predicted taxonomy and bacteriophage life cycle status related to BC-VP. In this result, BC-VP is predicted as a virulent bacteriophage (96.88%). In addition, VIPTree revealed that BC-VP was categorized as Caeruvirales genera.

Discussion

In this study, two lytic bacteriophages targeting B. cereus were successfully isolated from two different samples from lake water due to its high concentration [5]. Commonly, bacteriophages have two distinct infection cycles. Obtaining bacteriophages with a lytic life cycle is crucial for the creation of biocontrol agents [6–7]. The isolated bacteriophages were named Bacillus cereus bacteriophage VP (BC-VP) and Bacillus cereus bacteriophage AJ (BC-AJ). The lytic activity of bacteriophages was indicated by the formation of clear plaque on the bacterial lawn due to bacterial cell destruction [8].

The concentration of isolated bacteriophage was found to range between 108 PFU/mL and 109 PFU/mL, which indicates high bacteriophage concentration. A high titer value is essential for the successful implementation of bacteriophage therapy as it increases the probability of bacteriophage and bacteria interaction [9].

The evaluation of a bacteriophage’s host range is crucial for determining its ability to lyse bacteria beyond its reference host [10]. In this study, both bacteriophages demonstrated lytic activity against ETEC and EPEC, categorizing them as having a broad host range [10]. Broad host range bacteriophages, also known as polyvalent bacteriophages, possess the ability to lyse bacteria across different genera [9, 10]. This characteristic offers significant advantages, including a wider target spectrum, making them more versatile for treating infections caused by mixed bacterial populations [9, 10]. Additionally, by targeting multiple bacterial species, these bacteriophages reduce the risk of resistance development, enhancing their potential for therapeutic applications [9, 10].

Bacteriophage receptors, which mediate the initial attachment of bacteriophages to bacterial cells, are located on various parts of the bacterial surface [8]. Common receptors include lipopolysaccharides (LPS) in Gram-negative bacteria, teichoic acids in the cell walls of Gram-positive bacteria, outer membrane proteins involved in essential bacterial functions, flagella, and pili [8, 11]. The initial interaction is normally a two-step process of reversible and irreversible binding of receptor-binding proteins (RBPs) to these surface receptors, with variations in surface structures playing a critical role in determining host specificity [11]. Polyvalent bacteriophages can overcome these variations by targeting conserved structures like the sugar cores of LPS or by employing mechanisms to bind multiple receptors, such as encoding diverse RBPs [11]. Further studies are needed to identify the specific receptors targeted by the bacteriophages studied here.

The efficiency of plating (EOP) was conducted to observe the effectiveness of isolated bacteriophages against target bacteria. An EOP value below 0.001 is not effective against the target bacteria 0.001 to 0.099 are moderately virulent, and values between 0.1 and 1.0 fall into the highly virulent categories [12]. The EOP against BC was recorded as 1, indicating optimal effectiveness, as BC served as the host bacterium for both bacteriophages. Against both EPEC and ETEC, BC-VP and BC-AJ were classified as moderately and highly virulent, respectively. The variation in efficiency against target bacteria could be caused by inadequate bacteriophage adsorption into target bacteria [13].

Minimum Inhibitory Multiplicity of Infection (miMOI) is defined as the minimum bacteriophage concentration required to inhibit bacterial growth while MOI itself is the ratio of bacteriophages to its reference host bacteria. Results obtained from this research showed that bacteriophage BC-VP and BC-AJ had different miMOI. BC-VP miMOI was determined to be 0.01 while BC-AJ was 1. Hence, BC-VP was considered to be more effective since it required lower bacteriophage concentration to inhibit bacterial growth. The result also showed that the decrease of bacterial growth happened due to an increase in bacteriophage concentration [14].

The TEM analysis of BC-VP showed that the bacteriophage structure consists of an icosahedral head and a contractile tail. The head of bacteriophage is made of a protein capsid containing the bacteriophage genome, which could be either DNA or RNA, and a lipid membrane whereas the tail consists of the tube and hooks [6–7]. In the new taxa update by ICTV, Caudovirales is replaced as Caudoviricetes. Likewise, BC-VP was categorized as Caerulivirales genera according to VIPTree. It means that the bacteriophage is related with bacteriophages that usually infect Bacillus genera, especially Bacillus cereus and Bacillus megaterium. In addition, BC-VP has high similarity with other bacteriophage references (Bacillus phage JBP901) with 91.3% genome results. Based on the head and tail length image from TEM determination, bacteriophage BC-VP was categorized as Caudoviricetes classification morphotype, which has an icosahedral head with contractile long tail [15]. In addition, according to the NCBI, BC-VP is categorized as the Herelleviridae family and the order of this family is still undefined [42].

Bacteriophage lytic ability was further tested in food samples with different matrices and storage temperatures. Cooked rice was used as the solid matrix and pasteurized milk as liquid matrix. The temperature used was 4℃ and 28℃ as a representative of storage conditions [16]. Highest bacterial reduction for both isolated bacteriophages can be seen in pasteurized milk samples at 28℃, with 93% of reduction by BC-VP and 90% of reduction by BC-AJ. Furthermore, all samples at both temperatures showed significant bacterial reduction after bacteriophage treatment.

In both temperatures, bacterial reduction was higher in pasteurized milk, which demonstrates that food matrices play a role in bacterial reduction. The different food matrices affect bacteriophage ability to lyse bacteria as bacteriophages are immobile. Therefore, they need to rely on Brownian motion, which occurred in liquid samples, to infect bacteria. Foods with solid matrices could restrict bacteriophage movement as they have a compact structure. This would make the bacteriophage render useless in killing bacteria, hence the lower bacterial reduction [5, 17].

Bacterial reduction also was seen lower at 4℃ in both food samples and phages. Bacterial reduction depends on the concentration of bacteriophages, with higher reductions requiring higher phages number. In order to increase their concentration, bacteriophages need to replicate inside the reference host bacteria. However, low storage temperature hinders the bacterial growth, which subsequently leads to slower bacteriophage replication and infection. Therefore, a higher temperature is preferable to achieve higher bacterial reduction [16].

BC-VP, upon subjecting each genome to NGS analysis, was found to be composed of 39.95% GC-content with 156,840 bp length of DNA [41]. Annotations using PHASTER were able to annotate coding sequences that produce up to 200 proteins necessary to serve as constituent material of bacteriophage or the end of lytic cycle. This assumption was supported by genes related lysogenic phase such as excisionase and integrase were not found. Likewise, various enzymes that promote host cell lysis such as peptidase, lysin, peptidoglycan hydrolase, and exonuclease have been confirmed in BC-VP that can be assumed as lytic bacteriophage characteristics.

The bacteriophage genome packaging that showed in Supplementary Table 3 comprises several genes. As a specific kind of virus, which infects bacteria, DNA polymerases are used to replicate the genome of DNA viruses [18]. Ribonucleoside diphosphate reductase is an enzyme that mediates the conversion of ribonucleotides to deoxyribonucleotides, the precursor of DNA synthesis of bacteriophage [19]. Ftsk SpoIIIE protein is an enzyme that plays important roles in intercellular chromosomal DNA translocation between bacteriophage and host cell that influence the division of the host cell as well as the lytic cycle of the bacteriophage [20]. Glycerophosporyl diester phosphodiesterase is also identified in the structural particles of bacterial viruses especially in the viral baseplate and work directly to the targeted host during the bacteriophage genome injection. In contrast, enzymatic activity could not be clearly identified [21]. Terminase also annotated in BC-VP. Commonly, terminase consist of small and large categories. Small terminases are used to initiate genome packaging and regulate large terminase activity. Meanwhile, large terminase are known to cleave, concatenate DNA molecules and initiate packaging mechanisms [22]. Putative transcriptional regulator is used to constitute super family protein to regulate bacteriophage packaging elements [23].

Bacteriophage gene assembly is a late phase of bacteriophage which is necessary for the basic formation of mature virus particles. The chaperone domain of endosialidase is known as a part of bacteriophage assembly that helps endosialidase to fold resulting in the trimeric tail spike protein of virus [24]. Bacteriophage portal protein plays an essential role for bacteriophage assembly. Only bacteriophages with functional portal complexes are able to package DNA, as the capsid must first be assembled into a shell-like head that facilitates DNA entry [25]. HTH binding proteins are commonly involved in the regulation of DNA replication, repair, transcription, and telomere maintenance. In addition, the error of this structure can lead to dysfunction [26].

Putative lysins and peptidoglycan hydrolase are enzymes that hydrolyze the cell wall of bacteria. Lysin enzymes have a rapidity action, high efficiency and low chance to develop bacterial resistance since they target peptidoglycan components that are essential bacterial viability [27]. Conclusion

BC-VP showed higher effectiveness in killing reference bacteria. This bacteriophage also could lyse EHEC and EPEC with different efficiency. Application of bacteriophage in food samples showed that they were able to significantly reduce bacteria numbers in cooked rice and pasteurized milk at 4℃ and 28℃. The purified bacteriophage DNA was obtained and there were no signs of antibiotic resistance and lysogenic gene in BC-VP genome. Those factors can be determined in further analysis that BC-VP are safe to use in the food industry as alternative food preservatives.

Limitation

Application assays of this bacteriophage are still limited, it needs to be tested with a wider variety of food matrices. Additionally, several assays for BC-AJ were not completed due to time constraints, which restricted comprehensive comparative analysis.
