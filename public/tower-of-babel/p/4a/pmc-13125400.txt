Normative range for MRI amniotic fluid volume from 16 to 36 gestational weeks: Absolute and relative values, and correlation to ultrasound
Authors: Amine Bouachba, Julie Chabourlin, David Grévent, Joana De Jesus Neves, Laurence Bussières, Sunniva Vaillant, Laurent Julien Salomon, Guillaume Gorincour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAmniotic fluid volume (AFV) is clinically important, yet ultrasound‐based indices correlate poorly with true volume. MRI provides accurate volumetry, but normative reference data for AFV remain scarce. This study aimed to establish MRI‐based gestational‐age reference ranges for AFV and the AFV‐to‐fetal body volume ratio (AFV/FBV) from 16 to 36 weeks' gestation and to compare ultrasound amniotic fluid index (AFI) with MRI‐derived AFV.Material and MethodsProspective observational cohort study conducted at a single tertiary fetal imaging center (LUMIERE, Necker Hospital, Paris, France). Healthy singleton pregnancies between 16 and 36 gestational weeks were included; pregnancies with fetal anomalies or maternal complications were excluded. T2‐weighted MRI with manual segmentation was performed to quantify AFV and fetal body volume. In a predefined subset, AFI was measured on same‐day ultrasound. The main outcomes were AFV, AFV/FBV ratio, and their gestational‐age normative ranges. The secondary outcome was the correlation between AFI and MRI‐derived AFV. Percentile curves were derived using quadratic polynomial modeling. Trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04142606.Results322 pregnancies were included (median gestational age 27.9 weeks; 45% male). AFV increased with gestational age (β = 6.61), peaking around 31 weeks, whereas AFV/FBV ratio declined (β = −0.08). No sex differences were observed. Weekly distributions were normally distributed. Quadratic polynomial modeling yielded physiologically plausible percentile curves (R 2 = 0.74 for AFV/FBV). In the ultrasound‐MRI subset (n = 103), AFI significantly correlated with AFV (Spearman ρ = 0.77), although with wide individual variability.ConclusionsThis study provides the first MRI‐based normative charts for AFV and AFV/FBV ratio between 16 and 36 weeks' gestation. The AFV/FBV ratio represents a novel physiological marker integrating fetal growth and fluid balance. These reference ranges may support future research and clinical decision‐making.Keywords: amniotic fluid volume, fetal body volume, fetal growth, magnetic resonance imaging, normative charts

Key message

This study provides the first MRI‐based normative charts for amniotic fluid volume and introduces the AFV/FBV ratio as a novel marker integrating fetal growth and fluid balance from 16 to 36 weeks' gestation.

INTRODUCTION

Amniotic fluid is a key component of the intrauterine environment, providing mechanical protection, facilitating fetal movements, and enabling lung development. Abnormalities in amniotic fluid volume (AFV), such as oligohydramnios or polyhydramnios, have long been associated with increased risks of perinatal morbidity and mortality. Accurate quantification of AFV is therefore clinically relevant, both for screening at‐risk pregnancies and guiding obstetric management. 1 , 2

Clinically, abnormal AFV is associated with adverse outcomes: oligohydramnios with placental insufficiency, growth restriction, malformations, and higher perinatal mortality; polyhydramnios with maternal diabetes, fetal anomalies, or genetic syndromes, often leading to preterm birth and neonatal complications. Reliable normative ranges are therefore crucial both as physiological references and for early risk stratification. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6

Traditionally, AFV assessment has been performed by ultrasound using either the amniotic fluid index (AFI) or the single deepest pocket (SDP). Despite their widespread use, both methods suffer from limited reproducibility and poor correlation with actual fluid volumes measured at delivery. Several studies have demonstrated the low sensitivity of AFI and SDP for detecting true oligohydramnios, raising concerns about unnecessary interventions and variable diagnostic thresholds across clinical practice. 7 , 8 , 9

MRI has emerged as a complementary tool for the evaluation of the fetus and placenta, providing superior soft‐tissue contrast and three‐dimensional coverage. Advances in image acquisition and automated segmentation now allow accurate volumetric quantification of intrauterine structures, including fetal body, fetal brain, placenta, and amniotic fluid. MRI‐derived volumetry offers the possibility of constructing normative ranges with improved precision, overcoming some of the limitations inherent to ultrasound‐based indices. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13

However, normative reference ranges for amniotic fluid volume derived from MRI remain scarce, and most available studies are limited by small sample sizes or lack of systematic modeling across gestation. Establishing robust gestational age–specific reference charts for MRI‐based AFV is therefore an unmet need. 12 , 13

Beyond absolute AFV, normalizing amniotic fluid volume to fetal size may provide a more physiologically meaningful marker. The AFV‐to‐fetal body volume ratio (AFV/FBV) integrates information about both fluid homeostasis and fetal growth, potentially offering improved sensitivity for detecting imbalances in conditions such as fetal growth restriction or renal anomalies. However, establishing normative reference values in healthy pregnancies is an essential prerequisite before any novel marker can be evaluated in pathological populations.

The present study aimed to establish normative reference ranges for amniotic fluid volume measured by MRI between 16 and 36 weeks of gestation. Using a large prospective cohort, the secondary objective was to assess, within a subset of the cohort, the correlation between AFI (Phelan method measured on ultrasound) and MRI‐derived AFV. 14 , 15

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design and population

We conducted a prospective study, part of the “lumiere on the fetus” trial between December 2021 and June 2025 (NCT04142606). Each participant was enrolled once at a single gestational age (cross‐sectional design), ensuring independence of observations for reference range construction.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) singleton pregnancies, (2) gestational age confirmed by first‐trimester ultrasound, (3) no known fetal anomalies or pathologic conditions such as congenital infections, chromosomal abnormalities, or structural malformations, and (4) no maternal conditions that could affect fetal growth or amniotic fluid volume, including pre‐existing or gestational diabetes mellitus, chronic or pregnancy‐induced hypertension, pre‐eclampsia, autoimmune disorders, and severe obesity (BMI > 40 kg/m2). We excluded fetuses with any detected anomalies during routine ultrasound scans or MRI, as well as pregnancies complicated by maternal infections or other significant maternal health issues. All subjects provided written informed consent approved by the Institutional Review Board (2019‐A01498‐49).

MR imaging protocol

Women underwent MRI without sedation or breath‐holding. MR imaging was performed using a clinical 1.5‐T whole‐body unit (Optima MR450W 1.5T, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) with a gradient field strength of 45 mT/m. Patients were scanned in the supine position using a combination of a six‐channel phased array body coil and six elements of the spine coil positioned over the lower pelvic region.

Following a scout scan to determine fetal orientation, T2‐weighted images were acquired using fast imaging with FIESTA (Fast Imaging Employing Steady‐state Acquisition) sequences in the three orthogonal fetal planes. On average, 20–30 adjacent slices were obtained, adjusted according to fetal size and position. Imaging parameters were as follows: slice thickness 4 mm, interslice gap 4 mm, field of view 380 × 309 mm2, matrix 166 × 256, TR/TE = 4.65/2.33 ms, voxel resolution 1.9 × 1.5 × 4.0 mm, and bandwidth 399 Hz/pixel. Sequences degraded by fetal motion were immediately repeated using identical parameters.

A board‐certified pediatric radiologist (D.G.) confirmed that no fetal morphological anomalies were detected on MRI.

Image post‐processing

Time to achieve the post‐processing tasks was recorded.

To evaluate FBV and AFV we used the open‐source software 3D‐Slicer. 16

We manually traced the borders of each liquid and fetus on each slice (approximately 40 slices for each), the total FBV and AFV were automatically calculated by the software as the sum of the liquid and fetal area in each slice (mm2) multipied by the slice thickness (4 mm).

Two trained investigators (A.B and J.C.) performed manual segmentations of the liquids and fetuses.

Secondary analysis: AFI vs. AFV

For this secondary analysis, we included 103 pregnancies with both MRI and ultrasound performed on the same day, enabling direct comparison between AFI and MRI‐derived AFV. This sample size was pragmatically chosen to ensure robust correlation analysis (power > 95% to detect ρ > 0.5 at α = 0.05), while maintaining feasible segmentation workload. The amniotic fluid index (AFI) was measured directly on US images according to the method originally described by Phelan et al. (division of the uterine cavity into four quadrants and summation of the largest vertical pocket in each). Absolute AFV was obtained from manual 3D segmentations using 3D Slicer. Correlation between AFI and AFV was assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient and polynomial regression modeling. All analyses were adjusted for gestational age. 15

Statistical analysis

All analyses were two‐sided, and a p‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant, performed using RStudio (version 2024.04.1+682). 17

AFV and AFV/FBV ratio were analyzed according to fetal sex, adjusting for gestational age. Gestational age in days was used as a continuous variable. A linear regression model was fitted with AFV and AFV/FBV ratio as the dependent variables, and gestational age and fetal sex (male vs. female) as independent variables.

To construct percentile curves, we employed the method described by Royston and Wright, which models both the mean and standard deviation as functions of gestational age. 18 , 19

This approach was chosen over more complex methods such as GAMLSS because several gestational weeks in our dataset included fewer than 10 cases. The evolution of mean AFV and AFV/FBV ratio across gestation was well fitted using simple quadratic polynomials; more complex models did not significantly improve goodness of fit. Detailed equations for percentile modeling are provided in Appendix S1.

RESULTS

Characteristics of the population

During the study period, 322 women were included in this study at a median gestational age of 27.93 weeks [22.86–32.39] weeks of gestation. The median maternal age was 33.4 years [31.1–36.6], and the median maternal BMI was 24.43 kg/m2 [22.38–26.29]. There were 116 (36%) nulliparous patients. Twelve of the pregnancies included in the study were conceived via in vitro fertilization (IVF) (3.73%). In our cohort, 144 of the fetuses were male (44.7%). Patients gave birth at a median gestational age of 39.43 weeks [38.86–40.54], with a median birthweight of 3270 g [2987–3520]. A total of 57 patients gave birth through a C‐section (17.7%). We were able to collect all but 11 birthweights (3.4% missing data) (Table 1).

Statistical analysis

Normality of the weekly distributions for both AFV and AFV/FBV ratio was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. None of the gestational weeks showed statistically significant deviations from normality (p > 0.05), supporting the assumption of approximate normality.

To complement statistical testing, quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots were generated for each gestational week. These plots showed good alignment of observed and theoretical quantiles in the central range, with only minor deviations at the distribution tails, consistent with expected biological variability. Taken together, these results supported the use of mean and standard deviation to construct parametric percentile curves.

In the regression analysis, gestational age was significantly associated with amniotic fluid volume (AFV), with a positive coefficient (β = 6.61, p = 0.001), indicating a progressive increase in AFV with advancing gestation. After adjusting for gestational age, fetal sex had no significant effect on AFV (β = 17.3, p = 0.44), suggesting that at comparable gestational ages, no meaningful difference in amniotic fluid volume was observed between male and female fetuses.

Similarly, the AFV/FBV ratio was also significantly associated with gestational age, but with a negative coefficient (β = −0.080, p < 0.001), reflecting a physiological decline in this ratio as fetal body volume increases more rapidly than amniotic fluid volume over time. As with absolute AFV, fetal sex had no significant effect on the AFV/FBV ratio after adjustment (β = −0.029, p = 0.387), confirming the absence of sexual dimorphism in this parameter within the studied gestational window.

In the raw dataset, scatter plots of amniotic fluid volume across gestation revealed substantial inter‐individual variability, with wide dispersion of values and no clear linear or higher‐order trend. Polynomial fitting applied directly to the raw data yielded very low coefficients of determination (R 2 ≈ 0.09 for degree 2 and R 2 ≈ 0.10 for degree 3), indicating poor explanatory power. Visual inspection confirmed that neither second‐ nor third‐order polynomials adequately captured the underlying distribution. To address this limitation, we averaged values by gestational week and modeled percentiles rather than individual data points. Among smoothed models, the second‐order polynomial was retained, as it provided a balance between goodness of fit and parsimony, while avoiding overfitting observed with higher‐order models.

Raw amniotic fluid volume data and corresponding polynomial fits are displayed in Table S2.

To model the evolution of the AFV/FBV ratio across gestation, we applied polynomial regression of second and third degree. Both models yielded similar performance, with R 2 values of 0.739 for the second‐degree fit and 0.741 for the third‐degree fit. Visual inspection confirmed that the second‐degree model provided a smooth and physiologically plausible trajectory, whereas the third‐degree fit showed marginal improvement at the cost of increased complexity and risk of overfitting, particularly at the distribution tails. Therefore, for reasons of parsimony and interpretability, we retained the quadratic (second‐degree) model for generating percentile curves.

Raw AFV/FBV data and corresponding polynomial fits are displayed in Figures S1 and S2.

Secondary analysis: AFI vs. AFV

In the subcohort of 103 cases, AFI measured on ultrasound showed a significant positive correlation with MRI‐derived AFV. Scatter plots revealed substantial inter‐individual variability, but the overall association was well captured by a quadratic polynomial model. The Spearman correlation coefficient between AFI and AFV was 0.77 (p < 0.001). The fitted polynomial equation was: AFV (mL) = 6.40·AFI2–96.92·AFI + 628.81.

The correlation between ultrasound‐based AFI and MRI‐derived AFV, modeled using a quadratic polynomial, is shown in Figure S3.

DISCUSSION

This study provides the first MRI‐based reference charts for both absolute AFV and the AFV/FBV ratio between 16 and 36 weeks, based on a large prospective cohort and standardized modeling approach.

The cohort included 322 healthy singleton pregnancies, with available birthweight data in 96.6% of cases and confirmed neonatal health, ensuring the robustness of reference modeling.

MRI is widely considered the gold standard for in vivo volumetric assessment due to its high spatial resolution, reproducibility, and independence from maternal habitus or acoustic windows. Prior studies have validated MRI against physical phantoms and ex vivo measurements, confirming its accuracy for fetal volume estimation. The use of standardized fetal MRI protocols in our study enabled detailed quantification of amniotic fluid and fetal compartments across gestation, enhancing the robustness and generalizability of the derived percentile curves. 20 , 21

To further validate our methodology, we compared our MRI‐derived percentiles with the dye‐dilution reference data from Ounpraseuth et al., who compiled 1190 AFV measurements using invasive gold‐standard techniques. Both studies identify maximum AFV around 30–31 weeks' gestation, with similar curvilinear trajectories. Our MRI‐derived absolute values are slightly lower, likely reflecting methodological differences: dye‐dilution captures the totality of amniotic fluid including small pockets that may be excluded during manual segmentation. Population differences between our prospectively recruited healthy French cohort and the mixed American cohort may also contribute. Critically, the concordant trajectory shape and timing of peak AFV support the physiological validity of our reference curves. 22

Although the Generalized Additive Model for Location, Scale and Shape (GAMLSS) is often considered the reference statistical method for constructing growth percentiles, it requires a large and evenly distributed sample across all gestational ages. 23 In our dataset—despite its overall size—several weeks of gestation included fewer than 10 cases, which could compromise the reliability of GAMLSS‐based fitting. We therefore chose the Wright and Royston method, which uses weekly means and standard deviations to model parametric percentiles in a more stable and interpretable manner under such conditions. 24

This approach provided smooth and physiologically plausible percentile curves while maintaining methodological transparency.

Only a few studies have attempted to quantify amniotic fluid volume using MRI, and most were limited by small sample sizes, narrow gestational windows, or retrospective designs. For instance, Uus et al. focused on 3D reconstruction of the fetal thorax and surrounding compartments, offering technical validation but not reference charts for AFV. To our knowledge, no prior study has proposed gestational age–specific percentile curves for AFV or the AFV/FBV ratio based on a large prospective MRI cohort. Our findings fill this gap by providing reproducible, physiologically consistent reference values from 16 to 36 weeks of gestation, based on a well‐characterized population of healthy pregnancies. The use of weekly aggregation and parametric modeling allowed us to overcome the limitations of scattered MRI datasets, and to propose usable tools for future clinical or research applications. 20

In clinical practice, amniotic fluid is typically assessed using ultrasound‐derived indices such as the AFI or SDP. These methods are practical and non‐invasive but suffer from inter‐operator variability and modest correlation with actual AFV measured via dye‐dilution techniques (r ≈ 0.3–0.4).

In our cohort, AFI showed a significant but imperfect correlation with MRI‐derived AFV (Spearman ρ = 0.77). This comparison was performed not to validate AFI as an accurate method, but rather to quantify the degree of concordanceand discordance between routine clinical measurements and the volumetric gold standard. AFI remains widely used worldwide, not because of its accuracy, but due to its simplicity and availability.

By demonstrating substantial individual variability despite overall correlation, our findings reinforce that AFI should be interpreted with caution in clinical decision‐making. Notably, SDP measurements were not systematically recorded in our ultrasound database, precluding direct comparison; future studies should compare both AFI and SDP with MRI‐derived AFV. These results underscore the added value of MRI‐based normative charts as a reproducible reference standard. 25 , 26

We aimed to include at least 10 pregnancies per gestational week, which we achieved for most weeks except 16, 17, 19, 20, and 36, due to recruitment challenges at the earliest and latest gestational ages. While these few gaps may limit the precision of percentile estimates at those timepoints, all other gestational weeks were adequately represented, ensuring a robust modeling foundation. Future studies should aim to complete these timepoints to further refine normative curves. Sex‐related differences in AFV and AFV/FBV ratio were not statistically significant, possibly reflecting either a true absence of dimorphism during this window or limited statistical power when stratified by sex. Another limitation is the absence of direct validation against dye‐dilution–based measurements; however, fetal MRI has been previously validated as a reference modality for volumetric assessment, and our study adhered to standardized acquisition and segmentation protocols. While MRI provides superior volumetric accuracy and reproducibility compared with ultrasound indices, significant barriers currently limit its routine clinical application for amniotic fluid assessment. These include the high cost of MRI examinations, limited availability of fetal MRI expertise outside tertiary referral centers, longer examination times, and the need for specialized post‐processing. At present, MRI‐based amniotic fluid volumetry is primarily suited to research settings and selected complex clinical scenarios where precise quantification offers clear added value, such as discordant ultrasound findings or suspected renal anomalies. Future advances in automated segmentation using artificial intelligence may eventually facilitate broader clinical implementation, but this remains a medium‐ to long‐term prospect.

Direct validation against dye‐dilution or aspiration at cesarean delivery was not performed. These invasive methods carry procedural risks that were not ethically justifiable in our cohort of healthy pregnancies recruited for normative range construction. Furthermore, cesarean delivery occurred in only 17.7% of cases, with a median interval of approximately 11 weeks between the last MRI and delivery, during which AFV changes substantially, rendering direct comparison invalid. However, prior studies have validated MRI volumetry against physical phantoms with excellent accuracy, supporting MRI as a reliable non‐invasive reference standard. 26

This study builds upon our previously published work in which we established normative MRI‐based reference ranges for fetal body volume (FBV) and fetal brain volume across gestation, using a cohort of 260 healthy singleton pregnancies between 16 and 36 weeks. validated datasets provided a physiologically grounded framework for deriving the AFV/FBV ratio, a novel volumetric marker that integrates fetal growth and fluid balance. Here, we complement our earlier contributions by providing normative charts for absolute amniotic fluid volume (AFV) using the same imaging pipeline, and by introducing the AFV/FBV ratio as a potentially more robust index than gestational‐age–based metrics alone. In the interest of consistency and transparency, placental volumes were also segmented and analyzed on the same cohort, given their distinct clinical and physiological implications; these data are currently under review. Fetus, amniotic fluid, and placenta can be assessed simultaneously from a single, rapid MRI sequence (~2 min), widely available in standard protocols. This opens the path for integrated, automated MRI‐based assessment of fetal physiology in future multicenter clinical workflows. 12 , 13 , 27

CONCLUSION

This study provides the first MRI‐based normative charts for absolute amniotic fluid volume and introduces the AFV/FBV ratio as a novel marker of fetal growth–fluid balance across mid‐ to late gestation. Based on a large, well‐characterized cohort and standardized imaging protocols, we offer reproducible reference tools with clear clinical relevance. While MRI is not yet used routinely for fluid assessment, our results support its role as a complementary modality in selected cases, and future work will focus on multicenter validation and automation to enable clinical integration. As a secondary analysis, we further showed that AFI correlates with MRI‐derived AFV, though imperfectly, underscoring the added value of MRI‐based normative charts as a reproducible reference standard.
