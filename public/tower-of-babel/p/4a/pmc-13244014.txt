Not Just Hematoma or Infection
Authors: Gal T Peleg, Cyrus A Hadadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: cardiac pacemaker, complication, diet, electrophysiology, imaging

Pocket-related complications remain an important source of morbidity in patients with cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs). When a patient presents after CIED implantation with new device-site swelling, the differential diagnosis is appropriately dominated by hematoma, seroma, and infection. However, rare lymphatic complications may occasionally masquerade as these more familiar entities and require a broader diagnostic framework.

In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, O'Neill et al1 describe a case of a 58-year-old woman with myotonic dystrophy who presented with CIED pocket swelling found during a routine clinic visit 6 years after implantation. Aspiration of the collection identified a milky fluid with elevated triglyceride levels and no evidence of bacterial superinfection, ultimately consistent with chylous fluid accumulation. Subsequent lymphangiography and lymphoscintigraphy demonstrated impaired thoracic duct drainage, and the authors attributed the presentation to injury to the thoracic duct during pacemaker placement. The swelling did not recur.

Chyle accumulation can be classified by etiology into traumatic and nontraumatic. Although most documented cases of chyle leakage are of chylothorax, the principle is similar. Chylothorax due to thoracic duct trauma usually presents acutely within days,2 whereas nontraumatic chylothorax often manifests within months to years, with the etiology being fibrosis and/or thrombosis of the venous system leading to stasis of blood flow in the left subclavian vein and thoracic duct.3

The thoracic duct terminates in the jugulosubclavian angle in 32% of cases,4 an area traversed during lead placement in pacemaker implantation, which may therefore result in thoracic duct complications.

The authors correctly identified thoracic duct damage as the most likely cause given the strength of their dedicated lymphatic imaging. However, the markedly delayed presentation also raises the alternative diagnostic possibility of progressive lymphovenous outflow obstruction at the jugulosubclavian angle. Progressive outflow obstruction caused by lead-associated stenosis or thrombosis occurs in up to 30% of patients with CIEDs.5

The lymphatic system passively drains into the low-pressure central venous system via a negative pressure gradient. Venous obstruction can increase hydrostatic pressure, disrupt this gradient, and impair lymphatic drainage. The resulting lymphatic hypertension can lead to retrograde flow and eventually to leakage of fluid into the interstitial space. This mechanism has been previously described, often resulting in chylothorax.3,6 In the case presented, impaired lymphatic drainage may have instead decompressed into a low-resistance pocket rather than the mediastinum or pleural space. This mechanism represents a gradual, progressive process rather than acute fluid accumulation, which is consistent with the patient's presentation.

This case also highlights the diagnostic challenges of pacemaker pocket infection. An American Heart Association scientific statement recommends the use of 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG PET/CT) to assist with diagnostic uncertainty.7 FDG PET/CT may represent a safer diagnostic alternative to needle aspiration in cases of fluid accumulation and suspicion of infection, with a sensitivity of 87% and specificity of 94%.8 Despite its diagnostic utility, the use of PET/CT is often limited by logistical and financial factors.9 Further studies are still needed to establish affordable and noninvasive tests for the diagnosis of pocket infection. So far, inflammatory biomarkers have not been shown to be effective in the diagnosis of pocket infection, and the American Heart Association does not recommend their use.7

O'Neill et al1 prudently assessed infection as low risk given the disproportionately large and fluctuant collection in the absence of either local or systemic signs of infection. In addition, aspiration was performed using meticulous sterile technique and with a plan to proceed to pocket exploration and system extraction should any evidence of infection be discovered. A triglyceride concentration above the conventional threshold (typically >200 mg/dL) strongly supports a diagnosis of chyle. Notably, the authors pursued dedicated lymphatic imaging, with evidence of failure of tracer to progress into the thorax, consistent with interruption or severe impedance of thoracic duct flow.

As leadless pacemaker use expands, these systems reduce infection risk and eliminate pocket-related complications, although most patients still receive transvenous devices in current practice.10

In conclusion, this case highlights chylous pocket collection as a rare but important cause of delayed swelling after device implantation. Although direct thoracic duct injury during pacemaker placement is possible, the delayed presentation also raises the alternative possibility of progressive lymphovenous outflow obstruction related to chronic transvenous leads. For practicing electrophysiologists, the key lesson is simple: Atypical, isolated pocket collections warrant physiology-based diagnostic reasoning and occasional consideration of uncommon lymphatic complications.
