Incidence of Primary Hyperparathyroidism in the Current Era: Have We Finally Reached a Steady State?
Authors: Robert A Wermers
Published: 2023

The epidemiology of primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) has changed substantially since initial reports demonstrating classic complications in severe disease. The first insights into current PHPT epidemiologic features were derived from population-based screening studies measuring serum calcium in adults. It became clear that hypercalcemia was a prevalent condition (6%) in asymptomatic adults (1). Subsequent US population-based assessments identified that after the introduction of automated chemistry panels in the 1970s, there was a dramatic increase in PHPT incidence, which subsequently declined in the early 1990s only to increase significantly again in 1998 (2). Indeed, PHPT is currently one of the most common endocrine disorders, especially in postmenopausal women.

The study by Soto-Pedre et al (3) is a welcome update to the incidence and prevalence of PHPT, expanding our knowledge up to 2018 in a well-defined community population. Previously, this group reported a higher PHPT incidence rate of 75.5 per 100 000 from 1998 to 2006 in Tayside, Scotland, UK (4). Subsequently, others also confirmed higher PHPT incidence rates during this period (2, 5, 6). A particular strength of this cohort is the robust medical record system providing comprehensive retrospective evaluations. Also, use of a biochemistry-based diagnosis in addition to diagnostic codes allows a more complete estimate of the true incidence of PHPT. This is crucial since most individuals with PHPT are asymptomatic and often do not have further evaluation of hypercalcemia in the community setting, including the measurement of parathyroid hormone (PTH). The authors’ methods also permitted comparison of the prevalence and incidence rates to their earlier cohort. Thus, community-based epidemiologic data regarding PHPT currently spans 5 decades (1965-2018) (2, 3).

There are several important findings from the updated Tayside cohort. The overall prevalence of PHPT from 2007 to 2018 was 0.84% and was much higher in women (1.18%) than men (0.48%). From 2007 to 2018, an increased prevalence was appreciated, with an estimated overall prevalence of 1.02% in 2018. Adjusted incidence rates on the other hand decreased from a peak of 11.14 per 10 000 person-years in 2007 to 4.78 per 10 000 person-years in 2009 and remained relatively stable until 2018, suggesting this may be the “true” incidence rate in Tayside adults. Interestingly, incidence rates in this population decreased from 1998 to 2007, which is consistent with observations in Rochester, Minnesota, USA, after 2007 (2). This is likely related to changes in case ascertainment after the introduction of more widespread measurement of serum calcium with the advent of osteoporosis screening guidelines and emerging availability of osteoporosis medications in a population at increased risk for PHPT. However, data on serum calcium measurements overall or in higher-risk individuals in this population were not provided. A key point from this and other population-based studies on PHPT is that both incidence and prevalence are highly influenced by the frequency of serum calcium measurement and the individuals in whom measurement is performed.

The Tayside results confirm that PHPT is a common endocrine condition in the community, especially in postmenopausal women. Although incidence rates have stabilized, prevalence has increased because of greater life expectancy in the Scottish population, especially in older individuals who also have the highest incidence rates of PHPT. From a clinical perspective, there are several implications. First, given risk of disease progression, long-term follow-up will be needed in observed PHPT patients. Another concern is how do we recognize patients with asymptomatic PHPT and alert providers about the possible diagnosis so appropriate management can be considered? The potential role of artificial intelligence and electronic medical record alerts may help in this regard. Finally, owing to the morbidity associated with PHPT, parathyroidectomy may become an increasingly common surgery given the high likelihood of cure with low risk of complications in high-volume centers.

There are several emerging concepts that may influence PHPT epidemiology in the future. First, recent investigations suggest that familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia (FHH) is more prevalent than previously thought. Pathogenic mutations in the calcium-sensing receptor causing FHH1 have an estimated prevalence of 74.1 per 100 000, which approaches the prevalence of PHPT in premenopausal women and men. If one also considers FHH2, FHH3, and the 30% of individuals with FHH who do not have an identifiable mutation, then the prevalence should be higher. Further complicating matters, PHPT and FHH can be biochemically indistinguishable. Also, some FHH patients will undergo parathyroid surgery and this should be considered in persistent and recurrent PHPT. It is likely that genetic testing will be used more frequently to distinguish these entities, especially in younger individuals, those with a family history of hypercalcemia or PHPT, or if hypercalcemia has been long-standing without evidence of prior eucalcemia (7).

Normocalcemic PHPT is increasingly recognized as a milder subtype of PHPT that presents with eucalcemia and elevated PTH. In this context, milder forms of PHPT may be unrecognized and underestimate the true incidence rate and prevalence of PHPT. Also, PTH and calcium levels vary depending on multiple physiological influences, but in general serum calcium remains remarkably stable with aging, whereas PTH increases with age (8). The challenge in identifying patients with normocalcemic PHPT is distinguishing this from other causes of secondary hyperparathyroidism.

Finally, it is clear that incidence rates and prevalence are growing in countries outside North America and Europe due to increasing serum calcium measurements, which have also been associated with a change in patient presentation from symptomatic to asymptomatic disease. Hence, a global increase in the incidence rates and prevalence of PHPT should be anticipated. It will be important to have population-based data from other countries to better understand the global burden of this condition. This would be helpful to clarify ethnic differences in PHPT and perhaps allow provision of individualized management. This is especially of interest since in the United States older Black women have the highest prevalence of PHPT (6). Such data may also provide insights into possible fundamental mechanisms for the development of PHPT, which remains largely unknown in sporadic disease. Indeed, it will be interesting to see how the landscape of PHPT changes over the next 5 decades.
