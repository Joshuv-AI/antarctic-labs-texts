Effect of boiling and extraction methods using water or 80 % ethanol on the physiological activities of the inner shell of chestnut (Castanea crenata)
Authors: Hajeong Kim, Jingyeom Kim, Jin Ah Cho, Inhwa Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chestnut inner shells, generated as waste during chestnut processing, were extracted either raw (RC) or after boiling (BC) using four different extraction methods to determine the effects of those on physiological activities. The hot-water extract of RC showed the highest radical scavenging activities among all the extracts, the autoclave extract of RC showed the highest SOD-like activity and the hot-water extract of BC showed the highest lipase and α-glucosidase inhibitory activities. The lowest NO production in the macrophages treated with the stirring extract of BC and hot water extract of RC most significantly reduced the mRNA expression of inducible NO synthase and pro-inflammatory cytokines, IL-6 and TNF-α. These results implied that the application of boiling and a certain extraction method enhances specific target activities and confirmed the potential of chestnut inner shell as a useful material with health benefits and economic value.

Introduction

Chestnuts (Castanea crenata), belonging to the family Fagaceae, are natively grown and consumed in various regions including Europe, Asia, and North America (Seo et al., 2016). However, their shells constitute wastes generated during chestnut processing, and account for approximately 10 % of the total weight of chestnut (Ham et al., 2015). Since the inner shell of chestnuts has a high tannin content, ranging over 7.83–71.42 %, reuse of this waste containing residual functional components would be cost-effective and environmentally beneficial (Ham et al., 2015). In East Asia, including Korea, the chestnut inner shell is used as a medicinal material and an ingredient in cosmetics owing to its anti-aging and anti-wrinkle properties (Jung et al., 2016). Recent studies have reported an improvement in blood lipid profiles (Noh et al., 2010), antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities (Kim et al., 2014), and anticancer effects (Jung et al., 2016) arising from this inner shell. Chestnut shell extract significantly reduced liver triglyceride and plasma lipid levels through the suppression on lipogenesis enzyme activity in rats fed a high-fat diet (Noh et al., 2010). Kim et al. (2014) evaluated the inhibitory activity of chestnut inner shell extract on xanthine oxidase, a key enzyme of oxidative stress; they reported an IC50 value of 490.6–591.3 μg/mL and that it inhibited cellular nitric oxide (NO) production by 83 % at a concentration of 3 mg/mL. Furthermore, Jung et al. (2016) reported that ethyl acetate extracts of chestnut inner shells exhibited anticancer activity against cancer cells such as human colon and breast adenocarcinomas and human liver carcinoma cell lines. These studies support the possibility of chestnut inner shell as a functional material, with the reduction of discarding it as food waste.

The physiological activities of chestnut inner shell are mainly attributed to their phenolic acid, flavonoid, and tannin contents (Hu et al., 2021). Gallic acid is the main simple phenolic acid, reaching 150.09 mg/g dry extract (Hu et al., 2021) and rutin and quercetin, main flavonoid of chestnut shell, significantly inhibit α-amylase and α-glucosidase activity (Liu et al., 2020). Contents of condensed tannins reaches 162.49 mg GAE/g dry extract of chestnut shell (Squillaci et al., 2018).

The activities of polyphenols are known to be influenced by the extraction solvent and method (Mojzer et al., 2016). Since polyphenols have different solubilities depending on their molecular size, bonds, and structure, different extraction methods and solvents can be applied depending on the target molecule (Mojzer et al., 2016). Although recent studies have explored the potential application of chestnut inner shells as functional materials in the food industry (Jung et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2014; Noh et al., 2010), the studies are deficient in the extraction and processing methods of chestnut inner shells. Therefore, in the current study, we aimed to evaluate the effect of extraction condition on the physiological activities, including antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, using various extraction methods for both raw and boiled chestnut inner shells that were generated before and after cooking the chestnuts. Overall, we aimed to contribute to the reduction of food waste by confirming the potential of chestnut inner shells as a functional material that can be applied in the food industry.

Materials and methods

Materials

Chestnut inner shells (Gyeryongsan Albam Farming Association Corporation, Gongju, Korea) were collected as raw material (RC) before boiling, and boiled chestnut inner shells (BC) were obtained after boiling the shells for 20 min. Both RC and BC, having 52.63 ± 0.45 % and 56.72 ± 0.48 % moisture, respectively, were dried in a food dryer (ZFD-C301, Zaigle, Seoul, Korea) for 6 h at 60 °C to reach the moisture content at 7.83 ± 1.67 % (Anurag et al., 2016) and then ground using a food grinder (M 20, IKA-WERKE, Staufen, Germany) for 3 min.

Preparation of the extracts of chestnut inner shells

The RC and BC extracts were prepared by stirring, ultrasonication, using hot water, or by autoclaving. RC or BC powder was mixed with 80 % ethanol at 10 times the weight of the powder and stirred at 150 rpm for 30 min. This was repeated thrice for each shell, and the extracts were combined and used as stirred extracts (Cho et al., 2023). The ultrasound extract was collected in the same manner as the stirred extract, using ultrasound (DH.WUC.A03H, Daihan Scientific, Wonju, Korea) at a frequency of 47 kHz for 15 min (Cho et al., 2023) instead of stirring. For hot-water and autoclave extracts, RC or BC powder was first mixed with distilled water at 10 times the weight of the powder. The mixture was boiled for 30 min using a hot-plate stirrer (HS1-A, LABTron, Seoul, Korea) for the hot-water extract, and was autoclaved (HB-506, Hanbaek Scientific co., Bucheon, Korea) at 121 °C and 0.135 MPa (15 min) for the autoclave extract. All extracts were centrifuged (Avanti JXN-30, Beckman Coulter, Brea, USA) at 27,000 ×g for 15 min at 5 °C, and the supernatants were collected as the final extracts (0.1 mg/mL).

Cell culture

Mouse macrophages (RAW 264.7) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). The cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich Co., Saint Louis, MO, USA) and 10 % heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C under an atmosphere containing 5 % CO2. The RAW 264.7 cells were treated with various extracts and concentrations of RC or BC for 48 h. After 24 h, 1 μg/mL of lipopolysaccharide was added and incubation continued for another 24 h.

Total polyphenol and flavonoid contents

Total polyphenol content was measured using the method reported by Zhang et al. (2020). Na2CO3 (2 %, 5 mL) was mixed with the extracts of RC or BC (50 μL). After 2 min of reaction, 50 μL of 50 % Folin-Ciocalteu's reagent was added. The mixture was incubated for 30 min at room temperature and absorbance was measured at 750 nm using a spectrophotometer (UV-1800, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The extraction solvents, water and 80 % ethanol, as the blank and ferulic acid were analyzed in the same manner as the extracts of the chestnut inner shell, and total polyphenol content was calculated in terms of ferulic acid equivalent (mg FAE/g).

Total flavonoid content was determined using the method described by Zhang et al. (2020). The extracts of RC and BC (100 μL) were mixed with 1 mL of diethylene glycol and 0.1 mL of 1 N NaOH. Absorbance was measured at 420 nm after a 1-h reaction in a 37 °C oven. The extraction solvents, water and 80 % ethanol, as the blank and quercetin content were measured in the same way, and the total flavonoid content was calculated in terms of quercetin-equivalent antioxidant capacity (μg QEAC/g).

Polyphenol profile analysis

Identification and quantification of the five polyphenolic compounds known to be present in high amounts in chestnut and inner shells (Nam et al., 2022; Squillaci et al., 2018; Tuyen et al., 2017) were conducted by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using a Waters HPLC system (Waters, Worcester County, MA, USA) coupled to a Waters 2414 UV/VIS detector and Waters 2707 autosampler. Additionally, TC-C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm × 5 μm; Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used and the column oven was kept at 35 °C. The flow rate was 0.8 mL/min and the injection volume was 10 μL. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1 % formic acid in distilled water (A) and acetonitrile (B) with the following elution gradient: 5 % B (0–3 min), 5–10 % B (3–8 min), 10–30 % B (8–22 min), 30–70 % B (22–25 min), 70–100 % B (25–27 min), 100 % B (27–37 min), and 5 % B (37–47 min). Each extract of RC or BC was concentrated to 1 mg/mL and filtered through a 0.45-μm polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) syringe filter (Whatman, Maidstone, UK) before the injection. Polyphenol compounds in the extracts were quantified at 280 and 320 nm. Their standard curves were as follows: gallic acid at 280 nm (y = 0.0000003× + 0.001, R2 = 0.9982), naringenin at 280 nm (y = 0.0000001 x + 0.1889, R2 = 0.9957), mericetin-3-O-glycoside at 320 nm (y = 0.0000006× + 0.143, R2 = 0.9984), quercetin-7-O-glycoside at 320 nm (y = 0.000002× + 0.0126, R2 = 0.9988), and quercetin-3-O-glycoside at 320 nm (y = 0.0000006× + 0.0133, R2 = 0.9991).

Antioxidant activities

The 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging activity was measured using a modified method described by Tuyen et al. (2017). The extracts of RC or BC (100 μL) were mixed with 1 mL of 0.004 % DPPH in methanol. The mixture was allowed to react for 30 min in the dark and absorbance was measured at 517 nm. The extraction solvents, water and 80 % ethanol, as the blank and trolox were prepared in the same manner as the chestnut inner shell extract. DPPH radical scavenging activity was calculated as the trolox-equivalent antioxidant capacity (mg TEAC/g).

The 2,2′-azinobis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) radical scavenging activity was determined using the method described by Jang and Han (2023). ABTS (7 mM, 5 mL) and potassium persulfate (140 mM, 88 μL) were mixed and left for 16 h in the dark to generate ABTS cation. The mixture was diluted with absolute ethanol until the absorbance reached 0.7 ± 0.002 at 734 nm. The obtained ABTS•+ solution (3 mL) was mixed with the extracts of RC or BC (150 μL), and absorbance was measured at 734 nm after 2.5 min in the dark. The extraction solvents, water and 80 % ethanol, as the blank and trolox were measured the same way, and the ABTS radical scavenging activity was calculated in terms of mg TEAC/g.

The reducing power was measured using the modified method of Jang and Han (2023). Extracts of RC or BC (100 μL) were mixed with 0.2 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.6, 500 μL) and 50 μL of 1 % potassium ferricyanide. After a 20-min reaction in a 50 °C oven, 2.5 mL of 10 % trichloroacetic acid was added. The mixture was centrifuged at 1000 ×g for 10 min (DM0408, Korea Process Technology, Seoul, Korea), and then the supernatant (500 μL), distilled water (500 μL), and 1 % ferric chloride (100 μL) were mixed. Absorbance was recorded at 700 nm, and the extraction solvents, water and 80 % ethanol, as the blank and trolox were measured the same way. The reducing power was expressed in terms of mg TEAC/g.

The superoxide dismutase (SOD)-like activity was measured according to the method described by Jang and Han (2023). Tris-HCl buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl/10 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, 1.5 mL, pH 8.0), 100 μL of 7.2 mM pyrogallol, and the extracts of RC or BC (100 μL) were mixed and allowed to react for 10 min in a 25 °C oven. HCl (0.1 N, 0.5 mL) was added to terminate the reaction. The percentage of SOD-like activity was determined using the following formula:SOD−like activity%=1−A−BC×100where A is the absorbance of chestnut inner shell extract, B is the absorbance of the control (water or 80 % ethanol), and C is the absorbance of blank (no addition of pyrogallol).

Lipase inhibitory activity

The lipase inhibitory activity was measured using the method described by Kim et al. (2007). The enzyme solution consisted of lipase from porcine pancreatic (0.3 mg), 30 μL of 10 mM MOPS/1 mM EDTA (pH 6.8), and 850 μL of 100 mM Tris-HCl/5 mM CaCl2 (pH 6.8); a blank buffer was prepared without lipase. Ten millimoles of 4-nitrophenyl butyrate in dimethylformamide were used to prepare the substrate solution. The extracts of RC or BC (100 μL) were mixed with 0.88 mL of the enzyme solution. After reacting for 30 min in a 37 °C oven, 20 μL of the substrate solution was added. Absorbance at 400 nm was recorded after 10 min of reaction in a 37 °C oven. Lipase inhibitory activity was calculated using the same formula used for SOD-like activity, and “C” in the formula was measured using a blank buffer instead of an enzyme buffer.

Α-Glucosidase inhibitory activity

α-Glucosidase inhibitory activity was measured using the method of Liu et al. (2020). The enzyme solution (25 mL) was prepared by dissolving 20 units of α-glucosidase (from Saccharomyces cerevisiae) in 2.5 mM NaCl and 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0, 12.5 mL). 4-Nitrophenyl-β-D glucopyranoside (PNPG, 0.7 mM) was dissolved in 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) to obtain the substrate solution. The enzyme solution (80 μL) was mixed with 20 μL of the extracts of RC or BC and the mixture was reacted in a 37 °C water bath for 15 min. The substrate solution (1.9 mL) was added next and allowed to react in a 37 °C water bath for 30 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 2 mL of 0.5 M Tris (hydroxymethyl)aminomethane, and absorbance was measured at 400 nm. The α-glucosidase inhibitory activity was calculated using the same formula used for determining SOD-like activity and “C” in the formula was measured without the addition of PNPG.

Cell viability and NO assays

RAW 264.7 cells were seeded on 96-well plates at 1 × 104 cells/well and treated with various concentrations of each of RC or BC extract (12.5, 25, 50, 100, 200, 400, and 800 μg/mL) for 48 h. Viability was examined using an EZ-Cytox WST assay kit according to the manufacturer's instruction (Daeil Lab Service Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (xMark™, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Cell viability was calculated based on the untreated control group. The amount of nitrite produced by the cytokines of RAW 264.7 cells treated with RC or BC was measured using Griess reagent (Promega Co., Madison, WI, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions, on an xMark™ Microplate Spectrophotometer. The amount of NO produced was calculated using a NO reference plot.

RNA extraction and reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

RAW264.7 cells were seeded on a 6-well plate and treated with various extracts of RC or BC at consecutive concentrations for 48 h (12.5, 25, 50 μg/mL). After 24 h, 1 μg/mL of lipopolysaccharide was added and the system was incubated for an additional 24 h. The cells were homogenized using TRI reagent (MRC Inc., Cincinnati, OH, USA) for RNA extraction. Next, chloroform (Junsei Co., Tokyo, Japan) was added and the homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, isopropanol (Duksan Co., Ansan-si, Korea) was added, and centrifugation performed at 12,000 ×g for 8 min at 20 °C. The supernatant was subsequently removed and RNA concentration in the pellet was quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). cDNA was synthesized using an RT Kit (Biofact Co., Daejeon, Korea). In order to quantify the mRNA expression of inducible NO synthase (iNOS), tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, and interleukin-6 (IL-6), RT-PCR was performed using 2 × Taq Basic PCR Master Mix according to the manufacturer's instructions (Biofact Co., Korea). The samples were loaded onto 1.5 % and 2 % agarose gels for electrophoresis and observed using a gel documentation system (AE-9000 E-Graph; ATTO Co., Tokyo, Japan) under UV light.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 28.0 software. All experiments were conducted in triplicate. Results are displayed as means ± standard deviation and were analyzed statistically using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Comparison between two groups was performed using the independent t-test, and that across three or more groups was performed using Duncan's multiple range test at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Polyphenol compound contents of raw or boiled chestnut inner shell extracts

Total polyphenol and flavonoid contents

The total polyphenol and flavonoid contents of chestnut inner shell extracts obtained using the four extraction methods are shown in Table 1. The hot-water extract of RC showed the highest total polyphenol and flavonoid content among all the extracts, and the stirred extract of BC showed the highest content among the extracts of BC. All extracts of RC were significantly higher in total polyphenol content than those of BC, except for the ultrasound extract. The hot-water extract of RC showed more than 10 times higher total flavonoid content than that of BC. Chestnut inner shell has polyphenols, such as gallic acid, protocatechuic acid, ellagic acids, ρ-coumaric acid, and epicatechin (Squillaci et al., 2018), and flavonoids, such as naringin, luteolin, kaempferol, isorhamnetin, and apiin (Zhang et al., 2020). The solubility of polyphenols is determined by chemical nature of the plant and the polarity of the solvents used (Dai & Mumper, 2010). Zhang et al. (2020) reported the extraction yield in water extracts to be higher than in aqueous ethanol extract of chestnut inner shells, implying that the polyphenols in the inner shells would be more polar. In addition, Ross et al. (2011) reported heat treatment during extraction to potentially increase the phenolic compound content by transforming insoluble phenolic compounds into soluble phenols or phenolic derivatives. Phenolic compounds in the chestnut inner shell are most effectively extracted under high-temperature aqueous conditions; however, overheating and longer extraction time can cause the oxidation of phenolic compounds, thereby decreasing their extraction yield (Dai & Mumper, 2010; Ham et al., 2015). Individual polyphenol compound contents

The contents of the five polyphenolic compounds in the RC and BC extracts are shown in Table 2. The most abundant compound was gallic acid in both RC and BC in the autoclave extracts using water, at 543.63 ± 20.47 mg/100 g and 304.47 ± 15.03 mg/100 g, respectively. Myricetin-3-O-glucoside was only detected in RC, possibly because heat treatment facilitated the conversion of flavonoid glycosides into their aglycone forms (Lin & Xiao, 2024). Quercetin-7-O-glucoside showed a lower content in BC than in RC, indicating the conversion due to heating; however, quercetin-3-O-glucoside had a lower content in BC only in the water extracts (hot water and autoclave), hence implying that the conversion by heating depends on the extraction media and the compound structure (Lin & Xiao, 2024). Contrary to the total polyphenol content (Table 1), the total amount of individual polyphenol compounds was the highest in the autoclave extract of RC because the contents of gallic acid and quercetin-3-O-glucoside were the highest or higher among RC and BC extracts although naringenin, myricetin-3-O-glucoside, and quercetin-7-O-glucosdie are lowest in the extract. This difference could be due to the presence of undetected derivatives or other polyphenols, such as glucosides of naringenin, ellagic acid, and proanthocyanidins, which had previously been reported in the inner shells of chestnuts (Nam et al., 2022). Gallic acid is a dietary polyphenol with potent effects, including anti-bacterial, anti-diabetes, anti-tumor, and anti-obesity (Bai et al., 2021). Owing to its ability to enhance antioxidant activity and scavenge reactive oxygen species, gallic acid has been shown to have a protective effect against multiple organ dysfunction linked to oxidative stress (Xiang et al., 2024). The amount of gallic acid increases at extraction temperatures above 100 °C and for longer heating durations that cause its release from the tannin polymeric complex (Ross et al., 2011) possibly resulting in a higher amount in the autoclave extract of BC. The majority of gallic acid in chestnut inner shells may be bound tightly to polymeric complexes, and gallic acid is released from these complexes during autoclaving. Naringenin is an aglycone form of naringin (Ma et al., 2020) that has a wide range of physiological effects, including lowering lipid peroxidation indicators, improving antioxidant activities, scavenging reactive oxygen species, regulating immune system activity, as well as anti-inflammatory, anticancer, and anti-atherogenic properties (Salehi et al., 2019). Myricetin, mostly in its glycosidic form in fruits and nuts, has several physiological activities, including anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, antioxidant, and anticancer effects (Taheri et al., 2020). Physiological activities of raw or boiled chestnut inner shell extracts

Antioxidant activities

The DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activities, reducing power, and SOD-like activities of chestnut inner shell extract are shown in Table 3. The DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activities of the hot-water extract of RC were the highest among all extracts, consistent with the results of the total polyphenol and flavonoid contents, whereas the autoclave extracts of BC and RC had the lowest activities in the DPPH and ABTS assays, respectively. Reducing power of the hot-water and autoclave extracts was higher than that of the stirring and ultrasound extracts in RC, and that of the autoclave extract of BC was the highest among all the BC extracts. This result was completely opposite to that of the reducing power.

Tuyen et al. (2017) reported that the IC50 of DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activities of the chestnut inner shell were 23.81 ± 0.07 and 270.73 ± 5.36 μg/mL, respectively. Therefore, chestnut inner shells were confirmed to possess radical scavenging capacity based on the results of this study and the study of Tuyen et al. (2017). Polyphenolic compounds are the main compounds that induce radical scavenging ability in plant materials (Mojzer et al., 2016); therefore, the above activities might be induced by the phenolic compounds in the inner shell of chestnuts. The mechanisms underlying the DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activities were predicted by the single electron transfer methodology, whereby the hydrogen ions from the hydroxyl groups of polyphenols directly transfer electrons to radicals, thereby arresting the oxidation processes (Platzer et al., 2022). The DPPH and ABTS scavenging activities are influenced by the quantity and position of phenolic hydroxyl (-OH) groups and methoxy (-OCH3) substituents within the molecular structure. Flavanols, a subgroup of flavonoids, exhibit pronounced scavenging activities (Hu et al., 2021). These aglycones are efficiently extracted by polar solvents (Rodríguez De Luna et al., 2020), hence suggesting that water extracts demonstrate superior activity over aqueous ethanol extracts, and explaining the lower activity in BC that discards the water after boiling.

In addition, an increase in temperature reduces the viscosity of solvents, facilitating their introduction into the matrix, and elevated pressures enable the solvent to permeate the matrix not exposed to atmospheric conditions, thereby enhancing the extraction of entrapped functional components (Richter et al., 1996) and explaining the higher antioxidant activities of water or autoclave extracts.

Polyphenols, including flavonoids, are also mainly responsible for the reducing power and SOD-like activity of plant materials (Benzie, 1999; Jang & Han, 2023) and this may explain higher those activities of the hot water or autoclave extracts, consistent with their radical scavenging activities. Minerals function as agents for the sequestration of metals through chelation, contributing to the biosynthesis of various antioxidants in the body (McDowell et al., 2007). The reducing power measures the metal chelating ability (Benzie, 1999). Thus, the enhanced accessibility of minerals, such as copper, zinc, manganese, and iron, in chestnut inner shells (Corregidor et al., 2020) during hot-water or autoclave extraction could increase the reducing power of the extracts. The liberation of minerals during boiling processes (Reddy & Love, 1999), supports the observation that the BC extracts exhibit superior reducing power compared to the RC extracts except the hot water extract which implies the oxidation of polyphenols by overheating (Benzie, 1999).

Lipase and α-glucosidase inhibitory activities

The lipase and α-glucosidase inhibitory activities of chestnut inner shell extracts are shown in Table 4. Lipase inhibitory activity was significantly higher in hot-water and autoclave extracts than in stirred or ultrasound extracts of RC or BC, and was higher in BC than in RC extracts. Lipase is responsible for the decomposition of triglycerides into fatty acids for the absorption and accumulation of lipids, leading to obesity. Therefore, the inhibition of lipase activity is considered to have an anti-obesity effect (Kim et al., 2007). The higher inhibitory activities of the hot-water and autoclave extracts were consistent with the antioxidant activity results; therefore, the higher inhibitory activity could be assumed to be induced by the higher extraction of polyphenol compounds. Additionally, the increased accessibility of the metal ion in boiling process (Reddy & Love, 1999) might facilitate interactions with enzymes or substrates, thereby stabilizing enzyme structure and augmenting lipase activity. The α-glucosidase inhibitory activity of chestnut inner shell extracts is presented in Table 4. The inhibitory activities of hot-water and autoclave extracts were higher than those of the stirring and ultrasound extracts, besides being higher in BC than in RC, as was the lipase inhibitory activity. The α-glucosidase is an enzyme that breaks down the α-glucopyranoside bond, liberating α-glucose from the non-reducing end of the sugar as a part of carbohydrate metabolism; natural materials with structural diversity can function as α-glucosidase inhibitors to relieve diabetes (Peng et al., 2016). The flavonoids in chestnut extracts, including quercetin, which are known for their enzyme inhibitory activity owing to their structural properties, are responsible for the α-glucosidase inhibitory activity observed in the inner shell of chestnuts (Zhang et al., 2020). Baker et al. (2013) reported that changes in the antioxidant activity of foods induced by cooking are inconsistent, since the phytochemicals in the food matrix respond to processing methods in a complex manner. Therefore, the difference in α-glucosidase inhibitory activity compared to polyphenol and flavonoid contents could possibly be due to structural changes in the compounds caused by boiling. Thus, the chestnut inner shells after boiling are considered to be more effective in α-glucosidase inhibition.

NO is a cellular signaling molecule in the animal kingdom that is produced during immune and inflammatory responses, offering cytoprotective effects but inducing toxicity in other tissues when in excess (Coleman, 2001). Compared to LPS-treated cells without the treatment, hot-water extract of BC up to 50 μg/mL and autoclave extract of BC up to 12.5 μg/mL did not significantly suppress NO production, although other extracts did (p < 0.05, Fig. 1C). Eventually, all extracts significantly inhibited NO production at concentrations of 100 μg/mL and above (p < 0.05,). Macrophages increase NO production in response to LPS, which is an endotoxin, by producing IL-6, TNF-α, and other factors that act as an inflammatory regulator (Kanno et al., 2006). However, at certain concentrations, each extract reduced NO production, implying the anti-inflammatory effects of RC and BC.

IL-6 is a central pro-inflammatory cytokine synthesized primarily by macrophages that initiate acute inflammatory responses and chronic inflammation (Lee et al., 2013). TNF-α, another pro-inflammatory cytokine involved in innate immune responses, acts as a regulator in inflammation-mediated diseases, such as cancer, multiple sclerosis, and diabetes, where its elevation can exacerbate the severity of inflammation (Sittisart et al., 2016). At 25 μg/mL, the stirring extract of BC and hot-water extract of RC significantly reduced IL-6 mRNA expression than in LPS-treated cells (p < 0.05, Fig. 2B). Similarly, the hot-water extract of RC significantly decreased TNF-α mRNA expression at 25 μg/mL (p < 0.05) while no significant difference was observed with other extracts (Fig. 2C). Certain polyphenols restrict kinases from being phosphorylated or ubiquitinated, thereby inhibiting IkB from breaking down later on and inhibiting pro-inflammatory cytokine transcription and translocation of NF-κB, a key target for activators or inhibitors of iNOS production, into the nucleus (Karunaweera et al., 2015). Furthermore, anti-inflammatory polyphenols inhibit the binding of NF-κB subunits to the target DNA, leading to the suppression of inflammatory cytokine production (Karunaweera et al., 2015). Quercetin glycoside, with high content in the stirring extract of BC among all the extracts, has the capacity to block NF-κB, preventing TNF-α and iNOS expression from being induced (Khan et al., 2016). Although the mechanism by which chestnut inner shell suppresses the inflammatory response is not known, polyphenols, including quercetin glycosides, are assumed to contribute to the regulation of iNOS and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression.

Conclusions

The current study examined the extracts of raw or boiled chestnut inner shells, which are produced as waste during chestnut processing, to confirm their potential as a useful material with economic value. The physiological and anti-inflammatory activities were assessed to determine the effect of extraction method and solvent on those. RC and BC extracts contained gallic acid, naringenin, quercetin-3-O-glycoside, and quercetin-7-O-glycoside. Myricetin-3-O-glucoside was detected only in the RC extracts. The total polyphenol and flavonoid contents and DPPH and ABTS scavenging activities were higher in the hot-water extract of RC than in the rest. The autoclave extract of RC showed the highest SOD-like activity and the hot-water extract of BC showed the highest inhibitory activities on lipase and α-glucosidase. The extracts of RC and BC had cytoprotective effects against LPS-induced inflammatory responses and the stirring extract of BC at 25 μg/mL exhibited cytoprotective effects against LPS-induced inflammation by reducing NO production. The hot water extract of RC showed the highest inhibitory activities on the expression of iNOS and inflammatory cytokines. Thus, the extraction method and the solvent can be applied differently for target activities, since each extraction method showed different efficacies for each functional activity. In addition, the results could be used to promote the use of chestnut inner shells in the food industry, leading to a reduction in food waste.
