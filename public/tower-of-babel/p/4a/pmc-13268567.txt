Mechanism of afatinib resistance in non-small cell lung cancer patients with nonclassical EGFR mutations: A multicenter, retrospective study
Authors: Ranpu Wu, Shencun Fang, Yu Zhang, Tong Zhou, Jie Yin, Yanwen Yao, Ming Ding, Hao Ding, Jipeng Wang, Qiudi Zhang, Hongbing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) are standard treatments for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients with EGFR mutations, but resistance inevitably develops. While resistance mechanisms in classical mutations are well studied, those in nonclassical mutations remain unclear. As a widely used EGFR-TKI, afatinib’s resistance mechanisms in nonclassical mutations warrant further investigation to improve clinical outcomes. This study included NSCLC patients from multiple hospitals with nonclassical mutations who were finally resistant to afatinib. Baseline information and samples, including peripheral blood, tissue, and pleural fluid, were obtained for panel gene testing to identify potential gene mutations of resistance. A total of 21 eligible patients were included in this study. EGFR-related gene mutations accounted for 29.7% of all mutations, with TP53 accounting for 16.6%, other genes accounting for 41.5%, and MET amplification 2.7%. What we found different from classical mutations was that T790M mutation was the highest proportion in this study, followed by A767V, L858R, R776G. Concretely, T790M accounted for 7.8%, while A767V, L858R, R776G for 4.8% each, then NF1, CDK4 amplification and ALK accounted for 3.3%, 3.2% and 2.4%, respectively. This study represents the latest investigation of resistance mechanisms to afatinib in NSCLC patients with nonclassical mutations. The mechanism of resistance to EGFR-TKI in this study was discovered different from that of patients with classical mutations.

1. Introduction

As a member of the Erb-B receptor tyrosine kinase family, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) is a recognized molecular target in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Most of the mutations are exon 19 deletion or exon 21 mutation. while there is still a portion of patients carrying nonclassical mutations.[1–3] Several studies have reported that nonclassical mutations account for approximately 11.9 to 22.5% of all EGFR mutations.[4–6] Among them, 20 exon insertion mutation (30.7%), G719X (21.1%), L858R (17.0%), L861Q, S768I, and primary T790M mutation had a relatively high incidence.[5,7–10]

LUX-Lung trials have provided prospective prognostic data for the largest series of samples with nonclassical mutations.[11,12] These trials found that afatinib, as a second-generation EGFR-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI), provided better therapeutic effects and survival benefits compared with gefitinib or erlotinib, especially in patients with G719X, L861G, L747X and S768I mutations.[13–20]

Despite the initial good response to EGFR-TKIs in patients with nonclassical mutations, a large proportion of patients eventually develop acquired resistance, leading to disease progression.[21–23] Studies have demonstrated that the T790M mutation is a major acquired resistance mechanism to first-or second-generation EGFR-TKIs used for advanced NSCLC, while MET amplification, HER2 amplification, and small cell histological transformation occur less frequently.[24–26] Available data suggested that the T790M mutation was also a maj0or cause of afatinib resistance in both first-or second-line treatments for classical mutations.[26,27]

As the underlying resistance mechanisms remain unclear, further analysis of post-resistance molecular alterations is needed to identify potential drivers of resistance and inform future prospective studies. This study aims to map the molecular profile of acquired resistance to afatinib in NSCLC patients with nonclassical EGFR mutations through large-cohort genetic testing.

2. Method

This study enrolled NSCLC patients with nonclassical mutations (except 19DEL, L858R, T790M) from February 27, 2017 to July 31, 2022 in Jinling Hospital, Nanjing Chest Hospital, Changzhou Cancer Hospital, Affiliated Hospital of Jiangsu University, Affiliated People’s Hospital of Jiangsu University, Huai’an NO.1 People’s Hospital and the Third Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University. This study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of the Affiliated Jinling Hospital (DBNJ20229).

For enrolled patients, peripheral blood, tissues, and pleural fluid were collected for large panel genetic testing conducted by independent third-party medical laboratories like 3D Medicines Clinical Laboratory, Nanjing Shihe Medical Laboratory, Anlong Gene Technology Co., Ltd, and Wuhan STEED Medical Technology. Frequencies of resistance mechanisms were calculated, and percentages of each mechanism were plotted. We constructed composite pie charts to analyze the resistance mechanisms of afatinib in nonclassical mutations.

3. Patients

3.1. Enrollment criteria

Inclusion criteria for this study were as follows:

Histologically or cytologically confirmed NSCLC;

Histologically or cytologically confirmed NSCLC;

Presence of EGFR nonclassical mutations (excluding 19DEL, L858R, T790M), and compound mutations;

Presence of EGFR nonclassical mutations (excluding 19DEL, L858R, T790M), and compound mutations;

Development of drug resistance after afatinib treatment (including combination therapies);

Development of drug resistance after afatinib treatment (including combination therapies);

Availability of large panel panel genetic testing results after afatinib resistance or collection of samples after resistance such as tissue, cellular wax block, pleural fluid, pericardial effusion, peritoneal effusion, or peripheral blood;

Availability of large panel panel genetic testing results after afatinib resistance or collection of samples after resistance such as tissue, cellular wax block, pleural fluid, pericardial effusion, peritoneal effusion, or peripheral blood;

Willingness to participate in the study and provide informed consent, with good compliance and coordinated follow-up;

Willingness to participate in the study and provide informed consent, with good compliance and coordinated follow-up;

3.2. Data collection

This study collected demographic and clinical information of enrolled patients, including age, gender, pathological type, cancer stage, metastasis, number of previous afatinib treatment lines, genetic testing results before treatment, specimens used for testing, starting dose and adjustments of afatinib treatment, progression-free survival (PFS) time, best objective response, adverse effects, and mode of progression and so on.

3.3. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM Corp.) and GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software ). Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to plot survival curves and calculate median PFS. The results of genetic testing were presented as percentages in pie charts. All statistical tests were 2-sided with a 95% confidence interval (CI).

Among the included patients, 2 patients (9.5%) had only EGFR G719X, 10 (47.6%) had L861X, 4 had S768I, and 3 (14.3%) had combined mutation of G719X and S768I. In the study, the initial stage of the patients was an important indicator, with 2 patients (9.5%) in stage I, 1 patient (4.8%) in stage III, and 10 patients (47.6%) in stage IV at initial diagnosis.

Of the included patients, 12 patients (57.1%) were treated with afatinib as first-line therapy, 4 (19.0%) as second-line, 3 (14.3%) as fourth-line, and 2 patients were lost to follow-up. The dose of afatinib was 30 mg or 40 mg. These non-first-line patients were treated with 4 cycles of chemotherapy in the first-line setting, consisting of pemetrexed + cisplatin or paclitaxel + nedaplatin. All 3 patients who received afatinib in the fourth line had progressed on targeted agents such as gefitinib or osimertinib in the second line, respectively at first and fifth months. The last patients received 2 cycles of bevacizumab emtansine as third-line treatment, 1 received anlotinib + cabozantinib, and another received apatinib.

Of the 21 patients evaluated by physicians, 10 (47.6%) achieved partial response, while 5 patient (23.8%) was deemed to have stable disease. Unfortunately, all patients eventually developed disease progression, with 7 patients (33.3%) experiencing a growth of the primary lesion, 7 patients (33.3%) developing metastases or new lesions, and the remaining patient experiencing both. Among them, 1 patient developed liver metastases, 3 brain metastases, and 5 bone metastases.

Samples collected from patients after resistance to afatinib were subjected to genetic testing. Of these, 16 (76.2%) were liquid samples, with 13 peripheral blood samples and 1 pleural fluid sample. The remaining 2 samples were collected in the form of plasma + cerebrospinal fluid and pleural fluid. Tissue samples were collected from 5 patients (23.8%), such as wax block sections. Specifically, 3 were neutral formalin-fixed lung or lymph node tissue obtained by needle biopsy, and the other 2 were wax block sections.

Complications that arose in patients after afatinib treatment were also recorded. These included 1 case of rash, 4 cases of diarrhea, and 1 case of oral ulcer and gastrointestinal reaction. Meanwhile, no complications of grade 2 or higher were reported in this study.

The results of genetic testing in this study indicated that a substantial number of patients still carried EGFR mutations after developing resistance, and TP53 mutations also accounted for a significant proportion of cases. In addition, patients with MET amplification and a range of other uncommon mutations were also observed. To facilitate analysis, this study classified mutated genes into 4 categories: EGFR-related, TP53, MET amplification, and other genes with ambiguous clinical meaning. A 2-layer pie chart depicting the distribution of gene mutations of 21 included patients is shown in Figure 2.

Two-layer pie chart of the proportion of mutations in the overall 21 included patients. ESFR = epidermal growth factor receptor.

The results of genetic testing indicated that among all patients, 29.7% had EGFR-related mutations, 16.6% had TP53 mutations, 41.5% had mutations in other genes, 2.7% had MET amplification, and 9.5% had an unknown mutation.

In the EGFR-related group, T790M accounted for the highest proportion of 7.8%; subsequently, A767V, L858R and R776G each accounted for 3.9%, ranking second highest. Then, EGFR amplification, G724S and G779C accounted for 4.1%, 1.6%, and 1.2%, respectively. While other mutations, such as ATM R2832H, JAK2 V617F also accounted for 4.8%, the remaining mutations, such as NF1, CDK4 amplification, ALK mutation, MDM2 amplification, and RB1 S811V occurred in 1 or 2 cases, representing 1.6 to 3.3%.

Among the 21 patients, only 1 patient (4.8%) developed an L858R mutation after resistance, whereas 3 patients eventually developed T790M mutation and EGFR amplification. Notably, no mutations were found in 1 patient after drug resistance, indicating that the original mutations had disappeared.

4.3.1. Specimen types

When developing a treatment plan, patients need to undergo tissue biopsy to identify relevant mutations, while a sensitive liquid biopsy was also optional, considering that not all patients were able to undergo or consented to a second tissue biopsy after resistance.[28–30] The detection rate of T790M in previous studies varied from study to study, which demonstrated a certain relationship between specimen types and results.[31–33]

Liquid specimens were collected from 16 patients (76.2%) in this study, while the remaining 5 patients (23.8%) used tissue or wax block sections. Considering the lower detection precision of liquid specimens compared to tissues, patients were divided into 2 groups, and 2 pie charts were drawn as shown in Figure 3.

As a result, EGFR, TP53, MET amplification, and other groups accounted for 29.1%, 14.6%, 2.3%, and 41.5%, respectively. T790M accounted for 10.3%, while L858R was the same as R776G, with each occupying 6.3% of EGFR mutations. Among other classes, ATM R2832H and JAK2 V617F accounted for 6.3%; an ALK mutation was discovered once, accounting for 3.1%; and 1 patient with negative test results also presented in the fluid specimen group.

Due to some patients providing tissue specimens, their final results differed from those in the fluid group. In this group, EGFR, TP53, MET, and others accounted for 31.7%, 23.0%, 4.0%, and 41.3%, respectively. Furthermore, A767V (20.0%) was the most common mutation in the EGFR group, followed by G724S (6.7%) and G779C (5.0%).

4.3.2. Gene mutation subtypes

As to the mutation types before treatment, an L861X mutation was found in 10 patients; S768I, G719X and S768I combined with G719X were found in the remaining 11 patients. These 21 patients were divided into 2 groups, as shown in Figure 3.

In the L861X group, 35.0% of patients had EGFR mutations, 20.7% were TP53, and 3.7% were in the MET amplification group. Among them, T790M, A767V, and R776G each accounted for 10.0%, representing the highest proportions, while EGFR amplification and G724S accounted for 1.7% and 3.3%, respectively.

In the non-L861X group, EGFR mutated patients accounted for 24.9%, and TP53 accounted for 13.0% while others accounted for 42.2%. Specifically, L858R, EGFR amplification and T790M accounted for 9.1%, 6.4%, 5.8% respectively. NF1 mutations accounted for 6.4% in the other group, and there was also 1 ALK mutation.

For first-line treatment, EGFR, TP53 as well as other groups accounted for 33.9%, 30.3%, 17.4%, respectively. In the EGFR group, T790M (13.7%) was the most prevalent mutation, followed by L858R, EGFR amplification (5.8%), and G724S (2.8%). One patient in this group had a negative mutation test.

No MET amplifications were identified in 7 patients with non-first-line treatment, whereas A767V and R776G each accounted for 14.3% in the EGFR mutation group, which took a proportion of 28.6%, and then TP53 and other groups accounted for 14.3%, 57.1%, respectively.

4.3.4. Disease progressions

Of the 21 included patients who developed resistance and subsequently progressed, the study evaluated the progression patterns as the growth of primary tumors, the appearance of new lesions or both. Meanwhile, each group was observed in 7 patients, accounting for 33.3% of the total.

In the primary lesion group, T790M as the most prevalent mutation, accounted for 21.4%, whereas EGFR amplification and G724S accounted for 10.0% and 4.8%. In the metastases group, the most common mutations were L858R and R776G (each 14.3%, whereas EGFR amplification accounted for 2.4%. There was a 9.5% proportion in the TP53 group, 2.4% in the MET amplification group and 57.1% in the other group. In the group with both types of metastases, 21.9% were EGFR mutations, 31.8% were TP53. A767V, G779C, E746_A750del, T790M accounted for 14.3%, 3.6%, 2.0%, 2.0% in the EGFR group.

5. Discussion

Previous studies have reported that after first-line application of EGFR-TKIs in EGFR mutated patients, approximately 60% of patients develop newly acquired resistance mutations, including T790M mutation, MET amplification, V769L mutation and others.[34–39] Nevertheless, whether afatinib exhibits similar resistance mechanisms in nonclassical mutations leaves space for further exploration.

To gain a precise understanding of the mechanisms of acquired resistance, we conducted this multicenter, observational study with NSCLC patients carrying EGFR nonclassical mutations. In this study, we collected peripheral blood, puncture tissue, and other samples from patients before and after resistance for large panel genetic testing. The frequencies of the various mutations were calculated, and the percentages of each mechanism were presented as composite pie charts.

Among a study of EGFR-TKI naive patients (n = 315), the median time to treatment failure was 10.8 months (95% CI: 8.1–16.6), and the median time to treatment failure for compound mutations was 14.7 months (95% CI: 6.8–18.5).[40] Another study observed a median PFS of 11.0 months in 51 nonclassical mutant lung adenocarcinoma treated with afatinib.[41] In this study, 10 patients (47.6%) finally achieved partial response and 5 patients (23.8%) were assessed as stable disease. The median PFS was 14.00 months (95% CI: 7.426–20.574) according to Kaplan–Meier’s survival curve, which was consistent with previous studies.[42]

This study reported the prevalence of EGFR-related gene mutations. Notably, T790M was found to be the most frequent mutation (7.8%), while A767V, L858R and R776G occurred at similar frequencies, each accounting for 3.9%. Other mutations, including EGFR amplification, G724S and G779C, were identified at lower frequencies, ranging from 1.2 to 4.1%.

This study also identified another 2 major mutation groups, TP53 and the other mutation group, which accounted for 16.6% and 41.5%. CDK4 amplification, GSTM1 polymorphism, NF1, ALK, FGF19 amplification, and RB1 S811V were identified in only 1 to 2 patients after developing drug resistance. In the study, 1 patient in the study showed disappearance of the original gene mutation, providing another potential explanation to the possible mechanisms of drug resistance.

Histologic transformation from NSCLC to SCLC has been previously reported as a unique phenomenon associated with TKI therapy, and HER2 amplification as another underlying mechanism, while no patient in this study was found to have undergone transformation from adenocarcinoma to small cell carcinoma, or HER2 amplification.[25,43–45]

In this study, multiple genetic mutations were detected in these resistant patients, which may represent potential resistance mechanisms and require further functional validation, but several limitations must be acknowledged. Due to the unavailability of pretreatment tissue samples and complete genetic testing data of all patients, although we acquired the results of panel genetic testing, the genetic changes identified cannot be definitively attributed to the treatment. Meanwhile, liquid samples were used as test specimens in some patients, therefore, bias resulting from different sample species cannot be excluded.

Furthermore, some patients exhibited numerous mutated genes in final genetic test results, indicating the possibility of multiple resistance mechanisms. We speculated that patients might have more than 1 possible resistance mechanism, which are awaiting further studies to confirm. Meanwhile, several mutations presented only once, such as ALK, FGF19 amplification, RB1 S811V, which had not yet been confirmed to be linked with afatinib or might simply reflect individual differences.

By exploring mutated gene alterations before and after acquired resistance in NSCLC patients with nonclassical mutations, we aimed to develop personalized treatment strategies for patients in both the initial and resistant settings, ultimately leading to improved overall survival. Although third-generation TKIs like osimertinib are effective against T790M-mediated resistance, patients with afatinib failure may benefit from combination strategies targeting MET or HER2 alterations, or histologic transformation, to overcome complex resistance mechanisms.[46,47]
