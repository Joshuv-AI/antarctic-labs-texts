Dose selection for biological enzyme replacement therapy indicated for inborn errors of metabolism
Authors: Yuen Yi Hon, Jie Wang, Henrietta Abodakpi, Anand Balakrishnan, Michael Pacanowski, Sushanta Chakder, Patroula Smpokou, Kathleen Donohue, Yow‐Ming C. Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper summarizes key features of the dose‐finding strategies used in the development of 11 approved new molecular entities that are first‐in‐class enzyme replacement therapy (ERT), with a goal to gain insight into the dose exploration approaches to inform efficient dose‐finding in future development of biological products for Inborn Errors of Metabolism (IEM). Dose exploration should preferably begin in in vitro studies, followed by testing multiple doses in an appropriate animal disease model, when available, which can provide important information for dose assessment in humans. Performing adequate dose‐finding in early phase clinical studies in a well‐defined study population, including pediatric subjects, is generally critical to inform dose selection for pivotal trials; alternatively, additional dose exploration can be incorporated as part of a pivotal trial. Two important considerations for successful dose selection include (1) identifying appropriate disease‐specific endpoints, including pharmacodynamic (PD) end points and intermediate clinical end points or clinical end points, and (2) designing a study with adequate treatment durations for evaluating these end points. Appropriately selected PD biomarkers is useful for dose selection, and early development of these biomarkers can facilitate the overall clinical development program. Optimization of ERT doses, as well as evaluations of patient intrinsic factors and/or immune tolerance strategies may be necessary to overcome antibody responses or increase efficacy in IEM.

INTRODUCTION

Development of enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) for various inborn errors of metabolism (IEM) conditions has many challenges, including the small patient population (in many cases pediatric population), heterogeneity in disease manifestations and phenotypes, and limited understanding of the natural history of the disease. Up to 2020, 16 biological products were approved for the treatment of 12 different IEM conditions, all of which are considered rare diseases in the United States because each affect fewer than 200,000 individuals. Although approved biological treatments are available for as many as 12 IEM diseases, the selection of an optimal dose/dosing regimen remains challenging for successful drug development of many novel ERTs that are currently under investigation.

IEMs represent a large class of inherited metabolic diseases due to enzyme deficiencies caused by pathogenic variants of single genes involved in the metabolic pathway of the disease. The deficiency of enzyme activity prevents the degradation of natural endogenous substrates which then accumulate in various tissues within the body. Fourteen of the 16 approved products for IEM are indicated for lysosomal diseases (LDs) in which substrates are located inside the lysosomes of the cells. Specifically, Pompe disease is associated with an accumulation of glycogen. Gaucher disease, Fabry disease, lysosomal acid lipase (LAL) deficiency, and tripeptidyl‐peptidase‐1 (TPP1) deficiency (also known as neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis 2 [CLN2]) are associated with the accumulation of various lipids. Five different mucopolysaccharidoses (MPS I, II, IVA, VI, and VII) are associated with the accumulation of a variety of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs). A progressive accumulation of substrates in the lysosomes leads to cellular enlargement which can eventually damage the cells across different tissues and organs, and the affected organs often are highly variable within and across diseases. Two of the 16 biological products are indicated for non‐LD disorders, hypophosphatasia (HPP) and phenylketonuria (PKU). HPP results from a deficiency of the tissue nonspecific alkaline phosphatase (TNSALP), a membrane‐bound glycoprotein tethered to the outer surface of osteoblasts and chondrocytes that hydrolyses extracellular inorganic pyrophosphate (PPi) and pyridoxal 5′‐phosphate (PLP). 1 PKU results from a deficiency of phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) causing an elevation of phenylalanine concentrations measured in blood. Of note, the approved product pegvaliase‐pqpz is a PEGylated bacterial phenylalanine ammonia lyase that substitutes for the deficient PAH enzyme for PKU. It is considered an enzyme substitution therapy rather than an ERT. Clinically, each IEM manifests a spectrum of disease phenotypes composing of various systemic and/or neurological symptoms.

As a therapeutic treatment, ERTs provide an exogenous source of the deficient enzymes and catabolize the endogenous substrates. The exogenous enzyme needs to be taken up by cells via an endocytosis pathway for effective treatment. ERTs depend primarily upon the cation‐independent mannose‐6‐phosphate receptors for routing to lysosomes in the target cells where they exhibit catalytic activity and reduce accumulated substrates. 2 Most of the ERTs are administered via intravenous (i.v.) infusion, with cerliponase alfa administered via intracerebroventricular infusion (ICV) and asfotase alfa via subcutaneous (s.c.) injection. The ICV administration is necessary for cerliponase alfa to address the neurological manifestations of the disease, whereas i.v. and s.c. administrations are suitable for products that address systemic manifestations of the respective IEM. The approved dose for the majority of the ERTs are based on body weight (i.e., on a mg/kg or U/kg basis), but cerliponase alfa uses a fixed dose. The dosing frequency of ERTs for LDs is either once weekly (q.w.) or every 2 weeks (q2w) administered by i.v. infusions. On the other hand, asfotase alfa and pegvaliase‐pqpz, both indicated for non‐LDs, are given s.c. and dosed more frequently. The asfotase alfa dosing is based on body weight (i.e., mg/kg), and the total weekly dose can be divided to be administered six times per week or three times per week. The pegvaliase‐pqpz dosing regimen varies across three phases (induction, titration, and maintenance phases) with fixed dose given either weekly or daily.

A key success element for developing a safe and effective treatment is finding the appropriate dose and dosing frequency, which is particularly critical for first‐in‐class ERTs for the treatment of IEM. In this paper, we summarize the experience with dose‐finding for 11 first‐in‐class ERT products.

METHODS

We surveyed for publicly available information related to dose‐finding in nonclinical and clinical studies that led to the initial approval of the first‐in‐class ERT products. Dosing regimen(s) approved in subsequent efficacy supplements are not included in this survey. We identified nonclinical studies designed for dose‐ranging evaluations and collected the study design information, including the animal disease models, types of recombinant enzyme (human or animal) used, end points used for dose selection, routes of administration studied, and dosing regimens explored (Table S1). We also summarized the study design elements for relevant clinical trials with a dose‐finding component and the pivotal trial(s), which include pharmacodynamic (PD) and/or clinical end points, dose regimens, and treatment durations. Additionally, we collected information about the in vitro kinetic deposition characteristics of the ERTs because it provides insight into the dosing regimens assessed in the nonclinical and clinical studies. The source documents used for this study included the product labeling, regulatory review documents that are publicly available at the Drugs@FDA and PharmaPendium websites, published literature, and other online resources as specified in the text.

RESULTS

Between 1991 and 2020, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved 16 new biological products for the treatment of IEM (Table 1). Of the 16 approved products, 15 are ERTs. The remaining product, pegvaliase‐pqpz, is an enzyme substitution therapy rather than an ERT and is excluded from this analysis.

Among the 15 ERTs, 11 are first‐in‐class products, with Myozyme and Lumizyme counted as one product. Myozyme and Lumizyme are both recombinant human acid α‐glucosidase for the treatment of Pompe disease but were produced at different manufacturing scales. Lumizyme was later determined to be the same product as Myozyme. Three of the 15 ERTs are not first‐in‐class products and are excluded from this analysis. Imiglucerase alfa, velaglucerase alfa, and taliglucerase alfa are recombinant analogue of the human enzyme β‐glucocerebrosidase that were subsequently developed and approved after the first‐in‐class product alglucerase for Gaucher disease. Alglucerase is currently no longer available in the market.

Nonclinical studies—in vitro/in vivo evaluations of cellular and tissue kinetics of ERT and substrates

To characterize the intracellular half‐life (t 1/2) of ERT products in the target tissues (i.e., lysosomal compartment of the cells), in vitro/in vivo studies were performed for 10 out of 11 first‐in‐class ERT products (Table 2). Tissue or fibroblasts obtained from patients with the respective IEM were the in vitro test systems for nine of 10 ERT programs, whereas macrophages were the in vitro test system for one ERT. The intracellular t 1/2 of the recombinant enzymes in different tissues was also evaluated in vivo for galsulfase, agalsidase beta, and cerliponase alfa. As shown in Table 2, the intracellular t 1/2 values in fibroblasts were consistently longer than those observed in the systemic circulation in clinical studies. For most ERTs, including laronidase, idursulfase, elosulfase alfa, galsulfase, alglucosidase alfa, sebelipase alfa, and cerliponase alfa, the reported intracellular t 1/2 in human fibroblasts ranged from 1 to 12 days. The longest intracellular t 1/2 was 23–40 days for vestronidase alfa in human MPS VII fibroblasts (Table 2). Shorter half‐lives (in hours) were reported for in vitro test systems other than fibroblasts. For example, the t 1/2 was ~30 h in macrophages for alglucerase and 14 h in lysosomal‐like buffer for agalsidase beta. In vivo studies demonstrated tissue t 1/2 values of 1 to 7 days in different tissues following administration of agalsidase beta 1 mg/kg in αGAL knock‐out SV 129 mice, 2.4 to 4.2 days following infusion of 1 mg/kg of galsulfase in normal cats, and 3 to 15 days in central nervous system (CNS) tissues following a 14 mg ICV infusion of cerliponase alfa in cynomolgus monkeys.

Nonclinical studies for two ERT products evaluated their ability to clear the accumulated substrates in cells or tissues and the rate of substrate clearance. A near complete clearance of intracellular keratan sulfate (KS) was demonstrated in cultured Morquio (i.e., MPS IVA) chondrocytes isolated from patients with MPS IVA when treated with 6 weeks of elosulfase alfa at 1 and 10 nM. Animal disease model

Of the nine ERTs with available nonclinical dose‐finding studies, six programs used rodent models for assessing the PD responses. Specifically, five programs (idursulfase, vestronidase alfa, agalsidase beta, alglucosidase alfa, and asfotase alfa) used mouse models and one program (sebelipase alfa) used a rat model. The other three programs utilized large animals (i.e., MPS I cats and dogs for laronidase, MPS VI cats for galsulfase, and TPP1 deficient Dachshund dogs for cerliponase alfa) for PD evaluation.

Study assessment end point

Most of the IEM programs evaluated PD response primarily based on changes from baseline in the concentration of biomarkers. These biomarkers include GL‐3 in plasma and in tissues for Fabry disease; tissue glycogen for Pompe disease; plasma PPi and PLP for HPP; plasma cholesterol, triglycerides, aspartate transaminase (AST), and alanine transaminase (ALT) for LAL deficiency; and urinary and tissue GAGs for various MPS disorders. Other end points evaluated in the animal disease models include (1) enzymatic activity in tissues for laronidase, (2) intermediate clinical endpoints, such as bone volume for asfotase alfa, and body and organ weights for cerliponase alfa, (3) tissue pathology for galsulfase and vestronidase alfa, (4) survival end point for asfotase alfa and cerliponase alfa, and (5) neurologic signs and symptoms, brain magnetic resonance imaging, vision, and cognition function for cerliponase alfa.

Administration route

The administration routes evaluated in animal studies were also the approved routes for clinical dosing for eight out of nine ERTs (i.e., i.v. for 7 ERTs and s.c. for asfotase alfa). The exception is cerliponase alfa which is approved for ICV administration and was evaluated in animal studies with intrathecal‐cisternum magnum (IT‐C) and intrathecal‐lumbar (IT‐L) in addition to ICV administration.

Dosing regimen

All ERT products explored multiple dosing regimens in animal studies (Table S1). Based on the total weekly dose for regimens that were evaluated for at least 12 weeks, the overall exposure ranged from two to 31‐fold between the lowest and the highest dosing regimens for eight ERTs, whereas the vestronidase alfa program evaluated an exposure range of 200‐fold (Table 3).

Translating animal data to the starting dose for initial clinical trials

As shown in Table 3, the starting dose regimen selected for clinical trials was within the exposure range evaluated in nonclinical studies of 12‐week duration or longer in the case of laronidase, galsulfase, vestronidase alfa, alglucosidase alfa, and cerliponase alfa, whereas it was lower for idursulfase, agalsidase beta, asfotase alfa, and sebelipase alfa. The explored exposure range, together with information such as dose–response relationship for PD end points, pharmacokinetic (PK), and safety in animal studies, and potential interspecies differences were considered in the selection of dose for early human trials (Table 4). Idursulfase

One mg/kg q.w. was found to be the most effective dosing regimen in reducing urinary and tissue GAGs in iduronate‐2‐sulfatase knock‐out mice. Several doses (i.e., 0.15, 0.5, and 1.5 mg/kg) were selected for the phase I/II clinical trial.

Elosulfase alfa

No in vivo nonclinical data were available due to the lack of an animal disease model. The q.w. dosing frequency was chosen based on the intracellular t 1/2 of 6 days in fibroblasts collected from patients. The starting dose in humans was 0.1 mg/kg q.w. and was 200‐fold lower than the no observed adverse effect level of 20 mg/kg/week in monkeys. The dose was later escalated to 1 mg/kg q.w. and 2 mg/kg q.w. sequentially.

Galsulfase

Among three doses (0.2, 1, and 5 mg/kg) of recombinant human galsulfase evaluated in MPS VI newborn felines, the weekly dose of 5 mg/kg was better in reducing GAGs from liver Kupfer cells and other tissues, providing greater flexibility, less spinal cord compression, and increased bone mineral density. In a separate cat study, the PD effect observed for a 5 mg/kg weekly dose of recombinant human galsulfase enzyme was equivalent to that observed following 1 mg/kg weekly dose of feline galsulfase enzyme, indicating that the same magnitude of PD effect may be expected to occur at 1 mg/kg when the species‐specific galsulfase was used (i.e., administering recombinant human galsulfase in humans). Hence, the 1 mg/kg dose was included for the clinical dose‐finding study, with an additional lower dose at 0.2 mg/kg. A tissue t 1/2 of 2.4 to 4.2 days of galsulfase following infusion of 1 mg/kg in healthy cats supported the q.w. dosing frequency in clinical trials.

Vestronidase alfa

The reductions of serum and urinary GAG concentrations were dose‐dependent until it reached a plateau at 4 mg/kg in MPS VII/E540ATG transgenic mice. The 4 mg/kg dose was the highest dose in the early clinical trial that assessed the dose–response relationship of uGAG reduction in three patients with MPS VII. The intracellular t 1/2 of vestronidase alfa of ~40 days in MPS VII fibroblasts in vitro supported the q2w dosing frequency.

Agalsidase beta

The i.v. administration of agalsidase beta at 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1, and 3 mg/kg reduced tissue globotriaosylceramide (GL‐3) levels at all doses tested in a time‐ and dose‐dependent manner in αGAL knock‐out SV129 mice 3 , 4 ; the three highest doses were also evaluated in the dose‐ranging trial. In mice, maximal clearance of GL‐3 was observed 1–2 weeks after a single administration of agalsidase beta at 1 mg/kg. Re‐accumulation did not occur for ~1 month postdose in the liver, heart, and spleen, but gradual re‐accumulation was evident in the kidneys at week 2. This finding, together with a tissue t 1/2 varied from 1 to 7 days in different tissues, supported a dosing frequency of 14 days in subsequent clinical studies.

Alglucosidase alfa

The 40 mg/kg q2w dosage was as effective as the 20 mg/kg q.w. dosage in Pompe knockout mice with respect to glycogen reduction in muscle tissues. The 20 mg/kg q2w and 40 mg/kg q2w dosing regimens were evaluated in the dose‐finding trial in patients with Pompe disease.

Sebelipase alfa

Dose‐dependent improvements in body weight, organ weights, liver and spleen lipid content, and serum AST and ALT levels were observed at doses greater than or equal to 0.35 mg/kg following q.w. and q2w administration in LAL deficient rats. In the first clinical trial, 0.35 mg/kg dose was the starting dose, and the trial explored a higher dose of 1 or 3 mg/kg administered q2w.

Cerliponase alfa

Chronic administration of cerliponase alfa at 16 mg q2w via an ~4‐h ICV infusion to juvenile TPP1‐null dogs resulted in attenuation of disease progression and improvements in motor function, gait, cognitive function, brain morphology, and lifespan. Although the animal lifespan was also increased with 32 mg ICV q2w, hypersensitivity reactions were observed at higher doses. Therefore, the 16‐mg q2w dose was chosen and was converted to the human equivalent dose using the allometric scaling approach based on the brain weight of juvenile dogs and humans. The projected effective dose in humans was 300 mg q2w ICV.

The dose ranges explored were mostly between two‐ and 20‐fold (Table 5).

Dosing frequency

The selected dosing frequency for investigation in clinical trials was consistent with the intracellular t 1/2 of ERT products determined in cell lines as well as the duration of the PD effects observed in nonclinical studies (Table 2). A majority of the programs evaluated one dosing frequency, either q.w. or q2w. The agalsidase beta program evaluated different dosing regimens with an every‐2‐day (q2d) frequency and a q2w frequency to determine PK, PD, and safety. Sebelipase alfa was administered q.w. for 4 weeks followed by q2w dosing in the early phase trial.

Study population

The first‐in‐human (FIH) dose‐ranging trials in two ERT programs (i.e., asfotase alfa and sebelipase alfa) included only adult patients, whereas nine other early phase ERT trials included pediatric subjects in addition to adults. Adolescent patients 16 and 17 years old were eligible for early clinical trials of agalsidase beta, whereas children as young as 3 years old were enrolled in seven programs (i.e., laronidase, idursulfase, elosulfase alfa, galsulfase, vestronidase alfa, alglucerase, and cerliponase alfa). Only infants were evaluated in the FIH trial of alglucosidase alfa. The number of subjects evaluated in four of the 14 early phase trials ranged from 12 to 23, with 10 or fewer subjects participating in the rest of the early clinical studies.

Study end point

Most programs evaluated PD responses, including reductions in disease‐specific biomarkers (e.g., the total GAGs or specific GAGs in MPS diseases, GL‐3 in Fabry disease, and glycogen in Pompe disease) and other clinical laboratory biomarkers (e.g., ALT and serum lipids in LAL deficiency) in dose‐ranging trials (Table 5). The alglucerase program evaluated liver biopsy samples from patients who received a single dose administration and determined the PD response based on the reduction in hepatic glucocerebroside concentrations. The programs for Pompe disease and HPP evaluated clinical end points including ventilator‐free survival and overall survival in addition to PD response. There are no disease‐specific biomarkers for CLN2, and the neurocognitive function rating scale was the primary end point assessed throughout the development program of cerliponase alfa. Neither PD biomarkers nor clinical end points were assessed in the early asfotase alfa clinical trial which was only 4 weeks long and designed to evaluate the PK and safety in patients with HPP.

Approach to dose‐finding

Two different dose‐finding approaches were used by 11 first‐in‐class ERT products: (1) parallel group dose‐finding, where groups of subjects were treated at different dose levels and their responses assessed at a time after a prespecified treatment duration, and (2) intrasubject dose‐finding, where each subject received all dose levels and the treatment period for each dose level was either prespecified as a particular duration of time (elosulfase alfa and vestronidase alfa) or determined by prespecified criteria based on response or safety for dose escalation/de‐escalation or titration (asfotase alfa and cerliponase alfa).

Selecting dose(s) for evaluation in pivotal trials

Data from early dose‐finding trials formed the primary basis for the selection of dose(s) to be evaluated in pivotal trials, whereas nonclinical information was also used to derive a target dose to be assessed in the pivotal trials of some ERTs. In most cases, the dosing regimen(s) for pivotal trials was/were among those evaluated in early dose‐ranging studies, whereas the pivotal trials evaluated higher doses in a few cases. Refer to Tables 5 and 6, respectively, for dosing regimens evaluated in the dose finding studies and the pivotal trials. The specific considerations varied across the approved ERTs and are summarized below.

Idursulfase

The dose of 0.5 mg/kg was selected for evaluation at q.w. and q2w dosing frequencies in the pivotal trial based on results of the early clinical trial and nonclinical studies. The early clinical trial data supported the dosing regimen of 0.5 mg/kg q2w because 1.5 mg/kg q2w resulted in a greater uGAG reduction but more infusion‐related reactions than the 0.5 mg/kg q2w dose. Nonclinical studies indicated q.w. dosing may offer advantages because IDS knockout mice received q.w. dosing appeared to have a larger effect on clearance of tissue GAGs than the q2w and the tissue t 1/2 was 1 to 2 days in animals.

Elosulfase alfa

The 2 mg/kg weekly dose, the highest studied dose, was selected for evaluation in the pivotal trial based on reductions in uKS levels and preliminary data of 6‐min walk test (6MWT) improvement in the dose‐ranging trial, in which 20 subjects received 0.1, 1, and 2 mg/kg each for 12 weeks in sequence and treatment responses assessed at weeks 12, 24, and 36. 5

Galsulfase

Dose‐finding results in the early clinical trial and in MPS VI cats contributed to the selection of 1 mg/kg q.w. dose regimen for the pivotal trial. The PD response for two dose levels (i.e., 0.2 and 1 mg/kg in 3 subjects each) showed significant reduction in uGAG excretion. The 1 mg/kg was the lowest dose that produced clinical benefit in MPS VI cats.

Vestronidase alfa

The highest dosing regimen of 4 mg/kg q2w was chosen for the pivotal trial based on the PD response, that is, dose‐dependent changes in urinary GAG (uGAG) excretion observed in three patients with MPS VII who received 2, 1, 4, and 2 mg/kg in sequence each for 12 weeks. The uGAG decreased after an initial treatment with 2 mg/kg for 12 weeks, followed by an elevation when the dose reduced to 1 mg/kg for 12 weeks, another uGAG reduction when the dose increased to 4 mg/kg for 12 weeks, and finally a uGAG increase when the dose decreased to 2 mg/kg for 12 weeks.

Alglucerase

The 60 U/kg q2w dosing regimen was selected for the pivotal trial based on the results of a single dose PK/PD study. The cited study evaluated single alglucerase doses ranging from 0.6 to 234 U/kg resulting in a reduction in hepatic glucocerebroside at all dose levels albeit somewhat inconsistently. 6 Liver glucocerebroside levels decreased in eight of 11 patients who received greater than 30 U/kg, whereas it decreased in only one of 10 patients who received a lower dose. 7 Plasma enzyme concentration increased proportionally from the lowest dose up to ~100 U/kg. 7 These data suggested that a minimum dose of 30 U/kg was required for initial therapy, and doses beyond 100 U/kg appeared not to be efficiently utilized by the body.

Agalsidase beta

The dosing regimen of 1 mg/kg q2w was selected for the pivotal trial. Although no dose–response relationship for a reduction of tissue GL‐3 could be discerned, the incidence of infusion reactions at 1 mg/kg q2w was lower than that at 3 mg/kg q2w in the 10‐week dose finding study, which provided support to assess the 1 mg/kg dose in the pivotal trial.

Alglucosidase alfa

The dosing regimen of 20 mg/kg q2w was selected to be evaluated in the pivotal trial in late‐onset Pompe disease (LOPD), based on the results from the dose‐finding trial that assessed both the 20 and 40 mg/kg q2w in infantile‐onset Pompe disease (IOPD). This dose‐finding trial was also the pivotal trial in the IOPD population. Whereas the 20 and 40 mg/kg dose groups did not show a difference in the clinical outcome (ventilator‐free survival) at 52 weeks, there was a higher rate of infusion reactions and higher antidrug antibody (ADA) titers in the 40 mg/kg dose group.

Asfotase alfa

Weekly doses of 3 to 9 mg/kg were projected to be effective doses and selected for pivotal trials. The projection was informed by data from the animal disease model and the early clinical data. In Akp2 −/− mice, a weekly dose of 8.2 mg/kg demonstrated improvements in the skeletal mineralization defects, which are also hallmarks in human HPP. The PK data in Akp2 −/− mice provided the estimated steady‐state concentration range that serves as the anticipated efficacious concentration range in humans. The weekly dose to achieve this anticipated efficacious concentration range was projected with model‐based simulation using a model built with PK data from the early clinical study.

Cerliponase alfa

A dose of 300 mg administered q2w was projected to be an effective dose and selected for the pivotal trial. Chronic ICV infusion of cerliponase alfa at 16 mg q2w to juvenile TPP1‐null dogs resulted in attenuation of disease progression and improvements in motor function, gait, cognitive function, brain morphology, and lifespan. The 16 mg dose translates into 300 mg in humans when allometrically scaled by the brain mass from juvenile TPP1‐null dog to patients, taking into account the age and the effect of disease on brain mass.

Final dose evaluation in pivotal trials

Of 11 ERT clinical programs, eight had a single pivotal trial and three had two pivotal trials each for a specific patient subpopulation (Table 6), namely, IOPD and LOPD for alglucosidase alfa, perinatal/infantile‐ and juvenile‐onset HPP for asfotase alfa, and rapidly progressive LAL deficiency presenting within the first 6 months of life and pediatric and adult patients with LAL deficiency for sebelipase alfa. Ten of 11 ERT programs demonstrated clinical benefit in the pivotal trial using functional end points with treatment durations ranging from 44 weeks to greater than 48 months to support regulatory approval. One ERT, agalsidase beta, demonstrated a reduction from baseline of GL‐3 levels in renal biopsy after treatment for 20 weeks to support accelerated approval in the original application.

Study population

All ERT programs included pediatric patients in the registration trials with various age ranges. The sample size varied across the 14 pivotal trials for 11 ERTs; one trial had greater than 100 subjects, five trials with 51–100 subjects, three trials with 26–50 subjects, and five with less than or equal to 25 subjects.

Clinical end point

Multiple functional end points were implemented to evaluate clinical benefit, and they vary across IEM due to the disease‐specific functional impairments. Among 14 trials, six used the walk tests (6MWT or 12‐Min Walk Test), three used forced vital capacity, and three used ventilator‐free/overall survival or growth. The CLN2 rating scale was used for cerliponase alfa in CLN2.

PD end point

Examples of PD end points used as primary efficacy end points include reductions of organ (liver and spleen) sizes and improvements of hematology parameters for type 1 Gaucher disease, reductions of LDL‐C levels for juvenile LAL deficiency, and reductions of kidney GL‐3 levels for Fabry disease. The first two PD end points (organ size and LDL‐C) are surrogate end points used to support traditional approval of the respective IEM. LDL‐c is a part of the causal pathway of LAL deficiency, as it is made up in part by cholesteryl esters and triglycerides that accumulate in the lysosome when LAL is deficient, thereby contributing to disease manifestations seen in pediatric and adult patients with LAL deficiency, who exhibit dyslipidemia and are at risk for atherosclerosis. 8 LDL‐C was used in conjunction with the mortality benefit in the patients with infantile‐onset LAL deficiency for traditional approval. Liver and spleen volumes, together with hemoglobin concentration and platelet count, were used as surrogate biomarkers for traditional approval of type 1 Gaucher disease. 9

Additional dose‐finding

A total of 14 pivotal trials were conducted for the 11 approved first‐in‐class ERT (Table 6). Of all pivotal trials, eight of them studied one single dose regimen, including one trial in older pediatric and adult patients with LAL deficiency (Table 6). The alglucosidase alfa pivotal trial was also the dose‐finding trial, in which two dosing regimens were assessed. Two ERTs (idursulfase and elosulfase alfa) each evaluated two dosing frequencies in a single pivotal trial. Two separate trials for asfotase alfa adopted intrasubject dose titration in the study of patients with perinatal/infantile and juvenile‐onset HPP. Similarly, the sebelipase alfa trial in rapidly progressive LAL deficiency less than 6 months of age implemented intra‐subject dose escalation based on clinical responses.

DISCUSSION

Clinical development programs for rare disease treatments are generally small and with limited dose‐finding. IEM have substantial heterogeneity in genotypes and phenotypes which presents further challenges for the assessment of clinical effects. Despite the challenges, the FDA has so far approved 11 first‐in‐class ERT products in IEM (Table 1). Across these ERT programs, the rationales for dose selection were diverse and highlighted the importance of using animal disease models to evaluate the effects based on PD biomarkers and clinical end points, as well as using in vitro cellular/tissue systems to evaluate the kinetics of clearance for the drug and its substrates. These nonclinical studies have produced critical information that facilitated not only a safe dose for FIH trials, but in some cases, supported the dose(s) used in pivotal trials (e.g., cerliponase alfa). Herein, we further summarize the lessons learned from these 11 FDA approved first‐in‐class ERT products, with hope that they will be informative to future development programs for other IEMs.

Importance of animal models

When relevant animal disease models are available, carefully designed dose‐finding evaluations can narrow down the dose range to be studied in clinical trials, especially when the end points used are relevant to the patient population. A desirable animal disease model should display a phenotype that mimics to a large extent the clinical manifestations and the overall course of the human disease. 10 Greater credibility and relevance can be given to animal data if the metabolic pathways and essential intermediary components are conserved between the selected animal species and humans. It is essential not only that the inactivated animal genes are the same as the human genes but also that the animal and human genes serve the same biological functions and that the metabolic pathways are preserved across species. 10 Moreover, studies in relevant animal disease models may provide evidence of pharmacological activity and potential proof‐of‐concept data to support enrollment of adults in clinical trials, as well as the prospect of direct benefit for enrollment of pediatric patients in clinical trials. They can also provide confirmatory evidence, which is essential for meeting the substantial evidence of effectiveness requirements for programs relying on one adequate well‐controlled clinical trial. 11 In certain instances, doses identified as effective in nonclinical studies may be critical where dose exploration in patients is not feasible, either due to the rapidly progressive nature of the disease or due to the absence of early markers of disease (e.g., elevation of PD biomarkers). Cerliponase alfa and asfotase alfa are such examples. For cerliponase alfa, the single selected dose for evaluation in the pivotal study showed a survival benefit in treated TPP1‐null dachshund dogs. For asfotase alfa, the phase III program evaluated the weekly dose projected to achieve the efficacious concentration range that was identified in improving skeletal mineralization in animal studies.

Clinical dose‐finding approaches

Most IEM programs adopted the conventional dose‐finding approach with parallel group design, whereas a few programs implemented intrasubject dose escalation/de‐escalation or titration. Parallel group design for dose‐finding is well‐suited for IEMs that have a larger patient population. Intrasubject dose escalation/de‐escalation or titration offers the advantage of reduced variability by comparing different doses within the same subject, which presumably addresses the challenges of intersubject variability in PD responses due to disease heterogeneity and a very small patient population. A potential disadvantage is the need for an extended study duration to ensure adequate treatment duration at each dose level to account for the carryover effect, which confounds dose effect. As such, PD biomarkers that have a quick treatment response requiring a short treatment duration to assess the effect are more suited for an intrasubject dose‐finding approach, whereas end points that require a long treatment duration to evaluate a response could render the approach impractical or uninformative. The dose finding study in elosulfase alfa program is an illustrative example. Whereas 6MWT improvement was observed in the early trial in patients with MPS IVA, it was unclear whether the therapeutic benefit observed was associated with the dose level received at the time of assessment or the cumulative treatment duration due to sequential dose escalation and de‐escalation within the same patient. On the other hand, the PD response (i.e., changes in the urinary KS excretion 5 ) was more informative to show that the highest 2 mg/kg dose of elosulfase alfa performed better.

Integrated dose‐finding in pivotal trials

For many rare disease programs, there are too few patients to conduct standalone phase II dose ranging studies and phase III registration trials. But adequate dose‐finding is critical for a successful clinical development program, it may be possible to maximize the information obtained from a small number of subjects by using an adaptive seamless design combining a dose‐finding phase II with a confirmatory phase III period to increase the efficiency of the clinical trials. An adaptive dose‐ranging trial with prospective planning could begin with two or three doses and incorporate interim analyses based on comparative data to select a dose(s) for continued evaluation and confirmation of efficacy and safety. 12

Use of biomarkers versus clinical end points

Biomarker concentrations, such as substrate levels in urine or plasma, which are indicative of upstream accumulation of metabolic intermediates due to the enzymatic blockage in the pathway, are frequently used in dose‐finding evaluation for ERT products when available or appropriate. Although these end points enable a shorter study duration, the dose–response (D‐R) or exposure‐response (E‐R) relationship for these PD responses may not be similar to that for efficacy end points or that for substrate levels in targeted tissues. Therefore, when feasible, it would be helpful to also explore D‐R/E‐R relationship for therapeutic effects based on intermediate clinical end points in early dose‐finding clinical trials. Preferably, the intermediate clinical end points chosen are relevant to the specific disease, and changes are detectable and observable within a short trial duration to provide clinically meaningful information. For diseases that are rapidly progressing or have no biomarkers that are easily accessible to measure (e.g., CLN2), it may be beneficial to apply an integrated dose‐finding in pivotal trials with an adaptive seamless design as described above.

Whereas the evaluation of biomarkers in early IEM clinical trials provides important information for dose selection, results from disease‐specific biomarkers may also be used as confirmatory evidence, together with efficacy results from a single adequate and well‐controlled trial, to support substantial evidence of effectiveness for approval. 11 In such circumstances, fully validated assays need to be used for measuring the concentrations of the biomarkers.

Considerations for GAG biomarkers for MPS diseases

GAGs are a widely used disease‐specific biomarker for screening and diagnostic purposes in the relevant MPS diseases; they include chondroitin sulfate, dermatan sulfate, heparan sulfate, KS, and hyaluronic acid. Although a given MPS is deficient in one specific enzyme, it may result in an accumulation of one or more types of GAGs. Therefore, the plan for PD response evaluation should address whether to involve a composite of multiple types of GAGs or a specific type of GAG.

The methodology and the performance characteristics of the bioanalytical assays used to measure GAGs may depend on the sample matrix (e.g., plasma, urine, or tissue) and the types of GAGs (e.g., a specific GAG or a composite of several GAGs). The methods used for measurement of GAGs and other disease‐specific biomarkers should be fit‐for‐purpose and they should generally be sensitive, specific, and reproducible for the moiety to be measured. It is preferable to validate the method and analyze the study samples at a central laboratory to reduce the variability of the measurement to better inform the dose selection.

Dosing frequency

The most often evaluated dosing frequencies for ERT indicated to treat LDs are q.w. and q2w. These dosing frequencies are aligned with the ERTs' t 1/2 (generally in days) in the lysosomal compartment of the target cells, which is longer than the t 1/2 (often in hours) in the systemic circulation. The exception is asfotase alfa (a non‐LD treatment) which has a plasma t 1/2 of ~5 days and a dosing frequency of three or six times per week to achieve and maintain concentrations in the anticipated efficacious concentration range. Because ERTs represent life‐long therapies, q.w. and q2w dosing frequencies, or longer, are desirable from a pragmatic point of view. For therapies that use an external device to administer the product directly into the CNS via IT or ICV injection, regimens that require less frequent dosing are particularly important. It reduces the number of needle punctures of the device septum prolonging the lifetime use of the device and reduces the risk of infections which can lead to serious complications.

Dose range

The dose ranges assessed in dose‐finding studies are generally wider in nonclinical studies (from 2‐ to 31.4‐fold) than in early clinical trials (from 2‐ to 20‐fold in most studies except one). The smaller evaluated dose range in clinical studies may merely reflect the relatively limited number of subjects available for clinical dose exploration, or it may suggest that data from animal studies are informative to complement clinical dose selection. Although the doses evaluated and the approved doses for the majority of ERTs were at the low or middle part of the dose range evaluated in animal studies, alglucosidase alfa dosages higher than the approved dosing regimen have been reported in patients with Pompe disease in literature of clinical studies and case reports. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 This raises the question of whether higher doses should be evaluated for ERT products in the absence of dose‐limiting toxicities in ERT clinical development programs.

Fixed dose or body weight‐based dosing

The approved doses for most ERTs (except for cerliponase alfa and pegvaliase‐pqpz) are given in a mg/kg basis, which typically results in lower drug exposure in infants and young children with smaller body sizes compared to older children and adult patients with higher body weights. A recently approved ERT for LOPD, avalglucosidase alfa‐ngpt, has two different dosages based on a body weight cutoff of 30 kg (i.e., 40 mg/kg for <30 kg and 20 mg/kg for ≥30 kg). The approved dosing regimen for patients with lower body weights was selected based on extrapolation of efficacy in pediatric patients with LOPD and PK exposure‐matching of the two weight groups. 21 It remains to be determined whether there is a need for higher ERT doses in patients with IOPD, who are mostly infants with the smallest body weight. Until more information is available, the dose‐finding strategy should take into consideration the patient age, weight, and disease phenotype in trial design during clinical development.

Cerliponase alfa is the only ERT product with a fixed dosage regimen of 300 mg ICV q2w. Such a dosing regimen was appropriate for pediatric patients with CLN2 who were 3 to 8 years old participated in the pivotal trial, as brain size in children reaches 80%–90% of adult volume by age 2 years. 22 , 23 Lower dosage is likely to be needed for pediatric patients younger than 2 years old with a smaller brain size based on the principle of allometric scaling.

PK/PD and quantitative system pharmacology modeling

Among all approved ERT products, significant E‐R relationships were only observed for asfotase alfa indicated for the treatment of patients with HPP, and PK/PD modeling was used to characterize these E‐R relationships based on the PD effects (i.e., PPi and PLP concentrations) and efficacy assessments (i.e., 6MWT, Bruininks‐Oseretsky Test of Motor Proficiency Second Edition, Radiographic Global Impression of Change, and Rickets Severity Score scores) of asfotase alfa to inform dose selection. Of note, E‐R relationship was also observed for pegvaliase‐pqpz, which is used to substitute for the non‐lysosomal enzyme PAH in PKU. The ability to demonstrate E‐R relationships of asfotase alfa in HPP may be related to the site of action. TNSALP is an ectoenzyme linked to the plasma membrane surfaces tethered to the polar head group of a phosphatidylinositol‐glycan moiety and can be released into the circulation. 1 Conceivably, TNSALP activity, and therefore asfotase alfa activity in the circulation, could better correlate with the PD response. PK and PD modeling can also be useful in evaluating E‐R relationships and facilitating dose translation across species when the target site of action is not inside a cellular compartment.

In contrast, there is a lack of significant E‐R relationships for ERTs indicated for LDs, in which ERT concentrations in plasma are unlikely representative of the enzyme concentrations within the lysosomes in the target tissues, where the enzymes exert their functions. For ERT products in these LD diseases, other modeling and simulation approaches, such as quantitative system pharmacology (QSP) approach can potentially be useful to characterize E‐R relationships and to inform dose selection. In 2022, olipudase alfa‐rpcp was approved for treatment of non‐CNS manifestations of acid sphingomyelinase deficiency (ASMD) in adult and pediatric patients. The QSP modeling approach has been used to explore higher maintenance dosing regimens and to assess the degree of mechanistic similarity of disease and response to olipudase alfa‐rpcp in adult and pediatric patients with ASMD. 24 Emerging data from these efforts will further provide insight into the usefulness of modeling and simulation in the development of ERT products.

Dose‐finding in pediatric population

Many of the IEMs are pediatric‐onset diseases, and children often have the most severe phenotype that may result in different clinical manifestations than those in adults. Due to the potential differences in disease pathophysiology, course of the disease, and expected response to therapy, FIH studies conducted in adults with attenuated disease may not provide relevant dose‐finding information for pediatric patients. As such, extrapolation of efficacy from adult to pediatric patients across different disease phenotypes may not be appropriate due to dissimilarity in disease trajectory or severity. For severe IEM where patients do not survive to adulthood, dose exploration should be performed in the pediatric population. Thus far, most of the approved ERTs have incorporated pediatric subjects in the dose‐finding evaluation.

It is important to perform adequate dose exploration using appropriate end points in animal disease models to demonstrate pharmacological activity and/or clinical benefit, which may provide data to support the prospect of direct benefit for enrollment of pediatric patients in clinical trials. Alternatively, efficacy data in adult studies may provide evidence for the prospect of direct benefit and safety information to enable clinical studies in children.

Disease phenotypes

There is substantial heterogeneity in disease manifestations, resulting in a wide spectrum of phenotypes observed both across and within an IEM. In general, two broad disease phenotypes representing two extremes of disease severity are often used to refer to patients with IEM: a childhood‐onset disease and an adult‐onset disease. Typically, the earlier the onset of disease, the more severe the disease manifestations and the faster the disease progression; patients in this category usually have null or nonsense mutations or large gene rearrangements with no or little residual enzyme activity. Adult‐onset disease tends to have milder manifestations and slower disease progression, and patients may have decreased enzymatic activity due to genetic variants such as missense mutations. Thus far, the same dosing regimen has been approved for both the severe and milder phenotypes of IEM, except that two dosing regimens are approved for LAL deficiency. Specifically, a weekly dosing regimen at 1 mg/kg of sebelipase alfa is approved for patients with rapidly progressive LAL deficiency, and it can be uptitrated to 3 mg/kg weekly based on the clinical response. On the other hand, the recommended dosage is 1 mg/kg q2w for other pediatric and adult patients with LAL deficiency.

Immunogenicity

One unique feature of ERTs is the propensity for high incidence (up to 90% to 100% in extreme cases) of immunogenicity that could have potential impacts on PK, PD, efficacy, and/or safety. These impact assessments are oftentimes performed using ADA status (i.e., ADA positive or ADA negative). However, evaluations using ADA titers may be more helpful in some instances as ADA titers can vary significantly depending on the patient endogenous enzyme level and pathogenic variants causing the disease. Assessing the presence and titers of neutralizing antibodies inhibiting the cellular uptake or enzyme activity may also be useful, although the availability of the specific immunogenicity assays is a prerequisite.

The impact of immunogenicity on PKs has been demonstrated in ERT products. The half‐life of elosulfase alfa observed after formation of ADAs was longer than that observed after the first dose (i.e., before ADA formation). Presumably, ADAs bind to the enzyme forming drug‐ADA complexes, which are influenced in part by the half‐life of ADA within the complexes and ADA is structurally an IgG with a long half‐life. The area under the concentration‐time curve and maximal concentration of agalsidase beta following multiple dose administration in pediatric patients with Fabry disease were higher compared to the values at the first dose; this increased exposure could be due to the formation of ADAs 25 or other unknown mechanism. On the other hand, immunogenicity increased drug clearance of pegvaliase‐pqpz and alglucosidase alfa.

Immunogenicity to pegvaliase‐pqpz also decreased the magnitude of phenylalanine reduction in patients with PKU, and the implementation of dose escalation/de‐scalation or titration was helpful in improving the treatment response to pegvaliase‐pqpz in the presence of ADAs. Similarly, high sustained ADA (IgG antibody) titers with inhibitory activity was associated with poor clinical response in cross reactive immunological materials (CRIM)‐negative infants with IOPD receiving alglucosidase alfa. Immune tolerance strategies involving the use of prophylactic and therapeutic immune tolerance induction (ITI) therapies to mitigate the immune response have been evaluated and shown to be successful in preventing and reducing immunogenicity, respectively, and improving efficacy in CRIM‐negative patients with IOPD. 26

The propensity for ERTs to induce an immune response may correlate with disease genotype, CRIM status, and/or other patient‐specific factors, such as HLA genotype. 27 The impact of CRIM status and disease genotype on efficacy have been demonstrated in IOPD and Hunter syndrome (i.e., MPS II), respectively. CRIM negative infants with IOPD treated with alglucosidase alfa have shown poorer clinical response in the presence of high sustained IgG antibody titers and positive inhibitory antibodies compared to CRIM‐positive infants. 28 A higher ADA incidence was observed in patients with MPS II with complete IDS gene deletion, large gene re‐arrangement, nonsense, frameshift, and splice site mutations and was associated with higher incidence of serious adverse reactions. 29 Conceivably, patients with pathogenic variants that result in very low or no endogenous enzyme protein (i.e., CRIM negative) are predisposed to a higher risk for developing immunogenicity to ERTs that could potentially affect efficacy and safety. Accordingly, evaluating immunogenicity risk associated with intrinsic factors, such as disease genotype and CRIM status may be critical in the development programs of ERTs, and consideration for implementing ITI protocols may be warranted in some cases.

Considerations for second generation ERTs for IEM

Dose finding may be even more challenging for the development of second‐generation IEM products given the availability of effective therapies leading to a diminished number of untreated naïve patients for an investigational study. Clinical trials may need to be performed in treatment experienced patients, which necessitates alternative designs for dose‐finding. Approaches such as add‐on therapy or switching therapy may be considered. However, evaluations in treatment experienced patients present additional challenges for dose‐finding, as biomarker changes and disease improvements may be substantially smaller, making it difficult to observe a D‐R relationship. Additionally, the natural progression of the disease may have been altered in these patients, creating new challenges to evaluate the efficacy of the second‐in‐class and subsequent therapeutic agents. Pre‐existing ADAs against prior therapy may also be present in treatment‐experienced patients, and cross‐reactivity of ADAs to the second‐generation ERTs needs to be considered and addressed.

Considerations for IEM with neurologic manifestations

Dose evaluation of ERTs that address neurologic symptoms presents another challenge for ERT clinical development. Changes in neurologic end points take years to observe, and dose exploration using neurologic measures is not a feasible approach. Potentially, dose‐exploration may be performed using biomarkers that are most proximal to the CNS (i.e., cerebrospinal fluid [CSF] biomarkers) or metabolite changes in the brain as measured by magnetic resonance spectroscopy. Alternatively, dose‐finding in nonclinical studies could be performed to inform dose selection, as exemplified by the cerliponase alfa program in which overall survival in a TPP1‐null dog study provided critical information supporting the dose for the pivotal trial. Of note, the neurologic improvement in patients with CLN2, a rapidly progressive disease, was observed after 96 weeks of drug treatment in the pivotal trial, highlighting the importance of a long follow‐up duration for neurologic measures.

To be effective in treating the neurological manifestations of IEM, sufficient ERT concentrations in the CNS are necessary to degrade the substrates accumulated in the lysosomal compartment of brain cells. However, systemic ERTs in their natural form are large proteins and at concentrations achieved with the currently approved therapeutic doses are not expected to cross the blood brain barrier, and hence, drug delivery into areas in close proximity to the CNS is needed (e.g., IT or ICV). Currently, cerliponase alfa is the only ERT product that was approved based upon the efficacy results from ICV administration, and the ICV dose was selected based on allometric scaling of brain weight in animals that showed survival benefit. Several other ERTs with novel constructs designed to interact with receptors at the blood‐brain barrier to transport the protein from the systemic circulation into the CNS to address the CNS manifestations 30 are under development. Evaluation of dose–response for these novel ERTs is mostly performed using biomarkers in CSF. These ongoing research and development activities suggest that therapeutic agents for IEM with CNS manifestations can be available in the near future, and they will provide insight into the utility of CSF biomarkers in dose selection of products with a novel mechanism of drug delivery to the target tissue for the treatment of neurologic disease.

CONCLUDING REMARKS

Tremendous advancements have been made in the development of IEM treatments as evidenced by the availability of ERTs for patients with unmet medical needs. Dose exploration is critical to a successful development program, which should preferably begin from in vitro studies and encompass dose‐finding in relevant animal disease models as well as in early phase clinical studies. Identification of appropriate disease specific end points including PD end points, intermediate clinical end points, or clinical end points for evaluation, together with adequate treatment duration, is paramount for successful dose selection. Testing multiple doses in an appropriate animal disease model can provide important information for dose assessment in humans. Dose‐finding evaluation in a well‐defined study population including pediatric subjects prior to the pivotal trial can help inform dose selection. Appropriately selected PD biomarkers are useful for dose extrapolation; early development of these biomarkers can facilitate the overall clinical development program. Optimization of ERT doses, as well as evaluation of patient intrinsic factors and/or immune tolerance strategies may be necessary to overcome antibody responses or increase efficacy in IEM. These lessons learned thus far could benefit future development of new molecular entities, as well as next generation ERTs with or without novel targeted delivery to address the neurological and somatic manifestations of IEM.
