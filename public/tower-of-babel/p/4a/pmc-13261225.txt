Artificial intelligence-enabled electrocardiography to triage echocardiography for structural heart disease diagnosis in a low-resource setting
Authors: Aline F Pedroso, Bruno Ramos Nascimento, Lovedeep S Dhingra, Sumukh V Shankar, Wanessa C Vinhal, Raldner Borges e Reges, Clareci S Cardoso, Craig Sable, Antonio LP Ribeiro, Rohan Khera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTimely diagnosis of structural heart disease (SHD) is often constrained by limited access to echocardiography in low-resource settings. Although electrocardiography (ECG) is widely available, traditional interpretation poorly reflects underlying SHD risk, leading to inefficient referral for echocardiography.MethodsWe used data from PROVAR+, a large community-based cardiovascular screening program in operation since 2014 in Brazil. In this program, adults underwent 12-lead ECGs paired with either screening point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) (screening cohort) or comprehensive transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) (imaging cohort). We evaluated the performance of a validated artificial intelligence model in identifying probable SHD from ECG images compared with Minnesota Code–defined major ECG abnormalities, with POCUS as a screening reference. We then assessed the health-system impact of AI-ECG–guided echocardiography referral using paired ECG–TTE data by simulating standard versus AI-prioritized referral strategies under an established health-system echocardiography capacity.ResultsAmong 3282 individuals in the screening cohort, AI-ECG demonstrated substantially higher discrimination for probable SHD than Minnesota Code–based major ECG abnormalities (area under the curve 0.73 vs 0.52), with higher positive predictive value and significant net reclassification improvement (35%), driven primarily by improved identification of individuals without disease. Decision-curve analysis showed consistently greater net benefit for AI-ECG across clinically relevant referral thresholds. In the imaging cohort (n = 1475), AI-ECG–guided prioritization accelerated SHD diagnosis under a previously known system capacity of 200 echocardiograms per month. The time to identify 50% of SHD cases was shorter with AI-guided care, with 19 days using AI-ECG-based triage vs 81 days under standard referral, which was consistent across demographic and clinical subgroups.ConclusionAI-enabled ECG interpretation improved the identification of SHD beyond conventional ECG assessment. Applying AI-ECG to echocardiography referral workflows can substantially accelerate diagnosis under limited imaging capacity, supporting more efficient and equitable use of cardiovascular diagnostic resources.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Structural heart disease, Triaging, Low-resource settings

Background

Structural heart disease (SHD) is a major, under-recognized contributor to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality worldwide, yet most health systems lack efficient pathways to detect it in early stages [1,2]. In many low-resource settings, echocardiography is scarce and waiting times are long, while electrocardiography (ECG) is generally more widely available across all levels of care, particularly where telemedicine resources exist [3,4]. Despite its ubiquity, standard ECG interpretation has limited ability to identify structural abnormalities and to adequately stratify risk and prioritize care, and current referral strategies are often driven by symptoms or visible ECG findings rather than true underlying disease risk [5,6]. This misalignment leads to missed opportunities for early diagnosis and inefficient use of limited imaging capacity, highlighting the need for scalable approaches that better target echocardiography to those most likely to benefit.

Artificial intelligence (AI) applied to electrocardiographic images offers a potential solution by extracting subtle, clinically meaningful patterns that are often imperceptible on visual assessment, even for the most experienced examiners [5,7,8]. Because ECGs are universally acquired and image formats are readily available even in resource-limited environments, AI-enabled ECG interpretation is particularly well positioned for global deployment [[8], [9], [10]]. This is particularly true in locations with well-developed telehealth networks, as tele-ECG is one of the most useful tools to broaden basic access to cardiovascular diagnosis, and thus generate data for AI development [11]. However, few evaluations have examined whether these tools can improve real-world triage decisions compared with conventional ECG assessment, or whether they meaningfully enhance the prioritization of echocardiography in health systems where imaging capacity is constrained [12,13]. Existing studies typically focus on diagnostic performance, but evidence on their impact within referral workflows and their ability to better align testing with disease probability remains limited [14].

To address this gap, we leveraged data from the PROVAR+ program in the state of Minas Gerais in Brazil, which conducts large-scale community cardiovascular systematic screening using tele-ECG, provided by the largest telemedicine network in Latin America, and cardiac point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) and/or transthoracic echocardiograms (TTE) [15,16]. We performed a two-part analysis: first, comparing AI-enabled ECG interpretation with traditional semi-automated and physician-centered Minnesota Code–based assessment, and second, evaluating how an AI-ECG–guided approach could reshape echocardiography triage using paired ECG–echo data. This framework allowed us to assess whether AI-ECG can enhance the precision and efficiency of SHD detection in a setting where imaging access is limited, and the need for targeted referral strategies is greatest.

Methods

Data sources

We used data from PROVAR+, a community-based cardiovascular assessment program conducted in Minas Gerais, Brazil, and implemented within the Telehealth Network of Minas Gerais (Rede de Teleassistência de Minas Gerais [RTMG]), a large telehealth system – the largest public network in Latin America that covers over 1400 municipalities 24/7, currently with over 12 million tele-ECGs provided – supporting cardiovascular evaluation across community clinics and municipal health units. PROVAR+, which was launched in 2014 as a rheumatic heart disease screening program and integrated with existing primary care in 2017, was conducted in two sequential phases for this analysis, which together defined two analytically distinct cohorts: a screening cohort and an imaging cohort. Data were collected in the region of Divinópolis, central area of the Minas Gerais state, Brazil [17,18].

The PROVAR+ screening cohort included adults enrolled through community-based screening activities between 2022 and 2025 and used a cross-sectional design with a 5:1 ratio of individuals with expert-adjudicated major ECG abnormalities to those without such abnormalities. All participants underwent paired 12-lead ECG and screening POCUS, performed by trained non-medical health personnel following standardized PROVAR+ protocols and prespecified definitions, reflecting a pragmatic triage setting rather than comprehensive diagnostic echocardiography, yielding limited two-dimensional views sufficient to identify probable structural and functional abnormalities. Handheld ultrasound devices included the VScan® Extend (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA) and Lumify® (Philips Healthcare, The Netherlands). This cohort enabled evaluation of the diagnostic performance of AI-ECG relative to manual ECG adjudication in a real-world screening context.

The PROVAR+ imaging cohort comprised sequentially referred adults, in waiting queues for standard echocardiography in the unified public health system, who underwent paired 12-lead ECG and comprehensive transthoracic echocardiography. These standard comprehensive TTE were acquired by trained and certified physicians using standard clinical protocols and fully functional echocardiography devices, including quantitative and Doppler-based assessments, and served as the reference standard for definitive characterization of SHD. This cohort was used to model echocardiography triage strategies under fixed imaging capacity.

Individuals were enrolled during routine clinical encounters or community screening activities, with enrollment scheduled in response to ECG trigger alerts for major ECG abnormalities or control recruitment (screening cohort), and also through large-scale joint efforts involving patients in waiting queues for echocardiography, conducted over 4 to 6 days (imaging cohort) [19]. All consented participants underwent a standardized clinical questionnaire followed by a standard 12-lead ECG acquired in XML format using program-issued clinical ECG devices, connected to the RTMG. For analytic use, all ECGs were converted to image files (JPEG or PDF) through a standardized preprocessing pipeline to enable consistent downstream evaluation. Demographic characteristics, basic cardiovascular symptoms, past clinical history, and structured intake information were collected at each encounter by trained personnel. All data were de-identified prior to analysis, and the use of these datasets for secondary research was approved by the institutional review boards at Yale University and the Universidade Federal de Minas Gerais (UFMG) under the CAAE number 37,228,120.9.0000.5149 and by the local boards of health. The procedures and methods of this study will be made available for replication upon reasonable request directed to the corresponding author.

Expert and AI inference on ECGs

In the screening cohort, all ECGs were centrally analyzed by a team of cardiologists through specific semi-automated software with measurement and magnification tools, with visual inspection and subsequent classification by the Minnesota code, the most widely used ECG classification system in the world, which uses a defined set of measurement rules to assign specific numerical codes according to the severity of findings [20]. ECGs were classified as normal or as having minor or major abnormalities [21]; ECGs with major abnormalities were considered a positive test, consistent with prior studies [20]. This classification served as the reference comparator for evaluating the diagnostic yield of an AI-enabled ECG approach in the screening cohort. AI-based inference was performed using PRESENT-SHD, a deep learning ensemble model developed to detect a composite of SHDs from ECG images [6]. PRESENT-SHD uses a series of convolutional neural networks trained on 12-lead ECGs with corresponding transthoracic echocardiographic labels and integrates their outputs through a gradient-boosted ensemble to generate a continuous probability score for SHD [6]. The model was originally trained on ECG–TTE pairs from the Yale New Haven Health System, a large and diverse US health system, and validated across multiple external cohorts, including population-based and international datasets, and is designed for broad generalizability to diverse ECG image formats. PRESENT-SHD was applied to all ECG images after standardized XML-to-image conversion, in a pre-standardized research framework.

AI-ECG vs Minnesota Code–based risk assessment for SHD in the screening cohort

We assessed the comparative diagnostic yield of AI-enabled ECG interpretation versus Minnesota Code–based ECG assessment for identifying individuals with probable SHD in PROVAR+, as identified by POCUS. All ECGs from the screening cohort were processed through a standardized pipeline for image conversion and then evaluated using the PRESENT-SHD model to generate a continuous probability score for SHD. In parallel, each ECG underwent Minnesota Code–based expert adjudication, representing the traditional ECG-based risk assessment method used within the program.

In this cohort, SHD was defined using POCUS, which provided screening-level imaging to classify individuals as having or not having probable structural abnormalities, in accordance with the ASE-REWARD study protocol [22]. Because the screening cohort relied on POCUS, the outcome definition was aligned with detection of clinically relevant structural disease consistent with the intended triage application. POCUS DICOM images were interpreted collaboratively in Brazil and the US by certified cardiologists (MCN, CS, CS), using a commercial cloud-based storage and reading software (Tricefy®, Trice International) and a proprietary cloud computing environment (SigTel®, UFMG, Belo Horizonte – MG, Brazil). Given that POCUS does not constitute a definitive diagnostic standard, but is rather a triage modality, analysis focused on the relative ability of AI-ECG and Minnesota Code–based interpretation to distinguish participants with probable major heart disease from those without structural abnormalities on POCUS. This approach leveraged the full screening cohort and enabled evaluation of whether AI-ECG provided incremental diagnostic information beyond that captured by manual electrocardiographic interpretation under programmatic, real-world screening conditions, with a lower-cost flowchart, potentially scalable in under-sourced settings.

AI-ECG–guided echocardiography triage in the imaging cohort

In the imaging cohort, we evaluated the potential impact of an AI-ECG–guided strategy for prioritizing comprehensive echocardiography. This cohort consisted of adults who underwent paired 12-lead ECGs and TTE during programmatic evaluations. Similar to the screening cohort, all ECGs were converted from XML to image format using a standardized pipeline and analyzed using the PRESENT-SHD model to generate a continuous probability score for SHD.

In this cohort, comprehensive TTE served as the reference standard for defining SHD, using prespecified quantitative and Doppler-based criteria, with the analysis provided at the point of care and revised post-hoc by the performing cardiologist [23]. To assess the potential health system implications of integrating AI-ECG into echocardiography referral workflows, we simulated two triage strategies: a standard approach, reflecting sequential referral without risk stratification, and an AI-ECG–informed approach, in which patients were prioritized according to their PRESENT-SHD probability score. For each strategy, individuals were ordered according to the relevant prioritization scheme, and echocardiography slots were filled within a fixed capacity of 200 studies per month, consistent with local service constraints in Divinópolis and surrounding municipalities where data were collected for both cohorts.

This simulation framework enabled evaluation of whether an AI-ECG–guided triage strategy could accelerate SHD diagnosis by directing limited imaging resources and specialized personnel toward individuals with a higher predicted probability of disease. To quantify system-level performance, we examined cumulative identification of SHD over a six-month period and the time required to diagnose prespecified proportions of cases, including subgroup analyses by age and sex.

In both cohorts, SHD was defined as moderate to severe valve disease (regurgitation or stenosis), ventricular dysfunction/hypertrophy, congenital heart disease, pericardial effusion, or any wall-motion abnormalities, considering the ASE criteria, with the exception of spectral Doppler measurements, absent in the handheld devices applied in the screening cohort [22,24].

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted separately for the screening and comprehensive imaging cohorts. Given the exploratory nature of the study and the use of a previously derived AI-ECG framework, all eligible individuals undergoing tele-ECG during the study period were included. Continuous variables are summarized as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), and categorical variables as counts with percentages. Group comparisons were performed using the chi-square test for categorical variables and appropriate distribution-based tests for continuous variables.

In the screening cohort, we compared the diagnostic performance of AI-ECG and Minnesota Code–based ECG interpretation for identifying individuals with probable SHD, defined by POCUS. Although the AI-ECG model produces a continuous probability output, predictions were binarized using a Youden index–based threshold derived within the screening cohort to enable direct comparison with the binary Minnesota Code classification. For each approach, we calculated the area under the receiving operating characteristic curve (AUROC), sensitivity, specificity, and positive and negative predictive values, acknowledging that POCUS provides a screening-level assessment rather than a definitive diagnostic reference standard.

To quantify incremental diagnostic value beyond discrimination alone, we assessed reclassification between Minnesota Code–based interpretation and AI-ECG using net reclassification improvement (NRI). We also performed paired comparisons of sensitivity and specificity between AI-ECG and Minnesota Code-based interpretation using the McNemar test. We then evaluated the clinical utility of each strategy using decision curve analysis, estimating net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities representing clinically relevant risk levels at which referral for confirmatory imaging would be considered. Net benefit for AI-ECG, Minnesota Code–based interpretation, and a combined strategy was compared with default strategies of testing all individuals or testing none. Confidence intervals for performance and reclassification metrics were estimated using nonparametric bootstrapping.

For the imaging cohort, we compared system-level performance under standard and AI-ECG–guided triage strategies. In the standard strategy, individuals were ordered in their original programmatic sequence to represent usual, non-prioritized referral patterns. In the AI-ECG–guided strategy, individuals were ranked in descending order of PRESENT-SHD probability. Under each strategy, echocardiography allocation was simulated assuming a fixed monthly capacity of 200 studies, as the current standard in the Divinópolis region, according to local health authorities. For each triage strategy, we estimated the cumulative proportion of SHD detected over time and the time required to reach 25 %, 50 %, and 90 % of all identified cases. Time-to-detection curves were generated using cumulative incidence functions. Subgroup analyses were performed by age group (<65 vs ≥65 years) and sex. Sensitivity analyses varied echocardiography capacity and considered alternative AI-ECG prioritization thresholds. All analyses were conducted using Python 3.9.6 and R-Software.

Results

A total of 3282 adults in the PROVAR+ screening cohort had evaluable 12-lead ECGs and POCUS. The mean age was 66.7 ± 11.2 years, 48.2 % were female, and nearly all participants (88 %) had at least one electrocardiographic abnormality. Probable SHD was identified in 567 (17 %) individuals based on screening POCUS. The median PRESENT-SHD probability score across the cohort was 39.5 % (IQR 17.7–65.9). Results for the screening cohort

AI-ECG vs Minnesota Code: relative diagnostic performance

AI-ECG demonstrated higher discrimination for identifying probable SHD compared with Minnesota Code–based ECG interpretation (area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve [AUC] 0.73 (95 % CI, 0.71–0.75) vs. 0.52 (95 % CI, 0.50–0.53), respectively; Fig. 1A). The positive predictive value was higher for AI-ECG (28 % [95 % CI, 26 %–31 %] vs 18 % [95 % CI, 16 %–19 %]), with modest improvement in negative predictive value (93 % [95 % CI, 92 %–94 %] vs 86 % [95 % CI, 83 %–89 %]). A combined model incorporating traditional ECG interpretation and AI-ECG did not improve discrimination beyond AI-ECG alone (AUC 0.73, 95 % CI, 0.71–0.75). Overall sensitivity and specificity for AI-ECG were 79 % (95 % CI, 76 %–83 %) and 58 % (95 % CI, 57 %–60 %), respectively, compared with 90 % (95 % CI, 88 %–93 %) and 13 % (95 % CI, 11 %–14 %) for Minnesota Code (Fig. 1B).

Reclassification and net improvement in risk stratification with AI-ECG

In the screening cohort, replacing Minnesota Code–based ECG interpretation with AI-ECG resulted in a net reclassification improvement of 0.35 (95 % CI, 0.31–0.39). This improvement was driven primarily by more accurate risk downgrading among individuals without probable SHD, as reflected by a positive non-event NRI of 0.46 (95 % CI, 0.42–0.53) (Table 2). Specifically, among participants without SHD on POCUS, 46 % were reclassified as lower risk by AI-ECG, reducing potentially unnecessary echocardiography referrals under a risk-based screening strategy.

Net reclassification improvement was consistent across prespecified subgroups (Supplemental Figure 1). Total NRI was higher among participants younger than 65 years (0.37; 95 % CI, 0.29–0.45) compared with those aged 65 years or older (0.28; 95 % CI, 0.23–0.32). Across all strata, positive non-event NRI consistently exceeded negative event NRI, underscoring that improvements in classification were largely attributable to reductions in unnecessary referrals for confirmatory imaging, with paired analyses confirming significant differences in sensitivity and specificity between tests (McNemar p < 0.001). Detailed reclassification results are provided in Table 2 and Supplemental Table 3.

Decision curve analysis for AI-ECG

In decision curve analysis, AI-ECG demonstrated consistently higher net benefit than Minnesota Code–based ECG interpretation across clinically relevant threshold probabilities (between 0.05 and 0.20) (Fig. 2). AI-ECG showed a substantially greater net reduction in unnecessary echocardiography compared with Minnesota Code–based interpretation across the same threshold range. At commonly considered referral thresholds, AI-ECG reduced the number of avoidable TTEs by approximately 20 to 55 per 100 individuals screened relative to a test-all strategy, while preserving detection of true cases. The combined model incorporating AI-ECG and Minnesota Code showed similar patterns of net benefit of AI-ECG alone. These findings indicate the utility of AI-ECG across screening-relevant thresholds, in accordance with real-life application of POCUS in primary care, and potential prioritization flowcharts.

Imaging cohort results

Impact of AI-ECG–guided echocardiography prioritization

AI-ECG–guided prioritization substantially accelerated SHD detection compared with standard, non-prioritized referral. Under a mean assessed system capacity of 200 echocardiograms per month, AI-ECG–guided triage consistently identified a greater number of individuals with SHD at earlier timepoints, if applied as a systematic prioritization tool. Across the six-month simulation window, the time required to diagnose 25 percent of all cases decreased from 46 days under standard referral to 6 days with AI-ECG, the time to diagnose 50 percent of cases decreased from 81 to 19 days, and the time to diagnose 90 % of cases decreased from 164 to 96 days (Fig. 3 and Supplemental Figure 2).

Cumulative structural heart disease diagnoses over time under standard and AI-ECG-based screening strategies in the imaging cohort.

Time to diagnosis by age and sex

The benefit of AI-ECG–guided prioritization was consistent across key demographic subgroups (Fig. 4). Among individuals younger than 65 years, the time to diagnose 50 percent of SHD cases decreased from 76 days to 34 days. Among those 65 years or older, the interval decreased from 81 days to 15 days, representing a mean >2-month acceleration considering the local capacity. Results were similar across sex groups, with time to 50 percent diagnosis reduced from 91 to 22 days in women and 76 to 18 days in men. Sensitivity analyses

Alternative simulation assumptions, including varying echocardiography capacity and applying alternative AI-ECG thresholds, produced consistent results, with AI-ECG–guided prioritization accelerating detection across all scenarios (Supplemental Figures 3 and 4).

Discussion

In two sequential phases of a large community cardiovascular assessment program, we found that AI-enabled interpretation of 12-lead ECGs meaningfully improved detection and prioritization of individuals with SHD compared with traditional ECG interpretation. In the screening cohort, AI-ECG provided substantially higher discrimination for identifying individuals with probable disease and led to net improvements in risk classification beyond Minnesota Code–based assessment, holding promise as a more accurate and clinically applicable triage tool, especially for large-scale prioritization programs. In the comprehensive imaging cohort, integrating AI-ECG into referral workflows accelerated the detection of SHD by several weeks to months under realistic echocardiography capacity constraints. Together, these findings demonstrate that AI-ECG can enhance both diagnostic accuracy and the efficiency of imaging allocation in low-resource screening environments. As a widely available, low-cost test, boosted by existing telehealth systems, it has the potential for large-scale application in resource-constrained locations.

These results extend prior work in several important ways [[17], [18], [19]]. Earlier studies have shown that individual AI-ECG models can detect specific structural abnormalities, but most have relied on signal-level ECG data and have been evaluated within high-resource hospital settings [[25], [26], [27]]. Our evaluation uses PRESENT-SHD, an image-based composite model that generalizes across diverse acquisition formats, offering a scalable, real-world implementation pathway for programs that store ECGs as printed or PDF images [6]. Moreover, by embedding AI-ECG evaluation within two complementary real-world cohorts, including a POCUS screening program, implemented in Brazil as a potential strategy to improve access to cardiovascular care, and a comprehensive echocardiography cohort as part of a large-scale population based assessment, we provide a unique, paired assessment of both diagnostic performance and health-system impact, which has been largely unaddressed in prior work. Although the demonstrated discrimination of AI-ECG seems moderate, these are based on POCUS-derived labels for probable SHD. Therefore, these results are best interpreted as a relative evaluation of AI-ECG against major clinical abnormalities noted on ECG. Additionally, there is growing evidence that AI-ECG may function as a relatively non-specific marker of underlying cardiovascular disease and physiological stress rather than a tool tied to a single disease subtype [28]. Our previous studies relied on POCUS-based screening, which requires access to portable echocardiography equipment, trained personnel, and remote expert interpretation [17,18], whereas the AI-ECG approach is substantially simpler to deploy and appears to be highly effective.

Beyond improving detection, our findings underscore an important operational insight: most of the gain from AI-ECG in the screening cohort came from more accurately identifying individuals without SHD, as a potential rule-out tool. This refinement in risk stratification – which still requires further research prior to generalization and clinical application – may reduce unnecessary referrals for echocardiography, preserving scarce imaging capacity without compromising identification of true disease [29,30]. In the triage cohort, AI-ECG-guided prioritization benefited all key demographic subgroups, accelerating time to diagnosis similarly among younger and older adults and among women and men. These observations highlight the potential for AI-ECG to support more equitable and efficient deployment of limited cardiovascular imaging resources and, at the same time, improve risk classification for those enrolled in screening based on characteristics other than ECG visible abnormalities, or based on demographic and/or clinical data. Although the Brazilian primary care system (Family Health Program) provides universal and decentralized care, based on a community approach, the indication of echocardiography is frequently less accurate than specialized secondary care, exceeding local capacities [18,31]. Thus, our data reinforces the potential of AI-ECG and the need for continued investment in telehealth resources.

The implications of these findings are considerable for health systems facing persistent workforce and equipment constraints. Echocardiography remains the definitive method for diagnosing SHD, yet access is uneven globally and particularly limited in low-resource settings [[32], [33], [34]]. AI-ECG provides a low-cost, scalable tool that can be embedded within existing ECG workflows, notably in the presence of telehealth networks, to guide downstream imaging [4,5,35]. In contexts where echocardiography capacity is fixed, prioritizing referrals using AI-based risk estimation could substantially reduce diagnostic delays and enable earlier initiation of guideline-directed therapies [36,37]. At the population level, such improvements may enhance the yield of community-based screening programs and support more strategic allocation of limited cardiovascular diagnostics [38,39].

This study has several limitations. First, POCUS was used as the reference in the screening cohort; although well-suited for community-based detection due to its lower costs and wider availability through task-shifting, it does not provide the definitive characterization available from comprehensive echocardiography and may contribute to misclassification. The lower AUC compared with the development cohort likely reflects differences in case mix and outcome ascertainment, as this study involved a community-based screening population with POCUS-based labels rather than those based on comprehensive echocardiographic reference standards, which would be expected to attenuate apparent performance. However, it reflects real-life data from a screening and prioritization flowchart under investigation in Brazil, and the results were adjudicated online by at least two certified physicians. Although POCUS has inherent limitations compared with full TTE, the primary aim of this study was to evaluate AI-ECG as a triage tool relative to traditional ECG interpretation within a real-world screening workflow; nonetheless, potential misclassification related to POCUS remains a limitation. Second, data came from a single regional telehealth program, and although the setting is representative of many low-resource environments, external evaluation in other health systems will be important. Third, all comers were enrolled via ECG-trigger alerts or health-system queues, and no stratified sampling was used, limiting the generalizability of the findings to other regions of Brazil. Fourth, the sample size, determined by convenience given the PROVAR+ program's capacity, was limited, and further refinement of the models is warranted. Finally, our analyses were based on retrospective data and simulated screening pathways rather than real prospective implementation, which may not fully capture real-world clinical workflows and adoption challenges. In addition, the relatively low prevalence of structural heart disease in a community-based screening population may influence predictive values and the efficiency of referral strategies. Deep learning models may reflect biases inherent to the populations in which they were developed, including underrepresentation of certain etiologies, with performance potentially varying in underrepresented groups. By evaluating model performance in real-world screening settings and diverse populations, our work contributes to building the evidence base needed to support trust, transparency, and practical implementation of AI-enabled screening strategies. Future studies, including larger samples and diverse geographic and clinical settings, will be important to better define subtype-specific performance and generalizability. Direct comparisons across AI-ECG approaches for SHD detection [2,27] will also be important to establish relative performance across populations.

In summary, AI-ECG improved diagnostic classification of SHD compared with traditional ECG interpretation and, when used to guide echocardiography referral, substantially accelerated disease detection under constrained imaging capacity. These findings highlight the potential of AI-enabled ECG analysis to strengthen SHD screening and imaging workflows, particularly in settings where access to echocardiography is limited.
