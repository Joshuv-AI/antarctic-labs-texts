Liver transplantations in Finland over 40 years: evolution and recent trends
Authors: Fredrik Åberg, Johanna Savikko, Eija Tukiainen, Ville Sallinen, Aki Uutela, Ines Beilmann-Lehtonen, Marko Vannas, Tea Kontio, Arno Nordin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLiver transplantation (LT) is a life-saving treatment for patients with end-stage liver disease, with significant improvements in short-term survival over the decades. However, long-term survival and trends in LT indications and outcomes remain areas of active research. This registry-based study evaluates 40 years of LT in Finland, assessing patient and graft survival, retransplantation rates, changing recipient and donor demographics, indications and benchmarking outcomes against international standards.MethodsData were extracted from the nationwide Finnish LT registry, covering all LTs performed in Finland between 1982 and 2024. Survival analyses were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier methodology and retransplantation rates were assessed using the cumulative incidence function. Benchmark cases were defined by international criteria.ResultsA total of 1763 LTs were performed, with a mean recipient age of 46 years. The most common aetiologies were alcohol-related liver disease (19%) and primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC; 16%). Over time, recipient and donor age, body mass index and prevalence of diabetes increased. One-year patient survival improved from 83% in the 1980s to 96% in 2020–2024, with the overall 5-year, 10-year, 20-year and 30-year patient survival being 83%, 73%, 53% and 28%, respectively. The retransplantation rate was 13% at 30 years overall and 28% in PSC. Benchmark cases had one-year patient and graft survival rates of 97% and 96%, respectively. Acute rejection rates declined from 62% during 1980–1989 to 19% during 2022–2023. Post-transplant kidney-replacement therapy peaked at 26% in 2010–2021, thereafter decreasing to 8% after the year 2022.ConclusionThe Finnish LT programme demonstrates sustained improvements in short- and mid-term survival, with evolving indications and recipient and donor demographics. Benchmark analyses confirm high-quality outcomes. Continued efforts are needed to optimise long-term survival and reduce the need for retransplantation, especially in PSC.Keywords: Liver Transplantation; Graft Survival; Graft Rejection; Non-alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease; Liver Diseases, Alcoholic

Introduction

Liver transplantation (LT) is a life-saving treatment for patients with acute and chronic liver diseases, as well as liver tumours, effectively converting high mortality rates into long-term survival.1 Globally, the estimated number of LTs performed annually has exceeded 40 000,2 with continued growth driven by advancements in surgical techniques, immunosuppression and donor management. Over the decades, one-year post-transplant survival rates have significantly improved, rising from less than 65% in the 1980s to 86% according to a 2018 report from the European Liver Transplant Registry.3 However, longer-term survival rates beyond the first postoperative year have not shown a corresponding increase, highlighting persistent challenges in managing post-transplant complications and comorbidities.4

Internationally, the LT landscape has evolved with notable trends, including an increasing proportion of older recipients, a higher frequency of cancer as an indication for LT and a shift in aetiologies, with rising numbers of cases attributed to alcohol-related liver disease (ArLD) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD).5 To meet growing demand, extended criteria donors are increasingly used, reflecting the need to expand the donor pool. In this dynamic field, ongoing monitoring of long-term outcomes and trends remains critical to ensuring the effectiveness and sustainability of LT programmes.

Finland established its LT programme in 1982, becoming the seventh country in Europe to do so. With a population of 5.5 million, all solid organ transplants in Finland are centralised at Helsinki University Hospital. This centralised structure offers several advantages, including standardised patient management protocols, equitable access to transplantation and uniform data collection. Since the start of the programme, the Finnish LT centre has maintained a comprehensive nationwide LT registry encompassing detailed baseline characteristics, survival outcomes and a range of complications, making it a valuable resource for evaluating long-term outcomes and identifying trends.

Despite the availability of these robust data, comprehensive and up-to-date reports on long-term outcomes and recent trends from the Finnish LT registry have yet to be published. Furthermore, recent international efforts to define benchmark outcome values—representing the best achievable results in ideal cases—have facilitated comparisons among transplant centres.6 However, no benchmarking analysis has yet been conducted for the Finnish LT programme.

In this study, we analyse the results, trends and evolution of more than 40 years of LT activity in Finland. We also report one-year survival rates using the recently introduced benchmark criteria, providing valuable insights into the programme’s performance in comparison to other centres.

Material and methods

This study is based on data from the nationwide and statutory Finnish LT registry, which has recorded all liver transplants performed in Finland since the start of the liver transplant programme in 1982. All liver transplants in Finland are performed exclusively at Helsinki University Hospital. Adult transplants are performed at the transplantation and liver surgery unit, and paediatric transplants at the paediatric transplantation unit.

After the initial post-transplant hospitalisation, patients follow a structured follow-up programme. For adults, this includes transplant clinic consultations at 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, 9 months, 12 months, 18 months and every 1–3 years thereafter for life. In addition, a more frequent follow-up occurs at local secondary or tertiary-care gastroenterological clinics up to once weekly in the beginning and every 3–4 months in the long term. The transplant clinic is consulted for liver-related complications. For example, all liver biopsies and treatments for rejection are performed at the transplant unit. Paediatric patients are followed even more frequently at the paediatric transplant unit in the beginning and once annually in the long term throughout childhood and adolescence until transfer to adult care.

The Finnish LT registry contains comprehensive clinical and follow-up data for all LT recipients in Finland, with only 0.7% of patients lost to follow-up (mainly due to emigration). Follow-up data are collected from scheduled outpatient clinic visits and hospitalisations at either local hospitals or the transplant unit. Local hospitals are requested to send patient records to the transplant clinic at least annually and whenever a patient who had LT is hospitalised. The data are entered into the registry by a transplant coordinator at the time of listing for LT, at transplantation, annually thereafter, and following predefined events such as rejection, need for kidney replacement therapy or death. Only a specialised team of transplant coordinators has entered the follow-up data into the LT registry during our more than 40 years of experience, ensuring uniform data recording. Formal quality control of the registry has demonstrated a completeness rate of 99.5% and a consistency rate of 99.3%.7

In this study, we included all patients who underwent LT in Finland between 1982 and 31 December 2024, without exclusions. Follow-up censoring was at 31 December 2024. All liver grafts were from donors after brain death. All patients received calcineurin inhibitor-based initial immunosuppression. Cyclosporine was the preferred choice for many years, while tacrolimus has gradually gained prominence since 2000. As of 2022, tacrolimus has become the standard of care. Calcineurin inhibitors have been routinely combined with an antimetabolite, mainly azathioprine until 2006, after which mycophenolate mofetil became the primary choice for adults. Steroids were part of the standard immunosuppressive regimen for adults until June 2022, when a steroid-free protocol was introduced, except for patients with autoimmune hepatitis, if needed for the treatment of other conditions, or retransplantation due to chronic rejection.

Outcomes of the current study were patient survival (time from LT to death, censored at the end of follow-up), graft survival (time from LT to re-LT or death, censored at the end of follow-up), acute rejection episodes within the first post-transplant year and need for kidney-replacement therapy within the first post-transplant year. All rejections were biopsy-confirmed.

Statistical analysis

We analysed numbers of transplants annually and the changes in key characteristics over calendar periods. Temporal trends were tested with linear regression for continuous variables and the Cochran–Armitage test for categorical variables. We plotted trends in numbers of transplants over calendar year by aetiology of liver disease using locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS). Survival estimates were calculated using Kaplan–Meier methodology and differences in survival tested with the log-rank test. The time-dependent probabilities of retransplantation were estimated using the cumulative incidence function considering death without retransplantation as a competing-risk event. The effect of recipient age on survival was analysed for LTs after the year 2000, because mostly young patients were transplanted during the 1980s and 1990s. Additionally, we analysed one-year patient and graft survival among patients meeting benchmark criteria previously established by a consensus group6 to evaluate outcomes against best achievable international standards, providing a reference for quality assessment and improvement. Subgroup analyses were performed within the two largest aetiology groups at our centre, namely primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) and ArLD, regardless of liver cancer status. To study the association between acute rejection occurring within the first 6 months and more than 6 month patient and graft survival, we used Cox regression analysis adjusted for age and sex. Separate multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed for patient death, graft loss and retransplantation with prespecified covariates and with time period as a stratification variable. Analyses used complete-case data without imputation. Proportional hazards assumptions of Cox regression models were tested with Schoenfeld residuals and showed no violations. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p value of <0.05. Data were analysed using R software (V. 4.0.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Between 1982 and 2024, a total of 1763 LTs were performed in Finland, including 166 retransplantations (online supplemental figure 1). The mean age at first transplantation was 46 years (standard deviation (SD), 19), and 51% (n=821) of recipients were male. The mean body mass index (BMI) at the time of first transplantation was 26 kg/m² (SD, 18), and 17% (n=269) of recipients had diabetes. The mean Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score at first LT was 19 (SD, 9). The three most common underlying aetiologies during the study period were ArLD, PSC and acute liver failure (of various causes), while 18% of recipients had liver cancer.

Over time, several significant trends were observed (table 1). There was a gradual increase in the mean age of both recipients and donors (table 1 and online supplemental figure 2), along with rising BMI and a higher prevalence of recipient diabetes. The proportion of male recipients also increased. In terms of aetiologies, there was a proportional decline in acute liver failure and primary biliary cholangitis, while the proportions of ArLD, MASLD and liver cancer increased. Waiting times for transplantation lengthened from an average of 41 days in the 1980s to 65 days in the most recent period. Furthermore, analysis of annual numbers of first transplants showed an increasing trend for ArLD, liver cancer and PSC, as depicted in figures1 2; this trend was observed for both men and women (online supplemental figures 3 and 4).

Patient survival rates were 92% at one year, 83% at 5 years, 73% at 10 years, 53% at 20 years and 28% at 30 years (figure 3). Corresponding graft survival rates were 89%, 79%, 68%, 47% and 23%, respectively. Substantial improvements in short- and mid-term survival rates were observed during the 1980s and 1990s, whereas rates became stagnant, although high, after the year 2000 (table 2). By aetiology, the highest 10-year patient survival rates were observed in polycystic liver disease, PSC and autoimmune hepatitis, whereas MASLD exhibited the lowest survival rates (table 2). Regarding 10-year graft survival, the highest rates were in polycystic liver disease and autoimmune hepatitis, and the lowest in MASLD (online supplemental table 1).

The variation in survival outcomes by recipient age is shown in figure 3, which depicts patient and graft survival for transplants performed between 2000 and 2024. For patients aged ≥70 years at the time of transplantation, the 5-year patient survival rate was 58% (95% CI, 34 to 81).

Re-LT probabilities by competing-risk analysis were 3% at one year, 6% at 5 years, 8% at 10 years, 11% at 20 years and 13% at 30 years (table 2). Temporal decreases in the need for re-LT from the 1980s and 1990s to the most recent time periods were observed overall (online supplemental table 1). Among aetiologies, PSC had the highest need for re-LT (18% at 20 years and 28% at 30 years) (table 2).

Multivariable analyses

In multivariable Cox regression analyses, higher recipient and donor age and higher MELD score were associated with increased risk of both patient death and graft loss, while tacrolimus use was protective (online supplemental table 2). Additionally, viral aetiology predicted death, and PSC predicted graft loss. Retransplantation was more likely in patients with PSC, diabetes, and older donor age, while tacrolimus use and higher recipient age were associated with lower retransplantation rates (online supplemental table 2).

Benchmark cases

Between 2000 and 2024, benchmark cases accounted for 42% of all first LTs performed (545/1284). The one-year patient survival rate for these cases was 97%, while the corresponding graft survival rate was 96% (online supplemental table 3). These outcomes remained similar across the study period and between the two major indication groups, PSC and ArLD (online supplemental table 3).

Acute rejections

The frequency of acute rejection episodes within the first year after LT decreased considerably, from 62% during 1980–1989 to 19% during 2022–2023 (figure 5). Similar reductions were observed in recipients with PSC or ArLD (online supplemental figure 5). By Cox regression analysis adjusted for age and sex, patient survival beyond 6 months among patients with an acute rejection episode within 6 months post-transplant did not significantly differ from that of patients those without such rejection (HR, 1.02; 95% CI, 0.84 to 1.23; p=0.85). The same applied to graft survival (HR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.88 to 1.26; p=0.55), and when results were similar assessed separately in PSC and ArLD (data not shown).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive insight into the evolution and trends in LT activity in Finland. We found that the 30-year patient survival rate since the programme’s inception was 28%. Considerable improvements in midterm survival were observed up to the year 2000, driven largely by advancements in one-year survival rates. The study also highlights the steady increase in LT activity in Finland over time, reflecting not only improved survival outcomes but also notable demographic and aetiological shifts. Both recipient and donor ages have risen progressively, and indications for LT due to ArLD and liver cancer have increased. These trends are consistent with data from both Europe and the USA.3 5

A distinctive feature of Finland, also observed in other Nordic countries, is the high rate of LT for PSC, reflecting the regional prevalence of this disease.8 In contrast, we have very low rates of LT for hepatitis C virus (HCV)-related liver disease, consistent with the low prevalence of HCV in the general population in Finland.

Benchmark cases accounted for 42% of all first LTs in Finland during the last 25 years, aligning with the range of 8–49% reported by Müller and colleagues in their multicentre study defining benchmark outcome values.6 Müller et al proposed benchmark thresholds of ≤9% for one-year mortality and ≤11% for one-year graft loss.6 The one-year patient and graft survival rates observed in our study translate into less than 5% for one-year mortality and less than 6% for one-year graft loss across time periods and main aetiologies, placing them well within the benchmark values outlined by Müller et al. These findings underscore the high-quality outcomes consistently achieved by the Finnish LT programme.

Of the recipients of the first LT, 13% needed a second LT during the 30-year follow-up period due to various retransplant indications. The retransplant rate was particularly high in PSC, reaching 28% at 30 years. Non-anastomotic biliary strictures and recurrent PSC are major causes for retransplantation in PSC,9 which highlights the urgent need for disease-modifying interventions to reduce this burden.

One key finding in our study was the marked reduction in acute rejection rates over time, achieved despite progressively lower target calcineurin inhibitor levels and reduced steroid use. Notably, even among patients with PSC, a group traditionally considered at high risk for rejection, rejection rates demonstrated clear declines. This improvement may be attributed to the gradual shift from cyclosporine to the more potent tacrolimus. Furthermore, the introduction of universal basiliximab induction therapy in Helsinki in June 2022, combined with a delayed-onset, reduced-dose tacrolimus regimen without steroids, may have further contributed to the decreasing rejection rates during the most recent years.

Another important trend observed was the reduced need for post-transplant kidney-replacement therapy recently. This need had initially increased after 2008, likely reflecting the increasing age of recipients and donors and the increasing prevalence of ArLD and diabetes, all of which are risk factors for perioperative kidney injury.10 However, the adoption of the new immunosuppression protocol in 2022 coincided with a marked decline in the need for kidney-replacement therapy, which is in line with findings from a randomised controlled trial demonstrating renal protective effects of calcineurin inhibitor minimisation strategies.11

Although patients with MASLD showed poorer survival than other aetiologies, these patients are often older and have an accumulation of comorbidity. Also, there were relatively few patients with MASLD and follow-up was shorter. MASLD did not reach statistical significance as an independent predictor of poorer outcomes in the multivariable analyses.

Beyond survival, LT in Finland has been shown to improve patients’ health-related quality of life, achieving levels comparable to the general population.12 Furthermore, 44% of working-aged patients transplanted in Finland between 1982 and 2007 successfully resumed work (more than 70% among those aged less than 40 years),12 highlighting the broader societal and economic benefits of LT. The cost-effectiveness of LT has also been demonstrated in the Finnish context, with the cost per quality-adjusted life year estimated at €44 854 at 5 years in a 2011 study.13 This underscores the value of LT not only as a life-saving intervention but also as a highly cost-effective treatment option that brings substantial improvement in quality of life and productivity.14 15

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include the use of a comprehensive and validated nationwide LT registry, achieved by consistent and high-quality data recording. The follow-up data cover up to 40 years with only 0.7% of patients lost to follow-up.

Given certain characteristics specific to Finland, such as the epidemiology of liver diseases and the centralised nature of LT services, findings from Finland may not be completely transferable to other countries and healthcare settings. Other potential limitations include survival bias, which may arise from differences in the length of follow-up duration; classification bias due to changes in disease definitions over time such as the transition from non-alcoholic fatty liver disease to MASLD; and the lack of sex-specific subgroup analyses, given the known differences in disease distribution between men and women, which highlights the need for future studies to explore these issues in greater detail.

In conclusion, the four-decade Finnish experience reinforces LT as an excellent treatment option for a broad range of liver diseases. LT not only offers excellent long-term survival rates but also restores quality of life and supports societal reintegration for transplant recipients. These findings underscore the critical importance of maintaining and advancing LT programmes as part of a comprehensive approach to managing liver disease.
