Design of an imidazole-derived chemosensor for selective recognition of Th4+ and UO22+ in environmental systems
Authors: Naveen Murugan, Prakash Seenu, Moumitha Govindarajan Vijayalakshmi, Santhosh Iyappan, Sanjay Mohan, Gangha Bojja Lakshmipathy, Gnanesh Palani, Sathiyanarayanan Kulathu Iyer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The present study reports the synthesis and characterization of 2-(4,5-diphenyl-1H-imidazole-2-yl)-3-methoxyaniline (GSNH), a new imidazole-based chemosensor, confirmed by NMR and HR-MS spectra. This chemosensor exhibited emission at 400 nm, which is red-shifted to 450 nm with enhanced emission on adding Th4+ and UO22+ ions. Most importantly, this sensing event reverted to its initial emission with EDTA treatment, making the sensing reversible. The sensing phenomenon has been explored based on Job plot analysis, mass spectrometry studies, DFT studies, NMR titration studies, and FT-IR spectroscopy, which demonstrated a clear 1 : 1 complex formation involving methoxy oxygen and nitrogen binding with metal ions. The chemosensor has higher sensitivity with detection thresholds of 1.49 nM and 3.48 nM for Th4+ and UO22+ ions, respectively, and a response time of less than 1 second. Most importantly, GSNH has been able to detect Th4+ and UO22+ ions in spiked environmental water samples, suggesting its applicability to act as an efficient analytical tool for monitoring thorium and uranyl ions in environmental samples.

Introduction

Fluorescent chemosensors have seen rapid proliferation across diverse industries and fields. Examples of these fields are as follows: Chemical Industry (Chemistry), Clinical Biology, and Environmental Sciences. Detection methods have to be precise for all of the aforementioned fields. The scientific community is especially excited about using fluorescence-based sensors for many reasons, such as ease of use, their ability to detect specific substances at very low concentrations and quick results in addition to the advantage that they are visible to the naked eye when illuminated by UV light, making them usable for a wide range of purposes.1–7 The element thorium has an important role to play in current agricultural and medical practices, nuclear power generation and aerospace.8–11 Since thorium is extremely dangerous when human beings are exposed to it, it has become very important to develop simple and effective methods to analyze the presence of Th4+ ions in the environment. Like uranium, thorium may also be found in deposits located in natural sources such as water, rocks and soil. For this reason, monitoring must be conducted to ensure the safety of individuals who may come into contact with this element. Furthermore, long-term exposure to thorium can lead to adverse health conditions, including lung disorders, pancreatic and liver dysfunction, and bone cancer.12–16 Multiple methods have been developed to measure the concentration of both environmental and human exposure to metals including thorium.17–19 using inductively coupled plasma/mass spectrometry (ICP-MS).20 nuclear methods,21 ion chromatography techniques,22 electrochemical methods,23 capillary zone electrophoresis,24 gamma-ray spectrometry techniques,25 cation exchange resin techniques,26 scintillation detection and other types of extraction methods.27

The major benefit of fluorescence systems for competitive analysis is the quickness (i.e., less than 1 second) in their detection processes when compared to other systems. Also, they can provide very sensitive detection at high sensitivity (ultra-fast) while still having an excellent level of specificity for the substances being detected.28 Multiple thorium sensitive probes for detecting thorium ions have been developed, using many different chemical classes, including thorin,29 phenanthridine,30–32 and naphthol.33 Numerous commercially available fluorescent compounds intended for the detection of Th4+ ions have limitations associated with solubility in aqueous solutions, showing low sensitivity readings, poor “turn off” response time, small Stokes shift, high value of detection limits, and low sensitivity due to interference from cations. It becomes imperative for researchers to initiate novel sensors to detect Th4+.

Uranium, a naturally radioactive element, is distinguished due to its widespread use in sectors that include nuclear arms and the nuclear energy sector, especially due to the remarkable rise in the consumption of nuclear energy globally that has been witnessed in recent years.34–36 The problem that exists concerning the disposal and conservation of radioactive materials, especially uranium, tends to pose a grave risk to humans and animals, being contacted with, in addition to grave concerns about the consequent impact on environmental health due to possible contaminations.37–39 Because of the health hazards of uranium ion, the development of technology to safely dispose is considered necessary to address this issue. Uranium is known to exist in various oxidation levels, although the major ion formed naturally is the uranyl ion, which is represented by the symbol UO22+ and is considered to be highly soluble in water with a strong affinity to move in the atmosphere.40–43 It is generally believed that uranium compounds exert high toxicity, further coupled with radioactivity, induce long-term damage to integumentary organs, the nervous system, immune system, neurological cells, and hematopoietic system. Potential effects include DNA impairment, kidney damage, and disruption of the reproductive system.44–47 In this regard, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency established a very low threshold of 1.3 × 10−7 M as the permissible concentration of uranium in drinking water, hence close monitoring becomes highly important.48

Numerous analytical techniques have been utilized in the detection of UO22+ ions using instrumental methods, some of which include surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy,49 electron-coupled plasma mass spectrometry,50 neutron activation analysis,51 atomic emission spectrometry,52 and many forms of spectrophotometry techniques,53,54 but all these methods are generally associated with complicated procedures for their operation, the need for expensive and highly advanced equipment, and the need for highly skilled professionals to carry out the process. Consequently, there has been increasing demand for techniques using fluorescence, which have the advantage and potential to ensure rapid detection and accurate assessment for the detection of UO22+ ions.55 In this pursuit for innovation, there has been considerable investigation into a variety of organic small molecules, polymer materials, biological molecules, and nanomaterials for use in creating fluorescence sensors for uranyl ions.56–58 Unfortunately, despite the advantages, research in the use of organic small-molecule fluorescence sensors exclusively designed for uranyl ion detection is sparse.59,60

A very sensitive Ru3+, Ir3+, Th4+, and UO22+ ion-selective quinazolinone-based chemosensor was reported from our laboratory.61 Amongst the tetraphenylethene (TPE) based chemosensor compounds for sensing UO22+, TPE is found to be the most exceptional molecule due to the interest it has drawn for its unexpectedly impressive AIE characteristics, ease of synthesis and excellent luminous properties.62,63 In 2019, Lin et al. developed for the first time a fluorescence sensor holding AIE requirements for uranyl ions UO22+ sensing, in the tetraphenylethene (TPE) molecule. The TPE molecule is mainly depicted by “off” emission properties, making it exceptionally effective for the identification of uranyl ions within ambient solution environments.64 Despite the novel design and functionality offered by these fluorescence sensors, it should be noted that they suffer from one or other disadvantage.

In this study, we report the design and development of an imidazole-based chemosensor, 2-(4,5-diphenyl-1H-imidazole-2-yl)-3-methoxyaniline (GSNH), for the detection of radioactive actinide ions like thorium (Th4+) and uranyl (UO22+) ions with high sensitivity. The binding process is facilitated by the coordination of the methoxy oxygen and tertiary nitrogen atoms, which have been evidenced through NMR, HRMS, and DFT studies. The chemosensor GSNH shows remarkable sensitivity with detection limits of 1.49 nM (Th4+) and 3.48 nM (UO22+).

Experimental methods

Materials and method

The necessary chemicals were purchased in analytical reagent grade (AR), including solvents like acetonitrile (ACN), ethanol, and chloroform from Hyma, Sigma Aldrich, and Spectrochem. The metal salts of Ag+, Al3+, As3+, Ca2+, Cd2+, Cu2+, Co2+, Cr3+, Eu3+, Fe2+, Gd3+, Hg2+, Ir3+, Mg2+, Mn2+, Ni2+, Pb2+, Ru3+, Sm3+, Th4+, Zn2+, and uranyl nitrate (UO22+) were utilized without further purification. The Bruker-Avance III 400 MHz instrument was used to characterize the produced compounds utilizing 1H and 13C NMR (nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy). IR-Affinity-1 FT-IR spectrometer, Thermo Nicolet iS50, and Waters-USA-XEVO-G2-XS QTOF were used to record IR and HRMS spectra respectively. A JASCO v-670 instrument was used to measure the absorption from UV-vis spectra, and a Hitachi model F-7000 fluorescence spectrometer was used to record the FL emission investigations, and a Malvern MicroCal ITC was used to record the thermodynamic parameters.

Stock solution preparation

Sensor investigation: Acetonitrile/water (8 : 2), a spectrochemical-grade solvent mixture, was used to prepare the stock solution of sensor GSNH. Analytical grade water was used to prepare the various nitrate metal cations at a concentration of 1 × 10−3 M. Studies of absorption and emission were conducted using a sensing receptor (2 × 10−5 M) and several metal cations (1 × 10−3 M).

Limit of detection

To determine the detection limit of the sensor, a simple calculation was done. LoD = 3σ/slope. In the calculation, to determine the slope value, the linear plot of FL intensity versus the Th4+ and UO22+ ion concentrations was used, and σ denotes the standard deviation, which can be computed by running 10 replicate scans of the sensing receptor alone.

Preparation of real sample solution

The water samples collected around VIT University, including sources such as the lake, well, and drinking water, were filtered to remove external particles, adjusted pH using a buffer solution, and then spiked with metal ions.

Synthesis of 2-(2-methoxy-6-nitrophenyl)-4,5-diphenyl-1H-imidazole (GS)

1.0 g of benzil was added to acetic acid, 0.86 g of 2-methoxy-5-nitrobenzaldehyde, and 0.365 g of ammonium acetate were dispensed to the reaction mixture and refluxed at 120 °C for 6 hours. The emergence of a new spot in TLC verifies that GS has been formed. Following TLC observation of the reaction's completion, the reaction liquid containing flask was allowed to cool at room temperature before being diluted with distilled water. After neutralizing the mixture with HCl, ethyl acetate was added. Anhydrous Na2SO4 was added to the extracted organic layer to dry off the moisture. Column chromatography was used to purify the concentrated crude mixture after ethyl acetate was extracted under low pressure. The resulting product was a yellow colour solid, 90% yield, and melting point was 178–180 °C (Scheme 1). The chemical structure was determined by the 1H NMR, 13C NMR, FT-IR, and GC-MS, Fig. SI(1–4).

Result and discussion

Selectivity studies

The absorption spectrum of GSNH revealed a peak at 225 nm due to the methoxy group, another at 300 nm from the π–π* transition and another peak at 333 nm from the n–π* transition due to nitrogen. The UV-vis absorption selectivity studies were carried out on GSNH in ACN : H2O (8 : 2) in the presence of Ag+, Al3+, As3+, Ca2+, Cd2+, Co2+, Cr3+, Cu2+, Eu3+, Fe2+, Gd3+, Hg2+, Ir3+, Mg2+, Mn2+, Ni2+, Pb2+, Ru3+, Sm3+, Zn2+, Th4+ and UO22+ metal cations. It exhibited no discernible changes upon the addition of metal cations, except for Th4+ and UO22+, which resulted in a blue shift from 300 to 285 nm, Fig. 1(a). Similarly, in the emission studies, we fixed the excitation wavelength at 300 nm, the peak at 400 nm obtained for GSNH, remained during emission measurements for GSNH when other metal cations were added, except for Th4+ and UO22+, which induced a red shift to 450 nm. Fig. 1(b).

Absorption and emission titration

The absorption titration for GSNH with 0 to 6 equivalents of Th4+ produced peaks at 200 nm, 235 nm, 300 and 340 nm. The peak at 235 nm increased, while the peaks at 300 nm and 340 nm decreased on adding Th4+. The accompanying blue shift is from 300 to 285 nm. The peak at 340 nm also decreases. Hence, we got two isobestic points at 226 nm and 245 nm, Fig. 2(a). The absorption peaks at 200, 240, 300, and 340 nm were obtained in the absorption titration by adding 0 to 7 equivalents of the UO22+ ion. While the peak at 240 nm increases, the absorption maxima at 300 and 340 nm decrease with accompanying blue shift from 340 to 290 nm. Consequently, two isobestic points were found at 230 and 247 nm, Fig. 2(c). On performing the emission titration, GSNH exhibits a peak at 400 nm, and on adding 0–6 equivalents of Th4+ ions, the peak is shifted from 400 to 450 nm with increased emission intensity leading to a red shift. However, we got the isoemissive point at 395 nm, Fig. 2(b). Similarly, the peak was at 400 nm for GSNH and on adding 0–7 equivalents of UO22+ ions, the emission intensity rose in proportion to the UO22+ ion concentration, causing a red shift from 400 nm to 450 nm, Fig. 2(d), with an isoemissive point at 402 nm.

Binding mechanism

Following FL titration, the Benesi–Hildebrand graph was plotted to illustrate the correlation between 1/concentrations of Th4+ and UO22+ and 1/[F − F0]. By using the Benesi–Hildebrand equation, the association constant (Ka) between chemosensor GSNH and Th4+ and UO22+ metal cations were determined to be 1.54 × 105 M−1 and 1.27 × 106 M−1 for the GSNH + Th4+ and GSNH + UO22+ complexes, respectively, Fig. 3(a and b), as well as the Isothermal Titration Calorimetry (ITC) results of both complexes (1.00 × 1012 M−1 for both Th4+ and UO22+), demonstrate the strong binding affinity between GSNH and Th4+ and UO22+, the results are given in SI Table-1. The Limits of Detection (LoD) of GSNH towards Th4+ and UO22+ metal ions was calculated from the straight line obtained from the graph between fluorescence intensity and concentration using the formula 3σ/slope, where σ is the standard deviation obtained from ten measurements. For Th4+, the result was 1.49 nM while for UO22+, it was 3.48 nM, Fig. 3(c and d). The 1H NMR titration experiment in DMSO-d6 was used to determine the binding interactions between GSNH and Th4+/UO22+ analytes. The methoxy peak shifted to upfield for both complexes, Fig. 3(e and f), indicating the attachment of metal ions to the oxygen atom of methoxy group. The GSNH, GSNH + Th4+ and GSNH + UO22+ complexes' HR-MS spectra were recorded. The exact mass with a proton adduct for GSNH was 342.1608, and the obtained mass was 342.1632, whereas the exact mass for GSNH + Th4+ was 821.1410, and the obtained mass was 821.1425. Likewise, the exact mass for GSNH + UO22+ was 735.1680, and the obtained mass was 735.1685. Furthermore, the HR-MS analysis of the complex verified the GSNH binding modes with Th4+ and UO22+, Fig. SI(8–10), indicating the interaction among the metal ions, the oxygen atom of methoxy group and the tertiary nitrogen (Scheme 2).

Interference, Job's plot, and time response

In the interference experiments, the GSNH was used with various metal cations like Ag+, Al3+, As3+, Ca2+, Cd2+, Co2+, Cr3+, Cu2+, Eu3+, Fe2+, Gd3+, Hg2+, Ir3+, Mg2+, Mn2+, Ni2+, Pb2+, Ru3+, Sm3+, Zn2+, Th4+ and UO22+. Except for Th4+ and UO22+, the emission spectra remained unchanged. Even when other metal cations were present, the emission intensity in presence of Th4+ and UO22+ increased. This demonstrates the chemosensor GSNH's selectivity for Th4+ and UO22+, Fig. 4(a and b). The binding ratio of the analyte to the chemosensor GSNH could be found using the Job's plot analysis. For these two metal ions, Th4+ and UO22+, the binding stoichiometric ratio appears to be 1 : 1, based on the maximum emission intensity at 0.5 mole fraction, Fig. 4(c and d). Furthermore, as the complexes GSNH + UO22+ and GSNH + Th4+ were formed, the emission intensity rose instantly, in a fraction of a second, when Th4+ and UO22+ were added to GSNH, Fig. 4(e and f).

Reversibility studies and pH studies

EDTA was added to GSNH with Th4+ and UO22+ complexes to assess the reversibility of sensor GSNH. The addition of Th4+ and UO22+ ions to GSNH caused the colour shift from blue to cyan for Th4+ and UO22+, as well as an increase in emission intensity. When EDTA was added, the emission intensity dropped to that of GSNH, and the GSNH was regenerated. This experiment was conducted four times. The fluorescence intensity was high before the addition of EDTA, but it decreased as a result of the formation of the EDTA + Th4+ and UO22+ complexes, which resulted in simultaneous regeneration of GSNH. The chemosensor GSNH was then recovered, and over the course of four cycles, this phenomenon was routinely checked for reproducibility, Fig. 5(a and b). There was a discernible shift in the pH range of 4.0 to 12.0 in the emission spectrum of the chemosensor GSNH. Because the supply of H+ decreased from pH 2 to pH 7, the emission intensity was high. Then, because of a drop in the H+ concentration, the emission intensity drastically decreased at pH 8 for the probe (GSNH) as well as all of the complexes. After 8, there is a slight variation in the emission intensity with pH. For two complexes, the emission intensity was found to be nearly constant between pH values of 8 and 12, Fig. 5(c and d).

Theoretical studies

The Density Functional Theory (DFT) and time-dependent studies (TD-DFT) were used to calculate the energy of the optimised structures of GSNH and GSNH with Th4+ and UO22+ complexes using the Gaussian 16 W package and theoretical calculations at the 6-31′G, ++, d and p, and SDD for Th4+ and UO22+ with (B3LYP) levels of theoretical analysis. The electron density in GSNH was distributed nearly evenly throughout the molecule in HOMO. The imidazole exhibits a wider electron density spread than all regions when compared to LUMO. ΔE, the band gap, is 1.44 eV. The electron density is higher across the group on adding the UO22+ metal in the HOMO in the GSNH + UO22+ complex. The electron density spread was more pronounced in and around the metal in LUMO. But the energy gap, ΔE, increased by 3.0637 eV. The same was happening in the case of GSNH + Th4+ complex. The band gap is increased by 3.5933 eV. The calculated increase in the HOMO–LUMO energy gap upon complexation of GSNH with Th4+ and UO22+ correlates directly to the experimentally observed hypsochromic (blue) shift in the absorption spectrum. This widening of ΔE reflects stabilization of the HOMO and lowering of the LUMO, confirming that actinide binding rigidifies the electronic structure and alters the excitation pathway. The computational results thus provide a clear rationale for the spectroscopic changes and strengthen the mechanistic interpretation of selective detection of thorium and uranium ions, which is reflecting the system is not influenced on stability by the formation of complexes of GSNH with Th4+ and UO22+, as shown in Fig. 6.

Application of real sample analysis

Water is polluted due to the presence of Th4+ and UO22+ ions, which is harmful to humans and living beings. Therefore, it is crucial to test the water samples for the presence of Th4+ and UO22+. The detection was carried out with different water samples in the Vellore Institute of Technology (VIT, Vellore), including lake water, well water, and drinking water. We prepared two different concentrations of 50 µM and 100 µM, respectively, by spiking Th4+ and UO22+ ions into the water. These spiked water samples were added to the solution of GSNH, and we secured a recovery rate of these metals between 99.1 and 99.7%. The recovery values are given in Table 1. Therefore, the results demonstrate that GSNH was very useful for the accurate detection of Th4+ and UO22+ ions.

In comparison, the GSNH sensor is able to obtain very high sensitivities at 3.48 nM for UO22+ and 1.49 nM for Th4+, alongside an ultrarapid response time of only one second and reversibility after four cycles. This, along with other aspects of this sensor, makes GSNH a suitable choice for use in monitoring radioactive actinides, the comparison study is given in SI Table 2. Its capability to detect ions by visual observation using fluorescence increases its suitability as a sensor.

Conclusions

We have synthesized and characterized GSNH, a novel imidazole chemosensor, using NMR and HR-MS analytical techniques. Th4+ and UO22+ enhance the emission intensity at 450 nm, accompanied by a redshift. The chemosensor GSNH can be rendered reversible with the use of EDTA. The emission intensity initially increased due to Th4+ and UO22+, then was restored to that of GSNH by the addition of EDTA to GSNH. Subsequently, we investigated the binding mechanism by employing Job's plot, mass spectrometry, DFT calculations, NMR titration, and FT-IR spectroscopy. A 1 : 1 complex was formed with coordination to methoxy oxygen and nitrogen atoms of GSNH with Th4+ and UO22+. The detection limits were 1.49 nM for Th4+ and 3.48 nM for UO22+, with a response time of under one second, and additionally, GSNH accurately detected the Th4+ and UO22+ ions in spiked environmental water samples. This study emphasises its usefulness for monitoring toxic and radioactive actinide contaminants.
