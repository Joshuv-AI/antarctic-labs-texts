Long-term patency of aortoiliac stenting with or without simultaneous infrainguinal revascularization: a propensity score-matched 10-year retrospective cohort study
Authors: Sungsin Cho, Jin Hyun Joh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study investigated the long-term impact of simultaneous infrainguinal revascularization on the primary patency rate (PPR) of aortoiliac artery stenting (AIS) for aortoiliac occlusive disease.MethodsA retrospective analysis of 334 consecutive AIS patients (2009–2024) was conducted. Patients were categorized into 2 groups: AIS with simultaneous infrainguinal treatment (Tx (+), n = 114) and AIS alone (Tx (−), n = 220). Baseline and lesion characteristics were compared. The primary outcome was 10-year PPR, analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier methods and log-rank tests, both before and after 1:1 propensity score matching for clinical and anatomical risk factors.ResultsThe Tx (+) group demonstrated a higher disease burden, including older age, lower body mass index, adverse lab findings, more severe calcification (P = 0.013), and greater external iliac artery involvement (P < 0.001). The overall PPR was excellent, stabilizing at 85.3% over 7 to 10 years. Before propensity matching, the Tx (−) group showed significantly superior patency (P = 0.004). However, after 1:1 propensity score matching, the difference in PPR between the Tx (+) and Tx (−) groups was no longer statistically significant (P = 0.093). Stent type did not significantly affect PPR (P = 0.220).ConclusionAortoiliac stenting offers durable long-term primary patency over 10 years. Although patients requiring combined infrainguinal revascularization have higher clinical and anatomical risks, simultaneous infrainguinal treatment does not independently compromise the long-term patency of the upstream aortoiliac stent. These findings support comprehensive revascularization to optimize limb perfusion without negatively impacting the iliac stent’s long-term performance.Keywords: Atherosclerosis, Arterial occlusive diseases, Aortic diseases, Iliac artery, Vascular patency, Stents, Survival

INTRODUCTION

Atherosclerotic lesions in patients with symptomatic limb ischemia are diffuse and multilevel, involving both the aortoiliac inflow and infrainguinal outflow vessels. These lesions can lead to disabling claudication or critical limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI). Therapeutic options for such multilevel disease include endovascular, surgical, or hybrid revascularization [1,2].

For aortoiliac lesions, surgical revascularization such as bypass grafting or endarterectomy has historically yielded durable outcomes [3]. However, endovascular therapy has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative offering comparable symptom relief with lower procedural morbidity. In cases of localized aortoiliac occlusive disease (AIOD), aortoiliac artery stenting (AIS) has become the treatment of choice, demonstrating excellent long-term patency outcomes [4,5].

Poor distal runoff is a well-known predictor of failure after peripheral revascularization. Because the infrainguinal segment serves as the outflow pathway for aortoiliac interventions, combined treatment of inflow and outflow lesions may be particularly important in patients with CLTI. However, simultaneous interventions may carry increased procedural risk, especially in clinically fragile patients [6].

Although combined aortoiliac and infrainguinal revascularization may improve outcomes, limited data exist comparing long-term patency between AIS performed with simultaneous infrainguinal treatment and AIS performed alone. Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate the impact of concomitant infrainguinal intervention on long-term aortoiliac stent patency.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Kyung Hee University Hospital at Gangdong (KHNMC-2022-06-043). The IRB waived the need for informed consent because of a lack of information on the participant’s identity. This study complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design

From the prospectively collected database of peripheral arterial revascularization from 2009 to 2024, consecutive patients who underwent AIS due to AIOD were selected for this study. We included patients who underwent aortic stenting and/or unilateral or bilateral iliac stenting and had at least 1 visit to the outpatient clinic following the procedure. Exclusion criteria were that patients with a history of previous stenting in the ipsilateral iliac artery, patients without preoperative CT angiography, patients with balloon angioplasty only, and patients with inadequate or missing medical records. For the evaluation of impact of the infrainguinal treatment on the AIS patency, we divided the patents with 2 groups including AIS with infrainguinal treatment (Tx (+)) and AIS without infrainguinal treatment (Tx (−)).

Variables

Baseline characteristics, including demographic variables (age, sex, and body mass index [BMI]) and comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), chronic kidney disease, and smoking status, were collected. The preoperative laboratory findings were evaluated. Anatomical lesion severity was classified according to the TransAtlantic Inter-Society Consensus (TASC) classification. Each lesion was classified as a stenotic or occlusive lesion. The calcification grade was classified according to the involvement degree of circumference.

Procedure

All procedures were performed at the hybrid operating suite in a single center by vascular surgeons. Procedures were performed after the femoral artery access under ultrasound guidance using 7 to 8 French standard sheaths. Intravenous heparin was given with doses up to 3,000 IU, and digital subtraction angiography was performed. If the procedure time took more than 1 hour, additional heparin 1,000 units was added every 1 hour, and protamine reversal was not performed after the procedure. Both balloon-expandable and self-expandable stents were used as per the surgeons’ discretion with regard to the artery being treated. A balloon-expandable stent was preferentially used to the aortic and common iliac arterial lesions. And external iliac arterial lesions and long abdominal aortic lesions were preferentially treated with a self-expandable stent. Covered stent (CS) was used to the shaggy aortic or severely calcified lesions. In addition, CS was used for the bailout procedure after an iliac artery rupture during the procedure. For the combined treatment of infrainguinal lesions, we performed infrainguinal procedures followed by iliac procedures. If hybrid revascularization was required, endarterectomy or infrainguinal bypass was performed through standard inguinal cut-down. Aortic or iliac artery stenting was performed by inserting a sheath just before arteriotomy completion. Arterial sheaths were removed immediately after the procedures.

Post-procedural medication protocol

All patients were prescribed a standardized antithrombotic regimen after the procedure. Dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin 100 mg/day and clopidogrel 75 mg/day) was administered for at least 3 months, followed by lifelong single antiplatelet therapy, usually aspirin. Patients with additional indications for anticoagulation (e.g., atrial fibrillation) received warfarin or a direct oral anticoagulant at the discretion of the treating physician.

Outcome measures

Procedural success was defined as the successful revascularization of the target lesion with ≤30% residual stenosis and without complications. Patients were evaluated at 1, 3 months, and every 6 months for 2 years, and then every 1 year thereafter. Physical examination was checked at follow-up, and an ankle-brachial index was performed before discharge and every year thereafter. Patency was defined as uninterrupted blood flow without occlusion and with less than 50% stenosis within 5 mm proximal or distal to the target lesion, as confirmed by duplex ultrasonography, CT angiography, or contrast angiography during follow-up. For duplex ultrasound evaluation, a peak systolic velocity ratio >2.4 was used to define hemodynamically significant stenosis. We evaluated primary patency rate (PPR) comparing the 2 groups.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were predefined. Continuous variables were compared using the t-test or Mann-Whitney U-test as appropriate, and categorical variables by the chi-square test. The Kaplan-Meier curves were constructed for primary patency, with comparisons made using the log-rank test. The propensity score was estimated for each patient using a multivariable logistic regression model. The group assignment (Tx (+) vs. Tx (−)) was used as the dependent variable, regressed on the predefined baseline covariates. Covariates included in the matching model were selected based on clinical relevance and observed baseline imbalances including baseline patients’ characteristics, laboratory findings, lesion characteristics, and procedure details. A 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching algorithm without replacement was employed using a caliper of 0.2. Findings were considered significant if the P-value was less than 0.05. Medcalc software ver. 20.110 (MedCalc Software) was used for Kaplan-Meier methods, and all other statistical tests were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows ver. 22.0 (IBM Corp.).

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

A total of 334 patients were analyzed in this study. Among them, 283 (84.7%) were male. The mean age was 69.0 ± 9.9 years. Patients in the Tx (+) group were notably older (72.3 ± 9.4 years) compared to the Tx (−) group (67.3 ± 9.8 years), a finding that was highly statistically significant (P < 0.001). A significant difference was also noted in patient body habitus, with the Tx (+) group having a lower BMI (22.8 ± 3.3 kg/m2) compared to the Tx (−) group (23.6 ± 3.3 kg/m2), which was statistically significant (P = 0.017). Regarding comorbidities, the prevalence of hypertension was significantly higher in the Tx (+) group (84.2%) compared to the Tx (−) group (70.9%) (P = 0.007). However, there were no significant differences found in the prevalence of other major comorbidities, including diabetes (49.4% overall), coronary artery disease (19.5% overall), cerebrovascular disease (19.8% overall), COPD (4.2% overall), dyslipidemia (16.2% overall), or end-stage renal disease (7.8% overall). Smoking status (never smoker, former smoker, current smoker) also showed no significant difference between the 2 groups (P = 0.657). Key laboratory findings further distinguished the groups. The Tx (+) cohort presented with significantly lower hemoglobin levels compared to the Tx (−) group (12.2 ± 2.1 g/dL vs. 13.3 ± 2.1 g/dL, P < 0.001). Similarly, albumin levels were significantly lower in the Tx (+) group than in the Tx (−) group (4.0 ± 0.5 g/dL vs. 4.2 ± 0.4 g/dL, P = 0.009). Platelet count, PT, and aPTT did not show a statistically significant difference (Table 1).

Lesion characteristics

Table 2 demonstrated the lesion characteristics. The properties of the treated lesions varied significantly between the 2 cohorts. In terms of morphology, the Tx (+) group had a higher proportion of stenotic lesions (78.9%) and a lower proportion of occlusive lesions (21.1%) than the Tx (−) group (stenosis, 67.7%; occlusion, 32.3%), representing a significant difference (P = 0.031). The anatomical distribution of the lesions was highly disparate (P < 0.001). The Tx (−) group primarily involved the common iliac artery (CIA, 62.7%), whereas the Tx (+) group had a higher concentration of external iliac artery (EIA, 36.0% vs. 16.4%). Conversely, the CIA was involved in only 50.0% of the Tx (+) cases. The proportion of lesions involving both the CIA and EIA was lower in the Tx (+) group (14.0%) than in the Tx (−) group (19.1%). Lesion calcification also differed significantly (P = 0.013). A higher proportion of patients in the Tx (+) group exhibited severe calcification, specifically the highest grade (circumference 270°–360°), accounting for 39.5% of the group compared to 22.7% in the Tx (−) group. TASC classification, however, showed no significant difference between the groups (P = 0.837). The majority of patients in both groups presented with TASC D lesions (56.3% overall).

Procedure details

The detailed procedures are demonstrated in Table 3. Procedural metrics revealed notable differences in technical execution. The mean stent diameter used was significantly smaller in the Tx (+) group (8.8 ± 1.4 mm) compared to the Tx (−) group (9.1 ± 1.1 mm) (P = 0.011). The kissing stent technique was used less frequently in the Tx (+) group than in the Tx (−) group (14.0% vs. 30.0%, P = 0.001). Stent types did not differ significantly between the groups (P = 0.682). Procedural complications were rare, with iliac artery rupture occurring in 1.5% of the total cohort, showing no significant difference between the groups (P = 0.500). Finally, while both groups demonstrated substantial and equivalent clinical improvement, the preoperative and postoperative ABI showed no significant difference between the Tx (+) and Tx (−) cohorts in either the preoperative (0.3 ± 0.2 vs. 0.4 ± 0.3; P = 0.440) or postoperative (1.2 ± 0.3 for both; P = 0.410) measurements.

Primary patency rate

The overall PPR for the entire cohort was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method. The patency rate remained high in the short term, with a 99.4% one-year survival probability. This rate gradually declined to 93.7% in 3 years, 91.6% in 5 years, and stabilized at 85.3% from 7 to 10 years (Fig. 1). The PPRs were compared between the Tx (−) and Tx (+) groups (Fig. 2). Before propensity matching, the Tx (−) group demonstrated a significantly higher patency rate than the Tx (+) group over the 10-year follow-up period (P = 0.004). However, after 1:1 propensity matching, which adjusted for baseline differences, the survival curves converged, and the difference in patency rates between the 2 groups was no longer statistically significant (P = 0.093). Patency rates were also stratified by the type of stent used (Fig. 3). The Kaplan-Meier analysis showed no significant difference in PPRs among the 3 stent types over the follow-up period (P = 0.220). At 5 years, the patency rates were approximately 87.6% for self-expandable, 87.5% for balloon-expandable, and 83.3% for CSs.

Safety outcomes

The 30-day mortality was 4 of 334 (1.2%). No procedure-related deaths were observed. All mortalities occurred in the Tx (−) group. Three patients died of myocardial infarction, and 1 patient died due to infectious colitis. During the follow-up, the overall mortality was 27 of 334 (7.9%). Among them, 22 patients were included in Tx (−) group: 7 cardiac origin (6 myocardial infarction and 1 ventricular tachycardia), 4 cancers (2 pancreatic, 1 ampulla of Vater, and 1 lung), 2 pneumonia, 2 coronavirus disease 2019, 2 cerebrovascular accident, 1 infectious colitis, and 4 unknown. And 5 patients died in the Tx (+) group, 2 with myocardial infraction, 2 with pneumonia, and 1 with cholangiocarcinoma.

DISCUSSION

The present study investigated the long-term impact of simultaneous infrainguinal revascularization on the primary patency of AIS in a cohort of 334 patients with multilevel peripheral arterial disease. The principal finding was that AIS demonstrated excellent long-term durability, with patency stabilizing at 85.3% over 7 to 10 years for the entire cohort. Although the combined-treatment group (Tx (+)) initially exhibited a significantly lower patency rate than the aortoiliac-only group (Tx (−); P = 0.004), this difference was no longer present after 1:1 propensity score matching (P = 0.093). These results indicate that the initial disparity was attributable to the more complex clinical and anatomical risk profile in the combined-treatment group, rather than any adverse effect of infrainguinal intervention itself.

Baseline comparisons underscored the need for propensity matching. Patients in the Tx (+) group were older, had lower BMI, and demonstrated adverse laboratory markers such as reduced hemoglobin and albumin levels—indices associated with frailty, malnutrition, and poorer outcomes following peripheral revascularization [7,8,9]. Hypertension was also more prevalent (P = 0.007), reflecting the greater systemic atherosclerotic burden among patients requiring adjunctive infrainguinal treatment. These findings collectively support the concept that combined inflow and outflow disease represents a more advanced and clinically challenging form of CLTI, warranting more extensive revascularization strategies [10,11].

Differences in lesion characteristics further reflected the increased complexity of the Tx (+) cohort. These patients had a higher prevalence of stenotic rather than occlusive lesions, more EIA involvement (36.0% vs. 16.4%, P < 0.001), and substantially more severe arterial calcification (39.5% vs. 22.7%, P = 0.013). External iliac lesions and severe calcification pose greater technical difficulty and are established predictors of endovascular failure [12,13,14]. The higher burden of such lesions among Tx (+) patients reinforces the notion that infrainguinal intervention serves as a surrogate marker of anatomically advanced disease rather than a contributor to inferior iliac stent outcomes. The propensity-matched analysis accounted for these factors, eliminating differences in long-term patency.

Procedural distinctions between groups were also consistent with the higher complexity of disease in the Tx (+) cohort. The smaller mean stent diameter (8.8 ± 1.4 mm vs. 9.1 ± 1.1 mm, P = 0.011) and less frequent use of the kissing stent technique (14.0% vs. 30.0%, P = 0.001) reflect treatment of smaller-caliber or more distal iliac segments. Careful stent sizing is particularly important in segments distal to compromised outflow to avoid over-dilatation and adverse outcomes [15,16]. These anatomic and procedural features align with the paradigm that combined inflow and outflow revascularization is often necessary in a higher-risk patient subset.

The findings of this study contribute to the evolving understanding of inflow-versus-outflow revascularization strategies. Traditional surgical practice has favored restoration of inflow as the priority, particularly for claudication [17,18]. However, in CLTI, adequate runoff is critical for wound healing and limb salvage, making simultaneous inflow plus outflow reconstruction frequently necessary [19,20]. The present results support the safety of this approach by demonstrating that combined infrainguinal intervention does not compromise long-term AIS patency when baseline risks are adequately accounted for.

Another notable finding was the comparable patency across self-expandable, balloon-expandable, and CSs (P = 0.220). Stent selection in this cohort reflected lesion morphology—balloon-expandable stents for focal common iliac lesions, and self-expandable stents for external iliac or longer lesions—yet outcomes were similar. This consistency reinforces existing evidence supporting the reliability of contemporary iliac stent platforms regardless of implant type [21,22,23].

Although the strengths of this study include a relatively large cohort, long duration of follow-up, and the use of predefined analytic methods including propensity score matching, there are several limitations. Limitations include its retrospective design and potential residual confounding due to unmeasured variables such as detailed runoff scoring or frailty indices. Surgeon-directed treatment decisions may have introduced selection bias. Finally, the number of long-term events in the matched cohort limited statistical precision for late outcomes. Future prospective studies with detailed runoff quantification and frailty assessment may further clarify the independent contribution of downstream disease.

In conclusion, AIS offers durable long-term patency, with a 10-year rate of 85.3%. Simultaneous infrainguinal revascularization, even in patients with more advanced systemic and anatomical disease, does not independently compromise long-term iliac stent durability. For patients with multilevel disease, particularly those presenting with CLTI, comprehensive inflow and outflow revascularization should be pursued when clinically indicated, as it does not impose additional long-term patency risk on the aortoiliac stent.
