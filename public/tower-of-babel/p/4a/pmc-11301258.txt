A developmental neurotoxicity adverse outcome pathway (DNT‐AOP) with voltage gate sodium channel (VGSC) inhibition as a molecular initiating event (MiE)
Authors: European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), Kevin M. Crofton, Martin Paparella, Anna Price, Iris Mangas, Laura Martino, Andrea Terron, Antonio Hernández‐Jerez
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe adverse outcome pathway (AOP) framework serves as a practical tool for organising scientific knowledge that can be used to infer cause–effect relationships between stressor events and toxicity outcomes in intact organisms. However, a major challenge in the broader application of the AOP concept within regulatory toxicology is the development of a robust AOPs that can withstand peer review and acceptance. This is mainly due to the considerable amount of work required to substantiate the modular units of a complete AOP, which can take years from inception to completion. The methodology used here consisted of an initial assessment of a single chemical hazard using the Integrated Approach to Testing and Assessment (IATA) framework. An evidence‐based approach was then used to gather empirical evidence combining systematic literature review methods with expert knowledge to ensure the effectiveness of the AOP development methodology. The structured framework used assured transparency, objectivity and comprehensiveness, and included expert knowledge elicitation for the evaluation of key event relationships (KERs). This stepwise approach led to the development of an AOP that begins with binding of chemicals to Voltage Gate Sodium Channels (VGSC/Nav) during mammalian development leading to adverse consequences in neurodevelopment evidenced as deficits in cognitive functions. Disruption of the formation of precise neural circuits by alterations in VGSC kinetics during the perinatal stages of brain development may also underlie neurodevelopmental disorders. Gaps in our understanding include the specific critical developmental windows and the quantitative relationship of binding to VGSC and subsequent disruption and cognitive function. Despite the limited quantitative information at all KER levels, regulatory applications of this AOP for DNT assessment have been identified.

SUMMARY

There is a growing concern that certain chemicals may increase the prevalence of neurodevelopmental disorders, because of the unique susceptibility of the developing nervous system, which can be especially sensitive to these substances. However, the current Organisation for Economic Co‐operation and Development (OECD) test guidelines (TG), specifically TG 426 (developmental neurotoxicity study) and TG 443 (extended one‐generation reproductive toxicity study) (OECD, 2007, 2018a, 2018b), which were designed for screening of potential chemical‐induced DNT hazards, have recognised limitations. A new framework is, therefore, needed for the assessment of chemicals' potential to disrupt brain development. A new framework for assessing the potential of chemicals to disrupt brain development is therefore needed.

In June 2023, the OECD published the Initial Recommendations on Evaluation of Data from the Developmental Neurotoxicity In Vitro Testing Battery (DNT IVB), which is the result of a major endeavour from EFSA, the US EPA, the US NIEHS/NTP and academic groups. Over the last years, these groups standardised a battery of 17 in vitro assays for DNT assessment (OECD, 2023). The OECD document provides guidance on the evaluation and interpretation of DNT IVB results for the identification of substances with DNT potential for use within an integrated approach to testing and assessment (IATA) framework. To assess the applicability of data from the DNT IVB in the current EU pesticides regulatory framework, EFSA published two IATA case studies for pesticide active substances deltamethrin and flufenacet under the OECD Case Study program. These case studies demonstrated the usefulness of the DNT IVB as part of the overall weight of evidence (WoE) in the context of the EU pesticide Risk Assessment framework. This illustrated how an adverse outcome pathway (AOP)‐informed IATA represents an important tool for the inclusion of data from a combination of methods, including new approach methodologies (NAMs), to address an a priori defined regulatory question. These case studies used the AOP framework to organise and correlate in vitro evidence, including the DNT‐IVB, with an adverse outcome (AO) measured in vivo. Nevertheless, it is acknowledged that very few pesticide active substances have been assessed using this framework. In addition, the development of an AOP‐informed IATA for the characterisation of a single chemical hazard requires a systematic description and analysis of all the available evidence, which is quite resource‐intensive, particularly if an AOP is not available.

The DNT IVB measures key neurodevelopmental processes (KNP) in human relevant test systems. These KNPs, once perturbed, represent changes in cellular Key Events (KEs) (i.e. a change in biological state that is both measurable and essential for the progression of a defined biological perturbation leading to a specific AO). However, a limited number of the KNPs in the DNT IVB are currently mapped into AOPs reviewed or endorsed AOPs by the OECD (e.g. synaptogenesis). The development of a robust mapping of the KEs covered in the DNT IVB will help understanding the mechanisms underlying DNT and will facilitate the single chemical hazard characterisation process using the DNT IVB as a stand‐alone source of data. Development of computational prediction models will also benefit from using data from multiple assays measuring MIE and KEs included in the AOP network.

In this report, we have developed a robust AOP mapping the KEs covered by the DNT IVB assays and uploaded them into the AOP Wiki database. A pragmatic approach has been used starting with the AOP‐informed IATA case study on deltamethrin developed by the EFSA PPR Panel, 2021, where the molecular initiating event (MIE) and early KEs had strong empirical support. However, the empirical support was weak or moderate for most of the downstream KERs (i.e. most KERs were not adjacent), particularly when relying solely on evidence from a single chemical stressor. As a follow‐up, several literature review tools were used in a stepwise approach to identify additional essential KEs, along with broad searches to expand the scope of empirical evidence. This culminated in the identification of a new downstream KE and the development of a chemically agnostic AOP with a transparent description of how the literature search conducted using the OECD AOP template was reported. Finally, an Expert Knowledge Elicitation (EKE) was done to assess the WoE for the overall AOP (EFSA, 2014). We concluded that an AOP‐informed IATA for single chemical hazard characterisation was very useful as a starting point for developing an agnostic AOP.

The AOP developed by an expert working group (WG) describes an AO that may result from the binding of a stressor to the VGSC during the development of the mammalian nervous system (i.e. the MIE). This MIE (KE1353) results in disruption of sodium channel gate kinetics (KE1977) and subsequent disruption of neuronal action potential (KE1983). This KE leads to a subsequent alteration in neurotransmission during development (KE2005) that can be measured using one of the standardised assays included in the DNT IVB. Neurotransmitter release is essential for neural activity and neural activity‐dependent processes are critical for controlling neuronal circuit formation during normal brain development. Disruption of neural activity during development in many brain regions, including the hippocampus, can negatively impact both neuroanatomy and neurophysiology, ultimately disrupting higher neurological functions. Therefore, chemicals that bind to VGSCs during perinatal stages of brain development have the potential to cause adverse effects on the developing brain by disrupting the formation of precise neural circuits leading to miswiring, altered connectivity and subsequent functional deficits. When this occurs in the developing hippocampus, it can ultimately impair some cognitive functions (e.g. learning and memory).

Herein, we discuss the implications of VGSC binding, disruption of action potential generation and propagation, and neurotransmission during brain development, leading to alterations in hippocampal anatomy, function and ultimately in higher hippocampal function. The biophysics of the VGSC and its relevance/essentiality for further neurotransmitter release is well known across species, as is the hippocampus which is known to be critically involved in cognitive function, including spatial learning and memory, which is a measurable AO in regulatory guideline studies. The adverse consequences of chemical perturbations on VGSCs will depend on both the severity of disruption and the developmental time window. Therefore, exposure to stressors could have different effects at different developmental time windows of exposure. It is important to note that this could also occur in other areas of the brain, as VGSCs are expressed in all neurons. The focus of this report is on the hippocampus, given its well‐known correlations to spatial‐based cognitive functions, and downstream outcome of concern for many chemical exposures.

The overall WoE for this AOP is strong, due to the strong biological plausibility for all KERs, with empirical support either strong or moderate. Knowledge gaps identified include the relationship between the AO and specific critical developmental time windows, as well as the quantitative relationship along the KERs up to the AO. Although quantitative information is limited for all KERs, new research is proposed to address this limitation. Importantly, several regulatory applications of this AOP have been identified for the assessment of DNT. Overall, this exercise allowed mapping one of the assays measured in the DNT IVB into a KE leading to a specific AO. This AOP should foster the use of data from the DNT IVB for single chemical hazard characterisation and supporting use in the chemical risk assessment process.

INTRODUCTION

Background and Terms of Reference as provided by the requestor

Developmental neurotoxicity (DNT) refers to “any adverse effects on the normal development of the nervous system structure or function”. Perturbations caused by chemical stressors during early neurodevelopment may manifest as non‐reversible deficits in neurobehavioral function (e.g., sensory, motor and/or cognitive), either in the early years or later in life. Such adverse outcomes are therefore of particular interest for the regulatory risk assessment of environmental contaminants, including pesticides. DNT studies, when triggered by specific conditions, are part of the data requirement according to Regulation No 1107/2009 and Regulation 283/2013. When triggered, the current regulatory testing paradigm is based on DNT guideline in vivo studies following perinatal (prenatal and early postnatal) exposure. However, due to its methodological complexity, interpretative uncertainties, cost and resource limitations, the use of the DNT guideline has been limited compared to other toxicity guidelines. As a result, the DNT characterization is limited to a small number of chemicals of concern to regulatory authorities. This has led to calls from regulatory agencies and stakeholders for the development of New Approach Methodologies (NAMs) for DNT.

The international recognition to improve risk assessment methodologies for DNT is exemplified by the foundation of the OECD forum to develop internationally harmonized guidance to test and assess chemicals for DNT, primarily based on cellular models. This resulted in the recent publication of the OECD Guidance 377 “Initial Recommendations on Evaluation of Data from the Developmental Neurotoxicity (DNT) In Vitro Testing Battery.” This significant achievement stems from collaborative efforts by the European Food Safety Agency (EFSA), the United States Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA) and researchers from the Leibniz Research Institute for Environmental Medicine (IUF), University of Konstanz, and the US National Toxicology Program (US NTP) (Blum et al., 2023). Over the last few years, this effort resulted in a standardized battery of 17 assays, testing of about 100 chemicals, and recommendations on how to use data for decision making processes.

An advantage of the DNT IVB assays is that they can be pragmatically framed within an AOP and thus used in an overall evaluation of the regulatory weight of evidence and prediction of possible AOs. In doing so, this allows missing data to be identified, directing prospective research to fill‐in existing knowledge gaps, and potentially advancing the mechanistic understanding of how chemicals can disrupt neurodevelopment. Thus, data from the DNT IVB can contribute to the DNT Risk assessment in a cost and time‐effective manner (EFSA PPR Panel, 2021). AOP‐informed IATAs, using NAMs and exposure models to fill the mechanistic data gaps, was the conceptual framework used to conclude on chemical specific hazard characterizations (IATA Cs 362 and 363). Nevertheless, it is acknowledged that very few pesticides (active substances) have been assessed through this framework.

The complexity of developmental processes and physiological features of the developing nervous system make it challenging to map neurodevelopmental toxicity pathways and critical windows of vulnerability. To address this challenge, the AOP conceptual framework was selected as a tool to apply the mechanistic understanding of toxicity pathways in the context of health protection (OECD, 2021, Proposal for a template and guidance on developing and assessing the completeness of adverse outcome pathways, Paris, France). AOPs have been demonstrated to help in the systematic organization and integration of complex information from various types of studies aimed at unrevealing the biological mechanisms underlying adverse outcomes (AOs), and to inform on the causal relationships observed in both experimental and observational studies. An AOP describes a sequence of events originating from a MIE, in which a chemical interacts with a molecular biological target, through a series of measurable KEs, ultimately resulting in an AO. Key event relationships (KERs) are based on scientific evidence for causality between adjacent KEs at the cellular, tissue and organ levels. Empirical evidence supporting these KERs is based on relevant data found either from existing literature, or studies specifically designed for AOP development. Therefore, if KERs are supported by a strong WoE analysis, in vitro assays anchored to these correlative KEs increase confidence in their relevance to predict AOs (e.g. cognitive defects). These KERs provide correlative linkages driving the progression within any given AOP, making them, to a large degree, the most important modules of any robust AOP.

In the context of NAMs, in vitro models describing KEs or KERs should provide a mechanistic linkage to a biological process or address biological plausibility of the model for predicting in vivo outcomes (ICVAM, 2024). The development of an endorsed AOP represents the culmination of the proof of an NAM demonstrating biological and mechanistic relevance. The development of AOPs linking KEs measured in the DNT IVB to in vivo AO will serve not only for understanding the mechanisms underlying DNT, but also to reduce uncertainties in the use of data from the DNT IVB for hazard characterization. AOPs also allow to move forward in the development of computational models that use data from multiple assays of the DNT IVB for toxicity prediction, similarly to the model describing oestrogen receptor activation (Judson et al., 2015). In this model, data from in vitro assays covering multiple KEs of the oestrogen receptor activation pathway were used to construct a computational model capable of predicting endocrine disruption in vivo. The development of additional AOPs will increase the capacity of correlating the DNT IVB KNP with human neurodevelopmental disorders. However currently, only a limited number of putative and reviewed AOPs exist for DNT (Bal‐Price et al., 2015; OECD, 2023; Spinu et al., 2019, 2020). Of these, only 12 DNT AOPs are available in the AOP Wiki (Jaylet et al., 2024), of which 5 are currently under review or have been endorsed by WNT, and the rest are under development. These AOPs are qualitative and provide scientifically credible information to link apical hazards of regulatory concern to perturbations of specific toxicity pathways.

There is currently no commonly detailed guidance on how AOPs with a strong WoE should be developed, nor KERs elaborated. In this regard, a systematic literature search approach is encouraged (i.e., searching literature using predefined search terms and, most importantly, providing a transparent description on how the literature was searched and selected). Recently, pragmatic approaches to AOP development have been proposed to facilitate faster development and endorsement of AOPs under the OECD program (Svingen et al., 2021a, 2021b; EFSA PPR Panel, 2023).

An AOP‐informed IATA for deltamethrin DNT hazard characterisation was developed by EFSA using this stepwise approach (EFSA PPR Panel, 2021). This exercise culminated in the postulation of the evidence‐based AOP network for deltamethrin with a probabilistic quantitative estimation of the WoE using a Bayesian network analysis. The intention was to use the AOP conceptual framework to establish a causal relationship between exposure to the prototypical chemical deltamethrin and an AO. The AOP network developed identified two major uncertainties. The first one was the network itself as potentially important KEs could be overlooked due to the inclusion of data and KEs with empirical support only from deltamethrin as a stressor. The second is due to a knowledge gap that drives uncertainty in the last two KERs: the lack of empirical data that establish correlative and/or causal relationships between disturbed neuronal network function measured in the DNT IVB (KE4) and/or altered behavioural function (AO). In this context, an EFSA WG was asked to develop a dedicated AOP to reduce these uncertainties. Two main objectives were planned to address this endeavour: (1) to close the knowledge gap for linking KE4 to the adverse neurodevelopmental outcome; and (2) to reduce the uncertainty on the KERs by including additional stressor‐agnostic biological knowledge and empirical evidence with other stressors. This was done using the principles of evidence‐based toxicology to systematically organize available data and knowledge that describes scientifically plausible relationships between the MIEs (binding to VGSC), KEs, and the AO.

Based on the results obtained, the structure of the AOP was revised with the addition of a KE5 preliminarily named as ‘Altered synaptic plasticity’ that can occur transiently during development. Synaptic plasticity has been used here as a broad definition for a KE, which may include changes at the level of receptors, neurotransmission, signalling pathways, gene expression and epigenetic mechanisms.

In Phase II activities, the canonical pathway (i.e. the binding to VGSCs, MIE, leading to disruption of sodium channel gate kinetics, disruption of the action potential and altered neurotransmission during development), was strengthened and extended by empirical data beyond deltamethrin as a stressor and generalised the available biological plausibility beyond the pyrethroid mode of action. For the non‐canonical pathway (i.e. altered neurotransmission leading to altered hippocampal anatomy and physiology ultimately resulting in cognition deficits), broad literature searches were conducted in two electronic databases (PubMed and SCOPUS), and for KE4 and the AO Topic modelling was used to explore possible downstream KEs.

In Phase III activities for the non‐canonical pathway included a systematic retrieval of evidence, screening for relevance and data extraction for the prioritised KERs, including the new one postulated in Phase II. This facilitated agile exploration of the results, provided suggestions for fine‐tuning the searches, and identified priorities and new data gaps requiring additional searches. Further searches were conducted combining presumed chemical stressors with the KERs, and these were also developed and implemented in AbstractSifter v5.6 (see Table 1). This exercise allowed strengthening the non‐canonical pathway by introducing two KEs, altered hippocampal anatomy and altered hippocampal function (preliminarily named as ‘Altered synaptic plasticity’ in Phase II). These KEs were already available within the AOP Wiki (KE757 and KE758, respectively) and allowed linking the canonical pathway with an existing endorsed AOP in the Wiki through the development of the KER4 (Altered neurotransmission during development leads to altered hippocampal anatomy).

For easy reference and to avoid redundancy, a concise overview of the AOP is provided here. In addition, this section includes a series of discussions and reflections on the regulatory implementation of the toxicological pathways covered by the AOP.

The AOP describes an AO that may result from the binding of xenobiotics to VGSC during mammalian brain development. The MIE (KE1353) involves the binding to VGSC, which subsequently disrupts the sodium channel gate kinetics (KE1977) and, consequently, action potential generation (KE1983). This disruption alters neurotransmission during development. Neurotransmitter release is essential for neural activity and neural activity is critical for normal brain development. However, when neural activity is disrupted during development in many brain regions, including the hippocampus, it may negatively impact neuroanatomical, neurophysiological development and ultimately higher neurological functions. Therefore, chemicals that bind with VGSCs, either inhibiting or enhancing neurotransmission, have the potential to cause adverse effects on the developing brain. When this occurs in the developing hippocampus, it can ultimately lead to cognitive impairments. Herein, we discuss the implications of VGSC binding, disruption of action potential generation and neurotransmission during brain development, which can alter hippocampal anatomy and function, ultimately impacting the neural function controlled by the hippocampus. The biophysics of VGSC and its essentiality for subsequent neurotransmitter release is well known across species. The hippocampus is known to be critically involved in cognitive function, including spatial learning and memory. The adverse consequences of chemical interference at VCSCs will depend both on severity and developmental timing, indicating that exposure could produce different effects depending on the developmental stages of exposure. It is important to note that this could also occur in areas of the brain other than hippocampus as VGSC are foundational to the structure and function of all neurons. Here, we focus on the hippocampus because, despite its well‐known links to cognitive function and downstream outcome of concern for many chemical exposures, empirical evidence and biological knowledge on the adverse consequences remain scarce for other brain regions.

From the scientific point of view, improving the non‐canonical pathway of the AOP is of critical importance and helpful for strengthening the regulatory relevance of the KE ‘Altered neurotransmission during development’. From the regulatory point of view, improving the non‐canonical pathway of the AOP is also of paramount importance since the developed AOP now forms a network with the Thyroid Hormone Disruption AOP, which is already being used to better understand the DNT hazard posed by chemicals, and for which a regulatory confidence and methodology for mapping the uncertainties exist (EFSA/ECHA ED guidance, 2018). However, this was beyond the scope outlined in the ToRs of this report and quantitative information from all KERs is limited. Section 3.5 presents a proposed experimental design for this qAOP, as developed by the WG.

The AOP informed IATAs as a selective framework for current DNT risk assessment

The actual application of AOPs in hazard and risk assessment processes remains limited and discussions on how to best use AOPs for regulatory purposes are still ongoing. The use of AOPs for DNT and neurotoxicity hazard characterisation, together with the use of AOPs for assessing MoA in the identification of endocrine‐disrupting properties of pesticide active substances for human health, represents an ongoing area in the EFSA Next generation risk assessment.

This was exemplified in a previous Scientific Opinion (EFSA PPR Panel, 2021). In this Opinion, a systematic review of all available toxicological data for a chemical of interest (i.e. deltamethrin) was carried out. When the number of scientific papers retrieved was overwhelming, strategies such as limiting the search string using cues like the pesticide MoA or topic modelling were employed. The resulting data were organised by lines of evidence to identify potentially relevant KEs in a postulated AOP. These KEs were mapped against postulated or existing AOPs. In case where inconsistencies or critical data gaps were identified for the selected stressors of interest, new data could be generated to enhance mechanistic understanding. In addition, the available AOPs and AOP networks may be amended with data and knowledge generated during such assessments. Over time, this iterative process allows for the gradual refinement of assessments, leveraging a more robust approach that considers mechanistic understanding, empirical evidence and specific in vitro methods within a chemical‐specific AOP‐informed IATA (OECD, 2022a, 2022b, 2022c).

During the discussion of the deltamethrin‐specific AOP‐informed IATA with the regulatory bodies, an important observation emerged: When a non‐endorsed AOP exists for a specific pathway, the development of an evidence‐based AOP‐informed stressor agnostic IATA, in line with a systematic approach, is very resource‐intensive due to the need to assess a huge number of chemicals (OECD, 2022d). The existence of a robust AOP with KEs mapping NAMs, assessed as reliable and robust, leading to a specific AO, would be a step forward and would facilitate the development of an AOP‐informed IATA and provides the context for integrating these assays into the chemical risk assessment process.

It was also clear from this discussion that AOPs can serve as the basis for designing NAM testing strategies and that the data generated by NAMs mapped in robust AOPs can be used to identify a reference point (RP, also known as Point of Departure, PoD) within the AOP‐informed IATA framework, or other accepted frameworks, for decision‐making (Terron et al., 2018; US EPA, 2020a, 2020b, 2023; OECD, 2020).

The comprehensive AOP‐informed IATA framework, incorporating all available information or based on a robust AOP, is the most appropriate approach to increase the level of confidence along the decision‐making process by including many sources of data (e.g. in vitro, physiologically based kinetic (PBK) modelling). As such, currently, the AOP‐informed IATA approach allows for an efficient use of the AOP and of all available information and it remains the best available tool for assessing DNT potential.

Pyrethroids: Acute versus developmental neurotoxicity

It is well recognised that for many substances with neurotoxic MoAs, the distinction between acute neurotoxicity and DNT can be challenging. This holds particularly true for substances like pyrethroids that disrupt a crucial molecular target affecting physiological neurotransmission across all life stages (Shafer et al., 2005). This was a particularly challenging area in the development of this AOP.

In vivo studies conducted in adult animals have consistently revealed a well‐characterised set of acute toxic effects associated with pyrethroids (i.e. clinical signs typical of Type I or of Type II pyrethroids). However, reviews of in vivo guideline DNT studies involving several pyrethroids failed to identify any common pattern of AOs (Scollon, 2010). Nevertheless, it is worth noting that while pyrethroid‐induced neurotoxicity in the context of adult animal studies manifests as a transient effect, acute exposure during the development is known to produce permanent effects.

In 2021, EFSA published a Scientific Opinion including an IATA case study for deltamethrin that built an AOP‐informed IATA, which integrated negative and positive in vivo data, as well as mechanistic in vitro data (EFSA PPR Panel, 2021). The overall evidence for DNT was deemed to be mechanistically and biologically plausible, based on data for the mechanistic intermediate KEs from some DNT IVB assays. This experience consolidated the opinion that mechanistic information and an appropriate uncertainty analysis are pivotal to interpret the outcome of in vivo studies, whether they be positive or negative.

In conclusion, it can sometimes be controversial to scientifically separate acute neurotoxic effects from a DNT effect. However, for the regulation of chemicals, an effect observed within a DNT relevant test system should not be dismissed due to the possibility of acute effects, unless specific additional data are available. The AOP‐informed IATA approach fostered a comprehensive and scientifically justified decision‐making process. In addition, when considering the relevance of measuring critical brain development processes, the DNT IVB, together with appropriate exposure considerations and uncertainty analysis, currently stands as the optimal approach for DNT screening and protecting human health.

KER4: Complexity of measuring a key neurodevelopmental process

It is acknowledged herein that understanding the variety of the mechanisms that regulate neurological functions is a complex exercise, and while the field of neurodevelopment has made a tremendous progress, many uncertainties remain (Faust, Gunner, & Schafer, 2021). With the development of KER4, the complexity of current knowledge has been translated into a straightforward AOP that does not pretend to map the full biology of this crucial and complex process. Further details of this complex pathway are briefly discussed below.

During brain development, as neurons migrate to their ultimate destinations and extend axonal processes for establishing long‐range connections, early patterns of electrical activity occur in the hippocampus that control many intracellular and intercellular neurodevelopmental processes. Mutual interactions between early electrical activity and gene expression patterns have been shown in various immature neuronal circuits (Khazipov & Luhmann, 2006). It is well known that neurons express and release neurotransmitters before their axons establish contacts with their target cells for synapse formation. Neurotransmitters therefore help establish the intricate wiring of neuronal circuits by playing a crucial role in shaping the structural and functional properties of brain structures (Nguyen et al., 2001; Ojeda & Ávila, 2019). GABA is one of the most predominant neurotransmitters during brain development and controls cell cycle kinetics of neocortical precursor cells by acting on GABAA receptors (LoTurco et al., 1995). Once neural circuitries are formed, neurotransmission involves the release, binding and subsequent removal of neurotransmitters at synapses, as occurs in the mature brain. During neural development, alterations in neurotransmission can significantly influence axonal growth, branching and the guidance cues essential for the formation of neural circuits (West & Greenberg, 2011).

Neuronal activity is known to drive a number of developmental remodelling processes (e.g. apoptosis, synaptic pruning, dendritic and axonal growth). Early‐use activity during circuit‐specific critical periods refines brain circuitry by the coupled processes of eliminating inappropriate synapses and strengthening maintained synapses (Doll & Broadie, 2014). For example, dendrites extending from hippocampal neurons form complex arborisations, which are essential for the establishment of neuronal circuitry, and synaptic activity regulates both spine number and shape during brain development (Swanger et al., 2011) by unfolding activity‐dependent transcription of neurodevelopmental genes. Moreover, alterations in neurotransmission can disrupt dendritic branching, spine density and spine morphology, which in turn influences information processing and plasticity (Gibson & Olive, 2017).

Neurotransmission orchestrates synaptic formation and pruning, two critical neurodevelopmental processes. Synapses formed during initial stages of development are only maintained if they are active, and lack of activity will lead to synaptic pruning that removes unused synaptic connections. These two processes refine neural circuits, which are essential for proper brain function. However, aberrant neurotransmission induced by chemical exposures can disrupt these activity‐dependent processes, thus alter connectivity between pre‐ and post‐synaptic neurons. This can lead to neuronal remodelling and loss of neural structures from synaptic buttons to whole cells, thus affecting hippocampal microcircuitry. If these changes are large enough and not compensated, they may contribute to the later onset of neurodevelopmental disorders.

The development of hippocampal circuity is shaped by the balance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission during development. When perturbed, this balance can result in abnormal network activity and structural alterations in the hippocampus, potentially compromising its function (Sears & Hewett, 2021) and contributing to behavioural deficits (Gilbert et al., 2020).

The preponderance of studies identified in the systematic literature review that support KER4 were conducted in knockout (KO) mouse models. In them, electrophysiological methods were combined with immunohistochemistry to define the role that KO proteins play in hippocampal anatomy and function. Several of these studies characterised the relationship between altered neurotransmission and morphological changes in synapses during the development. Since in KO studies the two KEs are commonly measured in the same study, it is difficult to ascertain which occurs first (KEup) and which occurs later (KEdown), thus hampering drawing firm conclusions on the temporal relationship between KE3 and KE4 as both KEs are commonly measured at the same time in the same study. A summary table of the several KO animal models retrieved and assessed by the WG is reported in Annex A.

This AOP and all the KERs developed are a pragmatic simplification of the current knowledge of this biology with the aim of having an easy approach to using this knowledge to improve the efficiency of regulatory actions. It is recognised that, with further insights and scientific development, this AOP as the other would need to be revised.

KE 2005: Measuring altered neurotransmission during development as a critical KE for regulatory assessment of DNT

The development of the new KE (KE 2005) referred to us ‘Altered neurotransmission during development’ has been a critical knowledge compilation in this AOP (see Annex C for detailed information on this KE and its KER). The regulatory relevance of this in vitro testable KE ‘Altered neurotransmission during development’ is now, with this enhanced AOP, further supported by characterisation of the mechanistic KERs. These KERs link the KEs ‘altered hippocampal anatomy’ and ‘altered hippocampal physiology’ to the AO ‘decreased cognitive function’. The biological plausibility and empirical evidence supporting these KERs have been assessed as moderate to strong.

This KE occurs in all life stages. As the balance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission shapes hippocampal circuitry, any perturbation of this balance can lead to abnormal network activity (Cherubini et al., 2021). The methods and test systems used to measure abnormal network activity are the same in developmental or adult life stages. In addition, upstream KEs and pathways occurring in all life stages can lead to alteration in neurotransmission. However, in this AOP, KE 2005 pertains to the developmental period, because it is recognised that the biological and toxicological consequences can be different when disruption of neurotransmission occurs during development, versus adult life stages. It is well recognised that an infant's brain contains more neurons at birth than that of an adult, and the developing brain undergoes remarkable remodelling to achieve mature neural circuits via processes like apoptosis and synaptic pruning. As brain development matures further activity‐dependent remodelling will strengthen circuits that prove more relevant and weaken others that are less frequently used. Such remodelling is more prominent in the first 2 years of life in humans and again during adolescence, with neural activity being a key driver for synaptic pruning. Thus, disruption of the formation of precise neural circuits during critical stages of brain development (i.e. perinatal and adolescence) may underlie neurodevelopmental disorders (Faust et al., 2021; see KER4 description and life stage applicability).

In mammals, activity‐dependent neuronal remodelling and the timing of this process depends on the brain region and cellular subtypes. This AOP focused on the hippocampal region (as detailed in Annex C) since hippocampal circuits have been more extensively studied, particularly in relation to regulated chemicals, and the hippocampus has been causally linked to measurable AOs (e.g. learning and memory) in rodent models. While several model circuits for studying activity‐dependent neuronal remodelling are available for many brain regions, future work is required to develop an AOP and KERs for other brain areas.

There are uncertainties in this new chemically agnostic AOP, including but not limited to knowledge gaps regarding quantitative relationships between KEs and the subsequent adverse impacts on cognitive functions, species extrapolation issues common to all animal based AOPs, and possible lower sensitivity of rodent cognition models commonly used in regulatory studies. This also holds true for developing AOPs for other brain regions, since this AOP is focused on the AO of altered hippocampal‐based cognitive function. However, regarding the utility of the MEA‐based neural network formation (NNF) assay (OECD, 2023) for use in chemical regulation, it is important to note that it uses cortical cell cultures. Thus, an effect on the NNF assay may not necessarily correlate with changes in the hippocampal‐based spatial cognitive tests commonly used in regulatory in vivo DNT studies. It is biologically highly plausible that disturbed NNF represents an adverse effect within other brain regions. For chemical regulation, the derivation of relevant PoDs is more important than the prediction of any specific neurodevelopmental disorder at the organism level.

The newly developed KE 2005 can be measured using methodologies that examine neural connectivity (i.e. neurotransmission) in vitro through the NNF assay. A standardised NNF test system to assess the potential impact of chemical exposure on neural network formation and function has been developed using rodent cortical neurons (Frank et al., 2017). This NNF assay is considered valid, biologically relevant and reliable by OECD (OECD, 2023) and the US EPA (US EPA, 2020a, 2020b). An analysis for the regulatory use of the rodent primary cortical cell‐based NNF assay and the additional 16 in vitro DNT assays has also been performed, and this may be contextualised with the uncertainties for in vivo data‐based uncertainties (Paparella et al., 2020).

The NNF assay represents a developing and relatively complex in vitro multicellular test system that includes many KNP and provides a readout of neurophysiological function measured by changes in synaptic activity (i.e. general network activity, network bursting, network connectivity). If such activity is disturbed, it is likely caused by one or more upstream KEs that have previously been disturbed and not compensated at the (multi)cellular level. If these types of functional changes in vitro are large enough, they can represent a disruption which may negatively affect neurological functions of an organism. Within experimental systems, a positive response often holds greater regulatory relevance than a negative one, be it in vitro or in vivo. This is because none of these systems fully encompass all aspects of human higher cerebral functions, or the characteristics exhibited by humans in their natural state, including aspects such as metabolism, kinetics, molecular and cellular characteristics. Thus, positive effects observed in in vitro models, or in in vivo studies, should be considered indicators of toxicity. Their impact in real life human conditions depends on additional factors such as (epi)genetic background, socioeconomic status, diet, lifestyle, stress, infections and chemical co‐exposures.

The direct regulatory relevance of these disruptions, if integrated with other toxicological information, can be used to derive a PoD, which will be the basis for setting a health‐based guidance value. To facilitate regulatory use in decision‐making, an agreed tiered testing strategy approach for use of in vitro data would be helpful. This approach should include the MEA‐based NFF assay as well as the remaining assays in the DNT IVB together with interpretive guidance on the MEA/NFF outcomes for quantitative human health risk assessment.

Disruption of neural network transmission as measured in the NNF assay represents a DNT effect of concern that needs to be weighted in human health risk assessments. The assay provides an opportunity to overcome some of the challenges associated with assessing DNT (ECHA, 2023, EFSAPPR Panel, 2021; Paparella et al., 2020). By evaluating the underlying critical processes of neurodevelopment and incorporating human‐relevant information, the MEA‐based NNF assay contributes to a more comprehensive risk assessment.

A proposal for development of a quantitative AOP

qAOPs theoretically allow discrimination between adaptive changes observed in KEs from those that cause downstream adverse outcomes. Fully developed qAOPs have the potential ability to address the basic question of how much perturbation, at any of the upstream MIE or KEs, and under what conditions, the AO is likely to occur (Conolly et al., 2017; Patlewicz et al., 2015; Zgheib et al., 2019). They can also define the magnitude or the probability where the tipping points along the pathway are exceeded (LaLone et al., 2017). Although there are several references in the literature and some qAOP models have been proposed, a regulatory framework and guidance for the use of qAOPs are not available.

For the current AOP, quantitation of the relationships between KEs is limited. For KER 1 and KER 2, several software packages are available and give a clear and simple description of the voltage and current clamp methods. The software allows setting Na conductance levels and predicts resultant nerve action potentials. These models could be used to estimate the quantitative link between alterations in VGSC kinetics and action potential generation or disruption (MIE to KE2 in this AOP). In addition, as reported in this AOP, the quantitative relationship between the alteration of VGSC kinetics and the action potential generation has been modelled for tetramethrin but not for other pyrethroids. The timescale for the response–response of KER1 and KER2 should be considered as immediate.

Models for quantification of the remaining downstream part of the AOP are not currently available. For KER3, quantification could be possible if future work using the electrophysiological methods described in this AOP would be applied to calculate the response–response relationship between the concentration‐dependent perturbation of the action potential and the concentration‐dependent downstream effect in the NNF assay, and if this could be done at different stages of development. The NNF assay can indeed be used and tailored to produce data for the quantification of the KER and initial evidence has been produced in the NNF assay with Deltamethrin follow continuous exposure and recording on day in vitro (DIV) 5, 7, 9 and 12 (Tim Shafer, personal communication).

A similar experimental approach could be taken to define the quantitative relationship between all the remaining downstream KEs and the AO. However, considerations are needed depending on the type of neuron, brain region and function in the central nervous system that is used. A relationship between the type and frequency of activation of the neurons and the resulting neuroplasticity may represent the ideal quantification of the KER between altered neurotransmission during development and hippocampal physiology (KE 758) using known stressors like pyrethroids. A quantitative relationship between the KE3 and the KE 757 (changes in the hippocampal anatomy) can be performed in vitro correlating neurotransmission in the NNF assay to alterations in synaptogenesis in vitro. KE757 and KE758 could be measured in vitro in brain Spheres or brain organoids developed in three‐dimensional (3D) cell culture resembling hippocampus at different stages of hippocampus development (Sakaguchi et al., 2015). This approach could benefit from measuring synaptic, cell body and neurite proteins from the in vitro preparations as well as long‐term potentiation/depression (LTP/LTD).To better investigate the possibility to quantify the downstream KERs, the working group invited a hearing expert with experience in developing brain microphysiological system derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) to assess neurological diseases and toxicity (Pamies et al., 2017). Several compounds have been tested in the microelectrode arrays using different brainsphere models and the outcome was compared with rat neuronal network formation arrays (rNFA). The two models showed similar results, at least in the common ability to identify positive and negative compounds (Hartman et al., 2023). However, an unresolved uncertainty remains regarding the number of cells expressing pyramidal cells biomarkers (the main excitatory hippocampal cell) in the brainsphere. Currently, the number of cells (around 10% of the overall neuronal population) is considered a limitation of the test system. However, alternative solutions exist, such as using iPSC‐derived hippocampal spheroids (Pomeshchik et al., 2020) or human embryonic stem cell (hESC)‐derived brain organoids (Sakaguchi et al., 2015). Further research can be therefore be directed towards using these models to quantify the response/response relationship between the electrical perturbation of the neuronal network, changes in synaptic micro‐anatomy and functional alterations as indicated by perturbation in the LTP or LTD. Indeed, commercially available hiPSC‐derived dopaminergic cells have been used in 48‐well MEA plates to study the effects of Forskolin and Rolipram on LTP (Pré et al., 2022; Odawara et al., 2016).

In addition, plasticity could also be measured in vivo in treated animals. Structural and physiological models for plasticity have been proposed, in which synaptic strength is the product of a synapse‐specific Hebbian factor and a postsynaptic cell‐specific homeostatic factor, with each factor separately arriving at a stable inactive state (Toyoizumi et al., 2014). Exposure modelling would be, however, likely necessary for a correct substantiation of the response–response relationship. Electrophysiological experiments can also be complemented using other methods (e.g. nuclear transcription (Marble et al. 2022)).

Future research should be therefore directed to provide the data needed and/or expand and incorporate existing computational models to complete a qAOP. Quantification of this AOP would represent a relevant step forward to identify critical KEs that can be measured and quantified in their KER to substantiate the relationship between perturbation of neuronal action potential during development and permanent brain liabilities.

CONCLUSIONS

In this report, we present a robust AOP mapping of the KEs covered by the DNT IVB assays that has been uploaded to the AOP Wiki database. An AOP‐informed IATA was used as a starting point for DNT hazard characterisation and development of an agnostic AOP. The AOP developed describes an AO that may result from the binding of a stressor to the VGSC during mammalian nervous system development (MIE). This MIE (KE1353) results in disruption of sodium channel gate kinetics (KE1977) and subsequent disruption of neuronal action potential (KE1983). This KE leads to a subsequent alteration in neurotransmission during development (KE4) that can be measured using one of the standardised assays included in the DNT IVB. These early KEs and KERs are considered canonical based on established knowledge and methods in neurobiology. When these KEs are disrupted during development they can modify hippocampal anatomy (KE757) and physiology (KE758), ultimately impairing certain cognitive functions (e.g. learning and memory; AO402). This second part of the AOP, starting from KE4, benefits from an existing endorsed AOP. The WoE for the entire AOP developed herein is strong, due to the strong biological plausibility for all KERs, with empirical support either strong or moderate. Knowledge gaps identified include the relationship between the AO and specific critical developmental time windows, as well as the lack of quantitative relationships along the KERs up to the AO. New research is proposed to address these limitations. Several regulatory applications of this AOP have been identified for the assessment of DNT. This AOP should foster the use of data from the DNT IVB for hazard characterisation.
