Clinical and radiographic outcome of tension band suture fixation for displaced olecranon fractures
Authors: Liv Vesterby, Søren Ohrt-Nissen, Morten Grove Thomsen, Ilija Ban, Peter Toft Tengberg
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTension band wire fixation (TBW) is a well-described method for treating displaced olecranon fractures. Further surgery is often needed due to wound breakdown or prominent hardware. An all-suture technique has recently been described as an alternative to TBW but radiographic and clinical outcome are not well established. The aim of this single-center retrospective cohort study was to evaluate outcome after treatment with all-suture technique for simple displaced olecranon fractures.MethodsA retrospective review of olecranon fractures in patients (> 18 years) treated for displaced olecranon fractures with tension band suture fixation (TBSF) between February and August 2019 was performed in our facility. Primary outcome was revision surgery, which was assessed four years after surgery. Clinical and radiographical follow-up was performed at two weeks, six weeks, three months and six months to assess union rate, fracture displacement, range of motion (ROM), Quick-DASH and Oxford Elbow Score.ResultsA total of 24 patients were included. Median age was 64 years [IQR:39–73], 9 patients were male and median ASA score was 2 [IQR:1–2]. 15 fractures were Mayo type 2 A and 9 type 2B with minor comminution. At four-year follow-up, three patients had died. None of the remaining 21 patients had undergone revision surgery. At six months, the median Quick-DASH and Oxford Elbow Score were 2.3 [IQR:0-4.5] and 47 [IQR:46–48], respectively. Median elbow extension and flexion deficits were 0° [IQR:0-2.25] and 0° [IQR:0–0], respectively. Radiographic union was achieved in all patients. In two cases radiographic loss of reduction and malunion was observed but both patients were asymptomatic and had no functional deficits. One patient refractured the elbow due to a second trauma and was reoperated.ConclusionsTBSF is a promising technique for Mayo type 2 A and 2B fractures with minor comminution. There were no revision surgeries within the first four years. We found good functional outcomes and a high union rate.Keywords: Elbow, Olecranon fractures, Fractures of the olecranon, Displaced fractures, Internal fixation, Management, Suture, Tension band, Tension band wiring

Introduction

Olecranon fractures account for approximately 20% of fractures in the proximal part of the forearm and occur in all age groups [1]. To restore anatomical congruity of the proximal ulna and to prevent joint stiffness, surgical treatment may be indicated in displaced fractures. Simple, displaced fractures of the olecranon are traditionally treated with tension band wiring (TBW). This procedure is associated with a high risk of complications that often leads to reoperation due to loss of reduction, wound breakdown or prominent metalwork causing discomfort [2–4].

A new all-suture technique has been described as an alternative to TBW [5]. This tension band suture fixation (TBSF) technique aims to reduce hardware problems without compromising mechanical stability [6]. This technique has shown comparable strength to TBW in biomechanical studies [7, 8].

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the rate of reoperation and mid-term clinical outcome in adult patients with simple displaced olecranon fractures.

Materials and methods

We included a consecutive series of patients 18 years or older with simple displaced fractures of the olecranon and no ulnohumeral instability (Mayo type 2 A fractures and Mayo type 2B fractures with minor articular comminution). Patients were treated with TBSF between February and August 2019. Displacement was defined as interfragmentary diastasis of more than two millimeters in the articular surface on standard lateral radiographs. Based on these criteria, 24 patients were identified.

All patients underwent TBSF within two weeks after injury and the operation was performed by one of two experienced trauma surgeons as described by Phadnis et al [5] using Arthrex FiberWire® #2 (Fig. 1).

Surgical technique

A longitudinal posterior midline incision to the elbow was routinely used. Bilateral thick subcutaneous skin flaps were mobilized to allow exposure of the fracture. The fracture was cleared of debris and a pointed reduction clamp was used to achieve both reduction and compression of the fracture. The reduction was maintained with two temporary Kirschner wires. A bicortical 2.5 mm transverse drill hole was made through the dorsolateral aspect of ulna approximately 2–3 cm distal from the fracture line. The drill hole was made from medial to lateral to minimize the risk of injury to the ulnar nerve and sufficiently anterior to allow for a dorsal bone bridge of at least 10 mm.

A #2 FiberWire® with needle was passed through the transverse drill hole from lateral to medial, then grasped with a stitch horizontally in the medial aspect of the triceps tendon and brought back through the transverse drill hole from medial to lateral. A similar stich was made in the lateral aspect of the triceps tendon and then tightened with a surgeon’s knot. A second suture was passed through the transverse drill hole from lateral to medial and a horizontally stitch was made in lateral aspect of the triceps tendon. The suture was brought back through the transverse drill hole from medial to lateral and a final stitch was made in medial aspect in the triceps tendon. Care was taken to place the stiches in the triceps tendon close to the border of proximal fragment to prevent the sutures cutting through the soft tissue thereby minimizing tension across the fracture. Both sutures were tightened with the elbow in extension and the knots were placed in the lateral aspect of ulna. This way the sutures were placed in a configuration similar to the TBW with the first suture replicating the Kirschner wires and the second suture replicating the cerclage. Before wound closure satisfactory reduction and fixation of the fracture was confirmed in fluoroscopy during subsequent extension and flexion of the elbow. Subcutaneous closure was performed with single inverted absorbable sutures before skin closure with staples. Finally, an absorbable adhesive dressing was applied and the armed was placed in a sling for comfort.

Postoperatively, patients were allowed immediate full non-weight-bearing range of motion (ROM). Before discharge, patients received instructions from a physiotherapist on mobilization of the elbow.

Postoperative follow-up

Clinical and radiographic follow-up was performed at two weeks, six weeks, three months and six months following surgery (Fig. 2). We registered ROM, patient-reported outcomes (PRO) and complications. Major complications were categorized as loss of fracture reduction (defined as interfragmentary diastasis of more than two millimeters in the articular surface on standard lateral radiographs) and complications leading to reoperation. Minor complications were categorized as delayed wound healing, superficial wound infection and discomfort from suture knots. Range of motion in the affected versus unaffected elbow were measured using a long goniometer as data was reported as deficit on the injured side compared to the uninjured side. At each follow-up, patients completed the Quick-Disability of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (QuickDASH) [9, 10] and Oxford Elbow Score [11, 12] questionnaires. The Quick-DASH self-reported questionnaire is designed to measure physical function and symptoms in people with musculoskeletal disorders of the upper limb and ranges from ranging from 0 (no disability) to 100 (most severe disability). The Oxford Elbow Score ranges from 0 (severe disability) to 48 (no disability). Radiographic follow-up with anteroposterior and lateral views were used to assess loss of reduction (using the same definitions as initial displacement) and union. We defined radiographic union as endosteal healing, with ≥ 75% organized trabecular bridging at the fracture site [13, 14].

Four years postoperatively we reviewed medical charts for reoperation. Discharge letters are available from the medical chart, has a national coverage and are mandatory after hospital admissions.

Analysis

The primary outcome was revision surgery at four-year follow-up. Secondary outcomes were union rate, fracture displacement, range of motion (ROM), Quick-DASH and Oxford Elbow Score.

Categorical data are presented by frequencies and related percentages. Continuous data is reported as median with interquartile range (IQR).

Results

A total of 24 patients were included. Median age was 64 years [IQR:39–73], 9 patients were male and median ASA score was 2 [IQR:1–2]. 15 fractures were Mayo type 2 A and 9 type 2B with minor comminution (Table 1). At four-year follow-up, three patients had died. None of the remaining 21 patients had undergone reoperation. All 24 patients had completed clinical six-months follow-up although, in two cases, obtaining radiographs was not possible due to Covid-19 limitations. The two patients that did not complete radiographic six-month follow-up had radiographic union at three-months.

A total of 3 patients (12.5%) showed radiographic loss of reduction. One patient showed loss of reduction due to a relevant second trauma two weeks following primary surgery and was reoperated with TBSF. The patient completed all subsequent follow-up visits. Finally, two patients showed radiographic loss of reduction at six-weeks follow-up. Both patients presented a longitudinal fracture line in the dorsal cortex of the transverse drill hole. These two patients had subsequent malunion with fracture diastasis, but no functional deficits were observed and reoperation was not indicated. All patients obtained complete wound healing without superficial or deep infection and no antibiotics were required during the follow-up period. The median Quick-DASH and Oxford Elbow score were 2.3 [IQR 0-4.5] and 47 [IQR 46–48], respectively. Median elbow extension and flexion deficits were 0 degrees [IQR 0-2.25] and 0 degrees [IQR 0–0], respectively.

Discussion

The optimal aim of operative treatment of simple displaced olecranon fractures is to reestablish intraarticular congruity with a stabile fixation that allows early mobilization, union, reestablishment of functionality and few complications. Our results using TBSF showed no complications leading to reoperation. The minimal clinically important difference for the quick-DASH and Oxford elbow score is approximately 10 points and in a asymptomatic population median Oxford elbow score was found to be 48 points [15, 16]. As such, our cohort report good elbow function and minimal disability 6 months after surgery.

Traditional TBW is widely used and has the advantage of being low-cost and with acceptable clinical outcomes in the patients that do not experience complications [17]. The procedure is technically demanding [18, 19] and the desired tension-band principle of converting tensile forces into compression over the fracture site has been questioned in biomechanical studies [20, 21]. The revision rate after TBW varies widely between case series and likely depends on patient demographics, follow-up rate and different traditions for elective hardware removal. It is essential to differentiate between major and minor adverse events. Infection, implant failure or wound breakdown are significant complications that require early revision and can lead to inferior clinical outcomes. Minor complications such as implant prominence requiring hardware removal do not impact patient outcome the same way. In the current study we chose a six-month follow-up period as most major complications occur within this time frame. However, elective hardware removal due to implant prominence in TBW often occur at a later stage [2] and as such, our results may underestimate the rate of suture removal although no patient had any complaints in this regard.

TBSF was first described in 2016 and the available literature is sparse [5, 6, 22]. Das et al. first described the technique in 10 patients (seven simple transverse fractures of the olecranon, three chevron olecranon osteotomies related to distal humerus fracture fixation) with a wide range in age (18–88 years). The authors reported no complications and a high union rate. The mean Oxford score was 41. Phadnis et al. reported 17 patients with an acute fracture and 11 patients with an olecranon osteotomy. Radiographic union was achieved in all patients. The authors reported no data on function or disability and the follow-up period was not defined.

In the current study, radiographic loss of reduction and subsequent asymptomatic malunion was seen in two cases in patients with poor bone quality. In both cases, radiographs at six weeks follow-up showed a longitudinal fracture in the transverse drill hole. Phadnis et al. described a similar case in which the sutures “cheese wired” through the transverse drill hole likely due to osteoporotic bone and a too small bone bridge in the dorsal cortex [5]. We therefore advocate that caution should be taken using TBSF in patients with poor bone quality.

To our knowledge, this is the first retrospective study with a four-year follow-up presenting both radiographic, functional and PRO data for patients with stabile olecranon fractures treated with TBSF. The study is limited by a small sample size, but is strengthened by a 100% follow-up rate. Considering the promising results from a few case series, we encourage future studies to validate the clinical efficacy of TBSF in comparative clinical trials [23].

Conclusion

This is the first study to report radiographic, functional and patient-reported data on a homogeneous cohort with acute olecranon fractures with a follow-up of four years. TBSF is a promising technique for Mayo type 2 A and 2B fractures with minor comminution. There were no reoperations within the first four years and we found a good functional outcome and a high union rate. These data add to a growing body of evidence to support the use of TBSF.
