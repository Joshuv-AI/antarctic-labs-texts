Research progress on ejaculatory function preservation in minimally invasive surgical treatments for benign prostatic hyperplasia: a narrative review
Authors: Zhao Luo, Guihua Chen, Chao Niu, Young Beom Jeong, Sung Chul Kam, Yu Seob Shin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveAs the treatment paradigm for benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) shifts from solely relieving lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) to emphasizing quality of life and preservation of sexual function, minimally invasive surgical therapies (MISTs) have emerged as promising options for preserving ejaculatory function postoperatively. This review aims to summarize the technical features and evidence base of major MISTs, including prostatic urethral lift (PUL), prostatic artery embolization (PAE), water vapor thermal therapy (WVTT, Rezum), Aquablation, and robot-assisted simple prostatectomy (RASP), with a focus on their ability to preserve ejaculatory function.MethodsA structured literature review was conducted to identify representative studies on MISTs for BPH, with an emphasis on clinical indicators such as urinary symptom relief and preservation of ejaculatory function. Key data were extracted and summarized in a comparative table including surgical technique, efficacy, functional outcomes, advantages, disadvantages, and patient selection criteria. To assess the research attention and evidence strength of each technique, a stacked bar chart was generated illustrating the number of representative and other relevant studies per technique. Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers and cross-checked to ensure accuracy.Key Content and FindingsEvidence indicates significant differences among MISTs in balancing urinary efficacy with preservation of ejaculatory function. Techniques such as PUL and PAE, which minimize interference with midline prostatic structures, demonstrate higher rates of ejaculatory function preservation. Rezum and Aquablation also achieve functional preservation through targeted treatment, avoiding the verumontanum. Modified RASP techniques offer new structural-sparing strategies in large glands. Despite these advantages over traditional surgery, the current evidence base is limited by small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, and heterogeneity in outcome measures.ConclusionsFuture research should prioritize large-scale, multicenter randomized controlled trials with standardized evaluation of ejaculatory function to support the development of function-preserving treatment strategies. Urologists should tailor interventions based on individual patient needs while balancing symptom relief with functional outcomes to achieve patient-centered, precision therapy.

Introduction

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is one of the most common non-malignant urological conditions affecting middle-aged and elderly men. The prevalence of BPH increases significantly with age. Epidemiological data indicate that approximately 50% of men over the age of 50 exhibit varying degrees of prostatic enlargement, with this proportion rising to over 80% by the age of 80 (1). BPH-related lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), such as urinary frequency, urgency, nocturia, and difficulty in voiding, not only impair quality of life (QoL) but are also frequently associated with anxiety, depression, and sexual dysfunction, constituting a substantial burden on the health and well-being of aging men (2,3). Transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) has long been regarded as the “gold standard” for BPH treatment due to its proven efficacy in relieving urinary obstruction and improving symptoms (4). However, while TURP effectively enhances urinary function, it often compromises sexual function, most notably by causing ejaculatory dysfunction (EjD) such as retrograde ejaculation, with reported incidence rates ranging from 50% to 75% (5). This side effect significantly impacts the sexual QoL for a considerable proportion of patients.

With increasing awareness of men’s health and QoL, the preservation of sexual function, particularly ejaculatory function, has gained importance in BPH treatment decision-making. Many younger and sexually active patients now prioritize not only symptom relief but also the maintenance of ejaculatory ability, which has significant implications for psychological well-being and intimate relationships (6). Despite the established efficacy of conventional TURP and its variants in relieving bladder outlet obstruction and improving LUTS, these procedures often involve resection or manipulation of anatomical structures critical to ejaculation, such as the verumontanum, bladder neck, and adjacent neurovascular bundles (7). Consequently, these surgeries carry a high risk of EjD, including retrograde ejaculation, anejaculation, or painful ejaculation, an issue widely observed in clinical practice. In response, various minimally invasive surgical therapies (MISTs), such as prostatic artery embolization (PAE) (8,9), transurethral water vapor thermal therapy (WVTT, Rezum) (10,11), prostatic urethral lift (PUL), and Aquablation (12,13), have been developed to provide effective symptom relief while minimizing the risk of sexual dysfunction, thereby offering new treatment options for BPH management (14). Although a number of studies have reported on the short-term efficacy and impact on ejaculatory function of these procedures, limitations such as small sample sizes, heterogeneity in study design, and short follow-up durations have hindered the formation of robust, generalizable conclusions. Currently, there is a lack of systematic, comparative reviews that clearly delineate the ejaculatory function outcomes associated with each surgical technique (15).

This narrative review aims to comprehensively evaluate the existing literature on ejaculatory outcomes following minimally invasive surgical treatments for BPH. It also explores the relative efficacy of various surgical techniques, such as PAE, Rezum, PUL, and Aquablation, in preserving ejaculatory function while alleviating LUTS. By systematically synthesizing and contextualizing the current evidence, the review seeks to provide urologists with practical, evidence-based guidance to support the selection of appropriate treatment strategies based on individual patients’ anatomical characteristics, symptom severity, and QoL expectations, thereby promoting a more refined, patient-centered approach to the surgical management of BPH. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://tau.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tau-2025-393/rc).

Methods

This study is designed as a narrative review aiming to synthesize and analyze the impact of various surgical techniques for BPH on postoperative ejaculatory function, along with their potential underlying mechanisms. To enhance the transparency and methodological rigor of the review, the writing process partially adhered to the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA) (16).

Literature search and study selection

A systematic literature search was conducted on May 22, 2025, using three major electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, to identify relevant peer-reviewed English-language articles published between January 1, 2000, and May 22, 2025. The search employed a free-text keyword strategy combining terms related to BPH, ejaculatory function, and relevant minimally invasive or novel surgical techniques (e.g., PUL, PAE, Rezum, Aquablation, and robot-assisted procedures). Eligible studies included original clinical research explicitly reporting on ejaculatory function outcomes following surgical interventions for BPH. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text evaluation, with disagreements resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third reviewer. The inclusion criteria comprised the clinical study design [e.g., randomized controlled trials (RCTs), observational, or multicenter studies], a clear assessment of the relationship between surgical techniques and ejaculatory function (either preservation or dysfunction), and availability in English. Exclusion criteria encompassed articles focusing solely on erectile function, as well as case reports, editorials, expert opinions, conference abstracts, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and animal studies or basic science research.

To facilitate structured comparison, included studies were categorized according to the type of surgical intervention (e.g., TURP, PUL, PAE, Rezum, Aquablation, robot-assisted surgery). For each category, representative studies were analyzed, and data were extracted on ejaculatory function outcomes, patient-reported measures, and urinary metrics, including the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS), QoL, maximum urinary flow rate (Qmax), and postvoid residual (PVR) urine volume.

This approach emphasizes the clinical trade-offs between “urinary symptom relief” and “ejaculatory function preservation”, supporting a more individualized and function-preserving strategy in BPH surgical decision-making.

Results and discussion

Mechanisms of EjD following BPH surgery: from anatomy to surgical intervention

While the primary objective of surgical treatment for BPH is to relieve lower urinary tract obstruction and improve voiding symptoms, numerous studies have demonstrated that surgical disruption of anatomical structures involved in ejaculation can result in significant sexual side effects. These include retrograde ejaculation, weakened ejaculation, or complete anejaculation, all of which can adversely affect postoperative sexual satisfaction and overall QoL (17,18).

Ejaculation physiology: basis for understanding antegrade ejaculation preservation

Ejaculation is a highly coordinated physiological process consisting of two distinct phases: emission and expulsion, with the former driven by sympathetic nerves (T10–L2) stimulating the vas deferens, seminal vesicles, and prostate to propel seminal fluid into the posterior urethra, while the bladder neck closes to prevent retrograde flow; the latter is mediated by somatic (pudendal) and parasympathetic (S2–S4) pathways, inducing rhythmic contractions of the bulbospongiosus and pelvic floor muscles to expel semen through the urethra (19).

Antegrade ejaculation critically depends on coordinated bladder neck closure during emission, intact autonomic and somatic innervation, and an unobstructed ejaculatory pathway; initial reports emphasized the role of bladder neck closure during ejaculation as the main mechanism of forward ejaculation; hence, initial trials to preserve ejaculation relied on bladder neck incision only (20). Disruption of any of these components during surgical interventions for BPH may lead to retrograde ejaculation, reduced ejaculatory volume, or complete anejaculation (21).

Anatomical basis of EjD

The pathophysiological mechanisms of EjD following BPH surgery primarily involve the following aspects:

Injury to sympathetic and pudendal nerves: ejaculatory reflexes are coordinated through complex autonomic innervation, particularly sympathetic control over the vas deferens, seminal vesicles, and ejaculatory ducts. Intraoperative traction or transection of these neural pathways may lead to neurogenic anejaculation or functional weakening of ejaculation (22).

Sexual function trade-offs of conventional procedures

Established BPH procedures such as TURP and holmium laser enucleation of the prostate (HoLEP) are well-documented for their efficacy in LUTS relief but are associated with notable compromises in sexual function:

TURP: due to routine resection of tissue between the bladder neck and verumontanum, the incidence of EjD is reported to be as high as 60–80%, with retrograde ejaculation being the most common (4).

In recent years, a critical question has emerged in both clinical and patient communities: “Do patients have to choose between ejaculation and miction?” (30). This dilemma has gained prominence as the quality of sexual life is increasingly valued, particularly among younger and sexually active men.

Consequently, preserving ejaculatory function while effectively relieving LUTS has become a key driving force behind the development of minimally invasive BPH surgical techniques. The following sections will systematically review the mechanistic features and clinical evidence regarding several novel minimally invasive procedures aimed at achieving this balance.

Application and evidence-based progress of various minimally invasive techniques in ejaculatory function preservation

Ejaculation-preserving TURP (EP-TURP)

EP-TURP is a technical refinement of conventional TURP, aiming to reduce the incidence of postoperative retrograde ejaculation while maintaining effective relief of LUTS. The core strategy of EP-TURP is to precisely preserve the verumontanum and its surrounding structures to avoid damage to the ejaculatory pathways, thereby maintaining ejaculatory function (31).

Key technical aspects include: performing conservative resection within 1 cm proximal to the verumontanum to avoid damaging critical neural and muscular structures in this region; circumferential trimming of the inner bladder neck wall rather than deep resection, to minimize disruption of the bladder neck sphincter and the ejaculatory reflex mechanism; identifying and avoiding the ejaculatory duct orifices intraoperatively, reducing mechanical traction or thermal injury.

Notably, Alloussi et al. introduced a technique emphasizing the preservation of supramontanal and paracollicular tissues, reporting a 90.8% antegrade ejaculation preservation rate at 3 months in a prospective study of 89 patients, along with durable improvements in urinary flow and post-void residual volume during long-term follow-up. These findings further support the effectiveness of EP-TURP in achieving both functional and sexual outcomes (32).

In addition, a recent study compared a novel bladder neck and supramontanal sparing technique (ep-TUBRP) with conventional bipolar TURP (c-TUBRP) in 60 patients suffering from benign prostatic enlargement. Three months post-surgery, both groups exhibited improvements in urinary symptoms and flow rates. However, ep-TUBRP demonstrated a significantly better preservation of antegrade ejaculation (80% vs. 27%, P<0.001), with no severe adverse events reported. These findings suggest that bladder neck and supramontanal sparing techniques provide superior preservation of ejaculatory function while offering comparable relief of urinary symptoms (33).

Recent prospective cohort studies and retrospective analyses have demonstrated significant advantages of EP-TURP in preserving ejaculatory function—a prospective RCT reported that patients undergoing verumontanum-sparing TURP had an ejaculatory function preservation rate of 83% at 3 months postoperatively, compared to only 18.9% in the conventional TURP group (P<0.001) (34); another study showed that patients treated with a dual-preservation technique (preserving both the bladder neck and tissue above the verumontanum) had a 6-month postoperative ejaculation preservation rate of 88.89%, significantly higher than the 22.22% observed in the conventional TURP group (P<0.001) (35).

Although EP-TURP requires higher anatomical precision and surgical skill, it shows promising potential in achieving the dual goals of urinary symptom relief and sexual function preservation. As an optimized alternative to conventional TURP, it has been increasingly recommended in clinical guidelines, particularly for younger or sexually active men with a strong desire to maintain ejaculatory function (31).

PUL

PUL, exemplified by the UroLift® system, is an emerging minimally invasive technique for managing LUTS due to BPH. The procedure involves transurethral implantation of titanium sutures on both sides of the prostate to laterally retract hyperplastic prostatic tissue, thereby widening the urethral lumen and improving urinary flow. Unlike TURP, PUL does not involve tissue resection and specifically avoids injury to the midline urethral structures, bladder neck, and ejaculatory ducts, making it highly advantageous for ejaculatory function preservation (36).

Clinical studies have shown that PUL can significantly preserve ejaculation while effectively relieving urinary symptoms. In the multicenter, prospective, randomized BPH6 study, the PUL group demonstrated a 100% ejaculatory function preservation rate at 2 years postoperatively, compared to only 34% in the TURP group. Additionally, PUL patients showed improvement in ejaculatory function scores [Male Sexual Health Questionnaire-EjD (MSHQ-EjD)], whereas the TURP group experienced significant deterioration (37).

Data from Asian populations further support the efficacy and safety of PUL. A study in Korean patients showed that PUL improved urinary symptoms without any reported postoperative ejaculatory or erectile dysfunction (36). In a separate mid- to long-term follow-up study, younger men who underwent PUL maintained both ejaculatory and erectile functions during a mean follow-up period of 33.8 months, with no cases of retrograde ejaculation (38). These findings suggest that PUL is a suitable option for patients with smaller prostate volumes and high demands for sexual function preservation.

Further supporting evidence includes a prospective study of 20 young men undergoing UroLift with fertility preservation intent. Semen analyses before and 6 months after surgery showed no significant changes in quality parameters, and no retrograde ejaculation occurred during a 36-month follow-up. Uroflowmetry (Qmax) improved by 64.4%, IPSS decreased by 60%, and sexual function remained stable [International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF), MSHQ-EjD Short Form (MSHQ-EjD-SF)] (39).

In summary, PUL offers a non-resective, function-preserving approach to the treatment of LUTS secondary to BPH. Maximizing the preservation of ejaculatory function provides a valuable individualized therapeutic option for patients who prioritize sexual health. However, its effectiveness in large prostate glands has not been shown yet. Long-term studies are needed to evaluate the duration of the effect in comparison to other techniques.

PAE

PAE is a minimally invasive interventional treatment for BPH-related LUTS, in which selective embolization of the prostatic arteries induces ischemic shrinkage of the gland. Performed via transfemoral or transradial access under imaging guidance, PAE is non-surgical, associated with minimal bleeding, and allows rapid recovery. Importantly, by avoiding direct damage to the prostatic capsule and ejaculatory structures (such as the seminal vesicles and ejaculatory ducts), PAE is considered to have a relatively low impact on ejaculatory function (15).

Clinical studies have evaluated the ejaculatory outcomes of PAE from multiple perspectives. In one study involving patients with recurrent LUTS after previous minimally invasive therapies, PAE significantly improved symptom scores [IPSS, QoL, BPH impact index (BPH-II)] while maintaining stable sexual function. No significant decrease in the MSHQ-EjD score was observed, suggesting that ejaculatory function was preserved (40). A large prospective cohort study further confirmed that 88.5% of patients maintained ejaculatory function at 12 months post-procedure, with a gradual improvement in IIEF scores, supporting the sexual function-sparing potential of PAE (41).

Technological refinements have also enhanced functional outcomes. A comparative study of standard PAE and a modified technique incorporating penile collateral-sparing embolization (cPAE) found that cPAE reduced the risk of non-target embolization and further preserved both ejaculatory and erectile function without compromising treatment efficacy (42). However, caution remains warranted. For instance, a reanalysis by Müllhaupt et al. of two earlier randomized PAE trials reported that approximately 40% of patients experienced diminished ejaculation postoperatively. Although the complete loss of ejaculation was significantly lower than in the TURP group (16% vs. 52%), a reduction in ejaculatory quality was still common (43).

In summary, PAE offers a promising balance between symptomatic relief and functional preservation, particularly for ejaculatory function. It represents a viable alternative for patients who prefer to avoid conventional surgery or have concerns about sexual side effects. However, several limitations should be considered. PAE resulted in significantly more adverse events than TURP/open prostatectomy (OP) (41.6% vs. 30.4%). AUR after the procedures was significantly higher in the PAE group (9.4% vs. 2.0%). Concerns still exist about non-target embolization. Radiation exposure should be part of the counselling for patients considered for PAE. TURP resulted in significantly greater improvement in IPSS, Qmax, and prostate volume. The percentage of 21 patients who initially had PAE underwent TURP within two years. Further high-quality, long-term RCTs are needed to elucidate the mechanisms of functional preservation and the variability of individual outcomes.

Aquablation

Aquablation is an innovative surgical technique that combines real-time image guidance with robotic control to deliver high-velocity waterjet ablation, enabling precise resection of prostatic tissue without thermal injury. Intraoperative navigation is guided by transrectal ultrasound (TRUS) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and the robotic system autonomously controls the waterjet, minimizing the risk of heat-induced nerve damage. By sparing critical anatomical zones such as the bladder neck and verumontanum, Aquablation aims to enhance postoperative sexual function preservation (44).

The pivotal WATER trial, a prospective, multicenter, randomized controlled study, compared Aquablation with standard TURP in terms of efficacy and functional outcomes. The results demonstrated a significantly lower rate of retrograde ejaculation in the Aquablation group (10%) compared to the TURP group (36%) (P<0.001). Furthermore, the ejaculatory function preservation rate with Aquablation ranged from 60% to 90%, highlighting its superiority in protecting sexual function (45).

Subsequent studies further validated Aquablation’s dual advantages in symptom relief and functional preservation. In a study by Bhojani et al., which focused on patients with large prostates (80–150 mL), Aquablation led to a 17-point improvement in IPSS and a 12.5 mL/s increase in Qmax at 12-month follow-up. Ejaculatory function was preserved in 81% of patients, outperforming conventional surgical options (46).

Bach et al. conducted the first multicenter “all-comers” study, enrolling real-world patients without specific inclusion criteria. At 1 year postoperatively, patients showed an average IPSS improvement of 15.3 points and a Qmax increase of 11.8 mL/s. Aquablation also demonstrated a clear advantage over TURP in terms of ejaculatory function preservation, underscoring its broad applicability and neuroprotective properties (47). D’Agostino et al. explored a modified technique combining Aquablation with holmium laser coagulation in patients with medium-sized prostates. At 3-month follow-up, the ejaculatory function preservation rate was 72%, with no major complications reported, suggesting this strategy could optimize intraoperative hemostasis while maintaining functional outcomes, particularly beneficial for younger or sexually active patients (48).

Recently, Omidele et al. published the world’s largest single-center prospective real-world study (n=330), which included patients with a mean prostate volume of 110.3 mL and a maximum of 330 mL. Over a 4-year follow-up, Aquablation sustained substantial improvements in LUTS (IPSS reduced from 23.8 to 6.9, Qmax increased from 6.4 to 17.4 mL/s), while nearly complete preservation of ejaculatory function was achieved (99.6%), reaffirming Aquablation’s position as a leading function-preserving minimally invasive option (13).

In conclusion, Aquablation combines precise tissue removal, a non-thermal mechanism, and real-time visualization, delivering comparable or superior outcomes to TURP across a range of prostate volumes and anatomical complexities, while significantly reducing the risk of EjD. For men seeking to preserve sexual function, especially younger patients and those with large prostates, Aquablation offers a compelling, QoL-oriented treatment alternative. However, bleeding-related events were observed in 13.9% of cases, occurring either prior to discharge or within one month afterward. Blood transfusion and/or return to the theatre for fulguration may be required.

WVTT (e.g., Rezum)

WVTT, exemplified by the Rezum system, is a widely adopted minimally invasive thermal ablation technique for BPH. It delivers water vapor transurethrally into hyperplastic prostatic tissue, inducing cellular apoptosis and relieving lower urinary tract obstruction. Compared with traditional TURP, its targeted steam injection minimizes thermal spread to adjacent structures, aiding in the preservation of functional anatomy (15).

In terms of voiding improvement, multiple studies have consistently demonstrated that Rezum significantly alleviates BPH-related symptoms. Postoperative reductions in the IPSS range from 56% to 68%, while Qmax increases by 4.6–6.0 mL/s, and QoL scores improve by 61–67% (49,50). These therapeutic effects are maintained across patients with moderate-to-severe BPH, obstructive median lobe (OML), and large prostate volumes (>80 mL), with follow-up periods extending up to 5 years and retreatment rates remaining low (4.4–8%) (51-53).

Regarding ejaculatory function preservation, Rezum has shown promising protective potential. Most studies report a low incidence of postoperative EjD, with no significant deterioration observed over 5 years. Assessments using MSHQ-EjD and IIEF scales suggest that ejaculatory function is generally preserved or even mildly improved postoperatively (52-54). An RCT reported a 31% improvement in EjD scores from baseline among Rezum-treated patients, indicating potential for partial functional restoration (55). Nevertheless, approximately 30–40% of patients reported some degree of ejaculatory change, suggesting that while Rezum outperforms TURP in sexual function preservation, its performance may be intermediate compared with anatomically sparing techniques such as PUL and Aquablation (56).

In summary, WVTT offers a balanced approach between efficacy and minimal invasiveness, providing significant relief of LUTS while maintaining favorable outcomes in ejaculatory function preservation. Its simplicity and high safety profile make it especially suitable for younger or sexually active patients seeking to preserve sexual function. However, studies comparing convective radiofrequency WVTT to other active treatment forms, such as TURP, are limited. Randomized controlled trials against reference techniques are needed to confirm the initially promising clinical results and to evaluate the mid- and long-term efficacy and safety of water vapor energy treatment.

Application of robot-assisted and tissue-sparing techniques in ejaculatory function preservation

Robot-assisted simple prostatectomy (RASP), when combined with tissue-sparing strategies, is emerging as a promising surgical approach focused on functional preservation in the management of BPH. Utilizing three-dimensional (3D) visualization and precise robotic instrumentation, RASP enhances the ability to preserve critical anatomical structures such as the external urinary sphincter, verumontanum, and neurovascular bundles, factors integral to postoperative ejaculatory function (57).

Recent innovations in RASP techniques have demonstrated the potential for improved ejaculatory preservation without compromising voiding efficacy. For instance, the Madigan technique has shown superior outcomes compared to the traditional Freyer method. At 12 months postoperatively, patients undergoing the Madigan approach reported significantly higher scores on both the IIEF and MSHQ (IIEF: 20 vs. 17; MSHQ: 21 vs. 10; P<0.001), indicating enhanced preservation of sexual function (58). Although higher rates of postoperative residual urine and urinary tract infections were noted in the Madigan group, its overall advantages in functional outcomes and recovery were evident.

Another emerging technique, extraperitoneal urethra-sparing RASP (EUS-RASP), further advances anatomical preservation by maintaining the integrity of the prostatic urethra and verumontanum. In a prospective study of patients with normal preoperative ejaculation, 78.6% retained antegrade ejaculation postoperatively, with no significant decline in MSHQ-EjD-SF scores (preoperative: 12.8±2.0; postoperative: 12.3±2.1) (59). Additionally, this technique demonstrated favorable outcomes in terms of hospital stay, catheterization duration, and postoperative recovery, without increasing operative time or complication rates compared to conventional RASP.

In conclusion, the development of robot-assisted and tissue-sparing techniques offers new surgical pathways for preserving ejaculatory function in BPH patients. Compared to traditional open approaches (e.g., transvesical or transurethral Freyer prostatectomy), these minimally invasive options provide superior anatomical precision and functional outcomes, representing a significant advancement toward the dual goals of “minimally invasive surgery” and “functional preservation” in BPH management.

Exploration of emerging minimally invasive techniques and prospects for ejaculatory function preservation

With growing attention to the preservation of sexual function in the treatment of BPH, several emerging minimally invasive therapeutic technologies have gradually entered clinical research and application. These innovative treatments not only aim to alleviate LUTS but also emphasize the preservation of ejaculatory function, thereby better addressing patients’ QoL concerns.

The Optilume BPH Catheter System, which combines balloon dilation with a drug-coated technology, demonstrated stable and sustained improvement in urinary symptoms in the PINNACLE study. Two-year follow-up data showed a reduction in mean IPSS from 23.4 to 11.0 (a 50.8% improvement), with Qmax significantly increasing from 8.9 mL/s to 19.0 mL/s. Both QoL and symptom bother scores improved markedly. Notably, the intervention also showed promising results in sexual function: IIEF erectile function scores improved from 15.6 to 20.4, and ejaculatory difficulty and total ejaculatory function scores improved from baseline. Importantly, no adverse events such as retrograde ejaculation or anejaculation were reported. This makes Optilume one of the few minimally invasive interventions confirmed by RCTs to preserve ejaculatory function (60).

Another representative innovation is the temporary implantable nitinol device (iTind). This device is placed for 5–7 days and removed via cystoscopy, requiring no tissue resection and causing minimal disruption to critical anatomical structures such as the neurovascular bundles. In a study focusing on sexual function outcomes, patients treated with iTind showed no significant decline in erectile function (IIEF) or sexual satisfaction (Sexual Health Inventory for Men, SHIM) at 3- and 12-month follow-up, with some patients even showing a trend toward improvement. Importantly, no cases of EjD, retrograde ejaculation, or ejaculation-related complications were reported, indicating excellent potential for functional preservation (61).

The Urocross™ Expander System, another novel investigational device, showed promising efficacy in alleviating LUTS in the EXPANDER-1 trial, without significantly affecting ejaculatory function. Assessment using MSHQ-EjD and SHIM scales demonstrated stable scores in ejaculatory and erectile function at 6 months postoperatively, with no significant decline observed. Furthermore, no ejaculation abnormalities attributable to the procedure were reported, supporting the system’s functional preservation potential (62).

Overall, these emerging minimally invasive techniques have shown an encouraging balance between therapeutic efficacy and sexual function preservation. Although some of these technologies remain in early clinical phases and require larger sample sizes and longer follow-up for validation, their potential for preserving ejaculatory function has garnered considerable attention. In the future, these therapies may offer BPH patients novel treatment options that integrate efficacy, minimal invasiveness, and preservation of sexual function.

Value and limitations of this review

Strengths

This review systematically integrates anatomical mechanisms, technical approaches, and clinical evidence concerning the preservation of ejaculatory function and voiding efficacy across major minimally invasive surgical techniques for BPH. It encompasses representative procedures such as modified TURP, PUL, PAE, and Aquablation, with a broad literature scope. By combining anatomical and physiological foundations with evidence-based research, this review highlights the importance of patient-centered preferences and function-preserving concepts, offering substantial value for clinical practice. Furthermore, it emphasizes the importance of achieving dual objectives, voiding improvement and sexual function preservation, in the context of technological advancement, thereby providing both theoretical support and practical basis for individualized surgical strategies.

Limitations

As a narrative review, this study may be subject to selection bias and does not include a systematic review or meta-analysis. While some procedures, such as PAE and Aquablation, show promising potential in preserving ejaculatory function and improving voiding outcomes, high-quality, large-scale, long-term RCTs remain scarce, limiting the generalizability of current conclusions. Additionally, most studies rely on subjective questionnaires to assess ejaculatory function, which may introduce bias. Future research should incorporate more objective and standardized tools to enhance the reliability of functional assessments.

Advancing toward optimal treatment strategies: future research directions and clinical implications

With increasing patient attention to QoL, ejaculatory function preservation is becoming a key consideration in surgical decision-making for BPH. Future research and clinical practice should focus on the following directions to achieve the dual goals of urinary relief and sexual function preservation.

Strengthening the evidence base

There is an urgent need for long-term, multicenter, large-sample RCTs to evaluate the efficacy of various techniques in terms of ejaculatory function preservation rates, sexual satisfaction, and overall QoL. In parallel, efforts should be made to establish standardized, validated instruments for assessing sexual function outcomes to improve comparability and generalizability across studies.

Promoting function-oriented minimally invasive techniques

Innovation and refinement of surgical techniques should prioritize the preservation of anatomical structures such as the verumontanum, bladder neck, and ejaculatory ducts. Particular attention should be given to enhancing procedures like Aquablation, PUL, and EP-TURP to maximize ejaculatory function preservation without compromising voiding efficacy, advancing BPH treatment into a function-oriented era.

Incorporating patient preferences into surgical decision-making

Shared decision-making should be emphasized, especially for younger, sexually active, or fertility-concerned patients. For these individuals, ejaculatory function preservation is not merely a QoL or cosmetic concern but may directly relate to maintaining reproductive potential. Ejaculation-preserving procedures, such as EP-TURP, PUL, Rezum, Aquablation, and RASP, should be prioritized to prevent postoperative QoL decline. Patient education and expectation management are also essential aspects of clinical communication.

Developing personalized surgical decision tools

Future research should focus on creating precise preoperative evaluation models that integrate pelvic anatomical imaging, hormonal profiles, and baseline sexual function. Such tools could help predict the impact of different procedures (e.g., PAE, Aquablation, EP-TURP, RASP) on ejaculatory function and support individualized, precision-based urologic interventions.

Conclusions

As the therapeutic goals for BPH evolve from mere relief of LUTS toward comprehensive functional preservation, particularly of ejaculatory function, there has been growing clinical interest in minimally invasive surgical techniques. This review has summarized the evidence and technical characteristics of mainstream BPH procedures, including PUL, PAE, Rezum, Aquablation, and robot-assisted approaches (e.g., RASP), demonstrating that these modalities not only alleviate urinary symptoms but also reduce the risk of EjD typically associated with traditional TURP.

Significant differences in sexual function outcomes have been observed across techniques. PUL and PAE, by minimizing interference with midline structures and preserving the verumontanum and prostatic urethra, offer high rates of ejaculatory function preservation. Although Rezum and Aquablation involve tissue ablation or resection, their precise surgical targeting and protective strategies around the verumontanum also yield favorable functional outcomes. Modified RASP techniques further explore the feasibility of preserving the verumontanum and urethra, offering an effective and function-preserving option for patients with large prostates. These procedures embody the principle of preserving both anatomical structure and physiological function, aligning with the modern urological emphasis on QoL.

Nevertheless, current research still has several limitations: many studies have small sample sizes, short follow-up durations, and use non-standardized assessment tools for sexual function. There is a clear need for large-scale, long-term, standardized comparative studies across surgical techniques. Future research should focus on multicenter RCTs using standardized and validated tools, such as IIEF, MSHQ-EjD-SF, and semen analysis, to assess both objective ejaculatory function and subjective patient satisfaction, with follow-up periods of at least 12–24 months. Trials should also stratify patients by age, prostate size, and baseline sexual activity to better guide clinical decision-making.

In clinical practice, individualized treatment strategies are particularly important. Urologists should engage in thorough preoperative discussions with patients based on anatomical characteristics, symptom severity, and desire for sexual function preservation. For younger or more sexually active patients, function-preserving minimally invasive interventions can significantly improve postoperative satisfaction and reflect a shift toward a more precise and patient-centered approach to BPH management.
