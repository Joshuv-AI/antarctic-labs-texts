Diabetic retinopathy screening using machine learning: a systematic review
Authors: Fitsum Mesfin Dejene, Taye Girma Debelee, Friedhelm Schwenker, Yehualashet Megersa Ayano, Degaga Wolde Feyisa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Diabetic retinopathy (DR) stands as a leading cause of global blindness. Early identification and prompt treatment are crucial in preventing vision impairment caused by diabetic retinopathy (DR). Manual screening of retinal fundus images is challenging and time-consuming. Additionally, there is a significant gap between the number of DR patients and the number of medical experts. Integrating machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) techniques is becoming a viable alternative to traditional DR screening techniques. However, the absence of a retinal dataset with standardized quality, the complexity of DL models, and the need for high computational resources are challenges. Therefore, in this study, we studied and analyzed the research landscape in integrating ML techniques in DR screening. In this regard, our work contributes significantly in several aspects. Initially, we identify and characterize images of the retinal fundus that are readily available. Then, we discuss commonly used preprocessing techniques in DR screening. In addition, we analyze the progress of ML techniques in DR screening. Lastly, we discussed existing challenges and showed future directions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42490-025-00098-0.

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy is a medical condition in which damage occurs to the retina due to diabetes. According to Antonetti et al. [1], DR is the main cause of blindness in people between the ages of 20 and 74. More than half of all individuals with diabetes (49.7%) were projected to be not diagnosed, according to statistics [2]. Globally, it is estimated that 34.6% of diabetic patients have DR, with 10.2% of those individuals experiencing visual impairment [3].

As a result, DR is a potentially dangerous eye condition that affects people and requires early diagnosis. Currently, retinal fundus images are manually scanned by ophthalmologists to determine if DR is present in patients and to grade the severity of the disease. Additionally, in Sub-Saharan African countries 2.5 ophthalmologists per million people [5]. This highlights the gap between the limited number of ophthalmologists and the millions affected by DR. Additionally, manually screening retinal fundus images for DR diagnosis is time-consuming and tedious. Patients with DR, therefore, do not receive timely medical attention.

Nevertheless, the American Academy of Ophthalmology advises that individuals with type 1 diabetes should have their first eye exam five years after the disease first appears, and patients with type 2 diabetes should have an eye exam at the time of diagnosis [6]. However, the majority of diabetic individuals wait until their retinopathy gets severe enough to affect their ability to see clearly before undergoing a DR examination.

DR is a progressive eye disease characterized by various retinal lesions, which indicate the severity of the condition [7]. These lesions can be broadly classified into microaneurysms (MA), hemorrhages (HM), and soft and hard exudates (EX). Microaneurysms, the earliest signs of DR, appear as tiny, round red spots on the retina, typically smaller than 125 micrometers with well-defined boundaries, resulting from weakened vessel walls [8]. Hemorrhages, in contrast, are larger red patches with irregular edges and diameters exceeding 125 micrometers, categorized into flame-shaped (superficial) and blot-shaped (deeper) forms [9]. Hard exudates, caused by blood leakage, manifest as yellow patches with clear borders, whereas soft exudates, resulting from nerve fiber swelling, appear as white ovals [10]. Hemorrhages and microaneurysms are collectively referred to as red lesions, while exudates form white lesions [8].

The clinical stages of DR range from mild, moderate, and severe non-proliferative DR to proliferative DR, with or without macular edema [11]. Ophthalmologists rely on these lesions for screening, localization, classification, grading, and monitoring of disease progression. Recent advancements in AI and DL systems have significantly enhanced DR diagnosis, offering precise, efficient, and cost-effective solutions to screen individuals with diabetes while supporting accurate severity-level prediction [13]. This systematic review aims to analyze the existing literature, focusing on studies that have significantly contributed to the development of automated methods for DR screening. It highlights both the strengths and limitations of machine learning techniques, particularly in terms of computational complexity. This comprehensive review is intended to provide valuable insights for researchers looking to explore and advance the field of DR screening. Related works

The quest to find better DR screening techniques that can help physicians in DR has been an active research area. As a result, several systematic reviews have been completed to foster research in the field and recap techniques used in DR screening.

Ishtiaq, et al. [14] reviewed papers focused on DR grading and classifications. They identified the research gaps and future directions for research communities. Additionally, they provided an analysis of the datasets, pre-processing techniques employed, and comprehensive narrations on ML models used in the research papers. Lakshminarayanan, et al. [15], articulated the publicly available dataset and accessed the requested dataset. Furthermore, they quantified the performance of ML models. In their review, Nadeem, et al. [16] have reviewed the development of DL models in the domain of DR segmentation, detection, and classification. Although Tumi Karason, et al. [17] assessed the efficacy of DR grading using the seven standard fields, questioning its significance and optimality. They explored whether comparable results could be attained through alternative methods, including the smaller field of view (FOV) or ultra-wide-field imaging.

Islam, et al. [18] have performed a systematic review to assess the performance of DL models for automated detection of DR in the retinal fundus image. On the other hand, Ademola, et al. [19] have reviewed literature in which their main objective is to build a model that is used to segment and classify DR and provide valuable information, limitations, observations, and future work. Although Sebastian et al. [20] quantified the performance of DL models and identified the best-performing models.

Additionally, Tsiknakis et al. [21] cover various DL approaches for DR detection and classification, including analyses of model performance. However, the review does not discuss the computational complexity of each paper. Selvachandran et al. [22] comprehensively cover research articles in the domain of DR, providing in-depth analyses of models like CNN and ANN. The review also discusses AI trends in the field of DR but lacks a comparative analysis of the models. In contrast, Alyoubi et al. [23] provide a thorough overview of current research articles, including a detailed examination of available datasets for DR and identification of challenges in the research area. However, they do not discuss model complexity or the resource intensiveness of the models. Furthermore, Chu et al. [24] review only 15 research articles, focusing on a few prominent algorithms. Despite identifying barriers, the paper lacks sufficient actionable recommendations. Grzybowski et al. [25] provides an overview of various AI technologies for DR, including several systems used, but it lacks a comparison and in-depth analysis of the methodologies employed in each research article. On the other hand, Wewetzer et al. [26] reviewed only 10 research articles, whereas Wu et al. [27] demonstrates significant strengths, including a comprehensive review of 60 studies and adherence to systematic methodologies. However, it fails to discuss the computational complexity of each proposed model in the research articles in greater depth.

Our systematic review bridges the gaps in prior review articles by delivering a comprehensive analysis and comparison of recent studies related to DR. Unlike previous works, our systematic review discusses the computational complexity of the reviewed studies in terms of model parameter count, providing insights into their resource intensiveness. Additionally, we identify key challenges, such as dataset limitations, artifacts, preprocessing techniques, and lack of model robustness, while offering actionable recommendations to address these issues.

Methodology

This section outlines the method used to review DR screening. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines are employed to define the research questions, databases, and search terms necessary for conducting the systematic review. PRISMA serves as a framework for the literature review process [28].

Search strategies

The selection of the database and search strings is tailored to address the research questions highlighted in Sect. 3.1. The search primarily focused on the identification of relevant literature from key databases such as Google Scholar, PubMed, IEEEXplore, ScienceDirect, MDPI, and Scopus.

In this review, we conducted a comprehensive literature search using specific search strings across multiple databases to gather relevant studies related to the application of ML and DL techniques in DR. The search strings were carefully designed to target studies focused on the detection, classification, screening, segmentation, and grading of DR, with an emphasis on imaging techniques such as funduscopic and ophthalmoscopic methods. The strings used in Google Scholar and Scopus database are the following: [(“Machine Learning” OR “Deep Learning” OR “CNN” OR “Convolutional Neural Network” OR “Neural Network”) AND “Diabetic Retinopathy” AND (“Detection” OR “Classification” OR “Screening” OR “Segmentation” OR “Grading”) AND (“Funduscopic” OR “Ophthalmoscopic”)], for PubMed, IEEE Xplore, and MDPI is: [(“DNN” OR “CNN”) AND “Diabetic Retinopathy” AND (“Detection” OR “Classification” OR “Screening” OR “Segmentation” OR “Grading”)], for ScienceDirect is: [(“Machine Learning” OR “Deep Learning” OR “CNN” OR “Convolutional Neural Network”) AND “Diabetic Retinopathy” AND (“Funduscopic” OR “Ophthalmoscopic”)], for ACM Digital Library is: [(“Machine Learning” OR “Deep Learning” OR “CNN” OR “Convolutional Neural Network” OR “Neural Network”) AND “Diabetic Retinopathy” AND (“Detection” OR “Classification” OR “Screening” OR “Segmentation” OR “Grading”)]. Diabetic retinopathy datasets

DL algorithms rely extensively on large datasets to understand patterns in images that depict abnormalities and normal conditions. Training ML or DL models with substantial, high-quality datasets enhances model performance, emphasizing the significance of both image size and quality. The common retinal fundus datasets used for DR screening are APTOS 2019 [29], MESSIDOR [30], MESSIDOR 2 [31], DIARETDB0 [32], DIARETDB1 [33], DDR [34], EyePACS [35], DRIVE [36], STARE [37], CHASE DB1 [38], and IDRiD [39]. Additionally, extensive and various aspects of fundus imaging technology and its applications in healthcare as been covered [40].

The APTOS 2019 dataset [29], available on Kaggle, includes 5,590 retinal fundus images with five severity levels (0, 1, 2, 3, 4) to detect DR.

The MESSIDOR dataset [30] contains 1,200 color posterior pole eye fundus photos, captured using a Topcon TRC NW6 non-mydriatic retinograph and a 3CCD camera across three ophthalmologic departments. The photos, with 8 bits per color plane, include 800 taken with pupil dilation and 400 without, primarily for DR grading

The DIARETDB0/1 dataset [32, 33] contains images captured with various digital fundus cameras with a 50-degree FOV. The camera settings and photometric information are unknown, leading to potential variance in image quality due to factors like noise, distortion, and chromatic aberrations, making it challenging to standardize retinopathy findings.

The DDR dataset [34] contains 13,673 color fundus images collected from 147 hospitals across 23 provinces in China. The images, captured using 42 different fundus camera models with a 45-degree FOV, include popular models like Topcon D700, Topcon TRC NW48, Nikon D5200, and Canon CR2. All images have been desensitized to ensure privacy and utility.

The EyePACS [35] dataset includes pictures taken with different camera models, which leads to differences in how left and right are presented visually. There are five possible ratings for each image: 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4. A few pictures show the retina anatomically (the optic nerve in the right eye is shown on the right, and the macula on the left). Conversely, some replicate an inverted microscope-condensing lens image, which is similar to a live ocular examination.

The DRIVE dataset [36] consists of 40 retinal fundus images, 7 of which show abnormal pathology, captured using a Canon CR5 3CCD camera with a 45-degree FOV. Each image has a resolution of 584\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}565 pixels and 8 bits per color channel. The dataset is divided into a training set and a testing set, each with 20 images. The training set includes a circular FOV mask and expert manual segmentation, while the testing set features two manual segmentations, with the first serving as the benchmark for performance evaluation.

A STARE [37] dataset for segmenting retinal vessels is called STARE (Structured Analysis of the Retina). It includes 20 retinal fundus images with equal size (700\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}605 pixels).

The CHASE_DB1 dataset [38] is a retinal vascular segmentation dataset consisting of 28 color retina images, each with a resolution of 999\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}960 pixels. These images are taken from the left and right eyes of fourteen school-age children. Each image has been annotated by two independent domain experts.

The IDRiD dataset [39] contains 516 fundus images, categorized into normal retinal images and those showing signs of diabetic macular edema (DME) or DR. It includes ground truth data for DR and DME labels, fovea center and optic disc coordinates, pixel-level annotations of lesions, and disease severity grading.

The MAPLES-DR dataset [41] includes 198 fundus images from the MESSIDOR database, enhanced with pixel-wise annotations for 10 anatomical and pathological biomarkers related to DR and ME. It aims to improve the explainability of screening models by providing detailed segmentation maps and diagnostic labels.

The E-Ophtha dataset [42] serves as a benchmark for evaluating automated methods for detecting exudates and microaneurysms. The dataset consists of two subsets: E-Ophtha-EX, which contains 47 images with exudates and 35 images without lesions, and E-Ophtha-MA, which includes 148 images of microaneurysms or small hemorrhages and 233 images without lesions.

The ROC Dataset [43] was developed for the Retinopathy Online Challenge to identify microaneurysms in retinal images. It includes 100 images, split into a training set and a testing set of 50 images each. The training set provides reference locations for microaneurysms. Images were captured using Topcon NW 100, Topcon NW 200, or Canon CR5-45 NM cameras and saved in JPEG format.

Retinal fundus image artifacts and preprocessing techniques

This section discusses the most common artifacts in retinal fundus images and the pre-processing techniques employed in research papers we have reviewed. Image pre-processing involves identifying the artifacts, removing noise or variations, enhancing the quality of the retinal fundus image, and making it suitable for the model.

Artifacts in retinal fundus image

Retinal fundus images are crucial for the diagnosis of various eye diseases, including diabetic retinopathy, glaucoma, and macular degeneration. However, artifacts often degrade the quality of these images, making automated analysis more challenging. Noise can arise from several sources, such as the image acquisition process, environmental factors, or limitations of the imaging system.

A medical image is considered good if all the important structures are clearly visible [44]. In a healthy eye fundus image, the main parts that can be seen are the optic disc, macula, and blood vessels, as shown in Figure 1. There are several things that can reduce the quality of a retinal image: dust and dirt on the camera, dusty camera lenses, eye blinking, and eyelashes blocking the view [44]. Common problems caused by these factors include uneven lighting, low contrast, blurriness, and light flares, as shown in Fig. 4. Dust and dirt create light spots scattered across the image, as seen in Fig. 4a. A full eye blink results in a very poor-quality image, as shown in Fig. 4b. Incorrect gamma or flash settings can cause color issues like overexposure, underexposure, or uneven lighting, as shown in Fig. 4.Fig. 4Samples of low-quality fundus images with different distortions [44] a) Light flares, b) Total eye blink, c) Uneven illumination, d) Overexposed, e) Underexposed, f) Blur

Additionally, Fundus tessellation refers to the increased visibility of large choroidal vessels due to a thinner retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and choroid [45]. fundus tessellation itself is not noise (artifact), but if not accounted for properly, it can be challenging for AI models, resulting in misclassifications. Leopard print is shown in Fig. 5a. Furthermore, improper cleaning of the camera lenses can create circular light rings in the center of the image, as shown in Fig. 5b. Additionally, there are various types of noises such as Speckle noise, salt and pepper noise, Brownian noise, Gaussian noise, and Poisson noise [46]. Speckle noise is a granular noise that appears due to interference from scattered light or the imaging system’s imperfections. In retinal imaging, speckle noise can distort the texture and details of the retinal layers [47]. It is especially challenging in images of the macula, where fine textures are crucial for detecting early signs of disease. Speckle noise can be mitigated using curvelet-based sigmoid mapping of histogram equalization [47] as shown in Fig. 6.Fig. 6Speckle noise analysis [47]; (a) retinal image with speckle noise, (b) denoised retinal fundus image with curvelet-based sigmoid mapping of histogram equalization

Furthermore, salt-and-pepper noise is characterized by random pixels that are either black or white, resembling salt and pepper particles [48]. This type of noise is common in image transmission processes or due to sensor defects [48]. It can obscure small structures, such as blood vessels or microaneurysms, which are critical for the diagnosis of diabetic retinopathy [49]. Image blurring is caused by factors like motion, focus issues, lens quality, low light, optical aberrations, sensor limitations, subject movement, or atmospheric conditions [50]. This type of noise can significantly affect the accuracy of retinal image analysis, making it difficult to identify small structures or subtle changes in the retina. Blurring can be reduced using Wiener filtering and techniques proposed by the study [50]. Color and geometric transformation

In color space transformation and channel operation, techniques such as green channel extraction and grayscale conversion have been utilized in various research studies. Green channels are often used to enhance contrast or brightness and reduce noise in images of the retinal fundus [51–61]. Although grayscale conversion, which eliminates color information, is used to reduce computational overhead [62–64]. Furthermore, in the geometric transformation of retinal fundus images, cropping and resizing are two basic techniques employed in the preprocessing stage. Cropping removes irrelevant pixels from the images and is used to extract regions of interest. Resizing adjusts the size of the image to the desired resolution, ensuring compatibility with the input size of the model. Geometric transformation preprocessing techniques have been utilized in the majority of research papers we have reviewed.

Image intensity transformation and noise reduction

In image preprocessing techniques, intensity transformation is a step in which the intensity values of pixels are adjusted to improve useful features. Gamma correction, adaptive histogram equalization (AHE) [51, 65], normalization, and standardization are a few intensity-enhancing techniques employed for the image of the retinal fundus. Gamma correction is used to adjust the contrast and brightness of the image and to improve the visibility of the features in the image. On the other hand, normalization is often used to scale pixel values into the standard range [0,1], while standardization is to center and scale pixel values so that they have zero mean and unit variance. Additionally, AHE is an image processing technique that enhances local contrast by independently applying histogram equalization to small regions within an image. Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) is an image-processing technique that extends AHE by limiting the amplification of contrast in regions with high local contrast to prevent over-enhancement and the introduction of artifacts [66]. CLAHE is one of the frontiers that has mainly utilized preprocessing techniques in the analysis of retinal fundus images [52, 54, 57, 64, 67–77].

In noise reduction techniques, Gaussian filtering [78–80], median filtering [71], and discrete wavelet transform (DWT) [67] are a few of the employed techniques in retinal fundus image analysis. Gaussian filtering is a noise reduction method in which the image is convolved with a Gaussian kernel, blurring it to reduce Gaussian noise while preserving important details [81].

Although median filtering is an image noise reduction method where each pixel’s value is replaced by the median value of its neighboring pixels [82]. It’s effective for removing salt-and-pepper noise.

DWT is also a powerful tool in image processing for multi-resolution analysis and denoising [83]. It is used to capture both coarse and fine-grained details effectively. It decomposes an image into approximation and detail coefficients across different scales or resolutions. The DWT operates by filtering the signal or image with a series of high-pass and low-pass filters, followed by downsampling to obtain coefficients at different scales. The process can be repeated iteratively to achieve multi-level decomposition.

Diabetic retinopathy screening

Diabetic retinopathy can be classified into five distinct stages, according to the International Clinical Diabetic Retinopathy (ICDR) [11] classification system: mild nonproliferative DR (NPDR), moderate NPDR, severe NPDR, and proliferative DR (PDR). The severity levels of DR are depicted in Fig. 9. According to the study [11], mild NPDR is characterized by the presence of MA only. Moderate DR is defined by the presence of more than just MA but not meeting the criteria for severe DR [11]. Severe DR involves more than 20 intraretinal HM in each of the four quadrants, definite venous beading in two or more quadrants, or prominent intraretinal microvascular abnormalities in at least one quadrant, with no signs of proliferative retinopathy [11]. Proliferative DR is characterized by neovascularization, abnormal blood vessel growth, or vitreous/pre-retinal hemorrhage [11].Fig. 9Severity levels of DR [29]: (a) No DR, (b) Mild NPDR, (c) Moderate NPDR, (d) Severe NPDR, (e) PDR

Severity levels of DR [29]: (a) No DR, (b) Mild NPDR, (c) Moderate NPDR, (d) Severe NPDR, (e) PDR

The research carried out on DR screening uses common CNN operations. Despite utilizing the same CNN operations, studies have introduced various CNN architectures by integrating them with different supporting techniques like attention modules (residual, spatial, and channel attention) and classical ML, mainly for DR grading. The CNN architecture depicted in Fig. 10 is a powerful tool for automated DR screening, leveraging its typical components to process retinal images effectively. The convolutional layers serve as the primary feature extraction mechanism, identifying local patterns such as edges, textures, and lesions by applying learnable filters across the image. Max pooling layers reduce the spatial dimensions of feature maps, retaining the most significant features while minimizing computational complexity, whereas min pooling captures the smallest activations, which can be useful for identifying subtle variations in features like microaneurysms. The fully connected layers integrate the extracted features to perform high-level reasoning, effectively mapping them to DR classifications. Additional components, such as activation functions like ReLU, introduce non-linearities to enable the model to capture complex patterns, and feature selection mechanisms prioritize the most relevant features for classification. This architecture systematically identifies and classifies retinal lesions, facilitating precise and efficient DR screening.Fig. 10Commonly used CNN architectures for diabetic retinopathy screening

Diabetic retinopathy grading

The objective of DR grading is to categorize retinal fundus images into different grades. Although most of the studies used five grades, some used three or four grades, and even seven grades. The literature on DR grading is discussed in the following subsection.

Custom CNN models

Custom CNN models are built entirely from scratch or adapted from pre-trained models with significant modifications. Studies [68, 96–98] employed custom-designed CNNs.

The integration of a custom-designed CNN model with techniques such as active DL [99] and siamese networks [100] presents a robust solution for image classification tasks, particularly in scenarios with limited data availability. Active DL, as demonstrated in the context of DR classification [98], enables the algorithm to actively select informative patches from retinal fundus image grids, optimizing model performance. Attention mechanisms are used for enhancing the effectiveness of DR classification, especially considering the tiny nature of DR lesions. Using residual attention modules and the spatial pyramid system helps extract relevant features from retinal fundus images [103]. Furthermore, employing a cascaded network that incorporates spatial and loss-based multibranch attention enables the model to capture the information of the lesion [97]. To extract even more informative features, techniques such as dense block, asymmetric convolution branches, channel attention, and auxiliary classifiers are utilized [79]. In contrast, a custom CNN model inspired by attention-based bidirectional long- and short-term memory (ABiLSTM) and the Komodo MLIPIR algorithm (KMA) showcases computational efficiency, minimizing processing time while achieving better performance [105, 107] as illustrated in Table 6. The integration of attention mechanisms and model architectures [105] underscores significant contributions in DR classification, which improve both performance and computational efficiency as indicated in Table 6.

Global channel attention(GCA)-EfficientNet (G-ENet), a lightweight model for DR classification that integrates a GCA mechanism into EfficientNetB0 [108]. The GCA mechanism combines Global Max Pooling with one-dimensional convolution and a fully connected layer to improve spatial feature embedding and global cross-channel interaction [108]. Replacing MBConv modules with GBConv reduces computational complexity while maintaining high accuracy, with G-ENet outperforming models like ResNet-50 and DenseNet-121 [108]. On the other hand, SE-MIDNet, a DL framework for DR classification that integrates an improved Inception module, dense connections, and a Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) module [109]. The improved Inception module enhances multi-scale feature extraction by utilizing smaller convolution kernels, which reduces computational complexity while maintaining effective feature representation [109]. Dense connections facilitate feature reuse and mitigate gradient vanishing, enhancing the model’s efficiency [109]. The SE module dynamically weights feature channels, allowing the network to prioritize significant features [109]. The end-to-end approach minimizes preprocessing complexity, making SE-MIDNet computationally efficient and suitable for real-world clinical applications in automated diabetic retinopathy classification [109].

Siamese Convolutional Neural Network (SCNN) that analyzes pairs of eye fundus images to measure their similarity has been employed [110]. This architecture, which shares weights across two identical networks, enables effective few-shot learning, allowing the model to perform well with limited labeled data [110]. Furthermore, SCNN is robust against data imbalances, focusing on anomaly detection to identify early signs of DR [110].

In the classification of DR, a hybrid approach that combines a custom-designed CNN for feature extraction and ML classifiers for classification has shown notable efficacy [68, 96]. The extreme learning machine (ELM) is used after feature extraction using parallel convolutional layers [68]. As shown in the Table 6, parallel CNN with ELM shows better performance on the APTOS 2019 dataset. Although multi-path CNN is used for extracting both local and global features, followed by classification using SVM, random forest, and J48 algorithms [96]. Remarkably, the J48 algorithm outperforms SVM and random forests. This integration of ML classifiers on parallel and multi-path CNN architectures not only yields promising performance but also offers reduced computational complexity, highlighting its potential for robust and efficient DR classification [68, 96].

Furthermore, graph neural networks (GNN) are used to capture complex relationships within deep features, thereby learning robust representations of graph-structured data [111]. These networks have been seamlessly integrated into CNN architectures to capture the relationships among features extracted from retinal fundus images through CNNs [86]. However, utilizing GNNs requires significant computational resources and extensive datasets to effectively learn the dependencies, particularly evident in the context of analyzing retinal fundus images [86].

Although a custom-designed CNN has been evaluated with different wavelet transformation techniques, such as Haar, Daubechies, and Symlet wavelet transformations, across seven grades of DR [112], the study demonstrates that Symlet wavelet transformation pooling significantly enhances DR classification. On the other hand, a deep CNN model that resembles the DenseNet-121 model, includes three dense blocks, transition layers, global average pooling, and dropout layers to enhance feature reuse, reduce parameters, and prevent overfitting, has been evaluated and shown a promising result [102]. Despite the computational complexity, the proposed model performs better on the EyePACS dataset [102] as shown in Table 6.

On the other hand, study [104] relied on handcrafted features rather than DL-based automatic feature extraction. Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) was used to capture texture patterns in retinal images, while histogram-based features provided intensity distribution information [104]. By feeding these extracted features into the XGBoost classifier, the study aimed to achieve efficient and interpretable classification of DR severity. The results indicated that traditional feature engineering, when combined with a powerful ensemble learning algorithm like XGBoost, could yield competitive performance compared to deep learning models, especially in scenarios with limited training data or computational resources [104].

In conclusion, the custom CNN models for DR grading [98, 101] showcase diverse methodologies and approaches for boosting the performance of the model and building a lighter model. Although some models achieve remarkable accuracy rates, challenges persist in terms of computational demand and model complexity.

Transfer learning

In ML, transfer learning is a technique whereby knowledge gained from one task is applied to improve performance on a related task. Similarly, a pre-trained CNN model is used in several DR grading studies [56, 70, 72, 113–129].

As the lesion of DR is highly inseparable, studies [63, 130, 131] emphasize enhancing the techniques used to improve the quality of images. While resizing the retinal fundus image, important features or lesions might vanish. To ensure that some important features remain intact, downscaling techniques are used to selectively retain essential features, preventing their potential loss [131]. Nevertheless, it is important to note that not all information is preserved through the downscaling process. On the other hand, studies [63, 130] re-categorized inseparable classes of DR. This not only deals with the complexities of the data but also makes things less complicated for the model. The thoughtful approach to both image preparation and model design shows a careful way to handle the challenges in DR grading [63, 130].

The VGG model was used for the classification of DR in various studies [70, 113–115, 132, 133] as it is a powerful classification tool. Densely connected networks optimized using hyperparameterization for DR grading [126–128]. Although MobileNet [141] would be better than DenseNet [126–128] in a resource-constrained environment like a mobile device.

Diabetic retinopathy segmentation

Segmentation techniques are essential for identifying the region of interest in DR screening. The regions of interest may include the optic disc, fovea, and blood vessels, lesions such as microaneurysms, hemorrhages, soft exudates, and hard exudates in the retinal fundus image. Manual segmentation techniques are laborious, resource-intensive, and time-consuming. However, segmentation can be easily carried out using ML models [57–60, 64, 65, 75–77, 161–166].

Blood vessel segmentation

One of the techniques employed in DR segmentation is abnormal blood vessel segmentation, as abnormalities in blood vessels might be caused by DR. Several studies used blood vessel segmentation techniques to identify the region of interest for blood vessel abnormalities.

Retinal vascular tortuosity is an excessive bending and twisting of the blood vessels in the retina that is associated with numerous health conditions [167]. The paper [167] used UNet-based automated assessment of retinal vascular tortuosity using color fundus images. The model extracts the arteriovenous tree, optic disc, and fovea using specialized neural networks and calculates tortuosity scores by incorporating anatomical factors such as vessel caliber, artery-vein distinction, and distances to key structures [167].

The study [168] focuses on diagnosing hypertensive retinopathy using CCN with an encoder-decoder architecture for artery-vein classification. The model employs a multi-loss function to optimize both pixel-wise and segment-wise classification, enhancing accuracy [168]. On the other hand, study [169] introduces an improved method for extracting vessel segments from retinal fundus images, addressing issues in traditional skeletonization techniques. Using morphological operations and optimized skeletonization, the method enhances vessel continuity, preventing unnecessary segment breaks [169].

Furthermore, study [170] aims to develop an automated system for detecting and grading hypertensive retinopathy using retinal vessel morphometry, specifically arteriovenous ratio (AVR) and vessel tortuosity severity. The proposed model integrates a CNN for artery-vein classification, an iterative AVR calculation method, and machine learning classifiers (Decision Tree and Rotation Forest) for tortuosity grading [170].

Classical ML models are used with a feature extractor, as mentioned in the studies [53, 64]. Average filtering and Otsu thresholding [171] are employed to obtain the binary form of the image, and overlaying techniques are used on the complete image and original RGB images. Additionally, they employed a coherence-enhancing energy-based regularization approach on the green channel of the retinal fundus image to segment blood vessels [53]. They also utilized Canny anisotropic diffusion [172], morphological transformations [173] for optic disc segmentation, and the Harris hawk optimization algorithm [174] for feature optimization.

The UNet model, characterized by its U-shaped design and utilization of encoding and decoding paths with skip connections, has demonstrated considerable effectiveness across segmentation tasks in image processing [175]. UNet was used with significant modifications to make the model lighter and get a better-performing segmentation model [161] that is robust enough, as shown in Table 11. The convolutional block in UNet was reduced from four to three in both the encoder and decoder layers [161]. On the other hand, multipath recurrent UNet is used to enhance discrimination in retinal fundus images and address pathological region issues [162]. The approach combines convolutional and recurrent neural networks, leveraging recurrent networks for temporal feature multipathing [162]. In contrast, the dense residual path UNet is trained on the retinal fundus image of patch 64 × 64 [176]. Lesion-based segmentation

The lesion-based segmentation technique is among the most widely used segmentation mechanisms, as it has various lesions that could be detected in each stage of DR. Those lesions are microaneurysms, hemorrhages, soft exudates, and hard exudates. Studies [75–77] used lesion-based segmentation to identify the type of lesion spotted on the retinal fundus image.

Multi-scale and multi-level feature fusion networks inspired by UNet and incorporating VGG-16 in the encoder enhance the feature learning and utilize transposed convolutional upsampling in the decoder to enhance the decoding capability of the model [178]. Although study [179] integrates advanced DL architectures for DR lesion segmentation and classification, leveraging Xception with DeepLabv3 for segmentation and EfficientNet-b0 with SqueezeNet for feature extraction [179]. The extracted features are optimally selected using the Marine Predators Algorithm (MPA), which enhances classification accuracy by reducing dimensionality and preserving discriminative information [179]. The selected features are then fed into KNN and NN classifiers to categorize DR severity into four grades. This approach is significant because it combines powerful pretrained models with an efficient feature selection mechanism, optimizing computational complexity while improving diagnostic accuracy, crucial for automated diabetic retinopathy screening [180].

The segmentation and classification of DR utilize a Deformable Ladder Bi-attention U-Net (DLBUnet) coupled with a Deep Adaptive Convolutional Neural Network (DACNN) [181]. The DLBUnet performs lesion segmentation through an encoder-decoder structure with a central processing module. In the encoder, deformable convolution replaces standard convolution to capture lesion variations by learning offset locations. The central processing module integrates Ladder Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling (LASPP) with variable dilation rates to enhance small lesion features and mitigate gridding effects. The decoder employs a bi-attention mechanism with spatial and channel attention to accurately reconstruct lesion contours and edges, improving the segmentation precision [181]. Another approach for lesion segmentation is the use of DR-UNet and an attention-aware convolutional network based on the ResidualNet architecture, which introduces a skip connection between convolutional blocks to enhance the model’s performance [122].

The study used a weakly supervised sensitive heatmap framework to interpret CNN models such as EfficientNetB5, DenseNet201, and ResNet50 for lesion segmentation [182]. The research found that the combination of EfficientNetB5 with the weakly supervised sensitive heatmap framework outperformed the other models [182]. The framework achieved an accuracy of 98.65%, surpassing the 95.4% accuracy achieved by the class activation map, as shown in the Table 12. Optic disc and fovea based segmentation

There are studies that have performed a segmentation focusing on an optic disc or fovea [65, 165, 166]. Automation in the segmentation of optic disc and localization of optic disc and fovea centers is crucial in computer-aided screening, although it is very challenging due to diverse artifacts and image variability [187].

Spatial color histogram binarization techniques employed for fovea segmentation [166]. This approach uses both spatial and color information, resulting in a two-dimensional histogram that encapsulates rich information [166]. The model stands out due to its lightweight nature, requiring minimal computational resources [166]. Additionally, FundusPosNet, which incorporated an encoder-decoder architecture inspired by UNet [165]. Despite the differently designed model, FundusPosNet falls short when it comes to accurately detecting the optic disc and fovea in retinal fundus images exhibiting severe anatomical pathologies [165]. This limitation raises concerns about the model’s robustness in handling diverse clinical scenarios [165].

A region proposal network to generate multiple optic disc regions was employed for segmentation [65]. The selection of the proposal with the highest probability forms the basis for identifying the region of interest containing the fovea [65]. Subsequently, a three-level cascade CNN is employed for the precise fovea region. While this approach demonstrates competitive performance, a critical evaluation is necessary [65]. Comparing the performance of models introduced by [65, 165, 166], it becomes evident that the model [166] holds a distinctive advantage specifically in situations where computational resources are a concern or where a balance between accuracy and efficiency is crucial. Current AI trends in diabetic retinopathy screening

Recent advancements in AI, particularly DL, have significantly improved the accuracy of DR diagnosis through automated retinal image analysis. However, challenges remain in terms of model interpretability and the computational resources required for training AI models. As a result, federated learning (FL) and explainable AI (XAI) have become active areas of research in AI-enabled DR screening. XAI enhances the transparency and interpretability of DL models, enabling clinicians to better understand model decisions and trust AI-driven diagnoses. Meanwhile, FL allows collaborative model training across institutions while preserving patient privacy and addressing data-sharing concerns. Together, these approaches are transforming DR screening, improving diagnostic accuracy, and expanding access to high-quality retinal datasets.

Explainable AI

Explainable AI (XAI) encompasses a suite of methods and techniques designed to make the decision-making processes of AI systems transparent, interpretable, and comprehensible to humans. This is particularly critical in addressing the “black-box” nature of many modern AI systems, especially those employing DL [188]. While such systems produce highly accurate results, the underlying processes often remain opaque, limiting their adoption in sensitive domains like healthcare [189].

An ensemble-based approach has been used to enhance the explainability of DL techniques in diagnosing DR [190]. This model integrates weights from multiple CNNs to effectively extract significant features from retinal images. It utilizes Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) and Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) to provide intuitive visual insights into the model’s decision-making process, highlighting the critical regions of interest in retinal scans as shown in Table 15. This dual approach not only improves diagnostic accuracy but also addresses the urgent need for interpretability in AI applications within healthcare. In DR, XAI techniques significantly enhance the interpretability of DL models used for diagnosis, empowering healthcare professionals to better understand the rationale behind predictions. For instance, these methods enable the extraction of pathological descriptors and the visualization of neuron activation patterns. Such insights establish clear links between specific retinal lesions and model outputs, fostering greater trust among clinicians and improving the overall quality of clinical decision-making. Furthermore, XAI facilitates the generation of diverse synthetic images, contributing to the robust training of diagnostic algorithms and ensuring their applicability across varied patient populations [197].

The XAI-enabled teleophthalmology for DR grading and classification model introduced a unique approach to DR grading and classification through XAI within a teleophthalmology framework [191]. It combines advanced techniques such as U-Net for image segmentation, SqueezeNet for feature extraction, and the Archimedes Optimization Algorithm (AOA) [198] and bidirectional gated recurrent convolutional unit (BGRCU) for enhanced detection and classification [191]. This model emphasizes transparency in AI decision-making, enabling clinicians to better understand and trust the results, while also improving access to eye care via remote monitoring. Its effectiveness is validated through rigorous testing on benchmark datasets, demonstrating significant improvements over existing methods [191] and shown in Table 15.

Federated learning

Federated learning is a decentralized approach to training ML models, where sensitive data remains localized at its source, such as hospitals or clinics, instead of being aggregated into a central repository [199]. Models are trained locally on distributed nodes, and only model updates (weights or gradients) are shared and aggregated to build a global model [199].

In DR, FL facilitates collaboration among healthcare institutions without exposing patient data, addressing privacy concerns and regulatory challenges [200]. This enables the development of robust models trained on diverse datasets, improving generalizability and fairness in diagnosis. Studies have shown FLs effectiveness in DR screening, achieving comparable performance to centrally trained models while maintaining data security [194].

The FL approach, combined with ViT, improves the accuracy and efficiency of DR detection across institutions while preserving data privacy [193]. By allowing institutions to collaboratively train a model without sharing sensitive data, this framework enhances privacy and improves classification accuracy by up to 3% [193] as indicated on Table 15. ViT’s attention mechanism further boosts performance, and FL-ViT has been shown to outperform local models across diverse datasets while enabling multi-institutional collaboration [193].

Instead of centralizing patient data, FL allows institutions to train local models, leveraging data from multiple sources to improve generalizability across diverse populations and image qualities [195]. This approach is efficient in resource-limited environments [195]. Comparative studies of CNNs like ResNet50, DenseNet201, AlexNet, EfficientNetB7, and VGGNet19 in FL environments highlight their performance [196]. Enhanced DenseNet architectures, incorporating dense blocks to improve feature utilization and address the vanishing gradient problem, further strengthen feature propagation [192]. As shown in Table 15, FL demonstrates promising performance, particularly for resource-constrained environments and ensuring data privacy.

Challenges and future directions

While ML methods have undoubtedly made significant strides in the realms of DR localization, grading, and segmentation, challenges persist, particularly when applying ML models to retinal fundus images. These challenges necessitate further attention and resolution to enhance the efficacy and reliability of ML-based approaches in this domain.

The shortage of high-quality labeled datasets remains a critical challenge, as the performance of ML models heavily depends on both the quality and quantity of annotated data. Obtaining a sufficient amount of accurately labeled data is particularly difficult due to the complex and time-intensive processes involved in retinal fundus image acquisition, filtering, annotation, and grading. Publicly available datasets often suffer from issues such as class imbalance, inseparability, and insufficient sample sizes, limiting the development of robust ML models. Furthermore, the small size and tiny nature of lesions, such as microaneurysms, hemorrhages, hard exudates, and cotton-wool spots, pose significant challenges for accurate segmentation and classification. These lesions often exhibit high intra-class variation and low inter-class contrast, making them difficult for models to distinguish. To advance research in this domain, it is essential to develop and provide access to high-resolution, well-annotated datasets for the research community.

ML models are resource-intensive, with many requiring significant computational resources for training and deployment. We suggest creating a lightweight model by incorporating attention mechanisms and utilizing model optimization techniques such as pruning, sparsifying, and knowledge distillation. This will enable deployment on edge devices such as mobile and IoT devices. Additionally, FL is expected to play a significant role in addressing resource constraints, particularly by enabling decentralized training without compromising data privacy. Its outcomes have shown promising potential in real-world applications, especially in fields like healthcare and finance. XAI will also be crucial in making AI models more understandable and interpretable, fostering trust and broader adoption in critical domains. However, XAI remains an active area of research, particularly in enabling AI models to accurately identify and explain the specific lesions associated with a disease. Achieving these capabilities would not only enhance diagnostic accuracy but also provide clinicians with actionable insights to improve patient care.

The study showcased the application of several assessment measures in reporting quantitative outcomes, indicating their capacity to cover many aspects of performance. However, a considerable number of ML methods assessed in this review have not been tested in clinical settings, raising doubts about their optimal performance in practical applications. Close collaboration between data scientists and ophthalmologists is crucial for ensuring the effective adoption of ML algorithms in clinical settings. Additionally, it enhances the reliability and efficacy of ML algorithms in clinical settings.

Conclusions

This systematic review explores the challenges and opportunities in using ML for DR diagnosis from retinal fundus images. It identifies several datasets, though issues such as imbalanced data, small sample sizes, and inconsistent grading pose challenges. Various preprocessing techniques were reviewed, and common ML methods for DR localization, grading, and segmentation were analyzed. Key obstacles highlighted include low data quality, model complexity, and the lack of clinical validation of ML models.

Moving forward, ML models for DR screening need to be tailored for clinical settings, with lightweight models introduced to reduce computational complexity. FL enable collaborative model training across multiple institutions while preserving data privacy and improving dataset diversity. Additionally, high-quality, well-curated datasets should be collected and made accessible to the research community to improve model performance. Additionally, the involvement of ophthalmologists in defining use cases and further validation in real-world clinical settings will be essential to achieving accurate, ophthalmologist-level grading for DR diagnosis.
