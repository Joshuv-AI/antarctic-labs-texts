Clinical and Pathogenic Characteristics of 45 Cases of Bloodstream Infection in Obstetrics: A Clinical Analysis
Authors: Jing Xie, Ling Zhu, Ting Wu, Guanglin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate the clinical characteristics, pathogenic profile, and outcomes of pregnant and puerperal women bloodstream infections.Patients and MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on the medical records of 45 pregnant women with positive blood cultures admitted to the First People’s Hospital of Chenzhou from January 2018 to March 2025.ResultsThe positive rate of blood culture is 1.64‰. According to whether the bacteria are multi-drug resistant, they are divided into non multi-drug resistant group (25 cases) and multi drug resistant group (20 cases). There were statistically significant differences included vaginal delivery numbers (P=0.035), mid-to-late pregnancy induced abortion (P=0.036), placental abnormalities (P=0.036), ectopic pregnancy history (P=0.033), cough/sputum (P=0.012), non-cesarean uterine surgery (P=0.014), failed vaginal delivery to cesarean (P=0.025), and manual placental removal (P=0.027). Twenty-nine percent had uncertain infection sites. The top three bacteria are Escherichia coli, Enterococcus faecalis, and Staphylococcus epidermidis. Multi site or mixed infections account for 37.78%. The resistance rate to levofloxacin and cefuroxime exceeds 30%, while the resistance rate to ceftriaxone exceeds 40%. The resistance rates of piperacillin/tazobactam, imipenem, and meropenem are all 0.ConclusionIn the event of bloodstream infection, there is a high possibility of infection with non-multidrug-resistant bacteria in patients with obvious cough/sputum symptoms, failed vaginal delivery to cesarean section, placental abnormalities, and artificial placental abruption; Patients with a history of ectopic pregnancy or who have undergone uterine surgery other than cesarean section during this pregnancy are more likely to be infected with multidrug-resistant bacteria. Notably, when infection and shock coexist, the cause of shock may not necessarily be infectious.Keywords: pregnant and puerperal women, blood culture, bloodstream infection bacteremia, sepsis

Introduction

Pregnancy is a state of “immune deficiency”, where the maternal body not only exhibits reduced resistance to invasive pathogens but is also susceptible to opportunistic infections. Pathogen infections during pregnancy can impact the health of the mother and fetus, serving as a high-risk factor for adverse pregnancy outcomes such as preterm birth, miscarriage, and stillbirth.1 The incidence rate of obstetric sepsis is 10.09‰2 (0.976‰~4‰ in China2,3), accounting for 6.6%~28% of maternal deaths,4–6 ranking third.2 Bundle management can avoid the vast majority of deaths.7 The pathogen detection rate of sepsis is 25% to 40%, mainly bacterial infections,8 and there are mixed infections.9 Early identification and intervention are the core strategies to improve the success rate of diagnosis and treatment of obstetric bloodstream infections, especially sepsis.10 Penicillin and cephalosporin are currently recognized as safe medication choices for pregnant and postpartum women. The physiological changes during pregnancy can affect the distribution and efficacy of drugs, but very few drugs have been approved to use pregnant women as research subjects for drug efficacy and toxicity studies.1 It is particularly important to regularly evaluate the characteristics of the pathogenic microbial community in pregnant women and their sensitivity to commonly used antibiotics.

Our obstetrics department, as the emergency and critical care center for pregnant and postpartum women in southern Hunan and our city, is responsible for the treatment, consultation, referral, and technical guidance of all emergency and critical pregnant and postpartum women in southern Hunan and our city. This study retrospectively analyzed a total of 45 pregnant women with bloodstream infections who visited the First People’s Hospital of Chenzhou in the past 8 years, aiming to provide reference and practical experience for early detection and empirical treatment of obstetric infections in antibiotic selection.

Materials and Methods

Vertical entry standard: A retrospective analysis of all cases of women from pregnancy to 42 days after delivery treated at the obstetrics department of the First People’s Hospital of Chenzhou from January 2018 to March 2025.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Clearly identify as contaminating bacteria. (2) Multiple identical specimens were sent for testing on the same day, and the same pathogen was isolated.

Diagnostic Criteria

Bloodstream infection (BSI):11 The presence of pathogenic microorganisms in the patient’s blood, with or without symptoms and signs of infection. Blood flow infections are classified into bacteremia, toxemia, sepsis, and sepsis according to the degree of infection. Bacteremia:12 the presence of bacteria or fungi in the bloodstream; Blood culture is the gold standard for its diagnosis. Quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA),13 obstetrically modified quick SOFA (omqSOFA),10 Obstetrically Modified SOFA (omSOFA)14 and the National Early Warning Score (NEWS) scoring system in the UK.15 Septicemia in obstetrics (sepsis 3.0):13–16 an infection with qSOFA ≥ 2 that occurs during pregnancy, childbirth, miscarriage, or postpartum period. Septic shock: On the basis of sepsis, the infection continues to worsen and persistent hypotension persists even after sufficient fluid resuscitation. Vasoactive drugs are required to maintain mean arterial pressure (MAP) ≥ 65mmHg and blood lactate > 2mmol/L. Multi-drug resistant bacteria (MDR)17 refer to bacteria that exhibit resistance to three or more commonly used antibiotics that are usually sensitive. Anaerobic bacteria18 are divided into: obligate anaerobe (such as Clostridium perfringens, Bacteroides fragilis, Propionibacterium acnes, etc), facultative anaerobic bacteria (such as Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae pneumonia subgroup, Enterobacter aerogenes, Proteus mirabilis, Listeria monocytogenes, Staphylococcus epidermidis, Streptococcus pyogenes, Streptococcus pyogenes, Streptococcus pyogenes, Enterococcus faecalis, etc), and microaerophilic anaerobic bacteria (such as Haemophilus parainfluenzae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, etc).

Research Methods

Specimen Collection: After disinfection, use aseptic syringes to draw venous blood (dual bottles from both sides). All specimens must be sent for testing within 2 hours post-collection. Blood samples are inoculated into aerobic and anaerobic blood culture bottles, placed in an automated blood culture incubator, and processed according to standard procedures.

Strain identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing: After pretreatment of all submitted specimens, Gram staining was performed on smears according to routine methods. Preliminary identification was based on morphology, followed by culture and isolation. The choice of culture media generally involves inoculating Gram-positive bacteria onto blood agar, Gram-negative cocci onto blood agar and chocolate agar, Gram-negative bacilli onto blood agar, chocolate agar, and MacConkey agar, while fungi were inoculated onto fungal culture media. Based on growth patterns, dominant pure colonies were selected for further biochemical identification. Bacterial identification was conducted using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry and the automated microbiology identification and antimicrobial susceptibility analysis system (VITEK 2 Compact), following standardized operating procedures and biochemical reactions provided by the bacterial detection system’s random in vitro diagnostic reagent strips. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the microdilution broth method according to VITEK 2 Compact susceptibility card requirements. Based on the turbidity principle, the light transmittance of susceptibility wells was compared with control wells, and the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined according to different antimicrobial drug algorithms. Sensitivity, intermediate, and resistant results were reported based on breakpoints from the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI). The antimicrobial susceptibility testing adhered to CLSI standards. The test results were reviewed by the Department of Laboratory Medicine at the First People’s Hospital of Chenzhou and submitted to the HIS system. The selection of susceptibility cards covered various antibiotics (including penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, monobactams, and folate metabolism pathway inhibitors).

Main instruments and reagents: Matrix assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometer (Merieux, France); VITEK 2 Compact fully automatic microbiological analysis system (Merieux, France); BACT/ALERT VIRTUO fully automatic blood culture incubator; Microbial identification card and drug susceptibility card (Merieux, France), including Gram negative bacteria identification card (ViteK 2 GN Test Kit), Gram positive bacteria identification card (ViteK 2 GP Test Kit), yeast identification card (ViteK 2 YST Test Kit), Gram negative bacteria susceptibility card (ViteK 2 AST-GN 334, 335), Gram positive bacteria susceptibility card (ViteK 2 AST-GP67 Test Kit), and yeast like fungal susceptibility card (ViteK 2 AST-YS08 Test Kit). Fungal culture medium, MacConkey agar medium, chocolate agar medium, Haemophilus influenzae chocolate agar medium. The quality control strains used in the VITEK 2 Compact fully automated microbiological analysis system are designated standard strains, including Escherichia coli ATCC700323, Staphylococcus saprophyticus ATCC-BAA750, Candida albicans ATCC14053, Escherichia coli ATCC25922, and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC29213.

The data collection mainly includes: basic information of pregnant and postpartum women (age, number of pregnancies, delivery, etc), previous pregnancy and examination related indicators, the occurrence of bloodstream infections (fever, surgery and operation, complications, etc), the mode of delivery (gestational age, induced labor, vaginal delivery, cesarean section, etc), maternal and fetal outcomes, etc.

Statistical Analysis

Using the statistical software SPSS 26.0 for analysis, the measurement data was expressed as mean ± standard deviation () and t-test was performed; Count data is presented in terms of examples and percentages, and a chi square test is performed. The distribution of pathogens is described by composition ratio, and drug resistance is described by frequency. P<0.05 represents statistically significant differences.

Discussion

Incidence of Bloodstream Infections in Pregnant and Postpartum Women

The monitoring results of MDR may vary among different monitoring networks, regions, hospitals, as well as different departments and periods within the same hospital.17 In this study, in the past near 8 years, there have been a total of 45 positive cases of blood culture in pregnant and postpartum women. Referring to relevant research in the same field,19 the standard sample size range for such studies is 29 people. Therefore, the sample size of this study meets industry standards and can meet research needs. The incidence of sepsis is 0.44 ‰, and the incidence of septic shock is 0.18 ‰, which is significantly lower than the incidence rate of research data in China (0.976 ‰~4 ‰),20 and there is no patient death.

Risk Factors Analysis of Bloodstream Infections in Pregnant and Postpartum Women

There are many risk factors for obstetric blood flow infection: (1) Maternal factors: race (especially ethnic minorities), low income, irregular prenatal examination, primipara, age ≥ 35 years, gestation status, multiple pregnancy, chronic medical diseases (such as malnutrition, obesity, diabetes, high blood pressure, iron deficiency anemia, heart, liver and kidney diseases), immune abnormalities (such as autoimmune diseases, glucocorticoid use, immunosuppressive use, low immunity), pelvic infection history, GBS history, intravenous drug use,6 chemotherapy history, etc. (2) Obstetric factors: prolonged rupture of membranes (rupture time > 12 hours, significantly increased risk of intrauterine infection), protracted active phase, excessive vaginal examinations (> 5 times), cesarean section (especially emergency cesarean section),21 soft birth canal lacerations, retained tissue in the uterus, invasive prenatal diagnostic procedures, cervical cerclage, assisted reproductive technology, forceps or vacuum-assisted delivery, poor sterile conditions,2 postpartum hemorrhage, mastitis, etc;22,23 And any one of the risk factor may lead to bloodstream infections in patients. In this study, there were 7 cases (15.56%) who underwent cervical cerclage during pregnancy, 10 cases (22.22%) whose age was over 35 years, 10 cases (22.22%) who had a history of non cesarean section surgery during pregnancy, 13 cases (28.89%) with abnormal amniotic fluid (including no amniotic fluid and amniotic fluid contamination), 14 cases (31.11%) with manual evacuation of placenta, 15 cases (33.33%) with a history of surgery other than cesarean section during pregnancy, and 21 cases (46.67%) who used dexamethasone to promote fetal lung maturation during pregnancy. Prenatal rupture of membranes [26 cases, accounting for 57.78%, of which 15 cases (33.33%) had a rupture time > 12 hours], cesarean section (including cesarean section for failed drug-induced abortion) [33 cases, accounting for 73.33%], and greater than or equal to two risk factors accounted for more than half of the cases. And between the two groups, there were statistically significant differences in the number of transvaginal deliveries (P=0.035) and induced abortion during mid to late pregnancy (P=0.036), failed vaginal deliveries to cesarean section (P=0.025), placental abnormalities in this pregnancy (P=0.036), and artificial placental abruption (P=0.027). This may be due to the fact that the cervix itself is connected to the vagina (there is a diverse microbial community in the vagina), and repeated vaginal delivery can cause repeated damage to the cervix, weaken the defense ability of cervical epithelial cells, and increase the risk of infection. This indicating that this pregnancy has obvious cough/sputum symptoms, failed vaginal delivery to cesarean section, placental abnormalities in this pregnancy, and patients undergoing artificial placental abruption have a high possibility of infection with non multi-drug resistant bacteria in the event of bloodstream infection; There was a statistically significant difference between patients with a history of ectopic pregnancy (P=0.033) and those who underwent uterine surgery other than cesarean section during this pregnancy (P=0.014), indicating that patients with a history of ectopic pregnancy and those who underwent uterine surgery other than cesarean section during this pregnancy are more likely to be infected with multi-drug resistant bacteria in the event of bloodstream infection, which may also be related to the fact that inflammation is the primary cause of ectopic pregnancy.

Clinical Manifestations of Bloodstream Infections in Pregnant and Postpartum Women

The diagnosis of infection during pregnancy is the same as that during non pregnancy, based on comprehensive judgment of high-risk factors, symptoms, signs, laboratory test results, etc. However, the clinical symptoms are often atypical,1 and there is currently no unified standard for early identification and diagnosis of infection during pregnancy and postpartum period.7

The clinical manifestations of bloodstream infections are easily overlooked and diverse: fever (or hypothermia), shiver, fatigue, cough, headache, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, difficulty urinating, erythema, changes in consciousness, oliguria, difficulty inhaling and exhaling, abdominal pain (including abdominal pain caused by uterine contractions), uterine tenderness, skin damage, etc. Pregnant women are very resistant to the use of medication and usually seek medical attention only when their symptoms are more severe. But pregnancy is a special period for women. Due to the action of progesterone, accelerated basal metabolism, increased blood volume, and increased blood flow velocity, physiological temperature rise (0.39–0.78°C),2 heart rate increase (10–15 times/min during rest in late pregnancy), blood pressure “U-shaped” change (low in early and mid pregnancy, slightly increased after 24–26 weeks of pregnancy; generally no change in systolic blood pressure, mild decrease in diastolic blood pressure), white blood cell count increase (mild increase, generally (5–12) × 109/L, sometimes up to 15 × 109/L; white blood cell count significantly increases during labor and postpartum period, generally (14–16) × 109/L, sometimes up to 25 × 109/L, neutrophils increase mainly),24 and so on, which overlaps with early manifestations of infection, making early identification difficult; Moreover, immune tolerance during pregnancy, increased blood volume, increased cardiac output, dysbiosis of the reproductive tract microbiota, weakened cervical barrier function, and the unique environment of the endometrium can all increase the risk of infection. In addition, non infectious diseases (such as preeclampsia – which may affect the judgment of the patient’s shock blood pressure – thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, etc.) can have symptoms similar to sepsis. More importantly, not all patients with bloodstream infections have fever,6 and elevated body temperature cannot be the sole diagnostic basis for infection.10 In this study, there were 7 cases with obvious cough/sputum symptoms (all non-MDR), and the symptoms were not severe. They were all obtained through follow-up medical history after clear infection, and the difference between the two groups was statistically significant (P=0.012). WBC were all greater than 3 × 109/L, which may be related to physiological elevation of white blood cells during pregnancy. There were 13 cases (28.89%) with a maximum body temperature of ≤ 39°C, and 23 cases (51.11%) with a fever of more than 3 days. None of hypothermia patients, which may be related to the lack of timely monitoring during the transfer from another hospital and blood loss rescue process; Two cases with positive blood culture did not have fever, and their omqSOFA, omSOFA, and qSOFA scores were all ≥ 2, indicating sepsis.

One case had only one fever (37.7°C), and its blood culture, cervical secretion culture and abdominal drainage fluid culture were all positive for the same bacterium – Proteus mirabilis. Domestic guidelines suggest that procalcitonin levels ≥ 0.5 ng/mL indicate the presence of infection (moderate systemic inflammatory response), and 0.05~< 0.5 ng/mL indicating no or mild systemic inflammatory response (local infection).20 When CRP is between 10–99 mg/L, it indicates focal or superficial infection, and ≥ 100 mg/L indicates sepsis or invasive infection. In this study, there were 3 cases (6.67%, including 1 case with normal CRP) with CRP ≤ 50 mg/L, 16 cases (35.56%) with CRP < 100 mg/L, and 16 cases (35.56%) with PCT <0.5 ng/mL. Among them, there were 2 sepsis patients with PCT < 0.5 ng/mL (one in each group was 0.07 ng/mL and 0.465 ng/mL), and 1 sepsis patient with CRP 15.5 mg/L and PCT 0.96 ng/mL, which may be related to the decreased immunity of pregnant women. There were 25 cases with blood pressure below 90/60mmHg (among them 7 cases using vasopressors), among which 4 cases did not only cause shock due to infection (1 case was cardiogenic shock; 1 case was hemorrhagic shock; 1 case was heart failure, pulmonary infection, and chorioamnionitis; 1 case was heart failure, uterine resection after massive hemorrhage and chorioamnionitis). Therefore, when infection and shock occur simultaneously, the cause of shock may not necessarily be infection.

Obstetric sepsis is one of the main reasons for being transferred to the ICU.6 A study has shown that 33% of sepsis patients in the ICU during pregnancy and postpartum period are multidrug-resistant bacteria.25 In this study, 13 cases (28.89%) were transferred to our ICU, including 5 cases of multidrug-resistant bacteria (11.11%), and 8 cases (17.78%) were transferred to the ICU for more than 3 days. In addition, there were 2 cases transferred to higher-level hospitals, and their subsequent length of stay in the ICU of other hospitals is unknown.

Analysis of Bacterial Species and Infection Sites of Bloodstream Infections in Pregnant and Postpartum Women

The positive rate of blood culture in sepsis is 25% to 40%, mainly caused by bacterial infections;8 The positive rate of blood culture in patients with sepsis during pregnancy and postpartum period is only 56.25%,20 which is largely related to the refusal of pregnant women and their families to draw blood and the increase in additional costs. During pregnancy and postpartum period, bloodstream infections – especially sepsis – are commonly caused by 3 types of bacteria: Escherichia coli (mainly, about 60.26%),26,27 coagulase negative Staphylococci (about 6.03%,26 Staphylococcus epidermidis accounts for 60% to 70%, hemolytic staphylococci, etc),28 Klebsiella pneumoniae;29 and others include group A hemolytic streptococcus (GAS, is the most lethal and can cause multiple infections, accounting for 7.6% of sepsis deaths in the UK),6,29 group B streptococcus (GBS), anaerobic bacteria, Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas,30,31 Listeria monocytogenes (about 2.82%),9,32 and mixed infections can also occur (about 15%).6,9 Due to virus screening and testing have not yet been fully developed, there is a high risk of missed diagnosis of viral sepsis. Influenza virus (such as novel coronavirus), varicella zoster virus and herpes simplex virus are common obstetric sepsis related viruses,33 which often show pneumonia.34 Fungal sepsis is extremely rare in obstetric patients.35 In this study, there were a total of 17 types of bacterial infections in the blood. There were 30 cases of Gram negative bacteria (60%) and 1 case of obligate anaerobe (2%). The top 3 bacteria are as follows: Escherichia coli (25 cases, accounting for 50%), Enterococcus faecalis (5 cases, accounting for 10%), and coagulase negative staphylococcus (5 cases, accounting for 10%). Two cases of fungal infection (4%). One case is novel coronavirus. There were 16 cases of mixed infection [accounting for 36%; including 4 cases (both in the MDR group, accounting for 9%) showed two types of bacteria in blood culture, 11 cases (accounting for 24%; including 6 cases in the MDR group, accounting for 13%) showed positive results in two site cultures, and 4 cases (accounting for 9%; including 1 case in the MDR group, accounting for 2%) showed positive results in three site cultures]. There were 10 cases (22%) of the same bacteria cultured from different parts, including 4 cases (9%) in the MDR group. Two cases of fungal infection were both sepsis (one of which was septic shock). Among the 12 cases of sepsis, 7 cases were transferred from outside the hospital (accounting for 58% of sepsis cases and 3 cases in the MDR group).

The most common sites of infection in pregnant and postpartum women are the urinary system (accounting for 5.2%), reproductive system (such as chorioamnionitis, accounting for 27.6%) and respiratory system (accounting for 13.8%).35,36 Other common infections include surgical incision infections and skin and soft tissue infections (accounting for 3.5%).37,38 Only 74% of pregnant and postpartum women with sepsis can identify the source of infection.6 In this study, 32 cases (71%) were determined the site of infection; Among them, there were 14 cases (31%) of chorioamnionitis and 17 cases (37.78%) of multi site or mixed infections; 9 cases of blood and cervical secretion culture were the same bacteria, suggesting retrograde infection of vaginal bacteria.

Antibiotic Selection for Bloodstream Infections in Pregnant and Postpartum Women

Due to the potential toxic side effects of antibiotics, the clinical options for antibiotics used in pregnant women are very limited. Penicillin and cephalosporin antibiotics are currently recognized as safe medication choices for pregnant and postpartum women. The common drug-resistant antibiotics are ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, and cefuroxime axetil.39 The 2025 WHO Global Antimicrobial Resistance Monitoring Report: The situation of Gram negative bacteria resistance is severe, and the top three pathogens causing bloodstream infections worldwide are Escherichia coli (44.9%), Staphylococcus aureus (21.9%), and Klebsiella pneumoniae (21.1%). The resistance rates of Escherichia coli to ceftriaxone and ceftazidime were 43.5% and 31.8%, respectively. Studies have shown that among blood cultures with positive results, the proportion of resistance to piperacillin tazobactam and imipenem is 3.3% and 26.2%, respectively; multidrug resistant bacteria accounted for 13.8% (8/61).36 However, these data were not sourced from pregnant women. In this study, the resistance rates of levofloxacin and cefuroxime exceeded 30%, and the resistance rate of ceftriaxone exceeded 40%. The resistance rates of Escherichia coli to ceftriaxone, cefuroxime, cefazolin, cefuroxime axetil, and ceftazidime were 76%, 48%, 44%, 40%, and 24%, respectively. The resistance rates of piperacillin/tazobactam, imipenem, and meropenem are all 0, which may be related to the special characteristics of the pregnant and postpartum group.

Early and broad-spectrum antibiotic use is key, and drug-resistant bacteria should be considered as much as possible. When bacterial sensitivity is unknown, it is recommended to empirically use piperacillin/tazobactam sodium or meropenem (for G-bacteria) and clindamycin (for G+bacteria and anaerobic bacteria).7 In this study, among the effective antibiotics used, there were 16 cases of special use grade antibiotics (accounting for 35.56%; 8 cases of imipenem cilastatin sodium and 8 cases of meropenem), 18 cases (40%) of cefoxitin and 19 cases (42%) of piperacillin tazobactam sodium. Antibiotic use for >5 days and >10 days was observed in 35 cases (77.78%) and 15 cases (33.33%), respectively.

Prevention of Bloodstream Infections in Pregnant and Postpartum Women

Standardize prenatal examination (including attached importance to examinations related to vaginal discharge, weight management and management of gestational diabetes mellitus), standardized blood culture testing (draw blood as early as possible before the use of antibiotics, standardize the operation process of sample collection, etc.),2 large-scale top-down training and drills (strengthened the identification and management of sepsis), hierarchical management (strictly control high-risk factors and timely referral), multidisciplinary cooperation (established multiple Multi-Disciplinary Teams, quicklyed start MDT); Breaking the previous situation where other disciplines were afraid to deal with complications in pregnant women, actively searching for the cause, selecting empirical antibiotics based on updated guidelines, expert consensus, and local resistance patterns, and conducting strict evaluations by obstetrics specialists on whether to terminate pregnancy, in order to reduce serious adverse pregnancy outcomes caused by bloodstream infections. However, patient compliance, refusal to go to regular hospitals for examination, concealment of medical history and time consumption for township county city referral are still challenges in early identification and definitive diagnosis of obstetric sepsis. In clinical practice, cases of recurrent bloodstream infections after cardiogenic shock, hemorrhagic shock, and other conditions need to be distinguished from the diagnosis and treatment of sepsis caused by simple infection. Faced with the severe situation of “aging” pregnant and postpartum women, a sharp decrease in birth rates, and a sharp increase in the proportion of critically ill pregnant and postpartum women,25 procedural, scientific, and standardized prenatal examination and disease diagnosis and treatment management are still the direction we continue to strive for.

As a final remark, this study is a single center, retrospective, small sample study with certain limitations. Further sample size expansion and multicenter prospective studies are needed to confirm the results of this study. Some microbial communities in this study, such as Enterococcus faecalis and Staphylococcus epidermidis, may lack representativeness in drug sensitivity results due to the small sample size, and may not fully meet the needs of complex statistical analysis.
