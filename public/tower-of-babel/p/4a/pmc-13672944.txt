Preventing frailty in multiple sclerosis is a realistic goal: No
Authors: Antonio Scalfari, Alessia Bianchi
Published: 2026

Over recent decades, the multiple sclerosis (MS) population has progressively shifted towards older ages, reflecting both an increasing proportion of patients experiencing onset later in life and overall improvements in disease outcome severity and survival. 1 Within this evolving epidemiological trend, frailty has emerged as an increasingly relevant clinical concern in MS.

Frailty is a multidimensional clinical state characterised by reduced physiological reserve and increased vulnerability to adverse health outcomes.2–4 Among people with MS, frailty is more prevalent than in the general population and is associated with impaired mobility, poorer cognitive outcomes, reduced quality of life, and greater disability burden.3,5,6 It is more commonly observed with longer disease duration and progressive disease phenotypes. However, frailty may also occur in MS patients with relatively low disability scores, suggesting that it is not only a surrogate marker of physical disability but it rather reflects broader biological vulnerability and loss of compensatory reserve irrespective of the disease course trajectory. 7

Given its close association with adverse clinical outcomes, frailty is receiving growing attention as a potential target for interventions in the optimisation of MS management. However, whether it can be meaningfully preventable remains uncertain and likely to be unrealistic. Frailty arises from the cumulative effects of currently unmodifiable neurodegenerative mechanisms, together with age-related declines in physiological function. It therefore represents a downstream and largely irreversible consequence of sustained injury and impaired repair mechanisms in the central nervous system (CNS) and systemic physiological deterioration.2,3,5 By relentlessly eroding the physiological reserve, these processes ultimately contribute to accelerated biological ageing, worsening disability and increased mortality risk. 4

In MS, pathological mechanisms underlying frailty are closely related to the irreversible axonal degeneration, which drives the gradual accumulation of progressive symptoms and becomes increasingly prominent over the course of the disease.6,8 Consistently, higher frailty index scores have been associated with lower relapse activity, suggesting that greater frailty severity may reflect the shift from focal inflammatory disease activity to predominant chronic neurodegenerative processes. 9

Effective prevention of frailty would require altering the trajectory of the underlying pathophysiological and neurodegenerative mechanisms, driving its development and progression. With currently available disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) and rehabilitative interventions, this remains an elusive therapeutic goal.2,9 Although DMTs can tackle effectively the focal inflammatory injury accumulation, their impact on the neurodegenerative mechanisms and on biological processes most closely linked to frailty is considerably more limited. Indeed, even in the context of successful therapeutic suppression of relapses or overt magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) inflammatory activity, subtle irreversible physical and cognitive decline may occur from the early stage of MS, progressively increasing the vulnerability to frailty. 8 This smouldering-associated worsening is thought to reflect a broad range of injury mechanisms that are not adequately addressed by currently available DMTs, including compartmentalised CNS inflammation sustained by chronic microglial activation, the accumulation of focal and global cortical pathology, mitochondrial dysfunction and age-related cellular senescence. 8

Age-related biological changes may provide an additional shared biological substrate linking MS progression and frailty. With advancing age, independently of the MS duration, patients are at greater risk of reaching severe disability milestones, and the progressive accumulation of irreversible symptoms becomes the dominant clinical feature. 10 This is driven by a higher burden of chronic inflammatory lesions, accumulation of cortical damage, axonal loss, and synaptic dysfunction, all of which contribute to neurodegeneration and brain tissue loss, the key determinants of irreversible physical and cognitive disability in MS. 11 Taken together, these observations reinforce the concept of accelerated biological ageing in MS, as illustrated by the ‘brain age’ paradigm. Individuals with MS often exhibit structural brain changes consistent with biological age older than expected for their chronological age, and a greater brain age gap has been associated with faster disease progression and future disability accumulation. 12 In addition, with ageing, oligodendrocyte progenitor cell differentiation becomes impaired and, as the remyelination efficiency progressively decreases, the lesion microenvironment becomes increasingly hostile to repair. 13 This further compromises endogenous regenerative mechanisms, contributing to the accumulation of irreversible tissue damage and functional decline over time.

Overall, accelerated biological brain ageing is likely to drive the progressive erosion of neurological reserve until a critical threshold is exceeded, at which point vulnerability to frailty emerges clinically. From this perspective, frailty, which is intrinsically linked to irreversible axonal degeneration and to progressive age-related exhaustion of compensatory mechanisms, may be better conceptualised as a late-stage and largely unmodifiable manifestation of the MS evolution. Importantly, no currently available treatment has been shown to reverse the brain age gap or to restore the lost brain reserve in MS, while effective remyelination remains an elusive therapeutic target, 12 overall challenging the plausibility that any currently available intervention can meaningfully impact frailty. Therefore, even in the context of an optimal response to immunotherapy, preventing frailty or halting its progression is biologically unattainable, as its pathological substrate largely persists unchecked and remains unmodifiable. 5

Despite its dynamic nature, with individuals potentially transitioning between different levels of severity over time, 2 frailty ultimately exerts a significant detrimental effect on MS patients, and may promote a self-reinforcing cycle of functional decline. Ageing, the MS-related mobility impairment, and the accumulation of vascular comorbidities may contribute to reduced cardiorespiratory fitness and lower levels of physical activity, thereby further exacerbating deconditioning, physiological vulnerability and ultimately frailty severity. 5 While appropriate management of these secondary contributors may help to mitigate the burden of frailty, it does not adequately address its primary biological drivers. 2 This distinction is critical, as rehabilitation programmes and lifestyle interventions, including nutritional support, exercises and sleep optimisation, may reduce deconditioning, and enhance resilience. However, there is currently insufficient evidence that they fundamentally alter long-term trajectory of frailty in MS. 6

Future research should therefore focus on identifying early biomarkers of vulnerability, characterising frailty evolution over time, and developing therapies targeting the biological mechanisms underlying progressive CNS ageing, smouldering neurodegeneration, and impaired repair processes, which remain the key unmet therapeutic challenges in frailty prevention.
