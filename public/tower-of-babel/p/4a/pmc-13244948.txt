Pilot study evaluating a newly developed nanoparticle-based lateral flow assay for the diagnosis of cutaneous leishmaniasis in Sri Lanka
Authors: Nirmitha Lalindi De Silva, Ken Hatano, Makoto Itoh, Nao Machida, Yutaro Kojima, Viraji Nefertiti Hiromel De Silva, Thishan Channa Yahathugoda, Hidekazu Takagi, Hirotomo Kato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the launch of Sri Lanka’s National Strategic Plan for leishmaniasis control, case diagnosis has been identified as a key strategic intervention. However, the routine slit-skin smear (SSS) demonstrates variable sensitivity (33–78%), underscoring the need for rapid and sensitive diagnostic tools. This study evaluated the diagnostic performance of a newly developed nanoparticle-based anti-rKRP42 (recombinant kinesin-related protein antigen-42) IgG immunochromatographic test (rKRP42-ICT) for detecting cutaneous leishmaniasis caused by Leishmania donovani.MethodsA cross-sectional pilot study was conducted among 58 adults with clinical CL at Base Hospital Tangalle. CL was confirmed by SSS (reference test). Serum from CL patients and Japanese negative controls was tested using rKRP42-ICT, rKRP42 IgG ELISA, and rK39-ICT. Diagnostic performance of the ICT (sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV)) was calculated against SSS. Clinical associations were analysed by Chi-square, and ELISA OD means were compared using non-parametric tests (with 95% confidence interval).ResultsThe majority of the CL cohort (62%) were male. The mean duration at presentation was 5.1 months. Most had single, small (< 2 cm) lesions, with ulcers being the commonest type (41%). rKRP42-ICT demonstrated a positive rate of 43.1% among the clinical CL cohort while SSS and rK39-ICT positivity was 55.2% and 5.2%, respectively. The rKRP42-ICT showed 43.8% sensitivity, 57.7% specificity, and 56% PPV and 45.5% NPV against the reference test. The rKRP42-ICT could detect positive samples across a wide range of ELISA OD values, from 0.017 to 2.535. rKRP42-ICT positivity correlated with higher ELISA OD values (p < 0.05), but SSS positivity did not. The rKRP42-ICT and SSS agreement was slight (Kappa = 0.014). Negative controls (n = 43) were negative by both ICTs. Male gender, ulcerated, large (> 2 cm), and multiple lesions were associated with higher rKRP42-ICT positivity (p < 0.05) and higher mean ELISA OD values. Defining confirmed cases as clinical CL with either SSS or rKRP42-ICT positivity increased case detection from 55.2 to 74.1% (20%).ConclusionsThe rKRP42 IgG-based ICT showed promise as a supplementary diagnostic tool for CL, potentially improving overall case detection beyond routine microscopy and providing rapid results for clinical decision-making. With further optimization and validation, it may support national diagnostic and surveillance efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41182-026-00981-z.

Background

Leishmaniasis is a group of neglected tropical diseases that occurs in three main clinical forms—visceral, cutaneous, and mucosal leishmaniasis—and is caused by protozoan parasites of the genus Leishmania. It is a vector-borne disease transmitted by infected female sandflies of the genera Phlebotomus or Lutzomyia [1]. The disease disproportionately affects poor and marginalized populations with limited financial resources. Leishmaniasis, especially the cutaneous and mucosal forms, is particularly associated with disfiguring scarring and stigma, leading to considerable psychosocial consequences [2].

In Sri Lanka, the causative parasite is Leishmania donovani zymodeme MON-37, a naturally attenuated strain of the L. donovani complex, which usually causes visceral leishmaniasis (VL) in India and East Africa [3, 4]. Although rare, locally acquired VL and mucocutaneous leishmaniasis (MCL) cases indicate the visceralizing potential of this strain [5, 6]. Leishmaniasis has a long history in Sri Lanka, with large caseloads documented during the colonial era, which were subsequently suppressed during malaria control activities [7, 8]. Since the early 1990s, and particularly over the past two decades, the disease has re-emerged, showing a steady increase in cases [9]. In response, leishmaniasis was made notifiable in 2008, and a WHO-mediated National Strategic Plan (NSP) for 2024–2028 was launched for its control and prevention [8, 10]. The three key strategic interventions of the NSP focus on disease surveillance, case diagnosis and management, and integrated vector control. Early diagnosis and treatment are essential to minimize scarring and reduce transmission by limiting the human parasite reservoir, and have therefore been a major research focus.

Despite the significant burden of leishmaniasis in Sri Lanka, diagnosis across the island continues to rely primarily on slit-skin smears, which have notable limitations, including variable sensitivity reported in local studies—ranging from as low as 33% to 77%—and the need for well-trained microscopists [11, 12]. Parasite culture from lesions is an alternative but requires time, technical expertise, and is not feasible for routine use. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) offers high sensitivity and specificity; however, both PCR and culture remain largely inaccessible for routine diagnosis, are costly, and are available only at a few reference centres in the country. Consequently, the continued reliance on the slit-skin smear as the routine diagnostic test remains a significant challenge, undermining the effectiveness of case detection strategies within the NSP. These limitations highlight the need for diagnostic tools that are sensitive, specific, simple, rapid, widely accessible, and affordable.

A point-of-care (PoC) test is performed near the patient at the initial point of contact with the health system—at the bedside, in clinics, or at home—rather than in a central laboratory. These tests provide rapid results, often within minutes, enabling immediate clinical decisions, earlier treatment, improved outcomes, and more effective patient management. PoC testing reduces repeat visits, supports timely therapy, limits empirical treatment, and contributes to disease prevention at the population level [13]. Rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) are a key category of PoC tests, delivering results at the site of care without requiring laboratory infrastructure. Accordingly, RDT development aligns closely with disease prevention and control goals.

RDTs, mainly lateral flow immunoassays, are widely used for visceral leishmaniasis (VL), particularly the rK39 immunochromatographic test (ICT) [14]. However, rK39 has shown negative or inconsistent reactivity in patients with cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL) without VL features [12, 15, 16] limiting its value for CL diagnosis. The only RDT specifically developed and FDA-approved for CL is the CL Detect Rapid Test (InBios, USA) [17], which received approval following clinical testing in L. major endemic regions [18]. This lateral flow assay detects Leishmania perioxidin antigen from lysed dental broach samples of ulcerative lesions (< 4 months). In Sri Lanka, its performance was suboptimal, with high specificity but low sensitivity (36% vs PCR) [19]. Performance has varied across other settings, and a recent systematic review concluded that the test is overall suboptimal for CL diagnosis [20], with sensitivities of 23–100% and specificities of 78–100%. Several molecular lateral flow assays have also been developed for CL. These include an L. major-specific DNA PoC assay using isothermal recombinase-based amplification [21], and a recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) assay targeting kinetoplast DNA with lateral flow detection from non-invasive ulcer swabs [22]. Overall, evidence for serology-based RDTs in CL remains scarce.

The recombinant kinesin-related protein-42 (rKRP42), a subunit of Leishmania donovani kinesin-related protein, shares homology with rK39, differing primarily by an additional 39 amino acids [23]. Detection of anti-rKRP42 antibodies using urine-based ELISA has demonstrated sensitivity comparable to that of serum-based ELISA (94.6%) in patients with visceral leishmaniasis caused by L. donovani [24]. In our earlier studies, we demonstrated that an ELISA based on the rKRP42 antigen had high sensitivity (> 90%) and could successfully serve as a supplementary diagnostic tool for CL in Sri Lanka [12]. Building on these findings, and in alignment with the NSP, we aimed to develop an RDT that could function as a PoC test in resource-limited settings in Sri Lanka to facilitate CL diagnosis. Such an RDT could also be a valuable tool for identifying subclinical infections in endemic areas of Sri Lanka. Detecting these otherwise unrecognized cases may serve well for control programmes, as they may subsequently progress to overt disease and could potentially serve as a reservoir for ongoing transmission [25]. Since these individuals lack visible skin lesions, immunological detection offers a practical approach for identifying subclinical infections and enabling timely intervention. In addition, knowing the distribution of subclinical cases could help guide targeted vector control strategies in high-risk areas, thereby strengthening control efforts.

The current pilot study investigated a newly developed aggregation-induced emission (AIE) nanoparticle-based lateral flow sandwich immunoassay incorporating the rKRP42 antigen of L. donovani (rKRP42-ICT). Recent advances in lateral flow technology have introduced fluorescent nanoparticles to overcome the limited sensitivity of traditional labels such as colloidal gold, particularly for low-titre analytes in PoC testing. AIE nanoparticles fluoresce strongly upon aggregation, providing high signal-to-noise ratios, excellent photostability, and minimal background interference compared with conventional fluorophores or colorimetric probes [26]. These characteristics enable the development of highly sensitive lateral flow assays with enhanced analytical performance.

Materials and methods

Study setting and population

The study was conducted at Base Hospital Tangalle (BHT) in the Hambantota District of Southern Sri Lanka. Previously untreated patients aged ≥ 18 years with a clinical diagnosis of CL who presented to the dermatology clinic at BHT were recruited after obtaining informed consent. Patients younger than 18 years, those who did not provide consent, and individuals with a history of prior treatment for CL were excluded.

Sample size

This study was conducted as a preliminary evaluation of the rKRP42-ICT. As the assay kits were newly developed, only a limited number were available during this phase, with a total of 110 kits produced for testing. Accordingly, the planned sample size was 100, comprising 50 clinically suspected patient samples and 50 negative control samples, to enable preliminary assessment of assay performance in both groups. While a larger sample size would allow more precise estimates of diagnostic accuracy, the present study was intended to generate preliminary data and inform subsequent larger-scale validation studies.

Sample collection

Slit-skin smear samples

Patients presenting with skin lesions suggestive of CL were examined by a specialist dermatologist, and a clinical diagnosis of CL was made. After informed consent, these patients were enrolled in the study. A slit-skin smear (SSS—routine diagnostic method) was performed on each lesion for routine hospital diagnosis; smears were Giemsa-stained, examined by trained hospital laboratory technicians, and the reported results were retrieved retrospectively. CL was confirmed when smears were positive for Leishmania amastigotes.

Serum samples

A 2 mL venous blood sample was collected from each participant into a plain tube and transported to the laboratory on ice. After allowing clot formation at room temperature for 1 h, serum was separated by centrifugation (2,500 rpm,10 min) and stored at − 20 °C until analysis. Demographic and clinical data were recorded, and a colour photograph of each lesion was obtained. Serum samples (n = 43) from healthy Japanese individuals were also used as negative controls.

ELISA to detect IgG antibodies against rKRP42 antigen (rKRP42 IgG ELISA)

We used the recombinant KRP42 IgG ELISA protocol of Islam et al. [23, 24] with minor modifications. The modified ELISA assay was previously validated for CL in Sri Lanka [12]. Serum samples of CL (n = 58) and negative controls (n = 22).

Briefly, flat-bottom 96-well microtiter plates (NUNC™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) were coated with 100 µL/well of rKRP42 antigen (1 µg/mL). After blocking, 100 µL of serum samples diluted 1:4000 was added and incubated overnight at room temperature. After washing, goat anti-human IgG HRPO conjugate (Invitrogen Corporation, Camarillo, CA, USA), diluted 1:10,000 in casein buffer, was applied at 100 µL/well, and the plates were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. The ABTS substrate [2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulphonic acid)], was used for colour development. Absorbance was read at 405 nm with an ELISA plate reader (BioTek™ ELx808, CA, USA) using 490 nm as the reference wavelength. Antibody concentrations were expressed as optical density (OD) values. The cutoff OD value was estimated using the OD values of the negative controls and was calculated using the formula: cutoff = mean OD of negative controls + (2 × SD), where SD is the standard deviation of negative controls. The estimated cutoff OD value was 0.0722. Standard curves for each plate were generated using serial threefold dilutions of pooled sera from visceral leishmaniasis patients starting from × 20,000.

Rapid test (Kalazar Detect™) to detect anti-rK39 antibodies in patients with CL

CL patient sera (n = 58) and negative controls (n = 43) were tested with the rK39 rapid test (InBios International, Inc., Seattle, WA, USA) in accordance with the company manual.

Preparation of the rKRP42-ICT

Reagents and apparatus

Styrene, divinylbenzene, 1,1,2,3,4,5-hexaphenylsilole (HPS), 1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide (EDC), N-hydroxysulfosuccinimide sodium salt (sulfo-NHS) were purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry (Tokyo, Japan). Methacrylic acid, sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), potassium peroxodisulfate (KPS) were purchased from FUJIFILM Wako (Tokyo, Japan). Bovine serum albumin (BSA) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Missouri, USA). Goat anti-human IgG (Fc-specific) antibodies were purchased from Bethyl Laboratories (Montgomery, USA). AffiniPure rabbit anti-mouse IgG (H + L) was purchased from Jackson ImmunoResearch (Pennsylvania, USA).

The laminated pads made of cupra and PET used for the sample pads were purchased from Asahi Kasei (Tokyo, Japan). The PE pads used for the conjugate pads were purchased from Ahlstrom (Helsinki, Finland). The nitrocellulose membrane was purchased from Toyo Roshi (Tokyo, Japan). The glass fibre used for the absorbent pads was purchased from PALL (Port Washington, USA). The adhesive backing sheet was purchased from Adhesive Research (Glenrock, USA). The housing cases were supplied by Immuno Probe (Saitama, Japan).

The ultrasonic processing equipment was purchased from Hielscher Ultrasonics (Teltow, Germany). The microwave synthesis system was purchased from CEM (Charlotte, USA). The desktop coating robot was purchased from Musashi Engineering (Tokyo, Japan). The particle size distribution was obtained using the nanoparticle analyzer from Malvern Panalytical (Great Malvern, England). The scanning electron microscope (SEM) image was obtained using the high-resolution scanning electron microscope from Hitachi High-Tech (Tokyo, Japan). The fluorescence spectra were obtained using a fluorescence spectrophotometer from Shimadzu Corporation (Kyoto, Japan). Quantum yields were measured using the absolute PL quantum yield measurement system from Hamamatsu Photonics (Shizuoka, Japan).

Synthesis of polystyrene nanoparticles encapsulating AIE (aggregation-induced emission) compounds

First, distilled styrene (St), divinylbenzene (DVB), and methacrylic acid (MAA) were mixed. 1,1,2,3,4,5-Hexaphenylsilole (HPS) was added to the mixture and dissolved. Next, MilliQ water containing dissolved sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) was added and subjected to ultrasonic treatment to form an emulsion. MilliQ water containing dissolved potassium peroxodisulfate (KPS) was added to the emulsion, and the polymerization reaction was carried out using a microwave synthesis system. After the reaction was complete, centrifugation was performed to remove the supernatant, and the sample was redispersed in MilliQ water. These steps were repeated to purify and wash the target compound. Finally, low-speed centrifugation was performed to remove the precipitate, thereby eliminating aggregates and other impurities.

Antibody immobilization on AIE nanoparticle surfaces

First, MES buffer was mixed into the synthesized AIE nanoparticle suspension. EDC and sulfo-NHS were then added and thoroughly mixed, followed by incubation at 24 ℃. After the reaction, the supernatant was removed by centrifugation to eliminate unreacted EDC and sulfo-NHS. The sample was redistributed into the MES buffer, anti-human IgG (Fc-specific) were added, and the mixture was incubated at 24 ℃. After the reaction ended, centrifugation was performed, and the supernatant was removed to eliminate unreacted antibodies. The sample was then distributed into MES buffer, and BSA dissolved in PBS buffer was added to block any unreacted carboxyl groups. After the reaction was complete, repeated centrifugation and dispersion in HEPES buffer were performed to purify and wash the reaction products.

Assembly of the ICT unit

The strip consists of a sample pad (SP), a conjugate pad (CP), a nitrocellulose membrane (NCM), and an absorbent pad (AP). The sample pad was prepared by soaking a laminated pad of cupra and PET in a solution containing components such as a buffer and surfactant, then drying it at 50 ℃ for 2 h. The conjugate pad was prepared by soaking a PE pad in a solution containing surfactant, drying it at 50 ℃ for 1 h, then soaking it in a solution containing components such as antibodies labelled with AIE nanoparticles and protein stabilizers, and drying it at 37 ℃ for 30 min. The nitrocellulose membranes were coated with a solution containing components such as 1 mg/mL rKRP42 on the T line and a solution containing 1 mg/mL anti-mouse IgG (H + L) antibody on the C line, then dried at 50 °C for 24 h. The absorbent pad was used without processing the glass fibre. These parts were affixed to the adhesive backing sheet and cut to a width of approximately 3.8 mm. Finally, each strip was placed into its respective housing case, sealed in an aluminium pouch together with a desiccant, and stored.

Assessment of rKRP42-ICT performance with CL patient serum and negative control serum

Serum samples from CL patients (n = 58) and negative controls (n = 43) were tested using the ICT kits. Patient samples were analysed in Sri Lanka and re-tested in Japan using transported aliquots, while Japanese control sera were tested in Japan. Serum was mixed with surfactant-containing buffer at a 1:500 dilution, and 70 µL of the mixture was applied to the strip. After allowing it to stand for 15 min, the ICT strip was irradiated with 365 nm ultraviolet light using a detection reader to confirm the detection of results.

Statistical analysis

Slit-skin smear (SSS—the routine diagnostic test) was used as the reference standard. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value of the rKRP42-ICT were calculated relative to SSS. Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized as proportions. Agreement between rKRP42-ICT and SSS was assessed using Cohen’s kappa and interpreted according to the Landis and Koch scale [27]. ELISA optical density (OD) values were summarized as mean ± standard deviation, and group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. Approximate two-sided 95% confidence intervals were calculated for all proportions.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, University of Ruhuna, Sri Lanka [approval no. 2025/P/006(28.01.2025)]. Written and oral informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment and sample collection.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the study population

A total of 58 clinically confirmed CL patients were included in the study. Participant ages ranged from 20 to 78 years, with a mean age of 47.88 years (SD = 15.53). The study population comprised 62.1% males (n = 36) and 37.9% females (n = 22). Overall, 70.7% (n = 41) of participants were self-employed or otherwise employed, while 29.3% (n = 17) were unemployed; among the unemployed, 76.5% (13/17) were housewives. Half of the participants (50%) were from lower-income households (monthly income < LKR 50,000), whereas 24% reported a monthly income exceeding LKR 100,000.

Most patients presented early, within 4 months of lesion onset (69.0%, n = 40), while 25.9% (n = 15) presented between 4 and 12 months. Only three patients (5.2%) presented more than 1 year after lesion onset. The mean duration of lesions at presentation was 5.08 months (SD = 5.00). Most patients had a single lesion (89.6%, n = 52), whereas 10.4% (n = 6) had multiple lesions. The majority of lesions (84.5%, n = 49) measured < 2 cm in maximum diameter, while 15.5% (n = 9) were ≥ 2 cm. Among classical CL presentations, ulcers were the most common lesion type (41.4%, n = 24), followed by papules (24.1%, n = 14), ulcerated nodules (22.4%, n = 13), plaques (10.3%, n = 6), and nodules (1.7%, n = 1). No atypical clinical presentations were observed in this study population. Most lesions were located on the upper or lower limbs (86.1%, n = 50), while 10.3% (n = 6) occurred in the head and neck region and 3.4% (n = 2) on the back. A history of leishmaniasis among household members or neighbours was reported by 45.6% (n = 20).

Giemsa-stained slit-skin smear microscopy

Microscopic examination of SSS by hospital laboratory technicians confirmed CL in 55.2% (32/58) of participants through the detection of Leishmania amastigotes.

rKRP42 IgG ELISA and its clinical correlations

The CL cohort (n = 58) demonstrated ELISA OD values in the range of 0.009–2.535, with a mean of 0.123 and a standard deviation of 0.426. The negative controls (n = 22) showed a range of 0.014–0.109 with a mean of 0.029 and a standard deviation of 0.021. The mean IgG ELISA optical density (OD) values were significantly higher among patients with ulcerative lesions (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2). Only one participant presented with a nodular lesion, and this individual exhibited an OD value higher than the mean OD of the ulcer group. Mean OD values were also higher among participants with larger lesions and multiple lesions (p > 0.05).

Performance of the rKRP42-ICT

Among 58 clinically diagnosed CL patients, 25 (43.1%) were positive by rKRP42-ICT. Visual comparison of band intensity enables relative assessment of antibody levels in patient serum (Fig. 3). Performance of the rK39-ICT

Only 3 (5.2%) out of the 58 clinical CL patients were positive by rK39-ICT (Table 1). All negative control samples (n = 43) were negative by rK39-ICT.

Clinical correlations of rKRP42-ICT

ICT positivity was significantly higher in males than in females (p < 0.05), with 80% (20/25) of the ICT positives being male. ICT results were also associated with lesion morphology (p < 0.05); ulcerative lesions were more likely to test positive, whereas papular lesions were more likely to be negative, while other lesion types showed no significant deviation. Consistent with ICT, mean IgG ELISA OD values were higher in patients with ulcerative lesions (p < 0.01; Fig. 4). ICT positivity increased with lesion size (p < 0.05) and was higher among participants with multiple lesions (p < 0.05). Mean OD values showed the same trends with lesion size and number, although these differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Combination of either SSS or rKRP42-ICT combined with clinically confirmed CL

In this study, all participants were clinically diagnosed with CL. When a confirmed case was defined as being clinically positive and testing positive by either SSS or rKRP42-ICT, the case detection rate increased from 55.2% (32/58), based on SSS alone, to 74.1% (43/58), representing a 20% improvement in detection compared with the routine clinic-based diagnostic method.

Discussion

With the rising case burden of CL in Sri Lanka over the past two decades, the NSP was launched [8] to control and potentially eliminate CL as a public health concern in Sri Lanka while preventing visceral and mucosal variants from increasing. Its three key objectives are disease surveillance, case diagnosis and management, and integrated vector control. In alignment with these national priorities, our research focused on strengthening diagnostic capacity at dermatology clinic levels, where the diagnosis and management of CL primarily occur. Early diagnosis using sensitive, affordable, and easily implemented methods is essential to reduce the human parasite reservoir and interrupt transmission. Moreover, empirical treatment is discouraged for leishmaniasis, making diagnostic tools particularly important.

The novel rKRP42-ICT is a lateral flow assay employing an AIE nanoparticle-based sandwich immunoassay format. Capture of the IgG–antigen complex at the test line leads to nanoparticle aggregation, producing a strong reader-detectable fluorescence. This format enhances sensitivity through fluorescence activation and signal amplification with a high signal-to-noise ratio, enabling detection of low antibody levels. Consistent with this, the rKRP42-ICT detected IgG levels as low as OD 0.017. Fluorescence intensity may reflect antibody titre and allow visual comparison. However, the test requires a handheld fluorescent reader.

rKRP42-ICT results positively correlated with ELISA OD values, indicating improved detection at higher antibody titres. However, the test could detect ELISA OD values in a wide range in this study, 0.017–2.535. Despite this, detection was not uniform across this range of OD values, with some samples yielding negative rKRP42-ICT results despite having comparable ELISA OD values. Such variability in lateral flow performance at similar antibody concentrations may reflect differences in IgG affinity, epitope availability affecting sandwich complex formation, or IgG subclass variation. Assay sensitivity is influenced by antibody affinity, with higher-affinity antibodies enhancing detection [28]. This may partly explain discordant results among samples with similar IgG levels in lateral flow assays, given the time-limited binding kinetics of ICTs compared with ELISA. In addition, localized CL due to L. donovani elicits variable humoral responses, which may further contribute to inconsistent IgG detection [12, 29]. The high fluorescence quantum yield (84%) and uniform nanoparticle size distribution highlight the robustness of the AIE nanoparticle platform, suggesting that optimization of antigen or antibody components could substantially enhance diagnostic performance.

Repeat rKRP42-ICT testing using serum diluted and transported at room temperature demonstrated that higher IgG titres were reliably detected. However, some initially positive samples became negative, likely reflecting antibody deterioration during transportation at room temperature. This may also explain the lower IgG titers observed among patients in the current study compared to our previous work, where ELISA was performed locally under optimal serum storage conditions. ELISA could not be performed locally for this study due to logistical constraints.

The findings of this study highlight ongoing challenges in diagnosing CL caused by Leishmania donovani in Sri Lanka. The routine SSS positivity was only 55.2% in this cohort, consistent with previously reported variable sensitivities [11, 12], indicating that up to 45% of clinic attendees may be missed at initial testing. Our previous work has demonstrated that true positive case numbers exceed those detected by SSS among both clinically confirmed and doubtful cases [30]. In the present study, despite its known limitations, SSS was used as the reference standard rather than a standard incorporating higher-sensitivity methods such as culture or molecular assays. This approach was adopted because SSS remains the only routinely and practically available diagnostic test in the healthcare sector settings in Sri Lanka, particularly in high-endemic areas, whereas more sensitive methods are not consistently accessible. Accordingly, this study was designed to reflect the real-world diagnostic capacity of these clinics and to evaluate whether the rKRP42-ICT could enhance case detection within these constraints. If performance estimates were based on higher-sensitivity methods, they would be less representative of the practical utility of the test in routine settings where such methods are unavailable.

The study population—predominantly middle-aged males presenting early with lesions on exposed areas—reflects known epidemiological patterns in Sri Lanka [31]. The absence of atypical lesions may indicate early healthcare-seeking and increased awareness in endemic districts, although the small sample size may also have contributed.

The rKRP42-ICT detected 43.1% of clinically confirmed CL patients. Although sensitivity (43.8%) and specificity (57.7%) relative to SSS were modest, the assay identified 11 additional smear-negative cases. The observed specificity of 100% among the independent negative controls suggests the rKRP42-ICT has a very low false-positive rate in individuals without disease and is less likely to incorrectly label the uninfected as infected, or other skin lesions as leishmaniasis. In the context of surveillance and control programmes, high specificity would support the identification of cases/subclinical cases and thereby the identification of transmission hotspots. However, these negative controls were healthy Japanese individuals and may not fully represent healthy endemic populations in Sri Lanka, where background exposure and potential cross-reactive immune responses may differ. Therefore, future phases of the study should include healthy endemic controls to provide a more representative estimate of specificity in the local setting. Furthermore, the estimated specificity of 57.7% for the rKRP42-ICT was calculated using SSS as the reference standard. However, given the known limitations of SSS, some true positive cases may have been misclassified as negative. If a higher-sensitivity method such as PCR had been used, additional true positive cases within the SSS-negative cohort might have been identified, which could have influenced the current specificity estimate.

Consistent with our previous findings using rKRP42 IgG ELISA [12], combining rKRP42-ICT with SSS increased case detection from 55.2 to 74.1%. The ICT offers several operational advantages over ELISA. Unlike ELISA, which requires batching, laboratory infrastructure, and longer processing times, the rKRP42-ICT provides a rapid, PoC result for a supplementary diagnosis from a small volume of serum within approximately ten minutes, enabling same-day decision-making. The lack of correlation between ELISA OD values and SSS results indicates that microscopy positivity does not directly reflect systemic antibody levels, supporting antibody-based supplementary testing for smear-negative but clinically evident cases. In routine practice, PCR is considered if SSS is negative. However, PCR availability is limited in the public sector, and patient-borne costs restrict its use in resource-poor settings. As a result, clinicians may be compelled to treat empirically, although this approach is discouraged. The rKRP42-ICT could potentially reduce reliance on PCR, save costs, minimize invasive lesion biopsies, and reduce the need for empirical treatment in resource-limited settings.

The microscopy/SSS results in the present study did not correlate with ELISA OD values. This is consistent with previous findings demonstrating that parasite scores obtained by SSS correlate well with qPCR estimates but not with rKRP42 IgG titres [32]. Furthermore, the rKRP42 ICT showed only slight agreement with SSS. This likely reflects the detection of different subsets of CL cases: those with microscopically detectable amastigotes and those mounting a measurable antibody response, which may not fully overlap. These findings suggest that parasite burden in lesions, as assessed by SSS, does not necessarily correspond to the magnitude of the humoral immune response, as reported previously [32]. This is also supported by rKRP42-ICT detecting antibodies in 11 cases who were SSS negative. Therefore, the use of rKRP42 ICT in smear-negative clinically suspected CL cases may offer additional value in identifying cases missed by microscopy.

In this study, rKRP42-ICT positivity and ELISA OD values were higher in patients with ulcerative and larger lesions. Previous studies have shown that large, active, and ulcerative lesions are associated with a robust pro-inflammatory response, including elevated TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-12, IL-4, and IL-10 in CL [33–36]. This has also been demonstrated in Sri Lankan CL [36]. They reflect an activated immune response of both cellular and humoral components of immunity in CL patients. Hence, the high pro-inflammatory response in active disease often coexists with higher IgG titres [37, 38]—reflecting disease severity and antigen load. This may explain higher IgG detection in patients with multiple lesions, likely reflecting greater antigen load due to multiple bites and enhanced immune activation. Hence, IgG detection can indicate active CL, which supports the utility of IgG-based ICTs in guiding treatment decisions at the clinic level. This cohort did not demonstrate a linear relationship between IgG detection and lesion duration, consistent with earlier studies from Sri Lanka with larger sample sizes, where IgG responses were variable and detected at high rates in both early and chronic lesions [12, 39]. Although this non-linearity may affect the predictive value of serological assays, it may also be advantageous, as such tests could detect antibodies regardless of lesion duration. This contrasts with the slit-skin smear, where parasite numbers are known to decline in chronic lesions, reducing detectability [40]. In such cases, supportive evidence from an immune-based assay may still be valuable. It is also possible that the small sample size contributed to these findings.

The rK39 ICT, based on a recombinant kinesin-like antigen of L. infantum, is widely used for VL serodiagnosis [14] but its performance in CL due to L. donovani has been inconsistent. In this study, rK39 positivity was low (3/58), consistent with previous reports of poor performance in localized CL in Sri Lanka [12, 41]. Even Sri Lankan VL cases have shown variable rK39 responses, possibly due to antigenic differences in the local strain [29]. We performed the rK39 test in the current study only as a comparator, because it is the currently available immunochromatographic test for leishmaniasis, and its antigenic sequence shares homology with rKRP42. In contrast to rK39, rKRP42-based assays (ICT and ELISA) showed higher reactivity, supporting the antigen’s relevance for CL in Sri Lanka. Notably, the three rK39-positive patients had among the highest ELISA OD values in this cohort, consistent with our previous findings that rK39 positivity was associated with very high rKRP42 IgG levels [12]. This likely reflects stronger overall antibody responses, possibly due to greater antigenic stimulation from active, high-burden, or multiple lesions, indicating a broad humoral response.

CL Detect™ is the only other rapid diagnostic test previously evaluated in Sri Lanka. Its performance has been variable across regions and Leishmania species, and a recent review concluded that sensitivity is insufficient for routine CL diagnosis despite high specificity [20]. In Sri Lanka, CL Detect™ showed poor sensitivity, detecting lesions with higher parasite scores > 2 + only, and failed to detect any additional cases that were negative by SSS or PCR. Hence, CL Detect™ did not demonstrate utility as a combination or supplementary diagnostic tool. In contrast, the rKRP42-ICT for CL by L. donovani achieved higher sensitivity and utility as a supportive tool in a patient with clinical CL. Compared with CL Detect™, the rKRP42-ICT achieved higher sensitivity and utility as a potential supplementary diagnostic tool, with the advantage of venous blood sampling rather than the invasive dental broach sampling and tissue lysis steps required for CL Detect™. Furthermore, CL Detect™ is recommended only for ulcerative lesions of < 4 months’ duration [17, 19], whereas the rKRP42-ICT showed no association with lesion duration, suggesting broader clinical applicability. However, IgG levels decline following cure of CL [42], suggesting that sensitivity may be reduced in chronic or healing lesions. A limitation of the present study is that parasite load in lesions was not quantified.

This is the first evaluation of an AIE nanoparticle-labelled IgG ICT for CL in Sri Lanka, demonstrating the feasibility of IgG detection and potential ability for supplementary diagnosis. Rapid diagnostic tests for the serodiagnosis of CL remain scarce globally. The rKRP42-ICT showed potential for improved case detection as an exploratory-level tool and is stable at room temperature without cold-chain requirements. The AIE platform provides a promising approach for point-of-care diagnostics due to its fluorescence stability. Limitations include enrolment of only clinically confirmed CL cases, without disease controls (e.g., other conditions mimicking CL), which restricts specificity estimates, and the small sample size, consistent with this pilot study. Sensitivity and specificity estimates were determined in comparison with the routine reference test, SSS, which has known limitations and may therefore have influenced the true values of these measures. Further optimization of antigen and detection chemistry may improve diagnostic performance. The test needs to be evaluated among a larger cohort of CL, along with local control samples, in future studies.

Conclusion

This pilot study demonstrates the potential utility of an rKRP42 IgG-based RDT (rKRP42-ICT) as a supplementary tool for diagnosing cutaneous leishmaniasis caused by L. donovani in Sri Lanka. While SSS microscopy remains the routine method, its limited sensitivity misses a substantial proportion of clinically confirmed cases. The addition of rKRP42-ICT improved case detection by 20% and correlated with higher IgG titers (by ELISA). The test offers rapid, minimally invasive, clinic-level detection, stability at room temperature, and complements SSS by identifying a subset of smear-negative cases. Based on an AIE nanoparticle-labelled sandwich immunoassay, it provides high fluorescence yield, strong signal-to-noise, and robust particle uniformity, enabling sensitive detection across a wide range of IgG titres. This first evaluation of an AIE nanoparticle-based serological ICT for CL in Sri Lanka supports its role as a potential complementary diagnostic tool. Further antigen and assay optimization, along with larger controlled studies, are warranted to refine performance and define its role in national surveillance and case-management strategies.
