Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a top global health problem that occurs when pathogens become resistant to the drugs designed to kill them [1, 2]. Drug-resistant pathogens are emerging due to selective pressure driven by the overuse and misuse of antimicrobials, self-medication practices, and inadequate infection prevention and control measures [3, 4]. As a result of drug resistance, these antimicrobials become ineffective and leading to a serious challenge for healthcare and the public health system by increasing the risk of disease transmission, mortality, and severe illness [5–8]. This makes AMR a global dilemma that jeopardizes the health of populations across the globe. Consequently, urgent interventions are required before AMR reaches a critical point, where many individuals may no longer be treatable for life-threatening infections due to the lack of effective drugs [9]. In response, WHO adopted the AMR Global Action Plan (GAP) in 2015, which contains five strategic objectives to address AMR challenges: improve awareness and understanding of AMR, strengthen surveillance and research, reduce the incidence of infections, optimize the use of antimicrobial medicines, and ensuring sustainable investment in the development of new treatments [10–13].

Ethiopia is among the countries that adopted the AMR GAP and established the national AMR surveillance system in 2017 [14]. The national AMR surveillance system generates laboratory-based antimicrobial resistance profiles and reports to WHO Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System (GLASS). This data flow supports evidence-based strategies to prevent and control antimicrobial resistance at both national and global levels. The national AMR surveillance system prioritizes six pathogens for the AMR surveillance system implementations. These pathogens include Klebsiella spp, Acinetobacter spp, Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas spp, Staphylococcus aureus, and Enterococcus spp from blood and urine specimens [15]. Bacterial infections remain a leading cause of death in Ethiopia [16, 17].

Globally, E. coli is the leading causative agent of urinary tract infection (UTI) [18]. Thus, understanding the influence of community- and hospital-acquired UTIs is essential in the implementation of AMR surveillance system. These infections are frequently associated with multidrug-resistant organisms that complicate treatment options and contribute to the spread of antimicrobial resistance [19]. The increasing emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) E. coli, particularly strains resistant to commonly used antibiotics, has become the main challenge for effective treatment [20]. In Ethiopia, due to limited laboratory capacity and delays in diagnostic results, empirical treatment of UTI is frequently initiated without conducting AST [16, 21]. This increases the resistance that complicates the clinical management of UTIs and limits available therapeutic options [22]. Consequently, a continuous AMR surveillance implementation is essential to support evidence-based treatment guidelines and improve patient outcomes. The establishment of the Ethiopian AMR surveillance system has a vital role in addressing this issue by collecting and analyzing laboratory-based AMR surveillance data to improve patient outcomes.

Studies across various regions in Ethiopia on E.coli resistance patterns confirm that the need for continuous monitoring and tailored interventions [23, 24]. Several studies have reported that high levels of resistance to widely used antibiotics, including ampicillin, amoxicillin, and tetracycline [25, 26]. For instance, a study conducted in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, reported that E. coli was the most predominant bacterium, showing the highest resistance rate of 100% to Amoxicillin and 98.9% to Ampicillin [26].

Previous studies of antimicrobial resistance conducted in Ethiopia were primarily based on cross-sectional and limited to specific health facilities. As a result, these studies provide only localized and short-term insights into the burden of antimicrobial resistance in the community setting. The current study, in contrast, utilized data from the national laboratory-based AMR surveillance system, which includes multiple sentinel sites across the country and covers five years period (2020–2024). This approach enables a more comprehensive assessment of antimicrobial profiles and trends at the national level. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence and antimicrobial resistance trends of E. coli over five years from 2020 to 2024 among patients with UTI processed in 16 laboratory-based AMR surveillance sites in Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study area, design and period

A laboratory-based retrospective AMR surveillance study was conducted in Ethiopia for five years period from 2020 to 2024. This national AMR surveillance program is designed to systematically and continuously collect microbiology data through a coordinated network of hospitals and public health laboratories. For this study, data were obtained from 16 laboratory-based sentinel sites representing five regions and one city administration at the national level. These microbiology laboratory data included detailed patient-level information, including sex, age, clinical characteristics, and specimen collection dates [24, 27].

Laboratory-based sentinel sites selection

The Ethiopian AMR surveillance system was initially started with a limited number of sentinel laboratory sites and later expanded in phases to improve national representativeness. The process of laboratory site selection began with identifying hospitals and public health laboratories that have the capability of performing microbiology laboratory tests. To validate this, an AMR readiness assessment was carried out using standard tools developed by the CDC’s International Infection Control Program and American Society for Microbiology (ASM). This assessment examined the laboratory’s practices through observations, laboratory document reviews, and interviews with staff and administration. Following these standard tools, over the five years from 2020 to 2024, 16 sentinel laboratory sites were identified and integrated into the AMR surveillance network across five regions and one administration. Based on the AMR capacity assessment results, the identified laboratory-based sentinel sites have been further divided into three implementation phases: phase-I includes four sites, phase-II five sites, and phase-III seven sites (Fig. 1). Ethiopia’s surveillance system is designed to link these sentinel sites with the national reference laboratory at EPHI to ensure their successful implementation and data management [14, 15, 24].

National laboratory-based AMR surveillance system implementation phases, Ethiopia

AMR data collection and processing

The AMR data collection process is carried out by sentinel site laboratories. At sentinel sites, following the standard operating procedures (SOPs) adapted from the Clinical and Laboratory Standard Institute (CLSI) guidelines (M100, 30–34 editions), various clinical specimens such as blood, urine, sputum, CSF, pus/wound, ear/eye discharges, and tracheal aspirates were collected from patients. These specimens are cultured to isolate priority pathogens, and then antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) is performed using the disk diffusion method. Patients’ demographic and clinical details, AST results, test methods, specimen date, and antibiotics were meticulously recorded in a logbook and then entered into WHONET software with regular backup data generated at each sentinel site.

The validity and efficiency of laboratory data collection at AMR surveillance sites are a principal concern for focal persons. Focal persons from all sentinel sites are responsible for collecting and sending raw data to the national reference laboratory (NRL) every month. The NRL compiles and analyzes these data. The NRL is also responsible for supervising these surveillance activities across all sentinel sites. At the end, the isolates collected from the sites are re-cultured at the NRL for quality assurance, and any discrepant results are further investigated for correction [15, 28].

Specimen collection, processing, and bacterial identification

Specimens were collected from both outpatients and inpatients attending health facilities. Specimens were collected from patients suspected of having urinary tract infections, following instructions given by trained health professionals, and placed in appropriate sterile containers for routine diagnostic purposes. Patient demographic data, type of specimens, time of collection, types of tests required, patient location, and other information were recorded in the standardized bacteriology request form by clinicians. The specimens were transported to the laboratory and processed with in 30 min of collection.

Specimens that met the laboratory’s acceptance criteria were received and inoculated onto appropriate culture media, then incubated at the appropriate temperature and time conditions. The identification of E. coli was performed using conventional biochemical tests methods available at the time of testing. For conventional identification, a macroscopic colony characterization (color, size, shape, and texture) were assessed, followed by gram staining and a series of biochemical tests (Triple sugar iron agar, lysine iron agar, sulfide indole motility, Simmons Citrate Agar, urea agar, and oxidase) were used. The biochemical and culture media used were obtained from different manufacturers: (Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, Hampshire, England), (Liofilchem, Roseto degli Abruzzi, Italy), (Hardy diagnostics, Santa Maria, California, and Springboro, Ohio, United States).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST)

For antimicrobial susceptibility testing of these organisms, either of the following methods available at the time of testing was used: the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method on the Muller Hinton agar, Vitek 2 compact, and BD phoenix M50 systems. The most recent version of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute M100 available during the study period was utilized to select the antibiotics and interpret the antimicrobial susceptibility testing results. For antimicrobial susceptibility testing by VITEK 2 Compact, we used the following cards: VITEK 2 AST-GN86, VITEK 2 AST-GN72, and VITEK 2 AST-GN67. The BD Phoenix NMIC/ID-431 panel was used for antimicrobial susceptibility testing using the BD Phoenix M50. Carbapenem resistance was defined as resistance to imipenem and/or meropenem.

Quality control

American-type culture collection (ATCC) strains, Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922, were used to check the quality of all the antimicrobial disks used as per CLSI guideline. Quality control of culture and biochemical media was done as per laboratory standard operating procedures and manufacturers recommendations.

Data analysis

The AMR surveillance data were registered into WHONET software and exported to .csv file to be analyzed using R programming language. Descriptive statistics were used to assess the prevalence of antimicrobial resistance and the distribution resistance along with patients’ demographic characteristics. Correspondence analysis (CA) was used to examine antimicrobial resistance profiles by visualizing the relationships between variables. The CA method is a graphical technique of data visualization, where the proximity of points indicates the degree of association. By applying CA to E. coli resistance data, the CA provides a clear understanding of resistance patterns and differentiates the E. coli resistance levels to antibiotics. In CA, points that are closer to each other are interpreted as being more strongly associated, while those farther apart indicate weaker associations [29]. The Cochran Armitage trend test was used to test the significance trends of antimicrobial resistance using the prop.trend.test() function in r program. It tests the null hypothesis of no linear trend in antimicrobial resistance over time against the alternative of a linear trend. Statistical significance was set at 5% alpha and p-values less than this threshold were considered as statistically significant.

Discussion

This study examined the prevalence and trends of antibiotic resistance in E. coli strains isolated from urine samples between 2020 and 2024 in Ethiopia. For this study, the laboratory-based retrospective AMR surveillance data were obtained from 16 sentinel sites in Ethiopia, 2020–2024. The AMR dataset included detailed patient-level and clinical information, AST results, specimen type and date, and antibiotic profiles, which are essential for understanding the growing concern of antimicrobial resistance in the country. A total of 4056 patients with 4696 E. coli isolates from urine specimens were included in the study. The number of isolates identified from different sentinel sites varied, with tertiary hospitals like TASH and SPHMMC reported the highest number of isolates over five years.

The study results showed a high prevalence of resistance (> 70%) was detected, particularly for commonly used antibiotics such as Ampicillin, Cefazolin, Cefotaxime, Cefuroxime, Tetracycline, Trimethoprim/Sulfamethoxazole, Ceftriaxone, and Ciprofloxacin throughout 2020–2024. These high resistance levels indicate a serious public health concern and higher burden of resistance in the community. In contrast, antibiotics such as Nitrofurantoin, Amikacin, Meropenem, and Imipenem showed low resistance rates (< 30%). These findings are consistent with the national reports from the EPHI for 2017–2021 [30]. Similarly, a retrospective study (2010–2019) at the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital reported high resistance rates (> 70%) for a common used antibiotics [23]. Other studies likewise reported the prevalence of antibiotic resistance comparable to our findings [31–34]. These consistent findings suggest a widespread burden of antimicrobial resistance in the country that requires urgent attention and underscores the need to strengthen antimicrobial stewardship to ensure the effectiveness of treatment options.

The resistance trends observed in the current study are broadly similar with that of resistance trends reported across the African region. For instance, studies from Kenya, Sudan, and Uganda where resistance rates to these antibiotics are reported to be high [35]. Similar findings have been reported in the WHO Africa region, indicating high resistance rates to commonly used antibiotics such as ampicillin, trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, and fluoroquinolones [36].

The analysis further revealed sex-based variations in resistance rates, with the overall prevalence in E. coli resistance were higher in male patients compared to females. Although higher resistance variation was observed in males, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as behavioral and healthcare utilization data were not included in this study. This finding is consistent with previous studies reported E. coli demonstrated a higher proportion of resistance in males compared to females [22, 37–39]. The age-specific analysis of resistance prevalence showed that high resistance distributions were more clustered in 45–54 age group. The previous study reported that aging leads to a decline in immune function, making individuals more resistant strains [40]. These findings provide insight into the importance of accounting for demographic factors when setting surveillance priorities.

The Cochran Armitage test results identified an increasing E. coli resistance trend for Amikacin, Cefepime, Meropenem, Nitrofurantoin, Ceftriaxone, Ciprofloxacin, and Cefazolin antibiotics, whereas Amoxicillin/Clavulanic acid exhibited a decreasing resistance trend. The increasing resistance to these antibiotics is particularly concerning, as it may reduce available treatment options and make infections more challenging to manage. Consequently, clinicians may be compelled to use less effective or more toxic alternative antibiotics. This situation is challenging for infection control efforts and may increase the overall burden on the healthcare system. Previous studies have reported that the overuse and misuse of antimicrobials contribute to the increasing trends of antimicrobial resistance in Ethiopia [41]. The high resistance rates observed in this study emphasize the need for the government to strengthen antimicrobial stewardship programs in the community setting and to effectively use surveillance data to develop national treatment guidelines.

The current study has some limitations. First, there is a methodological heterogeneity among AMR surveillance system sentinel sites in terms laboratory testing capacity and antimicrobial susceptibility testing practices. Second, the AMR surveillance data were obtained from 16 sentinel sites, the collected isolates may not fully represent all healthcare facilities in Ethiopia. Finally, the study relied on phenotypic antimicrobial AST, the molecular characterization of resistance mechanisms was not performed.

Conclusions

The study findings revealed that a high burden and increasing trends of antimicrobial resistance among E.coli isolates from urine samples. These results highlight a critical need to strengthen the national AMR surveillance systems and antimicrobial stewardship programs to help control the growing antimicrobial resistance in the country.
