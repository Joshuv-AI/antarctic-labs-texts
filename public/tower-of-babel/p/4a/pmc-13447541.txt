Epidemiology and Treatment of Inflammatory Bowel Disease in Pregnancy: Results from a Retrospective, Observational Study
Authors: Uma Mahadevan, Bruce E. Sands, Christina Ha, Doris Yimgang, Emma Yue, Carrie Huisingh, Lauren Law, Cecile T. J. Holweg, Lani Wegrzyn, Marla Dubinsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInflammatory bowel disease (IBD) can adversely affect conception and pregnancy outcomes. Disease management must balance the benefits and risks for both mother and foetus.AimTo investigate real-world pregnancy incidence and treatment patterns among women with IBD.MethodsThis retrospective, observational study used data from the MerativeTM MarketScan® Commercial and Multi-state Medicaid Databases and included women aged 15–44 years diagnosed with Crohn’s disease (CD) or ulcerative colitis (UC). The incidence analysis also included a non-IBD cohort matched 2:1 to patients in the IBD cohort. The treatment pattern analysis included women with a pregnancy occurring on or after January 1, 2017. Primary outcomes were pregnancy incidence for women with IBD vs. matched non-IBD controls, and IBD medications among pregnant women.ResultsAmong 59,652 patients with CD and 50,452 patients with UC, pregnancy incidence was lower for patients with IBD vs. non-IBD matched controls (incidence rate ratio [95% confidence interval]: CD, 0.78 [0.76, 0.80]; UC, 0.76 [0.74, 0.78]). Treatment patterns were assessed among 3862 and 3269 pregnancies from 3235 and 2785 patients with CD and UC, respectively. Medication use was generally lower during pregnancy vs. before or after pregnancy. The most common medications were biologics for CD (23.6–34.1% across trimesters) and aminosalicylates for UC (25.0–34.9%). Pre-conception counselling and IBD-related surgery/hospitalisation were associated with increased biologic use during pregnancy.ConclusionsPatients with vs. without IBD had lower rates of pregnancy. Treatment patterns in recent years reflect current guidelines for IBD management during pregnancy. Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) includes Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), two chronic, inflammatory conditions that frequently impact women of childbearing age [1]. While there is no clear sex difference in UC incidence, CD incidence is slightly higher for women vs. men [2]. Female sex hormones have been linked to the development of IBD in susceptible individuals and can exacerbate gastric symptoms [1]. Patients with IBD can experience sexual dysfunction as a result of their symptoms, and IBD surgeries, especially those involving the pelvis (e.g., ileal pouch-anal anastomosis, proctectomy), can affect fertility. Chronic inflammation may also impact conception, as well as absorption of key nutrients that are important for maintaining a healthy pregnancy [3]. Pregnancies that occur during disease flares have a higher risk of resulting in premature delivery or low birth weight [3].

Managing IBD during pregnancy can be challenging, as treatment decisions must consider both disease activity and the effects of medication on foetal and maternal health [3, 4]. Data on the safety and efficacy of emerging therapies can be limited by clinical trial requirements for contraceptive use and the need to discontinue participation if pregnancy occurs [5].

The American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) recommends that women maintain their pre-pregnancy biologic therapy dosing throughout all three trimesters and the postpartum period to prevent flares and avoid associated negative impacts to the mother and foetus [4]. Likewise, guidelines from the European Crohn’s and Colitis Organisation (ECCO) recommend continuation of biologics throughout pregnancy for women with active or difficult-to-control disease [3]. According to ECCO guidelines, discontinuation of anti-tumour necrosis factor (TNF) therapy before the third trimester is not recommended for patients in remission, as stopping treatment may result in symptom relapse and unfavourable pregnancy outcomes, although patients in long-term remission can consider discontinuation [3]. Decisions regarding discontinuation should be individualized for women in remission who are treated with non-biologic therapies, given the limited data assessing risks to the foetus [3]. Medications that are considered safe during pregnancy are also considered low risk for breastfeeding. Treatment should be resumed postpartum, whether women discontinue or continue biologic therapy throughout pregnancy [3]. Both ECCO and AGA recommend pre-conception counselling to improve disease management and medication adherence and, therefore, improve pregnancy outcomes [3, 4].

There is limited information regarding the frequency of pregnancy among women of childbearing age with and without IBD, as well as about the real-world treatment patterns of pregnant women with IBD. This study compares the incidence rate (IR) of pregnancy among women of childbearing age with and without IBD. This analysis also outlines the use of treatments before, during, and after pregnancy; evaluates treatment utilisation over time; and assesses factors associated with use of biologics during pregnancy.

Methods

Study Design and Cohorts

This observational claims-based study conducted a retrospective analysis using data from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2022, for incidence analysis, and from January 1, 2016, to December 31, 2022, for treatment-pattern analysis. Data were sourced from the MerativeTM MarketScan® Commercial and Multi-state Medicaid Databases. These databases contain deidentified, longitudinal, individual healthcare information (including medical or pharmacy claims, inpatient and outpatient diagnoses, and diagnostic testing or procedures) from over 200 million patients in the United States who have commercial or public health insurance [6]. As this study was noninterventional and used deidentified patient data, approval from an institutional review board was not required. All authors had access to the study data and reviewed and approved the final manuscript.

These analyses included women of childbearing age (15–44 years) diagnosed with CD or UC, except for the incidence analysis non-IBD cohort, which excluded women diagnosed with CD or UC. Diagnoses of CD or UC were identified from the most recent diagnostic codes and required ≥ 2 diagnostic codes for CD (International Classification of Diseases, 9th Revision—Clinical Modification [ICD-9-CM] 555.x or International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision—Clinical Modification [ICD-10-CM] K50.x) or UC (ICD-9-CM 556.x or ICD-10-CM K51.x) at any position occurring ≥ 7 days apart.

For the incidence analysis, patients were excluded if they had < 183 days of continuous healthcare plan enrolment (with gaps ≤ 45 days permitted) before or < 1 day of continuous enrolment after the index date. In the treatment pattern analysis, evidence of pregnancy that occurred on or after January 1, 2017, was required for inclusion. Pregnancy was considered using all available codes indicating weeks of gestation during the study period (Z3A.* codes or pregnancy outcome codes). Patients needed to be continuously enrolled for 12 months before their estimated last menstrual period (LMP) date. Patients were excluded from the study if they had not been diagnosed with UC or CD before the LMP date or if they had undergone a hysterectomy.

Assessments

The primary outcomes were the incidence of pregnancy among women with IBD and matched non-IBD controls, and exposure to medications for IBD among pregnant women with IBD, including biologics, systemic corticosteroids, immunomodulators, and aminosalicylates. Analyses of treatment during pregnancy were based on pharmacy and medical claims and assessed during the 12-month pre-pregnancy period, pregnancy period, and 12-month post-pregnancy period (see Supplementary Methods for detailed information regarding pregnancy periods). The overlap of medication with pregnancy was determined based on prescription fill dates and days of supply. Multiple exposures for the same biologic were considered a continuous treatment episode. For biologic exposure, the end date was considered three half-lives after the date of dispense or administration with an additional 7-day gap in coverage. Systemic corticosteroid, aminosalicylate, and immunomodulator coverage was considered using the number of days’ supply dispensed, allowing for a 7-day gap in coverage.

Further analyses examined the incidence of pregnancy across different age groups and examined the use of IBD medications during pregnancy across geographic regions, types of insurance, and whether patients had received pre-conception counselling within 12 months before their LMP. Baseline demographics, clinical characteristics, and comorbidities were determined using medical and pharmacy codes within the 12-month period before the index date. Pre-conception counselling was identified based on ICD-10-CM codes submitted by any healthcare provider (Table S1); similar approaches have been used in previous studies [7, 8].

Statistical Analysis

For the incidence analysis, the index date was defined based on the first diagnosis of CD or UC; for the treatment pattern analysis, the index date was the estimated LMP date for each pregnancy episode. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics, the IR of pregnancy among patients with IBD and non–IBD-matched controls, and the frequency of medication use. There was no imputation for missing data.

For the incidence analysis, the non-IBD cohort was matched 2:1 to patients in the IBD cohort based on age, sex, calendar time (inpatient or outpatient visit within 30 days of the index date of the matched IBD patient), and type of insurance. The pregnancy IR was calculated for UC and CD as the total number of cases divided by total person-years of follow-up (expressed as events/100 person-years). Adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards regression models to compare pregnancy rates between IBD and non-IBD cohorts, adjusting for comorbidities (obesity, diabetes, hypertension, anaemia, and Charlson Comorbidity Index scores), lifestyle factors (smoking and alcohol-related disorders, defined by ICD diagnosis codes), geographic regions, and insurance type.

For the treatment pattern analysis, medication use was stratified by trimester, region, year, insurance type, and whether pre-conception counselling occurred within 12 months before the LMP date. Multivariate logistic regression was used to assess the relationship between demographic factors and clinical characteristics, including age, geographic location, insurance type, comorbidities, medical history, and use of pre-conception counselling, as well as their association with exposure to biologic therapies.

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, North Carolina, United States).

Results

Pregnancy Incidence

Participants

Among 297,915 patients with CD and 385,175 patients with UC, 59,652 and 50,452, respectively, were included in this analysis (Fig. S1). The mean (standard deviation [SD]) age at the index date was 31.1 (8.0) years for women with CD, and 32.0 (7.8) years for women with UC. Most women with CD or UC had commercial health insurance (CD, 76.4%; UC, 83.9%; Table 1). Approximately one-fifth of patients in all cohorts used hormonal contraceptives during the baseline period. Demographics and baseline characteristics were generally similar between women in the IBD and matched non-IBD cohorts. However, the IBD cohorts had lower proportions of women with some comorbidities compared with the non-IBD cohorts, including obesity (IBD vs. non-IBD: CD, 9.7 vs. 17.8%; UC, 9.4 vs. 18.8%), diabetes (CD, 3.0 vs. 6.2%; UC, 2.9 vs. 6.1%), and hypertension (CD, 7.3 vs. 11.8%; UC, 6.7 vs. 12.2%). While the proportion of patients with any prior hospitalisation was similar between those with CD or UC and their respective matched cohorts, a greater proportion of patients with CD had a history of pre-index hospitalisation compared with patients with UC (13.1 vs. 8.8%, respectively). A greater proportion of patients with CD vs. UC had pre-index use of corticosteroids (13.6 vs. 9.3%) and biologics (16.7 vs. 8.1%). Pregnancy Incidence

When compared with matched controls, pregnancy rates were lower among women with IBD (IR ratio [IRR; 95% CI]: CD, 0.78 [0.76, 0.80]; UC, 0.76 [0.74, 0.78]; Table 2). While rates of pregnancy were consistently lower among patients with IBD vs. matched controls in all age groups examined, the IRR was highest among patients aged 25–30 years and lowest among patients aged 38–44 years (Table S2). The IR of pregnancy was generally similar between patients with CD and those with UC (Table 2). The aHRs for pregnancy were similar to the IRRs reported for the IBD vs. non–IBD-matched controls. Factors such as age, geographic region, and baseline comorbidities were not associated with pregnancy occurrence, except for Medicaid (vs. commercial) insurance, higher Charlson Comorbidity Index scores, and hypertension (Table S3).

Treatment Patterns

Participants

Among 103,866 women with CD and 96,785 women with UC, a total of 7186 and 6230 had evidence of a pregnancy occurring on or after January 1, 2017. Of these, 3235 and 2785 women met the full eligibility criteria, respectively (Fig. S2). There were 3862 pregnancy episodes among women with CD and 3269 among women with UC (Table 3). The mean (SD) age at estimated LMP (CD, 30.6 [5.5] years; UC, 31.7 [5.4] years) was similar between groups. A majority of pregnancy episodes occurred among women who had commercial insurance (CD, 66.1%; UC, 78.7%). Few pregnancies included formal pre-conception counselling (as defined by ICD-10-CM codes) within 12 months before the LMP (CD, 11.4%; UC, 12.2%). Medication Use in Pregnancy

The use of IBD medications was generally lower during pregnancy compared with pre- and post-pregnancy periods, with the lowest rate of medication use occurring during the third trimester for all classes of medication examined (Fig. 1). The greatest decrease in medication use during pregnancy occurred with systemic corticosteroids (from 17.1 to 16.0% within the 3-month period preceding pregnancy to 5.4 and 6.0% in the third trimester of pregnancy in CD and UC, respectively). The most commonly used medications during pregnancy were biologics in patients with CD (23.6–34.1% across trimesters) and aminosalicylates in patients with UC (25.0–34.9% across trimesters). The medication prescriptions that were most commonly filled after clinical confirmation of pregnancy were biologics in patients with CD (19.4%) and aminosalicylates in patients with UC (22.0%; Table S4).Fig. 1Medication use before, during, and after pregnancy among women with IBD. While there were no clear differences in medication use across different geographic regions (Table S5), the proportion of pregnancies involving treatment with biologic, immunomodulator, and aminosalicylate medications was consistently higher among patients with commercial insurance vs. those receiving Medicaid (Table S6). Discussion

In this real-world analysis of pregnancy rates among more than 300,000 women of childbearing age, those with IBD experienced a 22–24% lower incidence of pregnancy compared with matched women without IBD. This is slightly greater than rates reported in analyses of patients in Korea and Canada, which found an approximately 5 percentage point and 10% decrease in pregnancy incidence, respectively, among patients with IBD compared with age-matched controls [9, 10]. In our analysis, pregnancy IRRs between patients with IBD and matched non-IBD controls varied between age groups. Still, IRRs were consistently lower among women with IBD, with a 10–20% lower incidence in those aged 25–30 years and a 50% lower incidence in those aged 38–44 years. Although IBD may not directly impair female fertility [11], decreases in pregnancy rates among women with IBD may be related to disease flares, sexual dysfunction, potential impacts from pelvic surgery, and voluntary childlessness [3, 11–13]. In particular, rates of voluntary childlessness are 7–29% higher among patients with IBD, and rates of childlessness are particularly high among those in older age groups [14, 15]. However, in the population examined in this study, hormonal contraception use was generally similar between patients with IBD and non–IBD-matched controls, indicating voluntary childlessness may not fully account for the differences in pregnancy rates between the two groups.

Patients with CD and UC had similar pregnancy IRs, despite patients with CD having slightly higher rates of biologic use, corticosteroid use, and hospitalisation before the index date than patients with UC, indicating more severe disease among patients with CD. Additionally, patients with IBD and no prior treatment with biologic therapies had similar IRs of pregnancy as the overall IBD population, suggesting that less severe disease among these patients did not result in greater pregnancy rates. In contrast, a UK-based study reported lower fertility rates among women with indicators of more severe disease (including recent flares or pelvic surgery) compared with patients who did not have active IBD. This effect was not attributed to voluntary childlessness after adjusting for contraceptive use [11].

Medication use was lower during pregnancy compared with the periods before and after pregnancy. The medications that were most used and most initiated during pregnancy were aminosalicylates in patients with UC and biologics in patients with CD. An earlier ECCO consensus statement recommended stopping anti-TNF medications in the third trimester of pregnancy. However, updated guidelines from ECCO and recent recommendations from the AGA now advocate for the continued use of biologics throughout pregnancy [3, 4, 16]. These recommendations reflect the evolving understanding of the risks and benefits associated with managing IBD symptoms during pregnancy. These guidelines generally align with the temporal patterns observed in this US-based population, wherein the use of biologic therapies during pregnancy increased over the multi-year analysis period. This increase may also be partially attributed to the increasing availability of biologic therapies approved to treat CD and UC over time. While the use of biologic medications increased over time in each trimester, the use of medications generally decreased with advancing trimesters. Therefore, although the use of biologic therapies in the third trimester increased between 2017 and 2021, biologic use in the third trimester remained low compared with other pre- and post-pregnancy time points. Notably, in our analysis, the use of biologics initially increased in the post-pregnancy period, and then declined by 12 months postpartum. It is possible that women may have stopped taking their medications to breastfeed or that those who discontinued their medications during pregnancy did not restart postpartum as observed previously [17]. Our findings are generally consistent with a recent analysis of Merative™ MarketScan® data that focused on the use of biologics among patients with a variety of autoimmune conditions during and after pregnancy. The analysis noted that although use of biologics was reduced during pregnancy relative to the postpartum period, there was an increasing year-over-year trend of biologic use during pregnancy [17]. ECCO and AGA guidelines support the use of biologic therapies among patients with difficult-to-control disease [3, 4], which aligns with our finding that patients who had undergone prior IBD-related surgery were more likely to receive biologic therapies during pregnancy.

We found that the largest decreases in medication use during pregnancy were observed for systemic corticosteroids. This pattern aligns with current guidance stating that systemic corticosteroids should only be used as adjunctive therapy for flares. Long-term use of systemic corticosteroids for maintenance therapy may be associated with pre-term delivery, low birth weight, and an increased risk of gestational diabetes [4]. However, it is difficult to determine whether these effects can be attributed wholly to corticosteroid use or to the underlying effects of IBD [18]. For example, systemic corticosteroid use is associated with active IBD, and women with active disease may be less likely to conceive [11].

Most women in this analysis had commercial health insurance. Among these women, the rates of biologic, immunomodulator, and aminosalicylate medication use were higher, while corticosteroid exposure was lower compared with women receiving Medicaid. While this analysis did not assess pregnancy outcomes, healthcare equity and access to advanced therapies, pre-conception counselling, and longitudinal IBD care may contribute to successful pregnancy among women with IBD.

Both the AGA and ECCO recommend pre-conception counselling to improve treatment adherence [3, 4]. It is important to maintain symptom control during pregnancy, as active disease increases the risk for pregnancy complications [19]. Research has shown that treatment adherence among previously adherent patients with IBD decreases during pregnancy, possibly due to concerns about foetal outcomes [20]. Additionally, research indicates that voluntary childlessness in IBD can often be attributed to a lack of patient knowledge about IBD and pregnancy and that many patients with IBD are not satisfied with the level of available family planning counselling [12, 14]. Our findings, especially those from recent years, are consistent with AGA and ECCO guidelines for IBD management during pregnancy and demonstrate that patients are more likely to be treated with a biologic if they have had pre-conception counselling. It is possible that the pre-conception counselling rates observed in this study could be an indicator of patients’ overall access to healthcare resources, or for healthcare-seeking behavior [21]. Disease severity may encourage patients to be more proactive in managing their health, particularly if they already have more frequent interactions with healthcare providers [21]. The overall rates of pre-conception counselling observed in this analysis are low, which could indicate either a lack of pre-conception counselling or insufficient documentation of such counselling. In either case, these results highlight potential opportunities for better communication between healthcare providers and patients of childbearing potential. Further research is needed to determine how the interaction between healthcare access, disease severity, and pre-conception counselling may influence treatment with and adherence to biologic therapy in pregnancy.

Although these analyses provide real-world data on the incidence of pregnancy and the use of medications during pregnancy among thousands of women in the United States, there are several limitations that should be considered. This study was not designed to evaluate whether IBD specifically affects women’s ability to conceive. This is because the intention to conceive is not documented in such healthcare administrative data, and personal and demographic factors that may influence fertility, such as education level and race/ethnicity [22], are not captured in these data. Pre-conception counselling is likely under-captured in this analysis due to a lack of universal documentation for such counselling. While this study used a range of codes from a diverse group of providers to identify instances of pre-conception counselling, it is possible that pre-conception counselling is underreported based on the need to code for administrative purposes. The use of prescription fills as a proxy for medication use may also be a limitation of this analysis, as actual medication adherence may have varied. Likewise, disease activity is difficult to assess because clinical markers are not available in claims data. Although past hospitalisations, surgeries, and medication history could be used as an indicator of IBD severity, our analysis was not able to evaluate outcomes by whether patients had active vs. controlled disease at the time of conception or pregnancy, despite the known impacts of disease flares on fertility in IBD [11]. Selection bias may exist in some analyses, as patients with more severe disease may be more likely to seek counselling, receive regular monitoring, and require ongoing treatment with biologics. Outcomes, including efficacy of IBD management among pregnant women and neonatal health, were also not assessed, as they were outside the scope of this analysis.

Patients with IBD had lower rates of pregnancy compared with women without IBD. As IBD frequently affects women of childbearing age, understanding pregnancy incidence is valuable when designing studies to evaluate the safety of IBD medications for pregnant women. Future investigations are needed to evaluate the effects of disease severity and activity, as well as factors like voluntary childlessness, lifestyle choices, and comorbidities on pregnancy rates in women with IBD. Although the use of advanced medication to treat IBD in pregnancy is increasing over time, pre-conception counselling remains an important component of IBD management for women of childbearing age.
