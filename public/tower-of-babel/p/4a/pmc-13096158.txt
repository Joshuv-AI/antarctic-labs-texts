Mapping global avian influenza risk patterns through waterbird activity entropy
Authors: Yuzhe Li, Yuxin Qiao, Yue Zhan, Jinwei Dong, Mariëlle van Toor, Jonas Waldenström, A. Townsend Peterson, Qiang Zhang, Zhichao Li, Weipan Lei, Fanshu Du, Juan Pu, Dayan Wang, Xiangming Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Avian influenza viruses (AIV) pose a major zoonotic threat with pandemic potential. Waterbirds facilitate AIV spillovers into farm animals and humans through exposure and virus reassortment. Here, we propose waterbird activity entropy (WAE), an indicator of waterbird activity intensity based on monthly distributions of 779 species worldwide. WAE demonstrated high explanative power (AUC = 0.87 ± 0.001) for global avian influenza cases, particularly for H5N1, revealing the potential of WAE for identifying AIV exposure hotspots which cover 14% of global land area. Notably, the AIV exposure hotspots in the USA, EU, China, and India contain 52% of the globally exposed human population, 41% cattle, and 51% poultry. Despite reporting <1% of global cases, sub-Saharan Africa contains >300 Mha of hotspots area (15% globally), highlighting considerable surveillance gaps. This WAE-based framework enhances AIV risk assessment by incorporating waterbird residency time, offering critical insights for anticipating AIV emergence and improving surveillance.

Introduction

The last four influenza pandemics (in 1918, 1957, 1968, and 2009) originated primarily from avian influenza virus (AIV) strains or genetic reassortment of AIV1,2. Most spillover events among domestic animals and humans are related to various AIVs carried by bird species associated with wetlands, aquatic, and marine habitats3,4 (hereafter waterbirds). These various and recurring reassortments, coupled with frequent interactions between wild waterbirds and domestic animals, facilitate the virus’s ability to cross between diverse host species5,6, including wild and domestic birds, mammals, and occasionally humans7,8. For example, highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) subtype H5N1 viruses have rapidly expanded their host range among waterbirds (especially long-distance migratory seabird and shorebird species) in recent years, increasing the risk of cross-continental spreading of emerging AIVs3,9. Therefore, identification of waterbird diversity and activity is significant, with a high risk of AIV spillover from natural ecosystems.

Assessing cross-interface exposure risk areas (“hotspots” hereafter), where AIVs might be transferred from natural reservoirs to anthropogenic ecosystems, is critical for understanding AIV spread and emergence events6,10. The present avian influenza outbreak (H5N1, clade 2.3.4.4b) in the U.S. dairy cattle is thought to have originated from waterbirds4. This spillover event subsequently increased infection and transmission risk among occupationally exposed humans10. In late August 2024, 50% of herds in Colorado were reported to be affected, poultry infections led to the culling of nearly 1.8 million chickens, while nationwide, the situation was more severe, with over 50 million chickens culled11. Notably, phylogenomic analyses revealed that birds obtained from infected farms carried related viral sequences, suggesting future spillover risk could originate from a shared wild bird reservoir12. Concurrently, intensive poultry or cattle farms lacking adequate biosecurity measures may facilitate subsequent lateral spread among farms and increase the risk of zoonotic spillover to humans8. Therefore, identifying waterbird exposure hotspots for domestic animals (especially poultry and cattle) and humans are important for predicting and preventing potential and imminent avian influenza outbreaks5.

Most waterbird hosts for AIVs are generally highly mobile and tend to be pronounced long-distance seasonal migrants3,9, facilitating the lateral spread of AIVs13,14. For migratory waterbirds, mixed-species flocks with large numbers of individuals concentrated in relatively small areas likely increase cross-species transmission of the virus6,15, thus enhancing the risk of reassortants. Therefore, determining the seasonal migration patterns and residency times of waterbirds is critical for improving the understanding of the dynamic patterns of AIV exposure risk from waterbird hosts. Nonetheless, simulating the distributions of waterbirds is difficult owing to their high mobility and, in the case of migratory species, shifts in their seasonal distributions13,16,17. With the increasing availability of citizen-scientist observation data (e.g., GBIF or eBird)18,19, high-performance computing and the development of machine-learning-based species distribution models (SDMs)20, simulations of waterbird distributions at fine temporal resolution have become feasible.

To assess the risk of virus sharing via contact among waterbird species, different metrics are needed, including the richness (intensity), duration, and evenness (frequency) of the host species17,21. An effective indicator for quantifying spatial and temporal overlap would facilitate more accurate quantification of avian influenza exposure risk. The Shannon entropy metric has been widely applied to evaluate the α-diversity of species communities22,23, and it has also been used to incorporate residency time as a metric to quantify the intensity of human activity24. Here, we used waterbird activity entropy (WAE) to evaluate the richness, duration, and evenness of the dominant AIV natural host species. By integrating WAE with domestic animal and human densities, we infer contact possibility and estimate AIV exposure risk at cross-interfaces (i.e., among waterbirds, domestic animals, and humans)15,18.

Although the global dataset for avian influenza surveillance has improved25,26, data coverage remains uneven and is heavily concentrated in developed countries and regions27. This disparity is evident in the sparse and spatially skewed distribution of avian influenza detection data in developing areas such as Africa and Southeast Asia28. If WAE can effectively reflect exposure risk and predict AIV spillover, it can be used to map and identify high-risk regions, helping to address these surveillance gaps. This approach holds significant potential for guiding global efforts against the spread of AIV. In this study, we developed an AIV exposure risk assessment framework by considering the detailed spatial and temporal distributions of waterbird species richness and identifying global hotspots of human, cattle, and poultry exposure risk to waterbird activities. First, we used data obtained via the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) to simulate the monthly distributions of 779 waterbird species globally via three widely used machine learning models: random forest (RF), maximum entropy (Maxentropy), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Second, we introduced the WAE metric to quantify waterbird activity intensity, assess its spatial correspondence with AIV cases, and evaluate its predictive power for AIV cases after excluding likely poultry-to-poultry transmission (Supplementary Fig. 8). Third, we used WAE to estimate human, cattle, and poultry exposure risks, identifying global hotspots and waterbird functional groups contributing to high AIV exposure risk.

Results

Geographical relationship between WAE and reported AIV cases

Overall, species richness varied across Wallace’s zoogeographic realms (“Wallace’s realms” hereafter, see “Methods”)29, with the eastern Neotropical realm showing the highest richness (Fig. 1). The monthly richness pattern changes reflected waterbird migrations (Supplementary Fig. 3), with notable fluctuations captured by the coefficient of variation (CV) (Fig. 1a). Northern Hemisphere regions, such as the northern Nearctic and Palearctic, had lower richness but high CVs. The intra-annual variation in species richness due to waterbird migration exhibited latitudinal patterns (Supplementary Fig. 5), likely driven by the availability of forage (Supplementary Fig. 6). We examined waterbird activity intensity by using an innovative indicator, waterbird activity entropy (WAE), which considers both species richness and the residence time of each species (Supplementary Fig. 4). The spatial pattern of WAE revealed that the southeastern Nearctic, southeastern Neotropical, eastern and southern Palearctic, and eastern Ethiopian regions presented relatively high levels of WAE (Fig. 1b). In particular, the Oriental realm presented the highest WAE across different Wallace realms.

We evaluated the spatial predictive ability of WAE for the distribution of waterbird-associated AIV cases using global surveillance data and a confusion matrix (Methods, Fig. 2a). WAE demonstrated high explanative power (AUC = 0.87, s.d. 0.001) for global avian influenza cases after applying spatiotemporal filtering to exclude local poultry-to-poultry transmission events (Supplementary Text 2). The framework also exhibited robustness when compared with the unfiltered original avian influenza case data (Supplementary Fig. 17a, AUC = 0.86) and validated with the outbreak data excluding poultry-adapted subtypes (Supplementary Fig. 17b, AUC = 0.91). The optimal WAE threshold of 4.11 (Supplementary Fig. 8c), determined via the Youden indices from the ROC curve, identified high-risk regions. Over 80% of filtered AIV-positive cases occurred in these high WAE areas, which cover just 23% of the global land area, highlighting WAE as a reliable indicator for assessing AIV exposure risk and surveillance priorities.

We found higher predictive sensitivity for H5N1 (86%) than for all AIV cases and for HPAI (86%) than for LPAI (Fig. 2d). Additionally, the WAE’s predictive capacity varied by country, with accuracy correlated with GDP (Fig. 2b, c). Less developed African countries (e.g., Uganda, Mozambique, and Senegal) presented lower prediction accuracies, whereas developed European countries with robust surveillance efforts achieved higher accuracy (91%) in predicting AIV incidence on the basis of WAE. This disparity highlights that limitations in data availability, rather than the model itself, likely drive underestimations of AIV risk. It underscores the critical to strengthen surveillance and reporting infrastructure to better anticipate and mitigate spillover events.

Global hotspots of AIV exposure risk to waterbirds

We initially overlaid WAE with human, cattle, and poultry densities to identify hotspots of high WAE coinciding spatially with high densities of spillover hosts, suggesting a risk of exposure to AIV for humans, cattle, and poultry, respectively (see Methods, Supplementary Fig. 9). Regions with intense human exposure risk were distributed across the southeastern USA, Europe, southwestern and eastern Africa, and southern Asia (Supplementary Fig. 9a, d), which coincided with those for cattle and poultry exposure risk (Supplementary Fig. 9b, e, c, f), albeit with local discrepancies. Regions with only poultry exposure risks were observed in the southeastern USA and eastern Europe, whereas central India presented a high exposure risk for both humans and cattle.

Furthermore, we classified exposure risk hotspots into seven types on the basis of different combinations of these three exposure targets: human, cattle, and poultry (Fig. 3a). Notably, 4.27 billion people, 0.41 billion cattle and 9.45 billion poultry, were in exposure hotspots, covering approximately 14% of the global land area and representing 55%, 36%, and 51%, respectively, of the total human, cattle, and poultry populations. Specifically, 25% of the people, 16% of the cattle, and 26% of the poultry were considered to be exposed in areas where all three susceptible populations were jointly exposed (Fig. 3b). At the continental scale, Asia had the largest hotspot area (536 Mha), followed by Europe (522 Mha), North America (408 Mha), and Africa (352 Mha) (Fig. 3c). Nationally, the top four countries/regions with the largest hotspot areas were the USA, the EU, China, and India (hereafter “key exposure countries/regions”), each surpassing 110 Mha (Fig. 3c). The USA included the largest cattle-exposed region and cattle- and poultry-exposed region. China and India had the highest exposed human populations, each exceeding 0.65 billion people (Supplementary Fig. 10b), whereas India led in exposed cattle (0.07 billion). China and the USA hold 2.12 billion and 1.52 billion exposed poultry, respectively. These four key exposure countries/regions represent 46% of the global hotspot area (6% of the global land area) and 44% of the collectively reported AIV cases. They should lead in strengthening surveillance and bear the special responsibility of preventing and controlling the spillover and spread of avian influenza.

Waterfowl were the group for which human density was most strongly correlated with activity entropy in China compared with other functional groups (r = 0.71, Fig. 4a). The activity of confirmed waterfowl host was also most positively correlated with cattle density in the USA and China. In the USA, waterfowl constituted the highest proportion (84%) of confirmed AIV hosts and had a significantly higher Moran’s I related to spatial relationships (Moran’s I = 0.27) with cattle than other confirmed functional groups (Fig. 4b). In China, the activity of waterfowl with confirmed hosts was also positively correlated with poultry density in China (r = 0.74, Supplementary Table 3).

In addition to waterfowl, seabirds presented stronger correlations with human (r = 0.62, Moran’s I = 0.18) and poultry density (r = 0.54, Moran’s I = 0.21) than other confirmed functional groups in the USA (Fig. 4b). As a primary functional group with high WAE, seabirds are predominantly found in coastal regions of the EU and China (Supplementary Fig. 11). They occupied the largest hotspot area in the EU (290 Mha, 95% hotspots). In the past, surveillance efforts lacked specificity in targeting species or groups. Our findings indicate the need for more targeted surveillance at the species or group level with evidence of spatial overlap, highlighting the need to prioritize waterfowl and seabirds in future surveillance efforts.

Discussion

Capacity of waterbird activity entropy to explain patterns of AIV cases

Accurate information regarding the temporal and spatial distributions of waterbirds can capture patterns of past AIV detections (Fig. 2a), as the diversity and activity of potential waterbird hosts might effectively increase pathogen transmission3,9,30. Considering the temporal dimension is crucial, seasonal peaks in waterbird species richness can inform strategically focused surveillance efforts (Supplementary Fig. 5), thereby maximizing the efficiency of limited resources31. Our results indicate that the WAE metric proposed here, which integrates spatial and temporal waterbird activity intensity with improvements in waterbird distribution modeling and incorporates water availability (water proportion) in SDMs, is a promising candidate for predicting AIV exposure risk. Notably, the WAE demonstrated consistently acceptable predictive capacity (AUC > 0.7) across areas with varying poultry density levels, further underscoring its robustness (Supplementary Fig. 18), although its predictive performance declined as the assessment area narrowed to regions of higher poultry density. Therefore, WAE has the potential to predict AIV epidemics and guide effective surveillance strategies.

Furthermore, the sensitivity of WAE in predicting AIV cases varied among AIV subtypes. Notably, WAE showed better predictive power for highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI, Fig. 2d) than low pathogenic avian influenza (LPAI). Waterbirds have been verified as the natural main reservoirs of HPAI H53,9,13,32 and may act as dominant transmission vectors after the emergence of 2.3.4.4.b. Although LPAI viruses are often underreported, they may serve as evolutionary precursors to highly pathogenic strains, as illustrated by past emergence events such as the evolution of H5N8 and other reassorted subtypes33.

Dispersal of H5N1 HPAI viruses has been shown to occur mainly synchronously with waterbird movement13. As WAE captures wild waterbird migration and encounters, its predictive performance should be better for waterbird-associated AIV subtypes (e.g., H5N1, H5N5, H5N8, Supplementary Fig. 8d). Therefore, WAE performs better in predicting the spread of emerging H5Nx subtype AIVs, including the outbreak of H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b in the USA in spring 2024. A predictive deficit of WAE exists for LPAI subtypes (e.g., H7N9, for which the sensitivity was only 67%, Supplementary Fig. 8d), which are prevalent in poultry25, but it remains reliable when all subtypes of AIV cases are included (sensitivity of 80%, Fig. 2). Therefore, WAE can be applied to more effectively search for infected birds and develop strategies for safer poultry and cattle industries.

Enhanced surveillance is needed in developing countries

The spatial prediction accuracy of WAE for AIV-affected cases is greater in developed countries/regions, such as Europe and North America, but less clear in areas with limited surveillance, such as sub-Saharan Africa (Fig. 2b), primarily owing to lower surveillance levels in those areas. Specifically, Tanzania, Ethiopia, and Zambia each contained hotspots exceeding 34 Mha, yet no AIV cases were recorded, which constrained early detection and epidemic response27. Therefore, increased funding and resources for enhanced surveillance in developing and less developed countries are crucial to improving surveillance27,34, which would not only strengthen the prediction capacity of WAE but also help prevent HPAI from developing silently in under monitored hotspots32,35.

The surveillance efforts in developing countries may differ. First, varying cross-interfaces of AIV exposure highlight the need to prioritize specific anthropogenic ecosystems. The greatest exposure risk in China is from the interface between poultry (China produces nearly 5 billion, or 46%, of global domestic ducks and geese) and humans, especially in southeastern China, where waterfowl, large wading birds, and free-range domestic ducks and geese share habitats36. India and some African countries (e.g., South Africa and Ethiopia, see Fig. 3c) face similar challenges at the interface between cattle farming and humans37. AIV surveillance in these countries should be more targeted and tailored to the specific exposure types and populations at risk.

Second, global surveillance efforts are unevenly distributed, even spotty, across varied countries. Despite accounting for 15% of global hotspot areas, sub-Saharan Africa reports <1% of positive cases, with detections limited to a few countries (Fig. 2). In contrast, the recorded positive cases are numerous in Europe. Large populations of waterbirds migrate between Europe and Africa16, with infected hosts might carry and transmit AIV over long distances from Europe to those less developed regions in the south13. However, the uneven distribution of surveillance efforts has led to a systematic underestimation of risk in the developing countries/regions, as previous risk assessment methods have relied primarily on AIV-positive records25,28 and lack consideration of host migration. Given that AIV transmission via migratory birds crosses borders, it is essential to recognize that avian influenza prevention efforts must account for the interconnected health of the global population.

As waterbirds are the primary AIV reservoir host4, this study provides an exposure risk analysis framework that considers the effects of migrating waterbirds in the context of climate and land use status. This framework enables a more accurate and balanced assessment of avian influenza risk, particularly in developing countries with limited surveillance resources. By addressing these interconnected factors, this study aligns with the One Health strategy, which considers different interfaces among waterbirds, poultry, cattle, and humans38, to predict and manage AIV risk more effectively in the future.

Responsibility of key exposure countries/regions

Our identification of key exposure countries/regions, which encompass nearly half of the global waterbird-associated AIV hotspot area (Fig. 3c), highlights their strategic importance in mitigating spillover risk. These hotspots cover previously reported outbreak regions (e.g., North America, Europe) identified in earlier studies on the basis of avian influenza case distributions13,25,32. Therefore, effective management of AIV spillover risk in these key exposure countries/regions would significantly contribute to global AIV prevention efforts.

The poultry industry is the largest concern for the potential spread of AIV. In particular, southeastern China was identified as an exposure region for both humans and poultry (117 Mha), having experienced the spread of AIV (i.e., H7N9) among poultry flocks in 2013, which also spilled over to humans25. Besides, specific farming practices (e.g., backyard poultry and modern industrial farming) should consider different AIV transmission risk, as neglecting production mode differences may lead to incorrect assessment of risk.

In the EU and China, seabird activity poses significant AIV exposure risks, especially in coastal areas39, such as Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Rotterdam, where numerous urban water bodies and green spaces in these regions harbor large populations of waterbirds40. Enhanced AIV surveillance in cities with high water coverage and consequent human exposure risk might help mitigate these risks.

The USA, which has the largest hotspot area (333 Mha), is experiencing an outbreak of the H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b among dairy cattle, which poses a high spillover risk to poultry and humans12. The cattle exposure hotspot areas in the USA (226 Mha) suggest a need for stronger surveillance during this bovine flu outbreak, with targeted mitigation efforts recommended in dairy-intensive states such as Texas, California, and Colorado12,41. The hotspot interface between cattle and waterbirds is also densely distributed in Argentina, Brazil, Australia, South Africa and Ethiopia (Supplementary Fig. 9), indicating a significant spillover risk. Enhancing surveillance and implementing biosecurity measures in these regions could help prevent the spread of AIV from cattle to waterbirds and vice versa. Although India is far from the epicenter of the current outbreak in cattle, it covers a broad area of exposed cattle (115 Mha) and has a large cross-interface exposure area for humans, cattle, and poultry (56 Mha), especially in northern and eastern states42. Consequently, a zoonosis that can be transmitted among waterbirds, cattle and humans might lead to a considerable number of human infections in India.

Surveillance priorities among waterbird functional groups

Species within a functional group share similar behavioral traits, habitat preferences, and physiology3,43. These shared traits make waterbird functional groups effective units for internal AIV virome sharing and for evaluating potential spillover risk. Analyzing waterbird functional groups is crucial for early warning and identification of priority reservoir hosts for surveillance3,13. Functional groups have also proven effective in explaining the spread risk of other bird-borne diseases14,44.

Previous studies have emphasized the AIV transmission risks posed by waterfowl due to their role as competent hosts and their high AIV subtype diversity45,46. Our study confirms that waterfowl pose a greater transmission risk than other waterbird groups do, as their activity entropy is more correlated with human, cattle, and poultry density (Supplementary Table 3), and strongly spatially associated with the past avian influenzas outbreak records (Supplementary Fig. 13), indicating closer interactions with susceptible populations in anthropogenic ecosystems. In Asia, their spillover potential is particularly high because of their close contact with free-range cattle in central India and free-range poultry in southeastern China (Supplementary Table 3). Unlike other groups, waterfowl are often hunted47 and share habitats with free-range domestic ducks and geese22,48, underscoring the need for enhanced biosecurity in Asian poultry farming.

Conservation-based interventions are needed to reduce avoidable contact between waterfowl and poultry, such as avoiding farm construction in areas with high waterfowl activity49. Given the recent emergence of seabirds as HPAI hosts across multiple continents7,21, which is marked by their high activity entropy with waterfowl in regions such as the central EU and southeastern China (Supplementary Fig. 12), there is also a parallel need to strengthen biosecurity in industrial farms with high-density environments. Recommended measures include stricter movement restrictions, routine disinfection, and prompt isolation of suspected cases50.

In addition to waterfowl and seabirds, identified infections among other waterbird groups has increased rapidly13,38. Although our analysis included all waterbird species and not just confirmed hosts, we found that large wading birds (mainly Ciconiiformes and Gruiformes) strongly overlapped with humans and cattle not only in four key exposure countries/regions (Fig. 4) but also in African countries (Supplementary Fig. 14). Africa’s surveillance system development should prioritize both seabirds and large wading birds32. Ciconiiformes (e.g., Bubulcus ibis) tend to feed and roost near cattle herds, posing a potential risk as potential AIV transmission hosts. Rapid development and an increase in the number of host species listed7,26 necessitate vigilance against avian influenza spillover from large wading birds.

Uncertainty and implications

This study utilized citizen science data (e.g., the GBIF) to simulate the monthly distribution and richness of waterbirds worldwide. The use of citizen science data has been discussed extensively51; we identified at least two potential biases inherent in these citizen science datasets, which may affect our results. First, initial observation bias stems from vastly uneven global observation efforts, with available data being massively concentrated in areas with high human density, more developed economy, or easily accessible for observers. Although we attempted to remedy these problems via data preprocessing (see “Methods”), this bias may persist, leading to species distribution models (SDMs) favoring data-rich regions, even after cross-validation52. Second, detection bias arises, as some species are easier to detect than others are, such that the data and risk assessments may underrepresent cryptic species. Although difficult to address fully, this bias likely had a limited impact on our host exposure risk assessment, as rare and elusive species probably play only minor roles in effective virus transmission.

The WAE introduced here provides a flexible framework for assessing exposure risks from natural reservoir species. However, its predictive accuracy is constrained by data availability. In this study, AIV surveillance data was used to assess the reliability and predictive capability of the WAE in identifying AIV cases likely resulting from spillover events involving waterbirds, and subsequently to determine the optimal threshold for delineating high-risk regions of waterbird host exposure (see Methods). However, missing surveillance data and substantial gaps in disease monitoring may still affect threshold calibration, potentially resulting in either overgeneralization or underestimation of high-risk regions.

To address this, enhanced surveillance efforts (including the emerging environmental surveillance), particularly targeting susceptible waterbirds, are urgently needed in developing regions, especially in eastern sub-Saharan Africa, where WAE indicates high-risk regions yet recorded AIV cases remain scarce. While WAE effectively captures the aggregated activity intensity of waterbird groups, it does not inherently rank individual species by their relative importance in virus transmission. In this study, all species contribute relatively equally to WAE calculations and are affected primarily by residence time. Future improvements could integrate more host competence factors53 for evaluating host exposure and spillover risk importance and contributions, such as population density, host suitability, or susceptibility, to reflect better contributions of host species and enhance WAE’s predictive accuracy in avian influenza risk.

In addition, our 2-step filtering process was designed to exclude poultry-to-poultry transmission occurring after initial spillover from wild to domestic hosts. It should be noted that even after spatio-temporal filtering, the remaining AIV cases may still include instances of lateral spread cases within poultry populations, rather than solely representing true spillover events from wild waterbirds. This ambiguity could influence the interpretation of exposure risks and the calibration of predictive models, particularly in regions with limited surveillance data or imbalanced surveillance efforts between wild and domestic animal populations.

Overall, disparities in citizen science observation effort and AIV surveillance intensity across countries and regions may partially affect the delineation of high-risk regions. Our analysis still provides meaningful guidance, particularly in identifying data-deficient high-risk regions where enhanced host observation and pathogen monitoring are urgently needed.

Methods

In this study, we developed global monthly distribution models for waterbirds, characterizing their seasonal migration patterns and ecological niche responses to climate. We used activity entropy to quantify the activity of waterbirds from spatial and temporal perspectives derived from the monthly global distribution data of waterbirds. We evaluated the effectiveness of waterbird activity entropy (WAE) in predicting the geographic distributions of reported AIV cases. Moreover, we used WAE to predict global hotspots of avian influenza exposure risk, highlighting areas with high exposure risk from waterbird hosts to poultry/cattle and humans. We collected confirmed hosts from which the sequenced AIVs were isolated (Supplementary Table 4) to emphasize the importance of prioritizing surveillance of dominant waterbird functional groups across different countries/regions.

The analysis was based on publicly available datasets and did not include any direct interaction with or collection from human or animal subjects, and therefore ethics approval was not required for this research.

Waterbird data

Waterbirds are defined as bird species that inhabit wetlands, aquatic, and marine habitats3,4, and we classified them into five functional groups based on habitat preference and other shared traits: seabirds, shorebirds, waterfowl, large wading birds, and others (Supplementary Table 1). Occurrence records for all waterbird species were downloaded from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF: https://www.gbif.org/). GBIF data are widely used for species distribution models and are openly accessible, providing more than two billion occurrence data records19. We downloaded records spanning 1860–2022, including 1177 waterbird species and encompassing 15 orders. We considered as many waterbird species as possible. However, for precise monthly modeling, we need to filter out species whose records do not meet the required standards.

First, to reduce the potential error in the exact location of a geographical point, we filtered out records with observation distances greater than 50 km (approximately 0.5° resolution) via the “coordinateUncertaintyInMeters” field54. Second, considering the classification errors, taxonomic rearrangements, and vagrant records, we excluded records that fell outside the established species distribution baselines. Specifically, we used BirdLife data zones for 711 waterbird species and Wallace’s realms for the remaining 80 waterbird species to determine these baselines. Third, in the analysis, we included only species with more than 9 unique records in each month to ensure sufficient data for monthly model construction6. Fourth, to reduce spatial observation bias arising from uneven observation efforts across geographic regions, we applied sparsification procedure by retaining only one unique point for each waterbird species per 0.5° grid cell54. The comparison results (Supplementary Fig. 15a) showed that SDMs produced significantly higher TSS values after adjusting biases (mean = 0.923, P < 0.001), compared to the unadjusted results (mean = 0.918). Ultimately, we retained 791 waterbird species for subsequent species distribution modeling and successfully simulated 779 of them (Supplementary Fig. 2). Among the 779 waterbird species, 180 were detected with the virus and defined as confirmed hosts in this study55.

Environmental data

We downloaded monthly weather data and elevation data from WorldClim V2.1 at a 10-min resolution (approximately 17 km at the equator)56. WorldClim data are widely used in species distribution models (SDMs) and include monthly climatic variables that were averaged from 1970 to 20006,17,20,57. Given the importance of open surface water body to the waterbird species in question, we introduced the proportion of open water body area as a key driver variable into waterbird monthly SDM construction. The water proportion data were downloaded from the Copernicus Global Land Service58, which provides high-resolution global data on open surface water bodies. All the environmental data were resampled to 0.5° spatial resolution via the bilinear interpolation method corresponding to the spatial resolution of the occurrence. Ultimately, we used five environmental variables to construct species distribution models, including minimum temperature (°C), maximum temperature (°C) and precipitation (mm), elevation (m) and surface water area proportion (%).

Additional data

To analyze exposure risk posed by waterbird activity among anthropogenic ecosystems, we use human population density59, poultry (chicken and duck) and cattle density60,61 to conduct the risk analysis. To analyze the drivers of WAE, we selected 16 potential variables and classified them into three groups (geographic and climate factors, vegetation factors, and human activity factors) to conduct Mantel tests (Supplementary Table 2). The geographic and climate factors included longitude, latitude, precipitation, temperature, and frost days. The vegetation factors included monthly gross primary productivity62 (GPP), gross primary productivity coefficient of variation (GPP CV), net primary productivity (NPP), normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), normalized difference water index (NDWI), and leaf area index (LAI). The human activity factors included light density, road density, human density, cattle density, and poultry density. More specific data sources and processing methods are described in the Supplementary Information.

Monthly species distribution models (SDM) processing

The modeling workflow we present here includes the preprocessing steps of the required data, the spatial distribution modeling steps, and the final results. (Supplementary Fig. 1). By using the final waterbird occurrence record points as presence data, we extracted five previously specified environmental variables (minimum temperature, maximum temperature, precipitation, elevation, and water proportion). We then randomly extracted an equal number of background points to match the presence data as absence data month by month63.

To reduce model uncertainty and capture complex linkages between environmental conditions and distributions of species, we selected three widely used machine learning approaches: random forest (RF), maximum entropy (Maxentropy), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)64,65. Maxentropy is known for its robustness in presence-only data and its ability to handle complex interactions between variables66. XGBoost is highly efficient and effective for large datasets, providing accurate predictions through gradient boosting. The RF is valued for its versatility and ability to reduce overfitting by averaging multiple decision trees. By combining these three approaches, we enhanced the reliability and precision of our monthly species distribution predictions.

We used fivefold cross-validation with each fold to determine the regularization parameter and selected the best-performing model for each machine learning method to simulate the monthly distribution. Specifically, the dataset was partitioned into five equal subsets; each fold served once as the validation set, while the remaining four folds were used for training. After obtaining the three results from each machine learning approach, we average them to combine these three results. Finally, we converted continuous predictions of the ensemble to binary presence‒absence distributional elements by maximizing the true skill statistics67.

To avoid model overfitting, we used spatial restrictions via the BirdLife data zone and Wallace’s realms to mask binary distribution results. This approach successfully simulated monthly distribution data for 779 waterbird species, with the model demonstrating high accuracy (Supplementary Fig. 15) for 9348 monthly distributions. Here, we showed the monthly distribution of swan geese, a seasonal migrant behavior typical of waterfowl (Supplementary Fig. 3b).

Performance of WAE for predicting reported cases of AIV

To test whether WAE could distinguish between epidemic areas and non-epidemic areas, we collected all accessible global occurrences of AIVs (including those detected in birds, mammals, and humans) from the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO, https://empres-i.apps.fao.org/) during the period 2000-2022. To assess exposure risk relevant to potential spillover events sourced from waterbird activity, we developed a multi-criteria filtering process for restricting poultry-to-poultry transmission cases from the original poultry cases data (Supplementary Fig. 7). These data included various fields, such as the location, report date, origin of the reported area, diagnosis information, and virus subtype of reported AIV outbreak cases, which were used to test whether WAE was capable of anticipating epidemic and nonepidemic areas. Using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, we calculated the maximum area under the curve (AUC) to define the optimal threshold of WAE via the Youden index68. The optimal threshold was an effective cutoff point for WAE that discriminated grid cells where avian influenza outbreaks occur from those where they do not with minimal misclassification. Each cell of the grid was then classified into high or low WAE (referred to as “high-risk regions” and “low-risk regions” in the text) on the basis of the threshold.

The classified results were further evaluated via a confusion matrix. The matrix consisted of four categories: (1) high-activity-entropy grids with reported cases (true positive, TP), (2) low-activity-entropy grids with reported cases (false negative, FN), (3) high-activity-entropy grids without reported cases (false positive, FP) and (4) low-activity-entropy grids without reported cases (true negative, TN). Specifically, the optimal activity entropy is determined from the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve with the Youden index. High-activity entropy refers to activity entropy greater than the optimal activity entropy, whereas low-activity entropy refers to activity entropy less than or equal to the optimal activity entropy. All reported cases were reduced to unique records within each 0.5° grid cell to be consistent with the result of activity entropy. Hotspot identification

To identify global hotspots of exposure risk from waterbirds, we classified the spatial distribution of WAE, human, poultry, and cattle densities into binary maps on the basis of the specific threshold derived as described above. To achieve binary classifications for human density as defined by GHSL69, termed ‘Low Density Rural grid cell’, which represents the human population density above the 17th percentile. For consistency, we rounded and determined that 60 human inhabitants per 100 ha corresponded to approximately the 15th percentile globally. For the binary classification of livestock density, we standardized the thresholds using mean values, establishing final rounded thresholds of 7 cattle per 100 hectares and 110 poultry per 100 hectares, respectively.

We explored the exposure risk by overlapping waterbirds to poultry, cattle, and human with paired binary maps of WAE-cattle, WAE-poultry, and WAE-human density, respectively. Finally, a pixel-by-pixel overlay analysis was conducted with binary maps, including WAE, human, cattle, and poultry densities, to identify the hotspots of waterbird exposure risk. We then reclassified the hotspots into seven zones according to different combinations of the three exposure targets (human, cattle, and poultry). We also calculated the hotspot area, exposed human population, and cattle/poultry numbers within each continent and country/region. Ultimately, we utilized the area of hotspots as a criterion to rank different countries/regions, focusing on the top four as “key exposure countries/regions”. The “EU” mentioned in this article refers to 27 countries in the European Union.

Statistical analysis

Our analysis compared species richness, the coefficient of variation (CV) of monthly richness, and WAE across Wallace’s original zoogeographic realms (“Wallace’s realms” in the text), which include the Palearctic, Ethiopian, Oriental, Australian, Neotropical, and Nearctic realms29. These realms serve as rough biogeographical boundaries for understanding species’ diversity and patterns. Therefore, we chose to use Wallace’s realms instead of traditional continental divisions to specifically describe the distributional patterns pertaining solely to waterbirds. We explored spatial relationship between human/cattle/poultry density and WAE of different waterbird functional groups using both Pearson correlation and global Bivariate Moran’s I, including confirmed hosts and all suspected hosts (all waterbirds), in the key exposure countries/regions (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 3, Supplementary Table 4). We also identified the dominant functional group within each grid cell by identifying the highest WAE among the different functional groups. The proportions of different functional groups were then calculated among the key exposure countries/regions (Supplementary Fig. 11). All analyses were performed via R 4.1.270.
