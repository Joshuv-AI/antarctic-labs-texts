Tumor mitochondrial respiration rather than glycolytic activity predicts recurrence in colorectal cancer: an ex vivo bioenergetic profiling study
Authors: Wei-En Hou, Chun-Wei Chen, Yu-De Chu, Yang-Hsiang Lin, Jy-Ming Chiang, Siew-Na Lim, Wey-Ran Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolic reprogramming is a recognized hallmark of colorectal cancer (CRC), yet whether intratumoral bioenergetic capacity directly influences clinical outcomes remains unclear. In particular, the relative prognostic contributions of mitochondrial respiration and glycolytic activity to postoperative recurrence have not been systematically evaluated. This study aimed to determine whether tumor oxygen consumption rate (OCR) or extracellular acidification rate (ECAR), measured ex vivo by Seahorse bioenergetic assays, is associated with recurrence-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) in patients with resected CRC.MethodsIn a single-center prospective cohort of 104 patients who underwent surgical resection for CRC between 2012 and 2017, paired tumor and adjacent normal mucosal specimens were analyzed using the Seahorse XF24 analyzer to quantify basal OCR and ECAR. Tumors were stratified by cohort median values and by receiver operating characteristic (ROC)-derived cutoffs optimized for recurrence prediction. Associations with RFS and OS were assessed using Kaplan–Meier analysis and Cox proportional hazards regression, adjusting for clinicopathological covariates.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 69 months, tumor OCR was significantly lower than that of adjacent normal mucosa (p < 0.001), whereas ECAR did not differ between paired tissues. OCR and ECAR were positively correlated within tumors (R² = 0.268, p < 0.001). In multivariate Cox regression, higher tumor OCR was independently associated with shorter RFS, both as a continuous variable (hazard ratio 1.455 per 1000-unit increase, p = 0.010) and when dichotomized by median or ROC-derived cutoffs (all p < 0.05). ROC analysis demonstrated moderate discriminatory ability for OCR (area under the curve 0.650), whereas ECAR showed no prognostic value. No bioenergetic parameter was significantly associated with OS.ConclusionsIntratumoral mitochondrial respiratory capacity, rather than glycolytic activity, independently predicts recurrence in resected CRC. These findings suggest that tumor OCR may serve as a functional metabolic biomarker for recurrence risk stratification, warranting prospective validation in independent cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05045-4.

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is among the most prevalent malignancies worldwide, ranking third in incidence and second in cancer-related mortality [1]. Although curative-intent resection is achievable in a substantial proportion of patients, postoperative recurrence continues to critically influence long-term prognosis [2, 3]. Conventional clinicopathological staging provides important prognostic information; however, substantial heterogeneity in recurrence risk persists among patients with similar tumor stages, highlighting the need for biologically informed biomarkers that better reflect tumor aggressiveness [2–6].

Metabolic reprogramming is a fundamental hallmark of cancer [7]. The classical Warburg effect describes the preferential reliance of cancer cells on aerobic glycolysis, accompanied by relative suppression of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. This metabolic shift has been extensively documented in colorectal cancer and is thought to support rapid proliferation by providing biosynthetic intermediates [8, 9]. However, this tissue-level paradigm does not fully explain the biological diversity of tumor behavior, particularly with respect to invasion, metastasis, and treatment resistance.

Emerging evidence suggests that mitochondrial function is not uniformly suppressed in malignant cells and may, in fact, play a critical role in aggressive tumor phenotypes [10]. Experimental studies indicate that metastatic and therapy-resistant cancer cells frequently depend on oxidative phosphorylation to meet elevated energetic demands, challenging the notion that glycolysis alone dictates tumor behavior [11–13].

Despite extensive characterization of metabolic alterations in CRC, the prognostic relevance of intratumoral bioenergetic capacity remains unclear [14, 15]. Functional profiling using oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) enables direct quantification of mitochondrial respiration and glycolytic activity, respectively, yet their relationship to recurrence and survival outcomes has not been systematically examined.

In this study, we performed ex vivo bioenergetic profiling of paired tumor and adjacent mucosal tissues from patients with resected CRC. We aimed to determine whether intratumoral mitochondrial respiration or glycolytic activity more accurately predicts recurrence and survival, thereby clarifying the bioenergetic determinants of CRC progression.

Methods

Patient cohort and specimen collection

Patients undergoing endoscopic or surgical resection for colorectal neoplasms at a single tertiary referral center between January 2012 and December 2017 were prospectively enrolled. Eligible patients were required to be aged ≥ 20 years with histologically confirmed colorectal adenocarcinoma and availability of paired tumor and adjacent normal mucosal specimens.

Patients were excluded if they had a history of inflammatory bowel disease, prior malignancy or inadequate follow-up (defined as less than 6 months).

A total of 113 patients were initially recruited. After exclusion of one patient with prior malignancy, eight patients lost to follow-up, 104 patients were included in the final analysis. Clinical variables (age and sex), tumor characteristics (location, pathological T stage, and differentiation), and treatment information (receipt of adjuvant chemotherapy) were recorded. Tumor location was categorized as right colon, left colon. All patients provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee (IRB NO.101-0102B) .

During surgery or colonoscopy, paired specimens of tumor tissue and grossly normal colonic mucosa were obtained. Grossly normal mucosa was collected at least 5 cm from the tumor margin. Tissue handling procedures were standardized to preserve metabolic activity and minimize pre-analytical variability. Each specimen was immediately placed in chilled Seahorse assay medium and processed within 2 h of excision to minimize ischemic effects. Tumor sampling was performed under pathologist guidance or according to the pathology-guided sampling workflow. Grossly viable tumor regions were selected, preferentially from the tumor edge when available, while macroscopically necrotic, hemorrhagic, or markedly fibrotic areas were avoided whenever possible. The sampling region was recorded during tissue processing to improve traceability and consistency. Histologic quantification of tumor cellularity, stromal content, inflammatory infiltration, or necrotic fraction was not systematically performed for each Seahorse-assayed tissue fragment.

Seahorse XF bioenergetic analysis

The metabolic characteristics of the specimens were assessed via a Seahorse XF24 analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience, Billerica, MA) by monitoring the OCR and ECAR in parallel. These metrics serve as indicators for mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and glycolytic function, respectively. Adhering to previously established protocols [16–18], fresh tissue samples were cleansed with unbuffered Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (pH 7.4) and maintained on ice. Following manual sectioning into approximately 1-mm-thick slices, grossly comparable tissue pieces were transferred into an XF24 Islet Capture Microplate and secured with capture screens to facilitate medium perfusion while minimizing tissue movement. In this study, “equivalent amounts of tissue” referred to comparable gross tissue size and thickness before protein normalization, rather than an identical number of viable tumor cells. After a 30-minute equilibration period at 37 °C in a non-CO₂ environment, the XF24-3 system captured the metabolic data. OCR and ECAR were obtained from the Seahorse XF analyzer as absolute rate measurements and subsequently normalized to total protein content extracted from each corresponding tissue sample after the assay. OCR was expressed as mMole/min/mg protein, whereas ECAR was expressed as mpH/min/mg protein. The same protein-normalization procedure was applied consistently across all samples to reduce variability related to tissue size and manual sectioning, thereby improving inter-sample comparability. At least three assay replicates from each tissue specimen were measured and averaged for analysis.

Metabolic group definitions

For descriptive and exploratory analyses, tumors were stratified into “high” or “low” metabolic groups based on cohort median values of OCR and ECAR. Median-based stratification was used for baseline and clinicopathological comparisons and was not intended for outcome optimization. Baseline characteristics between metabolic groups were compared to assess potential confounding.

To further evaluate outcome-oriented metabolic stratification, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to identify thresholds of OCR and ECAR associated with recurrence. Optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden index and subsequently applied in survival analyses. Tumors exceeding both ROC-derived OCR and ECAR thresholds were categorized as having a “dual-high (ROC)” metabolic phenotype.

Clinical follow-up and outcome definitions

Patients were followed after resection with periodic clinical evaluations and imaging studies according to institutional practice. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) was defined as the interval from surgery to the first documented tumor recurrence. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval from surgery to death from any cause. Patients without events were censored at the time of last follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Associations between OCR and ECAR were assessed using linear regression analysis.

Survival curves for RFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with log-rank tests. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used for univariate and multivariate analyses, incorporating metabolic parameters and relevant clinicopathological covariates (including tumor stage, differentiation, and adjuvant chemotherapy). Variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analyses were considered for multivariate modeling. Proportional hazards assumptions were evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 and GraphPad Prism.

Results

Study cohort and baseline characteristics

A total of 104 CRC patients were included in the final analysis. The cohort comprised 60 males and 44 females, with a median age of 64 years (range 28–85). Tumor locations were categorized as 71 right colon, 33 left colon. Pathologic T category (pT1-pT4) at resection were: I (4.8%), II (19.2%), III (62.5%), and IV (13.4%); Overall, 61 patients (58%) received adjuvant chemotherapy. The median follow-up time was 69 months (range, 7-139 months).

Baseline characteristics of patients stratified by tumor and normal mucosal OCR/ECAR are summarized in Table 1. The median tumor OCR (~ median = 2930.31 mMole/min/mg) served as a cutoff to define OCR-high vs. OCR-low tumors, and similarly the median ECAR (~ median = 489.44 mpH/min/mg) for ECAR-high vs. ECAR-low. In tumor tissue, no significant differences were observed between OCR-high and OCR-low groups with respect to age, sex, tumor size, stage, differentiation, lymph node status, or adjuvant chemotherapy. Similarly, ECAR stratification in tumor tissue showed largely comparable clinicopathologic characteristics, except for tumor location and serum CEA levels. In contrast, when stratified by normal mucosal bioenergetic status, most clinicopathologic parameters remained balanced between groups, although left-sided tumor location was more frequently observed in the ECAR-high group (Table S1).

Tumor vs. normal tissue metabolism

Tumor OCR was significantly lower than that of adjacent normal mucosa (3008.44 ± 118.21 vs. 3620.94 ± 152.56 mMole/min/mg; paired t-test, p < 0.001; Figure S1). Although the OCR/ECAR ratio was also markedly decreased in CRC tissues (6.50 ± 0.25 vs. 8.85 ± 0.45 mMole/mpH; paired t-test, p < 0.001; Fig. S1), no significant difference in the ECAR was observed between CRCs and normal mucosae (502.16 ± 21.01 vs. 466.15 ± 24.18 mpH/min/mg ; paired t-test, p = 0.09; Fig. S1).

Intratumoral OCR–ECAR relationship

In normal tissues, OCR was positively correlated with ECAR (R² = 0.377, p < 0.0001; Fig. 1A). Similarly, a positive correlation between OCR and ECAR was observed in tumor tissues (R² = 0.268, p < 0.0001; Fig. 1B). The regression slope was comparable between the two groups, while tumor tissues demonstrated a higher ECAR intercept.

Impact of OCR and ECAR on recurrence-free survival

Tumors from patients who developed recurrence exhibited significantly higher OCR compared with those without recurrence (3500.81 ± 1066.76 vs. 2835.80 ± 1209.99 mMole/min/mg, independent samples t-test, p = 0.013);. In contrast to OCR, tumor ECAR did not differ significantly between patients with and without recurrence (515.79 ± 203.28 vs. 497.39 ± 219.14, mpH/min/mg, independent samples t-test, p = 0.703).

Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated significantly poorer recurrence-free survival in patients with OCR-high tumors compared with OCR-low tumors (p = 0.035; Fig. 2B), whereas ECAR stratification was not associated with outcome (p = 0.817; Fig. 2A). In addition, recurrence-free survival differed significantly according to adjuvant chemotherapy status (Fig. S2).

In univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis, tumor OCR, modeled per 1000-unit increase, was significantly associated with recurrence-free survival (HR 1.479, 95% CI 1.091–2.006; p = 0.012; Table 3). Adjuvant chemotherapy (HR 4.918, 95% CI 1.700–14.231; p = 0.003) was also significantly associated with recurrence-free survival (Table 3). After adjustment for clinicopathological variables, both adjuvant chemotherapy (HR 5.219, 95% CI 1.798–15.147; p = 0.002) and tumor OCR (HR 1.455, 95% CI 1.095–1.933; p = 0.010) remained independent predictors of recurrence-free survival. In contrast, tumor ECAR, normal mucosal bioenergetic parameters, and OCR/ECAR ratios were not significantly associated with recurrence-free survival (Table 3).

To further assess the robustness of the observed OCR effect, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to patients with AJCC stage II–III disease. In this subgroup, tumor OCR remained independently associated with recurrence-free survival after adjustment for AJCC stage and adjuvant chemotherapy (HR 1.536, 95% CI 1.120–2.105; p = 0.008; Table S4).

ROC-based risk stratification for recurrence

To compare the prognostic utility of tumor bioenergetic parameters, ROC curve analysis was performed to derive outcome-oriented thresholds for recurrence risk stratification. Tumor OCR demonstrated fair discrimination for recurrence (AUC 0.650, 95% CI 0.537–0.762; p = 0.009), with an optimal cut-off of 2735 mMole/min/mg determined by the Youden index (Fig. 3). In contrast, tumor ECAR showed limited discriminatory ability (AUC 0.519, 95% CI 0.3928–0.6462), with a Youden-derived threshold of 278 mpH/min/mg. OCR and ECAR derived from adjacent normal mucosa did not demonstrate discriminatory ability for recurrence (Fig. S3).

In the Cox regression model, high tumor OCR defined by the median cut-off was associated with shorter recurrence-free survival (HR 2.313, 95% CI 1.036–5.162; p = 0.041, Table S2). When the ROC-derived cut-off was applied, high tumor OCR remained significantly associated with recurrence-free survival (HR 2.963, 95% CI 1.190–7.377; p = 0.020, Table S2). Given the positive correlation between OCR and ECAR, a combined metabolic phenotype was additionally evaluated. Tumors classified as dual-high based on ROC-defined thresholds (high OCR and high ECAR) were also associated with recurrence (HR 2.752, 95% CI 1.155–6.558; p = 0.022, Table S2).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that OCR is independently associated with recurrence-free survival in colorectal cancer, whereas no bioenergetic parameter predicts overall survival. At the tissue level, colorectal cancer exhibited global suppression of mitochondrial respiration relative to adjacent normal mucosa, as evidenced by reduced OCR and OCR/ECAR ratios—findings consistent with the canonical Warburg effect [19, 20]. In contrast, ECAR did not differ significantly between tumor and normal tissues, suggesting that glycolytic activation is broadly established across colorectal cancers and therefore lacks discriminatory power for recurrence risk.

Within this metabolic context, intratumoral heterogeneity in residual mitochondrial respiratory activity emerged as the most clinically relevant determinant of disease relapse. While suppression of oxidative phosphorylation appears to represent a hallmark of malignant transformation, variation in preserved or reactivated mitochondrial respiration among tumors may account for meaningful differences in aggressive behavior and recurrence propensity. Together, these findings challenge the simplistic view that increased glycolysis alone defines tumor aggressiveness and instead highlight mitochondrial respiratory capacity as a critical bioenergetic feature underlying colorectal cancer recurrence.

Within tumors, OCR and ECAR exhibited a significant but modest positive correlation, indicating that glycolytic activity and mitochondrial respiration were concurrently activated rather than strictly mutually exclusive. In addition, the regression equation demonstrated a higher ECAR intercept in tumor tissues compared with normal tissues, suggesting a relative upward shift of ECAR values at comparable OCR levels. However, given that the overall mean ECAR did not significantly differ between groups, this observation should be interpreted cautiously. Furthermore, the lower correlation coefficient observed in tumor samples possibly indicate greater metabolic heterogeneity among tumors.

Recent studies have emphasized that metabolic reprogramming in colorectal cancer is characterized by coordinated, yet non-redundant, regulation of multiple bioenergetic pathways [14, 21]. Markov et al. demonstrated that mitochondrial respiration and glycolytic flux can be simultaneously upregulated to support distinct cellular functions, including redox homeostasis and biosynthetic flexibility [22]. Similarly, Qiu et al. highlighted that adaptive bioenergetic remodeling enables tumor cells to maintain metabolic resilience under stress conditions [23]. The positive OCR–ECAR correlation suggests coordinated activation of oxidative and glycolytic metabolism rather than mutually exclusive pathway usage. Together with the higher ECAR intercept in tumor tissues, this pattern supports a hybrid glycolytic/OXPHOS phenotype beyond a strict Warburg model [10]. Given that our Seahorse measurements were performed using bulk ex vivo tissues, this finding is best interpreted as metabolic flexibility—the capacity to engage both glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation according to energetic or biosynthetic demands—rather than direct evidence of intercellular metabolic coupling, such as lactate shuttling between glycolytic and oxidative cell populations [24]. Despite the OCR–ECAR association, only OCR was prognostically relevant for recurrence-free survival, suggesting a closer link between mitochondrial respiration and recurrence-prone tumor biology.

ROC-based analyses further clarified the relative prognostic contribution of tumor bioenergetic programs. Although tumors exhibiting a dual-high metabolic phenotype were associated with increased recurrence risk, high tumor OCR alone provided equal or superior prognostic discrimination, suggesting that glycolytic activity did not confer substantial incremental prognostic information beyond mitochondrial respiratory capacity in this cohort. These findings further support the potential relevance of mitochondrial respiratory capacity as a biologically meaningful prognostic marker in colorectal cancer. However, because tumor OCR demonstrated only moderate discriminatory ability for recurrence prediction (AUC = 0.650), OCR-based risk stratification should currently be considered a promising translational approach rather than an immediately applicable stand-alone clinical tool. Further validation in larger independent cohorts is warranted before clinical implementation.

Biologically, the stronger prognostic association of OCR compared with ECAR may reflect metabolic heterogeneity among colorectal tumors. Glycolytic activation appears to be a common metabolic feature across many established colorectal cancers, whereas preserved or reactivated mitochondrial respiratory capacity may identify a subset of tumors with distinct metabolic characteristics associated with recurrence risk. The present study was not designed to investigate the biological mechanisms underlying this association. However, previous experimental studies have suggested that oxidative phosphorylation–dependent cancer cells may exhibit stemness-associated features, enhanced metastatic potential, and adaptive responses to therapeutic stress [25–27]. These observations provide a possible biological context for our findings, although such mechanisms were not directly evaluated in the current study and therefore remain speculative.

From a translational perspective, Seahorse-based OCR measurement is unlikely to be directly incorporated into routine clinical practice. Therefore, OXPHOS-related transcriptomic or immunohistochemical markers may provide a more feasible surrogate approach. Potential candidates include PGC-1α, an upstream regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis and OXPHOS-related gene programs, and COX5B, a nuclear-encoded subunit of cytochrome c oxidase/mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV that may more directly reflect electron transport chain activity [28]. Prior CRC tissue studies have linked relatively increased tumor COX5B expression to higher OCR and poorer postoperative outcomes, supporting its potential as a candidate tissue-based marker of OXPHOS-related tumor biology. However, these markers should be regarded as candidate translational biomarkers rather than validated substitutes for direct Seahorse-based assessment, and future studies integrating OCR measurement with transcriptomic and immunohistochemical validation are warranted.

Together, these observations support a model in which glycolysis represents a common metabolic background, whereas preserved or reactivated mitochondrial respiration may define a recurrence-prone tumor phenotype.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was retrospective in nature and included a limited number of recurrence events, underscoring the need for prospective validation in independent cohorts.

Second, tumor OCR and ECAR were assessed using ex vivo Seahorse-based assays, which are not routinely available in clinical practice. Although tissue handling, slice thickness, and protein normalization were standardized, viable tumor cell number and histologic tumor cellularity were not directly quantified in each tissue slice. Therefore, OCR and ECAR should be interpreted as protein-normalized bulk tissue-level bioenergetic activities rather than measurements per viable tumor cell. These signals may reflect the integrated metabolic activity of tumor epithelial cells together with stromal, inflammatory, vascular, and other non-tumor components, and the relative contribution of each cellular compartment could not be determined in this study. OCR may currently serve as a functional benchmark defining a high-risk metabolic phenotype rather than an immediately applicable clinical biomarker. Future studies integrating Seahorse-derived OCR with clinically accessible OXPHOS-related immunohistochemical or transcriptomic markers and matched histologic assessment are warranted.

Third, the observed association between adjuvant chemotherapy and increased recurrence risk should be interpreted with caution and likely reflects confounding by indication rather than a detrimental effect of chemotherapy itself. Patients receiving adjuvant chemotherapy had higher proportions of T3–T4 tumors (85.2% vs. 62.8%, p = 0.008) and lymph node metastasis (78.7% vs. 11.6%, p < 0.001), as well as more recurrence events (37.7% vs. 9.3%, p = 0.001), as shown in Supplementary Table S3. Thus, adjuvant chemotherapy use may partly represent baseline disease severity and recurrence risk, although residual confounding cannot be fully excluded in this retrospective cohort.

Finally, the enrichment of ECAR-high tumors in left-sided colorectal cancers was an exploratory finding and not a predefined objective of this study, and therefore should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that intratumoral oxidative capacity, as reflected by tumor oxygen consumption rate, is independently associated with recurrence risk in colorectal cancer, whereas glycolytic activity alone does not confer prognostic value. Although colorectal cancer tissues exhibit overall suppression of mitochondrial respiration consistent with the Warburg paradigm, variability in residual oxidative metabolism within tumors appears to meaningfully stratify relapse risk. Functional bioenergetic profiling may therefore complement conventional clinicopathological staging, although prospective validation in larger, independent cohorts is needed to confirm its clinical utility.
