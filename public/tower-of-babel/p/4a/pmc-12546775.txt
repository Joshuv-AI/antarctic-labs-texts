Unfavorable Social Determinants of Health and Obesity
Authors: Jerrin Philip, Kobina Hagan, Izza Shahid, Ryan Chang, Shriya Prakash, Michael Friedman, Anoop Titus, Harun Kundi, Zahir Amin, Umair Javed, Eleonora Avenatti, Sadeer Al-Kindi, Khurram Nasir, Zulqarnain Javed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObesity is a well-established risk factor for cardiovascular disease and mortality, but its interplay with social determinants of health (SDoH) remains inadequately studied. Socioeconomic disadvantage can exacerbate health disparities, yet the cumulative impact of SDoH on premature mortality in individuals with obesity is not well understood.ObjectivesSDoH are key contributors to cardiovascular disease, yet their cumulative impact on premature mortality among individuals with obesity remains underexplored.MethodsThis study analyzed data from participants from the United States aged 18 to 64 in the 2013-2018 National Health Interview Survey, linked to the National Death Index. A 38-item SDoH index was used to evaluate social disadvantage. Multivariable Cox regression and Kaplan-Meier analyses assessed premature mortality risk across different SDoH burdens and obesity classes. Premature mortality is defined as all-cause death prior to age 65.ResultsAmong 136,816 participants, those with obesity were more likely to be non-Hispanic Black and face low income, lower education, food insecurity, and lack of health insurance. Mortality rates increased with higher SDoH burden and obesity class, with the highest rates observed in those with both severe SDoH and class 3 obesity. Specifically, individuals with high SDoH burden (Q3/Q4) and class 3 obesity had a 3.5-fold increased risk of premature mortality (HR: 3.53; 95% CI: 2.81-4.45) compared to those with favorable SDoH profiles and no obesity.ConclusionsAddressing both social and clinical risk factors is essential for reducing premature mortality among individuals facing the combined challenges of social disadvantage and obesity.

Obesity, commonly defined by a body mass index (BMI) of 30 kg/m2 or more, is a major health risk affecting 2 in 5 adults in the United States.1 Despite numerous population-level weight management initiatives, obesity rates continue to rise, with a projected 18% increase over the next 3 decades.2 Socioeconomically disadvantaged groups bear a disproportionate share of this burden.3 Poor access to healthy foods and low spending on nutritious options are closely linked to individuals' income levels.4 Additionally, food deserts and transportation barriers further limit access to healthy food choices in deprived neighborhoods.5 Such barriers, in conjunction with additional factors like inadequate green space, can increase risk of obesity and diabetes.6 Social determinants of health (SDoH) are also thought to contribute significantly to the development of obesity. Previous research has shown that greater cumulative burden of adverse SDoH is associated with higher risk of obesity,3 with income status, food insecurity, and lower educational attainment highlighted as particularly significant contributing factors.7

These socioeconomic and environmental factors not only contribute to obesity but also impact overall health and mortality. Both obesity and adverse SDoH are associated with an increased risk of mortality, with the intersection and synergy of these factors posing a “double jeopardy” of elevated mortality risk. In a national survey of U.S. adults, individuals facing the highest burden of adverse SDoH had all-cause mortality rates at least twice as high as those with the lowest burden of adverse SDoH.8 Similarly, overweight and obesity are associated with mortality risk 1.5- to 3-fold higher than the risk associated with healthy BMI.2 Obesity is linked to 30% to 53% of new diabetes cases in the United States and is considered a leading risk factor for diabetes due to its role in the development of insulin resistance.9,10 Diabetes is a significant contributor to cardiometabolic morbidity and mortality, which is often compounded by increased weight gain in individuals with diabetes.11 Obesity also predisposes patients to additional cardiovascular risk factors, such as dyslipidemia, hypertension, and sleep disorders, thereby elevating risk of cardiovascular disease, the leading cause of death worldwide.12 There is also an established relationship between obesity and other medical conditions like stroke, cancer, and kidney disease.13 Similarly, increased burden of adverse SDoH has been linked with increased overall and premature mortality risk.14,15

Despite the clear relationship between cumulative social disadvantage and above-normal BMI, as well as their individual impacts on mortality, studies examining their combined effect on mortality risk are lacking. To address this gap, we examined all-cause mortality risk across different profiles of obesity and cumulative social disadvantage.

Methods

Data sources

This study utilized data collected from the 2013-2018 National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) linked to the National Death Index (NDI) for participants aged 18 to 64 years.16,17 The NHIS is an annual survey conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics, targeting the noninstitutionalized civilian population in the United States. It gathers information on sociodemographic characteristics, health status and disability, behavioral indicators, and health care access and utilization patterns of survey participants. For this study, NHIS data from 3 core files—household composition, family core, and sample adult core—were merged with mortality records from the NDI. The NDI is a centralized national data set of U.S. death record that indicates the cause of death as determined by International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revisions. Details of merging NHIS with NDI data are fully discussed in existing literature. Deaths in the NDI are adjudicated quarterly, covering the period through October-December 2019. Because of the deidentified nature of the NHIS, this study was considered exempt from Institutional Review Board of Houston Methodist Hospital.

Participants

Our analysis included participants of the 2013-2018 NHIS aged 18 to 64 years NDI (n = 136,816). Those without valid response to screening questions, insufficient identifying data, or without linkage to NDI were excluded from the study (Supplemental Figure 1).

Cumulative social disadvantage

Using a commonly adopted cumulative SDoH framework, we organized SDoH information from 38 NHIS survey items into 6 domains: economic stability, neighborhood/physical environment, community and social context, food insecurity, education, and health care system.8 For each participant, SDoH domains were aggregated into a cumulative index of social disadvantage. For each survey item, the responses were categorized as 0 for no deprivation/favorable or 1 for presence of SDoH factor/unfavorable. Each participant had a final score ranging from 0 (no deprivation) to 38 (deprivations in all SDoH). Participants were then categorized by quartiles of the cumulative SDoH index into 4 groups: SDoH-Q1 (least deprived) through SDoH-Q4 (most deprived). We further define relatively low SDoH burden as Q1-Q2 and relatively high SDoH burden as Q3-Q4 (Supplemental Table 1).

Obesity

We classified study participants by BMI ranges into the following groups: nonobese (<30 kg/m2), class 1 obesity (30-<35 kg/m2), class 2 obesity (35-<40 kg/m2), and class 3 obesity (≥40 kg/m2).

Covariates

Recorded covariates assessed in the study population include age (18-39, 40-64 years); sex (male or female); self-reported race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black (NHB), Hispanic); diabetes mellitus; hypertension, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) status; cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD); and kidney failure. All relevant covariates are self-reported responses and collected from the NHIS Sample Adult Core Questionnaire.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses accounted for the complex design, weighting for selection probabilities and nonresponse to NHIS, as well as NDI-linkage eligibility. Consequently, descriptive statistics and regression estimates were representative of the U.S. adult population. Participant characteristics were summarized across cumulative social disadvantage and obesity profiles. Poisson regression was used to generate age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMR) per 100,000 person-years and 95% CI for each SDoH quartile, overall and by subgroups of interest, including sex, race/ethnicity, and comorbidity burden in adults with obesity.

Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated to estimate survival curves between SDoH quartiles and adults with varying severity of obesity. We used HR estimates from Cox proportional hazards models to evaluate the association between cumulative social disadvantage, obesity, and all-cause mortality. Four models generated included model 1 adjusted for age, sex; model 2 adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity; model 3 adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, cardiovascular risk factors, and model 4 adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, cardiovascular risk factors, and comorbidities.

Those with increasing severity of obesity were associated with higher burden of SDoH across nearly all domains. Participants in the class 3 obesity category were found to be affected by the highest levels of unemployment (31.2%), lower family income (40.8%), and food insecurity (17.0%), while classes 1 and 2 had more incremental increases relative to those without obesity (Table 1). Comorbidity burden increased across increasing class of obesity with class 3, having the highest percentage of participants affected by diabetes mellitus, hypertension, COPD, and kidney failure. Those with class 2 and 3 obesity had the comparable prevalence of ASCVD (7.2% and 6.8%) and cancer (5.7% and 5.6%). Prevalence of ASCVD and cancer was slightly higher in participants with class 2 obesity compared to class 3 although these differences were marginal. This pattern may be influenced by survivor bias, where individuals with more severe obesity and higher comorbidity burden may experience earlier mortality, thus slightly reducing observed prevalence in the class 3 group.

Age-adjusted mortality rates

Higher adverse SDoH burden was a strong predictor of higher age-adjusted all-cause mortality rate (Figure 1). Across all obesity classes, AAMR increased between 1.5- to 3-fold SDoH-Q1 and SDoH-Q4. Multivariable regression

Cox proportional hazard regression models demonstrated an association with increasing SDoH burden and increased mortality risk, as well as increasing obesity severity and mortality. Compared to participants with lower SDoH burden and no obesity, participants with lower SDoH burden and class 2 or 3 obesity had significantly higher risk of premature mortality, with adjusted HRs of 1.77 (95% CI: 1.26-2.49) and 2.15 (95% CI: 1.60-2.89), respectively. High SDoH burden was associated with even higher risk of premature mortality, with marginal increases as obesity class increased (Table 2). These associations were somewhat attenuated when adjusted for cardiovascular risk factors and other comorbidities, but higher social disadvantage and increasing obesity class remained associated with incremental increases in premature mortality risk. No significant differences in this relationship were observed between non-Hispanic White, NHB, and Hispanic participants (Table 2). Discussion

This study reveals a synergistic interplay between social disadvantage and obesity in contributing to premature mortality, a “double jeopardy” that amplifies risk beyond the individual impact of each factor alone. SDoH factors including neighborhood deprivation, food insecurity, limited health care access often cooccur with obesity, creating a compounded risk environment.10,11 While multiple studies have demonstrated that higher SDoH burden is associated with higher all-cause mortality, little is known about the burden of disparities faced by the obese population and the cumulative effect of SDoH on all-cause mortality.18,19 In contrast to prior works, this is the first nationally representative study to comprehensively describe degree of SDoH within the obese population and compare AAMR between individuals without obesity and various obesity classification (Central Illustration).Central IllustrationCumulative Social Disadvantage and Increasing Degree of Obesity Compounds Risk of Premature MortalityAbbreviations as in Figure 1.

Obesity has been linked with social deprivation including high unemployment, low income, and lower level of educational achievement.3 In this study, a total of 38-item questionnaire was included, which will be useful for the future development of a SDoH index as a clinical tool. We frame the results using the World Health Organization conceptual model of structural and intermediate determinants of health. Structural determinants such as housing, employment, and policy-level barriers contribute to the broader risk landscape, while intermediate determinants like food access and education shape individual exposures. Adults with high SDoH burden (Q3/Q4) and class 3 obesity had 3.5-fold increased risk of premature mortality, compared to those with favorable SDoH profiles (Q1/Q2) and no obesity. This was observed across race categories and sex with a relatively stronger association in NHB and male populations (Central Illustration).

The term “double jeopardy” in this context refers to the convergence of clinical obesity and social vulnerability that together exacerbate mortality risk. Various associations were observed between SDoH and obesity within this study. With increasing severity of obesity, there was increasing burden of unfavorable SDoH. Low educational achievement is associated with higher levels of obesity, which may portend poor outcomes due to low health care literacy. Low family income status increases the environmental risk of living spaces and may limit the availability of healthy foods or cause food insecurity. Among the SDoH domains, economic instability and food insecurity emerged as potential predominant drivers of risk, given their strong associations with both obesity and adverse health outcomes. These factors may directly limit access to healthy lifestyle resources and indirectly increase chronic stress, compounding cardiometabolic vulnerability. Increasing levels of obesity were found to be associated with increased burden of medical comorbidity including hypertension, diabetes, COPD, and kidney failure which are all associated with higher morbidity and mortality.18,20,21 Those with class 2 obesity were found to have the highest levels of ASCVD and cancer rates. This may reflect the intermediate stage of metabolic disease where risk factor burden is maximal before mortality escalates thereby resulting in high prevenance of comorbid disease burden.

Mechanistically, SDoH influences obesity via chronic stress, environmental exposure, lack of green space, and structural racism.7,14,22,23 These mechanisms warrant further investigation via longitudinal studies. Clinical, documentation of obesity in the electronic medical record (EMR) can improve risk stratification and care planning. Currently, such documentation is lacking or inconsistently applied which represents a missed opportunity for proactive care.

Further data analysis demonstrated weaker SDoH effect on mortality in Hispanic and female participants. The reduced mortality association observed in these populations may reflect protective cultural and social buffers, such as stronger community support or differences in health care engagement.24 Additionally, there was a stronger relationship of SDoH burden and mortality within the male subgroup. Further research is warranted to investigate the relationships of SDoH burden differences across different ethnicities and gender.

The risk of developing obesity due to high SDoH burden and reverse relationship are notable. The financial implication of the development of obesity is linked to increased medical cost from medical visits, medications, hospitalizations, and durable medical equipment.25 This demonstrates the cyclical relationship of adverse SDoH burden and obesity. The clinical implication to this reaffirms the importance of identifying and addressing socioeconomic barriers to prevention of the development and progression of obesity within the clinical setting.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include the large, nationally representative population included and the multidimensional framework with 38 different variables to capture SDoH burden. The use of the NHIS linked to the NDI allows us to generalize our results to the U.S. population at large.

Limitations to the utilization of the NHIS is the cross-sectional nature of the data. This does not allow for us to account for the dynamic changes that may occur in SDoH burden or weight status over time. An opportunity may be present for future studies to capture the temporal variation of SDoH to demonstrate its dynamic effects on health and longevity. The population included did not account for certain demographic populations including non-Hispanic Asian (approximately 5%), Native American/American Indian (approximately 1.8%) due to their low sample sizes. While it is critically important to study the impact of SDoH and obesity on these underserve populations, our primary analysis would not have been sufficiently powered to reliably interpret the impact on mortality in these subgroups. Unmeasured confounders are present including physical activity, dietary quality, and mental health which we were not able to adjust for that may bias the observed relationships. The self-reported nature of the NHIS survey results may be considered a limitation as this may allow bias in self-reporting of certain measures. Although, self-reported responses and clinical measures demonstrated a good amount of agreement.

Implications

The findings from this study underscore the importance of developing polysocial risk scores for predicting premature mortality in obese populations, like those previously reported for ASCVD and diabetes populations.15,22 Currently, available risk prediction tools account for very limited SDoH categories or do not account for SDoH altogether. Utilizing the available literature will be instrumental in identifying those SDoH indices that are highly correlated with morbidity and mortality in these populations. Importantly, both BMI and a social deprivation index have recently been incorporated into the PREVENT risk calculator as primary and optional variables, respectively.26 This integration underscores the growing recognition of both clinical and social risk factors in predicting premature mortality. Further work is required to integrate validated polysocial risk scores into clinical workflow via EMR identifiers. Additionally, obesity class is often inconsistently documented in the EMR, limiting its prognostic utility—especially when combined with SDoH burden. Standardized EMR documentation of obesity class could enhance risk stratification and support more personalized clinical decision-making. Artificial intelligence integration within EMRs have begun to develop flags for clinicians to be notified that a particular patient may require additional services or more individualized clinical decision-making.

Barriers to health care intervention including lack of access to health care resources, financial burden of health care cost, low medical literacy, and access to green spaces.23,24,27 To address the compounded impact of obesity and adverse social determinants, specific strategies must be implemented across clinical and community settings. Community-clinical partnerships can improve care continuity and link patients to nonmedical resources such as food assistance, housing, and behavioral health services.24 Integrating SDoH screening into EMRs allows clinicians to systematically identify and respond to patients’ social needs, which has been shown to enhance care planning and reduce health disparities.28 At the policy level, interventions aimed at improving access to affordable housing, transportation, and healthy food environments are essential to address upstream drivers of chronic disease and obesity.29 Collectively, these strategies can support more equitable care delivery and reduce mortality linked to both clinical and social vulnerabilities. By addressing and overcoming these barriers, the goal is to reduce morbidity and mortality caused by negative SDoH in this at-risk population.

Conclusions

This study suggests that U.S. adults with obesity across all classes experience higher burden of unfavorable SDoH. The dual-burden is linked with significantly increased risk of premature all-cause mortality. These findings suggest the importance of proactive screening for unfavorable SDoH indicators and clinical BMI.

Interventions should focus on upstream social factors—such as food insecurity and income inequality—as well as integration of SDoH screening in clinical settings. Future efforts should aim to implement validated polysocial risk indices in EMRs, supported by artificial intelligence to identify high-risk individuals. Policies promoting equitable health care access, education, and healthy living environments may be key to reducing obesity-related disparities.

Overall, this study provides a strong rationale for integrating social risk assessments into routine obesity care to help reduce disparities in cardiovascular outcomes and premature mortality.PerspectivesCOMPETENCY IN PATIENT CARE: This study reinforces the need for clinicians to recognize the synergistic impact of obesity and social disadvantage on premature mortality.
