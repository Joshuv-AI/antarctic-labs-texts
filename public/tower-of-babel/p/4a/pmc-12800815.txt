Differential subclinical hemolysis after pulsed field ablation using the FARAPULSE pentaspline catheter vs the PulseSelect circular multi-electrode array catheter
Authors: Sayana Kuraoka, Masatsugu Nozoe, Hiroshi Mannoji, Ryo Miyake, Tomoki Uchikawa, Akihito Ishikita, Daisuke Nagatomo, Nobuhiro Suematsu, Toru Kubota
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulsed field ablation (PFA) is a nonthermal ablation method characterized by favorable tissue selectivity and a low complication rate. However, hemolysis has recently emerged as a PFA-specific concern, particularly associated with the FARAPULSE system.ObjectiveTo assess the incidence and clinical relevance of hemolysis following atrial fibrillation (AF) ablation using the PulseSelect system, in comparison with FARAPULSE and conventional radiofrequency (RF) ablation.MethodsThis retrospective study included 120 consecutive patients who underwent AF ablation between October 2024 and February 2025 (RF: n = 15; FARAPULSE: n = 39; PulseSelect: n = 66). Hemolysis markers—free plasma hemoglobin, haptoglobin, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), total bilirubin, and creatinine—were measured before and after ablation.ResultsPFA was associated with significantly higher the post-/pre-ablation ratio of free plasma hemoglobin, LDH, and total bilirubin, whereas the ratio of haptoglobin were significantly lower than with RF. The PulseSelect system resulted in less hemolysis than the FARAPULSE system, as evidenced by smaller increases in free plasma hemoglobin, LDH, and total bilirubin, and by higher haptoglobin. Notably, although free plasma hemoglobin increased after ablation with both the PulseSelect and FARAPULSE systems, in contrast to FARAPULSE, no correlation was observed between the number of PulseSelect applications and free plasma hemoglobin. No cases of acute kidney injury occurred in any group.ConclusionThe PulseSelect system induced only mild, subclinical hemolysis—comparable to that seen with FARAPULSE—without any clinically significant anemia or acute kidney injury, even in the absence of a limit on the number of applications. These findings support the safe and flexible use of PulseSelect, not only for pulmonary vein isolation, but also for additional lesion sets in more complex ablation procedures.Keywords: Acute kidney injury, Atrial fibrillation, Free plasma hemoglobin, Hemolysis, Pulsed-field ablation

Introduction

Pulsed field ablation (PFA) uses high-voltage, ultra-short electrical pulses to induce irreversible electroporation of cellular membranes, enabling nonthermal myocardial ablation. Myocardial cells exhibit greater susceptibility to PFA compared with surrounding structures, such as the phrenic nerve and esophagus, thereby allowing for tissue selectivity and reducing the risk of thermally mediated complications.1,2 Previous clinical studies have confirmed the safety of PFA, with few serious adverse events reported.3, 4, 5

Despite this favorable safety profile, hemolysis has emerged as a notable complication. Unlike thermal ablation methods, PFA involves the direct exposure of circulating blood to high-voltage electric fields, which can cause red blood cell disruption through electroporation.6,7 2 cases of severe acute kidney injury (AKI) secondary to intravascular hemolysis were reported following atrial fibrillation (AF) ablation using more than 120 applications of the pentaspline PFA catheter.8 Furthermore, in the MANIFEST-17K registry—the largest PFA registry to date—5 of over 17,000 patients (0.03%) developed transient hemolysis-related AKI requiring dialysis after receiving an average of 143 ± 27 applications.9 These findings suggest that hemolysis may be a dose-dependent phenomenon, prompting current recommendations to limit the number of PFA applications to ≤70 to mitigate this risk.8

To date, most reports of PFA-associated hemolysis have involved the FARAPULSE pentaspline catheter (Boston Scientific). In contrast, limited data are available regarding hemolysis with other PFA systems, such as the circular multielectrode array catheter (PulseSelect, Medtronic). Although preclinical studies have indicated that hemolysis may occur with the PulseSelect system, clinical evidence has been lacking.

This study aimed to evaluate the incidence and clinical relevance of hemolysis following AF ablation using the PulseSelect system in real-world practice. We compared hemolysis-related biomarkers among 3 ablation strategies—PulseSelect, FARAPULSE, and radiofrequency (RF)—and examined whether procedural factors, including the number of applications and hydration protocols, influenced hemolytic outcomes.

Methods

Study design and patients

This single-center, retrospective observational study was conducted at Saiseikai Fukuoka General Hospital. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Approval 2025-3-3) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients undergoing catheter ablation, which included agreement that anonymized clinical data could be used for research purposes, such as presentation at scientific meetings and publication in scientific journals. The study protocol was made publicly available on the institutional website, and patients were informed of their right to opt out.

We retrospectively evaluated peri-procedural hemolysis in 120 consecutive patients who underwent AF ablation between October 2024 and February 2025. This cohort included both first-time and repeat ablation procedures. The majority of first ablation sessions were performed using PFA, employing either the circular multielectrode array catheter (PulseSelect, Medtronic) or the pentaspline catheter (FARAPULSE, Boston Scientific). Patients with renal dysfunction (creatinine clearance <50 mL/min) were preferentially treated with the PulseSelect system.

In repeat ablation sessions, the ablation modality was selected at the discretion of the operator. PFA was used when feasible; however, RF ablation was employed in some cases based on clinical considerations or mapping results, using either the QDOT MICRO (Biosense Webster) or the TactiFlex™ Ablation Catheter (Abbott).

All patients received periprocedural hydration with intravenous normal saline at a rate of 1 mL/kg/h, starting the evening before the procedure and continuing until the following morning. This protocol was designed to replicate strategies for preventing contrast-induced nephropathy.

Ablation procedures were performed in accordance with institutional protocols and manufacturer guidelines.8, 9, 10, 11 PFA procedures were conducted under deep sedation; however, patients with sleep apnea were treated under general anesthesia. Intravenous heparin was administered at the time of vascular access and immediately after transseptal puncture, with continued dosing to maintain an activated clotting time >350 seconds, monitored every 15 to 30 minutes. Pulmonary vein (PV) angiography was performed unless contraindicated by contrast allergy or renal dysfunction.

In the PulseSelect group, ablation included at least 4 applications inside each PV and ≥4 additional applications to the PV antrum, guided by the EnSite X EP system (NavX mode; Abbott), depending on the expected isolation area. Ablation of the cavotricuspid isthmus (CTI), superior vena cava (SVC), and posterior wall was also actively pursued, with no preset limitation on the number of applications.

In contrast, the FARAPULSE group was limited to a maximum of 70 total applications, consisting of 4 “flower” and 4 “basket” configurations, plus 2 “olive” configuration lesions under fluoroscopic guidance.12 Ablation was primarily limited to PV isolation (PVI), with SVC isolation performed only when clinically indicated. In patients with roof-dependent atrial tahycardia after PVI, additional applications were delivered to the posterior wall.

In all patients, successful PVI was confirmed using a multielectrode, small-spaced catheter with a 3-dimensional (3D) mapping system (either EnSite X or CARTO 3; Biosense Webster).

Blood samples were collected before the procedure and 1 day after ablation to assess markers of hemolysis, including haptoglobin, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), total bilirubin, hemoglobin, and creatinine. Additionally, free plasma hemoglobin was measured using the HemoCue® Plasma/Low Hemoglobin System both before and immediately after the procedure. AKI was classified according to the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria as follows: Stage 1, serum creatinine 1.5 to 1.9 times baseline or an increase ≥0.3 mg/dL; Stage 2, 2.0 to 2.9 times baseline; and Stage 3, ≥3.0 times baseline or serum creatinine ≥4.0 mg/dL or initiation of renal replacement therapy.13

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using EZR 1.54 (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), which is a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).14

Data are presented as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range). Comparisons between pre- and post-procedural values were performed using paired t tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Intergroup comparisons among the 3 ablation modalities were conducted using 1-way ANOVA for normally distributed data and the Kruskal-Wallis test for non-normally distributed data, followed in both cases by Bonferroni post hoc analysis. Correlations between hemolysis biomarkers and the number of PFA applications were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient or Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient, depending on the data distribution. All statistical tests were 2-tailed, and a P-value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 120 consecutive patients who underwent AF ablation were analyzed. The mean patient age was 71.2 ± 10.3 years, with 46 (38.3%) being men. Paroxysmal AF was observed in 80 patients (66.7%), and 97 patients (80.8%) underwent first-time ablation. RF ablation was performed in 15 patients, whereas PFA using the FARAPULSE and PulseSelect systems was performed in 39 and 66 patients, respectively.

Demographic and clinical characteristics were generally well-balanced among the ablation modality groups (Table 1). However, the FARAPULSE group included a higher proportion of patients with paroxysmal AF (46.7% [7/15] vs 87.2% [34/39] vs 59.1% [39/66], following RF, FARAPULSE, PulseSelect, P < .05) and had a smaller left atrial diameter compared with the other 2 groups (41.1 ± 7.58 vs 37.5 ± 6.71 vs 41.3 ± 6.95, following RF, FARAPULSE, PulseSelect, P < .05).

Ablation procedures

Of the 97 patients (80.8%) undergoing their first AF ablation procedure, 3 (3.1%) were treated with radiofrequency (RF) ablation, 38 (39.2%) with the FARAPULSE system, and 56 (57.7%) with the PulseSelect system. Among the 23 patients (19.2%) who underwent a second or subsequent ablation session, RF ablation was used in 12 patients (52.2%), FARAPULSE in 1 patient (4.35%), and PulseSelect in 10 patients (43.5%). Only 1 patient who underwent a second ablation session with FARAPULSE due to the need for re-PVI and was not used in any other repeat procedures.

PVI-only ablation was performed in 66 patients (55.0%). The FARAPULSE was more frequently used for PVI-only procedures compared with the other 2 groups (6.67% vs 69.2% vs 57.6%, following RF, FARAPULSE, PulseSelect, P < .001). In the remaining 54 patients (45.0% [54/120]), additional ablation targeting extra-PV sites was performed using either a PFA or RF catheter. Extra-PV ablation was significantly more common in the PulseSelect group than in the FARAPULSE group. In the PulseSelect group, 1 patient required additional RF ablation for mitral isthmus flutter, and 2 patients underwent RF ablation for cavotricuspid isthmus (CTI). In the FARAPULSE group, 2 patients required CTI ablation with RF.

The total number of PFA applications was significantly higher in the PulseSelect group compared with the FARAPULSE group (46.4 ± 6.80 vs 57.5 ± 15.7, P < .001), as the study protocol limited the number of FARAPULSE applications to ≤70, whereas no such restriction was applied in the PulseSelect group. Similarly, the number of PFA applications for both PVI-only and extra-PV targets was significantly higher in the PulseSelect group than in the FARAPULSE group (PVI-only: 43.5 ± 4.10 vs 47.1 ± 9.70; extra-PV: 2.87 ± 5.25 vs 10.4 ± 15.8; P < .05).

There were no significant differences in the baseline values of hemolysis markers among the 3 groups.

Changes in free plasma hemoglobin at the end of ablation

Free plasma hemoglobin, a biomarker of hemolysis, was measured before and at the end of the ablation procedure. As summarized in Table 1, baseline levels of free plasma hemoglobin did not significantly differ among the RF, FARAPULSE, and PulseSelect groups (P = .280). In the RF group, free plasma hemoglobin levels significantly decreased after ablation (300 ± 158 vs 215 ± 141 mg/L; P = .0347). In contrast, levels significantly increased in both the FARAPULSE group (403 ± 443 vs 623 ± 341 mg/L; P < .001) and the PulseSelect group (283 ± 344 vs 405 ± 279 mg/L; P < .001).

As shown in Figure 1A, the ratio of post- to pre-ablation free plasma hemoglobin levels was compared among the 3 groups using ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc analysis. Compared with the RF group (0.733 ± 0.372), the ratio was significantly higher in both the FARAPULSE group (2.43 ± 1.77; P = .000110) and the PulseSelect group (1.89 ± 1.54; P = .000560), with no significant difference between the FARAPULSE and PulseSelect groups (P = .339).

As shown in Figure 1B, the free plasma hemoglobin ratio was significantly correlated with the total number of applications in the FARAPULSE group {r = 0.540 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.273–0.761); P = .000384}. In contrast, no significant correlation was observed in the PulseSelect group (r = 0.0304 [95% CI, −0.227 to 0.304]; P = .8100).

Baseline values of haptoglobin, LDH, total bilirubin, hemoglobin, and creatinine, measured before ablation, did not significantly differ among the RF, FARAPULSE, and PulseSelect groups, as summarized in Table 1. The ratios of these parameters before and 1 day after ablation and their correlation with the total number of applications are presented in Figure 2.

Haptoglobin levels decreased significantly after ablation in all 3 groups (RF: 0.859 ± 0.488 vs 0.722 ± 0.354 g/L, P = .0175; FARAPULSE: 0.990 ± 0.471 vs 0.385 ± 0.267 g/L, P < .001; PulseSelect: 0.836 ± 0.404 vs 0.458 ± 0.300 g/L, P < .001). As shown in Figure 2A, the ratio of post- to pre-ablation levels was compared among the 3 groups. Compared with the RF group (0.938 ± 0.327), the haptoglobin ratio was significantly lower in both the FARAPULSE group (0.405 ± 0.211, P < .001) and the PulseSelect group (0.556 ± 0.194, P < .001). Notably, the decrease in the PulseSelect group was more pronounced than in the RF group, but significantly milder than in the FARAPULSE group (P = .0027 for FARAPULSE vs PulseSelect). The haptoglobin ratio was significantly correlated with the total number of applications in both the FARAPULSE group (r = −0.386, 95% CI: −0.626 to −0.0807, P = .0151) and the PulseSelect group (r = −0.340, 95% CI: −0.539 to −0.105, P = .00554) (Figure 2B), in contrast to free plasma hemoglobin, which showed a significant correlation only in the FARAPULSE group.

LDH levels did not significantly change in the RF group (208 ± 51.0 vs 193 ± 34.8 U/L, P = .0976), but significantly increased in both the FARAPULSE group (183 ± 47.1 vs 268 ± 49.1 U/L, P < .001) and the PulseSelect group (195 ± 50.0 vs 259 ± 48.8 U/L, P < .001). Compared with the RF group (0.950 ± 0.145), the LDH ratio was significantly higher in the FARAPULSE group (1.51 ± 0.281, P < .001) and the PulseSelect group (1.36 ± 0.230, P < .001), with a significant difference also observed between the FARAPULSE and PulseSelect groups (P = .0100) (Figure 2C). The LDH ratio was not correlated with the total number of applications in the FARAPULSE group (r = 0.218, 95% CI: −0.105 to 0.499, P = .183) (Figure 2D). In contrast, significant correlation was observed in the PulseSelect group (r = 0.319, 95% CI: 0.0839–0.521, P = .00893)

Total bilirubin levels significantly increased in all 3 groups (RF: 0.587 ± 0.200 vs 0.933 ± 0.364 mg/dL, P = .000108; FARAPULSE: 0.668 ± 0.336 vs 1.33 ± 0.571 mg/dL, P < .001; PulseSelect: 0.682 ± 0.267 vs 1.08 ± 0.382 mg/dL, P < .001). Compared with the RF group (1.63 ± 0.483), the total bilirubin ratio was significantly higher in the FARAPULSE group (2.15 ± 0.685, P = .0230), but not in the PulseSelect group (1.70 ± 0.563, P = 1.00). A significant difference was also observed between the FARAPULSE and PulseSelect groups (P = .000700) (Figure 2E). The total bilirubin ratio was not significantly correlated with the total number of applications in either the FARAPULSE group (r = 0.219 [95% CI, −0.102 to 0.488], P = .180) or the PulseSelect group (r = −0.232 [95% CI, −0.449 to 0.0286], P = .0604) (Figure 2F).

Hemoglobin levels significantly decreased in all 3 groups (RF: 13.7 ± 1.34 vs 12.0 ± 3.30 g/dL, P = .0214; FARAPULSE: 13.7 ± 1.66 vs 12.0 ± 1.54 g/dL, P < .001; PulseSelect: 13.4 ± 1.78 vs 12.0 ± 1.81 g/dL, P < .001). However, as shown in Figure 2G, there were no significant differences in the hemoglobin ratio among the 3 groups (RF: 0.866 ± 0.206; FARAPULSE: 0.877 ± 0.055; PulseSelect: 0.889 ± 0.060, P = .611). The hemoglobin ratio was not correlated with the total number of applications in the FARAPULSE group (r = −0.259 [95% CI, −0.531 to 0.0616], P = .111) (Figure 2H). In contrast, significant correlation was observed in the PulseSelect group (r = −0.379 [95% CI, −0.568 to −0.150], P = .00172).

Creatinine levels did not change significantly in either the RF group (0.813 ± 0.189 vs 0.820 ± 0.293 mg/dL, P = .889) or the FARAPULSE group (0.844 ± 0.234 vs 0.813 ± 0.219 mg/dL, P = .0578). In contrast, creatinine levels significantly decreased in the PulseSelect group (0.917 ± 0.320 vs 0.866 ± 0.306 mg/dL, P = .000145). However, as shown in Figure 2I, there were no significant differences in the creatinine ratio among the 3 groups (RF: 0.999 ± 0.228; FARAPULSE: 0.985 ± 0.208; PulseSelect: 0.950 ± 0.116, P = .427). The creatinine ratio was not significantly correlated with the total number of applications in either the FARAPULSE group (r = −0.00823 [95% CI, −0.323 to 0.308], P = .960) or the PulseSelect group (r = −0.124 [95% CI, −0.356 to 0.121], P = .319) (Figure 2J). The incidence of AKI was not observed in the 3 groups.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to report subclinical hemolysis following PFA using the PulseSelect system in routine clinical practice. In this study, PFA was associated with significantly higher post-/pre-ablation ratio of free plasma hemoglobin, LDH, and total bilirubin, whereas haptoglobin levels were significantly lower than with RF. Notably, although the PulseSelect system induced hemolysis comparable to that observed with the FARAPULSE system, as indicated by increased free plasma hemoglobin and decreased haptoglobin, the increase observed in the PulseSelect group occurred despite the absence of an upper limit on the number of applications, unlike the FARAPULSE group, where the number of applications was intentionally restricted. Multiple studies of PFA using the FARAPULSE catheter have reported a close correlation between the risk of PFA-induced hemolysis and the number of applications, highlighting the importance of keeping applications within the recommended safe range.15, 16, 17, 18 In contrast, in this study, despite the absence of a predefined limit in the PulseSelect group, the extent of hemolysis—as indicated by changes in free plasma hemoglobin and haptoglobin—was comparable to that in the FARAPULSE group, where the number of applications was intentionally limited. Importantly, hemolysis associated with PulseSelect remained subclinical, as evidenced by the absence of significant declines in hemoglobin compared with the RF group and the lack of any cases of AKI. Our findings suggest that, under current clinical conditions, imposing an upper limit on the number of applications may not be necessary when using the PulseSelect system. Overall, this study offers valuable real-world evidence supporting the low hemolysis risk associated with PulseSelect.

Experimental studies have shown that electroporation can lead to red blood cell breakdown, making hemolysis a plausible outcome at ablative electrical field intensities and cumulative energy doses.6,15,18 During PFA, the electrodes on the ablation catheter are exposed to circulating atrial blood, increasing the risk of hemolysis. High-voltage pulses may induce hemolysis through a stepwise mechanism in which ion leakage causes osmotic imbalance, ultimately resulting in colloidal hemolysis.6 Although hemolysis can occur with any PFA system, its extent is influenced by waveform characteristics, catheter design, field size, and field strength. Kawamura et al19 reported that the hemolysis markers were consistently higher in the FARAPULSE group than the PulseSelect group. In vitro studies have demonstrated that PFA-induced hemolysis in both the PulseSelect and FARAPULSE systems is dependent on electrode–tissue contact and the total energy dose delivered.15,20 The PulseSelect catheter has 9 electrodes and delivers a bipolar waveform at 1.5 kV, whereas the FARAPULSE catheter has 20 electrodes and delivers a bipolar waveform at 2.0 kV. In addition, differences in catheter shape may affect the number of electrodes not in contact with the myocardium during energy delivery, potentially contributing to the observed variation in hemolytic effects. These structural and electrical differences may underlie the distinct hemolytic profiles observed between the 2 systems in clinical use.

Haptoglobin, a liver-derived plasma protein, binds free hemoglobin to form a stable complex, promoting its clearance via hepatic metabolism. During acute hemolysis, this scavenging capacity may be saturated, leading to increased plasma and urinary levels of hemoglobin and heme.21 Although indirect hemolysis markers such as haptoglobin, LDH, and total bilirubin may be influenced by other pathophysiological processes, free plasma hemoglobin is a more direct and specific indicator of intravascular hemolysis.16 Therefore, accurate assessment of hemolysis requires systematic analysis of specific markers, especially free plasma hemoglobin.17 Relying only on next-day samples may underestimate hemolysis due to clearance of free plasma hemoglobin. Popa et al16 reported free plasma hemoglobin levels at the end of the procedure were 4-fold higher with PFA than with RF ablation, whereas a difference was no longer detectable the day after ablation. By combining immediate post-ablation free plasma hemoglobin with next-day markers, our study offers a more accurate evaluation of PFA-induced hemolysis.

In this study, as previously reported, the FARAPULSE system showed a decrease in haptoglobin and an increase in free plasma hemoglobin that correlated with the number of applications, indicating application-dependent hemolysis. In contrast, the PulseSelect group also demonstrated a decrease in haptoglobin proportional to the number of applications—albeit to a lesser extent—whereas the increase in free plasma hemoglobin was not correlated with application number. These findings suggest that hemolysis associated with PulseSelect does occur but remains mild and sufficiently controlled by haptoglobin binding, making it unlikely to be of clinical significance.

Previous studies showed that hemolysis increases with the number of PFA applications, suggesting the need to tailor PFA deliveries to clinical needs.15, 16, 17, 18 Jordan et al22 reported that AKI is rare when PFA is used in a standardized fashion with no extensive high number of applications and is lower than that after thermal ablation. However, higher PFA application counts and ablation beyond the PVs are often needed to reduce AF recurrence.17 Given its milder hemolysis profile, PulseSelect may be suitable for such extended ablations. However, mean free plasma hemoglobin levels in the PulseSelect group (405 ± 279 mg/dL) exceeded 200 mg/dL, a level linked to hemoglobinuria in healthy individuals,16,21 indicating that unnecessary applications should be avoided.

In the present study, pre- and post-procedural hydration with normal saline at a rate of 1 mL/kg/h was routinely administered. The effectiveness of hydration in mitigating hemolysis and renal dysfunction following PFA has been previously reported.23,24 Mohanty et al23 evaluated 128 patients who underwent extensive PFA applications beyond the PVs. Among the 28 patients who did not receive postoperative fluid supplementation, 21 developed hemoglobinuria within 24 hours and experienced a significant increase in serum creatinine. In contrast, none of the 100 patients who received ≥2 L of postoperative normal saline developed detectable hemoglobinuria or showed increased serum creatinine. Similarly, La Fazia et al24 reported that administering 2 L of hydration before the procedure was more effective in preventing AKI than dividing the same volume into pre- and post-procedure portions.

Under this strategy—which included routine hydration, limiting FARAPULSE applications to ≤70, prioritizing the use of PulseSelect in patients with renal dysfunction, and allowing unrestricted PulseSelect use for extra-PV targets—no clinically significant hemolysis resulting in renal impairment was observed. A previous study using FARAPULSE reported that patients with baseline glomerular filtration rate <50 mL/min treated with PFA experience a transient deterioration of renal function and may therefore be at risk of developing AKI.16 If the procedures in the FARAPULSE group had been performed without limiting the number of applications and without selecting the device based on creatinine clearance, a more pronounced difference in hemolysis might have been observed—that is, FARAPULSE may have resulted in significantly greater hemolysis compared with PulseSelect. Notably, this infusion rate was generally well-tolerated, even in patients with impaired cardiac function, and appears effective in suppressing hemolysis that might otherwise lead to clinically relevant renal injury. Whether PFA-induced hemolysis becomes clinically relevant is dependent on numerous factors, including the baseline hematocrit, baseline renal function, and the volume of fluid infused intra-procedurally. Careful monitoring of renal function and the hydration protocol should be strongly considered in patients with a high number of PFA applications or pre-existing renal disease.25

There are several limitations to this study. First, there was procedural variability among the 3 ablation groups. The FARAPULSE system was primarily used during first-time ablation sessions and was mostly limited to PVI, whereas the PulseSelect and RF groups were more frequently employed in repeat procedures and for ablation beyond the PVs. These differences in procedural context may have influenced hemolysis outcomes and limit direct comparability between groups.

Second, this was a single-center, retrospective observational study without randomization. Device selection and procedural strategies—including the choice of ablation system, hydration protocol, and number of applications—were left to the operator’s discretion, potentially introducing selection bias and confounding variables.

Third, although the overall sample size was adequate, the number of patients undergoing extra-PV ablation was relatively small and unevenly distributed across groups. This may have reduced statistical power and limited the generalizability of subgroup analyses.

Finally, because blood sampling was performed after saline infusion, pre- and post-procedural hydration may have influenced some results. However, as the same infusion protocol was applied to both the FARAPULSE and PulseSelect groups, this influence is likely minimal given the comparable conditions between groups.

Taken together, these limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. Further multicenter, prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings in a broader clinical context.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the PulseSelect system was associated with only mild, subclinical hemolysis—comparable to that of the FARAPULSE system—without any cases of clinically significant anemia or AKI. Notably, the lack of correlation between the number of applications and free plasma hemoglobin levels underscores the system’s favorable safety profile, even in the absence of procedural limits. These results highlight the potential of PulseSelect as a versatile and reliable ablation tool, not only for PVI but also for more extensive lesion sets in complex procedures.
