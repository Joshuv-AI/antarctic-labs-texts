Dual functional rGO/Fe3O4/PANI nanocomposite anodes for enhanced performance of microbial fuel cells
Authors: Getabalew Shifera Weldegrum, Demise Alebachew Zemedagegnehu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A dual chamber microbial fuel cells was developed using biomass derived reduced graphene oxide (E-rGO) synthesized from Ensete ventricosum corm waste as an anode material for simultaneous bioelectricity generation and heavy metal remediation. To enhance electrochemical performance, an E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC anode was fabricated and evaluated for the removal of Cr (VI) and Pb (II) from wastewater. Incorporation of Fe3O4 NP and polyaniline improved electrical conductivity, surface characteristics, and interfacial charge transfer properties. Structural, morphological, optical, and thermal properties of the materials were characterized using UV-vis, SEM, XRD, FTIR, Raman spectroscopy, and TGA, while electrochemical behavior was examined by cyclic voltammetry and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy. The E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI anode achieved Cr (VI) and Pb (II) removal efficiencies of 88% and 86%, respectively, compared with 74% and 68% for pristine E-rGO. The E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI nanocomposite electrode also generated a maximum power density of 65 mW m−2 and a current density of 1312 mA m−2, representing a significant improvement over the E-rGO anode (8.55 mW m−2 and 612 mA m−2 under identical operating conditions. While the power density is moderate compared with highly optimized laboratory scale MFC systems, the results demonstrate enhanced performance in a system designed for concurrent wastewater treatment and energy recovery. These findings indicate that biomass derived graphene based nanocomposites can serve as sustainable and effective electrode materials for integrated heavy metal remediation and bioenergy generation in microbial fuel cell systems.

Introduction

The increasing demand for sustainable energy recovery and effective wastewater treatment has driven interest in microbial fuel cell (MFCs) as Bioelectrochemical systems capable of simultaneously generating electricity and removing environmental pollutants1–3. In MFCs, electroactive microorganisms oxidize organic substrates and transfer electrons to the anode via extracellular electron transfer (EET). Electrons then flow through an external circuit to the cathode, generating electricity, while protons migrate through the membrane to maintain charge neutrality2,4. Simultaneously, these reactions enable the degradation, reduction, or immobilization of contaminants, making MFCs suitable for integrated bioenergy and wastewater treatment applications5–7.

Despite these advantages, practical implementation is limited by low power density, high internal resistance, moderate pollutant removal efficiency, and insufficient long term electrode stability8,9. The anode is a critical component, influencing microbial adhesion, biofilm formation, EET, and overall system performance10. Conventional carbon based electrodes such as graphite, carbon cloth, carbon felt, carbon nanotubes, and graphene derivatives often exhibit limited surface functionality, low biocompatibility, and restricted electron transfer. Metal electrodes offer high conductivity but are prone to corrosion, microbial toxicity, and high cost3,4,11.

Rational hybrid material design integrates structural, conductive, and catalytic functionalities to overcome electrode limitations, with synergistic interface engineering principles demonstrated across advanced energy and catalytic systems directly informing high performance MFCs electrode development12–14. Biomass derived carbon materials have gained attention as sustainable alternatives for MFC anodes9. Graphene oxide (GO) from agricultural waste offers a versatile platform, but its oxygen rich groups disrupt the sp2 carbon network, limiting conductivity. Reduction to reduced graphene oxide (rGO) partially restores conductivity, surface area, microbial affinity, and electrochemical stability; however, pristine rGO still suffers from nanosheet restacking, limited redox activity, and suboptimal wettability, reducing accessible electroactive sites under practical MFC conditions15. Previous work integrating Fe3O4 nanoparticle (NP) with enset derived rGO produced an E-rGO/Fe3O4 nanocomposite (NC) anode with improved electricity generation and Cr(VI)/Pb(II) removal due to enhanced redox mediation and adsorption16. Nonetheless, the binary system remains limited by incomplete surface functionalization, inefficient interfacial charge transfer, and imperfect long term biofilm coupling.

Conducting polymers provide a strategy to address these limitations due to high conductivity, reversible redox behavior, and strong interaction with electroactive microorganisms17–19. Polymers such as polypyrrole, polythiophene, and PEDOT have been explored for MFC anodes but often face synthesis complexity, environmental instability, and long term performance degradation20. Polyaniline (PANI) is attractive due to low cost, facile synthesis, and high pseudo capacitance, but alone suffers from mechanical instability, volumetric changes during redox cycling, conductivity loss under neutral conditions, and structural degradation21.Integration of PANI with carbon nanomaterials and metal oxides mitigates these limitations while enhancing conductivity and redox activity.

Building on the E-rGO/Fe3O4 platform, we introduce PANI to form a ternary E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI nanocomposite anode. E-rGO provides a conductive and mechanically robust backbone, Fe3O4offers redox mediation and metal adsorption, and PANI forms a conductive, redox active interfacial network that improves surface roughness, microbial affinity, and charge transfer. This design suppresses NP aggregation, buffers PANI volumetric changes, increases electroactive sites, and stabilizes long term biofilm formation, promoting enhanced electricity generation and heavy metal remediation22,23.

The removal of toxic metals such as Cr (VI) and Pb (II) remains a critical environmental challenge due to persistence, toxicity, and bioaccumulation. Conventional treatments are energy intensive and produce secondary pollution, whereas MFCs allow simultaneous electricity generation and metal reduction with low sludge production and reduced carbon emissions24,25. Biomass derived multifunctional NC anodes further amplify these benefits through combined microbial reduction, redox mediation, and adsorption.

In this study, enset derived E-rGO and ternary E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC were synthesized and employed as anodes in dual chamber MFCs. Their structural, electrochemical, and Bioelectrochemical properties were systematically assessed, alongside simultaneous Cr (VI) and Pb (II) removal and electricity generation. While results demonstrate significant improvements in power output, electron transfer efficiency, and heavy metal remediation compared to binary E-rGO/Fe3O4 and conventional anodes, limitations include the absence of microbial community characterization, scale up validation, and long term operational assessment. Nevertheless, the findings provide strong evidence that rational hybrid NC design can substantially enhance MFC electrode performance, offering a sustainable strategy for integrated wastewater treatment and bioenergy recovery.

Experimental

Materials and reagents

Wastewater effluents used in this study were collected from a local pond near Mattu University, Ethiopia. Sweet potato waste was obtained from a local market in Mattu town, while Ensete ventricosum corm waste was collected from the Kaffa Zone, Southwest Ethiopia, and used as the biomass precursor for graphene oxide synthesis.

All chemicals were of analytical grade and used without further purification. Ammonium per sulfate (APS, (NH4)2S2O8, 98%), ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O, 97%), ferrous chloride tetra hydrate (FeCl2.4H2O, 99%), sodium borohydride (NaBH4), potassium permanganate (KMnO4, 99%), sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 98%), and sodium nitrate (NaNO3) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Aniline (C6H5NH2, 99.5%), ethanol (98%), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%), nitrogen (N2) gas, and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) were obtained from Merck. Lead (II) nitrate (Pb (NO3)2) and chromium (VI) nitrate (Cr (NO3)3.9H2O) were supplied by R&M Chemicals.

Preparation of carbonized corm and synthesis of E-GO

The corm of Ensete ventricosum was collected thoroughly washed with deionized water, sliced into small pieces, and oven dried at 105 °C for 24 h. The dried material was then ground into a fine powder and carbonized in a tubular furnace under a nitrogen atmosphere at 900 °C for 3 h to obtain calcined corm carbon, which served as the precursor for graphene oxide synthesis.

E-GO was prepared from the carbonized material using a modified Hummers’ method due to its simplicity, cost effectiveness, and ability to produce well structured graphene oxide without releasing harmful gases26,27.

Briefly, 5 g of carbonized corm powder was mixed with 150 mL of concentrated H2SO4 and 6 g of NaNO3, and the mixture was stirred continuously for 60 min. While maintaining the temperature between 0 and 5 °C in an ice bath, 15 g of KMnO4 was added gradually under constant stirring over 3 h. The mixture was then stirred at room temperature for 24 h to complete the oxidation process, during which the color changed from black to violet brown.

Subsequently, 150 mL of distilled water was added, and the solution was heated at 90 °C for 20 min, producing a dark brown suspension. After cooling to room temperature, an additional 200 mL of distilled water was added slowly. The reaction was terminated by the drop wise addition of 20 mL of H2O2 to remove residual KMnO4. The resulting E-GO was thoroughly washed with distilled water and ethanol until a neutral pH (~ 7) was reached, and finally dried in an oven at 40 °C for 24 h28,29.

Reduction of E-GO to E-rGO

E-GO was chemically reduced using NaBH4 as the reducing agent. In a typical procedure, 100 mg of E-GO was dispersed in 100 mL of distilled water in a round bottom flask and sonicated for 1 h to obtain a stable aqueous dispersion. Subsequently, 200 mg of NaBH4 was added under continuous magnetic stirring, and the mixture was stirred at 125 °C for 3 h. The reduction process yielded a black precipitate corresponding to E-rGO NP, which was collected by filtration and vacuum dried at 60 °C30,31.

Synthesis of E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI nanocomposites

Prior to preparing the E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC, E-rGO/Fe3O4 NC were synthesized following the procedure reported in our previous work16. The E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC were then fabricated via in situ polymerization of PANI on the surface of the pre synthesized E-rGO/Fe3O4 NC. In a typical synthesis, 500 mg of E-rGO/Fe3O4 NC powder was dispersed in 50 mL of 1 M H2SO4 and stirred vigorously until fully homogenized. Subsequently, 1 mL of C6H5NH2 monomer was added, and the mixture was thoroughly mixed. The solution was cooled to − 4 °C using an ice bath. Polymerization was initiated by the dropwise addition of 3 mL of 1 M (NH4)2S2O8 under continuous stirring for 8 h. After completion, the resulting E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC was filtered, washed with distilled water and ethanol to remove unreacted monomers and oxidant residues, and dried at 60°C32. A schematic representation of the overall process, from Ensete ventricosum corm carbonization to E-rGO NP formation and E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC synthesis is shown in Fig. 1.

Fabrication of anode

In MFCs, the anode is a key component that facilitates electron transfer from electroactive microorganisms to the external circuit, thereby enabling electricity generation. Anode modification was performed following previously reported procedures16,33. Initially, bare graphite electrodes were soaked in distilled water for 24 h and subsequently rinsed with 200 mM PBS to remove impurities. The electrodes were then coated with either E-rGO NP or E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC using a drop casting method. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) was employed as a polymeric binder to improve adhesion between the nanocatalyst and the graphite surface.

To prepare the coating suspension, the required amount of E-rGO NP or E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC was dispersed in deionized water and sonicated in an ultrasonic bath for 15 min at room temperature to obtain a uniform dispersion. Separately, a PVA solution was prepared by dissolving the polymer in deionized water at a 15:85 (PVA: nanocatalyst, wt/wt) ratio under magnetic stirring at 90 °C for 1 h. The two solutions were then combined and sonicated to ensure homogeneous distribution of the nanocatalyst.

Using a micro syringe, 300 µL of the final suspension was drop cast onto the surface of bare graphite electrode with dimensions of 12 cm × 0.5 cm (height × radius). The coated electrodes were dried in an oven at 60 °C for 12 h to enhance bonding strength and interface stability. The surface area of both synthesized modified electrodes (E-rGO and E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI) was 40 cm2 and served as anode and the bare graphite rods served as cathode electrodes in cathode chambers.

Characterization techniques

The synthesized E-rGO NP and E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC were characterized using multiple analytical techniques. Surface morphology and elemental composition were examined via field emission scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (FE-SEM/EDX). Crystalline structures were analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD; Bruker D2 Phaser), optical properties by UV-visible spectroscopy (Azzota SM-1600, USA), functional groups and chemical bonding by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR; JASCO FT/IR-6200), and structural defects by Raman spectroscopy (IHR550, 532 nm laser). Thermal stability was assessed using thermo gravimetric analysis (TGA; DTG-60 H, Shimadzu, Japan).

Physicochemical parameters of the wastewater, including pH, conductivity, and temperature, were measured using a pH meter (EUTECH Instrument-700, USA), conductivity meter (Alpha-800), and thermometer (GH, ZEAL Ltd., UK), respectively.

Electrochemical behavior was evaluated using a BAS 100B (USA) analyzer in a standard three-electrode setup, with Ag/AgCl as the reference, platinum wire as the counter, and bare or modified graphite electrodes (Gr, E-rGO, E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI) as working electrodes. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was performed in 2 M H2SO4 at a scan rate of 5 mV s−1 over 0 to + 1.5 V. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted in the same 2 M H2SO4 supporting electrolyte over a frequency range of 100 kHz to 10 mHz with an AC amplitude of 10 mV, ensuring reproducible evaluation of interfacial charge transfer and electrode properties.

Heavy metal concentrations (Cr (VI) and Pb (II)) in wastewater before and after treatment were quantified using atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS; BUCK SCIENTIFIC Model 210, USA) to assess remediation efficiency.

MFCs setup and operation

The study employed double chamber microbial fuel cells (DMFCs) with each chamber measuring 12 cm in length and 6.5 cm in diameter. Each chamber had a total volume of approximately 1000 mL, with a working volume of 750 mL. The anode chamber was loaded with 150 g of sweet potato waste mixed with pond wastewater supplemented with 150 ppm of Cr (VI) and Pb (II) ions. The cathode chamber contained 50 mM KMnO4 in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, with the pH maintained at 6.45 in the anode chamber and 7.0 in the cathode chamber using phosphate buffers. The use of KMnO4 as the catholyte was intentionally chosen over dissolved oxygen to provide a high redox potential, facilitating faster electron acceptance at the cathode, avoiding variability associated with oxygen reduction and improving overall MFCs performance.

The anode chamber was sealed without an external substrate inlet, as the organic content of the sweet potato waste was sufficient, while the cathode chamber was supplied with ambient oxygen to support any additional redox reactions. For each experiment, a prepared anode electrode and a graphite rod serving as the anode and cathode. Salt bridges were prepared following the method reported by34, by dissolving 3 g of agar in 100 mL of boiling water with stirring, then adding 12 g of KCl. The solution was poured into a high density polyethylene pipe (9 cm long, 2 cm diameter), sealed at one end with a glass stopper, and allowed to cool for 2 h.

To evaluate the electrochemical activity of the NC relative to bare graphite rods, three DMFCs were constructed using bare graphite rods, E-rGO, and E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI modified electrodes as anodes, with graphite rods serving as cathodes in all setups. The MFCs were operated continuously at room temperature for 30 days. All electrodes (Gr, E-rGO, and E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI) were connected using copper wires, and external resistances were varied over a wide range (10,000 to 50 Ω) to capture both low and high load operating conditions and allow accurate determination of maximum power density, polarization behavior and internal resistance of the MFCs35.

Results and discussion

Material characterization E-rGO, and E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI

FE-SEM/EDX analysis

Figure 2 presents the FE-SEM micrographs and corresponding EDX spectra of E-rGO NP and the E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC, providing clear insight into the morphological evolution and elemental composition associated with ternary NC formation. As shown in Fig. 2a, E-rGO NP exhibits a typical rGO characterized by thin, wrinkled, sheet like layers forming a two dimensional, interconnected porous network. The crumpled and partially restacked graphene sheets result from the removal of oxygen containing functional groups during the reduction process, which induces sheet corrugation and prevents complete layer restacking. This architecture offers a high specific surface area, abundant defect sites, and open diffusion pathways, making E-rGO highly suitable for subsequent functionalization and NP anchoring, consistent with previous reports37.

In contrast, the FE-SEM image of the E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI (Fig. 3b) reveals a pronounced transformation in surface morphology. The rGO sheets are uniformly decorated by Fe3O4 NP and subsequently coated with PANI, resulting in a rough, dense, and highly textured surface. Notably, nanowires like PANI structures are observed to grow in the surface of the E-rGO/Fe3O4 matrix, indicating successful polymerization and homogeneous coating of PANI throughout the composite. This hierarchical and well integrated structure suggests strong interfacial interactions among rGO, Fe3O4, and PANI, which effectively suppress NP agglomeration and establish a continuous conductive network. Similar PANI grown nanostructures and synergistic interactions have been reported to significantly enhance charge transfer behavior and electrochemical performance38,39.

The elemental composition of the synthesized materials was further examined using EDX. The EDX spectrum of E-rGO (Fig. 3a.i.) shows dominant peaks corresponding to carbon (C) and oxygen (O), confirming the successful reduction of GO while retaining residual oxygen-containing functional groups. These functional groups improve surface hydrophilicity and provide active sites for the anchoring of metal oxides and conducting polymers, which is advantageous for composite formation37. In contrast, the EDX spectrum of the E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC (Fig. 3bi) displays clear signals of C, O, nitrogen (N), and iron (Fe), verifying the successful incorporation of PANI and Fe3O4NP into the rGO matrix. The simultaneous presence of these elements confirms the formation of a well integrated ternary NCs rather than a simple physical mixture. Overall, the combined FE-SEM and EDX analyses provide strong evidence for the successful synthesis of structurally robust E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC, whose hierarchical morphology and tailored composition are highly favorable for enhanced electron transfer, microbial interaction, and electrochemical performance in MFC and related energy applications38–40.

XRD and UV–vis analyses

X-ray diffraction (XRD) was employed as a non-destructive technique to investigate the crystallographic structure, crystallite size, orientation, and interlayer spacing of the synthesized E-GO, E-rGO NP and E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC. The XRD patterns presented in Fig. 3a confirm the successful formation and structural evolution of the materials23. The diffraction pattern of E-GO exhibits a sharp and intense peak at 2θ ≈ 10–11°, corresponding to the (001) plane, which is characteristic of E-GO and indicates increased interlayer spacing due to the introduction of oxygen containing functional groups and intercalated water molecules during the oxidation of carbonized biomass23. Upon chemical reduction using NaBH4, this (001) peak disappears, and a broad diffraction peak emerges at 2θ value 25.8° indexed to the (002) plane of graphitic carbon. This shift confirms the effective reduction of E-GO to E-rGO NP, accompanied by partial restoration of the sp2carbon network and a decrease in interlayer spacing resulting from the removal of oxygen functionalities37,41. The broad nature of the (002) peak further reflects the disordered and few-layered structure of E-rGO, which is consistent with previous reports on biomass-derived rGO materials42.

For the ternary E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC, the XRD pattern displays additional diffraction peaks corresponding to the crystalline phases of Fe3O4, specifically the (200), (311), (400), (511), and (440) planes, which are in good agreement with the inverse spinel structure of magnetite. The presence of these characteristic reflections confirms the successful incorporation of Fe3O4 NP into the E-rGO matrix. Notably, no distinct diffraction peaks attributable to PANI are observed, which can be ascribed to its amorphous or semi crystalline nature and its uniform coating over the rGO/Fe3O4 surface. The reduced intensity and slight broadening of the Fe3O4 related peaks, as well as the apparent suppression of the rGO (002) reflection due to overlap with PANI related reflections, suggest strong interfacial interactions and effective coupling among rGO, Fe3O4, and PANI within the ternary composite32,39,43. Importantly, the absence of impurity peaks indicates the high purity and structural integrity of the synthesized E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC.

The optical properties of the synthesized materials were further examined using UV-visible spectroscopy, as shown in Fig. 3b. The UV–vis spectrum of E-rGO exhibits a prominent absorption peak at around 245 nm, which is attributed to the π→π* transitions of aromatic C=C bonds within the graphene framework. This absorption feature reflects the restoration of conjugated sp2 domains upon reduction, which is commonly accompanied by a red shift compared to GO, indicating improved electronic conductivity. In contrast, the E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC display multiple absorption bands at approximately 271, 376, and 459 nm. The peak at 271 nm is associated with the π→π* transitions of aromatic C=C bonds in E-rGO, while the absorption bands at 376 and 459 nm are characteristic of PANI and are assigned to π → π* transitions of the benzenoid rings, π→ polaron (radical cation) transitions, and polaron→π transitions, respectively32. These features confirm the formation of polaronic and bipolaronic species along the PANI backbone, which facilitate charge delocalization and enhance electrical conductivity and electrochemical activity.

FT-IR and Raman analyses

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) and Raman spectroscopy were employed to investigate the structural and chemical characteristics of the synthesized E-rGO and E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC, as shown in Fig. 4a and b. In the FTIR spectrum of E-rGO, a broad absorption band around 3060 cm−1 corresponds to O–H stretching vibrations, indicating residual hydroxyl groups from partial reduction of GO, consistent with previous reports on oxygen-containing functional groups in reduced graphene derivatives44–48.The peak at 1566 cm−1 is attributed to C=C stretching within sp2 carbon networks of graphitic domains, while peaks at 1339, 1224, and 1098 cm−1 are associated with C–O stretching from residual epoxy or alkoxy functionalities. A weak absorption at ~ 599 cm−1 is assigned to out of plane C–H bending vibrations.

The FTIR spectrum of the E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC exhibited additional absorption bands characteristic of PANI, confirming its successful incorporation. These include C–N stretching and bending vibrations at 1098 and 1318 cm−1, Fe–O stretching at 578 cm−1, C=C/C–C stretching in aromatic rings at 1404 and 1569 cm−1, benzene ring bending at 718 cm−1, and C–H/CH2 stretching at 2860 and 2924 cm−1. Further confirmation of PANI integration is provided by bands at 1288 cm−1(C–N stretch), 1592 cm−1(quinoid and benzenoid C=N), 873 cm−1(C–H stretch), and 794 cm−1(C–H out of plane bending). The broad peak at 3400 cm−1 corresponds to O–H and N–H stretching vibrations, likely due to hydrogen bonding between PANI and rGO sheets or absorbed moisture39,49–51. The slight shift of the quinoid C=C stretching compared to E-rGO NP indicates π–π stacking and electronic interactions between PANI and rGO49. Additional bands at ~ 1318, 1098, 718, and 578 cm−1 correspond to C–N and Fe–O vibrations, confirming successful incorporation of both PANI and Fe3O4 into the rGO matrix32.

Raman spectroscopy provided complementary information on the structural and electronic properties of the materials (Fig. 4b). E-rGO displayed two prominent peaks at 1346 cm−1 (D band) and 1575 cm−1(G band), corresponding to structural defects in sp3 carbon and graphitic sp2 carbon, respectively. The higher intensity of the D band suggests significant defect density introduced during chemical reduction52–54. In the E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC, the G band shifted slightly to 1583 cm−1, reflecting enhanced electronic interactions and π–π stacking between rGO and PANI. Characteristic PANI Raman peaks were observed at 1176 cm−1(in-plane C–H bending of quinoid ring), 1255 cm−1(benzenoid ring), 1398 cm−1(C–C stretching of quinoid ring), and 1489 cm−1(C=C stretching), confirming successful incorporation32,55–57. The increased baseline intensity indicates enhanced charge delocalization and electronic coupling among rGO, PANI, and Fe3O4 NP.

These structural and chemical modifications directly influence the electrochemical behavior of the NC. Hydrogen bonding and π–π interactions facilitate improved electronic connectivity and rapid charge transfer58. The incorporation of Fe3O4 NP introduces additional redox active sites, while residual functional groups on rGO enhance electrolyte wetting and increase active surface area32. Collectively, these features contribute to higher conductivity, improved charge storage capacity, and enhanced cycling stability, making E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC a promising candidate for good performance electrochemical applications such as super capacitors, MFCs, and sensors.

Thermo gravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermo gravimetric analysis (TGA) was employed to investigate the thermal stability, decomposition behavior, and compositional robustness of E-rGO and E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC, as illustrated in Fig. 5. Understanding the thermal response of these materials is essential for their application as electrode materials in MFCs and related Bioelectrochemical systems, where thermal stress may arise during fabrication and prolonged operation.

The TGA profile of pristine E-rGO exhibits a multistage weight loss behavior. An initial mass loss below ~ 100 °C is attributed to the removal of physically adsorbed and interlayer water molecules trapped between graphene sheets. The second weight loss region, occurring between 100 and 200 °C, corresponds to the decomposition of labile oxygen containing functional groups, such as hydroxyl and carboxyl moieties, introduced during the oxidation and reduction processes31,59. A more pronounced weight loss is observed in the temperature range of 200–590 °C, which is associated with the thermal decomposition of residual oxygen functionalities (C = O and C–O) and reduction byproducts remaining on the rGO surface. Beyond 590 °C, the mass loss rate decreases and stabilizes, indicating the dominance of a thermally stable sp2 hybridized carbon framework. Notably, E-rGO retains approximately 58 wt% residual mass at 750–800 °C, demonstrating relatively high thermal stability consistent with previously reported rGO systems60.

In contrast, the E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC display a more complex, multistep degradation profile due to the coexistence of organic (PANI) and inorganic (Fe3O4) components. The initial weight loss below 100 °C is again attributed to moisture evaporation; however, its reduced magnitude compared to E-rGO NP suggests improved moisture resistance resulting from the incorporation of Fe3O4 NP and the PANI network. The second degradation stage, observed between 100 and 220 °C, is ascribed to the deprotonation of the PANI backbone, loss of dopants and surfactants, and partial removal of functional groups introduced during PANI functionalization of E-rGO NP. The most significant mass loss occurs in the range of 220–660 °C, corresponding to the thermal decomposition of PANI chains and associated organic segments. Above 660 °C, the rate of mass loss decreases markedly, leaving a stable residue composed primarily of Fe3O4 NP and carbonaceous char. Consequently, the NC exhibits a higher residual mass (~ 62 wt%) than pristine E-rGO NP, confirming the role of Fe3O4 as a thermally stable reinforcing phase that suppresses structural collapse at elevated temperatures.

Overall, the TGA results reveal the synergistic effects of integrating PANI and Fe3O4 into the E-rGO matrix. Strong interfacial interactions among E-rGO, PANI, and Fe3O4 such as hydrogen bonding and π–π stacking restrict polymer chain mobility, delay thermal degradation, and enhance overall thermal robustness. Although the incorporation of PANI introduces additional organic decomposition steps, leading to slightly reduced high-temperature stability compared to E-rGO/Fe3O4 systems without PANI, the composite remains thermally more stable than bare E-rGO NP61,62.

From an application perspective, the enhanced thermal resistance imparted by Fe3O4 NP ensures improved structural integrity under prolonged electrochemical cycling, while the controlled decomposition behavior of PANI confirms adequate robustness for the typical operating temperatures of MFCs and super capacitors. Furthermore, the formation of a stable carbon metal oxide framework at high temperatures suggests improved resistance to thermal stress, electrode delamination, and long term performance degradation. These characteristics collectively demonstrate the suitability of E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC as durable and thermally stable electrode materials for Bioelectrochemical and energy storage applications.

Electrochemical characterization

CV and EIS analysis

The electrochemical properties of electrode materials critically influence MFCs performance because they govern EET, biofilm electrode interactions, and overall energy conversion efficiency63. CV measurements were conducted in dilute H2SO4 supporting electrolyte to evaluate the intrinsic electrochemical behavior of the electrodes under controlled conditions, independent of MFCs operation. This approach minimizes interference from organic constituents, suspended solids, and microbial activity present in wastewater that may obscure the fundamental electrode response. The Bioelectrochemical performance under actual MFCs conditions was assessed separately using polarization, power generation, and pollutant removal analyses. As shown in Fig. 6a, the bare Gr electrode exhibited an almost featureless CV profile without distinct redox peaks across the scanned potential window, indicating limited electrochemical activity and sluggish electron transfer kinetics. Such behavior is typical of unmodified carbon electrodes, which possess few electro active sites and poor interfacial conductivity, leading to low current generation and reduced power output in MFCs systems64,65.

In contrast, the E-rGO modified electrode displayed well-defined anodic and cathodic peaks and significantly higher current response (Fig. 6a). This improvement arises from effective rGO and partial restoration of the sp2 carbon network, which enhances conductivity and electron transport66. Additionally, the large specific surface area of E-rGO NP introduces abundant electro active sites that strengthen interactions with electro active microorganisms, facilitating improved electron harvesting in Bioelectrochemical systems67.

The E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC electrode showed the most pronounced redox peaks and the highest peak currents among all electrodes. Its larger CV enclosed area indicates superior capacitive and pseudo capacitive behavior resulting from synergistic integration of E-rGO, Fe3O4, and conducting PANI. PANI provides reversible redox activity and conductivity through proton doping dedoping processes, while Fe3O4 NP act as electron mediating centers that enhance charge mobility and interfacial transfer68. E-rGO NP serves as a conductive backbone, ensuring efficient electron pathways and uniform component dispersion. This synergy improves electrochemical activity and charge storage, contributing to stabilized current output and enhanced energy recovery during MFC operation69.

EIS further supported the CV observations (Fig. 6b). All electrodes exhibited Nyquist plots consisting of a high frequency semicircle followed by a low frequency linear tail. The semicircle represents charge transfer resistance (Rct) at the electrode electrolyte interface, while the linear region corresponds to Warburg impedance associated with ion diffusion70.

The bare Gr electrode showed the largest semicircle diameter, confirming high Rct and poor electron transfer kinetics. Modified electrodes exhibited smaller diameters, indicating improved interfacial conductivity and faster electron transfer. Quantitative analysis revealed a reduction in Rct from 123 Ω for E-rGO to 45 Ω for the E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI electrode (Table 2). The lowest resistance reflects enhanced electrode electrolyte contact, increased effective surface area, and facilitated EET due to intimate coupling among PANI, Fe3O4, and E-rGO.

Collectively, CV and EIS analyses demonstrate that the E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI modified electrode provides clear advantages for MFCs applications. Enhanced redox activity and capacitance indicate improved charge buffering, while reduced Rct signifies faster electron transfer and lower internal resistance. These characteristics correlate with higher current density, improved power output, and better operational stability71. Overall, the synergistic integration of E-rGO, Fe3O4, and PANI overcomes the limitations of bare graphite, highlighting the NC as a promising electrode material for advanced MFC systems. This behavior aligns with recent findings in hybrid catalytic frameworks emphasizing conductive networks combined with redox-active components to enhance interfacial charge transfer and electrochemical reactivity72,73.

Performance of E-rGO and E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI Anodes in MFCs

Open circuit voltage and current response

The electrochemical performance of anode materials in MFCs is strongly influenced by their ability to support biofilm formation, facilitate EET, and sustain efficient charge transport. In this study, bare Gr, E-rGO, and E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI modified electrodes were evaluated over 30 days using OCV measurements and current output under a 50 Ω external load. Sweet potato waste was used as the organic substrate to simulate real-world waste to energy conversion.

As shown in Fig. 7a, all electrodes exhibited a steady increase in OCV during the first 15 days, reaching peak values of 440 mV for Gr, 508 mV for E-rGO, and 1080 mV for E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI. The initial voltage increase is attributed to the proliferation of exoelectrogenic bacteria and the formation of a conductive biofilm on the anode surface. In the case of E-rGO electrodes, the improved OCV compared to bare Gr is due to the high surface area, enhanced electrical conductivity, and greater density of electro active sites provided by the rGO network. These features facilitate more efficient electron transfer from MFC to the electrode surface74.

The E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI modified electrode exhibited the highest OCV; more than double that of bare Gr, demonstrating the synergistic effect of its composite components. The conductive E-rGO network provides rapid electron pathways, Fe3O4 NP act as redox mediators to enhance charge transport across the biofilm electrode interface, and the redox active, biocompatible PANI supports both bacterial adhesion and electron transfer75. Together, these factors increase the effective charge density at the anode surface and promote highly efficient extracellular electron transfer, resulting in significantly improved OCV1.

After day 15, a gradual decline in OCV was observed for all electrodes, likely due to the depletion of readily metabolize able organic substrates in the sweet potato waste. Reduced substrate availability limits microbial metabolic activity, diminishing electron production and lowering the voltage. This trend emphasizes the critical role of substrate supply in sustaining long-term MFC performance and highlights the importance of anode modifications in maintaining biofilm activity during periods of substrate scarcity76.

Current generation under a 50 Ω external load (Fig. 7b) further highlighted the superior performance of the modified electrodes. The E-rGO electrode achieved a maximum current of 1.95 mA, while the E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI electrode reached 4.26 mA, illustrating the substantial enhancement in electron transfer facilitated by the NCs structure. This increase in current is consistent with the CV and EIS results, which showed higher redox activity and lower charge-transfer resistance (Rct) for the modified electrodes, confirming that improvements at the electrode interface directly translate into enhanced MFC performance.

The superior current output of the E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI anode can be mechanistically explained by several factors. First, the conductive network of E-rGO and PANI ensures rapid electron transport from the microbial biofilm to the electrode. Second, Fe3O4 NP facilitates electron hopping and act as electron mediators, reducing interfacial resistance and promoting faster charge transfer. Third, the modified electrode surface provides an optimal environment for biofilm growth, resulting in denser and more electro active microbial populations. These combined factors contribute to higher current density, increased power output, and improved Coulombic efficiency compared to bare Gr and E-rGO electrodes77.

Moreover, the enhanced OCV and current generation observed for the E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI electrode reflect its capacity to maintain biofilm integrity and electrical conductivity over prolonged operation. The redox active PANI not only contributes to electron transport but may also act as a temporary charge buffer, stabilizing the current output despite fluctuations in microbial activity or substrate availability. Such characteristics are particularly advantageous for MFCs utilizing complex organic waste, where substrate heterogeneity and microbial dynamics can impact overall performance. Overall, the OCV and current data clearly demonstrate that the E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI modified electrode significantly outperforms both bare graphite and E-rGO electrodes. Its ability to enhance microbial adhesion, facilitate efficient extracellular electron transfer, and maintain high charge density underscores the importance of synergistic electrode design for maximizing MFC efficiency, especially when using renewable organic substrates such as sweet potato waste.

Polarization behavior and internal resistance

Polarization curves are fundamental for evaluating MFC performance because they describe the relationship between voltage, current density, and power density under varying external loads. In this study, the polarization behavior of bare Gr, E-rGO, and E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI modified anodes was examined by applying external resistances ranging from 10,000 Ω to 50 Ω (Fig. 8a–d). A high resistance was initially used to promote biofilm maturation and prevent premature charge withdrawal. As the biofilm stabilized and electron transfer pathways developed, the resistance was gradually decreased to enhance current generation and obtain complete polarization profiles. This approach enables reliable voltage current characterization and aligns with established MFC evaluation practices78,79. As expected, maximum power output occurred when the external resistance approached the internal resistance of the system.

All electrodes exhibited typical polarization behavior characterized by three distinct regions. An initial rapid voltage drop at low current density reflected activation losses associated with initiating microbial extracellular electron transfer. At intermediate current densities, voltage decreased more gradually due to ohmic losses arising from electrode and electrolyte resistance. At higher current densities, sharper voltage declines indicated mass transport limitations related to substrate availability and proton diffusion within the biofilm. Across the entire operating range, the E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI anode consistently maintained higher voltages than E-rGO and bare graphite, demonstrating improved electron transfer efficiency and reduced interfacial resistance.

Power density analysis further confirmed the enhanced electrochemical performance of the composite electrode. The E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI anode achieved a maximum power density of 65 mW m−2, compared with 8.55 mW m−2 for E-rGO, representing a substantial improvement under heavy metal contaminated wastewater conditions. Although power densities reported for MFC systems vary widely depending on configuration, substrate, and operating conditions80, the observed enhancement demonstrates the effectiveness of composite modification. Internal resistance decreased from 434 Ω for E-rGO to 382 Ω for the E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI electrode (Fig. 9a, b), indicating improved conductivity and reduced energy loss. Similarly, at an external resistance of 50 Ω, current density increased from 612 mA m−2(E-rGO) to 1312 mA m−2 for the composite anode, further confirming improved charge transport pathways.

The superior performance of the E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI anode arises from synergistic structural and conductive contributions of its components. Fe3O4 NP provides redox active sites that facilitate electron hopping and reduce charge transfer resistance, while PANI enhances electrical conductivity, surface roughness, and microbial adhesion. SEM observations (Fig. 3) reveal that incorporating PANI into the E-rGO/Fe3O4 matrix produces a porous and heterogeneous surface favorable for dense electroactive biofilm formation. Meanwhile, E-rGO serves as a conductive backbone supporting efficient electron collection and transport throughout the electrode structure.

Beyond improved electricity generation, the composite anode enables simultaneous reduction and immobilization of Cr (VI) and Pb (II), demonstrating multifunctional capability. When benchmarked against recent studies targeting concurrent pollutant removal and power production, the system exhibits competitive performance under realistic wastewater conditions80.

Although the maximum power density achieved here is not among the highest reported for optimized laboratory MFCs, it represents a clear improvement over control electrodes and highlights the practical advantages of electrode modification. Comparative benchmarking values are summarized in Table 3.

Overall, polarization and internal resistance analyses confirm that the E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI modified anode enhances electrochemical activity through improved conductivity, optimized composite architecture, and reliable electron transport. These improvements lead to reduced internal resistance and increased power generation without overstating performance relative to broader MFC benchmarks, supporting the suitability of the material for integrated wastewater treatment applications.

Coupled bioenergy generation and Cr (VI)/Pb (II) reduction in MFCs

MFCs represent an environmentally sustainable and energy efficient platform capable of simultaneously treating metal contaminated wastewater while generating bioelectricity through the direct coupling of microbial metabolism with EET to solid electrodes1,30,31.

In this study, the bioremediation performance of DMFCs was assessed for the removal of Cr(VI) and Pb(II) using bare Gr, E-rGO, and E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI modified anodes during a 30 day operation with synthetic wastewater containing these heavy metals, prepared from Cr(NO3)3 and Pb(NO3)2. As summarized in Table 4, incorporation of nanostructured and redox-active components significantly improved both energy recovery and contaminant removal. The E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI anode exhibited superior performance compared with the reference electrodes, highlighting the importance of conductivity enhancement and redox mediation within the Bioelectrochemical interface.

The improved bioenergy generation is attributed to electroactive microbial consortia enriched from wastewater sludge, which catalyze organic substrate oxidation and transfer electrons to the anode. Although microbial community characterization was not conducted in this study, and therefore specific taxa cannot be identified, the observed electrochemical activity can reasonably be attributed to mixed electroactive populations capable of forming anodic biofilms and mediating EET processes25,85.

These microorganisms colonize the anode surface and develop electrochemically active biofilms in which organic substrates derived from sweet potato waste are hydrolyzed into simpler intermediates and subsequently oxidized. This metabolism releases electrons and protons while supporting stable electron transport through multiple mechanisms, including direct cell electrode contact, conductive pili (nanowires), and soluble redox mediators85,86.

The E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI anode provides a highly conductive and large surface area interface that promotes microbial adhesion, enhances biofilm development, reduces charge transfer resistance, and improves electron harvesting efficiency87.

Electrons collected at the anode flow through the external circuit to the cathode, generating electrical current, while protons migrate through the electrolyte to maintain charge balance. At the cathode, dissolved oxygen serves as the terminal electron acceptor and is reduced to water via the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR)25. The governing electrochemical reactions are expressed as:

Conclusion and future recommendations

E-rGO and E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI NC anodes were successfully synthesized from Enset biomass waste, exhibiting enhanced electrical conductivity, favorable surface morphology, and excellent compatibility for biofilm formation in MFC applications. The E-rGO/Fe3O4/PANI electrode achieved a maximum power density of 65 mW m−2 while simultaneously removing Cr (VI) and Pb (II) from synthetic wastewater, demonstrating its dual functionality for energy generation and heavy metal remediation under realistic operating conditions. The improved performance is attributed to the synergistic interplay of E-rGO, Fe3O4, and PANI, which collectively enhance electron transfer, reduce internal resistance, and support stable electroactive biofilm development.

Although microbial community analysis and long term continuous operation were not performed in this study, the results underscore the practical potential of biomass derived NC anodes as a cost effective and environmentally sustainable approach for integrating bioenergy production with wastewater treatment. Future studies should focus on optimizing NC composition and electrode architecture to further improve conductivity and mass transport, performing detailed microbial analyses to elucidate extracellular electron transfer mechanisms, and evaluating long-term performance under real wastewater conditions. Moreover, scale-up experiments and techno-economic assessments are recommended to determine the feasibility of industrial applications. Overall, this work provides a solid foundation for the development of multifunctional MFC electrodes, contributing to sustainable energy recovery and environmental remediation.
