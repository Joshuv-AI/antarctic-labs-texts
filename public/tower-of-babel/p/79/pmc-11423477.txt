Effect of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation on trigeminal‐mediated headshaking in 17 horses
Authors: Vanessa Franzen, Nina Alexandra Gruber, Sven Klußmann, Angelika Schoster, Anna May
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTrigeminal‐mediated headshaking is a neuropathic facial pain condition in horses. No treatment has been entirely successful. Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is used in human medicine as a treatment for various neuropathic pain conditions, and good results have been achieved in cases of trigeminal neuralgia.ObjectivesApply rTMS to horses with trigeminal‐mediated headshaking (TMHS) and to evaluate tolerability, application of the setting, and success rate.AnimalsSeventeen horses with nonseasonal signs of TMHS.MethodsOther underlying causes of headshaking were ruled out. The rTMS was performed under standing sedation on 5 consecutive days applying 3 sets of 500 stimulations each, with a stimulation strength of 5 Hz. Horses were evaluated on Day 1 (t0) and Day 5 (t1) of the treatment and 2 (t2) and 4 weeks (t3) afterwards using a special scoring system.ResultsThe rTMS was well tolerated. Headshaking signs during exercise were decreased by 70% (Day 5; t1). Four weeks after rTMS, signs were still decreased (mean reduction of 50%) during exercise. Improvement of mean resting and exercise scores was significant (P < .05) and effect sizes between pretreatment and all time points after treatment (t1, t2, t3) were large (>±0.8).Conclusions and Clinical ImportanceRepetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation may be a promising treatment for neuropathic pain and headshaking in affected horses. Pain‐free periods after treatment differ individually, and repeated treatment may be necessary. More studies should be performed to determine ideal settings for horses.

INTRODUCTION

Headshaking is a condition in horses characterized by mostly vertical flicking head movements and other clinical signs including snorting, nose rubbing, and increased lip or tongue movements. 1 In a study performed in the United Kingdom, the prevalence of headshaking was 4.6%, 2 whereas classical signs of headshaking severe enough to require veterinary consultation may affect approximately 1% of the equine population. 3 Although headshaking can be caused by various medical or rider‐related issues, many cases are referred to as trigeminal‐mediated headshaking (TMHS), a neuropathic pain condition, that has no obvious underlying cause. 4 , 5 , 6 Trigeminal neuralgia in people has clinical but not pathological parallels to TMHS in horses. 7 Affected humans describe the associated type of pain as stabbing or burning, numbness, and altered sensations. 8 , 9 The trigeminal nerve becomes very sensitive and the threshold for firing is lowered, so mild stimuli such as light, wind, touch, or sound are assumed to trigger intense pain in affected humans or horses, whereas reasons for sensitization of the nerve remain unknown. 10 , 11 In people, neuropathic pain arises from lesions or diseases of the nervous system, but studies failed to find histopathological abnormalities of the trigeminal nerve root in affected horses. 7

The condition is frustrating because usability of the horses is impaired and handling and riding may become dangerous. 12 Welfare can be severely compromised and therefore euthanasia may be the only humane option for horses that do not respond to treatment. 13 Standard analgesics are usually ineffective for neuropathic pain, and despite several therapeutic principles for headshaking, none of the treatments (local, medical, or surgical) have been consistently successful. 14 , 15

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) has been used in human medicine to treat various medical conditions including refractory neuropathic pain or other dysfunctional chronic pain syndromes, and many psychological disorders. 16 , 17 , 18 Promising results for long‐standing pain relief have been achieved when applied at high frequency (5‐20 Hz) over the primary motor cortex, 19 , 20 although analgesic effects have been inconsistent with respect to different settings and parameters. 18 In equine medicine, the technique has been performed as a diagnostic method to localize spinal cord lesions. It involves an apparatus that generates a magnetic field inducing small electric currents in targeted regions of the cortex when the coil is placed on the equine forehead. 21 , 22 Stimulation of the motor cortex modulates pain, but the exact mechanism of action remains unclear. Cortical and subcortical excitability and synaptic plasticity are changed, and structural and functional connections of brain regions associated with pain processing and regulation are affected. 23 Furthermore, rTMS decreases inflammation by decreasing proinflammatory cytokines including interleukin 1β and 6, and tumor necrosis factor α which may contribute to the analgesic effect. 24 , 25 In studies on humans, rTMS lead to analgesic effects in neuropathic pain which were associated with a better quality of life. 18

We aimed to apply rTMS in horses affected by headshaking to evaluate practicability, effect, and duration of pain reduction.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical approval was granted by the Ludwig‐Maximilians‐University's ethics committee, protocol number: 379‐23‐10‐2023.

Animals

Pre‐study group

Four healthy hospital‐owned horses (3 Warmblood mares and 1 Haflinger stallion) were included in the pre‐study evaluation of the tolerability and adverse effects of rTMS method and were stimulated once using the same device settings used in the study group (see Section 2.3).

Study group

Horses diagnosed with severe nonseasonal TMHS in the equine clinic of the Ludwig Maximilians University Munich were eligible for inclusion. Some of the horses (4/17) had been diagnosed earlier (horse no. 10:2015, horse no. 1 and 6:2021, horse no. 4:2023). For these horses, examinations were repeated or completed to make sure all horses had the same protocol. The study was conducted between November 2023 and May 2024, and owners were offered diagnostic testing for a decreased price if their horses were eligible and agreed to take part in the study. Inclusion criteria were nonseasonal headshaking which was clearly visible at admission to the clinic and on Day 0 during lunging, and a duration of clinical signs of >1 year (Figure 1). The horses were examined according to a fixed protocol to rule out headshaking caused by conditions other than primary trigeminal‐mediated pain and only included if all examinations were without clinically relevant findings. The examination protocol consisted of a general examination, exercise test (lunging with an assessment of History Rest and Exercise Scale; see 2.2), neurologic examination, ophthalmic examination in mydriasis (direct and indirect ophthalmoscopy, slit lamp), examination of oral cavity and teeth using a mouth speculum under sedation, ear examination, and otoscopy, endoscopy of upper and lower airways including guttural pouches, computed tomography of the head, lateral radiographs of the neck (caudal occiput‐first thoracic vertebra), and blood examination (CBC and serum biochemistry profile, serum amyloid A concentration). Exclusion criteria were other medical issues (eg, lameness) or medical treatment within the past 6 months.

Headshaking signs during exercise displayed by the horses (n = 17) at the beginning of the study.

Behavioral scoring using the Horse Grimace Scale and Equine Headshaking Scale‐History Rest and Exercise Scale

To grade horses with TMHS and assess disease severity, a score was developed based on history and easily observable clinical signs at rest and during exercise (History Rest and Exercise Scale [HRE‐S]). 26 , 27 The score consists of 3 sub‐scores 1 each for patient history, signs at rest and signs during exercise. The total score for the individual horse is calculated as the average of the 3 sub‐scores. The reliability for HRE‐S was shown to be excellent, regardless of observers' experience. 27 , 28 The pain face, which is assessed as part of the HRE‐S, was evaluated analogously to the Horse Grimace Scale (HGS). 26 , 27 Horses were graded independently by 2 of the authors (VF and AM) using the HRE‐S score at 4 time points (t0 = before treatment, t1 = after 5 days of treatment, t2 = 2 weeks after treatment, t3 = 4 weeks after treatment). The total score and each of the 3 individual sub‐scores were recorded at each time point. Examiners were not blinded to the horses because they had to examine and treat the horses. The difference in total score and for each individual sub‐score between time points was calculated for analysis.

Study design

Analytical, observational cohort design. The horses arrived at least 1 day before the beginning of the study. Headshaking signs were confirmed at rest and during exercise inside the riding arena and outside in the sun. The examination protocol detailed above (see Section 2.1.2) then was performed and horses were included in the study if eligible. Horses then were subjected to rTMS once daily for 5 days.

For the rTMS, horses were sedated using detomidine (0.01 mg/kg; Cepesedan, CP‐Pharma Handelsgesellschaft mbH, D‐31303 Burgdorf) and butorphanol (0.02 mg/kg; Alvegesic, Dechra Veterinary Products Deutschland GmbH, D‐88326 Aulendorf) according to the protocol used for diagnostic use of the technique. 21 Horses were put in a stall and stimulated using 5 Hz and 3 sets of 500 impulses each (1500 impulses total per day; Figure 2). A Nicolet Viking device (Natus Neurology Inc, Middleton, WI) connected to a magnetic circular coil (MagPro Compact MMC‐140‐II, MagVenture, Farum, Denmark) was used to treat the horses. Earlier studies 22 showed that the mean cortical motor threshold (CMT) for Warmblood horses was 34.7 ± 5.64 (mean ± SD), so the stimulation strength was set on 40 for all horses in our study. 29 The magnetic circular coil was placed in the median of the horse's forehead where 2 imaginary lines running from the lateral ear to the medial corner of the eye meet.

Positioning of the coil for rTMS on the horses' forehead as described for diagnostic use of transcranial magnetic stimulation. 21

During the 5‐day treatment in the hospital, clinical signs were evaluated daily at rest and by lunging the horses for 20 minutes in all gaits in the arena using a standardized protocol, and headshaking signs were scored by using a scale developed previously 27 (see Section 2.2). Lunging was performed before treatment to evaluate the effect of the treatment on the previous day. The most frequently presented behavior in a category was scored (eg, if a horse showed headshaking 3 times per round most frequently, a score of 3 was chosen; if the horse showed headshaking once per round most frequently, a score of 1 was given, even if the horse may have shaken 3 times per round in 1 round).

Two and 4 weeks after the treatment the horses' headshaking behavior again was assessed at rest and while lunging for 20 minutes in all gaits in the home stable environment—either by the authors or, when the horses were living too far away, by video sequences recorded by the owners (5/17 horses). Owners were interviewed regarding the pre‐ and post‐treatment use of the horses.

Statistical Analysis

All data analyses were performed using SPSS Version 19.0 statistic software package (IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac, Version 19.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp) with significance set at P < .05.

Results were expressed as mean ± SD and range. The distribution of response variables was visually examined and a linear model or a mixed‐effect linear model was applied. Clinical outcomes were compared using repeated‐measures analysis of variance. Effect sizes (ES) were reported to measure the degree of linear relationship between 2 quantitative variables (percentage of HRE‐S at different time points) with ESs defined as small (±0.2), medium (±0.5), and large (±0.8).

Because of the limited sample size and potential overfitting and multicollinearity, no multivariable analysis was conducted.

RESULTS

Animals—Study group

Eleven warmblood horses and 6 other breeds (1 Haflinger, 1 Appaloosa, 1 Thoroughbred, 1 Standardbred, 1 Friesian Pinto Mix and 1 Irish Cob) were included in the study. The study group consisted of 8 mares and 9 geldings (median age, 11 years; interquartile range [IQR], 10).

Tolerability of the method

Pre‐study group

All 4 horses tolerated the procedure well and did not show any adverse effects during or after treatment.

Study group

Horses of the study group also tolerated the procedure well. Three of 7 (18%) horses had adverse reactions evidenced by trying to go backwards and pulling the head away from the device in the beginning, which resolved after approximately 50 stimulations during the first session. All horses showed decreased reactions to the stimuli over the course of the treatment period resulting in a decreased dose of sedation, and no horse had to be excluded from the study because the procedure could not be executed. Nine of 17 horses (53%) subjectively seemed to experience pain relief and relaxation during the treatment because they lowered their head and neck and pressed their heads against the coil, a reaction that had not been discovered in any of the healthy horses the technique had been tried on before.

Effect on total HRE‐S and each individual sub‐score

Ten of 17 horses (59%) showed a marked pain face at rest when they arrived in the hospital and pretreatment (time point t0). Only 1/17 (6%) horse showed a pain face at rest after 5 days of treatment. Fifteen of 17 horses (88%) showed a marked pain face during lunging pretreatment (t0) whereas only 2/17 (12%) horses had a pain face after 5 days of treatment (t1).

Vertical headshaking was the clinical sign displayed by most of the horses: at t0 10/17 (59%) showed vertical shaking at rest and 17/17 (100%) showed headshaking during exercise. After 5 days of treatment, vertical headshaking only was displayed by 1/17 horses (6%) at rest and by 13/17 horses (76%) during exercise, but less frequently. The horses were given points according to the extent of vertical headshaking (0‐4). All horses showed lower numbers of points after treatment (Figure 3). 27

Change in the most frequently observed parameter “vertical headshaking” during exercise before (t0) after 5 days of rTMS treatment (t1) and 2 weeks (t2) and 3 weeks (t3) after treatment in 17 horses (100%). The horses were given points according to the score developed by Kloock et al. 27 : 0 points = no headshaking, 1 point = <1/round, 2 points = 1–3/round, 3 points = >3/round, 4 points = at walk. 27

Whereas 16/17 horses (94%) had improved clinically at rest, 1 horse that had not been showing any signs at rest at t0 developed vertical headshaking signs and increased lip and tongue movements on Day 5 of treatment (horse no. 7). Interestingly, the exercise scoring of that horse improved compared with pretreatment. Two horses (horses no. 1 and no. 2) that had not been ridden in years and had been retired because of severe headshaking signs improved markedly and were able to be ridden and competed at show jumping competitions. After rTMS, the owners reported these horses to be free of clinical signs for >5 and >7 months, respectively.

The mean total rest and exercise sub‐scores decreased significantly compared with pretreatment values (Table 1). Clinical signs improved in all horses (mean exercise score was decreased by 70% after 5 days) but partly returned between 2 (t2) and 4 weeks (t3) after treatment in 15/17 horses (t2 mean exercise score 49% of the initial mean exercise score and t3 mean exercise score 54% of the initial mean exercise score, respectively). All differences to the mean scores (total, rest, and exercise) before treatment (t0) were significant (P < .05). ES were large between mean resting score t0 (pretreatment) and all mean resting scores after treatment (t1 ES = −1.18, t2 ES = −0.88, t3 ES = −0.94). The same applied to the mean exercise score t0 (pretreatment) and all mean exercise scores after rTMS (t1 ES = −1.51, t2 ES = −1.07, t3 ES = −0.98), indicating a strong clinical relevance (Figure 4).

Between t1 and t2, 14/17 owners were satisfied with the treatment and reported improved rideability and markedly decreased clinical signs. Although the horses still showed milder clinical signs at t3, some owners were disappointed because the horses were not rideable like a healthy horse according to their expectations, which were influenced by the improvement observed immediately after treatment.

DISCUSSION

We aimed to investigate a new treatment for TMHS, a neuropathic pain disorder in horses. The study showed that rTMS was well tolerated by sedated horses and significant improvements in headshaking were observed immediately after treatment and lasted for up to 4 weeks.

Recurrent trigeminal neuralgia which is refractory to treatment causes severe chronic pain in affected human patients. Therefore, neuromodulative approaches such as rTMS have been used in human medicine to address neuropathic pain. 30 , 31 Repetitive TMS is a noninvasive method delivering repetitive pulses that create currents and a magnetic field that passes through the skull and stimulates underlying brain tissue leading to a slow increase in cortical excitability. 32 , 33 The induced currents may help to normalize activity in the stimulated region. Repetitive TMS yielded promising results in various studies for neuropathic pain 34 , 35 , 36 and has been shown to significantly decrease pain intensity scores in trigeminal neuralgia. 37 Reduction of pain is immediate and may be lasting because of changes in cortical excitability induced by the magnetic field and initiated electrical currents. 32 In human medicine, high frequency rTMS using stimulus strength of 5 to 20 Hz has been shown to have the best analgesic effect. 38 Duration of analgesia has been variable with studies on single stimulations inducing an effect for up to 1 week 38 , 39 and a prolonged effect when patients received repeated daily stimulations over a period of several weeks. 19 , 39 Based on these findings, an applicable and tolerable setting for owners and affected horses was applied in our study. Because multiple sessions have been shown to result in a more longstanding effect, treatment was extended over 5 days. 40 A long‐lasting analgesic effect was reported in human patients with refractory trigeminal pain after 5 consecutive days of treatment. 41 In studies in human medicine so far, there is no consensus on a consistent treatment regimen and a lack of knowledge regarding the appropriate pain‐relieving stimulation strength. 40

Because the stimulus rate of 5 Hz used in our study is at the lower limit of the strength described in previous studies, failure to provide sufficient analgesia in the horses could be explained by the lower stimulus rate. More repetitive pulses also may lead to a better and more longstanding effect in horses with TMHS. Whereas repeated treatment improved the analgesic effect in human patients with chronic facial pain, a protocol using a higher frequency (>10 Hz), a longer duration of the treatment session (>20 minutes) and a higher number of pulses (>2400 pulses/session) did not improve outcome. 42

In a study of human patients treated with rTMS, patients had greater improvement in pain scores than the control group, with an average pain reduction of 35% to 40% 2 weeks after the session. 18 This finding is in agreement with the results achieved in our study because horses showed significant improvement in the mean exercise score (HRE‐S) of approximately 70% after 5 days and of 49% and 53% after 2 and 4 weeks, respectively. Most of the owners participating in the study were still not satisfied because horses were not good enough to be ridden like normal horses. In humans, the settings of the technique vary according to the different studies, and the duration of pain reduction was also different in individuals. 18 These findings were also similar to what we discovered in our study group because some horses did not show much pain reduction whereas others (horses no. 1 and 2) remained free of clinical signs for several weeks and months.

In human medicine, rTMS is usually well tolerated with minimal adverse effects, such as scalp discomfort, facial muscle twitching, mild headache, or a feeling of light‐headedness. The procedure also has a small risk of inducing seizures. 43 We did not observe any adverse effects during our study. Horses subjected to the treatment showed few adverse reactions in the beginning (eg, withdrawal of the head), but rather tended to press their heads against the coil after a few stimulations which gave the subjective impression that rTMS was pleasant for them. The horses also became more relaxed during the course of the study. Adverse reactions in our study could be a consequence of the clicking noise of the machine next to the horses' ears, or as a result of the facial muscle contractions that were unfamiliar to the horses. As many as 40% of human patients experience pain or discomfort during or after rTMS. 44 In human medicine, pain related to cortical TMS is most likely caused by stimulation of the facial and trigeminal nerves but can also be caused by contractions of facial muscles or nociceptor activation in the bone around the coil. 45 These observations differed from the assessment of pain between treatments for the horses used in our study. Whereas some of the horses showed pain faces before initiation of treatment even during times when headshaking was not observable, no pain face was displayed between or after rTMS sessions in any of the horses.

Repetitive TMS as a treatment method has only been applied on a few occasions in veterinary medicine. Studies in a psychopathological animal model using rats showed that 3‐day series treatment using rTMS had behavioral and neuroendocrine effects in the frontal brain regions and that rTMS effects were comparable to antidepressant drug treatment. 46 Repetitive TMS has been used in dogs to treat drug‐resistant idiopathic epilepsy. 47 The seizure frequency decreased significantly in the treatment group and the overall effect of rTMS lasted for 4 months. 47 Considering that headshaking is a seasonal disease in most horses, a lasting effect of 4 months duration could be regarded as a successful treatment. Another study on anxious aggressive dogs showed that single‐day treatments with rTMS decreased anxious and aggressive behavior in affected dogs, 48 confirming the interaction between the frontal cortex and subcortical brain regions in anxiety disorders of dogs.

In humans, the CMT, considered as the stimulus intensity that causes a minimum motor response visible as an action potential, is determined for the therapeutic use of magnetic stimulation. This value is then used as the dose for stimulation intensity in rTMS to make the treatment as pleasant as possible. 49 In horses, the CMT has been assessed in studies using TMS for diagnostic purposes. 50 Based on these findings, the CMT was set to 40 for all horses included in our study. 22 , 29

The headshaking score developed previously 27 had its limitations when improvement of clinical signs had to be reported. For example, no distinction was made between the frequency of lip and tongue movements (no = 0, yes = 2), and thus an improvement in frequency cannot be expressed. 27 A decrease in frequency of occurrence of the clinical signs named “further symptoms” cannot be expressed either, because 1 = increased snorting, 2 = rubbing the nose, 3 = striking the forelimb at the nose, 4 = stopping and running backward. If, for example, a horse rubbed its nose constantly at the beginning of the treatment, but only sporadically after treatment, the improvement did not become clear by using the previously developed score. 27 Also, horses had to be scored Grade 4 when only minor signs were visible during walking and so improvement during trotting sometimes was not reflected in a better score either. We tried to decrease the influence of individually occurring minor signs by observing the horses over a longer time period and assessing the predominant clinical sign. All horses got better clinically, and 9 of 17 horses showed only very subtle signs after 5 days of treatment in the hospital. Another weakness of the score was the impossibility of classifying the pain face as mild, moderate, or severe because the HRE‐S score only allows classification as yes or no. Improvement in the situation for the headshakers therefore only can be evaluated very imprecisely.

One limitation of our study was the season for which the treatment was started in some of the horses. Headshaking is often seasonal and predominantly occurs in the warm months (spring and summer) in approximately 60% of affected horses. 51 Therefore, our choice to treat some of the horses during winter may not have been optimal. To rule out the possible effect of seasonality, all horses included in the study had been showing clinical signs consistently over at least 1 year and had been experiencing severe headshaking signs when rTMS treatment was initiated. On the one hand, this may decrease the chance of successful treatment, but it prevents seasonality from having an influence on the study results.

Another limitation of the study was the fact that horses was sedated using a combination of detomidine and butorphanol so they could tolerate the procedure. Butorphanol is a potent analgesic in horses and may have influenced neuropathic pain. 52 Because butorphanol and alpha‐2 agonists were used every day throughout the treatment period of the study, their administration may have influenced pain in the horses during the initial 5 days in the hospital. Duration of effect for butorphanol and detomidine has been described as being up to 4 hours 53 and up to 3 hours, 54 respectively, and thus should not have influenced the clinical signs because lunging was performed in the morning before treatment. In studies on humans, the opioid morphine has been shown to have limited efficacy on neuropathic pain. 55 , 56 Therefore, it can be assumed that the results of the control examinations were not affected by the administration of the sedative medication during the rTMS treatment.

Furthermore, 5/17 horses could not be re‐examined in person after 2 and 4 weeks because their home stables were too far away, and thus video recordings had to be used, which could have led to falsification of data. To limit the impact of this weakness, several video recordings at rest and exercise were requested of the owners and evaluated.

The use of a placebo group could have made results more objective but was not implemented for welfare reasons because horses showed severe headshaking as described previously. 3 Moreover, owners of affected horses were not compliant with hospitalization for 1 week without any treatment.

Another limitation of our study was the lack of randomization of the horses so that the behavior scorers were not blinded to the horses and their treatment conditions. We tried to compensate for this limitation by using an objective score (HRE‐S) and choosing a longer observation period over randomized short video sequences.

The treatment of headshaking with rTMS could be an alternative to applied percutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (PENS), to which approximately 50% of horses respond after the initial treatment, and the median duration of remission was reported to be 9.5 weeks. 57 Because treatment of headshaking in horses appears to be very variable and individual, horses that do not respond to other treatments may benefit from rTMS. Some of the horses in our study had been examined earlier and did not respond to PENS, which could have caused bias.

Moreover, in human medicine, rTMS is applied at different intervals, so that repeated application also may be helpful for horses.

In conclusion, rTMS is a promising, safe, and well‐tolerated method to modulate TMHS in horses. To determine whether the lack of long‐term efficacy is associated with low stimulation strength, number of pulses used per session or number of treatment days, more studies using different settings should be performed.
