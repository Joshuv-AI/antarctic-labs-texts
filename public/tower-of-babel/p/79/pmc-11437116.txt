Comprehensive input models and machine learning methods to improve permeability prediction
Authors: Mohammad Ali Davari, Ali Kadkhodaie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the use of machine learning techniques and the proper selection of input data to estimate permeability in geosciences, using six types of input logs: gamma ray (GR), resistivity (RT), effective porosity (PHIE), density (RHO), sonic (DT), and compensated neutron porosity (NPHI). A total of 57 models were constructed using combinations of these logs and tested using five machine learning methods: Extreme Learning Machine (ELM), Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting (GB), K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN), and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP). This approach produced 285 unique permeability predictions. RF had the highest correlation coefficient (0.925) and average error (0.196), indicating a precision-correlation trade-off. The ELM approach had the lowest average error, 0.083, and a correlation value of 0.871. Testing on a blind well revealed that the GB and RF approaches were highly effective in predicting permeability, with R² values of 0.92 and 0.90, respectively, even in untested settings. The findings emphasize the need of using appropriate machine learning algorithms and input data to improve model accuracy and reliability.

Introduction

Permeability is one of the most critical reservoir characteristics, and its prediction remains a fundamental challenge for both researchers and petroleum engineers. The complexity of predicting permeability arises from numerous variables, including depositional formations, lithologic mineral components, measuring instruments, data quality, and computational approaches. These factors collectively contribute to the intricacies that hinder accurate predictions.

Artificial neural networks have emerged as a valuable alternative in cases where traditional mathematical models fall short. With sufficient data for network training and learning, artificial neural networks demonstrate the capability to accurately approximate any nonlinear function1. In the realm of petroleum engineering, these networks have exhibited success across various applications2. Particularly in scenarios where core data are unavailable, neural network models have proven effective as predictors or estimators of related geophysical parameters such as porosity and permeability3–6.

The two most commonly employed learning methods in neural networks for estimating porosity and permeability are backpropagation (BP) networks and support vector machines (SVMs). A BP neural network is a conventional fully connected neural network comprising forward and error backpropagation components. To address nonlinear challenges, errors are backpropagated by adjusting the weights in the learning process until convergence to the desired value is achieved7. Another notable network, the SVM, is rooted in statistical learning theory and is designed specifically for addressing classification issues using various convolution kernel functions8.

Reservoir characterisation and permeability prediction have used a variety of approaches and machine learning (ML) techniques to improve accuracy and dependability, especially in heterogeneous formations. K-means clustering, the Kozeny-Carman (KyC) equation, and the K-nearest neighbor Density Estimate (KNN) technique have all been used to estimate permeability. These approaches were effective in identifying flow units and produced promising results with lower uncertainty levels9. The application of KNN to categorize rock types and estimate irreducible water saturation (Swi) in carbonate reservoirs demonstrates KNN’s superior performance over Random Forest and SVM. Integrating the traditional wireline and core analysis data resulted in a new workflow for petrophysical rock typing (PRT) without incurring additional data costs10.

The extreme learning machine (ELM), introduced as a generalized model, proves to be effective and processes data more rapidly than vector networks. The ELM method for training single layer feedforward networks (SLFNs) involves the random generation of hidden layer weights, followed by estimating the output layer weights through the least squares solution of a linear system of equations. This form of learning is not only swift but also yields accurate predictions7,11.

In numerous cases, the ELM approach demonstrates high generalization performance both theoretically and practically, showing significant speed advantages compared to other well-known learning algorithms for feedforward neural networks. Researchers, thus far, have extensively investigated and acknowledged the ELM for its robust generalization and prediction performance across various practical applications, such as predicting permeability from well log data7,12,13.

Several techniques paired with a gradient boosting regressor model have been used to predict permeability. The integration of these techniques has demonstrated not only low prediction errors but also an appreciable improvement in computational efficiency14.

The multilayer perceptron (MLP) has been frequently employed to address function estimation problems within the realm of artificial neural networks (ANNs). Integrating the MLP method with other techniques has been proven to yield superior results and minimize errors in permeability estimation. By combining the strengths of MLPs with complementary methods, researchers aim to enhance the overall performance of the model, achieving more accurate permeability estimates. This collaborative approach reflects the versatility of the MLP and its ability to synergize with other methods for improved outcomes in function estimation, particularly in the context of permeability prediction15,16.

Recent advances in estimating permeability and porosity in subsurface porous media and reservoirs have incorporated a variety of machine learning (ML) techniques to improve reliability and accuracy. Key parameters such as porosity, tortuosity, fractal dimension, and average pore diameter have been analyzed17,18 using a variety of machine learning methods, including support vector machines, artificial neural networks, decision trees, random forests, gradient-boosting machines, Bayesian ridge regression, genetic algorithms, fuzzy decision trees, the imperialist competitive algorithm (ICA), particle swarm optimization (PSO), and hybrid approaches17,19. Notably, approaches such as the artificial neural network with hyperparameter tuning and LightGBM have demonstrated higher performance, especially in difficult situations such as heterogeneous carbonate reserves20. These studies have also shown the value of including geology parameters, such as clay concentration and oil saturation, into predictive models20. The combination of these several ML approaches gives a solid framework for developing more accurate static reservoir models, which will ultimately aid in resource extraction efficiency17,19,21.

Despite advances in artificial intelligence methods, the impact of input data on results has not been adequately investigated. This study looks at how different machine learning approaches interact with conventional logs data and make predictions. By employing six types of input logs and creating 57 models with permutations ranging from 2 to 6 logs, the study assesses the influence of input factors on permeability predictions using five separate methods: extreme learning machine (ELM), random forest (RF), gradient boosting (GB), K-nearest neighbor (KNN), and multilayer perceptron (MLP). The study includes calculating variable importance scores to determine the significance of each log and running statistical tests to compare technique performance. The goal is to improve our understanding of how input data influences permeability estimates and to guide technique selection based on accuracy and correlation trade-offs. By optimizing both the machine learning approach and the input data, accurate and adaptive permeability estimations may be obtained, making them suitable to a wide range of wells.

Geological setting

The studied field is situated in the northwestern part of the Persian Gulf on the continental plateau, extending partly under Kharg Island and into the waters of the Persian Gulf. The geological structure of the field is characterized by an elongated shape with a north‒northeast and south‒southwest trend. This elongate anticline, originating from both the Zagros and Arab orogenies, plunges toward the NNE22. The elongated shape of the field is a result of two folding events during the Cretaceous and late Miocene. The first structural event, occurring in the late Cretaceous, impacted formations such as the Ilam, Mauddud, Kazhdumi, Dariyan, Fahliyan, and the deeper Hith formations. This event resulted in a NNE-SSW-oriented fold, similar to the Kazerun Fault23. The second structural event, the Zagros Orogeny in the late Oligocene-Pliocene period, led to the formation of the great Zagros Ranges in southwest Iran and contributed to the elevation of the field23.

The primary pay zone in this field is identified in the Lower Cretaceous Fahliyan Formation (Berriasian-Valanginian), which is equivalent to the Yamama and Manifa Formations. The main reservoir lithology is limestone, and the top seal is a micritic limestone from the lower Gadvan Formation24. The reservoir depth varies from 3200 m to approximately 3700 m subsea. The geological lithology of the studied field is shown in Fig. 1. The Fahliyan Formation in this field is characterized by high fracturing and faulting, a common feature in carbonate reservoirs. The presence of fractures in reservoirs leads to a reduction in permeability. In wells intersecting major faults, water breakthrough is frequently observed. Faults act as conduits for fluid communication, especially when the throws are significant enough to contact the Manifa and Yamama reservoirs. While most faults may have a local sealing capacity effect, accurate identification of fault and fracture distributions is crucial for future infill well placement, considering their impact on field production and behavior.

Theory and methodology

Petrophysical studies aim to evaluate and estimate reservoir characteristics, including permeability, in situations where direct measurements are challenging, such as the absence of cores or the presence of fractures in the cores. The difficulty in achieving permeability arises from the lack of established logging tools for direct permeability assessment25. In cases where mathematical models are not applicable, smart networks come into play.

Extreme learning machine method (ELM)

The Extreme Learning Machine (ELM) is a single-hidden-layer feedforward neural network that attempts to increase generalization performance and significantly decrease training time when compared to regular neural networks11,26. Unlike BP-based neural networks, which rely on the iterative back-propagation technique to alter weights over numerous epochs, the ELM randomly picks the input weights and produces hidden layer outputs before any learning occurs13,27. This method avoids the requirement for back-propagation and instead uses a direct analytical solution to generate the output weights, usually using least squares fitting. In an ELM, the weights between the input and hidden layers showed in Fig. 2, as well as the biases, are assigned randomly and kept constant, allowing the network to avoid the iterative weight adjustment procedure which is typical of back-propagation. The ELM may use a number of activation functions, allowing it to effectively handle both linear and nonlinear data12. The ELM’s randomized assignment of input weights and simplified structure, without the iterative weight updates of back-propagation, result in faster learning and efficient processing of different activation functions13.

Random forest (RF) method

Random forests are a collection of tree predictors where each tree relies on values from a randomly sampled vector, ensuring diversity across trees. With an increasing number of trees, the generalization error of random forests converges to a limit. Their efficacy hinges on both individual tree strength and their correlation. Random forests employ a random selection of features for node splitting, yielding error rates comparable to those of AdaBoost but with greater robustness to noise28. The algorithm internally tracks error, strength, and correlation to optimize the splitting process and measure variable importance29. Random forests, which are widely recognized for regression and classification tasks, are utilized for predicting carbonate rock permeability based on pore structure parameters. This ensemble method, rooted in decision tree classification, aggregates predictions from multiple trees, with key parameters being the number of trees and features chosen at each split. Optimizing these parameters is essential for achieving peak model performance, with each regression tree trained on bags sampled from the dataset30,31.

Gradient boosting (GB) method

Gradient boosting (GB) is a model ensemble that consists of a series of tree models arranged sequentially. In this ensemble, each subsequent model learns from the errors of the previous model. This machine learning approach employs boosting, which combines weak prediction models, often in the form of decision trees, to construct a more robust and accurate model14,29,32. It may evaluate performance using a variety of cost/loss functions, including mean squared error. K-nearest neighbor (KNN) method

The K-nearest neighbor (KNN) algorithm, employed as a classification method, predicts the labels of the test category based on the query and training set. In this process, KNN identifies k training samples that are the nearest neighbors of the query from the training set33. It only relies on a sufficient representative dataset to describe the relationship between inputs and outputs based on past data34. The algorithm utilizes various distance functions to compute classes, with the specific type of KNN determining the choice of distance function. Multiple layer perceptron (MLP) method

An artificial neural network is a type of machine learning algorithm that mimics the human nervous system to solve problems. The specific type of artificial neural network employed is the multiple layer perceptron regressor (MLP regressor). The structure of an MLP regressor includes at least three layers: the input layer, the hidden layer, and the output layer. The training process of an MLP regressor involves adjusting the weight factors. Initially, the model starts with guess values for the weight factors, and the predictions are compared with the target values. If the margin of error is not small enough, the model undergoes backward processes to iteratively adjust the weight factors until the differences between the predictions and training targets meet the specified error tolerance36.

Data and metrics

Data preparation

In the studied field, after collecting the required data, the information related to the logs of wells A and B was collected. The data were prepared in four stages: data loading, quality control and data editing, calculation of the required parameters before the main calculations and environmental corrections, and construction of the petrophysical model.

The primary goal of this research is to introduce diverse models that investigate various input data configurations. This study utilizes six main logs: gamma ray (GR), resistivity (RT), effective porosity (PHIE), density log (RHOB), sonic log (DT), and compensated neutron porosity (NPHI) logs. These logs, along with core data, serve as inputs for multiple machine learning methods, including the extreme learning machine (ELM), random forest regressor (RF), gradient boosting regressor (GB), support vector regression (SVR), and multilayer perceptron regressor (MLP). The six well logs were selected based on their common usage and significance in reservoir characterization and permeability prediction. Each of these logs provides essential information about different properties of the formation, contributing to a comprehensive dataset for machine learning models.

The objective is to achieve optimal permeability estimation by using the information provided by logs and core data. Each model, based on a specific machine learning method, is designed to accurately predict the formation permeability. The variety of models allows for a comprehensive exploration of different approaches to permeability estimation, considering the strengths and characteristics of each machine learning method.

The use of diverse models and input data configurations enhances the robustness of the study, providing a thorough analysis of permeability prediction in the studied field. This research aims to contribute valuable insights into the effectiveness of different machine learning approaches for permeability estimation, paving the way for improved reservoir characterization and management.

Each well in our study has a different number of data points. Specifically, Well A contains 86 data points of each desired well logs, and Well B contains 301 data points of each desired well logs, making a total of 387 data points across both wells. A summary of the input data is shown in Table 1.while Fig. 3 shows the linear relationships between the variables as well as their distributions.

In this study, Well C was utilized as a blind well to evaluate the effectiveness of the machine learning models. The data from Well C were not included in the training process of the models, ensuring that the models’ performance could be independently assessed. By using Well C as a blind well, the robustness and generalization capabilities of the models were tested, providing an unbiased evaluation of their predictive power. This approach helps in understanding the real-world applicability of the developed models and highlights their potential in accurately predicting permeability in unseen datasets.

Data processing and performance metrics

Preprocessing is a crucial step in preparing data for training a neural network. Special functions were applied to both the inputs and outputs before training to enhance the efficiency of the network. The steps involved in this preprocessing include scaling the input and output data within a specific range, typically between 0 and 1. One reason for normalizing the data in this range is the behavior of activation functions, such as the sigmoid function, which may struggle to distinguish very large values. Normalizing the data helps address this issue and improves the training process. The process involves applying normalization functions before training and reverting them after the simulation. The dataset was ultimately divided into three subsets: the training subset, the testing subset, and the validating subset. The distribution was 70% for training data, 20% for testing, and the remaining 10% for network validation. The choice of allocating a high percentage (70%) of training data is motivated by allowing the network to learn and adapt to the underlying patterns governing the input and outputs across various conditions.

The division was done randomly across all data (depth) points to ensure that each set contained a representative sample of the entire data range. This randomization helps in preventing any biases that could arise from sequential or depth-based ordering of the data.

During training, the network is exposed to the training data to adjust its parameters. Simultaneously, it is tested using the validation data to evaluate its performance and ensure that it generalizes well to new, unseen data. In the next step, various models were considered using six input logs, exploring each mode where these logs could be used as inputs together. This resulted in 57 different models representing permutations of two, three, four, five, and six input logs. These models were then employed to assess the degree of correlation and the impact of each input log on the permeability estimation. The goal was to obtain the best permeability estimation. The following five methods were used for all 57 models: extreme learning machine (ELM), random forest (RF), gradient boosting (GBR), K-nearest neighbor (KNN), and multilayer perceptron (MLP). The results from these models were recorded and analyzed to determine the effectiveness of each combination of input logs and regression algorithms for permeability estimation.

The next step involves comparing the 57 models to assess their performance. To effectively evaluate the proposed framework and conduct comparative studies with other models, common statistical qualitative measures are employed. Two key metrics used for this evaluation are the correlation coefficient (R2) and the root mean square error (RMSE).

Conclusions

In this study, first, the conventional permeability–porosity model related to microscopic rock parameters is used to evaluate reservoir permeability. Moreover, five machine learning methods, namely, the extreme learning machine (ELM), random forest (RF), gradient boosting (GB), K-nearest neighbor (KNN), and multilayer perceptron (MLP) methods, are applied to the core permeability and well logs to determine reliable permeability prediction models. 57 models were constructed, each consisting of permutations of 2 to 6 input logs, which led to 285 permeability predictions. The investigation included an in-depth examination of the relative importance scores of each variable for all methods, as well as the statistical results of the average R2 and RMSE.

The investigation of variable importance scores revealed considerable differences in how distinct input logs affect permeability estimation using different approaches. A constant finding was the significant influence of the RT variable, which was crucial in all models. This emphasizes the importance of taking into account method-specific characteristics when using input data to make accurate permeability estimates.The Random Forest (RF) approach showed great prediction capabilities, with the highest average correlation coefficient of 0.925 and the low average error of 0.196, showing accuracy and reliability. The Extreme Learning Machine (ELM) also performed well, with a lowest average error of 0.08 and an average correlation value of 0.879, and the Gradient Boosting approach outperformed other models with a R² of 0.842 and an RMSE of 0.282.Well C was used as a blind well to test the models’ efficacy. The Gradient Boosting (GB) and Random Forest (RF) approaches were highly effective, with R² values of 0.92 and 0.90, respectively. These results demonstrate the models’ accuracy and robustness in forecasting permeability even under untested situations.ELM performed well in training wells A and B, but struggled in the blind well (Well C) with a R² of 0.73. K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) models had even lower R² values of 0.34 and 0.06, respectively. some differences indicate that some models may not generalize well to new datasets, possibly due to overfitting or constraints in the input data.The variability in performance across models and wells could be attributed to several factors, including the limited amount of data from Well C, the dispersion of core permeability data, and the reliance on conventional logs.
