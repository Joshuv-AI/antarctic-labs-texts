Cystic fibrosis year in review 2023
Authors: David V Swetland, Adrienne P Savant
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis past year, there were many important advances for patients with cystic fibrosis (CF). Of the many publications related to CF in 2023, there was further evaluation of highly effective modulator therapy, new assessments and guidelines for clinical manifestations and therapies for CF, advances in newborn screening and diagnosis, and evaluation of outcomes for people with CF transmembrane conductance regulator‐related metabolic syndrome/CF screen positive, inconclusive diagnosis. The aim of this review article is not to provide a full assessment of the wide range of articles published in 2023, but to provide a brief review of publication that may lead to changes in clinical care.Keywords: CFTR modulators, cystic fibrosis, hepatobiliary, newborn screening, pulmonary exacerbations

INTRODUCTION

The cystic fibrosis (CF) community has published many studies across a wide range of topics in 2023. This review will not be exhaustive of all publications related to CF this past year but will focus on clinical topics. Specifically, clinically relevant manuscripts that might lead to changes in clinical care were included in several key areas: elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (ETI); pulmonary, hepatobiliary, and infectious manifestations; newborn screening (NBS) and diagnosis; and CFTR (CF transmembrane regulatory)‐related metabolic syndrome/CF screen positive, inconclusive diagnosis (CRMS/CFSPID). The aim of this review is not to provide a full assessment of the included references, but to provide enough information for the busy clinician to garner a quick review of publications. If desired, further detailed information can be found through evaluation of the full manuscripts.

ETI

New and exciting data continues to be published related to CFTR conductance channel modulators as we move further from approval to long‐term use. This section will review the key large clinical and observational trials; followed by evaluations of specific subgroups, clinical outcomes, and side effects; and end with additional information on changes in clinical care delivery for those on modulators and additional eligibility methodology.

Key clinical and observational trials

In 2023, the US Food and Drug Administration approved ETI for 2–5 years olds with CF. In Part B of the phase 3 trial, 75 participants were treated with open label ETI for 24 weeks; there was no placebo comparator group, which is routine for younger age studies as the main goal is safety and tolerability in the younger ages and less so efficacy. 1 The primary endpoints demonstrated safety and tolerability consistent with previous trials and, compared to baseline, secondary endpoints demonstrated: decreased sweat chloride (SC) (mean −57.9 mmol/L; 95% confidence interval [CI], −61.3 to −54.6), improved lung clearance index (LCI2.5) (mean −0.83 U; 95% CI, −1.01 to −0.66), stable nutritional parameters (absolute body mass index [BMI] and weight‐, height‐, and BMI‐for‐age z scores) and mean rate of pulmonary exacerbation (PEx) of 0.32 per year. There were some interesting differences compared to prior studies in older people with CF (PwCF): fecal elastase rose above 200 µg/g in six individuals during treatment and SC was more likely to decrease to <30 mmol/L in those with homozygous F508del mutations (64% vs. 13%). For children under age 6, ETI is available as granule packets, however if unable to tolerate pills, one published case report demonstrated effectiveness with crushing the tablets, which is specifically not advised in manufacturer packaging in the United States and Europe. 2

To assess continued safety and tolerability of ETI in PwCF aged 6–11 years, the phase 3 study was continued as an open‐label extension study. Initial evaluation after 96 weeks of treatment for 61 participants showed that ETI continues to be generally safe and well tolerated in this group. Interestingly, exposure‐adjusted rates of adverse events decreased compared to the phase 3 randomized, double‐blind study (407.74 and 987.04 events per 100 participant‐years, respectively). 3 Although the types of adverse events were similar to prior evaluations, one newly noted severe adverse event of idiopathic intracranial hypertension with papilledema (starting on Day 1 of ETI therapy, with normal vitamin A levels) was reported, requiring brief discontinuation of ETI and treatment with acetazolamide. 3 A case series of three PwCF with idiopathic increased intracranial pressure within the first 6 months of starting ETI was also reported, with concurrent elevations in vitamin A after starting ETI. 4

Although the goal of the open label extension of ETI in 6–11 year olds was safety, efficacy evaluation was also undertaken. 3 Secondary endpoints of efficacy showed maintained improvement from the original double‐blind placebo controlled study with improvements compared to baseline in mean forced expiratory volume in 1 s percent predicted (FEV1pp) (11.2 percentage points; 95% CI, 8.3–14.2), LCI2.5 (22.00 U; 95% CI, 22.45–21.55), SC (262.3 mmol/L; 95% CI, 265.9–258.8), anthropomorphic measurements (BMI‐for‐age z score from baseline, 0.24, and weight‐for‐age z score from baseline 0.24), and CF Questionnaire‐Revised (CFQ‐R) respiratory domain scores (13.3 points [95% CI, 11.4–15.1]). PEx rates were estimated at 0.04 per 48 weeks.

For older (≥12 years of age) PwCF, studies have examined continued outcomes for ETI. An open‐label, extension study evaluating long‐term safety and efficacy in 506 enrolled PwCF taking ETI presented interim analysis at the 144‐week mark, with primary endpoints showing lower exposure adjusted rates of adverse events (586.55 events per 100 participant‐years) and serious adverse events (22.42 events per 100 participant‐years) compared to the initial 24‐week trial (1096.01 and 36.93 events per 100 participant‐years, respectively). 5 Secondary endpoint clinical outcomes improvements were maintained (FEV1pp, CFQ‐R, BMI, PEx, and SC) from the parent study, with a mean annualized rate of change in FEV1pp of 0.07% specifically noted. 5 Using the CF Foundation Patient Registry (CFFPR), observational analysis of real‐world clinical outcomes for 11,116 PwCF on ETI over the first 2 years of use showed sustained improvements from baseline in FEV1pp, PEx, BMI, even when subgroup analysis by genotype, baseline FEV1pp, and prior modulator use were analyzed. 6 Decreases of bacterial infections across several common pathogens in this analysis will be discussed later. Comparing PwCF from the phase 3 trials and extension to a propensity score matched group from the CFFPR who were not eligible for CFTR modulators, one study showed no decline in ppFEV1 for those taking ETI compared to a rate of decline at 1.92% per year over a 2 year period. 7

Subgroup evaluations—Advanced lung disease

Data for PwCF with advanced lung disease is limited by exclusion of this population in phase 3 trials for ETI. Use of ETI for advanced lung disease in one center in France showed clinical effectiveness, based on a committee decision based on symptoms and not directly tied to degree of FEV1pp, weight, or SC changes, after 4–6 weeks for 23 PwCF and advanced lung disease with known responsive mutations that had not been approved for use in Europe. 8 Another single center showed sustained improvement over 2 years for 18 PwCF with advanced lung disease. 9

Subgroup evaluations—Sex differences

Sex disparity in CF may still be present with modulator therapy; nevertheless, long term outcomes are still unknown. Differences in outcomes varied by sex in an adult, single center evaluation with 251 PwCF taking ETI, such that men on ETI showed a larger decrease in PEx, however no differences were seen for FEV1pp, presence of Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA), or BMI. 10

Clinical outcomes—Infections/Inflammation

ETI has continued to show clinical improvement in pulmonary infections in PwCF. Decreases in positive cultures for PA were seen in several studies after initiation of ETI. 6 , 11 , 12 Cultures for Staphylococcus aureus (SA) were decreased in the aforementioned observational study of 11,116 PwCF in the CFFPR over 2 years. 6 Meanwhile in a smaller evaluation, a single US center evaluation did not show a decrease in SA after 12 months of ETI despite noting a decrease in PA in 124 PwCF. 11 The CFFPR evaluation also revealed decreases in Burkholderia cepacia complex and Aspergillus species growth on cultures. 6 Nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) was evaluated in a multicenter cohort study where 9 out of 15 PwCF achieved complete eradication after ETI initiation, while only 53% received any therapy for NTM. 13 NTM eradication was also seen in a case report of a patient who had previous failure of intensive antibiotic therapy. 14 In addition to decrease in specific infections, several studies demonstrated decreases in inflammatory markers. 12 , 15 , 16

Clinical outcomes—Multisystem effects: Weight/BMI

It has previously been shown that ETI can lead to increases in weight and BMI. Assessment in a prospective observational cohort study of 50 adults demonstrated these changes in BMI (median change 1.55 kg/m2) occurred in a setting of decreased caloric intake (median change −398 kcal). 17 A small cohort of eight pediatric and adult PwCF, a subset of a larger prospective multicenter study, also showed increases in weight z score (median 0.7), specifically with increases in fat mass with no changes in muscle mass. 18 This same study also showed increased insulin secretion and resistance and decreased insulin sensitivity, concerning for metabolic complications.

Clinical outcomes—Multisystem effects: CF‐related diabetes (CFRD)

The effects of ETI on CFRD, the most common extrapulmonary complication of CF, were evaluated this past year. A prospective, single‐center observational study of 24 patients with CFRD showed improvements in weight (median 1.9 kg), predominantly fat mass rather than fat‐free mass, and BMI (median 0.7 kg/m2, SD: 0.25). 19 This group also had decreased hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) and coefficient of variation, both markers of glycemic control, despite no changes in insulin administration. For those without CFRD, HbA1c levels were also seen to be decreased and 48.5% of 33 patients had improved oral glucose tolerance in a retrospective observational cohort study. 20

Clinical outcomes—Multisystem effects: Nutrients

Improved nutritional outcomes extended to absorption of vitamins and minerals. Elevations in both vitamin A and vitamin D levels were seen in a retrospective cohort study with 264 participants. 21 Relevant to the earlier topic of increased intracranial pressure and hypervitaminosis A, this study noted vitamin A levels continued to increase year‐over‐year, which was not seen with other fat‐soluble vitamins. 21 In contrast, a retrospective, single center review of 54 participants only saw increases in vitamin A and not vitamin D. 22 Iron levels, ferritin, and percent saturation of transferrin also increased after starting ETI, regardless of iron supplementation, in a single center, retrospective cohort study with 127 adult participants. 23

Clinical outcomes—Multisystem effects: Pregnancy/neonatal

Information related to the use of ETI during pregnancy and in the neonatal period continues to be reported, although there is still a paucity of data. In a case report, meconium ileus resolved in utero in a CF positive fetus born to a carrier mother who took ETI. 24 Clinically it is also important to know that children born to PwCF who are on ETI during pregnancy led to significantly lower immunoreactive trypsinogen (IRT) results, seen in a sample of 19 ETI‐exposed fetuses compared to neonates with positive NBS, including 489 infants who were CF carriers. 25 Further evaluation will need to be obtained on children born to mothers with CF taking ETI.

Side effects—Mental health

While ETI improves overall health, additional side effects regarding neuropsychiatric changes have been seen with phase 3 trials and evaluation of their continued use. In the phase 3 trial for younger children (2–5 years old), one adverse event of aggression improved with briefly stopping ETI but was also considered “developmentally appropriate.” 3 Early case reports and series after ETI initiation use have been fraught with these mental health concerns, including a small series discussing two suicide attempts in the adolescents with direct correlation to ETI usage published this past year. 26 Yet on a wider evaluation, a review of safety data from 14 pooled clinical trials with ETI, 10 clinical trials with modulators, postmarketing adverse events of 61,499 PwCF, registry data from United States and Germany found suicidal ideation and attempts while on ETI to be consistent with PwCF in general, although the authors noted a wide range of confounding factors. 27

In a short survey of 75 US healthcare providers for PwCF, providers were asked to estimate the percentage (either none or in quartiles of their patients) of new onset neurocognitive or mental health effects since ETI initiation, along with the impact of these effects. 28 There was a high estimated prevalence seen when evaluating new symptoms of depression, anxiety, and starting new psychotropic medications, with estimated prevalence in the lower quartile of their patients for 73%, 70%, and 72% of providers, respectively; a majority of providers saw these effects as having moderate or significant impacts on their patients' daily function, 65%, 61%, and 51%, respectively. 28 A literature review subsection in a previously mentioned article evaluated 12 studies with a total of 951 PwCF utilizing nine‐item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ‐9) data after ETI initiation; there was either no change or decreases in PHQ‐9 scoring across the studies over varying lengths of time (up to 12 months) since starting ETI. 27 In a single center, retrospective review over 5 years with 150 unique individuals, modulator (predominantly ETI) use was associated with lower PHQ‐9 scores, without an associated change in Generalized Anxiety Disorder‐7 scoring. 29

Side effects—Hepatic function

Alterations in liver function testing continue to be evaluated as more PwCF initiate and utilize CFTR modulators. Several single center evaluations demonstrated clinically significant increases in transaminase levels, defined as three times the upper limit of normal (ULN), ranging from 3% (of 255 PwCF after ETI initiation) at one center to 11% (of 83) in another that resolved primarily through a brief respite from or decreased dosage of ETI (except where alternative cause was discovered). 30 , 31 Bilirubin was also noted to be elevated in ∼10% of 503 PwCF on ETI across six centers in Italy, with one‐third being tested and found to have Gilbert syndrome, an error in bilirubin metabolism due to a mutation in UGT1A1 which can lead an elevation in unconjugated bilirubin. 32

Changes in clinical care delivery

With all the successful outcomes for PwCF who use ETI, assessment of need for ongoing treatments is pertinent. The SIMPLIFY study was a multi‐center, randomized, controlled study in PwCF on ETI that demonstrated that discontinuation of either dornase alfa (DA) or hypertonic saline (HTS), while maintaining airway clearance, was not associated with a decrease in FEV1pp, LCI2.5, or change in symptoms over a 6 week period. 33 A separate, single‐center adult study assessed decreasing therapies (HTS, DA, oral and inhaled antibiotics) every 3 months, similarly found non inferiority with respect to changes in FEV1pp. 34

Modulator eligibility

Personalized medicine has been used to assess for functionality of ETI for non‐approved CFTR mutations. For example, human nasal epithelial cells (HNEC) can be used as a tissue source to evaluate CFTR function in nonapproved variants, such as N1303K. As part of compassionate use for advanced lung disease in France, ETI was used in vivo for additional CFTR variants and demonstrated improvements in 22 individuals with 23 variants not previously approved in the United States or Europe, including the variant N1303K seen in eight individuals. 8 A sample of this group received theratyping via HNEC for analysis of CFTR function with ETI, correlating ex vivo increases in CFTR function and in vivo FEV1pp. 35 Furthermore, use of ETI in eight additional PwCF with the N1303K variant demonstrated clinical benefit with improved FEV1pp, BMI, and LCI2.5 despite no changes in SC. 36

PULMONARY

PEx remain a complication for PwCF, with many remaining unanswered questions regarding treatment. Many studies have started to better understand the current state of exacerbation therapy.

For adults, PEx treated with oral antibiotics was examined in a retrospective evaluation of the Toronto database from 2009 to 2018 in 547 PEx. 37 A greater drop in FEV1pp from baseline was associated with a greater increase in FEV1pp with PEx treatment. Overall, less improvement in FEV1pp was seen in older, female patients and greater improvement was seen with longer duration of treatment.

The Standardized Treatment of Pulmonary Exacerbations 2 (STOP2) examined several unanswered questions in the adult population. A description of the current care in 982 PwCF revealed initial treatment for intravenous (IV) antibiotics was inpatient (78%) with one‐third completing the full course in hospital. 38 The difference between location of treatment revealed pragmatic factors (such as closeness to center, length of treatment, insurance limitations) were more likely the drivers for location of treatment. Evaluation of 621 PwCF with a PEx, close to 40% had a viral infection and were more likely to be symptomatic at time of hospitalization, have increased inflammation, and a greater drop in FEV1pp. 39 Viral infection did not lead to a difference in early or late FEV1pp response or time to next exacerbation. Steroids prescribed during any of the 982 PExs showed no difference in FEV1pp response, time to next PEx, or symptom improvement with no increase in adverse events. 40

Children who have PEx can have differing clinical care and outcomes so additional information related to pediatric PEx care has been undertaken. PEx in children under 3 years of age was found to be common in a single center study where only 4 of 78 did not have an exacerbation in first 3 years of life. 41 Treatment with antibiotics in this age group was more likely with the presence of wet cough, nighttime cough, congestion, and >3 days of symptoms.

The Streamlined Treatment of Pulmonary Exacerbations in Pediatrics (STOP PEDS) study examined outpatient PEx treatment with oral antibiotics in patients 6–18 years of age. 42 In an open label pilot study, 121 children with PEx were treated with increasing airway clearance followed by randomization to immediate treatment (antibiotics for 14 days) or tailored treatment (antibiotics prescribed if worsening cough ≥4 days, cough not improving ≥7 days, improved but not resolved cough ≥14 days, or shortness of breath with activity or at rest). Unfortunately, the study was stopped early due to low enrollment as this was during the COVID pandemic, however the tailored treatment group avoided antibiotics 70% of the time.

Several analyses of PEx used the CFFPR linked with the Pediatric Health Information System. No difference in clinical outcomes was seen when IV antibiotics were changed during treatment in over 18,000 PEx. The ∼43% who had an antibiotic change were seen to have a smaller change in FEV1pp, lower odds of returning to within 90% of baseline, and higher odds of a future PEx requiring IV antibiotics. 43 Steroids were used in about 15% of nearly 9000 PEx and were not associated with better FEV1pp response or higher odds of returning to baseline. 44 Those treated with steroids had a 9% decrease chance of future PEx requiring IV antibiotics and, if treated early within the first 6 days, were observed to have a better increase in FEV1pp. Importantly, significant side effects were seen with an increased odds of transient insulin requirement in those with impaired glucose tolerance. Polymicrobial infections (≥2 in past 12 months) were seen in 75% of almost 28,000 PEx, with the most common being methicillin resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and PA. 45 Interestingly, 68% of PEx had incomplete antibiotic coverage for the known infections.

Beyond PEx, many aspects of care contribute to alterations in FEV1pp, such as nutrition, airway clearance, and ongoing care. At age 1, having a higher weight‐for‐age z score by 1 was associated with approximately 3.5% increase in FEV1pp at age 6 years when 1974 PwCF from the United Kingdom and 791 PwCF from the Canadian registries were analyzed. 46 Furthermore, FEV1pp at age 6 years was associated with weight‐for‐age at 1 year of age and the change in weight‐for‐age over time. Poor adherence (<50%) to nebulized therapies, assessed by data logging nebulizers, led to greater FEV1pp variability (median variability of 8.1%) when a subanalysis of 543 PwCF included in the development and evaluation of an intervention to support adherence to treatment in adults with CF program was performed. 47 A separate study using a bespoke Fizzyo sensor captured effectiveness of airway clearance at home. Out of 135 PwCF with analysis of a median of 224 treatments per person, improved efficacy of treatments led to higher FEV1pp when compared to ineffective or missed treatments. 48 Using over 24,000 individuals in CFFPR from 2004 to 2016, a gap in care of >12 months was seen in 6%–9% of 6–17 year olds, 15% of 18–21 year olds, and 30% in those over age 30. 49 Those who received discontinuous care had a peak difference in FEV1pp of 1.5%.

Nebulized medications, such as HTS and DA, also contribute to FEV1pp outcomes. Many PwCF cannot tolerate 7% HTS, thus a study of 134 PwCF demonstrated that 3% HTS had greater FEV1pp improvement compared to normal saline. 50 Comparison of 3% versus 6% HTS revealed no difference in improvement of FEV1pp or time to first PEx, while 6% HTS showed improvements in quality of life and delayed time to first PEx requiring IV antibiotics, that were not seen with 3% HTS. In a separate study that examined over 1200 PwCF from the CFFPR, DA alone or in combination with HTS found no difference in FEV1pp or incidence of PEx; the effect of adding HTS on FEV1pp was calculated at <4%, however the authors concluded the effect was due to confounding by indication. 51

If pulmonary health declines significantly, one treatment option is lung transplantation. The overall lung transplant rate has decreased in countries where CFTR modulators are available, however lung transplantation is still a reality for individuals with advanced lung disease so ongoing research to improve survival and posttransplant complications remains relevant. Worse posttransplant survival was not seen in 17 PwCF with B. cepacia complex (none had B. cepacia cenocepacia). 52 Lung transplantation is complicated by chronic allograft dysfunction leading to a second lung transplantation. Using registry data from the United States and Canada for 395 s lung transplant, linked with the United Network for Organ Sharing data, survival was worse after second transplant compared to initial with 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year survival rates of 77.4%, 52%, and 39%, respectively. 53

HEPATOBILIARY

Research and understanding related to CF‐related liver disease (CFLD) has expanded in recent years. The epidemiology of CFLD was explored in the 10‐year Irish longitudinal study (with a 92% retention rate) where 6.3% of 520 children developed CFLD with portal hypertension. 54 Those under 10 years had a higher incidence (10.77 vs. 2.13 per 1000 person‐years of follow up), leading authors to conclude CFLD is unlikely to develop if no evidence by age 10. The study also revealed PwCF and CFLD had a higher mortality and shorter life expectancy.

Understanding the epidemiology of CFLD helps to focus clinicians to better screen, diagnosis and manage CFLD as outlined in the new Consensus Recommendations, based on both evidence and expert opinion. 55 The first set of recommendations relate to screening (Table 1), focusing on labs, physical examination, and change in care for clinicians to include an abdominal ultrasound. The Prediction by Ultrasound of Risk of Hepatic Cirrhosis in CFLD (PUSH) Study, a prospective, longitudinal study over 9 years, helped to provide evidence related to the above recommendations. Most recently, the work from PUSH reported on PwCF with and without nodular liver disease. 56 Those with nodular liver disease were found to have a more rapid decrease in platelets and liver fibrosis indices, more rapid increase in spleen size, and increased liver stiffness on elastography. No differences were noted for FEV1pp, rate of decline of FEV1pp, BMI z score, height z score, or CFRD. When obtaining biannual ultrasounds, use of Doppler is not technically necessary to detect reversal of portal flow; however, it is best to consult with local radiologists to best determine modality. For adults, a baseline abdominal ultrasound is recommended but subsequent frequency is unknown and therefore no recommendations are provided.

If any abnormalities are found on screening, further evaluation and monitoring for progression is recommended in the second set of recommendations (Table 1). Note, that previously if lab variables were elevated to >1.5–2 times the ULN additional evaluation was recommended; however, the newest guidelines recommend evaluation if there are elevations of any amount that persist over 3–6 months. Lab evaluation is expanded and elastography and other imaging modalities should be obtained. The type of evaluation and frequency varies based on if CF hepatobiliary involvement (CFHBI) or advanced CFLD (aCFLD) is present.

CFHBI refers to having one (or more) of the following without features of advanced CFLD: hepatomegaly, liver fibrosis (Metavir score <F4), increased liver stiffness by elastography, hepatic steatosis, focal biliary cirrhosis, cholestasis, persistent (>3–6 months) elevated serum liver function tests (any level above ULN), abnormal liver imaging, cholelithiasis, sclerosing cholangitis, or hepatolithiasis. Once CFHBI is identified, monitoring and progression is unchanged apart from elastography annually.

aCFLD is defined as having one (or more) of the following: nodular liver, advanced fibrosis (Metavir score of F4), multilobular cirrhosis with or without portal hypertension, or noncirrhotic portal hypertension. Esophageal variceal management is per the most relevant currently published guidelines in adults, however, there is insufficient evidence for or against surveillance in children.

The final set of recommendations relates to treatment, managed in consultation with a gastroenterologist. Key points to note are a recommendation against use of ursodeoxycholic acid as no study has demonstrated use prevents development of aCFLD or liver transplantation. However, there are also no studies evaluating discontinuation. Recommendations for CFTR modulator use (which vary by clinical status), surgical interventions, treatment of varices, and transplantation are included in Table 1.

As some PwCF and CFLD progress to require liver transplantation, a review of patient registries (in the United States and Canda) and the Organ Procurement Transplant Network for 402 PwCF who underwent liver transplantation from 1987 to 2019 analyzed the effect on lung function. 57 After adjustment for confounders, the rate of FEV1pp decline was reduced by 69%. In addition, they analyzed survival postliver transplant to be 94%, 90%, and 87% at 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year time points.

INFECTION

The vicious cycle of obstruction, infection, and inflammation leads to pulmonary damage and decline. Numerous infections affect PwCF, such as PA, NTM, SA (methicillin sensitive Staphylococcus aureus [MSSA] and MRSA), and Achromobacter xylosoxidans.

PA can be associated with clinical decline, especially related to FEV1pp. In the Early Pseudomonas Infection Control (EPIC) observational trial which examined 1264 PwCF between 1992 and 2006, those who never had a positive culture for PA had less FEV1pp decline when compared to those with chronic PA. 58 As part of the EPIC trial, PA eradication protocols were studied, leading to use as a current standard of care (usually 28 days of inhaled antibiotics). A comparison of 14 days versus 28 days of aztreonam lysinate for inhalation for eradication of PA in 149 PwCF, failed to show noninferiority and therefore a shorter course is not recommended. 59 If PA is not eradicated and becomes chronic, regardless of how chronic PA is defined (ranging from 2 to 3 PA positive cultures over 1–4 years), FEV1pp decline did not differ when a review of data from the EPIC observational trial was analyzed. 58 Alternatively, a review of 205 PwCF in two CF centers over 20 years evaluated the effect of PA eradication, showing the rate of FEV1pp decline for those who never had PA was −0.77% per year, while those who had PA eradicated declined −1.11% per year and those with chronic PA had a decline of −1.57% per year. 60

NTM can be an infection with negative consequences for PwCF. Across the United States, there have been 58 cases of NTM per 1000 PwCF based on data from the CFFPR (2010–2019) with increases of 3.5% per year. The majority of the infections were Mycobacterium avium complex (48%) followed by Mycobacterium abscesses (25%). 61 As a caution to clinicians, discrepant results for identification, subspeciation, and susceptibility reports were seen in a small pilot study when 19 NTM samples were separated into 2–3 aliquots to send to different commercial labs and a lab specializing in NTM. 62

Achromobacter, was shown to be present in approximately 5% of over 38,000 PwCF in the European CF Society Patient Registry (over 40 countries). 63 Those PwCF with Achromobacter had similar FEV1pp and BMI z scores to those with PA, while those with PA and Achromobacter had worse outcomes.

From an equity/disparities perspective, a review of 10,640 PwCF from the CFFPR showed that compared to non‐Hispanic PwCF, Hispanic PwCF acquired MRSA and MSSA at an earlier age and had a 19% higher risk of acquiring MSSA and 13% higher risk of acquiring MRSA that persisted even when adjusted for confounding variables. 64

MULTISYSTEM EFFECTS OF CF

CF affects numerous body systems with many manuscripts written in the past year. The few included references in this section are specifically chosen for their potential to change care/education by providers to PwCF and their families. CFRD is a common complication for PwCF, however HbA1c has not been an adequate screening lab. In an interesting evaluation of 189 adults and 223 children with CF, a retrospective analysis showed that in the pediatric population a HbA1c > 5.5% led to a higher risk of progression to CFRD (6.0% in adults) within 7 years (11 years for adults). 65 Clinically, an elevated HbA1c may encourage families to obtain routine oral glucose tolerance tests. Previously, PwCF may have been counseled about minimal cardiac complications form CF (especially in light of a high fat diet). However, an analysis of the UK CF Registry and the TriNetX health care data network showed PwCF had a 2.5% baseline prevalence of major adverse cardiac events (myocardial infarction, left heart failure, or atrial fibrillation). 66 This study demonstrated that PwCF have a higher risk compared to those without CF (hazard ratio, 1.75 [1.59–1.92]) and thus counseling for clinicians may require some alterations.

Concern related to high pancreatic enzyme dosing leading to fibrosing colonopathy has limited some clinicians when prescribing. However, after introduction of reformulated pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy and dosing guidelines, the incidence of fibrosing colonopathy was 0.056 per 1000 person‐years (while confirmed cases was only 0.0242 per 1000 person‐years) when over 25,000 PwCF were studied, demonstrating the rarity of this complication. 67

In a small single center study of 22 PwCF and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), worse FEV1pp was correlated with worse ADHD and ADHD symptoms correlated with adherence to airway clearance, frequency of hospitalizations, and use of antibiotics for PEx. 68 , 69 Thus, working to ensure adequate control of ADHD could contribute to improved outcomes.

HOME SPIROMETRY

Providing care virtually or at a distance has increased since the COVID pandemic. Many researchers have been evaluating home spirometry with various devices. Agreement with recent clinic PFTs was seen in adults using the MIR‐Spirobank Smart Device however at least 1/3 discontinued routine use at home. 70 Home spirometry was lower compared to clinic when 67 PwCF used the Vitalograph Lung Monitor BT Smart, with more discrepancy in children (up to 35%) and overall variability of up to 10%. 71 Lower home values were also seen in a single center evaluation of 52 PwCF using the ZephryRx MIR‐Spirobank (up to 10% lower than clinic) however coaching at home improved values. 72 Week to week variation of up to 15% was seen with the PiKo‐6 nSpire, in 13 PwCF with a median of 204 days of spirometry. 73 Additionally, no association with time to PEx was seen. Despite the limitations of home spirometry, use of home spirometry in 60 PwCF in Turkey led to an overall improvement of FEV1pp over 1 year compared to conventional spirometry (only in those who were adherent) and PExs were identified earlier. 74

DIAGNOSIS AND NBS

In the United States, NBS has been present nationwide since 2010, yet variations among the process exist. Several studies provided a better understanding of NBS processes to work toward optimal NBS. In the United States, an evaluation of NBS in all 50 states and the District of Columbia reported a nearly even split between a fixed versus a floating cut off for IRT. 75 The floating cutoff improves sensitivity by limiting variation seen throughout seasons and different reagent lots, although it can still be limited by the cutoff value percentile. IRT was analyzed in all newborns (with and without CF) using data from NBS in Switzerland over 8 years, showing higher IRT levels in infants born <33 weeks or ≥43 weeks compared to term infants and higher IRT in samples taken <3 days or >6 days of life. 76 Variation was also seen in IRT levels between infants with CRMS/CFSPID, CF, and CF with meconium ileus.

After IRT, many NBS programs use genetic analysis. In the United States, the number of variants analyzed in CFTR ranged from full gene sequencing in four states to F508del only in two states. 75 Using 2020 CFFPR data to assess CFTR variant panels currently used in the United States, there was significant variation in detecting one or two CFTR variants in PwCF of different races and ethnicities, leading to higher false negative NBS and delays in diagnosis for non‐White populations; this improved, but was still present with increasing the number of CFTR variants assessed. 77 There was only minimal improvement when reevaluated with further updates to the CFTR2 database of pathogenic variants. 78 These disparities were also present in reproductive carrier testing in Australia comparing next generation sequencing to current NBS genetic analysis in approximately 2500 newborns. 79

Delays in diagnosis are associated with worse outcomes. When analyzing 3607 PwCF born between 2010 and 2018 in the CFFPR, infants with a late age of first event (AFE)—defined as SC testing, visit to a CF center for care, or hospitalization—showed no weight or length differences at birth. 80 However, those with a late AFE had a lower height‐for‐age z score and at 1 year of age had a lower median weight‐for‐age z score. Non‐White newborns had a delay in AFE by 9 days compared to White newborns and had lower median weight‐for‐age z score at 1 and 2 years of age. 81

CRMS/CFSPID

Understanding the progression of CRMS/CFSPID is important for patient care, as some of these individuals may convert to a diagnosis of CF based on changes in their SC concentration over time or updates in characterization of specific CFTR mutations and, therefore, need changes in care plans. A single‐center, retrospective evaluation of 63 patients diagnosed with CRMS over a 12 year period in the United States had 17.5% conversion to a CF diagnosis over time, with increased likelihood if their SC levels were initially 50–59 mmol/L or if there was a faster progression of SC levels toward diagnostic range over time. 82 Alternately, none of the individuals with a diagnosis of CRMS/CFSPID with a sweat < 30 mmol/L converted to a CF diagnosis. A multicenter evaluation in Italy found no difference in lung function between CFSPID/CRMS, those who converted to CFTR‐related disease, and those who converted to CF. 83 Interestingly, neither study had many conversions after 6 years of age. 82 , 83

CONCLUSION

This past year has been an exciting time for CF research and this review is not inclusive of the many publications from 2023. A plethora of studies on the widespread and long‐term effects of highly effective modulator therapy, specifically ETI were published. New consensus recommendations for hepatobiliary disease focused on screening, progression, and treatment. PEx and pathogens continued to be examined for optimal treatment across both adult and pediatric populations. NBS continues to expand, but research shows significant disparities. More has been done to evaluate the progression of CRMS/CFSPID throughout early childhood. Despite these advances, research is not complete until there is a cure found for all those with CF.
