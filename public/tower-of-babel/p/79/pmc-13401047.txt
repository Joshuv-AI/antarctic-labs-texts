Liquid–liquid phase separation and a phage-encoded inhibitor cooperatively drive transcriptional transition during phage SPO1 infection
Authors: Zonglan Yu, Zhenyue Hu, Qianqian Jin, Haoran Zhang, Menglan Chen, Huan Chen, Wei Chen, Juanjuan Gao, Yawen Wang, Yue Wu, Yingli He, Mengzhe Li, Yingqi Xu, James Garnett, Yan Li, Shuai Yuan, Bing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) organizes biochemical reactions in cells, yet whether this principle contributes to bacteriophage development has remained unclear. During infection, Bacillus phage SPO1 initially relies on host σA-dependent transcription to drive early gene expression before switching to phage-encoded σ factors for middle and late transcription. However, the mechanisms underlying these transitions, particularly how the middle σ factor Gp28 displaces σA, have remained elusive. Here, we show that SPO1 exploits LLPS to orchestrate its transcriptional program. We identify the phage-encoded transcription factor Gp27 as the principal driver of phase separation, forming biomolecular condensates both in vitro and in vivo. Structural and biochemical analyses reveal that Gp27 possesses a modular architecture that promotes condensate formation and concentrates the transcriptional machinery, thereby compensating for the intrinsically weak promoter-binding activity of phage-encoded σ factors. In parallel, we identify SPO1 Gp33 as an inhibitor of σA-dependent transcription whose expression itself depends on LLPS. Gp33 selectively suppresses σA-driven transcription by trapping the RNA polymerase holoenzyme in an inactive state and preventing σA-mediated promoter recognition. Together, these findings uncover LLPS as a previously unrecognized regulatory strategy exploited by bacteriophages, which acting in concert with a phage-encoded transcription inhibitor, as a mechanism governing phage transcriptional progression.

Introduction

Liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) is an essential biophysical mechanism by which cells organize intracellular components without membrane boundaries [1, 2]. Through LLPS, proteins and nucleic acids demix from the surrounding environment to form dynamic, droplet-like condensates that concentrate specific biochemical reaction. These biomolecular condensates, often referred to as membraneless organelles, play critical roles in diverse cellular processes, including transcription, RNA metabolism, chromatin organization, and signal transduction [3–5]. While LLPS has been extensively studied in eukaryotic systems, recent work has uncovered that prokaryotic cells also utilize LLPS to spatially and temporally coordinate their cellular functions [6–9]. In bacteria, several proteins, such as RNase E, Hfq, Rho, and RNA polymerase (RNAP), have been shown to form phase-separated condensates that contribute to RNA processing, stress response, and transcription regulation. These findings indicate that LLPS is not exclusive to compartmentalized eukaryotic cells but is a conserved organizing principle that also enables bacteria to respond rapidly and efficiently to environmental changes [7].

Among the diverse functions orchestrated by LLPS, transcriptional regulation is particularly prominent. In both eukaryotic and prokaryotic systems, transcription factors, RNAPs, and associated regulatory proteins can form condensates to modulate gene expression [10, 11]. In bacteria, for instance, RNAP has been shown to undergo LLPS in response to cellular conditions, contributing to the organization of transcriptional hubs [9]. These condensates can act as dynamic scaffolds to concentrate transcriptional machinery at active loci, facilitating robust and coordinated gene expression. RNAP of Gram-negative Escherichia coli (E. coli) is the best studied among the highly conserved bacterial RNAPs [12]. Bacterial transcription requires a σ factor to recognize a specific set of gene promoters and the core enzyme, which is composed of five subunits (α2ββ’ω), to catalyze RNA synthesis. While the core enzyme can carry out RNA synthesis, it lacks promoter specificity; association with a σ factor forms the holoenzyme, enabling promoter recognition. Compared to E. coli, the RNAPs of Gram-positive bacteria like Bacillus subtilis (B. subtilis) are more complex and contain two additional small non-essential subunits, δ and ε. Moreover, B. subtilis represents a group of Gram-positive bacteria whose genome has low G+C content [13], whose representative RNAP structures were only solved recently [14–16].

Bacteriophages, viruses that infect bacteria, must rapidly reprogram host gene expression to promote their own developments. Many phages hijack the host RNAP to drive expression of their genes, raising the possibility that they could leverage or manipulate LLPS to accelerate and coordinate their infection cycle [17]. For many phages, inhibiting or redirecting host RNAP after early gene transcription is essential for successful propagation of their parasitic life cycle [18]. Among the best-studied are E. coli phage RNAP inhibitors, particularly those targeting σ70-dependent transcription, such as Gp2 of phage T7 and AsiA of phage T4 [19–21]. In contrast, less is known about phage RNAP inhibitors targeting Gram-positive bacteria. The lytic phage SPO1, which infects B. subtilis, offers a model for studying host takeover events [22, 23]. SPO1 exhibits a temporally regulated transcriptional program, sequentially expressing early, middle, and late genes. Once the leading edge of the viral genome enters the host cytoplasm, its early genes are immediately recognized by host RNAP, initiating transcription [24]. Early phage promoters are immediately recognized by the host RNAP holoenzyme containing the primary sigma factor σA, resulting in rapid synthesis of early gene products [25]. Subsequently, transcription of middle genes is carried out by RNAP associated with the phage-encoded sigma factor Gp28 rather than σA. Therefore, Gp28 must at least partially displace or outcompete σA for binding to the RNAP core enzyme (E). However, biochemical studies have shown that Gp28 exhibits substantially lower affinity for E than σA and can only compete with σA under non-physiological conditions like high-salt [26]. Moreover, the RNAP recycling factor δ preferentially promotes reassociation of σA with the E, further favoring formation of the EσA holoenzyme. These observations make it difficult to explain how the highly efficient transition from EσA-dependent early transcription to EGp28-dependent middle transcription is achieved during SPO1 infection.

Here, we report the first LLPS that is utilized by a bacteriophage through hijacking the host RNAP. We identified LLPS as a central strategy employed by phage SPO1 to drive middle-gene transcription. Disruption of condensate formation completely blocked phage infection at low multiplicity of infection (MOI) by impairing middle- and late-gene transcription, underscoring the key role of LLPS in organizing the transcriptional landscape of SPO1. We further identified the phage-encoded protein Gp27, which exhibits a modular architecture characteristic of many LLPS-driving proteins, as the principal driver of condensate formation. In addition, we report Gp33 as a previously unrecognized σA-dependent transcription inhibitor. Biochemical and structural analyses reveal that Gp33 forms a stable complex with RNAP and σA, locking RNAP in the holoenzyme state and thereby preventing further transcription initiation. Together, these findings describe the first instance of LLPS in a bacteriophage and expand our understanding of how viruses spatially and temporally regulate gene expression during infection by synergistically deploying an LLPS driver to promote middle-gene transcription while using an RNAP inhibitor to shut down early transcription.

Methods

Bacterial culture conditions

Bacterial strains employed in this study are detailed in Supplementary Table S1. Escherichia coli strains were cultivated in Luria–Bertani broth (LB) containing 50 μg/ml of kanamycin or ampicillin (Millipore Sigma) and B. subtilis strains were cultured in LB broth containing 25 μg/ml of chloramphenicol (Millipore Sigma), with the antibiotic concentrations selected based on the resistant genes carried by the vectors.

Plasmid construction

Phage SPO1 genes 27, 28, 25.1, 33, 34, and 44 were amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) from the SPO1 phage genome, respectively. PCR products were resolved on 1% (w/v) agarose gels in 0.5× TAE buffer. gp27 (gp27NT, gp27CT, gp27NT+IDR, gp27IDR+CT), gp33 (gp3315-101), gp34, gp25.1, and σA genes were cloned into the pET-SUMO vector, while gp28 was cloned into the pET46 vector. And the resulted pET-SUMO-gp27, pET-SUMO-gp27NT, pET-SUMO-gp27CT, pET-SUMO-gp27NT+IDR, pET-SUMO-gp27IDR+CT, pET-SUMO-gp33, pET-SUMO-gp3315-101, pET-SUMO-gp34, pET46-gp28, pET-SUMO-gp25.1, and pET-SUMO-σA plasmids were transformed into E. coli DH5α competent cells, and all constructs were sequenced. All plasmids used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1. The primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Bacterial growth attenuation assays

The sequenced pHT01 vectors were electroporated into B. subtilis competent cells. Bacillus subtilis containing an empty circular pHT01 vector was used as the control and those bearing pHT01-gp33, pHT01-gp27, pHT01-gp27NT, pHT01-gp27CT, pHT01-gp27IDR, and pHT01-gp27NT+IDR vectors as experimental groups. The colonies were inoculated into 2 ml LB medium containing 25 μg/ml chloramphenicol and cultured overnight at 37°C and 220 rpm. The culture was then diluted to a fresh 2× YT medium containing 25 μg/ml of chloramphenicol and 2% glucose at a ratio of 1:100. For the induced group, 1 mM of Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added. Two-hundred microliter culture was transferred to 96-well microplate, and OD600 was measured by Bio-Tek Cytation 5 M microplate reader at 37°C. Three biological and technical replicates were performed for each growth assay.

Bacteriophage infection assay

To evaluate the impact of 1,6-hexanediol (Hex) on SPO1 replication, infection curves were determined at MOI of 10, 1, 0.1, 0.01, and 0.001. Overnight cultures of B. subtilis were subcultured into fresh LB broth and grown to an OD₆₀₀ ≈0.2, corresponding to ∼1 × 10⁸ colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU ml⁻¹). Experimental groups contained B. subtilis cells (1× 10⁸ colony-forming units per milliliter, CFU ml⁻¹), Phage SPO1 was then added at MOI of 10, 1, 0.1, 0.01, and 0.001, with a final Hex concentration of 5, 2.5, and 1% (v/v). To test the infective ability of SPO1 on the cells overexpressing the gp33 gene, Bacillus subtilis 168 harboring either pHT01-gp33 or the empty pHT01 vector with 1 mM IPTG was grown to the logarithmic phase (OD₆₀₀ ≈0.2). Phage SPO1 was then added at MOI of 0.001 and 0.1. All microplates were incubated at 37°C with continuous shaking, and OD₆₀₀ values were recorded every 15 min for 10 h using a Cytation 5 M microplate reader (Bio-Tek) in bottom-reading mode. Three biological and technical replicates were performed for each growth assay.

Bacteriophage plaque assay

To evaluate the effect of Hex on the plaque morphology of SPO1, the double-agar overlay assay was performed. Briefly, bottom agar plates were prepared using LB medium supplemented with 1.5% (w/v) agar and a final Hex concentration of 5% (v/v). Five milliliters of 0.5% (w/v) top agar in LB medium was mixed with 100 μl B. subtilis cells (OD₆₀₀ ≈0.2) and a final Hex concentration of 5% (v/v). After the top agar has completely solidified, drop 2 μl of the 10-fold-diluted SPO1 solution onto the surface of the top agar. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 10 h, after which plaques morphology were examined.

Adsorption rate assay

To assess the impact of Hex on the adsorption of SPO1 to B. subtilis at an MOI of 1. A 1-ml reaction mixture was prepared containing B. subtilis cells (1 × 10⁸ CFU ml⁻¹), phage SPO1 (100 μl, 1 × 10⁹ PFU ml⁻¹), and a final Hex concentration of 2.5% and 5% (v/v). Control groups used LB medium instead of Hex. The mixtures were incubated at 37°C for 15 min to facilitate phage adsorption, followed by centrifugation at 8000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was serially diluted 10-fold for use in the determination of phage titer. Phage titration was performed via the double-layer agar. Titer of unadsorbed phages were calculated.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction assay

To investigate the effect of Hex on the early, middle, and late genes expression of SPO1, we performed real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) assay under MOI of 10 with a final Hex concentration of 5% (v/v). The control group used LB medium instead of Hex. At the same time, to investigate the influence of overexpressing gp33 on early genes expression level, we also performed RT-qPCR assay under the same MOI. Immediately after mixing the SPO1 and B. subtilis, a sample was collected to serve as initial point (t = 1 min). Samples were collected at 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 45, and 60 min after the infection. Harvested samples were centrifuged at 8000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. Cell pellets were washed with 1 ml of pre-chilled LB medium. Total RNA was extracted from the cells using the RNAprep Pure Cultured Cells/Bacteria Total RNA Extraction Kit (TIANGEN, cat. no. DP430). RNA samples were denatured at 65°C and reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA using ReverTra Ace™ qPCR RT Master Mix (TOYOBO, cat. no FSQ-301). RT-qPCR was then performed using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (TaKaRa, cat. no RR820A) to quantify the transcription levels of the 16S rRNA endogenous control gene, as well as SPO1 early gene (gp28, gp25.1, and gp41), middle gene (gp30 and gp34), and late genes (gp13.1) at each time point. All RT-qPCR primers are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Prediction disorder region and phase–phase separation

Intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) within the target proteins were predicted using the MetaPredict web server (https://metapredict.net/). Phase separation propensity of the proteins was assessed via the MolPhase online platform (https://molphase.sbs.ntu.edu.sg/).

Protein expression and purification

All plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3), cells were grown at 37°C to OD600 of ≈0.6 and induced by 1 mM of IPTG. After 16 h of continuous induction at 18°C. The cultures were collected in binding buffer (50 mM NaH2PO4, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, pH 8.0) and lysed by sonication. The protein supernatant was loaded onto Ni-NTA HisBind resin (Novagen) purification column. Finally, the SUMO-tag was removed by ULP1 protease in 4 l of dialysis buffer [50 mM Tris–HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM TCEP, 5% (v/v) glycerol, pH 7.4].

Bacillus subtilis RNAP was expressed using the LK782 strain (ΔhelD), which contains the gene encoding His10-tagged β' subunit in its chromosomal DNA. After continuous culture at 37°C for 18 h, the cultures were lysed using the high-pressure homogenizer (Panasonic) and purified by immobilized metal-ion chromatography with a Ni-NTA column. The complex was further loaded onto a HiTrap™ Heparin Hp 1 ml × 5 column in ÄKTA pure system (GE) to remove nucleic acid. The eluate was again dialyzed overnight into buffer [50 mM Tris–HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM TCEP, 5% (v/v) glycerol, pH 7.4].

Size exclusion chromatography and SDS–PAGE

The protein sample was concentrated to 1 ml and loaded onto a Superdex™ 75 pg HiLoad™ 16/600 column for chromatographic separation. For sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE), protein samples were mixed with 5× SDS loading buffer and denatured at 100°C for 1 min. The experiments were performed at 220 V for 50 min using SDS–PAGE gels containing 20% polyacrylamide. After electrophoresis, the gel was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue to visualize protein bands and then decolorized with deionized water.

Size-exclusion chromatography coupled with multi-angle light scattering

The absolute molecular weights of Gp27 and its truncation variants were determined by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) coupled with multi-angle laser light scattering (SEC-MALS) and refractive index detection (SEC-MALS-RI). The system consisted of a Thermo Scientific UltiMate 3000 HPLC pump, a Wyatt DAWN HELEOS II 18-angle laser light scattering (MALS) detector, and a Wyatt Optilab T-rEX RI detector. A Wyatt Technology Corporation (WTC-010S5) column was equilibrated at 25°C with binding buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 0.2 mM TCEP, pH 7.4). For each analysis, 100 μl of filtered protein sample (Gp27NT, Gp27CT, or Gp27) was injected at a flow rate of 0.75 ml min⁻¹. Data acquisition and initial processing were performed using Wyatt ASTRA 8.1 software. Absolute molecular weight was calculated via Debye plot analysis, with the following solvent parameters defined: RI (1.331), viscosity (0.8945 cP), and RI increment (dn/dc = 0.185 ml g⁻¹). Baseline correction and target peak selection were subsequently performed, and final molecular weight analysis of Gp27NT, Gp27CT, and Gp27 were conducted using EasiGraph software.

NMR spectroscopy

All nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) experiments were performed at 298 K on a Bruker Avance III 800 MHz spectrometer equipped with a TCI CryoProbe. Data acquisition was controlled using TopSpin (version 3.6) (Bruker). For NMR study, the 15N and 13C labeling of Gp27CT or the 15N labeling of Gp27NT and Gp27IDR were achieved using M9 minimal medium containing 0.07% 15NH4Cl and 0.2% 13C-glucose. Samples were prepared in a buffer containing 20 mM KH2PO4 (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, and 0.2 mM TCEP, with 4% (v/v) D2O added for field locking. For NMR titration studies, samples were prepared in a buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM TCEP, 5% (v/v) glycerol, and pH 7.4 with 4% (v/v) D2O. A series of 2D 1H-15N HSQC spectra were recorded for 0.2 mM 15N-labeled Gp27CT with the stepwise addition of unlabeled (natural abundance) RNAP protein.

For structural determination, chemical shifts were referenced internally to DSS (4,4-dimethyl-4-silapentane-1-sulfonic acid) at 0.00 ppm. Backbone resonance assignments for Gp27CT were obtained using standard triple-resonance experiments, including 2D 1H-15N HSQC (hsqcetf3gp), 3D HNCACB (hncacbgp3d), CBCA (CO)NH (cbcaconhgp3d), HNCO (hncogp3d), HN (CA)CO (hncacogp3d), and HBHA (CO)NH (hbhaconhgp3d). Aliphatic side-chain assignments for Gp27CT were derived from 3D (H)CCH-TOCSY (hcchdigp3d2) experiment. Distance restraints were determined from 3D 15N-edited NOESY-HSQC (noesyhsqcfpf3gpsi3d, τmix = 120 ms) and 13C-edited NOESY-HSQC (noesyhsqcetgp3d, τmix = 120 ms) spectra collected on the 13C/15N-labeled Gp27CT sample. Distance restraints were derived from 13C- and 15N-edited NOESY-HSQC spectra, with cross-peaks assigned through a combination of manual verification and automated assignment using ARIAweb [27]. Backbone dihedral angle restraints () were predicted using TALOS-N [28]. Structure calculations employed a simulated annealing protocol with 10 000 steps for high temperature, 10 000 and 8000 steps for the first and second cooling phases,respectively, and 8000 steps for refinement. From 50 structures calculated in the final iteration, the 20 lowest-energy models were selected as the final ensemble and validated using MolProbity [29] and PROCHECK [30]. NMR spectra were processed using NMRPipe [31] and analyzed with POKY [32]. All NMR experiments were recorded on a Bruker 800 MHz (Avance III) spectrometer at 298 K. Structure statistics are summarized in Supplementary Table S3.

Molecular dynamics simulations

The initial structure of Gp27 middle domain (residues 41–110) was modeled by AlphaFold Server. All-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was performed using the GROMACS [33]. The protein was parameterized based on the CHARMM36m force field and solvated in a cubic box using the CHARMM-modified TIP3P water model. Sodium and chloride ions were added to neutralize the system charge and reach a salt concentration of 150 mM. Energy minimization was performed using the conjugate gradient algorithm to remove steric clashes. The system was then equilibrated for 200 ps in the canonical (NVT) ensemble and 200 ps in the isothermal–isobaric (NPT) ensemble, with position restraints applied to the protein backbone. During equilibration and production runs, the temperature was maintained at 298 K using the V-rescale thermostat, and the pressure was kept at 1.0 bar using the C-rescale barostat. The production simulation was conducted for 500 ns with a time step of 2 fs. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald method with a cut-off of 1.0 nm. Van der Waals interactions were calculated using a cut-off of 1.0 nm, with long-range dispersion corrections applied to both energy and pressure. Bond lengths involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm.

Simulation trajectories were processed using GROMACS built-in tools and custom Python scripts utilizing MDAnalysis, NumPy, Pandas, Matplotlib, and Seaborn libraries. Coordinates were saved every 4 ps for analysis. The conformational landscape was visualized by calculating the joint probability density of the radius of gyration (Rg) and end-to-end distance (Ree). Secondary structure propensity was analyzed using the GROMACS internal DSSP implementation. For clarity, the native DSSP output was grouped into five simplified categories: Helix (α-, 310-, and π-helices), Sheet (β-strand and β-bridge), PPII (poly-proline II helix), Turn (hydrogen-bonded turn and bend), and Coil (loops and irregular structures). Inter-residue contact maps were generated with a cut-off distance of 1.2 nm to assess tertiary interactions. Visualization and rendering of representative structures were performed using PyMOL (Schrödinger, LLC).

To visualize the dynamic conformational changes, a supplementary movie was generated using Visual Molecular Dynamics (VMD) [34]. The trajectory was downsampled to a 500-ps stride, and trajectory smoothing (window size 2) was applied to reduce thermal noise. To ensure accurate spatial proportions, an orthographic projection was employed. The protein was visualized using a composite of Tube (AOShiny material) and QuickSurf (Transparent material) representations. Rendering was executed via the internal Tachyon engine with ambient occlusion. Frames were rendered at 4K resolution (3840 × 2160) and subsequently downscaled to 1080p MP4 video (H.264 codec) using FFmpeg.

For evaluating the dynamic stability of the Gp33-containing RNAP complexes, the GROMOS96 54a7 force filed was applied, and the Simple Point Charge water model was used to solve the complex [35]. Cl− and Na+ were added to stabilize the electroneutral system. Furthermore, energy minimization was performed to eliminate steric clashes and structure optimization. After initial energy minimization, the system was equilibrated under NPT (constant pressure and temperature) ensembles at a temperature of 298.15 K and a pressure of 1 bar. Finally, MD simulations were performed using a 1-fs time step over a total duration of 50 ns. The graphical presentations were made using VMD.

Multi-round in vitro transcription assay

Mango III transcription assays were performed as described previously. Reaction mixtures (50 μl final volume) contained 0.4 μM B. subtilis RNAP, 0.04 μM DNA scaffold (Mango III-PE5 promoter, Mango III-PM12a promoter, and Mango III-PL16 promoter) 0 or 0.8 μM sigma foctor (σA, Gp28, Gp34), 3 μM Gp33/Gp27/Gp25.1 or Gp27/Gp25.1+PEG, 1 mM ATP, 1 mM GTP, 1 mM UTP, 1 mM CTP, and 1 μM TO1-biotin in transcription buffer [50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl₂, 1 mM TCEP, 5% (v/v) glycerol]. For assembly of the transcription complex, B. subtilis RNAP was first incubated with σA/Gp28/Gp34 at 22°C for 10 min, and then incubated with Gp33/Gp27 (or Gp27+PEG) at 22°C for 10 min, then with the nucleic acid scaffold at 22°C for 10 min, and finally with NTP mix and TO1-biotin at 37°C for 20 min. Fluorescence emission intensities were measured using a Varioskan Flash Multimode Reader (Bio-Tek Cytation 5M) with excitation at 510 nm and emission at 535 nm. All transcription assays were performed with at least three biological and technical replicates. Templates containing the Mango III sequence are listed in Supplementary Table S4.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

The electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA) were performed to assess the binding of Gp27 and its truncation variants (Gp27NT, Gp27CT, Gp27IDR+CT, Gp27NT+IDR) to double-stranded DNA. Reactions contained 1 μM dsDNA and serial concentrations of each protein (4, 8, 16, 32, 64, and 80 μM) in binding buffer (150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris–HCl, 0.2 mM TCEP, pH 7.5). The reaction volume was adjusted to a final volume of 10 μl with binding buffer, and mixtures were incubated at 4°C for 30 min. Samples were loaded onto 1% (w/v) agarose gels prepared in 0.5× Tris–borate–EDTA (TBE) buffer, and electrophoresis was performed on Novex TBE Gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 90 V for 30 min at 4°C. Fluorescent signals were visualized using a ChemiDoc™ MP Imager (Bio-Rad). All EMSA were performed in duplicate, and representative gels are shown.

The promoter binding EMSA assays were preformed by co-incubating RNAP (5 nM) and σ factor (Gp28 or σA, 5 nM) in a reaction buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5) at 37°C for 1 h. For the Gp28 assay, Gp27 (50 μM) and the Cy5-labeled PE5 or PM12a (1 nM) with 10% polyethylene glycol (PEG). For the inhibition assay, Gp33 was added at various concentrations (0, 100, 200, 300 nM) either prior to the addition of PE5 or PM12a and incubated for 1 h, or after the addition of PE5 or PM12a and incubated for an additional hour. For the RNAP–σA–promoter complex (RPc) inhibition assay, the reaction mixture was incubated for 1 h before Gp33 was added. The samples were separated on Novex TBE Gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 120 V for 30 min at 4°C. The florescence images were taken using a ChemiDoc™ MP imager (Bio-Rad).

In vitro phase separation

EGFP-tagged full length Gp27 (Gp27-EGFP) and truncation variants (Gp27NT-EGFP, Gp27CT-EGFP, Gp27NT+IDR-EGFP, Gp27IDR+CT-EGFP), EGFP-tagged Gp25.1 (Gp25.1-EGFP) and Gp44 (Gp44-EGFP), and free EGFP (negative control) were used in this study. For the in vitro phase separation assays, Gp27-EGFP, Gp25.1-EGFP, Gp44-EGFP, Gp27NT-EGFP, Gp27CT-EGFP, Gp27NT+IDR-EGFP, and Gp27IDR+CT-EGFP were adjusted to a final concentration of 50 μM in the buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT, pH 7.4), and incubated with 10% (v/v) PEG-8000 at 37°C 10 min. Ten microliters of each reaction mixture was transferred to glass microscope slides to minimize protein degradation and fluorescence quenching. Fluorescence signals were visualized using a 20× objective lens on a Axio Observer 7 inverted fluorescence microscope (Zeiss). For quantitative analysis, the area and diameter of phase-separated droplets were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, NIH).

Turbidity measurements

Gp27-EGFP, Gp27NT-EGFP, Gp27CT-EGFP, Gp27NT+IDR-EGFP, Gp27IDR+CT-EGFP, Gp25.1-EGFP, and free EGFP (negative control) were used for turbidity-based phase separation assays. Each sample was adjusted to serial final concentrations (5, 10, 20, 30, and 50 μM), followed by the addition of 10% (v/v) PEG-8000. All samples were incubated at 37°C for 10 min, and turbidity (an indicator of phase separation) was subsequently measured as OD₆₀₀ using a BioTek microplate reader (Cytation 5M).

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) assays were performed to characterize the dynamic behavior of phase-separated Gp27 protein. Phase-separated droplets were deposited onto a glass microscope slide. Coverslips were placed over the samples. EGFP fluorescence was monitored at room temperature using a Leica TCS SP8 STED 3X confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) equipped with an argon-ion laser. Excitation was conducted at 488 nm, and emission signals were collected at 507 nm. Imaging was performed using a 63× objective lens with an image resolution of 512 × 512 pixels. For each droplet, three non-overlapping, circular regions of interest (ROIs) were selected for bleaching, and a non-bleached background region was chosen for subsequent background fluorescence subtraction.The FRAP protocol consisted of three sequential steps. (i) Pre-bleach: 5∼10 pre-bleach frames were acquired to establish a baseline fluorescence intensity. (ii) Photobleaching: ROIs were rapidly and completely photobleached using the 488-nm laser at 100% laser power. (iii) Recovery: Immediately after bleaching, imaging was resumed using a low-intensity 488-nm laser. Frames were acquired at 0.5–1 s intervals for up to 10 min, until fluorescence intensity plateaued.

Aggregation in vivo

To investigate the phase separation in vivo behavior of the SPO1 phage Gp27 in its host B. subtilis, an overexpressed plasmid (pHT01-EGFP-gp27) was transformed into B. subtilis cells. Single colonies were inoculated into fresh LB broth and grown to OD₆₀₀ ≈0.6, followed by induction with 1 mM IPTG for 4 h at 37°C. For negative control experiments, cells were induced with IPTG for 2 h, then treated with a final Hex concentration of 5% (v/v), and incubation was continued for an additional 2 h. Following two washes with PBS, cells were immobilized on glass slides for microscopic analysis. Intracellular phase separation was visualized using an Olympus FV3000 laser scanning confocal microscope. To observe the aggregation of Gp27-EGFP within the cells, we used ImageJ software (NIH) to select the ROI and observed the fluorescence aggregation in a single cell.

Cryo-EM sample preparation, data collection, and processing

Tag-less Gp33 and RNAP were purified using the protocol described above. The mixture was then subjected to standard purification protocol and SEC to obtain the sample for cryo-EM study. The sample complex (4 μl) was applied to a Quantifoil 1.2/1.3 copper grid pretreated by glow-discharging at 8 mA for 20 s. The grid was blotted at 4°C with 100% humidity and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane using FEI Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI Thermo Fisher). The grids were stored in liquid nitrogen before data collection. Cryo-EM data acquisition was performed at a CRYO ARM 300 electron microscope (JEOL, Japan) operating at 300 kV with a K3 direct electron detector (Gatan, USA). Cryo-EM images were recorded automatically using Serial-EM software in super-resolution mode with the physical pixel size corresponding to 0.95 Å. A total of 21 951 movie series were collected at a frame rate of 40 frames per second. The total electron dose was 40 e/Å2. In the reconstruction, standard data processing pipeline was carried out in cryoSPARC [36]. Patch motion correction and patch CTF estimation were performed before particle picking. After rounds of 2D classification, 2 902 506 particles were selected for further refinement. Ab initio models were generated. Heterogeneous refinements and 3D classifications were performed to classify the particles. Non-uniform refinement was conducted to get the density and local resolution estimation was performed. The final average resolution is 3.02 Å for the RNAP–Gp33 complex, 2.94 Å for the RNAP–Gp33–σA complex.

Model building and refinement

PDB 6WVJ and 6WVK were used as initial models [37]. The models of the RNAP–Gp33 and RNAP–Gp33–σA complexes were manually built in COOT [38] and refined with phenix.real_space_refine module in PHENIX [39]. All structural figures were generated using ChimeraX [40]. The refinement statistics are summarized in Supplementary Table S5.

Binding affinity determination by BLI

The binding affinities between RNAP–Gp33, and RNAP–Gp3315-101 were measured using Octet R2 (Sartorius). All experiments were performed in a reaction buffer composed of 50 mM Tris–HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM TCEP, 5% (v/v) glycerol, and 0.02% (v/v) Tween 20, pH 7.4. RNAP was diluted to 100 nM in the reaction buffer and immobilized on Ni-NTA biosensors for 420 s, followed by washing with reaction buffer for 120 s. Gp33 was incubated with immobilized RNAP at varying concentrations for 300 s, and dissociation was monitored for 600 s in the same buffer. Data were analyzed using Octet Analysis Studio 12.2. Prior to fitting a 1:1 binding model, raw association curves were baseline-corrected. Binding curves were then globally fitted to calculate overall kinetic parameters.

RNAP–Gp33- σA and RNAP–Gp33 complex assembly

The RNAP–Gp33–σA and RNAP–Gp33 complexes were assembled in vitro by loading purified B. subtilis RNAP core enzyme on Ni-NTA column. Excess amount of purified tag-less Gp33 or Gp33 and σA was then incubated with column-immobilized RNAP for 10 min on ice. Excess amount of Gp33 or σA was washed, and the elution was subject to SDS gel analysis.

Phylogenetic analysis

To perform the phylogenetic analysis, the Gp27 sequence was retrieved from the NCBI database, and the all sequences were downloaded. These sequences were then aligned using Mafft v7.515 with the “-auto” parameter. The resulting alignment was used to construct a phylogenetic tree using FastTree v2.1.11 and visualized with Chiplot [41].

Results

Hex inhibits SPO1 phage infection by blocking middle and late gene transcription

LLPS is frequently associated with transcriptional regulation in eukaryotic cells and has been recently described in bacteria [9, 42]. Since transcription is central to phage replication, we sought to investigate whether bacteriophages might also exploit LLPS-like mechanisms during their gene expression. We selected B. subtilis and its lytic phage SPO1 as a model system, as SPO1 relies on host RNAP modification to orchestrate its transcriptional program. We performed infection assays in the presence or absence of Hex, a well-established aliphatic alcohol known to disrupt LLPS-mediated assemblies, including those formed by bacterial RNAP [9], using the established concentration of 5% (v/v) for E. coli. Consistent with the previous report that Hex impairs the formation of RNAP clusters and subsequent transcription in E. coli during log phase in rich media, and may additionally affect other biomolecular condensates as reported in eukaryotic systems [43], Hex-treated B. subtilis displayed impaired growth in LB medium (Supplementary Fig. S1A and B). Upon infection with SPO1, Hex treatment markedly impaired phage propagation. At 5% Hex, phage-induced lysis was delayed at high multiplicities of infection (MOIs = 10 and 1), indicating a slower infection process (Supplementary Fig. S1A), whereas at lower MOIs (0.1, 0.01, and 0.001), SPO1 infection was almost completely inhibited (Supplementary Fig. S1B). To determine whether these effects were attributable to Hex-mediated inhibition of host growth, we examined eight Hex concentrations ranging from 5% to 1% and found that concentrations of 2.5% (v/v) or lower did not affect bacterial growth (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Nevertheless, 2.5% (v/v) Hex continued to strongly inhibit SPO1 propagation: phage-induced lysis was substantially delayed at MOIs of 1, 0.1, and 0.01 (Fig. 1A), while infection was completely blocked at MOIs of 0.001 and 0.0001, with bacterial growth remaining nearly identical to that of uninfected cells (Fig. 1B). Even at 1% (v/v) Hex, SPO1 propagation remained significantly impaired (Supplementary Fig. S1D), although the inhibitory effect decreased in a concentration-dependent manner compared with the 5% and 2.5% (v/v) treatments. Consistent with these observations, plaque assays showed that Hex reduced SPO1 infectivity by approximately one to three orders of magnitude in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 1C). Together, these results indicate that the inhibitory effect of Hex on SPO1 infection is consistent with a role for LLPS-mediated processes during the phage infection cycle, rather than the non-specific inhibitory effect due to the growth delay that Hex induced.

To exclude the possibility that Hex interferes with phage adsorption, we measured the binding efficiency of SPO1 to B. subtilis cells in the presence of 2.5% or 5% (v/v) Hex. Hex did not alter the adsorption rate, indicating that it does not affect the initial attachment of SPO1 to its host (Fig. 1D). This suggests that the impaired infection results from disruption of a downstream process, such as transcription, rather than interference with phage entry. To investigate this further, we examined phage gene expression using RT-qPCR at multiple time points post-infection (p.i.) at an MOI of 10. The onset of transcription of SPO1 early genes (gp28, gp25.1, and gp41) was largely unaffected by Hex, indicating that the initiation of early-stage transcription proceeds normally (Fig. 1E). However, these early genes remained highly expressed, although at levels lower than their initial peak after 30 min p.i., in contrast to the apparent decline observed in the untreated control. This sustained expression indicates that SPO1 failed to inhibit σA-dependent early gene transcription, suggesting that Hex treatment resulted in the absence or disfunction of a putative σA-dependent inhibitor. Meanwhile, transcription of the SPO1 middle genes gp30 and gp34, as well as the late gene gp13.1, was markedly suppressed in the presence of Hex (Fig. 1F and G). This suppression suggests a failure to initiate middle gene transcription, which may be attributable either to the absence of the putative σA-dependent inhibitor alone or to the loss of an additional transcription factor required to promote middle gene transcription.

The transition from early to middle gene transcription requires RNAP to switch from the host σA to the phage-encoded σ factor Gp28 [44], while other components of host the transcription machinery remain largely unchanged. The σ-factor switch in SPO1 is considered to require additional phage-encoded transcriptional regulators and inhibitory proteins that fine-tune promoter recognition and ensure precise temporal control [24], as Gp28 alone is thought not a strong competitor of the host σA. However, the exact molecular mechanisms underlying this switch remain incompletely understood. To validate whether σA inhibits Gp28-dependent middle transcription, we performed in vitro transcription competition assays between the host and phage σ factors. In these assays, the reaction contained both early and middle promoters together with the two transcription factors, σ and Gp28; however, only one promoter was fused to the Mango III reporter at a time to enable fluorescence detection [45, 46]. When the transcription from early promoter PE5 was monitored by the aptamer-based method, the fluorescence intensity was not affected by adding Gp28 into the reaction (Supplementary Fig. S1E). However, in the same reaction when PM12a was monitored, no transcription from PM12a was observed. These results indicate that Gp28 alone is unable to effectively compete with σA for RNAP and activate middle-gene transcription, suggesting that additional factors are required to facilitate the σ-factor switch. Importantly, the two major consequences of Hex-induced LLPS inhibition, failure to repress early-gene transcription and serverly impaired activate middle-gene transcription, provide important mechanistic clues. Because early-gene transcription initiation is unaffected and Hex inhibits LLPS only at the protein level, the LLPS driver should be encoded by an SPO1 early gene. This factor could correspond either to a putative early-gene inhibitor and/or to a putative promoter of middle-gene transcription.

Gp27 is an LLPS driver and Gp33 is a early gene inhibitor

To identify SPO1 proteins that can drive condensate formation, we compiled a list of all SPO1 early genes (Table 1), together with eight SPO1 genes (Supplementary Table S1) known to be associated with transcription, taking advantage of prior systematic analyses of the SPO1 genome [22, 24, 47]. We first analyzed the propensity of all early gene products to form LLPS structures using predictive modeling [48]. Among all early genes, only Gp25.1, Gp27, and Gp44 contain characteristic sequence features associated with phase separation, including predicted IDRs, clusters of oppositely charged residues, dispersed aromatic residues (tyrosine and phenylalanine) that promote π-interactions [49], and are predicted to have high LLPS potential. Gp44 is known to inhibit transcription non-specifically in both E. coli and B. subtilis, likely contributing to suppression of SPO1 early gene expression [50]. It contains a large, negatively charged IDR that functions as a DNA mimic, enabling interaction with the β and β′ subunits of RNAP to form a stalled transcription complex. However, because LLPS typically arises from weak, multivalent interactions, the strong and highly specific binding between the DNA-mimicking IDR of Gp44 and RNAP β/β′ is unlikely to be disrupted by Hex. Furthermore, using the same concentration as antitermination factors like NusA (25 μM), which drives LLPS for E. coli RNAP [9], Gp44 failed to undergo LLPS when examined using fluorescently labeled EGFP-Gp44 in the presence of 10% PEG in the in vitro assay (Supplementary Fig. S2A). This suggests that LLPS is not required for Gp44-mediated inhibition, consistent with the absence of PEG in previous Gp44 studies, and confines the putative LLPS-driving activity to Gp25.1 and Gp27.

In contrast, both Gp25.1 and Gp27 are capable of forming condensates in the presence of PEG. Gp25.1 formed droplets with an average diameter of ∼3 μm, and its phase behavior was readily disrupted by Hex (Fig. 2A). In comparison, Gp27 formed substantially larger droplets, with an average diameter of ∼8 μm, and its LLPS was also strongly inhibited by Hex (Fig. 2B). Consistently, turbidity assays showed that Gp27 became turbid in solution in the presence of PEG with or without EGFP tag, and that Gp25.1 also exhibited increased turbidity but to a much lesser extent (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Furthermore, we used the Mango III aptamer-based in vitro transcription assays to assess the functional consequences of LLPS on transcriptional activity. In the presence of PEG, Gp27 strongly stimulated transcription from the SPO1 middle promoter PM12a, whereas in the absence of PEG, it showed no statistically significant enhancement (Fig. 2C). In contrast, Gp25.1 had no measurable effect on transcription. These results indicate that Gp27-driven condensate formation substantially promotes middle-gene transcription. Furthermore, we evaluated the effect of B. subtilis RNAP on Gp27 condensate formation. The addition of RNAP to Gp27 resulted in markedly larger droplets, with an average diameter of ∼11 μm (Fig. 2D), which also supports that Gp27 promotes transcription and implies potential protein–protein interaction between Gp27 and RNAP. Because Gp27 is transcribed from both early and middle promoters and is known to be essential for SPO1 late-gene transcription [51, 52], we next examined its effect on late-gene expression using the late promoter PL16. In the presence of PEG, Gp27 also enhanced Gp34-initiated late-gene transcription, suggesting that LLPS likely also contributes to the stimulation of late-gene expression in a similar manner (Fig. 2E). Together, these findings identify the early/middle gene product Gp27 as a key driver of LLPS that concentrates macromolecules to promote transcription, while Gp25.1 might serve a supportive role. The results further suggest the existence of a σA-dependent early-gene transcription inhibitor encoded by a middle gene. Based on Supplementary Table S6, the candidate pool for this putative inhibitor is narrowed to Gp33, which is the only middle-gene product known to be associated with transcription, aside from the late σ factor Gp34.

We then examined the potential inhibitory role of Gp33 in comparison with Gp27 in regulating σA-controlled early gene transcription. In the in vitro competition transcription assays, Gp27 in fact increased the fluorescence intensity likely due to the role of LLPS in concentrating transcription components, whereas Gp33 resulted in a significant decrease in fluorescence when early promoter PE5 was monitored (Fig. 2F). Furthermore, the combined addition of Gp27, Gp28, and PEG led to an even stronger inhibition of σA-dependent early-gene transcription. We then investigated the effects of Gp27 and Gp33 on Gp28-dependent middle-gene transcription. When transcription from the middle promoter PM12a was monitored, addition of either Gp33 or Gp27 (with PEG) alone did not significantly stimulate transcription. In contrast, the simultaneous addition of Gp33 and Gp27 (with PEG) resulted in a substantial increase in transcriptional output (Fig. 2G), demonstrating a synergistic effect of Gp27 and Gp33 in vitro. Meanwhile, the addition of Gp33 and σA did not measurably alter the morphology or size of Gp27-RNAP condensates, suggesting that LLPS formation and inhibition of early-gene transcription are likely two independent processes (Supplementary Fig. S2C). These observations are consistent with a role for Gp33 in inhibiting σA-initiated transcription and LLPS in promoting the Gp28-initiated middle transcription. Although the molecular mechanism underlying Gp33-mediated inhibition remains to be elucidated, the results suggest that a model that SPO1 may utilize distinct gene products with complementary functions: an LLPS-driving factor that promotes middle-gene transcription, and a transcriptional inhibitor that represses early-gene transcription. Together, the two proteins may act in a coordinated manner to support orderly transcriptional progression during infection.

Characterization of Gp27 liquid droplets

To characterize the LLPS properties of Gp27, we next examined its condensate-forming ability in vivo. Confocal microscopy of B. subtilis cells overexpressing EGFP-Gp27 revealed discrete intracellular foci (Fig. 3A), reminiscent of RNAP clusters previously observed in E. coli [9], and treatment with Hex dispersed these foci, consistent with their LLPS nature. Because RNAP cluster formation is partially driven by interactions involving DNA, we next investigated whether Gp27 directly binds DNA. EMSA using double-stranded DNA showed that Gp27 induced a concentration-dependent shift of the DNA probe (Fig. 3B), confirming its intrinsic DNA-binding activity. These results support the role of Gp27 in transcriptional regulation; however, the contribution of protein–protein interactions to Gp27-driven LLPS remains to be determined.

We next assessed the physicochemical parameters and dynamics of Gp27 droplet formation. Increasing salt concentrations progressively inhibited condensate assembly, indicating that electrostatic interactions are critical for LLPS, while phase separation increased with protein concentration (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig S3A and B). Temperature also influenced droplet formation: Gp27 formed substantially more droplets at 37°C than at 25°C (Fig. 3D). Droplets formed rapidly but gradually diminished over time, indicating they are fast-forming and transient (Fig. 3E). FRAP revealed slow molecular exchange within droplets, reflecting relatively stable internal interactions (Fig. 3F). Collectively, these observations show that Gp27 droplets assemble quickly under physiological conditions, are DNA-associated, and display low internal fluidity. These biophysical features align well with the short infection cycle of phage SPO1, which likely requires condensates that form rapidly around DNA but persist only briefly during infection.

Structural and functional analysis of Gp27 domains

Gp27 can be divided into two subdomains separated by a predicted middle intrinsically disordered (ID) region (Fig. 4A and B): an N-terminal region (Gp27NT, residues 1–40), a predicted ID region (Gp27IDR, residues 41–110), and a C-terminal region (Gp27CT, residues 111–155). The Gp27NT domain is predicted by AlphaFold3 to contain two discontinuous α-helices (Fig. 4C). This prediction is supported by the 1H NMR spectrum, which shows well-dispersed amide and methyl resonances indicative of a folded structure (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, SEC-MALS analysis revealed two species corresponding to monomeric and higher-order states, suggesting that this domain has the ability for self-association (Fig. 4E).

To examine the middle domain, we first evaluated its dynamic properties using MD simulations. The MD results show that Gp27IDR adopts a highly dynamic conformation characteristic of ID proteins (Fig. 4F, Supplementary Fig. S4A–D, and Supplementary Video S1). Consistent with this behavior, amino acid composition analysis revealed a strong enrichment of lysine residues (Supplementary Fig. S4E), suggesting a potential role in DNA interaction. Because Gp27IDR could not be efficiently cleaved from the SUMO tag, we used Gp27NT and Gp27NT+IDR (residues 1–110) to assess DNA-binding activity. Whereas Gp27NT caused only minimal retardation of the dsDNA probe at high protein–DNA molar ratios, Gp27NT+IDR induced a clear, concentration-dependent mobility shift that observed with the full length Gp27 (Fig. 3B). Together, these results demonstrate the ID nature of the middle domain and indicate that the IDR mediates DNA association.

For the Gp27CT, SEC-MALS analysis showed that it is monomeric, and NMR spectra revealed a well-folded conformation (Supplementary Fig. S4F and G), enabling high-resolution structural determination. Using standard solution NMR methods, we solved the structure of Gp27CT (Fig. 4H and Supplementary Fig. S4H). Gp27CT adopts a simple fold comprising two α-helices connected by a flexible loop, with the relative orientation of the helices defined by inter-helical side-chain NOEs. We next investigated whether Gp27CT interacts with RNAP. NMR titration experiments showed substantial peak broadening upon addition of 0.5 molar equivalents of RNAP to 15N-labeled Gp27CT (Supplementary Fig. S4I). To further explore this interaction, we modeled the RNAP–Gp27CT complex using AlphaFold3. The highest-scoring model exhibited a high ipTM score, indicative of reliable subunit positioning within a protein complex, and placed Gp27CT in contact with the β subunit of RNAP. Together, these results demonstrate that Gp27CT is a folded domain capable of binding RNAP.

We then examined the condensate-forming ability of Gp27 using a modular approach. Using EGFP-fused constructs, we evaluated the LLPS behavior of individual domains and truncation variants (Supplementary Fig. S5A and B). Notably, Gp27NT formed small droplets, likely reflecting its intrinsic self-association properties. Inclusion of the IDR further enhanced phase separation, as Gp27NT+IDR exhibited substantially increased droplet formation. In contrast, Gp27CT alone was insufficient to drive LLPS and failed to form visible droplets in vitro. However, addition of the IDR to the C-terminal region (Gp27NT+IDR) resulted in detectable droplet formation, indicating that the IDR is required to promote phase separation in this context. Consistent with the fluorescence microscopy results, turbidity assays showed that all truncation variants displayed reduced or no condensate formation compared with full-length Gp27 (Supplementary Fig. S5C). To determine the functional importance of LLPS and RNAP binding in transcriptional switching, we next performed in vitro transcription competition assays using full-length Gp27 and mutant derivatives. While full-length Gp27 efficiently supported the transition from early- to middle-promoter transcription, neither the LLPS-deficient mutant (Gp27CT) nor the RNAP-binding-deficient mutant (Gp27NT+IDR) was able to promote this switch (Fig. 4J). Together, these results indicate that the modular architecture of Gp27 underlies its function during transcriptional switching (Supplementary Fig. S5D). Phase separation driven by the self-associating N-terminal domain and the ID middle region, together with RNAP binding mediated by the C-terminal domain, both contribute to the transition from early to middle transcription in vitro.

Molecular basis of Gp33 as a transcription inhibitor

Because Gp33 is essential and could not be deleted from the phage genome, we instead examined the effects of Gp33 overexpression to verify and characterize its role as a σA-dependent transcription inhibitor. Overexpression of Gp33 in B. subtilis strain 168 resulted in marked growth attenuation, consistent with the phenotype expected for a transcription inhibitor (Fig. 5A). To determine whether early gene transcription was affected, we analyzed the expression of SPO1 early genes during SPO1 infecting Gp33-overexpressing B. subtilis by RT-qPCR at multiple time points p.i. using an MOI of 10. The results showed that the transcription of SPO1 early genes (gp28 and gp25.1) was significantly impaired in the Gp33-expressing cells (Fig. 5B), supporting the conclusion that Gp33 inhibits σA-dependent SPO1 early gene transcription.

As it remains unclear at which stage of transcription Gp33 exerts its inhibitory effect, we sought to probe the specific RNAP states to which Gp33 binds. For this reason, we mixed purified tagless Gp33 with B. subtilis cell lysate (strain LK782) that engineered to express His-tagged RNAP complexes via auto-induction, in attempt to assemble the RNAP–Gp33 complex in situ. This approach closely mimics the native physiological environment during phage infection. SEC coupled with SDS–PAGE analysis confirmed the formation of RNAP–σA–Gp33-complex (Supplementary Fig. S6A), which was then subjected to cryo-EM analysis. Interestingly, two RNAP complexes both exhibiting additional densities at the β’ subunits were identified during cryo-EM processing: RNAP–σA–Gp33 and RNAP–Gp33 (Supplementary Fig. S6B). Notably, although the overall resolutions of these complexes reached 2.94 Å and 3.02 Å, respectively, the Gp33-associated density in the latter showed reduced local resolution (>4 Å) and could not be fully resolved, suggesting that Gp33 may be conformationally flexible. In the reconstructed RNAP–Gp33–σA complex (Fig. 5C), all non-essential subunits, including ω, ε, and the N-terminal folded domain of δ, are present in the model but nucleic acids are absent. The de novo-built Gp33 has an elongated structure, and contains an N-terminal (NT) helix and C-terminal region (CT) domain formed by two α-helices and three β-strands (Supplementary Fig. S6C). The N-terminal helix of Gp33 extends away from the rest of its structure and is sandwiched between the β subunit of the RNAP and σA, while its CT contacts the β′ subunit. Analyzed by PDBsum [53], the β-Gp33NT interface spans ~300 Å2, including one salt bridge between βE1111–Gp33H14, 4 hydrogen bonds, and 16 non-bonded contacts. The Gp33NT also mediates interaction with σA, primarily through binding to region 4 of σA, which is responsible for recognition of the −35 region of the promoter sequence, with a buried surface area of 730 Å². This interface contains 2 hydrogen bonds between σAY296 and Gp33T15 and 68 non-bonded contacts (Fig. 5D). In contrast, the β′-Gp33 interaction occurs at the Gp33CT region of Gp33, and has a much larger interface of 1 400 Å2, including 2 salt bridges between β’D273 and Gp33K49, and β’K71 and Gp33E24, 5 hydrogen bonds, and 105 non-bonded contacts (Fig. 5E). In parallel, we also attempted to assemble the two complexes by using column-immobilized core RNAP and purified tagless Gp33 and σA. The SDS–PAGE analysis confirmed the formation of RNAP–Gp33–σA and RNAP–Gp33–σA (Supplementary Fig. S6D), in agreement with the in situ assembly results. In summary, the RNAP–Gp33–σA complex is stabilized by two key interactions: (i) β′ binding to the C-terminal domain of Gp33, and (ii) the N-terminal region of Gp33 being sandwiched between the β subunit and σA.

The absence of DNA in the RNAP–Gp33–σA complex suggests that Gp33 likely functions by binding to the holoenzyme to prevent promoter recognition. To validate whether Gp33 affects promoter binding, we performed EMSA assay using the early promoter PE5. In the absence of Gp33, a promoter–RNAP complex was readily detected. However, the addition of Gp33 led to a marked, concentration-dependent reduction in complex formation, accompanied by the reappearance of free DNA bands (Fig. 5F), indicating that Gp33 inhibits formation of the RPc.To determine whether Gp33 can also disrupt an already assembled complex, we allowed the RPc to form before adding Gp33. Under these conditions, EMSA analysis showed that Gp33 had little effect on the pre-formed RPc. Thus, these results suggest that Gp33 prevents promoter complex formation but is unable to destabilize an established closed complex.

Gp33 represents a new class of σA-dependent transcription inhibitor

To further illustrate the effect of Gp33 on RNAP, we compared the RNAP–Gp33–σA complex with the SPX-activated transcription initiation complex [54]. In the RNAP–Gp33–σA structure, the primary channel of RNAP becomes partially occluded by the β subunit due to a conformational change induced by Gp33 (Supplementary Fig. S7A). Similarly, region 2 of σA, which recognizes the −10 element, undergoes substantial rearrangement as a cascade effect of Gp33 binding (Supplementary Fig. S7B). A distortion is also observed in region 4, which interacts with the −35 element (Supplementary Fig. S7C). Together, these conformational changes provides the molecular basis of Gp33 as a σA-dependent inhibitor, showing that Gp33 inhibits transcription by preventing closed RPc formation through structural rearrangements.

We next compared Gp33 with other well-characterized lytic phage transcription inhibitors, including Gp2 and Ocr from T7, AsiA from T4, and P7 from Xp10. Although no phage-encoded transcription inhibitor has yet been identified in Gram-positive bacteria, structural mapping of these phage inhibitors for Gram-negative bacteria onto E. coli RNAP revealed that Gp33 binds to a distinct site compared to its counterparts (Supplementary Fig. S7D). Among these inhibitors, P7 functions as an antitermination factor that occludes the RNA exit channel, thereby preventing the formation of RNA hairpins at intrinsic terminators [55]. AsiA, in contrast, binds to and remodels region 4 of σ70, inducing a conformational change that prevents σ70 from contacting the −35 element. Consequently, AsiA not only inhibits transcription from σ70-dependent early promoters but also activates transcription from middle genes with assistance from other factors [19]. In T7, complete inactivation of host RNAP after early gene expression is essential, as the phage subsequently relies on its own RNAP for middle and late transcription. To achieve this, Ocr functions as a DNA mimic that binds in E. coli RNAP cleft, obstructing sigma factor recruitment [56], and Gp2 binds to the β′ jaw domain of the holoenzyme, inhibiting RPo formation. Distinct from these mechanisms, Gp33 targets the housekeeping σA, inhibiting transcription at an even earlier stage by preventing RPc formation. Therefore, this mode of inhibition distinguishes Gp33 from other previously characterized RNAP inhibitors.

Gp33 does not hinder middle promoter binding

However, since SPO1 still relies on the host RNAP for its middle and late gene transcription, an important question remains: how does SPO1 ensure that Gp33 does not interfere with middle promoter recognition? To address this, we examined whether the smaller phage σ factors involved in middle and late transcription, Gp28 and Gp34, lack the key residues required for Gp33 binding. Sequence alignments revealed that the σA residues responsible for Gp33 interaction (L307–R328; Supplementary Fig. S8A) are largely absent in Gp28 and completely missing in the late σ factor Gp34 (Supplementary Fig. S8B). Structural mapping of these differences onto the RNAP–Gp33–σA complex demonstrated that the helix of σA responsible for Gp33 binding is missing in the middle region of σ factor Gp28, while the entire corresponding domain is absent in Gp34 (Fig. 6A). To determine whether Gp33 interferes with Gp28-dependent promoter binding, we performed EMSA using the middle promoter PM12a. Notably, in the absence of Gp27 and PEG, no RNAP–promoter complex formation was detected (Supplementary Fig. S8C), consistent with the in vitro transcription results. In contrast, inclusion of Gp27 and PEG supported RNAP–promoter complex formation, and under these conditions the addition of Gp33 had no detectable effect on the RNAP–promoter complex (Fig. 6B). These results support the conclusion that Gp33 does not inhibit middle promoter recognition, whereas LLPS is essential for Gp28-mediated recognition of the middle promoter in vitro.

Furthermore, in the RNAP–Gp33 complex, the electron density corresponding to Gp33NT (residues 1–15), which is critical for σA interaction, is absent in the cryo-EM map, suggesting substantial structural flexibility (Fig. 6C). This flexibility implies that, in the absence of σA, Gp33 may not stably engage the β subunit, thereby failing to confine RNAP in the holoenzyme state. To test this hypothesis, we first carried out MD simulations on both the RNAP–Gp33–σA structure and the RNAP–Gp33 complex generated by removing σA from the holoenzyme structure. The simulations showed that RNAP–Gp33–σA remained stably associated throughout the trajectory (Supplementary Video S2). In contrast, in the RNAP–Gp33 complex, Gp33 remained anchored to the β′ subunit via its C-terminal region, whereas the N-terminal region, which initially contacted β, exhibited increased flexibility and failed to maintain stable interactions with β during the 100-ns simulation (Supplementary Video S3 and Fig. 6D). We further constructed an N-terminal truncation mutant, Gp3315-101 and compared its binding affinity to RNAP with that of full-length Gp33. The 1H-NMR spectrum indicated that the mutant remained folded (Fig. 6E), and biolayer interferometry (BLI) measurements showed that both Gp33 and Gp3315-101 bound RNAP with similar affinities (∼200 nM, Fig. 6F). These results suggest that the Gp33 N-terminus does not significantly contribute to RNAP binding in the absence of σA. Taken together, our results support a model in which the Gp33 C-terminus anchors the protein to the β′ subunit of RNAP, positioning its N-terminus to engage σA and inhibit promoter binding.

Discussion

While LLPS has been reported in bacteria, eukaryotes, and eukaryotic viruses as a mechanism to spatially organize transcription and genome regulation, our findings reveal a previously unrecognized strategy: Gp27 from Bacillus phage SPO1 is the first phage protein shown to undergo LLPS. In eukaryotic systems, LLPS is typically driven by proteins containing multivalent interaction domains and IDRs, which promote the formation of dynamic condensates through weak, transient interactions [57–59]. Consistent with this paradigm, structural and functional analyses reveal that the three-domain architecture of Gp27 supports LLPS through self-association, DNA binding, and interacting with RNAP. Importantly, the ability of Gp27 to concentrate RNAP within condensates is particularly important because SPO1 middle and late gene transcription depends on the phage-encoded σ factors Gp28 and Gp34, both of which have weaker promoter-binding activity than the host σA. By forming transcriptionally active condensates, Gp27 provides a favorable environment for the transition to middle-gene transcription. This LLPS-driven mechanism is essential for SPO1 under low MOI, a condition frequently encountered in natural environments. This strategy differs from that described for E. coli transcription, in which phase separation is nonessential and primarily responsive to environmental cues, manifesting as a transition from dispersed to clustered localization as cells enter logarithmic growth in nutrient-rich media. Taken together, this comparison highlights a difference between bacteria and its virus: LLPS can function not merely as an adaptive cellular strategy, but as an essential viral tool to hijack and dominate host molecular resources.

In addition to condensate formation, transcriptional progression is further assisted by Gp33, a selectively transcription inhibitor whose expression depends on LLPS. Gp33 bridges the β, β′, and σA subunits of RNAP: its N-terminal helix inserts between the β subunit and σA region 4, while its C-terminal region engages extensively with β′, thereby locking the RNAP holoenzyme in an inactive conformation. As a result, Gp33 acts at an early stage of transcription initiation by remodeling holoenzyme architecture and blocking promoter engagement. In bacterial cells, ~2%–50% of RNAP is associated with σA, depending on the growth stage and physiological conditions [60]. Therefore, even in the presence of Gp33-mediated inhibition, a pool of free core RNAP is expected to remain available for subsequent recruitment by phage-encoded factors during infection. Importantly, this strategy allows Gp33 to selectively inhibit host σA-dependent transcription while sparing transcription driven by phage-encoded σ factors at later stages, thereby contributing to temporal control of SPO1 gene expression. Together, these findings establish Gp33 as the first phage-encoded transcription inhibitor in a Gram-positive host to be characterized at both structural and mechanistic levels. Furthermore, Gp33 complements Gp27 by repressing transcription from strong σA-dependent early phage promoters, while Gp27-driven LLPS concentrates the transcriptional machinery to promote middle phage gene transcription that is recognized by phage-encoded σ factors unable to directly compete with σA.

While the essentiality of both genes further supports their synergistic roles in ensuring efficient and properly timed SPO1 transcriptional transitions, LLPS-deficient Gp27 mutants and inhibition-defective Gp33 mutants are likely not viable, thereby limiting in vivo genetic approaches. Nevertheless, using the currently available experimental tools, our findings establish LLPS as a previously unrecognized regulatory paradigm in bacteriophage biology and identify Gp27 as the first phage-encoded transcriptional regulator shown to undergo phase separation. Although phylogenetic analysis indicates that Gp27-like proteins are currently confined to SPO1-related phages infecting Gram-positive bacteria (Supplementary Fig. S9), this restricted conservation likely reflects limited functional exploration rather than rarity. Indeed, several well-studied phages, such as T4 and SPP1 [61], are known to encode proteins containing IDRs. And our analyses of several recently described jumbo phages and dsRNA phages identify multiple proteins with predicted LLPS potential (Supplementary Table S7), raising the possibility that phase separation is a widespread but underexplored strategy for spatial organization during phage infection. Given the frequent occurrence of ID proteins in phage genomes, LLPS may constitute a general principle by which phages reprogram host cellular processes. In this context, LLPS inhibitors such as Hex, combined with RT-qPCR-based transcriptional profiling, provide a powerful framework for systematically identifying additional phage-encoded LLPS regulators that are essential for productive infection.
