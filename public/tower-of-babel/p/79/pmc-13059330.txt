Ebstein’s anomaly with life-threatening arrhythmia: an unusual cardiovascular manifestation of Williams syndrome – a case report

Background

Williams syndrome (WS), also known as Williams-Beuren syndrome, is a multisystem disorder affecting the cardiovascular system alongside development, personality, facies, growth, connective tissue, and endocrine system [1–3]. It is a relatively rare disorder that occurs in approximately 1:7,500 individuals and is caused by a 7q11.23 microdeletion [1, 4]. The resulting haploinsufficiency of the elastin gene (ELN) results in an arteriopathy, which is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in WS patients [1]. Overall, cardiovascular abnormalities occur in 80% of patients with WS, with supravalvular aortic stenosis (SVAS) or pulmonary arterial stenosis (PAS) being the most common congenital heart defects reported, followed by aortic coarctation and mitral valve prolapse [1, 5].

Ebstein’s anomaly (EA) is a rare congenital cardiac defect (0.3–0.6% of all congenital heart defects), occurring sporadically in most cases. However, some genetic associations, such as copy number variants (CNV) 1p36 and 8p23.1 and single nucleotide variants in MYH7 and NKX2.5 genes, have been described [6–9]. EA is an atypical cardiovascular defect in patients with WS. Previously, only two cases with this association have been reported [10, 11].

Here, we describe a third case of concurrent WS and EA. The occurrence of out-of -hospital cardiac arrest due to ventricular arrhythmias originating from the Purkinje system represents a highly unusual presentation of these two rare conditions.

Case presentation

We describe the case of an 11-year-old patient with both WS and an EA. Prenatal ultrasound screening had revealed a severe EA without signs of cardiac decompensation. The child was born at a gestational age of 39 weeks in good clinical condition despite cyanosis and a saturation of 83%. Cardiac examination revealed a systolic murmur graded 2–3/6, without signs of right heart failure. Echocardiography showed moderate tricuspid valve regurgitation with severe displacement of the tricuspid valve towards the apex of the right ventricle, confirming the diagnosis of EA type C [12]. The atrialized portion of the ventricular septum was markedly dyskinetic and lax. Additionally, slightly thickened pulmonary valve leaflets were observed. Electrocardiography (ECG) showed a regular sinus rhythm of 170 beats per minute, PR interval of 0.2 s and a right bundle branch block (RBBB).

Dysmorphic features were characteristic of WS, which was confirmed by genetic testing with a SNP-array, showing a de novo 7q11.23 deletion (arr[GRCh37] 7q11.23(72,718,277 − 74,142,190)x1 de novo). An additional interstitial deletion of chromosome 4q13.3 was found, which was inherited from a healthy parent.

During his first year, the patient showed mild supravalvular and peripheral PAS on echocardiography and angiography. Echocardiography during follow-up displayed moderate tricuspid valve regurgitation, good left and right ventricular function, intact atrial septum, and the markedly dyskinetic and lax atrialised portion of the ventricular septum (Supplemental Fig. 1). Follow-up ECGs showed sinus rhythm with prolonged PR interval around 0.2 s, an RBBB pattern with a very fragmented QRS complex and QRS duration of 0.17 s, without ventricular pre-excitation (Fig. 1).

ECG made 1 year prior to the out-of-hospital cardiac arrest showing a sinus rhythm with a prolonged PR interval (0.2 s), an RBBB pattern, and a fragmented QRS complex with duration of 0.17 s, without ventricular pre-excitation

The cardiac follow-up remained uneventful without symptoms of heart failure, cyanosis, or palpitations until the age of 11, when the patient experienced an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest and was successfully resuscitated in the home-setting by a family member. On arrival in the local hospital, ECGs showed polymorphic premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) in bigeminy and frequent runs of non-sustained polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (Fig. 2). Ventricular arrhythmias were well controlled with intravenous betablocker (esmolol). Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) subsequently showed a slightly dilated left ventricle with global hypokinesia (ejection fraction 34%) and a dyskinetic and lax perimembranous interventricular septum with dyskinesia of the atrialized basal part. Late gadolinium enhancement imaging and T1 mapping indicated associated subepicardial myocardial fibrosis (Fig. 3, Video 1). Measurements on MRI showed a right ventricle volume of 135 ml, end-systolic volume of 96 ml, ejection fraction of 29%, cardiac output of 3.01 L/min*m2, and an end-diastolic and end-systolic volume by body-surface area of 128 ml/m2 and 91 ml/m2, respectively. To identify the origin of the ventricular arrhythmias, an electrophysiological study with 3D electroanatomical mapping (including a voltage map (CARTO 3, Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA)) was performed, demonstrating that the PVCs originated from different fibrosed areas of the Purkinje system, corresponding to the dyskinetic portion of the ventricular septum on echocardiogram and MRI. As the ventricular contractions were polymorphic and the area of the fibrosed Purkinje system spanned both anterior and posterior fascicles, no clear ablation target was identified.

Additional whole exome sequencing was performed on DNA from the patient and both parents. MOON (Diploid) and Franklin (Genoox) software were used for interpretation of variants using Human Phenotype Ontology terms: “abnormality of the cardiovascular system morphology” (HP:0030680) and “ventricular arrhythmia” (HP:0004308), followed by analysis of variants in genes associated with congenital heart defects, cardiomyopathy, and pediatric or syndromic forms of cardiomyopathy (gene panels: https://www.lumc.nl/over-het-lumc/afdelingen/klinische-genetica/genpanels/ and https://panelapp.genomicsengland.co.uk/panels/749/). Neither analysis yielded any (likely) pathogenic variants.

Ventricular arrhythmias were eventually successfully controlled with oral betablocker therapy, and a submuscular abdominal implantable cardioverter-defibrillator was implanted.

Discussion

Here, we describe a third reported case of Ebstein’s anomaly in the setting of Williams syndrome, complicated by an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest due to ventricular arrhythmia originating from diseased parts of the Purkinje system.

The hallmark of EA is the inferior or apical displacement of the tricuspid valve away from the atrioventricular junction [13]. In its most severe form, this displacement can extend into the outflow tract of the right ventricle. Secondary to this valve displacement, part of the right ventricle becomes atrialized, leading to functional right ventricular hypoplasia [13, 14]. EA accounts for 0.3–0.5% of congenital heart defects, with mortality rates up to 27% in fetuses and approximately 18% in neonates. Late effects include heart failure in 40% of adults caused by right ventricle dysfunction or worsening hemodynamics due to recurrent atrial arrhythmias [15, 16]. In unoperated cases, the 20-year survival is estimated at 41% in the adult population [17].

Patients with EA are at significant lifetime risk for sudden death (SD) from malignant ventricular arrhythmias. In a large single-center EA cohort (n = 968), the 10-, 50-, and 70- year cumulative incidences of SD from birth were 0.8%, 8.3% and 14.6%, respectively [18]. The dilatation of the right atrium, thinning of the arterialized right ventricle, and fibrosis of the ventricles can underly the ventricular arrythmias in EA, although these arrythmias are rare in patients with EA [19]. In our case, the ventricular arrythmia were attributable to a fibrosed Purkinje system in the interventricular septum.

The etiology of EA is not fully elucidated. Although the majority of EA cases are not inherited, pathogenic variants in MYH7 and NKX2.5 have been identified in familial cases [7–9, 15]. Rare CNV’s can be found in almost half of isolated EA cases, and up to 50% of those CNV’s overlap or are in close proximity to known cardiac development genes [20, 21].

Congenital defects of the cardiovascular system are common in WS patients, and many cardiovascular features can be partially explained by elastin defects. The gene encoding elastin, ELN, is located in the Williams-Beuren syndrome critical region, and deletion results in haploinsufficiency. The most common manifestations of elastin arteriopathy are SVAS and PAS, which occur in 45 to 65% and 37 to 45% of WS patients, respectively [5, 22, 23]. In our patient, mild peripheral PAS on was seen on echocardiography during his first year. Other structural cardiac anomalies include ventricular septal defects (7.9–21%), mitral valve abnormalities (19%), valvular pulmonary stenosis (9–16%), and coronary artery stenosis (10–30%) [1, 5, 22, 23].

As far as we can ascertain in the available literature, genetic alterations of the ELN gene have not been directly linked with EA or similar fibrosis of the interventricular septum resulting in Purkinje fiber abnormalities. However, elastin is a key component of the extracellular matrix (ECM) in the heart and vessels, most explicitly in the heart valves [24–27]. The loss of elastin in porcine anterior tricuspid valve leaflets reduces the compliance, thereby affecting their biomechanical properties, that are highly attributable to the ECM components [28]. Additionally, elastin fibers along with collagen fibers are closely interweaved with the Purkinje cells in porcine hearts [26]. The precise role of interstitial collagen and elastin fibers in the organization of subendocardial and intramural Purkinje fibers remains incompletely understood. We hypothesize that elastin haploinsufficiency may alter ECM structure and, in turn, influence the organization and function of Purkinje fibers, as well as the development of the interventricular septal matrix surrounding these fibers and the tricuspid valve.

Conclusion

Our case report adds further evidence to the limited literature describing congenital cardiac anomalies and associated complications in patients with WS. Ventricular arrhythmia and out-of-hospital cardiac arrest constitute an unusual clinical presentation that has not previously been reported in cases with concurrent WS and EA, but may be attributed to elastin haploinsufficiency. This potentially fatal complication underscores the importance of vigilant clinical surveillance in patients with WS and comparable congenital heart defects.
