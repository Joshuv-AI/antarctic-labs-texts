Predicting overall survival in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma using 18F-FDG PET/CT radiomics: a two-center study​
Authors: Yang Xu, Yunmei Shi, Tao Jiang, Qingxia Wu, Ren Lang, Yuetao Wang, Min-Fu Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate prognostic prediction in pancreatic cancer is of paramount clinical importance for patient management. However, a reliable and non-invasive method for preoperatively predicting overall survival (OS) remains a significant unmet need. We aim to evaluate the utility of pre-operative 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) positron emission tomography (PET)/ computed tomography (CT)-derived signatures in predicting OS in patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC).ResultsThis study included 109 patients (70 males, 39 females). Over a median follow-up period of 28 months (range, 1-64 months), 68 patients (62.4%) died (median OS, 15 months). As stated above, a series of models was established using selected radiomics features and clinical factors. In the test set, the C-index of the clinical, CT-based, PET-based, PET/CT-based, and integrated model were 0.586, 0.724, 0.730, 0.730, and 0.743, respectively. The PET-based model (P = 0.027) significantly outperformed the clinical model, unlike the CT-based model (P = 0.057); further feature integration did not improve performance. ConclusionIn this study, 18F-FDG PET/CT radiomics features showed promise in predicting OS in patients with PDAC, suggesting potential as a tool for personalized management and warranting large-scale studies to confirm its applicability in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13550-026-01399-8.Keywords: Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, Survival analysis, 18F-FDG PET/CT, Radiomics

Background

Pancreatic cancer (PC) is a recalcitrant malignancy with a poor prognosis and high mortality rate, standing in stark contrast to the declining mortality rates seen in most other malignancies thanks to recent therapeutic advances [1, 2]. This discouraging prospect is attributable to its elusive nature, characterized by metastasis in most patients (> 80%) at initial diagnosis [1], compounded by profound therapeutic resistance conferred by its characteristically dense desmoplastic stroma and highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [3, 4].

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) accounts for 90% of PCs and is this cancer’s predominant histological subtype [5]. Its 5-year survival rate is < 12% even after successful resection [1, 6]. Therefore, surgery must be carefully deliberated to avoid performing a high-risk procedure with minimal benefit for patients with limited life expectancy [7, 8]. At the same time, against the backdrop of multiple novel therapeutic developments in PDAC [9, 10], there is a pressing need for survival outcome assessment tools at diagnosis to stratify patients for clinical trials and further discern those who may derive benefit, thereby paving the way for future precision medicine. However, accurate survival prediction has consistently posed a challenge in clinical practice.

Currently available prognostic tools are substantially limited in accuracy and clinical applicability due to their inherently indirect nature. Several simple prognostic scores based on routine clinical parameters, such as the Glasgow Prognostic Score [11, 12] and Naples Prognostic Score [13], can assess the prognoses of PDAC patients to a certain degree, but they fundamentally rely on inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and albumin (Alb) that merely reflect systemic inflammatory status and nutritional compromise. Even more—recently developed scores -e.g., the Heidelberg Prognostic Pancreatic Cancer (HELPP)-score [14], which incorporates tumor biomarkers including carbohydrate antigen 19–9 (CA19-9) and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), still fail to directly capture the actual biological heterogeneity and metabolic characteristics of the tumor itself.

These limitations have spurred growing interest in advanced imaging techniques that enable more direct and comprehensive tumor characterization. Preoperative evaluation of PDAC has evolved considerably since the turn of this century, driven by the integration of techniques such as positron emission tomography (PET/)/computed tomography (CT) into routine clinical practice [15, 16]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) PET/CT, which reflects leisions’ metabolic activity, offers greater diagnostic sensitivity and accuracy in PDAC than conventional CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [15, 17–21]. Further research has shown that baseline PET parameters such as standardized uptake value (SUV) are significantly correlated with both overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival, enabling prediction of PDAC recurrence and prognosis [22–24]. However, conventional PET metrics provide information on global tumor metabolism only and fail to capture the details of intratumoral metabolic heterogeneity.

The emergence of radiomics has opened the door to circumventing this barrier. Previous studies have demonstrated the feasibility of predicting patient survival based on CT or MRI [25–29]. Yao et al., developed and validated a prognostic model based on dynamic contrast-enhanced CT for predicting outcomes in PDAC patients, achieving a Harell’s concordance index (C‐index) of 0.64 [30]. While 18F-FDG PET/CT-based radiomics studies have been explored for PDAC diagnosis [31], histological grade prediction [32], and treatment response evaluation [33], a significant gap remains in the literature on systematic application of PET/CT-derived radiomics features to predict survival in PDAC patients.

Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the prognostic value of pre-operative 18F-FDG PET/CT-derived radiomics features for OS prediction in PDAC patients and compare their performance with that of conventional clinical parameters.

Methods and materials

Patients

This retrospective two-center study was approved by the ethics committees of both centers (Ethical Committees of Beijing Chaoyang Hospital [Beijing, China]; No. 2023-ke-721; and of the Ethical Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University [Changzhou, China]; No. 2025-ke-016). The requirement to obtain informed consent was waived. We enrolled patients who were admitted to either hospital between January 2018 and December 2023, underwent 18F-FDG PET/CT imaging within two weeks prior to surgery, and had a postoperative pathological diagnosis confirming PDAC. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) poor image quality not meeting research requirements, (2) patient received neoadjuvant radiotherapy or other treatments for pancreatic space-occupying lesion before 18F-FDG PET/CT scan, and (3) ​​lost to follow-up after discharge. The whole cohort was randomly divided into training and test sets at an 8:2 ratio.

Clinicopathological information

The appropriate surgical approach was selected per clinical guidelines. Pathological diagnosis based on surgical specimens was performed in accordance with the criteria outlined in the 2010 World Health Organization Classification of Tumors of the Digestive System (4th edition). Final histological grade was categorized into two types: low-grade for moderately or well-differentiated tumors and high-grade for undifferentiated or poorly differentiated ones. Demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, initial symptom, smoking and diabetes history, CA19-9 level, histological grade, and Tumor, Node, Metastasis (TNM) stage were retrieved from institutional electronic medical records. The initial symptom, namely the chief complaint leading to medical consultation, was categorized as abdominal pain, jaundice, or elevated tumor biomarker levels. TNM staging was based on the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer’s Cancer Staging Manual.

Image acquisition

Following a ≥ 4–6-h fast, patients received an intravenous injection of 18F-FDG (3.7 MBq/kg) and were subsequently left to rest in a quiet, dimly lit environment. A whole-body PET/CT scan was acquired from the skull base to the upper thighs 57 ± 14 min after injection. At the first center, 18F-FDG PET/CT imaging was performed using a 16-slice scanner (Discovery STE; GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) per a standard protocol. CT scanning parameters were as follows: 140 kV; 120 mA; pitch, 1.375; collimation, 16 × 0.625 mm; section width, 5 mm; slice thickness, 3 mm. PET acquisition settings included a 192 × 192 matrix and scan times of 2.5 min per bed position for the body and 5 min for the head, performed in three-dimensional (3D) mode. Attenuation-corrected PET images (voxel size, 3.9 × 3.9 × 3.3 mm) were reconstructed from CT data using a 3D ordered-subset expectation maximization (OSEM) algorithm (2 iterations, 14 subsets). At the second center, 18F-FDG PET/CT images were acquired on a Biograph mCT scanner (Siemens Healthcare, Forchheim, Germany). CT parameters were as follows: 140 kV; 64 mA; pitch, 0.8; collimation, 16 × 1.2 mm; section width, 5 mm; slice thickness, 3 mm. PET parameters were as follows: matrix, 200 × 200; 2.5 min/bed from the skull base to the upper thighs; 5 min/bed for the head in 3D mode. Images were smoothed using a full-width Gaussian filter at half-maximum value of 2 mm. Attenuation-corrected PET images were reconstructed from the CT data using a 3D OSEM algorithm (2 iterations, 14 subsets).

Tumor segmentation and feature extraction

Using uAI Research Portal (uRP) software version 1.1 (Shanghai United Imaging Intelligence, Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), two nuclear-medicine physicians (Y.X. and Y.M.S.) independently manually delineated the primary tumor regions of interest (ROIs) layer by layer on both PET and CT images. Intra- and interobserver variability was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). One month after initial ROI delineation, 30 randomly selected patients were re-delineated by the same physicians, and the features were compared via ICC. In cases where metabolic alterations within a lesion were indistinct on PET, the lesion contour was defined using the corresponding CT image for guidance. Radiomics features were automatically extracted from each ROI on the platform, including first order; shape; gray-level run length matrix (GLRLM); gray-level size zone matrix (GLSZM); gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM); neighboring gray-tone difference matrix (NGTDM); gray-level dependence matrix (GLDM); and the resulting features based on transformation, which mainly included wavelet, Laplacian of Gaussian, and other types of filters. Image discretization was performed using a fixed bin width of 1 SUV for PET images and 25 Hounsfield units (HU) for CT images. To ensure model stability, we assessed feature reliability via ICC; only features with an ICC > 0.75, indicating high intra- and interobserver agreement in tumor segmentation, were retained for subsequent modeling. Conventional PET parameters including SUV, metabolic tumor volume (MTV), and total lesion glycolysis (TLG) were also extracted and dichotomized using optimal cutoff values determined by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.

Feature selection and establishment of the model

Radiomics features extracted from PET and CT images underwent Z-score normalization prior to modeling. Features were selected via a two-step approach: (1) univariate Cox regression, and (2) least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, applied independently to PET and CT feature sets. Features with absolute coefficient values (ACVs) > 0.09 were retained. Selected PET and CT features were then combined and subjected to an additional LASSO selection step. Clinical variables and conventional PET parameters underwent separate LASSO-based selection. Five prognostic models were constructed using Cox proportional-hazards regression: (a) clinical model (clinical variables and conventional PET parameters), (b) CT-based radiomics model, (c) PET-based radiomics model, (d) PET/CT–based radiomics model (combined imaging features), and (e) integrated model (all selected features). The C-index was used to evaluate model performance in the test set, with comparative analysis performed against the clinical model.

Follow-up

The primary endpoint of this study was OS, defined as time from PET/CT scan to death from tumor progression or recurrence or the last follow-up assessment, with July 31, 2024 as the cutoff date. Follow-up data in patient medical records were reviewed.

Statistical analysis

All calculations were conducted using SPSS version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software version 4.5.1 (https://www.r-project.org). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range depending on data distribution. Differences in patient characteristics between the training and validation sets were assessed using independent-sample t tests, Mann–Whitney U tests, and χ2 tests. Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed to evaluate clinicopathological information and PET parameters to select potential predictors of OS, and multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed to determine independent predictors of OS. Statistical comparison of C-indices among different models was conducted using the survcomp packages in R. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinicopathological characteristics

A total of 109 patients from two centers were ultimately included in this retrospective study. Figure 1 presents the flowchart of the patient selection process. This cohort included 70 men and 39 women with a mean age of 65.1 ± 9.9 years. A total of 87 patients were assigned to the training set and 22 to the test set. Median follow-up duration was 28 months (range, 1–64 months). For included patients, median survival time was 15 months, and 68 patients (62.4%) were deceased at time of analysis. Prognostic factors analysis

Conventional PET parameters were dichotomized using optimal cutoff values from ROC analysis. Univariate Cox regression analysis identified CA19-9 (P = 0.021), T (P < 0.001), N (P = 0.006), TNM (P < 0.001), MTV (P = 0.001), and TLG (P = 0.004) as significant prognostic factors. In multivariate Cox regression analysis, only TNM staging remained a significant independent predictor for OS (P = 0.013; Table 2).

PET/CT radiomics feature extraction and selection

The PET/CT radiomics workflow is shown in Fig. 2. For each patient, a total of 2288 features from CT images and 2297 from PET images were extracted automatically by uRP. Of these features, 2241 from CT images and 2214 from PET images exhibited exceptional robustness and reproducibility, with ICCs exceeding the threshold of 0.75. Following initial screening using univariate Cox regression analysis, the numbers of features extracted from PET and CT images were reduced to 1392 and 1257, respectively. Subsequent feature selection via LASSO regression identified 11 PET-derived and 17 CT-derived features. Of these, five PET features and four CT features with ACVs > 0.09 were retained for subsequent establishment of PET- and CT-based models, respectively. Combined analysis of these nine features yielded eight features for the PET/CT model. Finally, to construct an integrated model, the aforementioned five PET features and four CT features, plus six clinical parameters identified via univariate Cox regression analysis, were pooled together. A final LASSO regression analysis on this combined dataset yielded 10 features with ACVs > 0.09, which were subsequently used to build the integrated model. Performance of the radiomics model

Five prognostic models were developed: clinical, PET-based, CT-based, PET/CT–based, and integrated. The results showed that in the test set, the C-indices of the clinical, PET, CT, PET/CT, and integrated models were 0.586, 0.730, 0.724, 0.730, and 0.743, respectively. Detailed metrics for each model are provided in Table 4. Figure 3 shows the Kaplan–Meier curves of the five models in the test set. The results demonstrated that, compared to the clinical model, the other four models exhibited superior discriminative ability in predicting prognosis in PDAC. Figure 4 shows representative images of PDAC patients with two different outcomes. Moreover, calibration curves of the CT- and PET-based models showed good agreement between predicted and observed survival outcomes (Fig. S1). Decision curve analysis of the four nonclinical models demonstrated greater clinical net benefit than treat-all or treat-none strategies (Fig. S2).

Discussion

Prognostic stratification in PDAC is crucial for personalized treatment but remains challenging. Conventional imaging and routine clinical information provide limited prognostic insight, necessitating more advanced methods. Radiomics addresses this by decoding subvisual tumor characteristics through high-throughput feature extraction from medical images. In this study, we developed and validated 18F-FDG PET/CT-based radiomics models for predicting OS in PDAC. Importantly, PET-based features added complementary information to conventional clinical factors, holding promise as complementary tools for improving survival prediction. Our results affirmed PET/CT radiomics as a powerful noninvasive tool for improving the accuracy of prognostic prediction and further guiding personalized therapy.

Neoplastic diseases are a significant threat to human health. Over the years, considerable efforts have been made to accurately predict survival in cancer patients using various methodologies. The Glasgow Prognostic score, which is based on serum CRP and Alb levels, and its derivatives [11, 12] have been widely applied in prognostic evaluation of patients with cancers of the pancreas [11], colon/rectum [34], esophagus [35], lung [36, 37], and other sites [38]. However, researchers have gradually come to recognize that relying solely on inflammatory and nutritional markers might not sufficiently reflect the biological nature of tumors. In the case of PC, its highly invasive biological characteristics contribute to widespread cachexia in patients, which further amplifies the effects of these markers [39, 40]. Although the subsequent HELPP score subsequently proposed by a German team [14] incorporated additional clinical information, including tumor biomarkers, it remained limited by a lack of direct information on the tumor itself, ultimately failing to effectively identify high-risk patients [41].

Medical imaging provides a unique window into the lesion and its surrounding environment. Anatomical imaging modalities, such as CT and MRI, excel at delineating the tumor’s structure and spatial relationship to adjacent structures. In contrast, 18F-FDG PET/CT adds a functional dimension by characterizing the metabolic activity of the tumor, offering complementary insights into its biological behavior. The prognostic value of medical imaging in PC has been extensively investigated, yet the evidence remains mixed across various modalities. For example, Koch et al. found that diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) alone did not predict OS in PDAC patients [28], whereas Chen et al. identified the apparent diffusion coefficient value derived from DWI as an independent prognostic factor [42]. In comparison, quantitative parameters derived from 18F-FDG PET/CT have demonstrated a higher degree of consistency in predicting outcomes. Lee et al. found that SUVmax and MTV have certain prognostic value in predicting patient outcomes [22], which was further confirmed by Yoo et al. [43] and a study conducted in the UK [44], which identified MTV and TLG as independent prognostic factors for OS. These findings align with our findings: in univariate analysis, metabolic and volumetric parameters such as MTV and TLG were significantly correlated with OS, with higher values linked to shorter OS. However, in multivariate analysis, these PET parameters were not statistically significant, with only TNM staging emerging as an independent predictor of OS. We propose that the observed limitations of these PET parameters might be due to their not fully capturing detailed lesion characteristics such as tumor heterogeneity, necrosis, invasiveness, or cellular proliferation [31, 43]. Consequently, more advanced methods are required for a more thorough lesion analysis.

The advent of radiomics has, to a certain extent, provided a solution to this challenge. Several studies have employed this technique to investigate intratumoral heterogeneity in PDAC, yielding promising outcomes. Park et al. conducted radiomics analysis based on contrast-enhanced CT images and developed a model to predict OS, achieving a final C-index of 0.732 [26]. Similarly, Qiu et al. [45] used T2-weighted images to build a model for OS prediction with an area under the curve of 0.77, which improved to a C-index of 0.79 after incorporation clinical features. Numerous 18F-FDG PET/CT-based radiomics studies have been conducted, focusing on prediction of histological grade [32], treatment efficacy [33], and even molecular characteristics [46] in PDAC. However, research specifically aimed at predicting OS in PDAC using PET/CT-based radiomics remains relatively sparse. Cui et al. identified PET/CT-based radiomics features describing diverse tumor phenotypes as independent predictors of OS [47]. Similarly, a research team from South Korea also extracted radiomics features from the 18F-FDG PET/CT scans of 140 PDAC patients and developed a Rad-score to predict OS, achieving a C-index of 0.681 [48]. Inspired by these findings, we carried out this study, and the results were consistent with our expectations. While previous studies have provided valuable insights [48], our approach differed in that we did not use Rad-score, instead focusing on the individual patient, to capture richer, more-nuanced prognostic information. This strategy was supported by emerging evidence, such as the significant correlation between PET/CT radiomics features and monocarboxylate transporter 4 (MCT4) gene expression in PDAC [46], which suggests that different features might represent distinct biological mechanisms. This highlights the importance of avoiding blind linear fusion of features. Although our models might sacrifice some clinical practicality compared to Rad-score [49], this study served as an exploratory effort to uncover the highest-precision predictive model and to investigate the unique role of each feature. Our results also confirmed that, even with a smaller patient cohort, the C-index of our model outperformed that of the previously reported Rad-score model. Of course, we acknowledge the clinical advantage of Rad-score for implementation, and our future work will focus on translating the outputs of our model into a clinically user-friendly format.

We further compared four models (PET-based, CT-based, PET/CT-based, and integrated) against a clinical model. The results demonstrated that the PET-based model achieved a significantly higher C-index in predicting OS in PDAC patients, indicating superior predictive performance. Although the CT-based model showed a numerical improvement over the clinical model, the difference was not statistically significant. Notably, integration of PET features with either CT or clinical features did not yield a statistically significant enhancement in the C-index of the combined model; moreover, a decline in calibration accuracy, reflected by integrated calibration index and calibration index at 50, was observed. Subsequent direct comparison of these models revealed no statistically significant differences in their C-index, indicating comparable performance. This suggested that simply combining image or clinical features did not lead to a significant improvement in predictive performance and failed to yield any synergistic enhancement, a finding consistent with previous studies [49–51].

Collectively, these findings support the role of PET-derived metabolic information as an independent prognostic factor in PDAC, offering biological insights into tumor metabolic activity [52]. However, its simple integration with other features may be hampered by informational redundancy or model overfitting, potentially obscuring its standalone predictive utility [50]. From a practical standpoint, this suggests that future research efforts could be more efficiently directed toward refining and validating PET-derived biomarkers, rather than pursuing complex multi-modal integrations that may add little incremental value.

This interpretation was further supported by an intriguing observation in our feature selection: first-order features were predominantly derived from PET images, whereas texture features were mainly selected from CT images. This divergence stemmed from the distinct biological and physical nature of the two imaging modalities. For CT images, pixel values directly correspond to tissue physical density, highlighting the value of second-order texture features for describing spatial heterogeneity and precisely quantifying internal tumor structure [53]. Conversely, PET images, with their functional imaging characteristics and complex reconstruction process, introduce more sources of variation. Therefore, first-order features, due to their high robustness and well-established clinical interpretability, offer a distinct advantage [54]. These characteristics provide a fundamental guarantee of the stability and reliability of our multicenter study results. Integrating PET and CT information provides more comprehensive characterization of lesions from both the functional and structural perspectives. Our results confirmed that, although the difference was not statistically significant, the model’s C-index improved modestly after PET- and CT-derived radiomics features were combined. Given that MRI offers more detailed visualization of tissue composition, it is reasonable to hypothesize that PET/MRI, which combines functional imaging with superior soft-tissue resolution, represents a highly promising avenue for future research in this field [55].

This study had several additional limitations. First, this was a retrospective study with a relatively small sample size and no genuine external validation. Given these constraints, the model’s performance estimates are exploratory, and its potential for clinical translation remains highly preliminary. Moreover, despite the use of LASSO regularization to mitigate overfitting, the small absolute size of the test set necessitates that the reported performance metrics, like C-index, should be viewed as preliminary estimates whose generalizability requires confirmation in larger, independent cohorts. Another trade-off in this study was the decision not to restrict post-surgical treatments to maximize the cohort size, which introduced heterogeneity in adjuvant therapy as a potential confounding factor for survival outcomes. On the other hand, by including patients from two independent centers, we enhanced the sample diversity and mitigated single-center bias, which supports the preliminary robustness of the model, while not directly addressing external generalizability. As mentioned earlier, this study was exploratory in nature; and such retrospective analyses are necessary to lay the groundwork for future prospective studies. Secondly, although manual delineation of ROIs by experienced physicians can more accurately depict the lesion, this approach can introduce interobserver variability. To minimize this effect, we performed ICC analysis and retained only robust features with high consistency for subsequent modeling. Finally, we acknowledge that inter-center differences in imaging equipment and protocols constitute a recognized limitation, as they may affect feature stability and model performance. The decision to forgo techniques like ComBat was based on concerns regarding model stability in small samples, the preservation of biologically relevant feature correlations, and the robustness of parameter estimation with non-normally distributed features.

Looking ahead, large-scale, prospective, multicenter studies are essential to validate our findings in more controlled cohorts. Furthermore, integrating radiomics with complementary multi-omics data is critically needed to uncover the underlying biological mechanisms driving the image-based predictions and achieve a deeper mechanistic understanding. Concurrently, the development of fully or semi-automated segmentation algorithms will be crucial to eliminating delineation variability and enhancing the models’ clinical translatability. Although not every patient will benefit immediately, it would be short-sighted to disregard the transformative potential of radiomics in personalized OS assessment for PC. This study serves as an important step toward this goal.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study comprehensively investigated the value of radiomics features derived from 18F-FDG PET/CT in predicting OS in patients with PDAC and established a series of radiomics models with favorable performance, highlighting the potential of radiomics as an effective tool for noninvasive preoperative assessment of OS in PDAC patients. Nevertheless, further validation in larger, prospective, and multi-institutional cohorts that incorporate batch-effect harmonization is essential to facilitate clinical translation.
