Bioethanol from Lignocellulosic Feedstock: with Different Co-Cultivation Strategies
Authors: Zahra Fathi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe continuous rise in crude oil prices, coupled with environmental concerns surrounding greenhouse gas emissions, has spurred growing interest in bioethanol as a renewable fuel alternative. Lignocellulosic biomass—such as straw, softwood, sugarcane bagasse, corncob, fruit waste, and algal sources—has emerged as a promising feedstock due to its abundance, low cost, and rich carbohydrate composition. Efficient and economical production of bioethanol from such materials requires optimized pretreatment methods, minimized process costs, and the application of high-yield or genetically engineered microbial strains. Pretreatment techniques range from enzymatic and chemical (acidic or alkaline) to physical methods like autoclaving and ultrasonication, with microbial enzymes from fungi and bacteria offering eco-friendly alternatives. Co-culturing fermentative yeasts such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae with cellulase- and xylanase-producing fungi like Aspergillus, Trichoderma, and Pichia can enhance sugar release and ethanol yield through synergistic enzymatic activity. Effective co-cultivation depends on microbial compatibility and precise control of fermentation conditions. This review explores the impact of various co-culture strategies—particularly involving S. cerevisiae and cellulolytic or xylose-fermenting microorganisms—on bioethanol production from diverse lignocellulosic substrates.Keywords: Aspergillus niger, Bioethanol, Co-cultivation, Lignocellulosic feedstock, Pichia stipites, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Trichoderma reesei

1. Background

The continuous rise in crude oil prices, along with growing international concern over the environmental impact of fossil fuels such as gasoline and diesel, has significantly boosted interest in renewable and eco-friendly fuel sources. Over the years, researchers have focused extensively on converting ligno-cellulosic biomass—such as agricultural residues, forest byproducts, and municipal organic waste—into sustainable alternatives including bioethanol, biodiesel, biogas, biomethane, biohydrogen, and biobutanol. This shift toward renewable energy solutions is driven largely by environmental priorities and the pressing demand for cost-effective substitutes for fossil-based energy ( 1 - 7 ).

Among the many types of lignocellulosic feedstocks, agricultural byproducts, forestry residues, and muni-cipal organic waste have drawn significant research interest because of their rich carbohydrate composition, widespread availability, and cost-effectiveness. In particular, agricultural residues are considered highly promising in regions with large-scale farming activities, as they offer a plentiful, low-cost, and carbohydrate-dense resource, making them especially suitable for biofuel generation ( 8 ).

Lignocellulosic biomass is recognized as a highly valuable feedstock for bioethanol production, primarily because of its rich carbohydrate content. Sources such as agricultural waste, forest-derived residues, and municipal solid waste are not only abundantly available on a global scale but also economically feasible for industrial use. Notably, bioethanol produced from lignocellulosic materials offers substantial environmental advantages, including an estimated 85% reduction in greenhouse gas emissions compared to conventional gasoline ( 9 , 10 ).

On a global scale, the availability of lignocellulosic biomass reaches approximately 1.5 billion dry tons annually. This diverse biomass pool includes agricultural residues such as corn stover, rice straw, wheat straw, sugarcane bagasse, and sorghum stalks; forest-derived materials from species like poplar, pine, bamboo, and aspen; as well as dedicated energy crops like Miscanthus giganteus, elephant grass, and switchgrass ( 11 - 13 ). Despite this vast potential, a large portion of agricultural biomass remains underutilized. For example, rice straw alone accumulates globally at a rate of 600 to 900 million tons per year, yet most of it is either burned or discarded, with only small amounts repurposed as livestock feed ( 11 ). Similarly, in the United States, although annual production of corn stover ranges from 6.4 to 139 million tons, only 1–5% is collected for industrial processing or animal feed, with the majority left unused or incinerated ( 14 ).

Although lignocellulosic bioethanol offers several environmental and resource-based advantages, its economic viability is still limited by key challenges. These include elevated costs associated with transportation and material handling, the technical complexity of pretreatment and enzymatic hydrolysis steps, and the relatively low efficiency of microbial fermentation. Addressing these limitations requires significant improvements in pretreatment methods, optimization of the overall bioconversion process, and the use of genetically engineered or naturally high-performing microbial strains ( 15 ).

The process of producing bioethanol from ligno-cellulosic biomass generally involves four essential stages: pretreatment, enzymatic hydrolysis, fermentation, and distillation. Recent advancements in genetic engineering and enzyme technology have significantly enhanced this process, particularly by improving the ability of Saccharomyces cerevisiae to simultaneously ferment various types of sugars ( 16 , 17 ). However, one major limitation remains: xylose—comprising nearly one-third of the total sugars in lignocellulose—cannot be efficiently utilized by wild-type S. cerevisiae. Although certain recombinant bacterial and fungal strains have been developed to ferment xylose, their overall performance is still constrained by lower ethanol yields and reduced tolerance to ethanol stress, indicating the need for continued optimization ( 18 , 19 ).

Pretreatment plays a vital role in the conversion of lignocellulosic biomass, as it enhances the material’s susceptibility to hydrolysis and subsequent fermentation. A variety of techniques—ranging from enzymatic and chemical (acidic or alkaline) methods to physical processes such as autoclaving, microwave heating, and ultrasonication—are employed to break down cellulose into more accessible fermentable sugars. Ideally, an effective pretreatment approach maximizes sugar recovery while minimizing the generation of inhibitory byproducts ( 20 ). Nevertheless, certain methods can lead to the formation of toxic compounds such as furfural, hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF), acetic acid, and phenolic substances, all of which can adversely affect enzyme performance and fermentation efficiency ( 21 - 23 ).

Given the inability of S. cerevisiae to effectively ferment xylose, research efforts have increasingly turned toward identifying and utilizing microorganisms with a higher capacity for metabolizing this pentose sugar. At the same time, more sustainable pretreatment approaches involving microbial enzymes—particularly those derived from fungi and bacteria—have gained growing interest. Filamentous fungi such as Aspergillus and Trichoderma are widely recognized for their robust production of cellulases and xylanases, which are key enzymes in lignocellulosic biomass degradation ( 24 - 25 ). Recent investigations have shown that co-cultivating enzyme-secreting fungi like Aspergillus Niger and Trichoderma reesei alongside fermentative yeast strains such as S. cerevisiae can significantly boost ethanol production ( 26 , 27 ). These microbial consortia outperform monocultures by enabling synergistic enzymatic actions and more efficient sugar conversion during fermentation ( 28 - 30 ).

Effective microbial co-cultivation hinges on the strategic selection of compatible strains and the careful alignment of environmental conditions such as temperature, pH, and oxygen availability to support the growth and metabolic activity of all participating organisms ( 31 , 32 ). For instance, Zymomonas mobilis exhibits optimal performance at a neutral pH (approximately 7) and elevated temperatures around 37 °C. In contrast, xylose-fermenting yeasts like Pichia stipites and Candida Shehata prefer mildly acidic environments (pH near 5) and lower temperatures, typically around 30 °C. Achieving a suitable balance between these parameters is essential for enhancing the efficiency of ethanol fermentation.

Microbial interactions are central to the effectiveness of co-culture systems, encompassing mechanisms such as substrate competition, metabolic cooperation, cell-to-cell communication, and the secretion of compounds that may either promote or inhibit growth ( 33 ). A thorough understanding of these dynamics is essential to ensuring consistent and efficient fermentation outcomes.

This review highlights recent developments in the production of bioethanol from lignocellulosic biomass, focusing on the co-cultivation of S. cerevisiae with cellulolytic species such as A. niger, T. reesei, and P. stipitis. These microbial partnerships offer notable advantages in enhancing ethanol yields, demonstrating the efficiency of simultaneous cultivation approaches for the effective conversion of a wide range of lignocellulosic feedstocks into sustainable biofuels.

2. Co-Cultivation Methods (Simultaneous Cultivation)

Microbial co-cultivation has gained considerable attention as an effective strategy to boost the generation of fermentable sugars from lignocellulosic biomass. By leveraging the complementary enzymatic and metabolic functions of different microorganisms, this approach enhances overall biomass breakdown efficiency while also reducing the impact of inhibitory byproducts in later processing stages. The synergistic interaction among microbes ultimately leads to improved ethanol yields and better process economics. A well-studied example involves the co-culture of T. reesei and A. niger. While T. reesei is known for producing three key cellulolytic enzymes, it has relatively low β-glucosidase activity—an essential enzyme for converting cellobiose into glucose. On the other hand, A. niger produces high amounts of β-glucosidase but is less efficient at cellulase synthesis overall ( 34 ). When cultivated together, the enzymatic systems of these fungi complement one another, creating a more balanced and efficient enzymatic cocktail for lignocellulose hydrolysis. Numerous studies using various strains of T. reesei and A. niger have confirmed enhanced enzyme production and increased sugar yields from lignocellulosic substrates through this cooperative approach ( 35 ). In addition, mixed microbial cultures offer enhanced metabolic flexibility, allowing for more efficient conversion of complex lignocellulosic substrates into valuable end products. These systems often outperform single-strain setups in terms of substrate utilization and product yield ( 36 , 37 ). This approach proves particularly beneficial when working with biomass rich in xylan—such as sugarcane bagasse—since xylan is a major component of hemicellulose that presents unique challenges ( 38 ). The difficulty arises from the fact that S. cerevisiae, the most commonly used yeast in ethanol fermentation, lacks the ability to metabolize xylose, a key sugar produced through xylan degradation. As a result, considerable research has focused on alternative yeasts like P. stipitis and C. shehatae, which are capable of fermenting xylose effectively ( 39 - 44 ). Despite their ability to ferment xylose, these alternative yeast strains often exhibit lower tolerance to inhibitory substances typically present in unrefined lignocellulosic hydrolysates, which can significantly impair their fermentation efficiency at an industrial level ( 45 , 46 ). Effective enzymatic hydrolysis of cellulose relies on the synergistic activity of three key enzyme groups: endoglucanases, exoglucanases, and β-glucosidases. Each plays a specific role—endoglucanases initiate random cleavage within cellulose chains, exoglucanases progressively remove cellobiose units from the chain ends, and β-glucosidases subsequently convert those units into glucose monomers ( 47 ). While filamentous fungi are recognized as the primary producers of cellulolytic enzymes, several bacterial species and actinomycetes have also been shown to possess cellulase activity. Among fungal genera, Aspergillus, Trichoderma, and yeasts such as Pichia are widely employed in commercial enzyme production, owing to their high output levels and suitability for industrial applications ( 48 , 49 ). In particular, microbial co-cultivation—especially the combination of T. reesei with A. niger—has proven to be an effective method for enhancing enzymatic hydrolysis and increasing the yield of fermentable sugars from lignocellulosic feedstocks. Utilizing the complementary enzymatic capacities of different microbes within a consortium facilitates more thorough biomass breakdown, which in turn improves ethanol production. Additionally, co-culturing cellulase-producing fungi with xylose-fermenting yeasts such as P. stipitis and C. shehatae expands the range of fermentable sugars, especially pentoses like xylose. Despite ongoing limitations due to inhibitory compounds present in pretreated biomass, microbial co-cultures remain a promising pathway toward more sustainable and cost-effective bioethanol production (Fig. 1).

3. Co-Cultivation of A. niger with S. cerevisiae

A. niger is an exceptionally versatile fungal species commonly found in a wide array of agricultural products, such as fruits, corn, onions, and various nuts. Its resilience across a broad spectrum of environmental conditions—including temperatures ranging from 6 °C to 47 °C and pH values spanning from strongly acidic (1.5) to alkaline (9.8)—contributes to its widespread applicability ( 50 ). A key advantage of A. niger is its genetic stability; it maintains strain-specific traits even after long-term storage or repeated use of its spores, making it a dependable candidate for numerous industrial and food-related processes. Among its most valuable characteristics is its ability to produce several food-grade organic acids, such as gluconic acid ( 51 ), gallic acid ( 52 ), and most notably citric acid, which is extensively used as an acidulant in the food and beverage sectors. Beyond acid synthesis, A. niger also contributes significantly to the fermentation of traditional foods including vinegar, wines, sauces, and certain cured meat products ( 53 ). The involvement of A. niger in fermentation processes plays a key role in enhancing the formation of desirable flavors and aromas, establishing it as a critical microorganism in the production of various food products. Throughout fermentation, A. niger synthesizes a variety of compounds that contribute favorably to the sensory quality of food, particularly in terms of flavor development ( 54 , 55 ). These sensory-enhancing substances are largely attributed to the fungus’s strong proteolytic enzyme activity and its capacity to produce essential amino acids ( 56 ). The safe and efficient processing capabilities of A. niger, along with its ability to generate high enzyme yields even from low-grade substrates, make it highly valuable for the production of food-grade additives and ingredients. Thanks to its metabolic efficiency and adaptability, A. niger has also become a widely preferred host in the biotechnology industry, frequently used as a platform organism for large-scale enzyme production. Numerous biotech firms capitalize on its natural biosynthetic potential to manufacture commercial enzymes, which are applied extensively across sectors such as food processing, pharmaceuticals, and biofuels ( 57 ). In essence, the robustness, environmental flexibility, and genetic consistency of A. niger have established it as a cornerstone organism within the food, beverage, and biotech industries—contributing not only to the production of edible organic acids and traditional fermented foods but also to the industrial-scale synthesis of high-value enzymes (Table 1).

3.1. Straw

Straw ranks among the most widely available lignocellulosic biomass resources worldwide. On average, for every kilogram of harvested grain, approximately 1 to 1.5 kilograms of straw are generated as agricultural residue. Rice straw, in particular, is rich in carbohydrates, containing roughly 32–47% cellulose, 19–27% hemicellulose, and 5–24% lignin ( 77 ). Due to this favorable composition, straw is considered a highly promising feedstock for sustainable bioethanol production. Various microbial fermentation techniques have been employed to convert rice straw into ethanol. In a notable study, modified strains of A. niger and S. cerevisiae were used in two approaches: sequential saccharification and fermentation and in-situ saccharification and fermentation. Although both methods successfully produced ethanol, the in-situ process proved more efficient, yielding 14.56 g of ethanol per kilogram of pretreated rice straw, compared to 12.76 g.kg-1 obtained via the sequential method. Additionally, the in-situ technique offered economic advantages due to fewer processing steps and reduced operational time ( 78 ). In another investigation, rice straw was efficiently converted to ethanol through a two-step process involving pretreatment with Myrothecium roridum LG7, followed by enzymatic saccharification using holocellulase derived from A. niger SH3. The resulting hydrolysate, which contained a glucose concentration of 19.23 mg. mL-1, was subsequently fermented by S. cerevisiae LN1. Optimal fermentation was achieved at 30 °C and pH 5.0, without the addition of external nutrients, producing 4.46 g. L-1 of ethanol and reaching a fermentation efficiency of 47.2% in a 7-liter bioreactor ( 58 ). In a separate approach, rice husks were subjected to sulfuric acid (H2SO4) pretreatment, followed by fermentation using A. niger and S. cerevisiae. At a sulfuric acid concentration of 10%, the process yielded the highest ethanol output (32.13 mg. mL-1) and sugar content (13.4%), indicating improved efficiency with increased acid concentration ( 61 ). Rice stalks have also been explored as a potential feedstock for bioethanol production, utilizing either sulfuric acid or alkaline hydrogen peroxide for pretreatment prior to simultaneous saccharification and fermentation (SSF). Under optimized conditions—35 °C, pH 5.0, 4% substrate concentration, 300 rpm agitation, and a 4-day incubation—co-cultures of A. niger and S. cerevisiae yielded 5.06 g/100 mL of ethanol following sulfuric acid pretreatment and 3.91 g/100 mL with hydrogen peroxide pretreatment. The presence of ethanol in the final product was verified using FTIR spectroscopy, confirming effective bioconversion of the rice stalk biomass ( 59 ). Collectively, these findings highlight the strong potential of agricultural residues—such as rice straw, husks, and stalks—as renewable feedstocks for bioethanol production, offering a promising pathway for sustainable waste management and environmental impact reduction.

3.2. Bagasse

Sugarcane bagasse, primarily composed of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, represents a valuable lignocellulosic biomass for bioethanol production. However, the presence of lignin–hemicellulose complexes limit enzymatic accessibility, making pretreatment a critical step to improve hydrolysis efficiency ( 79 ). Efficient pretreatment is essential to ensure economic viability, particularly at an industrial scale ( 80 , 81 ). Two widely studied strategies include direct enzymatic hydrolysis and a combined process involving acid pretreatment followed by enzymatic hydrolysis. In the direct enzymatic method, customized blends of cellulase and hemicellulase enzymes achieved sugar release up to 614 mg of reducing sugars per gram of bagasse. Alternatively, acid hydrolysis primarily released pentose sugars (41.19 g. L-1), while additional alkaline pretreatment followed by cellulase digestion produced mostly hexoses at a concentration of 571 mg. g-1. Among these, direct enzymatic hydrolysis demonstrated superior efficiency, offering higher yields with fewer process steps ( 64 ). Additional research has investigated the use of fungal strains such as Trichoderma viride and Aspergillus terreus in combination with yeasts like Candida tropicalis and S. cerevisiae under a separate hydrolysis and fermentation framework. Notably, the pairing of T. viride with C. tropicalis produced ethanol yields as high as 226 kg per ton of sugarcane bagasse, outperforming single-strain fermentation systems in terms of productivity ( 62 ). In an effort to reduce costs, some studies have replaced commercial enzymes with in-house enzyme production using fungal solid-state fermentation. Pretreatment with alkaline agents—specifically NaOH, KOH, and Ca (OH)2—was evaluat-ed, with NaOH proving the most effective. Enzymes generated by strains like A. niger and Trichoderma longibrachiatum offered a cost-efficient alternative, although ethanol yields were slightly lower than those achieved using commercial enzyme preparations ( 63 ). In addition, mutagenesis was applied to indigenous S. cerevisiae strains isolated from wine sources, leading to the development of improved mutant strains (SUV, SCD, SCK) that exhibited both higher ethanol yields and enhanced ethanol tolerance. Among them, the SCK mutant stood out, producing 11.2 g.mL-1 of ethanol—substantially surpassing the performance of wild-type strains ( 65 ). Overall, these advancements underscore the significant potential of sugarcane bagasse as a cost-effective and sustainable feedstock for bioethanol production, particularly when integrating fungal–yeast co-cultures or employing genetically enhanced yeast strains.

3.3. Corn

Corncobs, often regarded as agricultural waste, present substantial promise as a feedstock for bioethanol production. While traditionally burned by farmers, recent studies have demonstrated that corncobs can be effectively converted into ethanol using enzymatic hydrolysis and microbial fermentation. For example, a co-culture of A. niger and S. cerevisiae significantly improved ethanol output when applied to defective maize kernels—a common agricultural byproduct. Under optimized conditions (31 °C, pH 5.6, and 12% substrate concentration), ethanol concentrations reached up to 4.24 g/100 mL, highlighting the potential for efficient biofuel conversion ( 82 ). Corncobs themselves, being plentiful and not in direct competition with food sources, have also been widely studied. In one experiment, sun-dried and ground corncobs were subjected to simultaneous saccharification and fermentation using a co-culture of A. niger and S. cerevisiae. After a seven-day period, ethanol production peaked at 10.08% (v/v), corresponding with increased microbial biomass and sugar consumption. Notably, the fermentation process exhibited dynamic pH fluctuations ranging from 3.05 to 7.58, indicating active metabolic activity throughout ( 67 ). Further investigation into pretreated corncobs evaluated ethanol fermentation at two substrate concentrations (1% and 10% w/v), using synthetic glucose media as control benchmarks. At the 1% concentration, corncobderived sugars yielded higher ethanol output (4.17%) compared to the glucose control (3.45%). At the 10% level, results were nearly equivalent, with corncob substrates producing 6.17% ethanol versus 6.23% from glucose. These findings indicate that sugars extracted from natural corncob biomass can match, or even outperform, refined glucose under certain conditions—particularly at lower concentrations ( 83 ). In a separate study, yeast cells immobilized in calcium alginate beads and supplemented with key micronutrients—including biotin, calcium pantothenate, zinc sulfate (ZnSO4·7H2O), magnesium sulfate (MgSO4·7H2O), and myo-inositol—achieved enhanced ethanol production. Under these optimized conditions, yields reached 10.23% (w/w), corresponding to 98.08% of the theoretical maximum, with a productivity rate of 2.13 g. L-1.h-1 ( 66 ). Various pretreatment strategies—including dilute acid application, steam explosion, and mechanical grinding—have been assessed for enhancing ethanol production from corncobs. Among these, dilute acid pretreatment demonstrated superior effectiveness, yielding the highest ethanol concentration at 25.03% (w/v), as supported by statistical analysis ( 69 ). In a related investigation, researchers identified the most efficient microbial pairings for fermenting both corncobs and sugarcane bagasse. The co-culture of A. niger and T. viride achieved the best results, producing 282.3 mL of ethanol per kilogram of substrate, along with a substrate conversion efficiency of 90.35%, significantly outperforming single-microbe systems ( 68 ). Collectively, these studies underscore the potential of maize-derived agricultural residues—particularly corncobs—for sustainable and efficient bioethanol production through the integration of optimized microbial consortia and pretreatment techniques. This approach not only enhances renewable energy generation but also contributes to environ-mentally responsible waste management.

3.4. Fruit Waste

Recent research has investigated the potential of fruit and vegetable waste as a renewable source for bioethanol production, offering a sustainable alternative to conventional disposal practices. By employing various enzymatic saccharification and fermentation techniques, notable ethanol yields have been achieved from agricultural residues including potato, orange, and pineapple peels, as well as banana stalks and pea pods. In a particularly significant study, a crude enzyme cocktail produced in-house generated total reducing sugar concentrations between 370 mg. g-1 and 610 mg. g-1 of substrate, with potato peels exhibiting the highest conversion efficiency at 97–99.4%. Subsequent fermentation of these sugars resulted in ethanol yields ranging from 80 to 262 µL per gram of dry substrate, underscoring the considerable promise of food waste as a viable feedstock for biofuel production ( 84 ). Additional research on lignocellulosic biomass has explored the use of co-cultures combining cellulose-degrading strains of A. niger with ethanol-tolerant S. cerevisiae isolated from palm wine. After a 120-hour fermentation period, one strain combination achieved ethanol concentrations of 11.3% (v/v), while another yielded 7.0% (v/v). The fermentation was accompanied by a drop in pH from 4.1 to 3.3, as well as reductions in soluble solids and reducing sugars—clear indicators of efficient substrate utilization ( 71 ). In another study, red dragon fruit waste was assessed for ethanol production using either a co-culture of A. niger and S. cerevisiae, or a monoculture of S. cerevisiae alone. Interestingly, the monoculture approach resulted in a significantly higher ethanol yield—64.5% in just two weeks—compared to 48.0% over a three-week period for the co-culture, suggesting that under certain conditions, single-strain fermentation may offer greater efficiency ( 72 ). Another promising strategy involved the fermentation of banana and chikki peels using a co-culture of A. niger and S. cerevisiae. Under optimized conditions—30 °C, a 6% yeast to 4% fungal inoculum ratio, 48-hour fermentation, and initial agitation—enzymatic saccharification yielded 66 g.L-1 of reducing sugars, which were subsequently converted into 26.59 g. L-1 of ethanol, corresponding to a fermentation efficiency of 78.99% ( 70 ). Similarly, watermelon peels, which contain a high glucose content (79.22%), produced ethanol yields up to 57 g.L-1 when fermented with microbial co-cultures, significantly surpassing the yields obtained from monocultures. The ethanol produced met commercial quality standards, confirming the suitability of watermelon peel as a viable feedstock ( 73 ). In a separate study, kitchen waste was subjected to steam pretreatment followed by solid-state fermentation using A. niger CJ-5, which generated a broad-spectrum enzyme cocktail without requiring external nutrient supplementation. This enzyme mixture effectively hydrolyzed the carbohydrate-rich kitchen residues into fermentable sugars, presenting a low-cost and resource-efficient method for bioethanol production ( 84 ). Collectively, these findings reinforce the significant potential of fruit, vegetable, and kitchen waste as economical and renewable substrates for bioethanol generation through optimized microbial fermentation, while simultaneously addressing waste management and environmental sustainability.

3.5. Algal Biomass

Algae have emerged as promising candidates for biofuel production, owing to their rapid growth rates, ability to accumulate carbohydrates such as starch, glucose, and mannitol, and the high cellulose content within their cell walls. Certain algal species also generate lipid droplets, enabling their use in the production of both biodiesel and bioethanol ( 74 ). A study conducted near Rourkela evaluated native algal strains and identified Cladophora sp. as particularly suitable for biofuel applications due to its fast proliferation and substantial carbohydrate reserves. To optimize carbohydrate extraction, researchers applied various pretreatment methods, including enzymatic hydrolysis, dilute acid treatment with 0.2N HCl, and biological treatment using A. niger and T. reesei. These strategies effectively facilitated polysaccharide degradation, resulting in notable fuel yields—65% conversion efficiency for biodiesel and 16% ethanol from fermentation—underscoring the potential of Cladophora sp. as a versatile, dual-purpose bioenergy feedstock ( 74 ). The filamentous green alga Spirogyra, known for its high cellulose and starch content, also demonstrates strong potential as a bioethanol feedstock. In one study, researchers compared acid pretreatment followed by enzymatic saccharification using A. niger with direct enzymatic hydrolysis without any prior chemical treatment. Interestingly, the direct saccharification approach yielded a higher ethanol concentration (6% w/w) compared to the acid-treated method (4% w/w), highlighting the greater efficiency and cost-effectiveness of skipping chemical pretreatment ( 76 ). In another experiment, ethanol was produced from Spirogyra biomass through saccharification by A. niger followed by fermentation with S. cerevisiae. Once again, untreated algal material resulted in superior ethanol output relative to chemically pretreated samples and even outperformed conventional agricultural feedstocks in terms of yield ( 75 ). Together, these findings emphasize the remarkable potential of algae—particularly Cladophora and Spirogyra—as sustainable, efficient, and cost-effective alternatives to traditional lignocellulosic biomass in bioethanol production.

4. Co-Cultivation of T. reesei with S. cerevisiae

Once biomass is pretreated, it undergoes enzymatic hydrolysis facilitated by cellulase enzymes, predominantly secreted by fungi such as T. reesei. These enzymes catalyze the cleavage of β-1,4-glycosidic bonds in cellulose chains, effectively breaking down complex carbohydrates into simpler fermentable sugars ( 85 ). As members of the glycoside hydrolase family, cellulases play a central role in bioconversion systems aimed at biofuel production. Most cellulolytic enzymes used at industrial scales are extracellular and derived from filamentous fungi, which are highly efficient producers. Notably, Trichoderma harzianum, a species better known for its biocontrol properties against plant pathogens, has also attracted attention for its strong enzymatic capabilities. Owing to its proficiency in synthesizing cellulases and related enzymes, T. harzianum is considered a promising microbial candidate for large-scale biomass degradation ( 86 ) (Table 2).

4.1. Straw

Straw is among the most plentiful forms of agricultural residue worldwide, especially in countries with intensive crop production such as China. Composed primarily of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, crop straw accounts for approximately 72.2% of China’s total biomass resources—equivalent to an estimated 600 to 800 million tons per year. Despite this substantial availability, a large portion remains unexploited: around 30–40% is incinerated, contributing to serious air pollution issues, while an additional 10–20% is simply discarded as waste ( 100 , 101 ). To better utilize straw as a bioethanol feedstock, researchers have investigated novel pretreatment strategies aimed at enhancing enzymatic digestibility. One such approach involves microwave-assisted pretreatment of rice straw using ferric chloride, designed to disrupt structural barriers within the biomass. This method significantly increased sugar release, yielding up to 3.46 g. L-1of reducing sugars (equivalent to 0.17 g sugar per gram of straw), in contrast to untreated straw, which yielded only 1.24 g. L-1 (0.06 g. g-1). Following fermentation with T. viride and S. cerevisiae, ethanol production improved markedly, reaching 2.14 g. L-1 (0.11 g. g-1), nearly doubling the yield obtained from untreated straw (1.13 g. L-1, 0.06 g. g-1). Additionally, the study explored the use of immobilized and co-immobilized microbial systems, suggesting that these configurations could further enhance ethanol productivity while enabling microbial reuse in successive fermentation cycles ( 90 ). Wheat straw has also been extensively explored as a feedstock for ethanol production. In one study, the straw was subjected to alkali pretreatment combined with microwave irradiation, followed by enzymatic hydrolysis using enzymes derived from Aspergillus flavus and T. reesei. Enzyme optimization was performed using Plackett–Burman and Box–Behnken experimental designs, resulting in a hydrolysis efficiency of 82%. The generated hydrolysate was then fermented using three different systems: individual cultures of S. cerevisiae and P. stipitis, as well as a co-culture of the two. Ethanol yields reached 0.48 g. g-1 for S. cerevisiae, 0.43 g. g-1 for P. stipitis, and 0.40 g. g-1 for the co-culture setup. Substantial carbon dioxide production during fermentation further confirmed active metabolic performance ( 102 ). Another innovative strategy utilized a mixed fungal culture comprising T. reesei Aq-5b and T. viride NSW-XM for the direct degradation of rice straw. This co-culture system effectively disrupted lignin structures, with T. reesei contributing key ligninolytic enzymes such as laccase and lignin peroxidase. Under optimized conditions, the process yielded 22.74 g. L-1 of reducing sugars. To further enhance ethanol production, yeast strains—S. cerevisiae and C. tropicalis NSW-NW—were co-immobilized within polymer beads made of sodium alginate, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), and silicon dioxide (SiO2). These immobilized yeast cells offered the advantage of reusability, thereby reducing operational costs and increasing process efficiency. The fermentation system achieved a maximum ethanol productivity of 2.17 g. L-1.h-1, highlighting significant improvements in both output and processing time ( 103 ). Collectively, these advances in the bioconversion of rice and wheat straw—through refined pretreatment techniques, robust microbial systems, and cell immobilization—underscore their growing potential in sustainable bioethanol production. Such approaches not only minimize agricultural waste and reduce air pollution from open-field straw burning but also contribute meaningfully to renewable energy development.

4.2. Bagasse

Sugarcane bagasse, a fibrous residue generated during sugar processing, has emerged as a key focus in biofuel research due to its widespread availability, high cellulose content, and comparatively low lignin concentration. With global sugarcane production reaching approximately 1.6 billion tons annually, vast quantities of bagasse are generated—providing a readily accessible and renewable feedstock for bioethanol production ( 104 - 106 ). In parallel, sweet sorghum bagasse has also attracted attention for its high cellulose content, estimated at around 48.65%. When T. reesei was cultivated on sweet sorghum bagasse, sugar concentrations reached up to 10.90 g. L-1, offering a favorable substrate for ethanol fermentation. Rather than employing traditional co-culture methods, researchers introduced S. cerevisiae 36 hours after initiating fungal growth, transitioning from aerobic to anaerobic conditions mid-process. This bioprocess engineering adjustment markedly improved ethanol production, yielding 7.99 g. L-1 (0.30 g/g substrate) compared to 6.60 g. L-1 (0.17 g. g-1) with the simultaneous approach. Moreover, ethanol productivity increased to 1.48 g. L-1.h-1 versus 1.04 g. L-1.h-1, clearly demonstrating the enhanced efficiency of the modified fermentation strategy ( 91 , 94 ). The simultaneous saccharification and fermentation (SSF) technique, which merges enzymatic hydrolysis and fermentation in a single step using T. reesei and S. cerevisiae, has shown considerable promise. In one study, the application of alkaline pretreatment with 2% sodium hydroxide and 7.44% hydrogen peroxide led to ethanol yields of 10.73 g. L-1 (equivalent to 0.33 g ethanol per gram of substrate), significantly outperforming conventional SSF methods, which yielded only 6.6 g. L-1 (0.17 g. g-1). These results underscore the effectiveness of enhanced SSF processes in improving both ethanol yield and overall productivity ( 95 ). In a separate study, sugarcane bagasse was pretreated using strong alkaline solutions, followed by enzymatic hydrolysis with T. viride and fermentation with S. cerevisiae. By fine-tuning the fermentation conditions—maintaining 45 °C, 140 rpm agitation, and a 48-hour duration—researchers achieved an ethanol concentration of 2.75% ( 96 ). Solid-state fermentation has also shown significant potential for converting sugarcane bagasse into bioethanol, particularly when combined with wheat bran. In a representative study, this substrate mixture was treated with cellulolytic enzymes derived from Trichoderma and Penicillium species, leading to the release of 20.19 g. L-1 of fermentable sugars within 72 hours. Subsequent fermentation using S. cerevisiae produced 5.825 g. L-1 of ethanol in just 24 hours—equivalent to approximately 40.84% of the theoretical yield. The process was further validated by high enzymatic activity and notable fungal biomass accumulation, confirming its efficiency ( 88 ). Taken together, recent progress in fungal–yeast co-cultivation, targeted pretreatment methods, and advanced fermentation strategies has significantly enhanced the bioconversion of sugarcane and sweet sorghum bagasse. These developments affirm the viability of bagasse as a renewable and cost-effective feedstock for sustainable bioethanol production.

4.3. Corn

Corn stover stands out as one of the most abundant lignocellulosic biomass resources in China, with annual production estimated at approximately 0.84 billion tons. However, a large proportion of this material is still burned in open fields, contributing significantly to air pollution. This practice results in the loss of 17% to 26% of the carbon originally stored in the stover, releasing it into the atmosphere and undermining environmental quality as well as public health ( 107 , 108 ). In response to the environmental concerns associated with corn stover burning, researchers have turned to sustainable methods for converting corn residues into bioethanol. A promising strategy involves leveraging fungal species to produce cellulolytic enzymes that degrade corn fiber into fermentable sugars. Among the fungal strains evaluated, the white-rot fungus (Phanerochaete chrysosporium), the soft-rot fungus (T. reesei), and the brown-rot fungus (Gloeophyllum trabeum) all demonstrated notable enzymatic efficiency. Through solid-state fermentation, these fungi generated enzyme complexes capable of hydrolyzing maize fiber into simple sugars, which were then fermented by S. cerevisiae into ethanol. Ethanol yield varied by fungal type: 7.8 g per 100 g maize fiber for white-rot, 8.6 g for brown-rot, and 4.9 g for soft-rot fungi. The brown-rot fungus yielded the highest conversion, representing approximately 35% of the theoretical ethanol potential based on the fiber’s cellulose and starch content ( 87 ). Further progress in bioethanol production from corn stover has been achieved through genetic enhancement of fungal strains. In a notable development, T. reesei Rut C30 was genetically modified to express a β-glucosidase gene derived from Aspergillus aculeatus, regulated by the pdc1 promoter. The resulting recombinant strain, designat-ed T. reesei PB-3, exhibited a dramatic improvement in enzyme production: β-glucosidase activity increased to 310 CBU.mL-1 —approximately 71 times greater than the parent strain—while cellulase activity reached 50 FPU.mL-1. When applied to corn stover at 20% solids loading using a separate hydrolysis and fermentation approach, the process generated 119.9 g. L-1glucose in just 12 hours and produced 54.2 g. L-1 ethanol. These findings underscore the potential of genetically engineered fungi in significantly improving the efficiency of lignocellulosic biomass conversion ( 92 ). Collectively, fungi such as T. reesei, Phanerochaete chrysosporium, and Gloeophyllum trabeum have proven highly effective for enzymatic hydrolysis of corn stover. Through advanced methods including solid-state fermentation and genetic engineering, these organisms are transforming agricultural waste into a promising renewable energy resource.

4.4. Fruit Waste

The fruit juice industry generates large quantities of waste materials, including peels, seeds, pomace, and pulp residues. In 2009, fruit juice production reached approximately 11.3 billion liters in Europe and 9.5 billion liters in North America, contributing to substantial organic waste streams. Among these, apple pomace—a by-product constituting roughly 25–35% of the original apple mass after juice extraction—stands out as a significant residue ( 109 ). To address both environmental and resource efficiency concerns, researchers have explored the valorization of apple pomace for bioethanol production. In a promising study, a co-culture system involving T. harzianum, Aspergillus sojae, and S. cerevisiae was employed to ferment hydrolyzed apple pomace. Using statistical optimization techniques, the researchers determined optimal fermentation parameters, including fungal and yeast inoculation rates (6% and 4%, respectively), aeration strategy, and agitation speed (200 rpm). Under these conditions, the system achieved a bioethanol concentration of 8.748 g. L-1and a high yield of 0.945 g ethanol per gram of total reducing sugars ( 89 ). Pineapple peel, another major by-product of the fruit processing industry, has also been investigated as a substrate for bioethanol production. In one study, researchers examined the effects of alkaline pretreatment and microbial hydrolysis using T. harzianum. Interestingly, the samples that did not undergo sodium hydroxide (NaOH) pretreatment produced higher concentrations of total sugars (458.44 ± 13.6 g. L-1) and reducing sugars (279.67 ± 21 g. L-1) compared to pretreated counterparts. Following microbial hydrolysis and fermentation, ethanol production reached 5.98 ± 1.01 g. L-1 within 48 hours, demonstrating the effectiveness of enzymatic processes without the need for chemical pretreatment ( 93 ). These results, alongside those from apple pomace bioconversion, highlight the promise of fruit processing residues as low-cost, high-yield feedstocks for sustainable bioethanol production. Utilizing such waste streams not only mitigates environmental burden but also supports circular economy strategies by turning agricultural waste into renewable energy sources.

4.5. Algal Biomass

Microalgae are gaining recognition as a highly promising feedstock for renewable bioethanol production, owing to their rapid growth rates, rich carbohydrate profiles, and remarkable ability to sequester atmospheric CO2 Unlike traditional lignocellulosic biomass, microalgae lack lignin in their cell walls, which significantly simplifies the conversion process into fermentable monosaccharides—key intermediates in ethanol synthesis ( 110 ). Recent studies have demonstrated effective approaches for converting microalgal biomass into bioethanol without relying on intensive chemical pretreatment. In one notable example, T. harzianum was shown to grow using whole cells of the green microalga Chlamydomonas reinhardtii as its exclusive nutrient source. The fungal secretome included enzymes capable of degrading the algal cell wall structure, thereby releasing fermentable sugars. This enzymatic conversion process resulted in 22.4 g. L-1 of reducing sugars, which were subsequently fermented by S. cerevisiae into ethanol. The study highlights a promising biological route to overcome the recalcitrance of microalgal cell walls, offering a sustainable and efficient bioethanol production method ( 97 ). Macroalgae have also emerged as promising candidates for bioethanol feedstock. A notable example is Codium tomentosum, which underwent acid pretreatment using varying concentrations of sulfuric acid. The optimal conditions—4% H2SO4 at 100 °C for 60 minutes—resulted in a substantial sugar release of 16.45 ± 0.33 g. L-1. This was further enhanced through enzymatic hydrolysis using T. reesei-derived cellulase, yielding 17.05 ± 0.25 g. L-1 of fermentable sugars under favorable parameters (50 °C, 48 hours, and 5% biomass loading). The hydrolysate was then fermented by S. cerevisiae at 30 °C and pH 5 over 72 hours, producing 4 ± 0.33 g. L-1 of ethanol ( 98 ). Collectively, these findings affirm the potential of both microalgae (C. reinhardtii) and macroalgae (C. tomentosum) as viable, sustainable sources for bioethanol production, reinforcing the broader applicability of algae-derived biofuels as eco-friendly alternatives to fossil energy.

4.6. Non-Routine Substrate (Napier Grass)

Napier grass (Pennisetum purpureum) has gained increasing attention as a viable energy crop due to its exceptional biomass productivity and favorable biochemical composition. Rich in structural carbohydrates, its cell wall components typically include 45–50% cellulose, 30–32% hemicellulose, and 18–22% lignin. Beyond structural biomass, the plant’s dry matter is composed of approximately 30.9% carbohydrates, 27% proteins, 14.8% lipids, 18.2% ash, and 9.1% crude fiber. This nutrient-rich profile highlights its strong potential for conversion into bioethanol and other biofuels ( 111 , 112 ). A recent study explored the bioethanol production potential of Napier grass through simultaneous saccharification and fermentation, utilizing a co-culture system comprising T. reesei and S. cerevisiae. Pretreatment with 3% sodium hydroxide significantly enhanced the grass’s fermentable quality by increasing cellulose content from 52.85% to 82%. Employing an orthogonal experimental design, researchers determined optimal process parameters: 15 grams of Napier grass, 15 grams of microbial inoculum, and a 7-day fermentation period. Under these conditions, ethanol concentration reached 16.90 g. L-1. When scaled up twentyfold, the system demonstrated even greater efficiency, yielding 33.24 g. L-1of ethanol without any added sugars—surpassing the 25.02 g. L-1yield observed with sugar supplementation. These findings underscore Napier grass’s high suitability as a renewable substrate for industrial-scale bioethanol production, reinforcing its value in future sustainable energy frameworks ( 99 ).

5. Co-Cultivation of P. stipites with S. cerevisiae

P. stipitis, recently reclassified as Scheffersomyces stipitis, is recognized as a highly effective yeast for bioethanol production due to its remarkable ability to ferment xylose—one of the primary sugars present in lignocellulosic biomass ( 113 ). This yeast can metabolize various sugars including glucose, xylose, and cellobiose, typically achieving high ethanol yields while minimizing the formation of undesirable by-products ( 114 - 116 ). However, its fermentation efficiency is strongly influenced by oxygen availability: elevated oxygen levels promote biomass growth but suppress ethanol production, whereas anaerobic conditions severely impair both ethanol output and cell viability ( 115 , 117 ). Additionally, P. stipitis exhibits a tendency to ferment glucose preferentially, delaying the utilization of xylose due to its inherently slow and microaerophilic metabolic nature. To address this bottleneck, co-cultivation with S. cerevisiae has proven beneficial. In such systems, S. cerevisiae efficiently ferments glucose, enabling P. stipitis to focus on xylose conversion—thereby enhancing the overall ethanol yield from lignocellulosic substrates (Table 3).

5.1. Straw

Rice straw represents one of the most abundant forms of lignocellulosic agricultural waste worldwide, especially in rice-producing countries such as India—the second-largest rice producer globally. With nearly 30,000 distinct rice varieties cultivated across the country, India generates approximately 1 to 1.5 kilograms of straw for every kilogram of harvested grain. Compositionally, rice straw contains 32–47% cellulose, 19–27% hemicellulose, and 5–24% lignin, making it a viable candidate for bioethanol production ( 120 ). To efficiently convert rice straw into bioethanol, co-cultivation of P. stipitis and S. cerevisiae has been adopted, capitalizing on their complementary sugar fermentation abilities. In particular, the use of immobilized yeast cells has significantly enhanced xylose consumption compared to single-strain systems. In one study employing a packed-bed reactor, immobilized co-culture cells achieved nearly 74% xylose utilization and produced up to 41.68 g. L-1/day of ethanol from wheat straw hydrolysate at a hydraulic retention time (HRT) of six hours. When operating under optimized conditions with a synthetic medium, ethanol productivity further increased, reaching 356.21 g. L-1/day at a reduced HRT of just 0.75 hours. These results highlight the high efficiency and industrial potential of co-cultivation strategies in continuous bioethanol production systems ( 118 ). Another promising strategy for maximizing ethanol yield from lignocellulosic biomass is simultaneous saccharification and co-fermentation, which involves both S. cerevisiae and P. stipitis in optimized proportions. Using statistical modeling, researchers identified ideal simultaneous saccharification and co-fermentation parameters: a yeast cell ratio (S. cerevisiae: P. stipitis) of approximately 0.31, an agitation speed of 116 rpm, and a fermentation temperature near 33 °C. Under these conditions, the system achieved an ethanol concentration of 15.2 g. L-1, representing 99% of the theoretical yield. Increasing the biomass concentration further enhanced ethanol output to 28.6 g. L-1 ( 119 ). In a related approach, biphasic acid hydrolysis followed by co-culture fermentation using vacuum-distilled rice straw hydrolysate produced 12 g. L-1 ethanol, with a fermentation efficiency of 95% ( 120 ). Sequential fermentation has also proven effective: glucose was first fermented by S. cerevisiae, which was then heat-inactivated to allow P. stipitis to ferment xylose. This two-step process led to an ethanol concentration of 21.1 g. L-1 from lime-pretreated rice straw ( 121 ). In parallel, advances in genetic engineering have further improved efficiency. Engineered strains of S. cerevisiae and Pichia pastoris, capable of expressing cellulase and xylanase enzymes, facilitated direct hydrolysis and fermentation of wheat straw without requiring external enzyme supplementation. This approach yielded an impressive ethanol concentration of 32.6 g. L-1 ( 122 ). Together, these integrated biotechnological strategies—ranging from optimized co-fermentation to metabolic engineering—underscore the vast potential of lignocellulosic biomass as a renewable and scalable feedstock for sustainable bioethanol production from agricultural waste.

5.2. Bagasse

Sugarcane bagasse, the fibrous by-product remaining after sugar extraction, is commonly burned for energy in sugar mills. However, due to its high carbohydrate content, it holds considerable potential as a feedstock for bioethanol production—provided it undergoes appropriate pretreatment and enzymatic hydrolysis ( 135 ). One of the primary challenges in utilizing bagasse lies in its substantial xylan fraction. Since S. cerevisiae, the standard yeast used in industrial fermentation, cannot metabolize xylose, alternative xylose-fermenting yeasts such as P. stipitis and C. shehatae have been explored. Nonetheless, these organisms often exhibit low tolerance to fermentation inhibitors present in untreated lignocellulosic hydrolysates, which hampers their practical application in large-scale bioconversion processes ( 45 , 46 ). In a comparative study, steam-pretreated sugarcane bagasse was fermented using a genetically engineered xylose-fermenting strain, S. cerevisiae TMB3400. Under optimized conditions (32 °C and 5% solids loading), this strain achieved near-complete xylose consumption, outperforming the naturally xylose-fermenting P. stipitis CBS6054, which required higher pH levels (around 6.0) and microaeration to function effectively ( 123 ). In a separate investigation, sugarcane bagasse underwent alkaline pretreatment with 2.5% NaOH at 121 °C, resulting in enhanced cellulose accessibility—raising cellulose content to 81% and removing approximately 68.5% of lignin. Subsequent fermentation, conducted with either individual yeast strains or co-cultures, led to substantial ethanol production, with co-cultivation approaches showing particularly high ethanol yields ( 124 ). Additionally, co-fermentation techniques involving mixed cultures of S. cerevisiae (VSI 1011) and P. stipitis (NCIM 3498) have demonstrated significant improvements in ethanol yield from sugarcane bagasse cultivars. Under high solids loading conditions (16%), the fermentation process achieved an ethanol concentration of approximately 31.0 g. L-1, corresponding to a fermentation efficiency of nearly 78% ( 125 ). Taken together, these findings underscore the economic and environmental potential of sugarcane bagasse as a sustainable feedstock for bioethanol production. The integration of co-fermentation strategies, tailored pretreatment techniques, and advanced enzymatic systems has markedly enhanced ethanol output, presenting a scalable and efficient solution for converting lignocellulosic agricultural residues into renewable fuels.

5.3. Corn

Corn cobs, as a major agricultural by-product, offer considerable potential for bioethanol production due to their richness in fermentable hexose and pentose sugars. However, the conversion of xylose—particularly from acid-treated corn cob hydrolysate—poses challenges, as the hydrolysate often contains inhibitory compounds such as acetic acid and lignin-derived phenolics. To overcome this barrier, researchers explored whether immobilizing P. stipitis cells could enhance their tolerance to these inhibitors and improve fermentation outcomes compared to free-cell cultures. In a study conducted by Kashid and Ghosalkar (2018), calcium alginate bead-based immobilization significantly enhanced the performance of Pichia in non-detoxified corn cob hydrolysate. Through optimization of key parameters—such as sodium alginate concentration, calcium chloride levels, and agitation speed—agitation emerged as the most influential factor affecting ethanol production. Immobilized cells achieved an ethanol yield of 0.43 g. g-1, compared to 0.40 g. g-1 for free cells, while ethanol productivity increased substantially from 0.31 g. L-1·h to 0.51 g. L-1·h. These findings underscore the effectiveness of immobilization in mitigating the adverse effects of fermentation inhibitors and improving ethanol yields from corn cob-derived substrates ( 126 ).

5.4. Fruit and Food Waste

Food waste, generated across the supply chain, represents a valuable and renewable biomass resource due to its high content of carbohydrates, proteins, and lipids. Owing to its diverse and nutrient-rich composition, numerous strategies have been developed to convert food waste into high-value biofuels, particularly bioethanol. Household food waste, in particular, is rich in fermentable sugars and starches, making it well-suited for microbial fermentation processes. In a study by Jeong et al. (2012), sequential fermentation was employed, starting with Saccharomyces coreanus under anaerobic conditions, which produced 40.59 g. L-1 of ethanol with a productivity rate of 1.69 g. L-1·h. When P. stipitis was introduced in a subsequent fermentation stage, ethanol output increased to 48.63 g. L-1 and productivity rose to 2.03 g. L-1·h, clearly demonstrating the benefits of sequential co-fermentation ( 127 ). In another approach, food waste was enzymatically hydrolyzed through multi-stage treatment to release fermentable sugars, followed by co-culture fermentation using S. cerevisiae and P. stipitis. This strategy significantly outperformed monoculture systems, yielding 10.32 g. L-1 of ethanol with a productivity of 0.16 g. L-1·h and a fermentation efficiency of 88.6% ( 128 ). These findings highlight the considerable potential of food waste as a feedstock for sustainable ethanol production through integrated microbial systems. Demiray et al. (2018) investigated the potential of pomegranate peel waste as a substrate for ethanol fermentation, focusing on optimizing sulfuric acid pretreatment conditions, substrate loading, and fermentation duration. Supplementation with nitrogen sources (such as yeast extract, peptone, and ammonium sulfate) and key metal ions significantly improved ethanol yields. Under optimized conditions, S. cerevisiae produced 5.58 g. L-1 of ethanol within 12 hours, achieving a productivity of 0.46 g. L-1 h, while P. stipitis yielded 2.95 g. L-1 ( 129 ). In a separate study, Shahid et al. (2023) evaluated onion waste as a fermentation feedstock using S. cerevisiae, P. pastoris, and a co-culture of the two. The monoculture of S. cerevisiae outperformed other systems, generating 30.56 g. L-1 of ethanol, compared to 21.06 g. L-1 from P. pastoris and 22.72 g. L-1 from the co-culture. Ethanol production was validated using FTIR and GC-FID analyses, reinforcing the feasibility of onion waste as a viable substrate ( 131 ). Furthermore, Pathania et al. (2017) demonstrated successful bioethanol production from apple pomace—a common agro-industrial waste—through microwave-assisted saccharification, which released 27.50 mg/g of reducing sugars ( 132 ). Utilizing immobilized co-cultures of S. cerevisiae and P. stipitis, ethanol production reached 44.46 g. L-1 with 58% fermentation efficiency, notably higher than free-cell systems, which achieved only 34.46 g. L-1 (45% efficiency). Collectively, these findings support the viability of fruit and food waste streams as cost-effective and sustainable substrates for bioethanol production, offering promising pathways toward circular bioeconomy models ( 132 ).

5.5. Algal Biomass

Algae, categorized as red, green, and brown types based on pigmentation, represent promising biomass resources for bioethanol production. In Korea, brown seaweeds like Laminaria japonica and Undaria pinnatifida are extensively cultivated, containing 30–67% polysaccharides by dry weight (alginate, laminarin, mannitol), which fluctuate seasonally ( 133 - 137 ). Sunwoo et al. (2017) optimized pretreatment conditions for ethanol production from mixed seaweed biomass (brown, red, and green algae) collected from Korea’s coast ( 133 ). Optimal pretreatment (286 mM H2SO4, 121 °C, 90 min) produced a monosaccharide-rich hydrolysate (30.2 g. L-1 sugars). Subsequent co-culture fermentation by S. cerevisiae (utilizing glucose) and P. angophorae (utilizing galactose) generated ethanol at a concentration of 13.5 g. L-1, demonstrating efficient utilization of mixed seaweed sugars. Gracilaria verrucosa, a red seaweed, was also studied ( 133 ). Lee et al. (2013) developed an immobilized two-step fermentation process utilizing Ulva pertusa Kjellman hydrolysate ( 134 ). Immobilized yeasts (S. cerevisiae and P. stipitis) successfully converted glucose and xylose into approximately 5.0 g. L-1 ethanol with a high overall yield of 0.37. The volumetric productivity of this sequential fermentation method was significantly higher (2.7-fold) compared to fermentation by P. stipitis alone, underscoring the effectiveness of sequential immobilized co-culture fermentation in algal biomass conversion ( 134 ). Optimal pretreatment (286 mM H2SO4, 121 °C, 90 min) produced a monosaccharide-rich hydrolysate (30.2 g. L-1 sugars). Subsequent co-culture fermentation by S. cerevisiae (utilizing glucose) and P. angophorae (utilizing galactose) generated ethanol at a concentration of 13.5 g. L-1, demonstrating efficient utilization of mixed seaweed sugars. Gracilaria verrucosa, a red seaweed, was also studied ( 133 ). Sukwong et al. (2019) used hyperthermal acid hydrolysis (130 °C, 15 min, 0.2 M H2SO4), yielding 50.7 g. L-1 monosaccharides, further enhanced to 58.0 g. L-1 after enzymatic saccharification ( 138 ). Adapted yeasts P. stipitis and Kluyveromyces marxianus showed varying fermentation efficiency. K. marxianus signi-ficantly outperformed P. stipitis, yielding 29.0 g. L-1 ethanol (productivity 0.35 g. L-1·h), highlighting strain-specific optimization importance ( 138 ). In summary, these studies demonstrate that algal biomass, fruit waste, food waste, and corn cobs represent valuable resources for sustainable bioethanol production. Immobilization, sequential fermentation, co-culture strategies, and pretreatment optimizations significantly improve fermentation efficiency, productivity, and ethanol yields, highlighting their potential role in renewable energy and waste management solutions.

Notably, Zymomonas mobilis has emerged as an efficient ethanol-producing microorganism capable of fermenting both glucose and xylose, with enhanced ethanol tolerance up to 120 g. L-1 ( 139 - 141 ). Additionally, co-culture strategies employing Bacillus licheniformis and S. cerevisiae in simultaneous saccharification and fermentation (SSF) processes have demonstrated promising synergy, enabling direct bioconversion of lignocellulosic materials into ethanol. 6.1. Straw

Rice straw, a globally abundant agricultural residue (approximately 731 million metric tons per year), represents an enormous untapped potential for bioethanol production, potentially generating up to 205 billion liters annually ( 155 ). To maximize bioethanol yields from rice straw, advanced pretreatment and enzymatic hydrolysis techniques have been developed. Singh and Bishnoi (2012) demonstrated that alkaline-microwave pretreatment followed by optimized enzymatic saccharification significantly enhanced fermentable sugar release ( 102 ). Using co-cultures of S. cerevisiae and P. stipitis, ethanol yields reached up to 0.48 g. g-1 sugar, representing improvements of 25–31% over individual monocultures. The combined fermentation also achieved notable CO2 production (50.31 µmoL.mL-1), indicating robust microbial metabolism ( 102 ). Das et al. (2013) developed a three-stage process involving co-cultures for enzyme production and ethanol fermentation. ( 143 ). Initial co-cultivation of Aspergillus fumigatus ABK9 and T. reesei SAP3 efficiently produced β-glucosidase (265.4 U. g-1), followed by effective saccharification of rice straw, yielding 24.9 g. L-1 of reducing sugars. Subsequent ethanol fermentation by co-cultivating S. cerevisiae MTCC 173 and Z. mobilis MTCC 2428 resulted in a high ethanol concentration of 40.1 g. L-1 ( 143 ). Molaverdi et al. (2019) utilized solid-state SSF with the fungus Mucor indicus, employing sodium carbonate pretreatment ( 156 ). Under optimized conditions (0.5 M Na2CO3, 10 hours), saccharification generated 89.2 g. L-1 sugars, leading to an ethanol yield of 99.4 g. L-1 (89.5% theoretical yield).

Shorter pretreatments at lower enzyme loads still yielded substantial ethanol (66.3–90.9 g. L-1) ( 156 ). Furthermore, Thakur et al. (2013) demonstrated suc-cessful bioethanol production from banana stem and wheat straw pretreated by fungal (Pleurotus ostreatus HP-1) and mild acid hydrolysis ( 144 ). Subsequent enzymatic hydrolysis and fermentation by S. cerevisiae NCIM 3570 yielded ethanol concentrations of 0.15–0.54 g.g-1, emphasizing the efficacy of combined biological and chemical pretreatments ( 144 ). Sharma et al. (2021) expanded the potential of wheat straw by co-culturing B. licheniformis and S. cerevisiae in an optimized SSF process ( 142 ). This approach achieved bioethanol yields of 4.11 g. L-1 at small scale, which scaled effectively to approximately 14.70 g. L-1 in larger fermenters, showcasing industrial potential ( 142 ).

6.2. Bagasse

Sugarcane bagasse is an important lignocellulosic feedstock with significant potential for ethanol production ( 157 ). Traditional ethanol production primarily relies on food-based crops; however, interest has shifted toward non-food biomass like bagasse, promoting sustainability and reducing competition with food production ( 158 , 159 ). Ndaba et al. (2014a) investigated the synergistic effect of co-fermentation between S. cerevisiae and Z. mobilis (ATCC31825), achieving optimal ethanol production (0.5 g. g-1) at a 5:10 g. L-1 ratio of these microorganisms under controlled conditions (pH 4.8, 32 °C). This cooperative interaction maximized sugar utilization efficiency, thereby enhancing ethanol yields compared to monocultures ( 145 ). Innovative genetic engineering strategies were explored by Wang et al. (2019a), who co-cultured a genetically engineered ethanologenic Escherichia coli strain LW419a with Turbo yeast. The engineered E. coli effectively metabolized xylose, complementing Turbo yeast’s efficient glucose fermentation. When applied to pretreated sugarcane bagasse slurries (10–20% solids), ethanol concentrations of 24.9–48.6 g. L-1 were achieved, with impressive xylose consumption rates (1.58 g. L-1·h), demonstrating the power of engineered co-cultures in lignocellulose bioconversion ( 146 ). Sequential co-cultivation strategies were also implemented by Yoon et al. (2019a), utilizing the white-rot fungus Pycnoporus sanguineus to enzymatically saccharify NaOH-pretreated sugarcane bagasse. Subsequent fermentation by S. cerevisiae yielded ethanol concentrations of 4.5 g per 100 g of treated bagasse. This study highlighted the efficiency of sequential microbial processes in biomass valorization ( 147 ). Lopes et al. (2017a) developed a unique biorefinery concept involving simultaneous production of ethanol and rhamnolipids from sugarcane bagasse via co-cultivation of Pseudomonas aeruginosa and S. cerevisiae. Optimal fermentation conditions (30 °C, 86 hours) yielded 8.4 g. L-1 ethanol and 9.1 g. L-1 rhamnolipids. The residual fermented bagasse retained significant calorific value, providing a complementary energy source and demonstrating the versatility of integrated co-fermentation strategies ( 148 ). Meethit et al. (2016) demonstrated the effectiveness of immobilized co-cultures (S. cerevisiae and C. shehatae) using calcium alginate beads in a packed-bed bioreactor, significantly enhancing ethanol yields from sugarcane bagasse and rice straw hydrolysates. This immobilization approach, combined with a recycled batch fermentation system, achieved ethanol yields near theoretical maxima (up to 98% for rice straw hydrolysate), underscoring its potential for commercial-scale bioethanol production ( 149 ). Overall, these innovative co-cultivation and bioprocessing strategies illustrate substantial advancements in the efficient and sustainable pro-duction of bioethanol from lignocellulosic resources. By exploiting complementary metabolic capabilities, optimizing fermentation conditions, and employing biotechnological enhancements, researchers continue to overcome traditional fermentation challenges, advancing toward economically viable, en-vironmentally friendly bioethanol production processes.

6.3. Corn

Corn residues, including corncobs and corn stover, represent an attractive raw material for bioethanol production due to their abundance, low cost, and high sugar content. Annually, about 70 million metric tons of corncobs are generated globally, with major contributions from countries like the United States and China ( 160 , 161 ). Researchers have explored various co-cultivation strategies and process optimizations to efficiently convert these residues into ethanol. One significant approach involves simultaneous saccharification and fermentation using yeast strains such as S. cerevisiae and C. tropicalis. Latif and Rajoka (2001) studied ethanol production from dried maize cobs at different substrate loadings (5%–20% w/v). At the highest substrate concentration (200 g. L-1), S. cerevisiae yielded the highest ethanol concentration (27 g. L-1), followed by C. tropicalis (23 g. L-1) and their co-culture (21 g. L-1). Interestingly, reducing substrate concentration to 50 g. L-1 improved theoretical ethanol yields to 82%, 71%, and 63% for S. cerevisiae, C. tropicalis, and the co-culture, respectively. Moreover, this study also highlighted the co-production of xylitol, with C. tropicalis achieving the highest yield (79%), followed by the co-culture (77%) and S. cerevisiae (58%). While increased substrate loading improved volumetric productivity, overall yields for ethanol and xylitol tended to decline ( 150 ). In another innovative approach, Zhu et al. (2020) leveraged the complementary strengths of two specialized yeast strains—one thermotolerant and the other xylose-fermenting—in a simultaneous saccharification and co-fermentation system. This temperature-profiled fermentation (up to 42 °C) of corncob-derived biomass at 12% glucan loading achieved an impressive ethanol concentration of 59.8 g. L-1. Importantly, this co-culture method maintained high yeast viability even under thermal stress, dramatically outperforming mono-culture fermentations ( 152 ). Additionally, Wang et al. (2018) employed a hybrid co-fermentation system combining S. cerevisiae and Bacillus coagulans for converting corn stover hydrolysate. This hybrid strategy yielded 50.5 g. L-1 ethanol with a productivity of 1.84 g. L-1·h and concurrently produced 21.5 g. L-1 of valuable L-lactic acid. Further optimizations, including pre-aeration and adjusted inoculum densities, boosted L-lactic acid production up to 24.25 g/L, with yields as high as 0.86 g. g-1. These studies underscore the effectiveness of microbial co-cultivation and innovative hybrid fermentation approaches in enhancing bioethanol productivity from corn-derived biomass ( 151 ). By optimizing fermentation conditions, such as temperature profiling, substrate loading, and co-culture configurations, these methods present promising pathways toward sustainable, high-yield bioethanol production.

6.4. Fruit Waste

The global fruit processing industry generates vast quantities of residues annually, providing a rich source of fermentable biomass. Citrus residues, especially orange waste, have garnered attention due to their high soluble carbohydrate content and relatively low lignin content compared to traditional lignocellulosic feedstocks ( 162 - 164 ). Awan et al. (2013) demonstrated an innovative approach by converting orange waste into fermentable sugars using cost-effective enzymes derived from Xanthomonas axonopodis pv. citri strain 306, a bacterium naturally associated with citrus canker ( 165 ). Subsequent co-culture fermentations involving S. cerevisiae with Candida parapsilosis strains achieved exceptional ethanol purity, with 98.9% conversion of hexose sugars into second-generation ethanol ( 165 ). Research by Derman et al. (2022) explored bioethanol production from pretreated empty fruit bunches, another fruit-derived biomass ( 153 ). Acid-alkali pretreatment effectively enhanced enzyme accessibility, facilitating high ethanol production. Through statistical optimization using response surface methodology, fermentation with co-cultured S. cerevisiae and T. harzianum yielded 11.76 g. L-1 ethanol, achieving conversion efficiencies up to 90.6%. Apple pomace, an agro-industrial byproduct rich in fermentable sugars, has also been investigated ( 153 ). Kumar et al. (2020) screened microorganisms from apple-pomace-rich environments, identifying strains with superior enzymatic activities ( 166 ). Co-culturing a selected cellulase- and ethanol-producing Actinomyces strain (APW-12) with S. cerevisiae resulted in a notable increase in ethanol production (49.64 g. L-1), surpassing yields obtained from mono-cultures (37.6 g. L-1) ( 166 ). Dey et al. (2021a) utilized pulp and paper sludge, another cellulose-rich industrial residue, demonstrating its potential as a viable bioethanol feedstock. Optimization of enzyme loadings, surfactants, and solid loadings produced significant glucose and xylose yields (79.56 g. L-1 and 8.65 g. L-1, respectively), which were effectively converted into ethanol (42.34 g. L-1) by a co-fermentation strategy employing P. stipitis and baker’s yeast ( 154 ). These studies collectively illustrate how fruit-based agro-industrial residues, through targeted pretreatment and microbial co-cultivation, can be efficiently transformed into bioethanol, supporting the transition to a circular bioeconomy.

6.5. Azolla Weed and Algae

Aquatic plants like Azolla weed and algae have emerged as promising feedstocks for bioethanol production, benefiting from rapid growth rates and high carbohydrate content. Chupaza et al. (2021) optimized pretreatment and enzymatic saccharification conditions for Azolla weed, achieving up to 61.6 g. L-1 monosaccharides ( 167 ). Fermentation using different yeast strains revealed that Kluyveromyces marxianus yielded the highest ethanol concentration (26.8 g. L-1), efficiently fermenting both glucose and xylose. Conversely, S. cerevisiae yielded significantly less ethanol (18.2 g. L-1) due to its limitation in fermenting xylose. Algal biomass has also demonstrated potential, particularly when combined with enzymatic enhancers ( 167 ). Singh and Trivedi (2013) evaluated the fermentation efficiency of Z. mobilis versus S. cerevisiae, finding that adding α-amylase significantly boosted ethanol yields ( 76 ). Specifically, Z. mobilis produced superior ethanol yields (up to 6.6%) compared to S. cerevisiae, highlighting its enhanced capability in converting algal-derived sugars ( 76 ). Collectively, these studies confirm the viability of Azolla weed and algal biomass as renewable resources for bioethanol. They emphasize microbial selection’s critical role, process optimization, and enzyme supplementation, which collectively enhance the sustainability and economic feasibility of biofuel production from unconventional feedstocks.

7. Conclusion

Research on microbial co-cultures has revealed that they play a key role in boosting the production of fermentable hydrolysates from lignocellulosic materials while also minimizing the impact of inhibitory substances. Different microorganisms bring unique capabilities to this process. For instance, T. reesei produces three types of cellulase enzymes but has relatively low β-glucosidase activity. On the other hand, A. niger excels at producing β-glucosidase but generates only two types of cellulase enzymes, and at a slower rate. When these two microorganisms are co-cultured, they complement each other’s strengths, significantly enhancing the metabolic capacity of the system. This co-culture approach also boosts the bioconversion efficiency of solid substrates, making it a more effective method for bioethanol production. A. niger is a well-known microorganism found in various agricultural products, such as fruits, onions, and corn. It plays a crucial role in the production of food-grade products, particularly due to its strong capacity for enzyme production. Many biotechnology companies employ A. niger as a “cell factory” to produce commercial enzymes. Meanwhile, T. reesei is recognized for its role in biomass utilization, as its cellulase enzymes are responsible for breaking down β–1, 4–glycosidic bonds in cellulose, a vital step in converting lignocellulosic material into sugars. T. harzianum is another important player in biomass processing. It is a highly productive source of enzymes and is regarded as a promising option for biomass degradation. When it comes to fermenting sugars into ethanol, P. stipitis stands out due to its ability to ferment glucose, xylose, and cellobiose with high ethanol yield and minimal production of unwanted byproducts. However, P. stipitis has unique oxygen requirements. While enhanced aeration increases biomass production, it reduces ethanol yield. Additionally, in the absence of oxygen, P. stipitis cells are unable to produce ethanol and cannot survive beyond one generation, making oxygen regulation crucial for optimal ethanol production. S. cerevisiae is one of the most widely used yeasts for glucose fermentation to produce bioethanol. However, it has a significant limitation — it cannot ferment xylose or arabinose, which are common sugars found in lignocellulosic biomass. This limitation has led researchers to explore the use of other microorganisms, such as Z. mobilis, which can ferment both glucose and xylose, making it a more versatile biocatalyst for ethanol production. An alternative approach involves using B. licheniformis in combination with S. cerevisiae. B. licheniformis breaks down lignocellulosic biomass through hydrolysis, releasing sugars that S. cerevisiae can then convert into bioethanol. This process, known as simultaneous saccharification and fermentation, allows the breakdown of biomass and fermentation to occur in a single step, reducing process time and increasing efficiency. Corncobs, which account for approximately 30% of maize agricultural waste, offer a promising feedstock for bioethanol production. Researchers have developed methods to convert this waste into bioethanol by using enzymes to break down the lignocellulose and employing microorganisms like S. cerevisiae and P. stipitis for fermentation. Similar strategies have been explored for other forms of agro-waste, such as pineapple waste. Given that pineapple trash is a byproduct of agricultural activity, it is considered a renewable source of biomass. By using enzymes to release fermentable sugars from this waste, researchers have been able to convert it into bioethanol, providing a sustainable and cost-effective energy source. Cellulosic bioethanol is increasingly seen as a sustainable alternative to fossil fuels, as it is produced from non-food-based materials such as agricultural residues and industrial byproducts. Lignocellulosic materials, in particular, are viewed as one of the most promising feedstocks due to their availability and low cost. These materials, including corn stover, sugarcane bagasse, and agricultural waste like pineapple trash, are abundant and renewable. Current research focuses on optimizing processes for the efficient conversion of these materials into fermentable sugars and improving the performance of microorganisms used for fermentation. Co-culture fermentation and the use of robust microbial strains are key areas of development, offering the potential for more cost-effective and sustainable bioethanol production.
