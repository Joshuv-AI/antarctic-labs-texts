Deep learning-based visual algorithms for identity and action recognition in engineering practical courses
Authors: Jun Ma, RuoYu Wang, WenQi Lan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Engineering practice is an important component of engineering education. In this teaching scenario, students are frequently moving around, making the identification of their identities and actions using computer vision methods a prominent and ongoing research challenge. This is a challenge for AI-based identity recognition algorithms. Some facial recognition algorithms and person re-identification algorithms have attempted to solve the problem of identity recognition, but they all face difficulties in recognizing angles and low recognition accuracy. Some action recognition algorithms, such as the optical flow estimation, still face characteristics such as a lack of practical teaching scenarios, a lack of action training sets, complex networks, and complex operations. This paper introduces an identity recognition algorithm based on facial recognition algorithm and person re-identification algorithm, which improves the accuracy and effectiveness of recognition by introducing dynamic feature caching. And based on the target classification algorithm of torso and limb recognition, achieve action recognition. Finally, we validated the effectiveness and accuracy of the algorithm in practical engineering courses and conducted comparative experimental analysis.

Introduction

The current artificial intelligence (AI) has greatly facilitated human life, such as autonomous driving1, service robots2. The rich AI technology has also brought great changes to classroom teaching. For example, tools such as ChatGPT3 enable students to engage in interactive and independent learning. Dreambox4, Smart Sparrow5, Knewton5 and other methods facilitate students in making learning plans. Gradescope6 uses machine learning algorithms to grade student assignments.

The increasing role of AI in classroom teaching is evident. However, the application of AI technology in certain engineering practical courses still faces challenges. A typical requirement is the identification of student identity and the recognition of actions. This paper proposes a computer vision based algorithm to address the requirements in this scenario.

CDIO practice courses

Conceive Design Implement Operate (CDIO)7 The engineering education model is the latest achievement of international engineering education reform in recent years, and it has been widely applied and promoted worldwide. It emphasizes the cultivation of students’ initiative and practical skills, providing them with a learning experience that is closer to real engineering environments.

CDIO engineering practice teaching is different from the traditional teaching philosophy of classroom teaching, which is teacher led and students passively receiving knowledge. Teachers impart knowledge to students through lectures, demonstrations, and other methods according to established teaching plans, while students mainly learn through listening, taking notes, and other methods.Fig. 1Two students are learning how to control the movement of a motor through wiring and programming.

Two students are learning how to control the movement of a motor through wiring and programming.

The CDIO engineering practice teaching adopts the life cycle of product development to product operation as its framework, enabling students to integrate knowledge, skills, and attitudes through the process of identifying engineering problems, designing system modules, implementing design outcomes, and conducting operational maintenance in specific project developments.

This pedagogical approach emphasizes “learning by doing” and “doing while learning,” facilitating a comprehensive understanding of engineering knowledge and proficiency in technical abilities among students.

The teaching of CDIO engineering practice is often conducted in a non-traditional learning environment, characterized by staff mobility, dynamic student behavior, and changing student locations as Fig. 1 . Identifying individuals within this instructional process poses a significant challenge.

Face recognition

Face recognition8 as a vital subfield within artificial intelligence, it serves a pivotal function in diverse applications including automatic face recognition, identity verification, face retrieval, and video surveillance.

In recent years, the emergence of deep learning technology has significantly advanced the state of facial recognition algorithms. Models based on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) , such as FaceNet9, DeepFace10. By training on extensive datasets, they have achieved unprecedented levels of recognition accuracy and robustness.

These algorithms are adept at capturing complex features in facial images, such as texture, shape, and variations in lighting, thereby excelling in a wide range of real-world scenarios.

In typical educational settings, such as classroom teaching, these algorithms can naturally capture students’ frontal images, leading to their rapid adoption. However, in the hands-on courses of engineering education, students are constantly in motion. If they move their heads or take other actions, the algorithm loses track of them.

Person re-identification

There are also some deep learning based algorithms attempting to authenticate body images, called person re identification (Re-ID)11 . They use You Only Look Once (YOLO)12 real-time detection algorithm to detect human bodies, obtain images of human bodies, extract features through deep networks, and finally determine identity through matrix multiplication method.

This type of algorithm is more suitable for scenarios where individuals are relatively stationary. However, in engineering practice courses, the number of students in front of the camera, their body movements, and angles are all dynamic, making it challenging for these algorithms to align, or they may result in substantial matching errors.

In summary, this paper will introduce a deep learning algorithm framework that can meet the personnel identification needs in engineering practical courses, and complete multiple sets of comparative experiments in actual teaching to verify the excellent performance of our algorithm.

Related work

Recent advancements in face recognition have leveraged deep learning models, integrating 2D and 3D implicit representations such as multi-plane images and neural radiance fields to recover facial geometric details13, achieving state-of-the-art performance in facial reconstruction and verification tasks on synthetic and real-world datasets.

Other methods, such as those in comprehensive surveys on deep face verification, incorporate attention mechanisms and large-scale datasets to handle variations in pose and expression14. However, these algorithms struggle with dynamic face recognition, as they are sensitive to lighting changes, occlusions, and non-frontal angles, leading to tracking loss issues in dynamic environments such as practical classrooms.

For person re-identification (Re-ID), latest approaches include FusionSegReID15, which fuses image and text modalities via a dual-path encoder to improve matching in occluded scenes. Similarly, MFRNet16 employs sparse mixture-of-experts for multi-modal integration, enhancing robustness across cameras.

Despite these advances, the focus is still on tracking and identifying individuals who have already appeared, and it is not possible to identify new individuals unless they are dressed the same as in the dataset. And they rely on the training of classification tasks, which incurs high time costs and demands high computational resources during training and inference, making them less suitable for resource constrained educational environments.

Face detection

Determining the range of face regions in a picture is the basis of face recognition. There are already a number of mature frameworks and tools to implement this requirement, such as the Dlib library17. The Dlib library provides a face detector with excellent performance, which combines a variety of advanced algorithms, such as Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG)18 features and linear classifiers such as Support Vector Machine (SVM) .Fig. 2On the left is the original face image, and on the right is the visualized HOG feature image.

On the left is the original face image, and on the right is the visualized HOG feature image.

The feature of HOG shown in Fig. 2 , by calculating the gradient direction histogram of the local area of the image, it can effectively capture the shape and edge information of the target, and has a certain robustness to the change of illumination and shadow.

By combining these local histograms, the feature vector of the whole image can be obtained. SVM classifiers can use these features for face detection. The classifier learns the ability to distinguish between faces and non-faces by training a large number of samples.

The face detector of Dlib has achieved high detection accuracy on many public data sets19.

Facial features extraction

Once the face region is detected, facial features are extracted and compared to determine if faces in different images belong to the same person as shown in the Fig. 3.

Keras_vggface20 is a Keras implementation based on the VGGFace project. Keras_vggface supports a variety of pre-trained CNN models, such as VGG16, ResNet50, and SENet50, which are trained by image classification tasks. This paper uses the network structure of the SENet50. The input image will be mapped to a 256x1 dimensional feature vector.

It is worth mentioning that Euclidean distance can be used to calculate the distance between feature vectors in different classes, thus helping to determine whether samples belong to the same class. Samples that are closer together may belong to the same face, while samples that are farther apart may belong to different faces.

Person re-identification

Body recognition algorithms have become an important and extensive research field, especially when combined with deep learning algorithms, which not only improve recognition accuracy but also increase image processing speed.

The YOLO series of algorithms, with their high efficiency and accuracy, are important algorithms in the field of object detection. YOLO v8 not only inherits the advantages of previous generations of algorithms, but also further optimizes performance and accuracy. This algorithm adopts a more efficient network structure, improved anchor generation strategy, and multi-scale prediction technology, making object detection tasks in complex scenes faster and more accurate. Using YOLO v8’s pre trained model, it is possible to quickly identify body regions in images, as shown in Fig. 4 .

Then, using the residual network ResNet50 model, 2048x1 dimensional feature vectors can be obtained from body images. ResNet uses multiple residual blocks with residual connections internally to solve the problem of gradient vanishing or exploding, and enables the network to develop deeper.

It is worth mentioning that when dealing with the similarity between high-dimensional vectors, Euclidean distance often requires a lot of time due to its large computational complexity. Using the Hungarian algorithm not only enables matching between vectors, but also greatly saves time.

The Hungarian Algorithm is a combinatorial optimization algorithm used to solve assignment problems. The core of the Hungarian algorithm is to find an augmented path, gradually expanding the matching by continuously searching and increasing the augmented path until the maximum match of the bipartite graph is found. When a new match conflicts with an existing match, the algorithm recursively adjusts the existing match to free up space for the new match, ensuring that the final result is the maximum match.

However, when students move around in the environment, the size and shape of their body areas change accordingly, which is highly likely to lead to feature matching failure, as shown in Fig. 4.Fig. 4The figures show the successful and failed scenarios of the body re-identification algorithm, with green boxes indicating success and red boxes indicating no match with anyone.

Body movement

Human motion capture has always been an important research field for studying human behavior, and how to classify and recognize different actions has always been at the forefront of behavior recognition research.

Relatively complex algorithms such as two-stream convolutional networks21 and optical flow estimation22 have achieved good results. Although these algorithms take into account temporal action sequences, they require a huge amount of computation during processing and require a large training set for training.

In practical engineering courses, it is necessary to identify student behavior. Students’ movements in class are relatively fixed, and in order to meet the coherence of the course video, the recognition speed requirement is relatively high.

In summary, this paper proposes a deep learning algorithm framework for personnel identification and action recognition in dynamic scenarios that meet the requirements of engineering practical courses. To meet both the accuracy requirements for recognition and the speed requirements.

Finally, we validated the effectiveness of the algorithm in a practical motor drive course experiment and compared the advantages and disadvantages of different identification methods.

Approach

We propose an algorithmic framework to fulfill the requirement of person Re-ID in engineering practice courses. The execution process of the algorithm is shown in Fig. 5 . The workflow begins on the left side of the system, where students’ facial features are first pre-registered into the Registration Database. During login, students authenticate via facial recognition while the system simultaneously captures their body image features as shown in the Algorithm 1. The “Identification during the process”phase initiates dynamic real-time identification in the classroom, utilizing inputs including: (1) pre-registered facial features, (2) body features acquired during login, and (3) dynamically detected real-time facial and body features. The system validates the recognition result as trustworthy only when both facial and body recognition outcomes are consistent.

Furthermore, the dynamic feature library update mechanism is critical for enhancing system generalizability: Upon successful facial recognition, the system updates the body feature buffer with the latest reliable data (e.g., body orientation and scale in the scene). If all subjects’ body recognition results are correct and their positions remain unchanged, the system further refines the facial feature buffer to mitigate interference from directional and lighting variations. The complete implementation is detailed in the pseudocode of Algorithm 2.

By leveraging face image, body image, and location information, our framework will establish a dynamic fusion feature database encompassing these data throughout the entire class based on our algorithmic rules. This approach significantly enhances the feature data essential for Re-ID purposes.Fig. 5This figure shows the processing flow of the core algorithm in this article, where the registered database provides student facial features. Initial features

Initial characteristics refer to the original information we can obtain that can be directly identified with the student. In engineering practice courses, students will submit their positive image photos to the educational administration system.

After the educational administration system associates student information with course information, students can log in during class time by using the algorithm in this paper. When we log in to match, we will get the student’s body image information.

We must explain why we do not use student body images in the teaching system, as students do not wear the same clothes during practical classes.

In addition, the reason why the on-site face features when students log in are not added to the dynamic face image buffer is that it is not only small enough with the face features in the educational administration system, but also the face features in the dynamic face image buffer are more likely to be from different angles of students.

To summarize, the algorithm incorporates two initial features of students: the facial characteristics recorded in the educational administration system, and the body image information captured during practice class login.

Feature extraction

Facial features

Whether it is the login link or the course process, we identify the student’s face by comparing the similarity between the feature values of the face image, the specific steps are as follows. First, we use the face detector in the Dilib framework introduced above to get all the face region images in the figure, and then we use the SENet50 network classifier to get 256 × 1 dimensional face features.

It should also be explained here that we use Euclide distance (formula) to judge the similarity of two face features, that is, the larger the calculated distance result indicates that the face is not similar, and the smaller the distance result, the more similar the description.

Dynamic feature construction

Using only one face image in the educational administration system and one body image when logging in, obviously can not meet the needs of student identity confirmation in the dynamic environment of engineering practice courses.

Therefore, the main innovation of this paper is to enrich the information of students’ face and body characteristics in a dynamic scene. This job is challenging, and in later chapters, we will analyze in detail when they can be added.

Body dynamics

As mentioned earlier, we confirm a student’s identity information by the similarity of both body and facial features reaching a threshold.

At the same time, we are more confident about the face matching results, and the correct rate after testing is more than 95%. And based on the fact that we’re processing images at 10 to 15 frames per second.

Our specific practice is that when the student’s face matches in each frame, the body does not match, and the student’s face and body matches in the previous frame. The student’s body image features of the frame are added to the dynamic buffer.

The scenes that match static and dynamic features are shown in the Fig. 7, in the left image, the pink facial bounding box indicates a successful match between the detected facial features and those in the registered database, while the green body bounding box corresponds to the matching result of body features extracted during login. In the right image, the blue bounding boxes highlight both facial and body regions that have been successfully matched against the dynamic database.

Experimental results demonstrate that the system achieves accurate identity recognition even under significant variations in pose, scale, and lighting conditions for both face and body in video footage.

Facial dynamics

We found that the accuracy of the body recognition algorithm was less than 90% when validating it, so we cannot absolutely believe that at any given moment, the body features match but the facial features do not match, regardless of whether the student’s previous frame was a facial feature or all body features match.

But considering the fact that the image processing speed is much faster than the student’s movement speed, our specific approach is as follows.

When a student’s body features have been matched in the frame, but their facial features have not been matched. If the position of the person in this frame of the image changes within a minimum range compared to the position of the person in the previous frame. We believe that at this moment, the student made a movement of turning their head and looking up or down. We can add the facial features of this student to the dynamic buffer.Fig. 7The figures show the scene where our algorithm correctly matches the identity of the students through static features. Behavior recognition

This paper adopts an image classification algorithm based on human limb framework recognition for student behavior recognition as shown in Fig. 8 .

Specifically, we capture human postures based on the OpenPifPaf framework23. OpenPifPaf can use simple lines to construct the body frame of a person, as shown in the Fig. 8. The head, torso, and limbs can all be defined with different colored lines, similar in shape to a“stick figure”.

Next, we will use LabelImg24 to mark some of the actions selected in the box to create a training set, including programming, wiring, looking at the phone, etc. By using the YOLO v8 object recognition algorithm to train on this training set, we can obtain our action recognition model. Its reasoning speed is very fast, with a processing speed of over 20 frames per second when capturing images at 24 frames per second.

Experimental setup

In this study, we utilize two pre-trained models in the identity recognition phase that integrates face recognition and pedestrian re-identification, without constructing a training set, which is also an advantage of our method. Our test data is based on six Asian students, with a total of six videos that include login and practical course. We use the students’ names as the file names for their frontal selfie photos, which serve as labels for login and testing in the course. The experiment is conducted entirely indoors under normal lighting conditions.

It should also be noted that data augmentation techniques were not employed in this study.

All evaluations were conducted on a single host machine equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU, an Intel Core i9-14900 CPU, and 32 GB of RAM. Experiments primarily focused on a two-person scenario (typically one teacher and one student, as common in this teaching environment), testing and recording data from video footage captured during the servo drive motor control practice course at 24 frames per second using standard surveillance cameras. The algorithm also demonstrated stable and correct performance in 4-6 person scenarios, handling dynamic movements without significant errors.

For action recognition, we collected behavioral videos for three action types: playing with a phone (phone), connecting wires (connect), and programming (program), comprising approximately 240 labeled frames (80 per action). Labeling used LabelImg to annotate bounding boxes around human poses from OpenPifPaf skeletons, creating a custom training set. The YOLOv8 model was fine-tuned with pre-trained weights, achieving inference speeds over 20 frames per second. Conclusion

This paper proposes an algorithm that combines facial recognition and body re-recognition to construct a dynamic feature library for personnel identity recognition tasks in dynamic teaching scenarios, while also considering action recognition. The effectiveness of the algorithm has been verified in actual courses, and comparative experiments have been conducted using facial recognition and body re-recognition algorithms separately. The results indicate that our algorithm performs better.

Although this study utilized portrait images with informed consent, the importance of privacy protection remains paramount as the system is scaled for broader teaching applications. We advise adopting strict data minimization practices, preserving only essential information such as anonymized pose data. Encrypting original images using student-specific keys can effectively prevent the misuse of private data. Future work will explore privacy-preserving techniques, such as federated learning or differential privacy, to balance monitoring needs with ethical data management.

Although our algorithm has achieved good performance in practical courses, there are still many issues with identity recognition under time and posture, largely due to the inability to match side face images with front face images.

Our next plan is to use 3DMM26 facial modeling technology to collect facial information and gradually construct a three-dimensional student head model, combined with skin materials, in order to achieve matching of side face images.

In engineering practical courses, due to the complex and inconsistent problems encountered by students in practice, it has always been a research difficulty in AI teaching. We will attempt to use reinforcement learning and self-play methods27 to collect students’ questions and train AI problem-solving skills in virtual simulation software, ultimately applying AI to help solve students’ problems in actual classrooms.
