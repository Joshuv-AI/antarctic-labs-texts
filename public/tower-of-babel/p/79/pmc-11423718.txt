Are Ethiopian health facilities providing diabetes services capable of managing tuberculosis? Policy implications for introducing diabetes and tuberculosis collaborative care
Authors: Getahun Asmamaw, Tefera Minwagaw, Mastewal Samuel, Wondim Ayenew
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the availability and preparedness of health facilities offering diabetes mellitus (DM) to manage tuberculosis (TB) in Ethiopia.DesignSecondary data analysis of institution-based cross-sectional national survey data.SettingData were obtained from the 2021–2022 Ethiopian Service Provision Assessment (ESPA) survey which includes all active health facilities in Ethiopia.ParticipantsThis study included all health facilities that provide DM services (both diagnosis and treatment) and recently collected DM data during the 2021–2022 ESPA survey.Outcome measuresThe service availability was computed as the percentage of facilities offering DM services to provide TB management. The preparedness of these facilities for managing TB was measured using three service tracer indicators (staff training and guidelines, diagnostics and essential medicines used in TB management) defined by the WHO Service Availability and Readiness Assessment Manual. The extent of preparedness was categorised as low level (<50%), moderate level (≥50% to 75%) and high level (≥75%). A descriptive statistic was employed to present the study findings.ResultsOnly 170 out of 338 facilities were reported to provide DM services across the country, with 136 (70%) also offering TB management services. Among these facilities, the majority were health centres (n=82; 60.1%), publicly owned (n=98; 72.0%), rural (n=83; 60.7%) and located in the Oromia region (n=53; 39.0%). Regarding preparedness, facilities offering DM services had an overall moderate to high preparedness for managing TB, with 47 (27.5%), 53 (31.1%) and 70 (41.3%) facilities classified as low-level, moderate-level and high-level preparedness, respectively. Specifically, these facilities were less prepared in the domains of ‘trained staff and guidelines’ and ‘diagnostics’, but had better preparedness in providing first-line TB drugs, with an overall score of 79.7%.ConclusionsAlthough the percentage availability of TB services in facilities offering DM services and their preparedness was unsatisfactory compared with WHO standards, Ethiopia has a greater capacity to implement WHO strategies to reduce the burden of TB-DM comorbidity. However, given the high epidemiological risk and the high burden of both diseases in the country, our findings emphasise the urgent necessity to establish and implement a collaborative TB-DM care plan to integrate TB services within facilities providing DM care.Keywords: tuberculosis, health policy, general diabetes

Background

The twin diagnosis of diabetes mellitus (DM) and tuberculosis (TB) has emerged as a significant public health concern worldwide and a major public health challenge in several countries.1 2 Globally, more than 10.6 million individuals equivalent to 127/100 000 population were diagnosed with TB and it caused more than 1.3 million deaths in 2022.3 4 The global rate of decrease in the incidence of death due to active TB was reduced by 19% from 2015 to 2022.3 However, this rate is so far from the WHO’s ‘End TB Strategy’, milestone reduction of TB mortality by 75% by 2025.3 Meanwhile, the global trends show that DM prevalence has increased from 422 to 537 million individuals from 2014 to 2021, and in 2019, it was directly responsible for around 1.2 million deaths.5 6 Moreover, globally, between 2000 and 2019, there was a 3% increase in DM mortality rates by age.6

In 2019, 1.5 million individuals were diagnosed with dual TB-DM globally. Existing studies show that the coexistence of TB and DM has long been associated with high mortality and morbidity rates.7 8 Patients with DM are three times more likely to develop active TB and have a significant risk of treatment failure.7 Evidence also showed that patients with TB have poor glycaemic control, which complicates the treatment outcome of diabetes.9 Furthermore, patients with coupled TB and DM experience severe symptoms and a higher risk of mortality than patients with TB without DM.10 Currently, their dual existence is one of the biggest constraints in meeting WHO’s ‘End TB Strategy’, milestone by 2030.11 12

TB remains one of the main causes of morbidity and mortality in Ethiopia and other low-income and medium-income countries (LMICs).13 For example, Ethiopia is among the top 30 countries with a high TB burden in the world.3 The annual incidence rate of TB in Ethiopia has decreased slightly from 214 to 119/100 000 population from 2014 to 2021.14 15 The estimated number of deaths due to TB was reduced slightly from 26 to 19/100 000 population in 2015 and 2019, respectively.16 Interestingly, LMICs accounted for the majority of the increase in prevalence and incidence of DM over the aforementioned years. According to the International Diabetes Federation report, there were over 14.2 million individuals with diabetes in Africa, and this figure is expected to rise to 55 million by 2045.5 Likewise, Ethiopia has recently seen a substantial increase in the burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) such as DM, particularly in the recent two decades.16,18 According to the review of Bishu AG et al, the pooled estimates of DM prevalence in Ethiopia ranged from 2.0% to 6.5%.17 In 2021, 3.3% of Ethiopians aged 20–79 years had DM, and a total of 1.9 million cases of the disease were reported nationwide.5 16 In addition, diabetes ranked eighth among all causes of mortality among those 5 years and older in 2019, in Ethiopia.16

A rise in the incidence of TB-DM comorbidity is emerging as critical for public health in Ethiopia and other LMICs.2 19 A recent systematic review of 14 articles indicated that the pooled prevalence of TB among patients with DM and DM among patients with TB was 4.1% and 12.8%, respectively.19 In Ethiopia, there is no comprehensive nationwide data on the burden of TB-DM comorbidity, and the healthcare system does not undertake specific strategies to address the country’s growing burden of TB-DM comorbidity.19 The WHO recommends bidirectional screening for TB-DM comorbidity.8 However, this has been applied to different extents in LMICs, with Ethiopia yet to begin.8 19 20 In Ethiopia, both diabetic and TB services are available at all levels of health facilities, which are interconnected in the hierarchical manner to provide basic and advanced level services for each of those diseases.21 22 The Ethiopian health system features a service delivery-based three-tiered framework, comprising both public and private health facilities on each level. Primary-level healthcare includes health posts, health centres and primary hospitals. Health posts are designed to offer preventative care and health-promoting services. Health centres offer both preventative and curative services. Primary hospitals provide inpatient and outpatient services, along with emergency glycaemic control. Secondary and tertiary healthcare are made up of general hospitals and specialised hospitals, respectively. In terms of private health facilities, primary-level facilities include private pharmacies, drugstores, and lower and medium clinics; secondary-level facilities include specialty clinics and primary hospitals and tertiary-level facilities include general hospitals and specialty centres.21 23 Across the Ethiopian healthcare tier system, health institutions should provide DM-related preventative treatments, conduct routine examinations and investigations, diagnose and treat patients with DM and refer severe cases to higher-level facilities.20 21

Ensuring access to quality health services is one of the primary functions of a health system.20 24 Thus, such a function is supposed to be satisfied by ensuring service availability, expanding physical facility reach and improving preparedness capacity to provide services. The WHO Collaborative Framework for Care and Control of TB and DM Comorbidity developed guidelines for implementing collaboration strategies, such as joint coordination, bidirectional screening surveillance and collaborative action for TB and comorbid DM management.3 Moreover, the WHO-Service Availability and Readiness Assessment (SARA) set basic requirements for health service delivery and readiness for particular health services, such as diabetes and TB.24

Currently, there is inadequate evidence of Ethiopian healthcare facilities’ capacity and preparedness for managing NCDs, including DM. According to the Ethiopian 2016 SARA report, Ethiopian health institutions were not sufficiently prepared to care for patients with the majority of NCDs, including DM.20 Additionally, the status, availability of services and preparedness of the limited number of facilities providing DM services to manage TB remain unrealised. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the service availability and preparedness of health facilities providing DM care for managing TB in Ethiopia. The topics of our study can be best addressed by analysing national service provision assessment surveys, which were designed to collect vital data about the state of fundamental health services, specifically health facility service readiness and availability, across all levels of health facilities in a given country.20 21 24 Furthermore, given the existing epidemiological risks and high burden of TB and DM in Ethiopia, the findings of this study will contribute to a better understanding of the potential and extent of preparedness of Ethiopian healthcare facilities for managing TB and DM comorbidity. This could benefit decision-makers and other relevant bodies to identify areas where action is needed in the country.

Methods

Data source

In this study, secondary data analysis was conducted using data from the most recent Ethiopian Service Provision Assessment survey (ESPA 2021–2022). The recent ESPA survey was the second of its kind after the 2014 ESPA+in Ethiopia.21 The Ethiopian Public Health Institute and the Ethiopian Ministry of Health collaborated to undertake the current survey. The US Agency for International Development and International Classification Functioning Company (ICF-International) (under the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) Program) provided funding and technical support for the survey, respectively. The survey was designed to gather vital information about the state of basic health services, particularly their service preparedness and availability, at all levels of health facilities in Ethiopia. Its goal was to provide a thorough overview of the Ethiopian healthcare delivery system by collecting data on the availability and functionality of key elements of services that are essential to delivering high-quality medical care. The survey addresses a wide range of healthcare service categories, including child health, maternity and newborn care, family planning, sexually transmitted infections, HIV/AIDS, TB, malaria, NCDs and neglected tropical diseases.

Sampling technique and sample size

The 2021–2022 ESPA survey represented all functional health institutions in the country except for the Tigray regional state.21 A comprehensive list of health facilities was obtained from the Ethiopian Ministry of Health, which accounts for 25 711 active health institutions for the survey. The list included hospitals, health centres, health posts and private clinics (specialty/higher, medium and lower clinics). Those included facilities were either governed by the government, private for-profit agencies or non-governmental organisations. The data gathering instrument used in the survey comprised primarily of three types of questionnaires: facility inventory questionnaires, health provider interviews and client-exit interviews. Moreover, the current ESPA survey used stratified random selection and systematic sampling methods to choose 1407 health institutions. Hence, it was intended to offer nationally representative data for each of the eleven regional states in Ethiopia, besides data for overall national facilities and facility types such as hospitals (both public and private), health centres, clinics and health posts.

This study included all health facilities that provide DM services and recently collected DM data in the 2021–2022 ESPA survey. Because this study analysed data from the 2021–2022 ESPA survey’s facility inventory file, which served as the unit of analysis at the facility level, the final inclusion of health facilities was determined after filtering facilities eligible for the survey. The 2021–2022 ESPA survey included all health facilities being willing to participate in the survey, being open/operational on the day of the interview, having no security issues, being reachable and not having been converted into a coronavirus centre. Consequently, among the total 1407 facilities, the following were excluded: 125 facilities due to security/unreachability concerns, 96 were closed/not active on the day of collecting data and 28 facilities switched to COVID-19 centres. Among the eligible facilities for the 2021–2022 ESPA survey, 36 did not offer any type of DM services, 192 facilities provided either DM diagnosis or treatment only and all health posts as the DM data were not provided in the data set. Hence, only 577 (weighted=170) health sectors were included in the analysis of this study (figure 1).

Patient and public involvement

There was no direct public and patient involvement during in implementation of this study.

Measuring variables

Outcome variables

The ‘availability of TB services’ among facilities offering DM services and their ‘preparedness’ to manage TB were the two outcome variables of this study. In this study, ‘DM services’ means providing both diagnosis and treatment for any type of DM (type I and type II). ‘Availability’ was computed as a percentage of facilities offering DM services to provide TB care. Service availability is the percentage availability of fundamental tracer indicators of TB management services in facilities providing DM services. For this study, a basic tracer indicator for TB management services that must be available in each facility structure was adopted from the recent Ethiopian Ministry of health-SARA manuals and ESPA reports.14 20 21 According to WHO-SARA standards, tracer indicators are intended to offer objective information about whether a health facility meets the minimum requirements to ensure the provision of basic or specific services at a consistent level of quantity and quality.24

In this study, WHO-SARA protocols were used to determine whether facility services were available or not.24 According to the protocol, the TB service was categorised as ‘available’ if the facility reported 100% of the TB service availability indicators. The second outcome variable was facility ‘preparedness’, defined as the preparedness of the diabetic facility to offer TB management. Since the preparedness score is a relative measure, the preparedness index scores were classified by clusters labelled low-level, medium-level and high-level preparedness groups in this study. Clusters with a score (preparedness index score of all facilities) falling in the top ≥75% were considered in the high-level prepared group; those with a preparedness index score between ≥50% and 75% were categorised into the moderate-level prepared group; and the rest (<50%) were put in the low-level prepared group. Based on the WHO-SARA standard, the responses were pooled into an index score to generate an aggregate score.24 The index score was computed by adding each available indicator and dividing it by the total number of indicators in that domain. To find the preparedness, the calculated index scores in each three domains were summed up once again and divided by the total number of domains (three) and converted to a percentage. The cut-off point employed in this study was also used somewhere else to trichotomise the outcome variable.25,27

Preparedness was also rated as an index that classified into three basic tracer indicator domains as recommended by the WHO-SARA standards.24 In this study, the first domain was ‘staff and guidelines’ which has four indicators; the presence of a guideline for TB diagnosis and treatment, guidelines for guideline for multidrug resistant (MDR)-TB management, at least one staff who received a refresher training in TB diagnosis and treatment. The facilities with guidelines were categorised as ‘yes’ while those without such guidelines were categorised as ‘no’. Similarly, facilities with at least one staff member who had received refresher training in TB diagnosis and treatment within 24 months were categorised as ‘yes’, otherwise they were categorised as ‘no’. The second domain was diagnostic equipment which had one indicator as the main TB diagnostic method across the health facility structures in the Ethiopian context; the sputum smear microscopy.21 The facilities having the aforementioned diagnostic equipment were classified as ‘yes’ otherwise they were classified as ‘no’. The third domain was key medicines which had one indicator; the availability of the first-line fixed TB-drugs combination; isoniazid, pyrazinamide, rifampicin and ethambutol. The facilities with the four TB-drug combinations were categorised as ‘yes’, otherwise, they were categorised as ‘no’.

Independent variables

The facility type was categorised as ‘hospital’, ‘health centre’ and ‘clinic’; the place of residence was categorised as ‘urban’ and ‘rural’; managing authority was categorised as ‘public’ and ‘private’; the region was categorised as 10 regional states (‘Afar’, ‘Amhara’, ‘Oromia’, ‘Somali’, ‘Benishangul Gumuz’, ‘South Nation Nationalities and People (SNNP)’, ‘Sidama’, ‘Gambela’ and ‘Harari’) and 2 city administrations (‘Addis Ababa’ and ‘Dire Dawa’).

Data analysis

In this study, all estimates were weighted to adjust for non-responses and sampling disproportionality. The STATA V.14.2 (StataCorp) statistical software was used to analyse the survey data. A ‘svy’ prefixed Stata command was used throughout the analyses to adjust for the complex sampling design of the ESPA survey. The descriptive method of statistical analysis was used to present the findings of the present study.

Results

Distribution characteristics of healthcare facilities

In the Ethiopian SPA 2021–2022 survey, a total of 170 out of 338 (50.3%) health facilities reported providing DM care. Of the 170, diabetes facilities, 148 (87%) were health centres and clinics, and 106 (62.4%) were located in an urban setting. When assessed by managing authority, the majority of the facilities (n=100; 58.8%) were publicly owned. The availability of DM services varies by region, ranging from 66 (38.8%) in Oromia to 1 (0.6%) in the Benishangul Gumuz regional state (table 1).

As shown in figure 2, only 71 (45.9%) facilities offering DM services had at least one trained staff member for the management of TB. The percentage of facilities reported to have any type of TB guidelines was 57.1% (n=97), and only 40.0% (n=68) of them had guidelines for MDR-TB management. In terms of the availability of diagnostics for TB, 60.0% of (n=102) facilities offering DM services had TB sputum smear microscopy. Concerning the availability of essential medicines, only 84 (49.4%) of them were reported to have first-line fixed anti-TB drugs.

Preparedness of health facilities

The overall preparedness analysis indicated nearly more than 70% (n=123) of included facilities had moderate to high levels of preparedness to provide TB management (figure 3). In the meanwhile, the share of facilities laid under low-level, moderate-level and high-level preparedness was 47 (27.5%), 53 (31.1%) and 70 (41.3%), respectively. Health centres showed better preparedness than hospitals and clinics. Thus, health centres account for the majority (32.0%/41.3%) at the level of higher preparedness. However, it was estimated that the majority of clinics (15.4%/27.5%) had a low to moderate level of preparedness. Facilities offering DM services that were publicly owned (37.6%/41.3%) and located in rural settings (21.9%/41.3%) shared the largest among facilities that scored a higher level of preparedness to provide TB care. Additionally, facilities located in the Oromia regional state showed a higher (16.6%/41.3%) level of preparedness, but the majority (9.5%/27.5%) of facilities that were hardly prepared were located in the SNNP regional state.

The percentage distribution of health facility preparedness for treatment of TB by facility characteristics. DM, diabetes mellitus; SNNP, South Nation Nationalities and People; TB, tuberculosis.

Concerning the specific domains, none of the facilities offering DM services were highly prepared to provide TB management in terms of ‘trained staff and guidelines’ and ‘diagnostics’. However, only facilities located in the Benishangul Gumuz region were highly prepared in the domain of trained staff and guidelines to manage TB, with a preparedness level of 87.7% (table 2). Thus, hospitals (68.0%), public facilities (65.3%) and urban facilities (60.8%) showed better preparedness in this domain. Moreover, only DM hospitals (83.9%) were prepared in the domain of diagnostics (TB microscopy). Public (67.9%), urban (64.5%), and Oromia region (68.2%) dweller facilities demonstrated better diagnostic preparedness. In terms of the medicine’s domain, all facility characteristics were highly prepared to offer TB management accounting for the overall score of 79.7% (SD=40.3%).

Discussion

The findings of this study revealed the status and extent of DM facility capability to manage TB. The capability was demonstrated by the percent service availability and level of preparedness of facilities offering DM services to provide TB services in Ethiopia. Hence, our analysis revealed that, despite the limited number of health facilities providing DM care in Ethiopia, a majority of them also provide TB management. Additionally, the findings of this study illustrated a disparity in the proportion of TB service availability, with relatively high availability of diagnostics, but a relatively lower score in terms of guidelines, trained staff and essential medicine availability. Interestingly, among facilities offering DM services that also provide TB services, about three-quarters scored moderate to high levels of preparedness to provide TB management.

In this study, about 136 (80%) of the facilities offering DM services reported to provide TB services. The observed higher proportion of health facilities providing both TB and DM dually could be attributed to the retained burden of both diseases in the last three decades prompting the government to expand these services across the country.35 12 14,19 28 29 This study also revealed that TB services were mainly available in health centres, public health facilities and rural facilities that also offer DM services. These findings are consistent as reported in a prior study conducted in Tanzania.30 Our findings could be explained by the fact that about 77.9% of the population of Ethiopia lives in rural areas, which also have a majority of publicly owned health centres.21 31 Another possible explanation is that the positive impact of measures such as universal healthcare coverage initiatives implemented over the last three decades might have improved the distribution of TB services across private and rural settings, in Ethiopia.32 For example, before 2005, the provision of TB services was majorly limited in public and urban health facilities.28 33 However, in recent times, there has been a relatively significant rise in the availability of TB services among private sectors and rural settings of the country.34 However, this study showed that there was a low proportion of facilities offering DM services that have all of the three tracer domains such as guidelines, trained professionals, equipment and essential medicines for TB control. This could imply gaps in access to TB care, as the availability of each domain is required to ensure access to TB services. A similar study from Tanzania was also consistent with our findings.30 Ensuring the availability of TB care at diabetic facilities may contribute to lowering the burden and incidence of active TB, improving the likelihood of achieving the 2030 ‘End TB Strategy’ goals.3

This study revealed that facilities offering DM services had an overall moderate to higher preparedness to offer TB services. Interestingly, the findings of this study could indicate, the potential feasibility of implementing TB-DM collaborative care among health facilities in Ethiopia. On the other hand, in this study, one-third of facilities offering DM services were not adequately prepared to provide TB services. This could be owing to a lack of professionals trained to jointly manage TB in facilities providing DM services. Other contributors could include insufficient TB care guidelines, medicines and diagnostics.21 33 Furthermore, this study showed variations in preparedness across the three domains. Thus, the higher levels of healthcare, public and urban facilities with DM services had adequate TB-microscopy needed to manage TB. In contrast, higher preparation for medicine was demonstrated across the facility characteristics. It is crucial to emphasise that each of the three domains such as staff and guidelines, diagnostics and medicine are required for the effective provision of TB services in facilities offering DM services.24 Our findings are consistent with two prior studies conducted in Tanzania and Ethiopia to examine the readiness of health institutions to provide dual TB-DM and NCD services, respectively.27 28 For example, another study performed based on a secondary analysis of the 2021–2022 ESPA indicated that health facilities in Ethiopia were not adequately prepared to offer NCD care, including DM.33

It has been observed that bidirectional screening for TB and DM is feasible and produces significant outcomes for TB in patients with DM, and the opposite is true.2 35 Moreover, the dual TB-DM active screening and diagnosis could lower the rate of transmission and incidence of TB while also preventing the development of complications associated with DM.35 The findings of this study also indicated that even though the proportion availability of TB services and preparedness among facilities offering DM services is unsatisfactory as compared with WHO specifications, Ethiopia has a high potential to implement the WHO strategies to combat TB-DM comorbidity and its burden.

WHO suggests dual synchronisation for bidirectional patient monitoring, screening, diagnosis and treatment of TB-DM cases.8 However, TB-DM collaborative care strategies have not yet been developed or implemented in Ethiopia. Thus, given the high burden of TB and DM is a significant concern in Ethiopia, and our study findings illustrate that facilities providing DM services in Ethiopia are not adequately prepared to manage TB despite having a high potential capability to manage TB, government intervention is needed to develop joint TB and DM care strategies. These strategies should also be incorporated into the current national Strategic Action Plan for the prevention and control of major NCDs in Ethiopia.36 Additionally, we also recommend that now is an ideal time for implementing these strategic measures to effectively prevent and control TB-DM comorbidity in the country. Recently, recognising the burden of TB-DM comorbidity, other LMICs have begun taking measures and are responding to WHO recommendations. For instance, although it has not yet been fully implemented, the Tanzanian government developed a specific guideline for joint TB-DM care in 2016.30 37 Furthermore, the country has incorporated these efforts into the Tanzanian Priority NCDs strategy plan II (2016–2020), which outlines priority actions such as training healthcare providers in collaborative TB-DM management, selecting phase implementation sites, strengthening referral and linkage techniques and expanding dual TB-DM services.37

The current study has several limitations. First, since the study design is cross-sectional, it is difficult to tell changes that might have occurred over time regarding the preparedness of diabetes facilities to manage TB. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution when compared with other study designs. Also, the current study did not assess the availability of other important diagnostic methods for TB such as culture and molecular diagnostic techniques (nucleic acid amplification test and whole genome sequencing). This is because the letter is very sophisticated, costly and rarely available in peripheral healthcare facilities.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, to our knowledge, this is the first study to provide additional information regarding the extent to which Ethiopian facilities offering DM services are available and prepared to provide TB services. Second, the sample size was nationally representative, implying that the current results appropriately reflect the present status of health facility preparedness to integrate DM and TB services in Ethiopia. Furthermore, considering the complicated sampling strategies used in the ESPA survey, the findings of this study were adjusted for the clustering effect and weighted to compensate for non-response and disproportionate sampling. This study has some limitations, but they are not sufficient to invalidate our study outcomes. Therefore, the findings of this study should be interpreted with caution due to the following limitations. First, as this study used a cross-sectional survey, the findings could not detect the long-term trends in the preparedness of facilities offering DM services to manage TB. Hence, interpreting our findings should consider the drawbacks of the study design used in this analysis. Second, the current study did not analyse the availability of other essential TB diagnostic procedures, such as culture and molecular techniques (nucleic acid amplification test and whole genome sequence). This is because these diagnostics are rarely available in peripheral health institutions due to their sophistication and high cost in the country. However, this could have altered the findings. Finally, applying arbitrary cut-off thresholds might incorrectly classify the capability of facilities offering DM services to provide TB services.

Conclusions

In this study, facilities offering DM services had an overall unsatisfactory service availability and level of preparedness to provide TB services as compared with WHO recommendations. However, Ethiopia has a higher potential to implement the WHO measures to combat the burden of TB-DM comorbidity. Given the high risk of active TB infection in patients with DM, their vice versa risk of treatment failure, and the high burden of both diseases in Ethiopia, the findings of this study further highlight the urgent need to develop and implement a collaborative TB-DM care plan to integrate TB services within facilities offering DM services. The proposed integration strategy should focus on expanding the availability of TB guidelines, providing refresher training courses for TB-DM comanagement and enhancing the availability of basic diagnostic equipment and essential medications, particularly in urban and private facilities.
