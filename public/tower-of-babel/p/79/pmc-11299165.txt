Peripheral and central pathogenesis of postherpetic neuralgia
Authors: Qiuping Liu, Jingxian Han, Xuezhu Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostherpetic neuralgia (PHN) is a classic chronic condition with multiple signs of peripheral and central neuropathy. Unfortunately, the pathogenesis of PHN is not well defined, limiting clinical treatment and disease management.ObjectiveTo describe the peripheral and central pathological axes of PHN, including peripheral nerve injury, inflammation induction, central nervous system sensitization, and brain functional and structural network activity.MethodsA bibliographic survey was carried out, selecting relevant articles that evaluated the characterization of the pathogenesis of PHN, including peripheral and central pathological axes.ResultsCurrently, due to the complexity of the pathophysiological mechanisms of PHN and the incomplete understanding of the exact mechanism of neuralgia.ConclusionIt is essential to conduct in‐depth research to clarify the origins of PHN pathogenesis and explore effective and comprehensive therapies for PHN.

INTRODUCTION

PHN is a classic neuropathic pain syndrome characterized by persistent sharp, burning, or stabbing pain with multiple abnormal sensory symptoms. 1 It occurs when the latent varicella‐zoster virus (VZV) in the sensory ganglia is reactivated. The virus then migrates to the skin along the innervating sensory fibers. 2 PHN is the most common and difficult‐to‐treat complication of herpes zoster (HZ). 3 Prospective cohort studies show a 20%–30% risk of PHN in elderly HZ patients. 4 , 5 Age is the most significant independent risk factor for PHN, possibly due to decreased cellular immune function. Also, acute pain and rash severity in HZ increases with age. 6 The elderly cannot effectively clear VZV from their body, resulting in prolonged high virus levels. This severely impairs neurorepair function and increases the risk of PHN. 7 , 8

PHN is associated with various central and peripheral neuropathic symptoms. 9 Patients with PHN may develop anxiety, depression, sleep disturbance, and suicidal ideations because of long‐term persistent pain, which significantly lowers their quality of life. 10 , 11 Currently, there is no specific treatment for PHN. Due to its complex pathogenesis, neuropathic pain is difficult to treat with traditional pharmacological interventions, which can cause multiple drug‐related side effects. 12 A systematic review of current evidence suggests that more research is needed to determine optimal drug choices, combinations, and treatment sequences for PHN. The underlying pathology or pain‐causing factors have not been identified; thus, effective treatment options are limited. 13 This paper provides a systematic review of the possible pathological mechanisms of PHN and a detailed pathological classification to advance efficient and targeted PHN treatment (Figure 1).

The possible pathological mechanisms of PHN.

PERIPHERAL PATHOLOGICAL MECHANISM OF PHN

Damage to peripheral nerves

The pathophysiology of PHN is similar to that of peripheral nervous system disorders. Inflammation activates stress nociceptors in peripheral tissues, causing skin pain. Cutaneous nerve dysfunction occurs via injury‐related nociceptor excitation and inflammatory processes. 14 PHN skin lesions directly invade nerve roots, causing nociceptive hypersensitivity and abnormal pain. The skin innervation density is a measure of the density of central projections from primary afferent neurons to secondary sensory neurons. Skin biopsy studies of painful neuropathy syndrome revealed episodic skin innervation loss. 15 and extensive primary afferent neuron loss was observed in HZ. 16 VZV induces a neuroinflammatory response that causes anatomical and functional peripheral nerve, dorsal root ganglion, and spinal nerve damage. Subsequently, the injured tissues release inflammatory mediators, which lower the nociceptive threshold and activate peripheral nociceptors. This causes neuropathic pain and sensory abnormalities in the affected skin areas, promoting peripheral sensitization. 17 Peripheral sensitization lowers the cutaneous nociceptor's threshold and enhances cutaneous pulse discharge, which causes abnormal pain. 18 Additionally, PHN patients experience significantly reduced cutaneous nerve density and extensive loss of nociceptive nerve endings in affected areas. 19 The absence of cutaneous innervation density was also negatively correlated with the severity of abnormal pain. Skin puncture biopsies of the most painful sites revealed a direct correlation between thermal sensitivity and cutaneous innervation density. 19

The role of peripheral inflammation in PHN

Dormant VZV is reactivated and enters the skin and bloodstream through peripheral sensory nerve fibers when immune function declines. The reactivation causes skin inflammation, triggers an immune response, and damages peripheral fibers. 16 VZV infection reactivation leads to the production of inflammatory factors, including IL‐1 and TNF‐α, from infected epidermal cells and fibroblasts. 20 , 21 Therefore, inflammation contributes to the pathogenesis of PHN. 22 Microglia and astrocytes are activated after peripheral injury. 23 and secrete growth factors, cytokines, and nitric oxide. 24 which are believed to be mediators of acute and chronic pain. 25 , 26 Nerve cells are a potential source of pain in peripheral nerve inflammatory diseases. 27 Altered expression of neuroinflammatory factors in glial cells promotes the development of neuropathic pain. Developing and maintaining neuropathic pain is mediated by microglia and astrocyte activation, proliferation, and subsequent overproduction of inflammatory mediators. 28 , 29 In addition, the blood‐nerve barrier is damaged by neurogenic inflammation triggered by α1‐adrenergic receptors and injury‐evoked inflammation aggravated by keratinocytes. 30 Subsequently, α1‐adrenoceptor activation causes nociceptor hyperexcitability, facilitates pain and axon reflexes, and aggravates the neurogenic inflammation cascade. 30

VZV reactivation causes nerve injury, inflammatory response, and tissue edema, which releases inflammatory mediators from injured tissues that lower the nociceptive threshold and activate peripheral nociceptors. 17 Tissue or nerve injury causes the release of inflammatory mediators or neurotrophic factors, which increase nociceptive receptor terminal membrane excitability. 31 Pro‐inflammatory cytokines have been implicated in neuropathic pain. 32 While anti‐inflammatory cytokines are believed to alleviate it. 33 , 34 Inflammatory factors promote the release of pain‐inducing substances and synergize to amplify their secretion via autocrine and paracrine signaling pathways, resulting in persistent pain. 35 The highly active IL‐10 inhibits pro‐inflammatory cytokines, promotes the production of anti‐inflammatory factors, and blocks the cytokine cascade response, leading to analgesic effects. 36 Chen et al. found extracorporeal shock wave therapy (ESWT) for PHN may have immunomodulatory and analgesic effects. 37 ESWT lowers plasma TNF‐α, IL‐6, and substance P levels, inducing anti‐inflammatory and analgesic effects. 38 , 39 Ma et al. also found that intravenous lidocaine injection downregulated serum TNF‐ α and IL‐ 1 expression. 40 In neuropathic pain models, prolonged elevated IL‐17 levels may lead to abnormal nociception and thermal nociceptive hyperalgesia. 41 , 42 Clinical observations have shown that T cell immunity, a key component of adaptive immunity, maintains the balance between the host and VZV during latency. 43 , 44 A study found that patients with PHN had significantly lower CD4+ T lymphocyte percentages and CD4/CD8 ratios than those without PHN. 45 An evaluation of PHN patients’ immune mechanisms showed increased CD4+ T cell levels and improved T cell translocation after pregabalin treatment. 46 In the future, targeted anti‐inflammatory cytokine therapy may become the cornerstone of neuropathic pain treatment.

CENTRAL PATHOLOGICAL MECHANISMS OF PHN

Abnormalities in the central pain modulation system

Central nervous system afferents are altered after peripheral nerve injury, causing sensory processing mechanism abnormalities in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord. This leads to increased injury and noxious stimulus afferents. Central sensitization occurs when dorsal horn nociceptive neurons become more excitable, resulting in a prolonged and significant response to sensory input. 47 Acute pain evolves into chronic pain through central sensitization, which can cause secondary pain outside the injured tissue. About 70% of patients with PHN experience persistent and severe abnormal pain, as well as neuronal degeneration and loss of function in the affected tissues. 48 This may be due to nociceptive pathway stimulation amplifying the spinal cord dorsal horn excitability. The spinal cord dorsal horn is the first site of nociceptive information delivery and modulation, which may affect central nervous system pain areas and lower pain thresholds depending on pain duration and intensity. 49 , 50 Nerve injury can also increase spinal cord afferents by generating ectopic discharges, the main cause of neuropathic pain. 51 The central nervous system is the primary generator of ectopic discharges that cause persistent nociception. After trauma, the spinal cord's dorsal root ganglion causes afferent nerve block, which may damage the central nervous system and increase neuropathic pain. 52 Chronic pain causes central sensitization of the brain and altered central plasticity. It may also alter brain morphology due to neuroplasticity from frequent injury receptor activation. 53 Structural and functional magnetic resonance imaging is an effective tool for studying the underlying mechanisms of chronic pain. This tool reveals the urgency and necessity of elucidating the underlying neurobiological mechanisms of PHN. MRI‐based neuroimaging studies show that patients with PHN exhibit differential changes in the structure and function of brain regions in the attention, default, and pain network systems. 54 , 55 , 56 Compared to healthy brains, the brains of PHN patients have altered gray matter volume (GMV) in regions such as the insula, middle frontal gyrus, posterior cerebellar lobe, and parahippocampal gyrus. 57 Moreover, PHN brains had significantly reduced diffusion kurtosis imaging (DKI) values of brain gray matter and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) values of white matter, indicating abnormalities in the microstructural integrity. 58 , 59 The “pain matrix” is generally defined as the area activated in response to pain and usually includes the cerebellum, thalamus, cingulate gyrus, and frontal lobes. 60 , 61 , 62 The brain areas that undergo changes in PHN overlap with the pain matrix areas. Each “pain matrix” region interacts with the sympathetic nervous system, triggering, promoting, or enhancing chronic pain. 30 An analgesic response is observed when sympathetic blocks are administered to patients with neuropathic pain because pain is mediated by active sympathetic nerve fibers. 30 Zhang et al. showed that treatment significantly increased the fraction anisotropy (FA) values in the right superior temporal gyrus and axial kurtosis (AK) values in the right middle temporal gyrus of the PHN brain. 63 This suggests that analgesia may relieve chronic pain and reverse functional and structural abnormalities in the PHN brain, indicating central plasticity.

Abnormalities in brain structure and function

Alterations in the central nervous system accompany the transition from HZ to PHN. Neuroimaging studies reveal the close association between pain migrations from HZ to PHN and significant structural and functional brain abnormalities, which profoundly affect multiple aspects of PHN pain processing. 64 , 65 , 66 In addition, the complex neural mechanisms associated with PHN are due to structural and functional alterations in regions outside the pain matrix, such as the insula, brainstem, and precuneus. Using fMRI, Bingel et al. found that painful stimuli induce hemodynamic changes in the hippocampus, suggesting the transmission of pain signals to this region and its involvement in nociceptive perception. 67 A multivoxel pattern analysis and structural MRI study in HZ patients found GMV abnormalities located mainly in the middle frontal lobe, posterior insular cortex, and central cingulate gyrus. 68 Another voxel‐based brain morphometry and structural MRI study indicated abnormal GMV in the parahippocampal gyrus, thalamus, insula, precentral gyrus, and middle frontal gyrus as HZ progressed to PHN. 57 , 65 This suggests that structural changes in these brain regions may precede the onset of pain and lead to chronic pain. The regional homogeneity (ReHo) and fractional amplitude of low‐frequency fluctuation (fALFF) in the cerebellar and middle frontal gyrus increase after HZ progression to PHN, while the ReHo and fALFF of the middle occipital and posterior cingulate gyrus decrease. 65 , 69 These modifications in brain structure and function explain the pathophysiological changes from HZ to PHN.

Central nervous system inflammation

Histopathological studies in patients with PHN show that subacute or chronic inflammatory processes involve lymphocyte infiltration and accumulation around the spinal cord, suggesting that inflammation may contribute to PHN. 16 Patients with intractable PHN have higher IL‐8 concentrations in their cerebrospinal fluid, which is believed to be associated with pain from inflammatory responses. 70 , 71 Watson et al. also found higher IL‐8 concentrations in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with PHN pain, which negatively correlated with pain duration. 16 In addition, there is growing evidence that CNS inflammatory response and glial cell activation play a crucial role in developing and maintaining peripheral neuropathic pain. This is because the subsequent release of cytokines and inflammatory mediators causes brain edema, axonal degeneration, and glial cell proliferation, which in turn promote PHN formation and development. 72 , 73 , 74 , 75 Glial cells, like neurons, express various neurotransmitters and neuromodulator receptors during central sensitization. 76 , 77 These cells, particularly microglia and astrocytes, activated after central injury, produce inflammation that contributes significantly to central sensitization and persistent pain. 23 , 78 , 79 , 80 An increasing number of studies have confirmed that activation of glial cells in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord produces neuropathic pain. 81 , 82 , 83 In addition, activated glial cells can trigger neuroinflammation by stimulating the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL‐1, IL‐6, TNF‐a), exacerbating neuropathic pain. 84 This may be related to the production of pro‐inflammatory factors that cause central sensitization of pain. 85 , 86 Neuropathic pain is usually accompanied by multiple mood disorders, such as anxiety and depression. 87 , 88 Neuroinflammation caused by microglia activation is generally accepted as the leading cause of anxiety, depression, and other mood disorders. 89 , 90 Anxiety and depression are linked to microglia activation in the prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate gyrus, and hippocampus. 91 , 92 , 93 Ma et al. found that intravenous lidocaine inhibited neuroinflammation caused by microglia and astrocyte activation in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate gyrus, and hippocampus. It also downregulated the spinal cord expression of TNF‐α and IL‐1 in the dorsal horn, ultimately reducing neuroinflammation and PHN symptoms. 40 Activation of spinal cord dorsal horn glial cells in neuropathic pain models has been shown to secrete large amounts of cytokines essential for the development of neuropathic pain. 81 , 94 , 95

EFFECT OF PERIPHERAL AND CENTRAL SENSITIZATION ON PHN

Munnangi et al. suggested that PHN, a typical neuropathic pain, is caused by a combination of pathophysiological changes in peripheral and central sensitization. 96 Neuropathic pain is a manifestation of plasticity in neuronal function and morphology, which includes peripheral and central sensitization. 97 , 98 Peripheral sensitization refers to the increased sensitivity and excitability of primary sensory neurons in the peripheral nervous system. In contrast, central sensitization is increased sensitivity and excitability of injurious sensory neurons in the central nervous system. Both peripheral and central sensitization contribute to the development and maintenance of neuropathic pain. 99 , 100 Stimulation of peripheral nociceptors, such as unmyelinated C fibers, myelinated Aβ fibers, and primary sensory ganglia by metabolic toxins, cytokines, or inflammatory mediators can alter neural plasticity and increase excitability under certain pathological conditions. 101 , 102 This may manifest as amplified signal processing within neurons and alterations in nerve surface receptors and ion channels, which ultimately strengthen the primary afferent pain signal. 103 , 104 These changes can be collectively referred to as peripheral sensitization. Central sensitization is the collection of changes in the central nervous system, including the increased responsiveness of nociceptive neurons to their standard or subthreshold afferent signals. 105 VZV infection causes peripheral nerve injury by stimulating low‐threshold mechanoreceptors in the deep dorsal horn of the spinal cord and C fibers to form new synapses with Aβ fibers. This sets up non‐injurious afferents that trigger and enhance the activity of pain‐sensitive neurons, resulting in a much lower response threshold. 106 Non‐neural cells, mainly immune and glial cells in the peripheral and central nervous system, also contribute to chronic pain. After peripheral nerve injury, locally produced inflammatory factors activate microglia. The microglia then produce various inflammatory factors and chemokines, which further activate several protein kinases and signaling cascade reactions. 107 , 108 , 109 The microglia repeatedly act on the neurons to promote glial cell‐mediated neuroinflammatory responses and peripheral sensitization. 110 , 111 , 112 The specific marker for astrocytes is glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and upregulation of GFAP activates astrocytes, while the specific marker for microglia is leukocyte differentiation antigen 11b (OX‐42). Lei et al. found that peripheral nerve injury increased OX‐42 and GFAP expression, indicating that microglia and astrocyte activation induce and maintain PHN pain. 113 A large body of evidence suggests that neuroinflammation, characterized by glial cell activation mediating the release of inflammatory mediators, plays a decisive role in the central sensitization of neuropathic pain. 114 , 115 Glial cell activation releases pro‐inflammatory factors and chemokines that prolong pain and induce and maintain PHN. 116 In summary, PHN is a complex process resulting from VZV infection, involving multiple peripheral and central factors.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, inflammation‐induced peripheral and central sensitization, structural and functional abnormalities of the brain, and afferent nerve block play key roles in the complex pathophysiology of PHN. However, more specific and reliable studies are needed to thoroughly analyze the details of its complex pathogenesis. In‐depth studies are necessary, given the high prevalence of PHN and its extensive harmful effects on individuals and society. A more comprehensive understanding of the possible pathogenesis of PHN is required for effective management and treatment.
