Analyzing barriers to construction waste minimization and circular economy culture in Building projects using fuzzy DEMATEL
Authors: Muhammad Usman Shahid, Majid Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The rapid expansion of the construction industry has led to significant waste generation and environmental issues. While the circular economy (CE) offers a solution to reduce this waste, its implementation faces numerous barriers. While many global studies have explored barriers to waste minimization (WM), few have compared how different stakeholders perceive and prioritize these barriers. Moreover, country-specific research is needed, particularly in developing nations, due to differences in construction practices. Furthermore, there is currently a significant research gap in terms of the barriers and policy guidelines for WM in Pakistan’s construction industry. Therefore, this study aims to identify these barriers, compare stakeholders’ perceptions, and propose a conceptual framework to address them. A review identified 40 WM barriers, with the top 13 shortlisted barriers identified through frequency analysis. Root cause barriers were identified through fuzzy DEMATEL technique, while thematic and word frequency analyses of interviews led to the development of the theoretical framework. The fuzzy DEMATEL method identified root-cause barriers. Comparative analysis revealed the highest agreement (69%) between contractors and regulators on key barriers such as lack of regulations (B1) and financial constraints (B2), whereas clients and consultants showed moderate alignment (62%). Overall, all stakeholders agreed that B1, B2, poor awareness (B3), and unclear specifications (B12) are major barriers to WM adoption. Identifying these root causes lays a strong foundation for developing targeted mitigation strategies. A theoretical policy framework is proposed, emphasizing financial support and awareness programs at the macro level and contract document modification and material recycling at the micro level. The study concludes with implications for social, economic, and environmental sustainability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23188-4.

Introduction

In the past decade, rapid urbanization and major development projects have greatly increased construction activities globally1. The construction industry typically contributes approximately 10% of a country’s economic growth. However, this sector remains heavily dependent on natural resources, which are finite and risk eventual depletion2. Consequently, it is essential to use these resources efficiently to safeguard future generations and sustain economic progress. Despite this need, the construction sector often fails to implement sustainable practices, leading to substantial resource waste. Approximately 200 million tons of waste are estimated to be produced annually in the UK, with 59% originating from the construction sector3 and almost half being generated from building projects. Similarly, approximately 2 billion tons of construction waste are generated annually in China4. Furthermore, construction waste fills landfill sites by 29%, 40%, 44%, 27%, and 29% in the USA, Brazil, Australia, Canada, and Hong Kong, respectively5. It means, globally, construction and demolition waste (C&DW) occupy 29–44% of landfill space in countries such as the USA, Brazil, Australia, and Canada. In addition to environmental concerns, such high levels of waste also have economic consequences, increasing project costs by up to 3.32% of the total budget6, a loss that can amount to millions of large-scale projects7. A recent special issue of the circular economy (CE) emphasized that the overconsumption of natural resources and high levels of waste, especially from construction, threaten global sustainability8. This issue stems largely from the linear economy model dominant in the industry, in which materials are produced, used, and discarded. To address this, the construction sector must shift toward CE principles that emphasize reducing, reusing, and recycling resources to maximize their utility and minimize waste. Globally, CE is now central to sustainability agendas. The European Union’s Circular Economy Action Plan9, China’s Circular Economy Promotion Law10, and India’s Construction and Demolition Waste Management Rules11 represent policy-driven efforts to transition towards circularity in construction. These frameworks advocate for eco-efficient material use, design for deconstruction, and regulatory compliance for WM.

CE signifies a fundamental transformation in the production, distribution, use, and recovery of materials, aiming to preserve natural resources by maximizing material efficiency12,13. Thus, the CE entails a comprehensive systemic shift that requires coordinated efforts at both the macro and micro levels across industries to foster a CE-oriented culture through efficient resource utilization and waste minimization (WM) practices. However, implementing CE and WM practices in building projects presents several challenges. In this context, numerous studies have identified barriers to adoption as well as strategies to promote CE and WM cultures in building projects in the construction sector, particularly in developed countries14–19. Existing CE and WM studies tend to focus on high-level policy analysis or isolated technical barriers, often ignoring the critical influence of stakeholder perspectives. There are very limited studies that provides a comparative analysis of how different stakeholders perceive and rate WM barriers. Around 50% significance scale was observed among owners, investors, engineers and architects for pointing out the barriers in adoption of CE practices20. Similarly, in another study barriers to waste management were explored through a comparative analysis among the perception of managers and researchers from academia21. Grasping the differences in how stakeholders assess these barriers is vital, as it indicates the extent of agreement among major stakeholders in recognizing the underlying issues. Overall, there is a scarcity of research that provides a comparative analysis of how various stakeholders such as clients, contractors, consultants, and regulators perceive and prioritize CE-related barriers. Understanding these differences is vital for policy alignment, implementation feasibility, and industry-wide buy-in.

In developing countries, there is a significant lack of research offering insights into the barriers to effective WM. As a result, these nations produce millions of tons of solid waste annually, with construction sites contributing roughly one-third of the total solid waste22. Consequently, the ongoing generation of waste continues to drive material depletion, posing a significant environmental threat in these countries19,23–25. Few studies have been reported in terms of the identification of barriers and policy development in these countries. The barriers and policy measures for WM identified across various studies differ significantly. This variation is largely attributed to differences in construction practices, cultural contexts, and the unique characteristics of construction industries in each country26. Despite global momentum, CE adoption in the construction sectors of developing countries remains extremely limited, hindered by institutional voids, fragmented supply chains, lack of regulatory enforcement, and low stakeholder awareness. While countries like China and India have made progress by designing frameworks tailored to their national contexts, these models cannot be directly replicated in countries such as Pakistan, where the socio-economic conditions, construction practices, labor structures, and cultural values differ markedly. So, there is a strong need for more comprehensive studies to identify and analyze WM barriers specific to each developing country, considering their distinct cultural values and practices.

Pakistan faces a situation comparable to that of other developing nations, generating approximately 30 million tons of solid waste each year, with 9 million tons coming from the construction industry27 and 4 million tons from building projects. Construction activities are projected to expand significantly, driven by the government’s focus on this sector as a key indicator of national progress. This anticipated growth will be a challenge faced by many developing nations, and this expansion threatens both environmental and resource sustainability28. A report by the Asian Development Bank highlights the urgent need for waste management policy guidelines in Pakistan’s construction industry, as the current rate of waste generation poses a significant environmental threat27. Consequently, the initial step should involve identifying the challenges faced by the local construction industry. These context-specific barriers can then serve as a foundation for formulating effective policy guidelines tailored to the needs of developing countries.

This study makes a distinct and valuable contribution to the literature by employing an integrated methodological approach that combines both quantitative and qualitative methods, thereby offering a comprehensive understanding of the challenges associated with CE implementation within the context of a developing country. Specifically, it advances the discourse in several significant ways. First, it provides country-specific insights that are carefully tailored to Pakistan’s unique regulatory, economic, and cultural conditions, thereby filling a critical gap in the regionally contextualized CE research. Second, it undertakes a comparative stakeholder analysis, an approach that remains underutilized in the context of CE in the construction sector, particularly in the South Asia. The study also employs fuzzy DEMATEL, a sophisticated decision-support tool, to systematically identify, prioritize, and structure barriers based on their underlying cause-effect relationships. This analytical rigor enhances the reliability and practical relevance of the findings. Finally, the research proposes a theoretically grounded policy framework, developed through stakeholder engagement, aimed at promoting sustainable construction practices. This framework not only supports industry-level transformation but also contributes to shaping the national policy discourse on CE in Pakistan. Literature review

Barriers to WM and CE on Building projects

This section explains major barriers in adaptation of WM and CE culture in construction industry. In this regard, number of studies had been conducted worldwide to identify the major barriers in implementation of WM and CE strategies. Detailed list of forty (40) barriers which were taken out from past literature is attached as Annexure-1. Further, these barriers have been classified into multiple categories in past literature including regulatory, financial, technical, organizational, cultural and behavioral barriers as shown in Table 1.

Institutional and regulatory barriers

Institutional weaknesses and inadequate regulatory frameworks are consistently highlighted as major challenges to effective WM and CE adoption. The key barriers identified include the lack of enforcement of environmental regulations, low public awareness regarding waste management, cultural resistance, inadequate penalties for illegal dumping, limited recycling infrastructure, contractual limitations, inefficient material handling, and design-related errors29–35. Hasan et al36. identified barriers to WM in Bangladesh as negligence of workers, poor supervision, unskilled labor, and lack of onsite material storage. Furthermore, the Sri Lankan context illustrates how insufficient institutional support and weak regulatory oversight create barriers that discourage industry adoption of WM practices37. In Nigeria, governance issues combined with limited penalties for non-compliance have led to systemic inefficiencies in addressing construction-related waste38. These findings underscore the importance of strong institutional backing as a precondition for successful CE implementation. Recent studies also situate institutional barriers within broader de-carbonization pathways. For instance, a study demonstrates how policy enforcement and regulatory frameworks can simultaneously advance CE adoption and national carbon reduction goals39. Such interdisciplinary perspectives highlight that overcoming regulatory weaknesses in developing countries is not only a matter of compliance but also of aligning with global sustainability agendas.

Economic and financial barriers

High investment and operational costs remain among the most frequently cited barriers. Several studies demonstrate that contractors and developers perceive WM and CE initiatives as financially burdensome due to high initial costs, limited incentives, and a lack of dedicated funding frameworks37,40. In Somaliland, limited financial resources and absence of economic incentives were considered central barriers to WM adoption40. From an interdisciplinary lens, financial challenges are also intertwined with behavioral and market mechanisms. For example, recent work on perceived benefits and tourist willingness to pay more in national forest parks suggests that economic instruments, when paired with awareness and social norms, can influence willingness to invest in sustainability initiatives41. Translating this insight to the construction sector implies that incentive schemes and cultural value shifts may help reduce the perception of CE as a cost burden and position it as an economic opportunity.

Technical and infrastructural barriers

A persistent issue across many developing countries is the lack of sufficient infrastructure to support recycling and reuse activities. Bangladesh, for instance, faces challenges related to inadequate on-site material storage, inefficient handling practices, and lack of recycling facilities36. Similarly, Somaliland suffers from underdeveloped infrastructure that limits opportunities for effective waste processing40. Recent interdisciplinary research expands this discussion by offering micro-level reuse strategies. The study by Iqbal et al39. illustrates how technical innovation can directly convert waste into usable products. Similarly, advances in landfill cover design with enhanced anti-seepage and oxygen barrier performance show how post-construction waste can be managed under climate stress. These contributions enrich the technical discourse by linking CE practices with material reuse and resilience under environmental pressures.

Organizational and managerial barriers

At the organizational level, managerial inefficiencies and resistance to change present significant challenges. Hasan et al36. highlighted barriers such as poor supervision, negligence of workers, and unskilled labor in the Bangladeshi construction sector. In Nigeria, organizational resistance, weak collaboration, and complex documentation processes have further obstructed the implementation of WM initiatives. Emerging studies point to the role of advanced monitoring and process tools in addressing such barriers. For instance, Cheng, et al.42 exemplifies how digital technologies can improve operational efficiency and data-driven decision-making in construction projects. Integrating such modern tools into WM strategies could reduce inefficiencies, encourage managerial innovation, and foster CE adoption.

Cultural and behavioral barriers

Cultural resistance and behavioral patterns also play a decisive role in shaping WM outcomes. Low awareness among stakeholders, negligence on construction sites, and cultural attitudes resistant to change have been identified as critical barriers in multiple developing contexts38. Recent research emphasizes the interconnectedness of cultural, environmental, and social dimensions. The study on integrated sustainability perspectives to interconnect CE, environmental development, and social status shows that cultural and behavioral drivers cannot be isolated from broader sustainability transitions43. Similarly, evidence on public willingness to pay higher fees for sustainability in recreational contexts suggests that awareness campaigns and incentive alignment may be powerful tools for changing cultural resistance in construction.

Environmental barriers

Beyond economic, technical, and cultural dimensions, emerging literature also highlights the environmental and social urgency of addressing C&DW. For example, assessment of the impacts of the life cycle of construction waste on human health underscores that unmanaged waste poses direct risks to community well-being44. Likewise, the numerical study on the influence of surface vegetation on the aerodynamics of high-rise buildings illustrates how sustainable design interventions can preempt waste and environmental damage during early project stages45. These perspectives reinforce the need to situate CE not only within industry practices but also within broader human and ecological health outcomes.

Stakeholders perception on barriers to WM and CE

This section presents a comparative analysis of stakeholder perceptions regarding WM in building projects.

Importance of stakeholder perspectives

Since construction projects involve diverse actors with differing priorities, understanding stakeholder perceptions of WM and CE barriers is crucial. These perceptions influence the extent to which actors support or resist waste reduction strategies. Clients, contractors, consultants, regulators, and environmental agencies often hold conflicting views on which barriers are most pressing, making it necessary to analyze consensus and divergence systematically.

Comparison among stakeholders’ perceptions

Past studies have engaged stakeholders to varying degrees but with important limitations. For instance, a Turkish study incorporated multiple perspectives but was dominated by environmental and chemical engineers, limiting representation from the broader construction value chain46. A cross-country study examined root-cause barriers across clients, consultants, contractors, and academics, but it did not explicitly compare differences in perceptions among these groups47. More advanced methods, such as fuzzy DEMATEL applied in India23, revealed significant divergences between government officials and technology experts in how they rated root-cause barriers. These findings highlight the methodological value of comparative stakeholder analysis but also expose its limited application.

While existing studies provide valuable insights into context-specific barriers, most remain descriptive and fragmented. Few efforts have been made to benchmark barriers against established taxonomies such as institutional, economic, technical, organizational, and cultural categories limiting comparability across studies. Furthermore, interdependencies among barriers are rarely examined. For instance, weak regulatory enforcement often intensifies organizational resistance, while high costs are compounded by technical infrastructure deficiencies. Incorporating recent and interdisciplinary literature demonstrates that WM and CE are deeply interlinked with climate change mitigation, material innovation, social behavior, and human health. However, these dimensions remain underexplored in mainstream construction research. Despite these contributions, the literature has not systematically mapped areas of convergence and conflict among stakeholder groups. This gap is particularly pronounced in developing countries, where institutional and cultural contexts strongly shape stakeholder perspectives.

Research method

The study was carried out as illustrated in Fig. 1. An initial pool of forty (40) barriers was identified through a structured review of approximately sixty peer-reviewed journal articles published in reputable journals, each addressing challenges related to WM in building projects. To ensure transparency and rigor, clear inclusion and exclusion criteria were established during the screening process. Studies were included if they were peer-reviewed, published in English between 2011 and 2025, and specifically focused on WM barriers in building projects with sufficient methodological detail, while non-peer-reviewed works, duplicates, studies outside the scope of building projects, or those emphasizing only macro-level policy issues were excluded. The selected papers were reviewed independently by two researchers, and all barriers reported were extracted and coded. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s Kappa48,49, which indicated substantial agreement, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

Given the large number of potential barriers, it would have been impractical and overly burdensome to evaluate all forty barriers in later stages, as doing so could lead to respondent fatigue and compromise data quality. To address this, a frequency analysis was conducted as a preliminary screening tool, consistent with prior studies, to identify the most commonly cited barriers. Importantly, this approach has been adopted in several previous studies (e.g.,50–52, lending additional credibility to its use in the present research. A threshold of 10% was applied, meaning that only barriers mentioned in at least 10% of the reviewed sources were retained. This approach balanced comprehensiveness with practicality, ensuring that only empirically significant and widely recognized barriers were included. Frequency analysis involved systematically scanning the selected literature, tallying each barrier based on its occurrence, and assuming that more frequently cited barriers are more likely to be practically relevant and impactful. To further enhance validity, the refined shortlist of barriers was subjected to expert validation through a pilot survey. A panel of four experts, two academics and two industry practitioners with expertise in construction WM and the CE, reviewed the shortlisted barriers. Their feedback was crucial in confirming the relevance of frequently cited barriers, merging overlapping items, and recommending the retention of certain contextually significant but less frequently cited barriers. Minor modifications were also made to the wording of some barrier items for clarity and contextual accuracy. This comprehensive two-stage process of frequency analysis and expert validation minimized bias, prevented oversimplification, and ensured contextual robustness. As a result, the initial pool of 40 barriers was refined into a final set of thirteen (13) barriers, each exceeding the 10% frequency threshold and validated by expert judgment. These barriers, labeled B1 to B13, are presented in Table 2 (e.g., B1 = “Lack of rules & regulations,” B2 = “Financial issues,” etc.). The final questionnaire was then developed on the basis of these selected barriers and distributed to a diverse group of experts using purposive and snowball sampling (non-probability methods). Purposive sampling ensured the participation of individuals with relevant expertise in WM, while snowball sampling expanded the pool through referrals. Although effective for capturing in-depth expert insights, this sampling strategy limits the generalizability of the findings beyond Pakistan’s construction industry.

After setting the criteria for selection of experts, questionnaire was sent to approximately 45 experts, of whom 34 responded, yielding a response rate of 76%. Out of these, 30 responses were deemed valid, while 4 were excluded based on specific criteria. Responses were considered invalid if the questionnaire was incomplete, if the respondent answered nearly all questions with the same rating (such as consistently using a 4 or 5), indicating a lack of seriousness, or if significant inconsistencies were identified during cross-questioning. The valid responses of the survey were then completed by thirty (30) stakeholders, comprising clients (23%), consultants (30%), contractors (27%), and representatives from regulatory agencies (20%). These experts were carefully selected to ensure comprehensive input from all major stakeholders directly involved with WM issues in the country in the light of previous studies23,46,53. Each participant possessed at least a bachelor’s degree and had a minimum of ten years of professional experience23,46,53,54, as individuals with fewer than ten years of experience were deemed unlikely to provide well-informed insights55. The decision to involve 30 experts was guided by methodologies adopted in previous studies23,46,53,54,56–58. The reason for such small size of sample is to note that the fuzzy DEMATEL technique is fundamentally expert driven and qualitative in nature, focusing on deriving structural insights rather than making statistical generalizations59. Unlike inferential statistical techniques, DEMATEL does not require large sample sizes or power analysis, as its primary aim is to extract causal relationships and understand system dynamics under uncertainty through informed expert judgment60. In this study, a purposive and snowball sampling strategy was adopted to ensure the inclusion of a well-informed panel of 30 experts representing all four major stakeholder groups, clients, consultants, contractors, and regulators. All selected participants had more than 10 years of relevant experience in the construction sector, ensuring that the input collected was both credible and contextually rich. Moreover, the fuzzy DEMATEL method has been widely recognized in previous literature for its robustness even with small expert panels, provided that participants possess domain expertise. Its strength lies in its ability to identify cause-effect relationships and prioritize influencing factors using qualitative knowledge, rather than relying on statistical representativeness. So, the emphasis was placed on the quality and relevance of expert input over quantity, which aligns with the methodological intent of fuzzy DEMATEL.

Further, ethical approval for this study was granted by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the Capital University of Science and Technology (CUST), Islamabad with IRB No. CUST/ORIC/IERB dated 9th July, 2024. All procedures adhered to the institution’s ethical standards, and informed consent was obtained from every participant. Participants were guaranteed anonymity, and strict confidentiality of their data was upheld throughout the research by taking consent from each of the participant as attached Annexure-2. To ensure anonymous reporting, each participant was assigned a sector-specific alphanumeric code. After confirming the reliability and validity of the collected data, the fuzzy DEMATEL technique was applied to identify the causal and effect relationships among the barriers to WM. Additionally, a comparative analysis was conducted across all four stakeholders groups based on the fuzzy DEMATEL results. Finally, a conceptual framework for WM aimed at addressing the identified barriers was proposed, developed through thematic analysis of semi-structured interviews with field experts.

Analysis of collected data for WM & CE barriers on building projects

The internal consistency and reliability of the data collected through the questionnaire were assessed via Cronbach’s alpha and validity tests, respectively. Furthermore, the fuzzy DEMATEL technique was employed to identify the root cause barriers. Details of each of these techniques are discussed in the following sections.

Cronbach’s alpha test for internal consistency

Cronbach’s alpha was used to evaluate the internal consistency and reliability of the data. Reliability is concerned with the stability of responses and indicates whether similar results would be obtained if the measurement procedure were repeated with another sample of respondents. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha value for all 13 identified barriers was 0.80. Since the minimum acceptable threshold is generally 0.7026, the collected data demonstrate an acceptable level of internal consistency and can be considered reliable.

Validity test

The validity test is primarily associated with the data collection tool (questionnaire), which was designed to ensure that the items accurately measure the intended variables61. To assess construct adequacy, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy exceeded the recommended value of 0.6, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (p < 0.05), confirming the suitability of the data for factor analysis. Furthermore, factor loadings for all items were above the recommended threshold of 0.70, supporting item-level validity. Taken together, these results indicate that the questionnaire possesses satisfactory validity and reliability for use in this study.

It is important to distinguish between reliability and validity. Reliability refers to the consistency of responses whether similar outcomes would be achieved if the measurement procedure were repeated. Validity, on the other hand, reflects the accuracy of the measurement tool whether the questionnaire truly captures the intended constructs. In this study, both reliability (via Cronbach’s alpha) and validity (via PCA, KMO, and Bartlett’s test) were examined to ensure the robustness of the measurement instrument.

Application of fuzzy DEMATEL method for identification of root-cause barriers

Several multi-criteria decision-making (MCDM) techniques have been employed in past studies to identify barriers to WM in building projects, primarily due to the inherent subjectivity associated with qualitative data. These techniques include the Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP), Analytical Network Process (ANP), Structural Equation Modeling (SEM), and fuzzy DEMATEL (Decision-Making Trial and Evaluation Laboratory). The AHP is a widely used method that structures decision problems into a hierarchical format and evaluates criteria through pairwise comparisons62. However, AHP is limited by its unidirectional hierarchical relationships and the requirement to construct multiple independent matrices, which must subsequently be integrated thereby constraining its analytical depth63. To overcome these limitations, ANP was developed as an advanced extension of AHP. ANP enables the modeling of interdependencies among decision elements, making it more suitable for complex decision-making scenarios64. Nevertheless, its methodological complexity often impedes its broader adoption in practice. SEM, on the other hand, is a robust multivariate analysis technique that integrates aspects of factor analysis, regression, and path modeling. It facilitates the evaluation of interrelationships among latent variables, accounts for measurement error, and validates theoretical constructs. Despite its strengths, SEM typically necessitates large sample sizes to produce reliable and generalizable results65. Among these methods, fuzzy DEMATEL has emerged as particularly effective for analyzing WM barriers. By integrating fuzzy logic with DEMATEL, it facilitates the identification of causal relationships among variables, especially when expert judgments are expressed in ambiguous or linguistic terms23,66. This method is especially advantageous in qualitative studies, as it systematically reduces vagueness and enhances the clarity of expert assessments. The rationale for employing fuzzy DEMATEL lies in three core strengths: (1) it addresses the challenge of analyzing subjective interactions among WM barriers, (2) it provides a quantitative representation of these interactions, and (3) it elucidates cause-and-effect linkages, thereby informing the formulation of regulatory and strategic interventions by decision-makers. In conclusion, while each of these techniques offers unique advantages in identifying WM barriers, their suitability depends on the specific problem context and data conditions. AHP is user-friendly but limited in modeling interdependencies; ANP addresses this shortcoming but at the cost of increased complexity; SEM is methodologically rigorous yet data-intensive. In contrast, fuzzy DEMATEL offers a well-balanced and practical approach for capturing subjective judgments, modeling causal relationships, and supporting evidence-based decision-making in construction WM initiatives aligned with CE principles. Comparative analysis among stakeholders in ratings of WM barriers

After completing the fuzzy DEMATEL analysis, a comprehensive comparative study was conducted to identify the similarities and differences in how various stakeholders groups perceive barriers to WM and CE in building projects. For this purpose, separate datasets were analyzed for each stakeholder category, namely, clients, consultants, contractors, and regulatory authorities. Specifically, the questionnaires completed solely by clients were processed via the fuzzy DEMATEL method to determine how they rated the cause‒effect relationships among WM barriers. This same process was independently repeated for the other stakeholders groups. Following the individual analyses, a comparative evaluation was conducted to assess the degree of consensus (percent similarity) or divergence (percent difference) among stakeholder groups. These comparisons were performed in pairs. Percent similarity was calculated by dividing the number of common barriers (both cause and effect) shared between two stakeholder groups by the total number of barriers considered in that specific pairwise comparison. For example, the client–consultant group shares B1, B2, B3, B9, and B11 as common cause barriers, and B8, B10, and B13 as common effect barriers. Thus, 8 out of 13 barriers were found to be common, indicating a 62% similarity between clients and consultants. The findings from this analysis offer valuable insights into the varying perceptions of key players within Pakistan’s construction industry, highlighting both shared concerns and differing viewpoints regarding WM challenges. Conceptual framework development process

In the last step of this study, mitigation measure to overcome these influential barriers were formulated through a conceptual framework. In this regard, the industry experts were again contacted and briefed about the identified barriers and asked about their opinions to mitigate these barriers. Experts’ opinions were gathered through semi-structured interviews. This method was chosen because it enabled experts to share their perspectives on relevant topics while also offering them the flexibility to provide additional insights beyond the scope of the interview questionnaire68. The collected data underwent word frequency and thematic analyses. Word frequency analysis was conducted using NVivo software, with the word length set to 10 and a maximum of 20 words. Running the query and incorporating additional terms into the stop list produced a graphical representation of the most frequently used words. This approach has also been employed in previous research to summarize interview data69. This analysis provides insights into the words most frequently mentioned in the participants’ responses during the interviews. The greater the frequency of a word, the more significant or prominent it is likely to be. Words with the highest frequency occupy larger areas in the diagram generated at the conclusion of the word frequency analysis. Additionally, thematic analysis was conducted to identify the main themes and sub-themes within the collected data70. The thematic analysis process consists of several stages: (1) becoming familiar with the collected data, (2) generating initial codes based on the interviewees’ responses, (3) thoroughly searching for potential themes, (4) reviewing the extracted themes to ensure all relevant ones are included, and (5) refining the themes by adding or removing them based on their relevance to the topic. In the end, a WM framework was proposed to foster sustainability and promote a CE culture.

Results for the CE and WM barriers to Building projects

The detailed results of the analysis are presented in the following sections.

Root cause barriers to WM and CE (Fuzzy DEMATEL Results)

Based on the fuzzy DEMATEL analysis, a multi-level conceptual framework was developed to clarify the causal relationships and relative prominence of barriers hindering WM and CE adoption in building projects. While some of the identified barriers such as non-availability of rules and regulations (B1), financial issues (B2), and poor stakeholder awareness (B3) are consistent with challenges commonly reported in developing countries, the added value of fuzzy DEMATEL lies in its ability to distinguish between causal and effect-type barriers and to model interdependencies across macro- and micro-levels as shown in Fig. 2. For instance, unclear specifications (B12), a project-specific factor, emerged as the most influential causal barrier with the highest D–R = 0.33 value, greater than any other barriers, highlighting how technical ambiguities at the project level can trigger downstream inefficiencies even when macro-level issues are addressed. The framework distinguishes macro-level structural barriers (e.g., institutional gaps, inadequate enforcement, economic disincentives) from micro-level operational inefficiencies (e.g., design flaws, poor collaboration). Similar results have been reported in another study71. Importantly, the analysis revealed that macro-level weaknesses particularly regulatory voids and financial constraints tend to influence negatively, intensifying project-level challenges. This systems-oriented perspective moves beyond merely listing known issues to provide a diagnostic hierarchy of control points for intervention. These theoretical underpinnings not only help interpret the results in a deeper manner but also offer a basis for future empirical research aimed at testing or expanding this framework in other contexts or regions.

In terms of CE, this insight is critical: CE adoption is not only constrained by technical feasibility at the project level, but also by the lack of enabling institutions and financial models that support circular practices such as reuse, recycling, or design-for-deconstruction. The results also align with institutional theory, where the absence of coercive (rules, enforcement) and normative (awareness, industry norms) pressures limits the adoption of CE practices. While it may be expected that regulatory and financial barriers are dominant, their quantified causal prominence (D–R as well as D + R scores) in this analysis justifies focusing reform efforts on policy-level interventions such as financial incentives for circular construction methods, mandatory CE-oriented design guidelines, or stricter enforcement of WM bylaws. Thus, this study’s contribution lies not in identifying familiar challenges, but in clarifying their hierarchical influence and systemic interactions, offering practical and theoretical implications. By visually mapping these interdependencies, the framework highlights where policy action can unlock important benefits throughout the construction process.

Comparison of stakeholders’ perception for WM and CE barriers

The findings of fuzzy DEMATEL provide an overview of the similarities and differences in the opinions of different industry stakeholders while rating these barriers. It reflects the level of agreement each stakeholder has with each other, which is also important72. Furthermore, it highlights the cause of the barriers that ultimately affect the other barriers.

Client’s perspective

As a result of applying fuzzy DEMATEL to the data collected from clients, the cause–and–effect diagram is presented in Fig. 3(a). In this figure, “Unclear specifications (B12),” “Low fines for illegal dumping (B7),” and “Non-availability of rules and regulations (B1)” emerge as the most influential barriers hindering the adoption of effective WM practices. Among these, unclear specifications are a critical contributor to construction waste, as poorly defined contract documents often lead to rework and material inefficiencies. Similarly, low penalties for illegal dumping reduce accountability for contractors68. In many cases, contractors face minimal legal consequences for violating WM practices, reflecting a systemic regulatory gap. While these findings align with known and expected issues in many developing countries, such as financial constraints, lack of regulatory enforcement, and poor stakeholder awareness, they raise an important question about the value added by fuzzy DEMATEL20,73. These barriers have been widely recognized in prior literature and could arguably be identified without the need for complex modeling. However, fuzzy DEMATEL offers an important advantage in establishing the causal structure and interdependencies among barriers something traditional identification methods often overlook. For example, the model reveals how upstream policy-level issues (e.g., lack of regulations) directly influence project-level practices (e.g., unclear specifications and poor design integration), thereby offering a systems-level understanding critical to policy and intervention design.

Furthermore, linking these findings to the CE concept provides additional insight. CE emphasizes minimizing waste through lifecycle thinking, closed-loop resource use, and systemic transformation of production-consumption patterns. The prominence of unclear specifications and poor design (B12 and B3) highlights missed opportunities for integrating CE principles at the design stage, where waste can be prevented rather than managed. Likewise, the weak regulatory framework undermines enforcement mechanisms essential for implementing CE-aligned practices such as resource recovery, material reuse, and life cycle-based procurement strategies. This suggests that strengthening institutional frameworks and embedding CE principles in construction project planning and policy can play a pivotal role in overcoming systemic WM challenges. Notably, financial issues (B11) and lack of awareness (B8) also rank highly, with D + R values of 9.124 and 8.819 as shown in Table 4 respectively, indicating their broad influence across the network of barriers. While these are generic challenges, fuzzy DEMATEL quantifies their relative prominence and centrality, helping prioritize intervention areas. In sum, although the identified barriers may appear intuitive, the fuzzy DEMATEL analysis adds analytical value by mapping causal relationships and highlighting leverage points for CE-based policy and practice reforms.

Consultant’s perspective

From the consultant’s perspective, the key cause barriers to effective waste WM are illogical design (B9), financial issues (B2), and lack of interdepartmental collaboration (B6), as shown in Fig. 3(b). Additional concerns include poor stakeholder behavior (B11), lack of awareness (B3), and weak regulatory frameworks (B1) as shown in Fig. 3(b). While these are well-known challenges in developing countries74, the fuzzy DEMATEL method adds value by mapping how these barriers interconnect and influence one another, providing a systemic understanding rather than a list of generic issues. Furthermore, poor behavior and a lack of awareness among industry stakeholders are serious concerns because both of these issues are linked to cultural issues within the industry75. So, the industry needs to put some effort into developing a WM culture through regular training and seminar sessions. Thus, the perceptions of the client and consultant perspectives are similar because the non-availability of rules and regulations, illogical design, financial issues, and poor awareness of waste control among stakeholders are the main barriers to adopting WM culture. This shows that most of the time, both clients and consultants have the same opinion because both have common interests in a project76. Financial constraints (B2) and low awareness (B3), with D + R values of 5.512 and 5.378 respectively from Table 4, emerge as prominent barriers. These findings reinforce the need for investment in infrastructure and capacity building to support CE implementation. The lack of collaboration (B6) highlights the importance of cross-sector coordination in integrating CE principles such as resource efficiency and design for reuse. Effect barriers like lack of legal enforcement (B4) and low fines for illegal dumping (B7) reflect downstream consequences of weak governance. The alignment between client and consultant perspectives particularly on design flaws, financial limitations, and regulatory gaps suggests a shared understanding of the systemic changes needed to promote WM and CE adoption in construction projects.

Contractor’s perspective

Considering the opinions of contractors, the primary cause barriers to WM are “Unclear specifications (B12),” “Lack of modern tools usage (B13),” and “Financial issues (B2),” as shown in the cause‒effect diagram in Fig. 3(c). Additional factors include low penalties for illegal dumping (B7), non-availability of rules (B1), and lack of innovation in product design (B10). Many of these causes are attributed by contractors to clients and consultants, suggesting a strong perception that upstream decisions such as poor design and lack of clarity in project specifications are central to waste generation. This attribution pattern reflects a broader tendency among stakeholders to deflect responsibility, which undermines integrated efforts toward CE implementation. While issues like financial constraints, lack of regulation, and poor awareness are well-known and expected in developing countries and do not require fuzzy DEMATEL to identify the value of the method lies in structuring these barriers, revealing their interdependencies, and prioritizing them based on causal prominence. For instance, B1 (lack of rules) and B2 (financial issues) emerge with the highest D + R values, indicating their systemic influence across the network. Meanwhile, effect-type barriers like poor construction practices (B8) and illogical design (B9) reflect downstream consequences that are less controllable once projects are underway. These findings align with previous research71, further validating the prominence of design-related issues in waste generation. Contractors believe that waste generation at construction sites is triggered mainly by the negligence of other stakeholders69. This is a serious concern because contractors perceive that a substantial amount of waste is generated due to the carelessness of clients and consultants rather than taking responsibility for themselves. Thus, addressing high-priority barriers through targeted policies and cross-stakeholder collaboration can not only reduce waste but also facilitate CE adoption by shifting from reactive waste management to proactive resource efficiency.

Regulator’s perspective

Regulators and environmental agencies are also major stakeholders in the development of waste control culture in a country. As a result, the perception of regulators is important, as shown in Fig. 3(d). According to regulators, significant barriers to WM are “Financial issues (B2)”, “Non-availability of rules (B1)”, “Unclear specifications (B12)”, “Lack of use of modern tools (B13)”, “Shortage of resources (B5)” and “Poor awareness (B3)”, with B1 and B2 identified as the most prominent barriers. Thus, the perceptions of contractors and regulators in terms of causing barriers to WM are very similar74. However, financial issues and the unavailability of rules and regulations are rated as important barriers by all industry stakeholders. Both of these barriers are considered serious issues in developing countries because they require considerable financial capital to support different initiatives, such as providing subsidies for recycled materials, constructing new recycling units, and constructing landfill sites. Furthermore, the confidence of customers in recycled materials must be increased77. On the other hand, the dividing line of D-R is the effect barrier. So, regulators have rated low fines on the illegal dumping of waste as an effective barrier. Regulators acknowledge the non- availability of rules as a major reason for waste generation. This shows regulators’ awareness of their importance and sense of responsibility. In case of regulators, it can also be observed from Table 4, that B1 and B2 are found as the most prominent cause barriers with the highest D + R values 4.47 and 4.431 respectively78. Therefore, regulators are required to develop policies and ensure their implementation to promote CE practices in the construction industry. To foster CE in construction, regulators must move beyond passive identification and actively formulate and enforce supportive policies.

Policy measures for waste minimization

In response to counter the identified barriers, semi-structured interviews were conducted from all major stakeholders and analyzed. Results are presented in following sections.

Word frequency analysis

The analysis of word frequency revealed the words most commonly spoken by interviewees during the interviews. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the statistical analysis identified “construction,” “management,” “specifications,” “authorities,” “implementation,” “designated,” “relaxation,” “business,” “collection,” and “incentives” as the primary clusters with the highest frequency. This indicates that the experts frequently mentioned these terms in their discussions to suggest environmental policy guidelines for waste management in the construction industry. Consequently, managing construction waste, implementing environmental regulations, organizing waste collection, setting up designated landfill sites, fostering businesses, easing tax burdens, and offering incentives can greatly decrease the volume of waste generated at construction sites. This effectively summarizes the themes discussed by the interview participants. Findings of this analysis shows importance of multiple strategies which can be implemented in different phases of project such as clear specifications, giving incentives to workers, collection of waste, dump the waste in designated landfill sites and imposing fines in case of violation or illegal dumping. Further, authorities must also play their role in implementation of waste management practices such as relaxation in taxes for companies following waste control practices, ensure collaboration among departments, provides financial assistance in business developments.

Conceptual framework against identified barriers

Based on stakeholder interviews and thematic analysis, a comprehensive WM and CE policy framework was developed to provide actionable guidance for policymakers, regulators, and industry practitioners. The framework, shown in Fig. 5, is structured into macro-level and micro-level enablers, translating broad recommendations into operational strategies tailored to Pakistan’s institutional context. At the macro level, interventions focus on governmental responsibilities, regulatory enforcement, and financial support mechanisms. Construction firms and suppliers can be encouraged to adopt CE practices through tax relaxation, duty exemptions, and low-interest financing options, while subsidies for projects utilizing recycled materials would help close the cost gap between virgin and recycled resources. Previous studies emphasize that business models built around recycling and resale of construction materials are central to advancing CE culture, especially in developing economies where waste reduction initiatives are constrained by limited financial resources79. Regulatory enforcement also needs to be strengthened through mandatory waste reporting, contractor certifications, and performance-based penalties for non-compliance80. Allocating designated landfill sites and introducing differential disposal fees for unsorted waste could deter illegal dumping practices. Alongside these measures, nationwide awareness campaigns should focus on educating clients, contractors, and the public about the financial and environmental benefits of CE practices, supported by public-private partnerships (PPPs) that promote waste segregation and recycling. Business development initiatives that stimulate entrepreneurship in recycling plants, material recovery facilities, and logistics networks would further support a circular economy, while green procurement policies can create market incentives for sustainable practices.

At the industrial level, represented in Cluster 2, construction organizations and industry bodies are tasked with integrating WM methods across operations. Mandatory environmental certification for all projects prior to approval would ensure that waste minimization is systematically incorporated into project planning and design, serving both as a regulatory requirement and a market indicator of sustainability. Training programs, certifications, and regular workshops for contractors and consultants would help raise awareness and improve practices across the sector, with prior studies highlighting stakeholder behavior and collaboration as key drivers of effective WM81. Industry platforms for collaboration among departments, contractors, and environmental authorities would also help align company-level initiatives with national objectives, promoting consistency in waste reduction efforts.

Micro-level enablers, presented in Cluster 1, emphasize project-specific strategies to integrate CE principles throughout the project lifecycle. During the design phase, architects and engineers should prioritize modular construction, prefabrication, and standardized components to reduce waste at its source. Interviewees noted that reducing late-stage design changes is critical to minimizing rework and unnecessary material use, while strategies such as modular design and standardization can make a significant difference in overall waste generation. In the construction phase, well-structured waste management plans, accurate material ordering, and proper handling practices are essential. Contractors should be held accountable through contractual obligations that mandate adherence to waste reduction measures, while safe storage protocols, procurement of materials with minimal packaging, and segregation of reusable items can further reduce losses. Studies also emphasize the role of contractual enforcement in ensuring that construction phase strategies are implemented effectively82. After project completion, systematic post-construction practices, including segregation of inert and non-inert materials, waste auditing, and the use of designated recycling bins, can ensure leftover materials are properly routed to recycling facilities, supporting the creation of a robust secondary materials market.

The proposed framework underscores the need for a coordinated and multi-tiered approach to WM and CE adoption in Pakistan’s construction industry. Macro-level reforms, such as tax relief, regulatory enforcement, and market development, provide an enabling environment for CE integration, while industrial-level measures, including certification systems and stakeholder training, ensure widespread compliance. Micro-level practices, tailored to project-specific requirements, institutionalize WM principles across all stages of a building’s lifecycle. Policymakers are encouraged to introduce a national WM regulatory code with recycling targets, implement tiered incentives tied to measurable waste reduction outcomes, establish centralized platforms for tracking waste flows, and collaborate with professional bodies such as PCATP and PEC to embed CE practices in design and construction guidelines. Pilot projects supported by government funding can serve as practical demonstrations of CE adoption and provide data for refining future policies. This structured, multi-tiered roadmap recognizes that WM and CE integration requires synergy across government, industry, and project levels, with each tier reinforcing the other to create a sustainable, circular construction sector.

Discussion of the results and practical implications

This study harnessed fuzzy DEMATEL to navigate the complex causal landscape of barriers to implementing WM and CE practices in Pakistan’s construction sector, offering a systemic rather than superficial insight. The analysis revealed that macro-level barriers specifically the non-availability of regulations (B1) and financial constraints (B2) serve as primary systemic drivers that cascade down, exacerbating project-level inefficiencies such as unclear specifications (B12), poor design (B9), and weak stakeholder collaboration (B6), as illustrated in the Web-Diagram (Fig. 6). This interconnected dynamic reflects the core premises of systems theory, wherein structural flaws at the institutional level radiate through operational layers, culminating in fragmented construction practices. At the same time, institutional theory sheds light on how weak coercive mechanisms (absence of strong regulatory enforcement) and limited normative influences (absence of sustainability norms in the industry) constrain CE-oriented innovations and holistic waste reduction. In linking our findings to broader literature, studies of South Asian and developing economies echo these barriers. For example, research on CE adoption in South Asian industrial sectors underscores interdependent institutional and operational challenges that mirror findings of current study83, while investigations into circular construction risks in Pakistan highlight “lack of political support and incentives,” “profit uncertainty,” and “circular material quality” as critical impediments84. Moreover, broader reviews affirm that policy gaps and limited business models are common obstacles in transitioning toward CE in Pakistan. These comparisons strengthen the theoretical relevance of our framework, situating it within a growing body of evidence that calls for integrated systemic reform rather than isolated project interventions.

Beyond confirming global patterns, current study’s unique contribution lies in mapping the hierarchical and reciprocal interplay between barriers. Fuzzy DEMATEL allowed us to quantify the centrality and causal prominence of each barrier revealing, for instance, that regulatory voids are not just present but structurally preeminent in driving downstream inefficiencies. This analytical layering moves beyond conventional barrier lists and aligns with CE scholarship that emphasizes multi-level intervention points (institutional, organizational, project) to drive systemic circular transitions. Figure 6’s overlay of stakeholder perceptions adds further nuance. The strongest alignment was observed between contractors and regulators, with a 69% similarity, a Jaccard index of 0.56, and Spearman’s ρ of 0.72, reflecting both overlap and concordance in barrier prioritization. The high alignment between contractors and regulators (69% similarity) and the shared views of clients and consultants, highlight stakeholder dynamics85, it is the underlying reasons for these (mis)alignments that provide meaningful insight. Moderate agreement was evident between clients and consultants (62%, J = 0.47, ρ = 0.65) and between clients and regulators (62%, J = 0.47, ρ = 0.60), suggesting shared recognition of regulatory inadequacy and financial constraints as critical impediments. However, consultants and contractors exhibited minimal alignment (38%, J = 0.23, ρ = 0.35), while clients and contractors showed only modest concordance (46%, J = 0.31, ρ = 0.42), revealing gaps in communication and divergent priorities linked to their roles within the project lifecycle. These misalignments are consistent with studies that highlight coordination challenges in developing contexts, where planning-stage actors often remain out of sync with on-the-ground implementers7. Such discrepancies exacerbate the risk that regulations remain ill-suited to operational realities, undermining policy efficacy. Taken together, the study underscores that effective CE adoption requires more than technical fixes, it demands systemic operational reform, institutional strengthening, and stakeholder alignment. In Pakistan, where CE discourse is emerging, existing CE models remain nascent and fragmented. There is growing attention to modular construction, design for disassembly, and resource recovery yet uptake remains constrained by structural limitations86. Proposed framework, therefore, provides both a diagnostic tool and a roadmap for integrating CE principles across governance scales, from national policy to project execution.

The theoretical insights translate into practical strategies captured in Fig. 5, which integrates macro (institutional), meso (industry-level), and micro (project-level) enablers. At the institutional level, policy reform should include the introduction of binding CE-oriented building codes, environmental certification systems, and digital enforcement mechanisms to close regulatory gaps and raise accountability. At the industry or sector level, financial stimuli such as tax reliefs, subsidies for recycling firms, and public procurement quotas for recycled materials can energize circular business models. Moreover, facilitating platforms to coordinate clients, consultants, contractors, and regulators can bridge perception gaps and align priorities. At the project level, interventions should emphasize design-for-deconstruction, prefabrication, material standardization, waste audits, and contractual obligations that promote resource-efficient execution. Collectively, these layered strategies respond directly to the causal structure identified targeting the root, not just the symptoms, of WM and CE barriers. By interweaving institutional theory, systems theory, and CE frameworks with empirical insights and stakeholder interplay, this study offers theoretical depth and contextually grounded recommendations tailored to Pakistan’s built environment. In doing so, it advances both scholarship and practice, supporting policymaking that addresses systemic constraints while promoting operational feasibility, stakeholder alignment, and sustainable construction, thus contributing meaningfully toward SDG 9 (sustainable infrastructure), SDG 12 (responsible consumption), and SDG 13 (climate action).

Conclusions

The present study seeks to provide insight into important root-cause barriers to WM practices in building projects in the construction sector, along with a comparison of the perceptions of major industry stakeholders. On the basis of the findings of the fuzzy DEMATEL approach, comparative analysis of stakeholder’s perspectives reveals varying levels of concordance across industry groups, with the highest degree of alignment observed between contractors and regulatory authorities, who exhibit a 69% agreement on critical issues such as inadequate regulatory oversight (B1) and financial constraints (B2). Similarly, clients and consultants demonstrate a moderate level of consensus, with 62% convergence in their views, indicating a shared perception of key impediments to effective industry performance. Notably, a broad-based agreement emerges among all principal stakeholders, including contractors, regulators, clients, and consultants, on a set of persistent and interrelated barriers. These include the absence of robust regulatory frameworks (B1), limited access to or mismanagement of financial resources (B2), deficient stakeholder’s awareness and education (B3), the imposition of insufficient penalties for illegal dumping practices (B7), irrational or impractical design choices (B9), and the lack of clarity in technical specifications and project requirements (B12). The collective recognition of these root causes underscores their systemic nature and provides a critical foundation for the formulation of targeted, evidence-based mitigation strategies. Furthermore, the framework proposed in the current study provides detailed guidance to policymakers regarding WM strategies at the national, industrial, and project levels in the construction sector. So, the issue of waste generation can be solved by making efforts at the macro- and micro-levels. It is found that financial support and awareness programs at the macro level, while modifying contract documents and collection and segregation of waste, reusing and recycling wasted materials at the micro level can be effective measure to promote WM and CE cultures in developing countries. Such strategies hold the potential to address underlying challenges effectively, thereby enhancing regulatory compliance and overall project sustainability across the industry, if implemented effectively.

This study has several limitations. It was conducted in a developing country, with data collected from various building projects. Further, use of purposive and sampling methods limits the generalization of results beyond construction industry of Pakistan. As a result, the findings may differ somewhat in other developing nations. Future research should aim to validate the outcomes of the current framework in other developing countries and assess its effectiveness.
