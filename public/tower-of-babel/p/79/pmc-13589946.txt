Targeting the SETD2–FTO–KIT pathway as a pharmacological strategy for TKI-refractory GIST
Authors: Zhi Li, Junju He, Zhen Cao, Shishan Zhou, Yongwei Zhu, Yumei Duan, Anfeng Jiang, Yidi Guan, Jie Gao, Jian Tian, Liyu Liu, Zhuoxian Rong, Heli Liu, Xiaowei Liu, Hengyi Xiao, Yuezhen Deng, Bin Li, Lunquan Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have significantly improved outcomes in gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GIST), but acquired resistance remains a major clinical obstacle. The underlying mechanisms that sustain oncogenic KIT signaling in TKI-refractory GIST are not fully understood. Here, we identify a previously unrecognized epigenetic–epitranscriptomic regulatory axis involving SETD2, FTO, and KIT that promotes TKI resistance by stabilizing KIT mRNA. FTO, a key N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA demethylase, was found to be markedly overexpressed in high-risk and recurrent GIST samples and correlated with poor prognosis. Mechanistically, FTO enhances KIT mRNA stability by erasing m6A modification in its 3′ untranslated region (UTR), thereby preventing YTHDF2-mediated degradation and sustaining oncogenic KIT expression. Furthermore, the histone methyltransferase SETD2 activates FTO transcription via H3K36 trimethylation at the FTO promoter, establishing a self-reinforcing loop that maintains FTO overexpression. Functional experiments using FTO knockout and pharmacological inhibition (CS1 and entacapone) demonstrated that disrupting FTO activity suppresses tumor proliferation, reduces KIT expression, and restores sensitivity to imatinib both in vitro and in vivo. In KIT-Asp818Tyr/+ genetically engineered GIST mouse models, the combination of entacapone and imatinib led to significant tumor regression. Most importantly, preliminary clinical results from a Phase I trial (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04006769) showed that treatment with entacapone plus imatinib resulted in partial remission in two out of three evaluable TKI-refractory GIST patients, supporting the translational relevance of this strategy. Collectively, our findings establish the SETD2–FTO–KIT axis as a critical mechanism driving drug resistance in GIST and reveal m6A demethylation as a key regulator of KIT mRNA stability. Targeting FTO represents a promising and actionable therapeutic strategy to overcome TKI resistance and improve outcomes for patients with advanced GIST.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GIST) are the most common sarcomas of the GI tract. It originates from the interstitial cells of Cajal (ICCs) that rely on high level KIT expression for survival. Most (75%–80%) of GIST have KIT mutations, which promote proliferation, survival and invasion of tumor cells1–2, 3. Imatinib, a small molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), is the first approved medical treatment for unresectable or metastatic GIST, however, resistance was unavoidable. This resistance derived mainly from the selection of heterogeneous subclones of the KIT mutants upon imatinib treatment, resulting in poor response to multiple alternative TKIs including sunitinib, regorafenib, and ripretinib4, 5, 67. Of note, 95% of GIST highly express KIT, which is regulated by the conserved core network of transcription factors (TFs), including ETV1, all of which are essential for GIST viability and predictive of patient outcomes8,9. However, it remains unresolved how the KIT and GIST transcription and post-transcriptional regulatory networks interact and what the clinical relevance of this interaction is regarding drug resistance in GIST.

N6-Methyladenosine (m6A) is the most prevalent and abundant post-transcriptional RNA modification in mammalian mRNA. A wealth of evidence indicates that m6A methylation plays a critical role in regulation of mRNA fate by methyltransferases (“writer”), demethylases (“erasers”) and RNA-binding protein (“readers”), nevertheless, most of context-dependent functions of each m6A residue is largely unknown10, 11, 12. FTO is the first identified mRNA demethylase, which regulates the stability of mRNA and is involved in tumorigenesis, progression and drug resistance13. Of note, Yan et al. reported for the first time that the development of resistant phenotypes during TKIs therapy depends on m6A reduction caused by FTO overexpression in leukemia cells14, which suggests a potential role for FTO and m6A dysregulation in GIST. Moreover, while several recent studies have indicated the role of METTL3 in imatinib resistance in GIST15,16, whether m6A modification is involved in GIST development and imatinib resistance requires further elucidation. Given the critical oncogenic roles of FTO in many types of cancers, extensive efforts have been dedicated to developing inhibitors targeting FTO for cancer therapy13,17,18, such as CS1 and CS2, which exhibit much higher antitumor activity than previously reported FTO inhibitors19, and entacapone, a clinically used COMT inhibitor with emerging FTO-targeting and anticancer potential20,21.

In this study, we identified FTO as the most prominently differentially expressed m6A-modifying gene in GIST, with high FTO expression serving as an independent prognostic factor for poor prognosis in GIST. The RNA-seq and m6A-seq results revealed that FTO enhanced the stability of KIT mRNA by removing m6A modifications in its 3′UTR, and activated the core transcriptional regulatory network of GIST. Using the FTO inhibitor-treated xenografts and KIT-Asp818Tyr/+ genetically engineered mouse model, we further revealed that loss of FTO function could inhibit GIST growth and increase imatinib sensitivity in vivo. In clinical settings, a combination treatment of imatinib and sunitinib resistant patients with imatinib and entacapone demonstrated a clinical benefit response. Overall, these data demonstrate FTO to be an effective promoter for GIST progression and drug sensitivity and suggest that the inhibition of FTO activity has therapeutic potential for the TKIs-refractory GIST patients.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

GIST-T1, GIST-882 and GIST-430 cells were maintained in RPIMI (Gibco, 11875093) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, A5256701), 2 mmol/L l-glutamine (Gibco, A2916801), 1% penicillin–streptomycin antibiotics (Gibco, 15140122). GIST-48 cells were cultured in IMDM (Gibco, 12440061) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% HEPES (Gibco, 15630106), 2 mmol/L l-glutamine, and 1% penicillin–streptomycin.

Western blot analysis

RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma) was used to extract the proteins. The protein concentrations were quantified using Bradford reagent (Bio-Rad). The proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (ThermoFisher) and quantified by UV spectrophotometry. cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg RNA using the PrimeSTAR GXL kit (Takara). qRT-PCR was performed with Bio-Rad iQ SYBR Green Supermix on a CFX Connect system. CQ values were analyzed in triplicate, normalized to GAPDH, β-actin, or 18S RNA.

Gene-specific m6A qPCR

MeRIP-qPCR was conducted using the Magna MeRIP m6A Kit (Millipore) per the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, 200 mg total RNA was fragmented (∼100 nt), incubated with m6A antibody- or IgG-conjugated beads overnight, and immunoprecipitated. Methylated RNA was eluted with free m6A, purified, and quantified by qPCR, normalizing m6A-IP Cq to input. One-tenth of fragmented RNA served as an input control.

Mouse procedures

KIT-Asp818Tyr/+ mice were constructed with CRISPR/EGE™ strategy by biocytogen (Beijing, China) replacing G2452 of mouse Kit CDS with T. The Ftoflox/flox; KIT-Asp818Tyr/+; UBC-Cre-ERT2 and the related transgenic mice were generated through standard mouse breeding within the institutional animal facility.

All animal experimentation was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee at the affiliated research institution and was in accordance with the National Policy on Humane Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. For the gastrointestinal tract of mice, the small intestine, large intestine or cecal GIST tumors were dissected, separated, and embedded in paraffin for subsequent analyses.

Immunohistochemistry scoring

IHC-stained sections were digitized as whole-slide images on a PhenoImager HT system (Akoya Biosciences) hosted at the Biomedical Center, Institute for Advanced Study of Central South University, under standardized illumination and acquisition settings. Using the platform's image-analysis software, tissue was computationally segmented and tumor areas were distinguished from non-tumor compartments, after which nuclei and perinuclear cytoplasm were automatically identified on the basis of hematoxylin counterstaining and a predefined peri-nuclear ring. For each marker, staining intensity and the proportion of positive tumor cells were quantified at the single-cell level and combined into semi-quantitative H-scores for subsequent statistical analyses. All automated segmentations and quantitative readouts were independently inspected and, when necessary, manually adjusted by two pathologists blinded to clinical data. Survival analyses were conducted using Kaplan–Meier estimates and log-rank tests.

Clinical study design and participants

This investigator-initiated phase I trial was conducted at a tertiary academic medical center, approved by the institutional Ethics Committee (No. 201907156) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (No. NCT04006769). Written informed consent was obtained. Patients had primary KIT exon 11 mutations and progressed on imatinib and sunitinib. Entacapone, repurposed as an FTO inhibitor, was administered at ∼3 g/day based on dose conversion (Detailed dosage calculation protocol is provided in the Supporting Information). The primary endpoint was safety (CTCAE 5.0), and secondary endpoints included ORR (CHOI criteria) and quality of life (EORTC QLQ-C30 V3.0). Routine blood tests, liver/kidney function, troponin, and ECG were monitored.

Statistical analyses in the study were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) in our study. Data were analyzed by unpaired or paired Student's t-test and presented with mean ± SD unless otherwise specified. Two-sided P value < 0.05 was statistically significant.

Results

FTO is highly expressed and correlated with poor prognosis in GIST patients

To contextualize our analyses, Fig. 1A summarizes the workflow. The study moves from clinical cohort mining and mechanistic/phenotypic validation to establishing an m6A-linked signaling axis, then to preclinical/clinical translation testing modulation of this axis with imatinib. Guided by this framework, we first explored the expression of all known m6A ‘writers’, ‘erasers’ and ‘readers’ in Cho dataset (GSE13861)22. Among all the m6A modification related genes, the mRNA levels of KIAA1429, FTO, ALKBH5, YTHDC2, IGF2BP2 and YTHDF2 were found to be differentiated significantly between gastric GIST and normal surrounding gastric tissue, in which FTO is the most significantly up-regulated one (Supporting information Fig. S1A and S1B). Furthermore, according to the MediSapiens IST Online database, the relative expression of FTO in GIST tissues, rather than other m6A enzymes, was significantly higher than that in non-GIST soft tissue sarcomas (Fig. S1C) or in other types of gastrointestinal tract tissues (Fig. S1D). In accordance, data mining of previously published proteomics data confirmed that FTO was highly expressed in both low/intermediate and high-risk GIST tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. S1E)23. The unique FTO expression profile was further validated in our clinical cohort. First, we found that the levels of m6A RNA modification were significantly lower in GIST tissues compared to the matched adjacent tissues via a dot blot assay (Fig. S1F and S1G), while the mRNA level of FTO was significantly increased in GIST (Fig. S1H). Then immunohistochemistry (IHC) analyses of the GIST tissues from 347 GIST patients of an institutional cohort (Cohort 1) showed that FTO expression was positively associated with the tumor grading classified by National Institute of Health (NIH) Consensus stratification scheme (Fig. 1B and C). Moreover, three established risk factors for recurrence of GIST including high mitosis, non-gastric location, and large tumor size, were all significantly correlated with enhanced FTO expression (H-Score) (Fig. 1D–F). Given that acquired imatinib resistance in advanced GIST is driven in the majority (∼90%) of cases by secondary KIT mutations3,24, we hypothesized that FTO-regulated m6A might be particularly relevant in this molecular setting. To interrogate this, we collected 30 GIST samples (Cohort 2), including 10 recurrent samples without secondary mutations, 10 recurrent samples with secondary mutations, and 10 non-recurrent samples (Supporting information Table S3). The mass spectrometry and dot blot analysis of m6A modification and protein expression assays of FTO were performed. We found that the expression level of FTO in recurrent samples with secondary mutation was significantly higher than that in the non-recurrent samples and the recurrent samples without secondary mutation (Supporting information Fig. S2A–S2C); Accordingly, the m6A level in the recurrent samples with secondary mutation was significantly lower than that in other two groups (Fig. S2D and S2E). Moreover, the m6A level in the GIST samples was negatively correlated with the FTO expression level (Fig. S2F). In support of these observations, the expression of FTO in 65 relapsed cases (all carried KIT secondary mutations) of Cohort 1 was found to be significantly higher than that in non-relapse samples (Fig. 1G and H). Furthermore, high FTO expression was significantly associated with worse overall survival (Fig. 1I). Collectively, these data indicate that FTO may be involved in tumor progression and drug resistance processes of GIST.

FTO promotes malignant growth and imatinib resistance in GIST cells

To define the functional role of FTO in GIST, we first performed loss-of-function studies in imatinib-sensitive GIST-T1 and GIST-882 cells. FTO knockdown reduced proliferation, increased apoptosis and lowered the IC50 of imatinib, indicating enhanced drug sensitivity (Supporting information Fig. S3A–S3H). Moreover, Tet-on induced knockdown of FTO in GIST-882 cells significantly impaired the colony formation ability in soft agar, while overexpression of FTO in GIST-882 cells substantially increased colony formation in soft agar (Fig. S3I–S3L). As expected, wild-type FTO rather than mutant FTO (FTO (M)) that harbors two point-mutations, H231A and D223A disrupting the enzymatic activity of FTO25, decreased m6A RNA levels and promoted proliferation of GIST-T1 cells (Fig. S3A–S3C). Consistent with these findings, knockdown of FTO in imatinib-resistant GIST-430 cells with two different siRNA caused a consistent increase of m6A RNA levels (Fig. 2A and B) and significantly inhibited cell proliferation assayed by CCK8 test and EdU staining (Fig. 2C–E). Given that imatinib resistance remains a major clinical challenge in GIST, we investigated whether FTO knockdown modulates imatinib resistance. To test this hypothesis, we assayed the effect of FTO knockdown plus imatinib treatment on GIST-430 cells. The combinational treatment significantly increased cell apoptosis compared to imatinib treatment alone in GIST-430 cells (Fig. 2F and G). In consistency, the IC50 of imatinib was markedly decreased in GIST-430 cells after FTO knockdown (Fig. 2H). To further explore the cell biological characteristics associated with imatinib resistance of GIST, we established imatinib-resistant GIST cell line GIST-T1R and found that knockdown of FTO in GIST-T1R significantly increased sensitivity to imatinib (Fig. 2I–L). FTO sustains a KIT-centered transcriptional program in GIST

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which FTO promotes GIST progression and sustains imatinib resistance, we treated imatinib-sensitive (GIST-T1 and GIST-882) and imatinib-resistant (GIST-430 and GIST-48) GIST cell lines with the FTO inhibitor CS119 for 48 h (at a concentration of 100 nmol/L) and subsequently extracted RNA from the cells for RNA-seq. Dot-blot analysis revealed that CS1 significantly enhanced m6A modification in GIST cells (Fig. 3A). Analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified a total of 885–1865 genes resulting from CS1 treatment, with 631–1172 genes upregulated and 121–326 genes downregulated in the four GIST cell lines respectively (|log2 FC| > 1, P adj <0.05) (Fig. 3B and C, Supporting information Fig. S4A and S4B, Supporting information Tables S4–S7). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) showed that CS1 treatment significantly enriched the apoptosis pathway in imatinib-resistant GIST-48 and GIST-430 cells (Fig. 3D). Consistently, in the imatinib-sensitive GIST-T1 and GIST-882 cells, CS1 treatment led to a marked enrichment of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the Pathway in Cancer and the PI3K–AKT signaling pathway (Fig. S4C). To further define functional genes regulated by FTO in GIST, we performed GSEA analysis on the DEGs after CS1 treatment using the 50 hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signature Database26, along with three GIST-signature gene sets from the Segal, Nielsen and ExpO datasets8, as well as the imatinib-downregulated gene set and the ETV1-upregulated gene set from the PingChi datasets8. The results indicated that FTO inhibition by CS1 led to a significant downregulation of the GIST-signature gene sets (Supporting information Fig. S5A and S5B), imatinib-downregulated gene set (Fig. S5C–S5E) and ETV1 (an oncogenic transcriptional factor regulated by KIT in GIST)6-upregulated gene set in both imatinib-sensitive and resistant GIST cells (Fig. S5C–S5E). To further elucidate the key downstream genes of FTO, we performed an intersection analysis of differentially expressed genes (|log2 FC| > 1, P adj <0.05) in four GIST cell lines treated with DMSO or CS1, respectively. Three genes, KIT, STIM2, and SPRY4, were found to be commonly downregulated in all four GIST cell lines after CS1 treatment (Fig. 3E and F, Fig. S4D). KIT is a canonical driver gene represented in all three GIST-signature gene sets27. SPRY4 has been reported as one of the most strongly downregulated transcripts in GIST cells after KIT inhibition with imatinib and is included in the Segal and Nielsen signatures28. STIM2 is likewise among the top ETV1-upregulated genes, and ETV1 transcriptional program is regulated downstream of KIT8. These results suggest that FTO helps maintain a KIT-centered transcriptional network and the high expression of key GIST-signature genes, thereby contributing to tumor progression and sustaining imatinib resistance.

KIT is a direct m6A target of FTO in GIST

To identify specific targets of FTO in GIST based on m6A modification, we treated GIST-430 cells with DMSO or CS1 and performed m6A-seq to pinpoint target genes regulated by FTO via demethylation of m6A. After CS1 treatment, 3914 upregulated and 1751 downregulated m6A peaks were detected, indicating that FTO inhibition by CS1 significantly enhanced m6A modifications (Fig. 4A–C, Supporting information Table S8). As reported previously29, m6A modifications were predominantly located near the stop codon of the RNA 3′UTR (Supporting information Fig. S6A–S6C). Most genes with increased m6A modification were single-base modified (Fig. S6D and S6E), and m6A modifications were enriched in the RRACH motif (Fig. S6F and S6G). To explore the relationship between differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and m6A modifications regulated by FTO, genes were classified into four categories based on the analytical strategy as described30: 1) increased m6A modification and decreased expression, 2) increased m6A modification and increased expression, 3) decreased m6A modification and decreased expression, and 4) decreased m6A modification and increased expression (Fig. 4D). Among the 324 genes with increased m6A modification, 69.4% (225/324) showed decreased expression, significantly more frequent than the 47.6% (60/126) in the 126 genes with decreased m6A modification (P < 0.0001, χ2 test). These results suggest that FTO regulates mRNA transcripts with hyper-methylated m6A peaks, which are often downregulated in FTO-inhibited GIST cells. Eight genes, including KIT and STIM2, were identified as overlaps between DEGs and differentially methylated genes (Fig. 4E and F). IGV visualization confirmed that CS1 treatment promoted m6A modification near the 3′UTR termination codon of KIT mRNA (Fig. 4G). Given the critical role of KIT3 and its significantly higher expression compared to STIM2 in GIST (Tables S4–S7), we focused on investigating its direct regulation by FTO. siRNA-mediated FTO knockdown increased m6A at the KIT 3′UTR and reduced KIT mRNA abundance (Fig. 4H and I). Moreover, overexpression of wild-type but not the enzymatically inactive mutant FTO (M), elevated KIT mRNA levels (Fig. 4J). To exclude off-target effects of siRNA, we transfected cells with a synonymous FTO mutation (FTO (S)) or enzymatically inactive FTO (M) after FTO knockdown. FTO (S), but not FTO (M), restored KIT expression and S6 phosphorylation (Fig. 4K and L). Consistent with the mechanistic data above, immunohistochemistry on GIST tissue samples from the institutional patient cohort (Cohort 1) showed a significant positive correlation between FTO and KIT levels, as well as FTO and S6 phosphorylation (Supporting information Fig. S7). Collectively, integrative m6A/RNA-seq, genetic/pharmacologic perturbations, and cohort IHC converge to establish KIT as a direct FTO target: FTO demethylates the 3′UTR of KIT mRNA, sustaining KIT expression and downstream S6 signaling in GIST.

Multiple pharmacologic FTO inhibitors validate the FTO–KIT axis

Building on the omics and genetic perturbation data in section 3.4—where CS1-based RNA-seq/m6A-seq and FTO knockdown established KIT as an FTO-regulated m6A target that drives downstream S6 signaling—we next asked whether pharmacologic FTO blockade using three structurally distinct small-molecule inhibitors (CS1, entacapone, and FB23-2) would phenocopy genetic perturbation at the levels of KIT mRNA m6A demethylation, KIT/S6 signaling and imatinib sensitivity. Treatment of GIST cells with CS1 led to dose-dependent suppression of KIT mRNA levels, without altering FTO mRNA levels, as revealed by RIP-qPCR (Fig. 5A). m6A-qPCR showed a significant increase in m6A modification of KIT mRNA after CS1 treatment (Fig. 5B). To establish KIT as a direct FTO target, we performed RIP-qPCR, which showed that FTO binds the 3′UTR of KIT mRNA and that CS1 treatment suppresses this interaction (Fig. 5C). Western blot analysis confirmed that CS1 suppressed KIT protein expression and its downstream phosphorylated S6 levels in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5D–F). CS1 also induced apoptosis in GIST cells (Fig. 5G and H). Following the reported IC50 in tumor cells31, entacapone20, another FTO inhibitor used in Parkinson’s disease treatment21 significantly increased total m6A levels in GIST cells (Supporting information Fig. S8A) and exhibited effects similar to CS1, including suppression of KIT mRNA and protein expression, enhanced m6A modification of KIT mRNA and reduced S6 phosphorylation (Fig. S8B–S8F). Entacapone also enhanced imatinib-induced apoptosis (Fig. S8G and S8H) and sensitized GIST cells to imatinib (Fig. S8I and S8L). Colony formation, anchorage-independent growth, and EdU assays further confirmed entacapone's inhibitory effect on tumor cell proliferation (Supporting information Fig. S9), supporting its potential as an anti-GIST agent. To exclude off-target effects, GIST cells treated with CS1 or entacapone were transfected with wild-type or mutant FTO. Wild-type but not mutant FTO restored KIT expression and reversed the inhibitory effects of CS1 or entacapone (Supporting Information Fig. S10). m6A-qPCR after stable TOP2 or COMT knockdowns showed no impact on m6A levels of KIT mRNA, ruling out effects via these known targets of CS1 (type II topoisomerases, TOP2) and entacapone (catechol-O-methyltransferase, COMT)18,32 (Supporting information Fig. S11A and S11B). m6A-seq confirmed that CS1, entacapone, or FTO interference increased m6A modification of KIT mRNA (Fig. 4G, Fig. S11C–S11E, Supporting information Tables S8–S10), validating the specificity of FTO inhibitors.

FB23-2, an optimized derivative of FB23, exhibits improved FTO inhibition and antitumor activity; crystallography shows FB23 occupies the substrate pocket rather than the 2-oxoglutarate pocket, supporting greater selectivity among reported FTO inhibitors33. In line with this, FB23-2 reduced global m6A demethylation (i.e., increased m6A), decreased KIT mRNA and protein, increased m6A on KIT mRNA, and lowered p-S6 (Supporting information Fig. S12A–S12E). Functionally, FB23-2 suppressed colony formation and cell viability and sensitized cells to imatinib (Fig. S12F–S12K), further validating FTO-dependent control of KIT in GIST.

In summary, our data suggest that across CS1, entacapone and FB23-2, pharmacologic FTO inhibition effectively suppresses KIT expression via preventing m6A demethylation and enhances imatinib sensitivity in GIST cells. These findings highlight the potential of FTO as a therapeutic target in GIST.

Previous studies have reported that m6A modifications affect RNA metabolism and function via over 10 types of m6A “readers” including YTHDF1-3, YTHDC1-2, IGF2BP1-3, HNRNPA2B1, HNRNPC and HNRNPG9,35. We next used unbiased screening approaches to determine the potential reader(s) of the m6A modification of KIT mRNA. We designed two different specific siRNAs against each of 10 candidate readers and confirmed the knockdown efficiency of these constructs (Fig. 6G and H, Fig. S13G). Among the 11 potential m6A readers, only YTHDF2 was shown to be involved in KIT down-regulation as knockdown of YTHDF2 markedly increased both the protein and mRNA expression of KIT in GIST cells (Fig. 6G–I, and Fig. S13H), which is consistent with previous report36. Furthermore, RNA immunoprecipitation-qPCR (RIP-qPCR) assay with YTHDF2 antibody showed the enrichment of 3′UTR of KIT mRNA in YTHDF2 binding (Fig. 6J and Fig. S13I), and YTHDF2 knockdown significantly increased transcript half time of KIT (Fig. 6J and K, Fig. S13J). Moreover, analysis of clinical specimens showed that YTHDF2 expression was lower in tumors than in adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. S13K), reduced in recurrent versus non-recurrent GIST (Fig. S13L and S13M), and inversely correlated with KIT expression (Fig. S13N–S13P), consistent with the Cho dataset (GSE13861)22, which reports low YTHDF2 in tumors and high in normal gastric tissues (Fig. S1A and S1B). Clinical parameter analyses further revealed negative associations between YTHDF2 and NIH risk category, tumor size, and mitotic index (Supporting information Table S13). Survival analysis indicated that low YTHDF2 expression predicts inferior overall survival (Fig. 6L). Collectively, FTO promotes the progression and sustains drug resistance of GIST cells via directly removing the m6A modification in the mRNA 3′UTR of KIT recognized by YTHDF2.

SETD2-mediated H3K36me3 modification sustains the expression of FTO in GIST

To elucidate the mechanism by which FTO is sustained in GIST, we first examined whether FTO undergoes changes in copy number variation (CNV) by analyzing exome sequencing data from 18 GISTs available in the European Bioinformatics Institute (EBI) Sequence Read Archive under accession number PRJEB640137. Neither CNV gain nor loss of FTO was observed, nor were any genetic mutations in FTO detected (Supporting information Figs. S14A and S14B, S15). Furthermore, we analyzed the DNA methylation microarray data of GIST clinical samples38 to determine whether the FTO promoter has methylation modifications. No significant changes in FTO promoter methylation were seen (Fig. S14C–S14E). Based on the above observations, we speculated that epigenetic remodeling possibly accounts for the sustained FTO expression. Utilizing the ChIPBASE v2.0 database (http://rna.sysu.edu.cn/chipbase/)39, several histone modifications were found in the promoter region of FTO (1 kb upstream/downstream of the transcription start site, including H3K36me3, H3K9me3, H3K27me3, H3K4me3, etc.), among which H3K36me3 is the most abundant type of histone modification (Supporting information Table S14). H3K36me3 modification was displayed in the promoter of FTO using the UCSC genome bioinformatics platform (http://genome.ucsc.edu/) (Fig. 7A). As H3K36me3 is catalyzed by SET domain containing 2 (SETD2)37,40,41, we examined if SETD2 has any impact on FTO expression. In GIST cells with SETD2 knockdown, transfection of wild type FTO, not mutant FTO, into the cells could effectively recover the KIT signaling and capacity of cell survival (Fig. 7E and F, Fig. S16D–S16F). To further validate the critical roles of SETD2 in GIST, we mined the data from the genome-wide CRISPR screening of GIST-T1 and GIST-430 cell lines7. Based on this analysis, genes were categorized into three groups: (1) Pan-essential genes, which are universally required for cell viability42,43; (2) GIST-essential genes, identified with an FDR <0.05 in the CRISPR screen of GIST cells but not included in the pan-essential list; and (3) Nonessential genes9. We found that SETD2 was catalogized as one of “GIST essential genes” among the reported GIST essential genes such as KIT, KAT6A, KMT2A, MEAF6, and ETV1, and the dependency (β) score of SETD2 were basically the same as those of KAT6A, KMT2A, MEAF6, and ETV1 (Fig. 7H). Moreover, we leveraged available comparative screening results from Project DRIVE44, which included GIST-T1 among 387 cancer cell lines profiled by RNAi. Among all cell lines profiled, GIST-T1 was in the top 5% for SETD2 (Supporting information Table S15 and Fig. 7I), sensitivity score being comparable to KMT2A and ASH2L, two reported GIST essential genes9. This analysis results with Project DRIVE further underscores the essential role of SETD2 in GIST through an independent and comparative screening approach.

In our imatinib-resistant GIST-T1R model, SETD2 and FTO expression exceeded that in parental GIST-T1 cells, whereas YTHDF2 was reduced (Fig. 7J). In clinical specimens, SETD2 was higher in tumors than in adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. S16H) and higher in recurrent than in primary samples (Fig. S16I and S16J). Across 347 GIST tumors, SETD2 and FTO expression were positively correlated (Fig. S16K and S16L). Clinicopathologic analysis further showed positive associations between SETD2 and NIH risk category, tumor size, and mitotic index (Supporting information Table S16). Notably, SETD2 expression was positively associated with overall survival (Fig. S16M).

Lastly, we examined if SETD2 inhibitor exhibited an inhibitory effect on GIST in vitro. GIST-430, GIST-T1 and GIST-TIR cells were treated with SETD2 inhibitor EZM041445,46. The results shows that EZM0414 significantly reduced FTO expression and its downstream signaling. It also decreased H3K36me3 levels at the FTO promoter and inhibited GIST cell growth. These effects were accompanied by an increased m6A modification in the 3′UTR of KIT mRNA (Supporting information Fig. S17). Together, SETD2 promotes the transcription of FTO by facilitating the trimethylation of histone H3K36 in the FTO promoter.

Of note, in xenograft models, the SETD2 inhibitor EZM0414 enhanced imatinib sensitivity and reduced FTO and KIT expression (Supporting information Fig. S23). This supports the role of SETD2-mediated H3K36me3 in sustaining imatinib resistance in GIST by maintaining KIT expression and activating downstream S6 phosphorylation.

Finally, to exclude the off-target effects of the FTO inhibitors in vivo, FTO knockdown in GIST-430 xenografts replicated the effects of CS1 or entacapone on imatinib sensitivity, with no additional benefit from CS1 or entacapone (Supporting information Fig. S24), indicating FTO as the primary target for increased imatinib selectivity. Moreover, TOP2 or COMT knockdowns showed marginal tumor growth reduction with imatinib, but CS1 or entacapone significantly inhibited tumor growth regardless of TOP2/COMT knockdown (Supporting information Fig. S25), confirming that imatinib selectivity is independent of the disruption of TOP2/COMT activities.

Clinical efficacy of entacapone plus imatinib in imatinib-resistant GIST patients

Encouraged by our preclinical data, we initiated a Phase I clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT04006769) to evaluate the repurposed FTO inhibitor, entacapone, in combination with imatinib for patients with KIT-mutant GIST who had previously failed at least imatinib and sunitinib therapies. The primary endpoint was safety, while secondary endpoints included Objective Response Rate (ORR) and Quality of Life (QOL). According to the trial flowchart (Fig. 9A), five patients were enrolled between October 2020 and January 2021 (Supporting information Table S19). Among the five enrolled patients, Case 2 experienced rapid disease progression by Day 21 of the first dose, while Case 5 withdrew from the trial on Day 9 due to fatigue and anorexia. Consequently, these two patients were excluded from the efficacy evaluation as stipulated in the protocol. The remaining three patients completed at least one cycle of treatment (31 days) and proceeded to efficacy evaluation. Results indicated that two of the three patients achieved an objective response according to the CHOI criteria (2/3, 66.7%), compared to historical data showing an Objective Response Rate (ORR) ranging from 0% to 18.4% (Fig. 9B). Among the two partial responders, Case 1 exhibited a progression-free survival (PFS) of 21 months, while Case 3 had a PFS of 5 months (Fig. 9B). Imaging studies revealed that multiple liver lesions in Cases 1 and 3 demonstrated significant shrinkage and cystic degeneration following treatment with imatinib plus the entacapone (Fig. 9C). Quality of Life (QOL), assessed using the EORTC QLQ-C30 V3.0 questionnaire, improved in both responding patients (Supporting information Tables S20–S23). Notably, all three evaluated patients exhibited a marked reduction in the allele frequency of KIT mutations within exons 11 and 17, or exons 11 and 13, as measured from plasma circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) after receiving the entacapone and imatinib (Fig. 9D). Case 4 displayed a similar downregulation trend in exons 11 and 17 at the DNA level compared to Case 1, yet experienced worse clinical outcomes. Further ctDNA analysis of the genes potentially involved in GIST, based on previous studies2, revealed unique mutation profiles in PIK3CA and the tumor suppressor genes PIK3R1, CDKN2A, CDKN2B, PTEN, and TSC1 in Case 4 but not in Case 1 during treatment (Fig. 9F), which may partially account for their divergent outcomes.

Discussion

GIST is the most common mesenchymal-origin tumor in the gastrointestinal tract, with 70%–80% of which harbor functional KIT-mutation, for which imatinib, a multiply targeted kinase inhibitor of KIT, PDGFRA and BCR-ABL, is the first-line treatment in recurrent or metastatic settings4,49,50. Unfortunately, the majority of GISTs progress on imatinib treatment, and usually present with co-occurred polyclonal mutation subpopulations, resulting in complex intra- and inter-lesion heterogeneity. This presents a formidable challenge in the clinical management of refractory GIST with monotherapy using any approved KIT-targeted Tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), as reflected by the limited efficacy of subsequent-lines of sunitinib, regorafenib, or ripretinib4,4,51. Emerging evidence suggests that the m6A modification is involved in tumor progression and TKIs resistance14, 15, 16. In the present study, we identified a novel mechanism by which GIST progresses and sustains imatinib resistance through FTO-mediated increase in KIT mRNA stability. This has very important clinical relevance as dural targeting of KIT and FTO would potentially provide an effective treatment schedule for advanced recurrent GIST patients.

The discovery of KIT mutations as the first and most prevalent drivers of GIST led to the development of KIT-TKIs, including imatinib, sunitinib, and regorafenib, followed by new generations of inhibitors, such as ripretinib and avapritinib, to combat emerging drug resistance. However, the patients continue to show disease progression4. Therefore, the search for novel strategies to target KIT has become an urgent clinical need. As a crucial regulatory mechanism of biological function, m6A modification is characterized by its reversibility and site-specificity in regulation. The m6A methyltransferase deposits m6A within a specific subset of cellular transcripts, primarily near stop codons/start of terminal exons and within unusually long internal exons10. Our study reveals that FTO promotes mRNA stability by removing the m6A modification from the 3′UTR of KIT mRNA. Theoretically, secondary mutations in KIT only occur in the coding sequence (CDS) region and do not affect the base composition or m6A modification in the KIT mRNA 3′UTR region, which suggests that the high expression of FTO in imatinib-resistant samples can still sustain the constant expression of KIT. These data imply that GIST drug resistance may largely be caused by secondary KIT mutation and sustained by high FTO expression induced constant expression of KIT. Targeting FTO could lead to selectively degrading KIT mRNA, akin to the targeted degradation of KIT protein triggered by heat shock protein 90 (HSP90) inhibition in GIST52,53, which can provide some advantages in overcoming drug resistance (Supporting information Fig. S26).

Another intriguing finding of our study is that H3K36me3 modification sustains FTO transcription and downstream KIT expression in GIST, which implies a link between H3K36me3 modification and GIST malignancy and imatinib resistance. Similar to previous reports, the epigenetic modifications of histones are involved in the transcriptional regulation of m6A modifying enzymes in tumors54, suggesting a potentially common regulatory mechanism of m6A modifying enzymes in cancers. Given that the functions of epigenetic modifying proteins are tumor context-dependent, and SETD2 has been reported to have both tumor-suppressive and oncogenic roles in different cancers45,55,56, further in-depth investigation is required to elucidate the function of SETD2 in GIST.

An intriguing observation was the elevated FTO expression in GISTs harboring secondary KIT mutations, raising the possibility that KIT mutations might upregulate FTO and imply a bidirectional relationship between the two. However, our preliminary experiments do not support a regulatory model in which mutated KIT upregulates FTO. First, treatment of GIST-T1 and GIST-882 cells with the RTK inhibitor imatinib (which targets KIT) did not reduce FTO mRNA or protein, and siRNA-mediated KIT knockdown likewise failed to diminish FTO levels in imatinib-sensitive and -resistant cells (Fig. S2G and S2H, S2L and S2M). Second, overexpression of the primary KIT mutant—the 57-bp exon 11 deletion endogenous to GIST-T1—did not increase FTO expression in GIST-T1 cells (Fig. S2I and S2N). Finally, we used CRISPR–Cas9 with homology-directed repair to introduce V654A (exon 13)—the secondary KIT mutation found in GIST-430—into the GIST-T1 background, generating heterozygous and homozygous V654A lines; across all V654A clones, FTO expression was unchanged relative to parental GIST-T1 (Fig. S2J and S2K, and S2N). Together, these data indicate that neither primary nor secondary KIT mutations regulate FTO expression in GIST cells.

Notably, in Cohort 2, we observed higher proportions of SETD2 and H3K36me3-high cases among tumors with secondary KIT mutations (Table S3). We therefore propose that, under long-term TKI pressure, clones co-harboring SETD2-mediated FTO upregulation and acquired secondary KIT mutations become selectively enriched: the former sustains high KIT expression, while the latter attenuates KIT kinase inhibition by TKIs—together prolonging KIT signaling within the same clone. In future work, we will expand the GIST cohort with and without secondary KIT mutations to rigorously test this hypothesis.

The association between m6A and cancer has attracted substantial interest, and an increasing number of small molecules now target m6A regulators, with FTO inhibitors being the most intensively explored57. Although multiple FTO inhibitors have been reported, most remain at the preclinical stage and warrant deeper investigation. Mechanistically, FTO inhibitors can be grouped as 2-oxoglutarate (2OG)-competitive, substrate/2OG dual-competitive, or substrate-competitive agent58. Among the compounds used in our study, entacapone is a dual-competitive inhibitor (substrate/2OG)21; By contrast, the crystal structure of CS1 in complex with FTO is unavailable, and its inhibitory mechanism remains unclear19; FB23-2 is a substrate-competitive inhibitor with improved selectivity supported by structural data33.

Taken together with the fact that entacapone and CS1 have known off-targets (COMT and TOP2, respectively), FB23-2 currently has the best-supported FTO selectivity among these three inhibitors. In our study, CS1, entacapone, and FB23-2 each destabilized KIT mRNA and sensitized GIST cells to imatinib. Together with genetic rescue experiments and off-target controls (e.g., COMT/TOP2 knockdown) that support on-target activity of entacapone and CS1, these data indicate that FTO inhibition with three distinct inhibitors (CS1, entacapone, and FB23-2) increases imatinib sensitivity in vitro, while CS1 and entacapone further enhance imatinib efficacy in vivo and entacapone shows exploratory clinical activity. Looking ahead, we will closely follow advances in FTO inhibitor chemistry and deploy next-generation, higher-specificity compounds to further validate these conclusions.

Regorafenib is generally recommended by various guidelines, such as NCCN, ESMO and CSCO, for the GIST patients who have progression on the treatment of first-line imatinib and second-line sunitinib. The evidence from the phase III clinical trial of GRID shows that the ORR was about 4.5%, and the mPFS was 4.8 months59. In our explorative phase I clinical trial using FTO inhibitor entacapone plus imatinib rechallenge, two of three evaluated patients showed promising objective responses to this combination therapy, with substantial improvement of QOL. The progression-free survival of these two response cases was 5 and 21 months, respectively, which was superior to the historic data of imatinib rechallenge alone, showing ORR of 0 and mPFS of 1.8 months60. The treatment-emergent adverse events were mild (grade ≤2), but no serious toxicity was observed in all five subjects, indicating better tolerance than that of regorafenib treatment57. Notably, the allele frequency of multiple KIT mutants including the exons 11, 13, and 17 mutations in plasma ctDNA, in all three exhibited a marked reduction or was maintained at a low level.

While these observations are encouraging, this Phase I safety-focused, single-arm study enrolled very few patients; accordingly, efficacy findings are exploratory and statistically imprecise (wide confidence intervals), precluding definitive conclusions. The observed CHOI response among the three evaluable patients (2/3) should therefore be interpreted with caution, without formal hypothesis testing and not as proof of clinical benefit. A Phase II study by our group is planned to further evaluate the efficacy and durability of imatinib plus entacapone in multi-TKI-resistant GIST.

Conclusions

Our study reveals a previously unrecognized epitranscriptomic mechanism contributing to tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) resistance in gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GIST), in which FTO enhances KIT mRNA stability through m6A demethylation. We demonstrate that SETD2-mediated H3K36 trimethylation sustains FTO transcription, forming a SETD2–FTO–KIT regulatory axis critical for GIST progression. Pharmacological inhibition or genetic ablation of FTO reduces KIT expression, suppresses malignant phenotypes, and sensitizes GIST cells to imatinib both in vitro and in vivo. Notably, combination therapy with the FTO inhibitor entacapone and imatinib induced tumor regression in a KIT-Asp818Tyr/+ GIST mouse model and yielded encouraging responses in a small phase I clinical trial involving TKI-refractory GIST patients. These findings provide compelling evidence that targeting FTO represents a promising therapeutic strategy to overcome TKI resistance and recurrence in GIST.
