Can open label placebos improve pain and gluten tolerance via open label placebos in fibromyalgia patients? A study protocol for a randomised clinical trial in an outpatient centre
Authors: Lena Paschke, Norma Dreyer, Margitta Worm, Regine Klinger
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
IntroductionFibromyalgia syndrome (FMS) is defined as a medical condition with chronic widespread musculoskeletal pain accompanied by mood disorders, fatigue and sleep disturbances. Treatment of this condition can often be challenging. As nutrition in general and nutritional interventions in the context of illness management become more and more important, current research also focuses on the relevance of diets for FMS, including gluten as field of interest. To date, there is no clear evidence that a gluten-free diet or other nutritional interventions are significantly important for the reduction of pain in the context of FMS. Only a very few studies show that FMS patients respond to a gluten-free diet and that cytokine production (also in FMS) can be reduced through the change. However, these studies have not investigated whether and to what extent cognitive factors, such as the expectation of symptom reduction triggered by diet, play a role. Recent research shows that treatment expectation plays an important role in the course of the disease and in the effectiveness of treatment approaches. For example, there are promising pain treatment options using open-label placebos (OLPs), which show that expectation alone, rather than the pharmacological substance of medication, can reduce pain experience. In our study protocol, we hypothesise that treatment expectation can be positively influenced by the given information regarding the placebos, resulting in improved treatment outcomes for pain and indigestions.Methods and analysisIn this trial, patients with FMS will undergo a food challenge and take an OLP (patients will be informed about the placebo), followed by a 3-week OLP treatment. The subjects will be randomised into four groups: (a) gluten-free porridge+neutral OLP instructions; (b) gluten-free porridge+positive OLP instructions; (c) gluten-containing porridge+neutral OLP instructions and (d) gluten-containing porridge+positive OLP instructions. Patients will be recruited via different institutions and support groups in Hamburg. The inclusion criteria are (a) diagnosed FMS, (b) absence of wheat allergy, coeliac disease or pain-related red flags and (c) being a minimum age of 18 years. The study requires 100 subjects to assess the primary outcomes: pain intensity and occurence of indigestion. Secondary outcomes are functional capacity, treatment expectation, and different pain-related and inflammation-related blood parameters. The measure time points will be before and after the food challenge and before and after the 3-week OLP treatment.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval was obtained in October 2021 from the Hamburg Medical Ethics Council. The results of the study will be disseminated through publications, presentations and conference meetings.Trial registration numberGerman Clinical Trials Register (DRKS; DRKS00027130).

Introduction

Background

Fibromyalgia syndrome (FMS) is described as a syndrome characterised by chronic widespread musculoskeletal pain, joint stiffness and other symptoms such as mood disorders, fatigue, cognitive dysfunctions and sleep disturbance.1 2

To date, treatments for FMS produce limited benefits, even drug approaches are not very effective and in most cases medication is prescribed off label. Research shows that general practitioners report a lack of knowledge and skills in treating the condition.3 Both professionals and patients express dissatisfaction with the management of symptoms, and patients report a greater need for moral support.4 Currently, the most promising treatment option is a multimodal, interdisciplinary approach with pharmacological and specific psychological components, tailored to pain.5 However, additional treatment options are urgently needed.

Gluten intolerance: the role of nutrition

The role of nutrition has become increasingly important in the general population.6 Studies show that the relevance of healthy foods has risen in recent years. Especially, gluten often appears as a matter for discussion, and sales and consumption of gluten-free products have significantly increased in the past 30 years.7 8 Although the prevalence of different forms of gluten intolerances has increased of late,9–11 the majority of people avoiding gluten in their eating habits do not suffer from any form of gluten intolerance.12 Following a gluten-free diet without suffering from coeliac disease, however, may have adverse effects.13 In some conditions, a gluten-free diet indeed leads to improvement of symptoms although coeliac disease or wheat allergy cannot be proven. These include non-coeliac gluten sensitivity (NCGS), and affected patients can benefit from excluding gluten-containing products.14 15 However, more research is needed to establish the extent and exclusivity of this clinical picture.16

Although some studies indicate an association between FMS and NCGS, data about their relationship are not sufficient. A recent study found a 5.6% prevalence of NCGS among FMS patients, which is similar to that estimated in the general population.17 The researchers also found 21.8% patients responding to a gluten-free diet. By contrast, in a smaller study, all participants revealed improvement in FMS symptoms after following a gluten-free diet.18 19 Another study showed these effects especially in FMS patients with lymphocytic enteritis.20 One study even found a reduction in proinflammatory parameters after participants followed gluten-free diet.21 However, alternative diets, such as hypocaloric diets or Khorasan wheat-based replacements lead to comparable symptom reduction,22 23 indicating that there is no clear connection between certain eating behaviours, such as gluten-free diet and FMS. Moreover, a review of nutritional interventions in FMS concluded that there is little evidence for nutritional interventions playing a significant role in FMS treatment.24 Reasons for pain reduction in FMS after diet adjustments could rather be seen as a combination of weight loss and psychosomatic component of the disease.25 To date, the German Pain Society’s 2017 guidelines on FMS do not give clear advice for certain dietary interventions, due to the lack of sufficient evidence. Therefore, the effectiveness of diet-related interventions for treating FMS is still of interest and thus pursued in the current research.

Physiological parameters

While the aetiology of FMS remains unclear, there is some evidence suggesting central and peripheral sensitisation as a potential pathophysiological mechanism for the syndrome. Therefore, some studies have investigated serum cytokine-levels as mediators of neuro-inflammation processes.26

Schinocca et al for instance, investigated the effect of a gluten-free diet on patients with FMS and a healthy control group. They found that almost all patients with combined FMS and NCGS benefited from the gluten-free diet in terms of clinical parameters measuring symptoms regarding FMS and digestion. Furthermore, these patients even showed reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels after the diet.21

Further, through conducting a meta-analysis, O’Mahony et al demonstrated that FMS patients have a different cytokine profile than the healthy control group. However, the role of these cytokines remains unclear, especially since FMS is associated with proinflammatory tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-6 and IL-8, as well as anti-inflammatory IL-10 and the chemokine eotaxin, which attracts eosinophil granulocytes that play a role in allergic reactions or collagenosis. According to O’Mahony et al, the strongest significance exists for TNF-α, IL-8, and eotaxin.27

Systemic inflammation and the resulting sickness behaviour, including affective symptoms, are transmitted through immunological messenger substances, such as TNF-α and other cytokines. In clinical trials investigating the immunological response to endotoxaemia, the highest levels of cytokines were detected 4 hours after the application of lipopolysaccharides. Moreover, higher levels of these cytokines resulted in a lower pain threshold and negative affection. Thus, expectation, and therefore open-label placebo (OLP), might influence not only pain perception, but immunological processes as well.28

Additionally, neuropeptides, such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and nerve growth factor (NGF), might play a role in the pathogenesis of neural sensitisation.29 30 In FMS patients, both neuropeptides are elevated in the cerebrospinal fluid, and some studies have shown even BDNF to be elevated in peripheral serum analyses. However, more recent findings from a clinical trial by Baumeister et al spread doubt about whether serum NGF and BDNF levels are elevated in patients with FMS, therefore there is a need for further exploration.31 The above studies, apart from lacking evidence for physiological parameters, also did not measure the influence of expectation on treatment results and the formation of indigestion in FMS, although expectation is known to be an important factor in this regard.

As expectations are able to modulate the immune response,28 cytokines and other experiential serum pain markers might be influenced by such manipulation through OLP and the corresponding instruction.

Treatment expectation

Positive and negative treatment expectation are known to be an important factor for placebo, nocebo effects and treatment results.32–37 Moreover, even food intolerances can be partly explained by expectation.38 A large proportion of patients with symptoms due to lactose malabsorption, for instance, show nocebo effects, making the current strategies to diagnose lactose intolerance invalid.39 40 However, the extent of these processes in FMS patients with gluten sensitivity is not well examined.

To make use of placebo effects in clinical practice, it would not be ethically acceptable to apply hidden delivery of placebos. In this regard, one possible solution to maintain the advantages of placebo analgesia would be giving the placebos openly. These so-called OLPs have been shown to lead to comparable levels of pain reduction as pain medication, despite patients being aware of having taken placebos. This effect has been established concerning several pain diseases such as irritable bowel syndrome and chronic lower back pain.41–43 The mechanism underlying this effect might be treatment expectation, which can be induced by different processes: observation or social learning, previous experience (classical conditioning) and verbal information.44–46

This study aims to influence the participants’ expectation via different forms of verbal information regarding the placebos. Studies have shown that access to information about prescribed medication can modulate the effects of the given substances.47–50 There is similar evidence that this mechanism also works for OLPs: in a study with patients with irritable bowel syndrome, OLP led to an improvement in symptoms.42 However, not every study has found evidence that provision of detailed information about placebos influences the efficacy of OLP.51 Instructions explaining brain mechanisms and positive expectation seem to be most persuasive for subjects at least in the general population.52 Although several studies indicate the important role of expectation for treatment outcome, its investigation is still lacking and future studies should include this topic in their research.

Objectives and outcome

Primary objectives

The aim of this study is to investigate whether neutral or positive instruction induces different expectations and whether these expectations lead to different treatment results after an oral food challenge regarding pain and indigestion. Primary objective will be the difference in pain before and after gluten provocation and after a 3-week OLP treatment on a numeric rating scale (NRS) 0–10 (0=no pain; 10=worst pain imaginable).

The key questions of this study are as follows:

Can the expectation of receiving gluten-contraining porridge impact the development of symptoms in fibromyalgia patients after their consumption of gluten porridge in comparison to placebo-gluten porridge?

Can the expectation of receiving gluten-contraining porridge impact the development of symptoms in fibromyalgia patients after their consumption of gluten porridge in comparison to placebo-gluten porridge?

Can a positive treatment expectation, in comparison to a neutral treatment expectation, impact the development of symptoms after the consumption of gluten-containing porridge or placebo-gluten porridge?

Can a positive treatment expectation, in comparison to a neutral treatment expectation, impact the development of symptoms after the consumption of gluten-containing porridge or placebo-gluten porridge?

Can a positive treatment expectation impact the development of physiological detectable (blood, microbiome) symptoms in fibromyalgia patients?

Can a positive treatment expectation impact the development of physiological detectable (blood, microbiome) symptoms in fibromyalgia patients?

Does the impact of treatment expectation on treatment outcome differ between subjective (pain, perceived limitation in moment ability) and behavioural (functional capacity) outcome measures?

Does the impact of treatment expectation on treatment outcome differ between subjective (pain, perceived limitation in moment ability) and behavioural (functional capacity) outcome measures?

Secondary objectives

Secondary objective will be the intensity of possible symptoms occurring in the context of irritable bowel syndrome or coeliac disease on a NRS (0=absence of symptom, 10=very high intensity of symptom). Further secondary outcomes that will be measured, are movement limitations, evaluated by the participants on a NRS (0=no limitation, 10=high limitation) and the time, participants need to perform certain exercises. To evaluate a possible specific inflammatory process, we will measure specific cytokines and neuropeptide levels (IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, eotaxin, BDNF, NGF, Substance P, CGRP) before and after the oral food challenge and after the OLP-treatment.

Methods and analysis

Setting

This study is part of a research association with Wheat-A-Baic (a model allergen to better understand food allergy and intolerance and to improve the healthcare of affected children and adults). The consortium consists of seven subprojects investigating physiological characteristics of wheat allergy from distinct perspectives. One subproject involves focussing on microbial and metabolomic factors in wheat allergy and also analysing stool samples of our FMS patients. The influence of expectation might manifest in changes of the microbiome and metabolomics. The research association is sponsored by the German Federal Ministry of Education and Research. Our subproject is conducted at the University Medical Centre Hamburg-Eppendorf, Germany. The prestudy started in May 2022. The start of the recruitment for the main study was in August 2023. We expect to be finished with the recruitment in May 2024.

Patient and public involvement

Patients were involved during the formation of the research questions as there appeared to be a rising interest in the role of gluten in FMS. During the first rehearsals, patients were asked to assess the feasibility of the trial. Support groups were contacted during the recruitment phase, and members were enquired about their experiences with gluten and FMS.

Recruitment

For participant recruitment, the study was presented in several fibromyalgia support groups. Further participants will be recruited via general practitioners, specialised institutions for pain and physiotherapists in Hamburg.

Study design

The study will follow a 2×2 factorial, fully balanced within-subject and between-subject research design. Expectation (positive instructions with the aim of high expectation=‘high’; neutral instruction with the aim of low expectation=‘low’) and food challenge (gluten-free/gluten-containing) will be fully crossed. The study will include three in-house appointments and one 3-month follow-up via video call. If patients meet the inclusion criteria, they will be informed about the procedure of the study and asked for their informed consent and material consent. A first set of questionnaires will be sent by mail. For a visual illustration of the study process, see figure 1.

Study design.

The study design involves the following three phases:

Phase 1

At the first in-house appointment, participants will submit their completed questionnaires and provide the first stool and blood samples. During this screening session, inclusion criteria will be checked in greater detail. To confirm the absence of gluten-sensitive enteropathy or wheat-allergy, we will collect blood samples and analyse them for IgA in total (to rule out an IgA deficiency), transglutaminase antibodies and wheat-specific IgE. After receiving some information about gluten in general and gluten-free food, participants will undergo a gluten-free diet for 10 days, apart from maintaining a symptom-and-pain diary.

Phase 2

After participants finish their diet and provide a further blood and stool sample, they will undergo a double-blinded oral food challenge at the second appointment. First, we will check for potential symptoms of the digestive system to detect contraindications for an oral food challenge, such as an acute infection. Before eating the porridge, participants will evaluate their current pain and current status of indigestions on an NRS (0=low severity; 10=worst severity) for a list of typical symptoms of gluten intolerance (eg, abdominal pain, feeling bloated, nausea, diarrhoea, heartburn). To analyse movement restrictions, we will ask participants to perform four ordinary exercises (picking up a coin; putting on and taking of socks; standing up from a lying position; putting a beverage crate on a table) and report the subjective level of pain and impairment. Then, we will randomly assign them to one of the four experimental groups: gluten-free/low expectation, gluten-free/high expectation, gluten-containing/low expectation and gluten-containing/high expectation, by pulling an envelope. The participants can observe the randomisation process but does not know the allocation. While the porridge is being mixed, the participant receives two placebo tablets. Dependent on the allocation, the participant’s expectation regarding the placebos will be modulated by different instructions (figure 2). Thirty minutes after finishing the porridge, the questionnaires for indigestion, pain and movement restrictions will be collected again. This procedure will be repeated 4 hours after the oral food challenge, and another blood sample will be taken. In the meantime, participants are asked to stay near the building. If symptoms occur during the waiting time, participants can document them on a symptom list with NRSs. The third stool sample (first stool after oral food challenge) will be collected by the participants at home.

Treatment information. OLP, open-label placebo.

Phase 3

The participants will then undergo a 3-week OLP treatment. They will take two placebos at each meal of the day and fill out their symptom-and-pain diary. The last on-site appointment will occur after OLP treatment, involving assessment of pain and indigestion and collection of blood and stool samples.

At the follow-up session after further 3 months, the assessment tools from the screening will be repeated and participants will be informed about their group allocation and the rationale of the study. The last stool samples will be collected at this point.

Blinding

Blinding will be employed regarding the allocation of the porridge. Neither the researcher nor the subject will know whether the porridge contains gluten or not.

Expectation manipulation via instructions

Corresponding to the randomisation, patients in the high-expectation group will listen to a detailed explanation of the placebos they will take. In this standardised description, the neurophysiological mechanisms of placebo effects will be explained in a comprehensible manner. To combine the different mechanisms underlying placebo effects in terms of somatic and visceral pain, we will mention both physiological correlates: the endogenous opioidsystem as part of pain processing in FMS and the gut-brain axis as part of proper digestion of gluten. Patients in the low-expectation group will just receive information about a placebo that does not contain any pharmacologically active ingredients.

Sample size calculation

Comparable studies in the field of placebo research on irritable bowel syndrome and other chronic pain disorders have found medium to large effect sizes related to pain intensity and irritable bowel symptoms after placebo interventions. Consequently, a medium to large effect size is also expected from this study, which should occur at least in the pre-post comparison for food provocation.

Assuming a medium effect size, a power analysis based on an analysis of variance (ANOVA, F-test) with repeated measures (interaction effects within-between factor, f=0.2, α=0.05, power 1−ß=0.9, four groups, five repeated measures) results in a case size of 100 participants including a drop-out rate of approximately 10%. Thus, the number of participants per group is n=25.

Statistical analysis

Differences between and within subjects regarding the different outcome measures will be assessed with ANOVA tests. The analyses will be performed with IBM SPSS Statistics Software V.27 using a 95% CI. In case of violation of sphericity, Greenhouse-Geisser correction for F-tests will be used. For all analyses, p values less than 0.05 will be considered as statistically significant. Exploratory post-hoc analysis will be conducted if the main results are found significant.

Outlook and perspective

Fibromyalgia patients usually report dissatisfaction with their treatment, as treatment of FMS is challenging and effective treatment approaches are lacking. Especially, medical treatment options often lead to insufficient results, and in recent years, prescription of opioid medications has increased. Thus, alternative approaches are urgently needed to improve medical care for FMS patients and to encounter the different aspects of this syndrome.

If positive instructions about placebos—not concealed but openly offered, prove to enhance patients’ responses to OLP regarding pain and indigestion, this outcome could have important implications for clinicians. Placebos then could serve as an ethically acceptable alternative or supplementary to alleviate both pain and indigestion in fibromyalgia patients.

The double-blind placebo design using porridge will also help us gain new insights on the role of expectation in the intake of certain foods such as gluten. This part of the experiment would also enable us to draw conclusions about the role of expectations in the development of subjectively experienced food intolerances. Nevertheless, an important question here concerns the extent to which participants’ expectations are reflected in objective parameters.

If the results show that a strong negative expectation toward food triggers subjectively experienced symptoms, and if this is also indicated in objective parameters (eg, inflammation parameters in blood or serum), a further treatment approach could be applied. In addition to education about expectations, specific expectation management, through which the patient learns to change their own expectations, also becomes relevant.
