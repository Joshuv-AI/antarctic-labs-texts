4′-hydroxychalcone nanofiber hydrogel dressing promotes diabetic chronic wound healing by regulating macrophage polarization via the TLR/IL-17/TNF signaling pathway
Authors: Hong-Yang Li, Mi-Yang Li, Chuan-Xin Cui, Hong-Mei Yu, Yu Sun, Ji-Jun Yang, Ya-Ru Li, Yi-Quan Li, Tie-Mei Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe activation and polarization of macrophages play a crucial role in the inflammatory response in chronic diabetic wound healing. Modulating macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype can facilitate wound healing. 4′-Hydroxychalcone (4HC) possesses antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, and the preparation of novel drugs through electrospun nanofibers and hydrogels can enhance its therapeutic effects. However, the effects of the 4′-hydroxychalcone nanofiber hydrogel dressing (P-4HC) prepared in this study on macrophage polarization and its potential mechanisms in the treatment of diabetic chronic wounds have not yet been elucidated.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the effect of P-4HC on macrophage polarization in diabetic chronic wounds and to elucidate its potential mechanisms of action.MethodsIn vitro, cell compatibility tests were conducted using L929, HUVECs, and RAW264.7, along with ROS, scratch assays, tube formation assays, immunofluorescence tests for macrophage phenotypes, flow cytometry, and Western blotting (WB). In vivo, a diabetic mouse skin wound model was established, and HE and Masson staining, immunohistochemical staining of COL3A1, HIF-1a, and VEGF, immunofluorescence staining of CD31, CD86, and CD206, WB, RT-qPCR, and ELISA were conducted.ResultsIn vitro and in vivo, P-4HC exhibited good cell compatibility, improved tissue hypoxia, and promoted angiogenesis and collagen deposition. It promoted the transition of macrophages from M1 to M2 type, downregulated pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, and upregulated anti-inflammatory cytokines and pro-angiogenic cytokines, thereby promoting the healing of diabetic chronic wounds. Transcriptome analysis indicated that the TLR/IL-17/TNF pathway was the main pathway mediating the effects of P-4HC. P-4HC promoted macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype and enhanced wound healing by inhibiting the expression of TLR9, IL-17A, and TRAF1, thereby suppressing the TLR/IL-17/TNF signaling pathway.ConclusionP-4HC promotes the polarization of macrophages from M1 to M2 phenotype by inhibiting the TLR/IL-17/TNF signaling pathway, thereby suppressing inflammation and mitigating tissue damage. These findings provide a scientific basis for the treatment of chronic diabetic wounds and lay a foundation for exploring new therapeutic strategies for diabetes-induced inflammation based on macrophage polarization.Keywords: 4′-hydroxychalcone, Nanofiber, Hydrogel, Macrophage polarization, Diabetic wound

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder that poses a significant threat to individuals' physical and mental health. Recent studies have indicated that approximately 537 million people worldwide are living with diabetes, and its prevalence is projected to increase by 46 % by 2045 [1]. Diabetic wounds, as one of the most common complications of diabetes, have always been challenging in clinical practice due to their long treatment duration, high medical costs, and high recurrence rates [2]. Generally, the normal wound healing process has typical overlapping stages, including hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and tissue remodeling [3]; however, diabetic wound healing lasts longer due to chronic inflammation, infection, abnormal neovascularization, and reduced collagen deposition [4,5]. Prolonged inflammatory response is a hallmark of difficult healing in diabetic wounds, characterized by significant infiltration of inflammatory cells, such as M1 macrophages [6,7], finally leading to delayed transition from inflammation to remodeling [8]. Therefore, how to suppress excessive inflammation in diabetic wounds is a significant issue that needs further research.

Macrophages are immune effector cells used to maintain balance and resist invasive pathogens, playing a critical role in tissue homeostasis and injury [9]. Macrophages can be roughly divided into two categories based on their functions: classically activated macrophages (M1), representing a highly pro-inflammatory state, and alternatively activated macrophages (M2), representing an anti-inflammatory state. In normal wound healing, M1 macrophages dominate from day 1 to day 3, followed by a transition to M2 macrophages, which peak around the 7th day of wound healing. However, in diabetic wounds, the dominant macrophage phenotype remains M1, regardless of the stage of wound healing. This is a key factor in the chronic nature of diabetic wounds, reflecting an impaired transition from M1 macrophages to M2 macrophages in diabetic wounds [4]. Accumulating evidence suggests that macrophage polarization is closely associated with inflammation and diabetic wound healing [10,11]. Excessive abundance of M1 macrophages leads to chronic inflammation; therefore, inducing M2 polarization of macrophages is believed to suppress the inflammatory response and enhance proliferation and diabetic wound healing [12]. Studies have reported that plant extracts and hydrogel dressings can promote the polarization of macrophages from M1 type to M2 type, thereby reducing inflammation and promoting wound healing [[13], [14], [15]].

Wound dressings with immunomodulatory and angiogenic properties have become a critical measure for diabetic wound healing [[16], [17], [18]]. This has led to the rise of many new treatment methods or dressings, such as various wet dressings and peptide therapy [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23]], but none of these methods are perfect, each has its own focus. The electrospinning technique can produce nanofibers with diameters similar to those of the natural extracellular matrix (ECM). These electrospun nanofibers possess a large surface area and high scaffold porosity, which facilitate cell adhesion, proliferation, migration, and differentiation [24]. Additionally, they can promote diabetic wound healing by delivering active agents in a controlled manner [25]. However, in addition to excellent biological properties, an ideal wound dressing should also possess favorable mechanical properties [26]. Hydrogels are highly hydrophilic [27], biocompatible [28], and feature a three-dimensional porous structure resembling the ECM [29]. They demonstrate superior water absorption and high gas permeability, which can prevent bacterial invasion [30]. Their adhesive properties enable sustained drug release in the wound [31], while their sufficient compressibility and recovery capabilities prevent mechanical rupture [26]. Studies have shown that the combination of hydrogels and nanofibers to form novel dressings can significantly enhance wound healing [32,33]. Conventional antibiotics often lead to drug resistance. Therefore, this study incorporated traditional Chinese medicine monomers into the wound dressing to more effectively promote wound healing. 4HC, an α-β unsaturated ketone, has a core structure of chalcone (1,3-diphenyl-2-propenone) [34]. It possesses antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, reducing IL-6 and TNF-α levels while increasing IL-10 levels, thereby alleviating inflammation [35]. Chalcones, aromatic ketones belonging to polyphenolic compounds, are natural antioxidants and precursors for the synthesis of flavonoids. Chalcones exhibit antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties. The main advantage of natural medicines is the lack of toxicity, along with their specific bioactivity and bioavailability. Therefore, this study employed the electrospinning method to prepare 4′-hydroxychalcone-loaded nanofibers, which were then incorporated into a hydrogel to develop a wound dressing with anti-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic properties. The therapeutic effects of this dressing on diabetic chronic wounds were investigated, and transcriptomic analysis was conducted to explore its underlying mechanisms.

Experiment

Fetal bovine serum (FBS), Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM), RPMI-1640 medium, Trizol Reagent and SYBR Green were purchased from Sparkjade Biotechnology Co., Ltd. ELISA kits and reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay kit were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (China). Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) was purchased from Dojindo. Tegaderm™ transparent dressings were procured from 3M Health Care. TRAF1 overexpression plasmids (PPL50124-2a) were purchased from Public Protein/Plasmid Library, China, and the transfection reagent was purchased from Invigentech, Shanghai, China. Anti-F4/80/APC, anti-CD206/FITC, and anti-CD86/PE were purchased from BioLegend. Antibodies against iNOS (2982), IL-6 (12912), TNF-α (3707), MyD88 (4283), and HIF-1α (48085) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (USA). CD206 (18704-1-AP), Arg-1 (66129-1-Ig), CD86 (30691-1-AP), IL-17A (26163-1-AP), IL-17RC (15648-1-AP), IL-10 (82191-3-RR), COL3A1 (68320-1-Ig), β-actin (66009-1-Ig), and CD31(80530-1-RR) were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). TLR9 (bs-23640R), IL17RA (bs-2606R), and VEGF-A (bs-1313R) were purchased from Bioss Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). TRAF1 (ab300075) was purchased from Abcam.

Cells and animals

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), mouse fibroblast L929 cells, and mouse monocyte macrophage leukemia RAW 264.7 cells were purchased from BNCC. Both L929 and RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in DMEM medium, while HUVECs were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5 % CO2, and other experiments should be carried out after the cells were exposed to high glucose (33 mM) to simulate the microenvironment of diabetes. Male db/db mice and C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Hangzhou Ziyuan Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Jilin University. The animal facility was equipped with air filtration systems, and the temperature was maintained at 22–24 °C with 40 %–70 % humidity. The animals had access to standard food and water ad libitum.

Preparation of P-4HC

First, polylactic acid (PLA) was mixed with 4HC and processed into PLA/4HC composite nanofibers via electrospinning. The resulting fibers were homogenized into short fibers and then dispersed into a PVA/HHA hydrogel solution to prepare 4HC-loaded nanofiber hydrogels at two concentrations: low-dose (1.5 mg/mL) and high-dose (3 mg/mL). The solutions were transferred into molds, such as 96-well plates (0.1 mL/well), 24-well plates (1 mL/well), 12-well plates (1 mL/well), and 6-well plates (2 mL/well), sealed with parafilm to prevent moisture loss, and subjected to freeze-thaw cycles (−20 °C for 22 h followed by 25 °C for 2 h). After 6 cycles, the following hydrogels were obtained: low-dose 4HC nanofiber hydrogel (P-4HC-L), high-dose 4HC nanofiber hydrogel (P-4HC-H), and without 4HC (P-4HC-0).

Drug release

The P-4HC hydrogel was fully immersed in PBS with pH = 7.4 and shaken at a constant temperature of 37 °C. 1 mL of release medium was taken out at the set times of 6h, 12h, 24h, 48h, 72h, etc., and the same volume of fresh release medium was added. The concentration of 4HC was detected by high performance liquid chromatography.

Swelling ratio

Dried hydrogels submerged in PBS at 37 °C. At 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h, the hydrogels were removed from the PBS, thoroughly cleaned with filter paper, and then weighed right away. The weights of the initial hydrogels and the swelling hydrogels at different times are denoted by Wd and Ws. The swelling ratio of the hydrogels was calculated as follows: Swelling ratio (%)= (Ws-Wd)/Wd × 100.

Water retention rate

The samples that have reached the swelling equilibrium are placed in the centrifuge tube (the weight is Ws). Centrifuge at 3000 g for 20 min at room temperature. After centrifugation, the sample was immediately removed, the surface liquid was gently absorbed with filter paper, and the sample was quickly weighed. This weight was the remaining weight after centrifugation (Wᵣ). The following formula was used to calculate the water retention rate: Water retention rate (%) = (Wᵣ/Ws) × 100.

Hemocompatibility assay

The hemolysis assay was used to assess the hydrogels' hemolytic qualities. Mouse blood was centrifuged for 10 min at 2000 rpm in order to extract the RBCs. P-4HC-0, P-4HC-L, and P-4HC-H were extracted for 24 h using PBS in order to get extracts. The diluted RBC solution was mixed with 1 mL PBS as a negative control, 1 mL P-4HC-0 extract, 1 mL P-4HC-L extract, and 1 mL P-4HC-H extract, along with 1 mL deionized water as a positive control. After 2 h of incubation at 37 °C, the mixtures were centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was then collected so that the absorbance at 540 nm could be measured by the microplate reader. Hemolysis (%) = [(A sample – A PBS)/(A DW – A PBS)] × 100.

CCK-8 assay

HUVECs, L929, and Raw 264.7 cells were passaged at 5 × 10^3 cells/well into 96-well plates containing P-4HC-0, P-4HC-L, and P-4HC-H, respectively (200 μL/well), with 6 replicates per group. The blank control group was cultured without hydrogel for 24 h. Then, CCK-8 was added to the aforementioned 96-well plates (20 μL/well) and incubation continued in the incubator for 2–4 h. Subsequently, 100 μL of the culture medium from each well was transferred to the corresponding well in a new 96-well plate. The new 96-well plate was placed on the microplate reader and the absorbance (OD value) of each well was measured at 450 nm. The highest and lowest values were discarded, and the average OD value of the four replicate wells was calculated as the OD value for each sample. Then, the relative cell viability was calculated.

Live/dead cell staining

L929 cells were seeded in 12-well plates (2.5 × 105 cells/ml) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. P-4HC-0, P-4HC-L, and P-4HC-H were soaked in DMEM (10 % FBS) at 37 °C for 24 h in advance. The medium in the cell culture wells was discarded, and the above hydrogel extract was added. After 24 h of culture, Calcein-AM and PI were added to stain the cells. The live cells were stained green, and the dead cells were stained red.

ROS detection

We used L929 to study the ability of P-4HC to scavenge intracellular ROS. L929 cells were seeded in a six-well plate (5 × 10^5 cells/well) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Rosup reagent (1 μL/mL) was added to the wells and incubated for 7 h to induce ROS production. P-4HC-0, P-4HC-L, and P-4HC-H were soaked in DMEM medium containing 10 % FBS at 37 °C for 24 h in advance. The culture medium was removed from the cell culture wells, the aforementioned hydrogel extract was added, and incubated for 12 h. Cells treated solely with Rosup were used as the positive control, and cells receiving no treatment were regarded as the negative control. The medium was discarded and gently washed twice with PBS 7.4. Subsequently, serum-free DMEM containing DCFH-DA (1 μL/mL) was added. After incubating for 20 min, the cell fluorescence images were observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM).

Migration and tube formation assays

We used HUVECs to investigate the effect of P-4HC on angiogenesis. The migration of HUVECs was assessed through an in vitro scratch assay. HUVECs were seeded into a 12-well plate (2.5 × 10^5 cells/well) and cultured at 37 °C for 24 h. A p200 pipette tip was used to make a horizontal scratch and gently rinse with PBS (1 mL, pH = 7.4), thereby removing suspended cells and cell debris. P-4HC-0, P-4HC-L, and P-4HC-H were soaked in RPMI-1640 medium at 37 °C for 24 h in advance. The hydrogel extract was added to the above-mentioned wells, and cultured at 37 °C, and photographs were taken under a microscope at 0 h, 24 h, and 48 h. Image J software was employed to calculate the relative scratch area: relative scratch area (%) = (A#/A0) × 100 %. A# and A0 represent the scratch area after # hours of incubation and the initial scratch area, respectively.

The formation of tubules on HUVECs was evaluated using Matrigel to assess the effects of P-4HC. Different groups of hydrogels were soaked in RPMI-1640 medium at 37 °C for 24 h. Matrigel was added to the 12-well plate (1 mL/well) and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Then, HUVECs were seeded onto the Matrigel-coated 12-well plates (2.5 × 10^5 cells/well) and incubated for 24 h. Thereafter, the respective extracts were added to each group and incubation continued for another 24 h. Tube formation was observed and statistical analysis was performed using Image J software.

Immunofluorescence

Raw 264.7 cells were diluted to 2.5 × 10^5 cells/mL. The diluted cell suspension was inoculated into a 12-well plate with sterile coverslips at the bottom (1 mL/well) and incubated for 24 h. LPS (1 μg/mL) or IL-4 (40 ng/mL) were dissolved in serum-free DMEM. The cell culture medium was discarded in the above 12-well plate and gently washed twice with PBS. Then, LPS or IL-4 solution was added, and culture continued for 12 h to induce macrophage polarization to M1 or M2 phenotypes. LPS or IL-4 solution was aspirated and gently washed twice with PBS. P-4HC-0, P-4HC-L, and P-4HC-H extracts were added. The extract of the anhydrous gel was used for the control group, and the culture continued for 48 h. The culture medium was removed and washed with PBS three times. 4 % paraformaldehyde (500 μL/well) was added and fixed at room temperature for 20 min. Paraformaldehyde was removed and washed with PBS three times. The cells were treated with 0.1 % Triton X-100 for 20 min to enhance membrane permeability. 5 % bovine serum albumin (500 μL/well) was added and blocked at room temperature for 1.5 h. Bovine serum albumin was removed and washed three times with PBS. Primary antibody against CD206 was added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibody was discarded and washed three times with PBS. The secondary antibody was added and dark incubated at room temperature for 1.5 h. The secondary antibody was discarded and washed three times with PBS. DAPI was added to stain the nuclei, which was incubated at room temperature for 10 min. The sample was washed with PBS three times. A drop of glycerol solution (glycerol: water = 1:1) was dropped onto the slide. Then, the stained coverslip was transferred onto the slide, and sealed with clear nail polish. Finally, the fluorescence images were observed and recorded using CLSM.

Flow cytometry

Raw 264.7 cells were cultured in a 12-well plate for 24 h, LPS (1 μg/mL) was dissolved in serum-free DMEM, and Raw 264.7 was polarized to M1. The LPS solution was aspirated and gently washed twice with PBS. P-4HC-0, P-4HC-L, and P-4HC-H extracts were added. There was no hydrogel extract for the control group. Culture continued for 48 h. The culture medium was discarded, and 1 mL of PBS was added to each well. A pipette was used to detach the adherent cells and collect them in an EP tube. The tube was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and 500 μL of PBS and 10 μL of F4/80, CD86, and CD206 fluorescent antibodies were added and dark incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The cells were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the cells were resuspended in 500 μL of PBS. The cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD C6 Plus).

Western blotting

The cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 10^5 cells per well in a 6-well plate and incubated for 24 h. The culture medium was discarded. We added P-4HC-0, P-4HC-L, and P-4HC-H extracts that had been soaked in the medium for 24 h. The cells were cultured for 48 h. Then, the medium was discarded and the cells were collected. Animal skin tissue was prepared into a homogenate. RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used to extract proteins. The proteins were quantified using the BCA protein assay kit. 12.5 % or 10 % polyacrylamide gel was used to separate 30 μg of protein extract per well. The protein sample was then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane, blocked with skim milk at room temperature for 2 h, and incubated with the primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. The membrane was washed with TBST three times, incubated with the secondary antibody at room temperature for 45 min, and washed with TBST three times. The bands were observed using a chemiluminescent solution.

Wound healing experiments

db/db mice were randomly divided into 5 groups: diabetes model group, P-4HC-0 group, P-4HC-L group, P-4HC-H group, and positive drug group, with 8 mice in each group. An additional 8 wild-type C57BL/6 mice were used as the non-diabetic control group (wild mice). Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane. The back of mice was shaved, and disinfected with medical alcohol. Subsequently, a full-thickness circular wound with a diameter of 8 mm was established in the depilated area on the back of mice using a punch. P-4HC-0, P-4HC-L, P-4HC-H, and the positive drug (the trade name is Beifuxin, and the generic name is bovine basic fibroblast factor gel) were applied to the wound surface. The diabetic model group and the non-diabetic control group were treated with saline. Tegaderm transparent dressings were used as secondary dressings to prevent the hydrogel from falling off. On days 0, 3, 6, 9, and 12 after the injury, photos of the wound site were captured and Image J software was used to measure the wound area. Compared to the original wound, the percentage of the wound area on the indicated days compared to the initial area was calculated using the following formula: wound area# (%) = (Wound area#/Wound area0) × 100 %. Wound area# and wound area0 represent the wound area on day # and day 0, respectively.

Histopathological analysis of wound tissues

The wound and surrounding skin tissue samples were harvested on the 12th day after surgery. The tissue samples were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. The tissue samples were cut into 2-μm to 4-μm thick sections. Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining, Masson trichrome staining (MTS), and COL3A1 staining were conducted to observe the degree of wound healing and collagen deposition. Immunohistochemical staining for hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF-1α) was performed to determine the degree of tissue hypoxia. Immunohistochemical staining for VEGF and immunofluorescence assay for CD31 were conducted to determine the degree of neovascularization. Immunofluorescence assays for F4/80, CD86, and CD206 were conducted to measure the expression of macrophage marker proteins.

ELISA for cytokine detection

The wound skin tissue was obtained on the 12th day after surgery. Wound tissue proteins were isolated and quantitative analysis of the following proteins was performed using ELISA kits: (IL-1β (PI301), IL-6 (PI326), TNF-α (PT512), iNOS (S0025), MDA (S0131S), SOD (S0101S), IL-4 (PI612), IL-10 (PI522), and VEGF (PV957)).

Following the manufacturer's protocol, total RNA was isolated from samples, and cDNA was obtained through reverse transcription. Then, quantitative PCR detection. Transcriptomic analysis

Transcriptomic analysis was conducted to determine the transcriptional characteristics associated with diabetic chronic wounds treated with P-4HC and explore the potential mechanisms by which P-4HC promotes wound healing. We performed transcriptomic comparisons on the tissues of db/db mice 12 days after wound modeling. The criteria for screening differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were log2 fold change ≥2 and P < 0.05. Their relative abundances were plotted as heatmaps and volcano plots to simplify the visualization of DEGs. GO enrichment and KEGG signaling pathway enrichment analysis were conducted.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. We used GraphPad Prism 9 (Graph Pad Software, USA) for data analysis and visualization. ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, and ∗∗∗P < 0.001 indicated statistically significant difference.

Results

The representation of P-4HC and its biocompatibility and antioxidant properties

First, PLA was mixed with 4HC and electrospun into PLA/4HC fibers (Fig. 1A). The fibers were then homogenized into short fibers. Finally, the short fibers were dissolved in a hydrogel solution to prepare P-4HC-0, P-4HC-L, and P-4HC-H (Fig. 1C). The morphology of the fibers and P-4HC was observed by SEM. The fibers had a uniform diameter, no beads, and no adhesion (Fig. 1B). Each hydrogel had a three-dimensional network structure, and short fibers were interspersed in the mesh structure of the hydrogel (Fig. 1D). According to the SEM results, P-4HC had a porous network structure that allowed for adequate drug permeability. In Fig. 1E the release curves of 4HC in P-4HC-L and P-4HC-H are similar. About 70 % of 4HC can be released in the first 24 h, and then it is gradually and slowly released to achieve the effect of sustained drug release. P-4HC's swelling rate rose over time, and all of the hydrogels reached a swelling equilibrium condition after 24 h (Fig. 1F). They then maintained a steady swelling rate, demonstrating their exceptional stability. The P-4HC can offer a favorable milieu for wound healing because of its swelling capacity, which facilitated the absorption of exudate. The water absorption rate of P-4HC at swelling equilibrium reflects its maximum water absorption capacity (Fig. 1G). The water retention rate of P-4HC can reach about 95 %, which can prevent the overflow of exudate and thus facilitate wound healing (Fig. 1H).

P-4HC, as a wound dressing, possesses good biocompatibility, which is very important. We investigated P-4HC's blood compatibility in order to assess its biocompatibility. The supernatant was comparable to PBS, following collection of the supernatants for quantitative investigation, it was discovered that the hemolysis ratios at various hydrogels were less than 5 %, indicating good hemocompatibility as a wound dressing (Fig. 1I). We also employed the CCK-8 method to detect the survival rate of L929 cells, throughout the entire experimental period, cells grew and proliferated well (Fig. 1J). In the live/dead cell staining assay, Fig. 1K shows that the cells in each group were normal in morphology, and almost all were green live cells. Therefore, the biocompatibility of P-4HC was verified again. In Fig. 1L, after stimulating cells to produce a large amount of ROS, P-4HC significantly reduced the cell fluorescence intensity, with the P-4HC-H group showing the weakest fluorescence intensity, almost approaching the negative control group. The above findings indicate that P-4HC has good biocompatibility and antioxidant properties.

P-4HC promoted angiogenesis and M2 macrophage polarization

P-4HC exhibited good biocompatibility in HUVECs (Fig. 2A). Moreover, P-4HC promoted angiogenesis. The scratch assay in HUVECs revealed that the addition of P-4HC-H significantly accelerated the wound healing rate, with nearly complete healing at 48 h. This finding suggests that P-4HC can enhance the migration of HUVECs (Fig. 2B). In vitro tube formation assay with HUVECs revealed that the tubular network in the P-4HC-H group had the highest density, and the length of the tubes was longer than that in the other two groups (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2D–F). Western blotting also demonstrated that P-4HC promoted angiogenesis, and its effect increased with the increase in the concentration of 4HC (Fig. 2G). P-4HC also exhibited good compatibility with RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 2I). Subsequently, we measured the effect of P-4HC on macrophage polarization in RAW 264.7 cells using flow cytometry. After treatment with P-4HC-0, P-4HC-L, and P-4HC-H, the proportion of M1 macrophages (F4/80+CD86+) decreased, while the proportion of M2 macrophages (F4/80+CD206+) increased (Fig. 2J and K). Immunofluorescence staining revealed that P-4HC can increase the fluorescence intensity of CD206. We used LPS and IL-4 to polarize macrophages into M1 and M2. After LPS-induced polarization, the control group showed almost no fluorescence for the CD206 protein. The control group was then incubated with fresh DMEM (10 % FBS) for 72 h, the fluorescence intensity of the CD206 protein was very weak, and almost not detectable. However, after LPS-induced polarization and P-4HC treatment, the fluorescence intensity of the CD206 protein significantly increased, and P-4HC-H was significantly superior to P-4HC-L (p < 0.001). However, after IL-4-induced polarization, the control group cultured with fresh DMEM (10 % FBS) for 72 h showed a significant decrease in the fluorescence intensity of the CD206 protein (p < 0.001). However, when P-4HC was added to macrophages treated with IL-4, the fluorescence intensity of the CD206 protein changed little and remained strong (Fig. 2L–N). This finding indicates that P-4HC can maintain the M2 phenotype of macrophages. Moreover, Western blotting indicated that P-4HC can promote the transition of macrophages to M2 phenotype, with the P-4HC group showing high expression of M2 markers, CD206 and Arg-1 (Fig. 2O and P). Once again, these findings demonstrated that P-4HC promoted the transition of macrophages to the M2 phenotype, and the P-4HC-H group more effectively promoted and maintained this transition compared to the P-4HC-L group.

P-4HC enhanced the healing of chronic diabetic wounds

Diabetes chronic wound healing experiments were conducted on db/db mice. The mice were divided into diabetes, P-4HC-0, P-4HC-L, P-4HC-H, positive, and wild-type groups. Saline was administered to the wounds in the diabetes and wild-type groups. These groups served as the diabetes model group and the non-diabetes control group, respectively. Based on the wound photos and closure curves of different groups on days 3, 6, 9, and 12 (Fig. 3A and B), the wound area in other groups showed significant contraction throughout the wound regeneration phase compared to the diabetic model group. The wound area in the diabetes model group showed almost no reduction in the first 6 days, confirming the significant prolongation of the inflammatory phase in the diabetes model group. P-4HC increased the rate of wound healing and significantly decreased wound size compared to the diabetes model group. Moreover, the wound healing speed was accelerated in the P-4HC-H group, which was comparable to that of the positive group.

H&E staining revealed (Fig. 3 C) that newly formed hair follicles, squamous epithelium, and stratum corneum were clearly observed in the P-4HC-H group, and the repair effect was similar to that of the positive and wild-type groups. Collagen deposition is a key factor determining the appearance and strength of the skin; therefore, we employed Masson trichrome staining to measure the extent of collagen deposition in each group. There was a significant difference in terms of collagen deposition between the groups (Fig. 3 C). Collagen deposition in the P-4HC-H group was similar to that in the positive drug group. COL3A1 staining revealed that the amount of collagen in the P-4HC-H group was significantly higher than that in the diabetic model group, P-4HC-0 group, and P-4HC-L group (Fig. 3 D). P-4HC-H accelerated granulation tissue formation, re-epithelialization, and collagen deposition, enhancing chronic diabetic wound healing. At the same time, HIF-1a staining indicated that P-4HC-H improved hypoxia similar to the positive drug, and was superior to P-4HC-L and P-4HC-0 (Fig. 3 D).

In addition, VEGF and CD31 staining in the wound tissue on the 12th day after surgery (Fig. 3 D) revealed that the number of new blood vessels in each group was significantly different on the 12th day. The expression level of VEGF and the protein fluorescence intensity of CD31 in the P-4HC-H group were comparable to those in the positive drug group, suggesting that neovascularization in this group was stronger than that in the P-4HC-L, P-4HC-0, and model groups. Western blotting of skin tissues demonstrated that P-4HC-H more effectively promoted angiogenesis, antioxidant capacity, and collagen deposition (Fig. 3 J). Since new blood vessels provide nutrients and oxygen to metabolically active wounds, they can promote the formation of granulation tissue, thus P-4HC-H more effectively promotes the healing of chronic diabetic wounds.

The phenotype of macrophages in the wound tissue was analyzed based on immunofluorescence staining. The wound tissue in the P-4HC-H group contained a large number of M2 macrophages (F4/80+CD206+), while most macrophages in the model group had M1 (F4/80+CD86+) phenotype (Fig. 4A–D). Western blotting showed that P-4HC-H can upregulate M2 macrophage markers CD206 and Arg-1. In contrast, the diabetic model group expressed more M1 macrophage markers CD86 and iNOS (Fig. 4E and F).

We measured the levels of chemokines in the wound tissue through ELISA experiments to assess the effect of P-4HC on immune function (Fig. 4G–I). Compared to the diabetic model group, the P-4HC group showed reduced levels of pro-inflammatory chemokines IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α, lower levels of oxidative stress markers iNOS and MDA, and increased levels of SOD. Additionally, anti-inflammatory factors IL-4, IL-10, and VEGF were all upregulated. At the same time, the expression of inflammatory factors IL-6 and TNF-α was decreased in the wound tissue, while the expression of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 was increased (Fig. 4M and N). RT-qPCR demonstrated that P-4HC significantly reduced the expression of inflammatory factors. Compared to other groups, CD206 expression significantly increased in the P-4HC group while CD86 expression significantly decreased in this group (Fig. 4J–L). These results indicate that P-4HC possesses anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects and promotes M2 polarization of macrophages.

The above results show that the therapeutic effect of the P-4HC-H group is comparable to that of the positive drug group. However, the positive drug has the risk of allergic reaction and abnormal proliferation leading to scars and promoting tumors, and P-4HC has better stability, better transdermal permeability, a stronger sustained release effect, and a lower cost. It is safer and more economical while playing a role in multiple targets.

Transcriptomic analysis

We conducted transcriptomic analysis to analyze the mechanism by which P-4HC promotes M2 polarization of macrophages. The BP classification in the GO enrichment analysis indicated that DEPs are mainly involved in immune response, inflammatory response, and defense response (Fig. 5C,G). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis found that the TLR, IL-17, and TNF signaling pathways were downregulated in db/db mice treated with P-4HC. These pathways were more strongly enriched in the P-4HC-H group compared to the P-4HC-L group (Fig. 5D,H). Among these three pathways, the key genes TLR9, IL-17A, and TRAF1 were significantly down-regulated in the treatment group. Thus, P-4HC may promote the transition of macrophages from the M1 phenotype to the M2 phenotype and enhanced chronic wound healing in diabetes via the TLR/IL-17/TNF pathway. Subsequently, RT-qPCR and Western blotting revealed significant differences in the expression levels of proteins related to the aforementioned pathways in the wound tissue (Fig. 5I–K). The key proteins associated with these pathways, such as TLR9, IL-17A, IL-17RA, IL-17RC, and TRAF1, were significantly upregulated in the diabetic model control group compared to the P-4HC group. The expression of these proteins in the P-4HC-H group was significantly lower than that in the P-4HC-L and P-4HC-0 groups. This is consistent with the changes in the expression levels of TLR9, IL-17A, and TRAF1 in transcriptomic analysis.

Elucidating the mechanism by which P-4HC promotes diabetic chronic wound healing

In vivo validation experiments were conducted using the activators of TLR, IL-17, and TNF signaling pathways, namely ODN, SR, and RSQ, to verify the role of the aforementioned signaling pathways in P-4HC-mediated M2 polarization. Activation of TLR, IL-17, and TNF by ODN (5 mg/kg), SR (5 mg/kg), and RSQ (3 mg/kg) neutralized the effect of P-4HC (3 mg/mL) on diabetic chronic wound healing. For the healing of chronic diabetic wounds, the combination of P-4HC with each one of the activators (the P-4HC + ODN group, P-4HC + SR group, and P-4HC + RSQ group) decreased wound healing speed compared to the P-4HC group (Fig. 6A and B). In HE, Masson trichrome staining, and COL3A1 staining, the combination of P-4HC with each one of the activators lowered the number of new hair follicles and epidermal layers and decelerated granulation tissue formation and collagen deposition compared to the P-4HC group. However, these groups still exhibited better outcomes compared to the diabetic model group (Fig. 6C and D). At the same time, HIF-1a, VEGF, and CD31 staining (Fig. 6 D) showed that the combined use of P-4HC with ODN, SR, and RSQ neutralized the effects of P-4HC in improving hypoxia and promoting angiogenesis.

Discussion

Approximately 25 % of diabetes patients will develop diabetic foot ulcers (DFU), which impose a significant burden on patients and society [25,36]. Among patients with DFU, 68 % have a life expectancy of less than 5 years, and their mortality rate is higher than that of most patients with cancer [37,38]. DFU are one of the most common and difficult-to-treat complications of diabetes [39]. Although various types of wound dressings have been used for wound healing, they all have certain limitations. Electrospun nanofibers are promising biomaterials. The porous structure of nanofibers increases their specific surface area, allowing for liquid and gas exchange, which promotes wound healing [40,41]. Moreover, studies have shown that combining hydrogels with nanofibers to form new auxiliary materials can significantly promote wound healing [32,33]. Therefore, this study incorporated the traditional Chinese medicine monomer compound 4HC into the dressing. 4HC has antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, which can reduce IL-6 and TNF-α levels, and increase IL-10 levels, thereby alleviating inflammation [35]. However, the specific mechanisms by which dressings containing 4HC hydrogels treat chronic wound healing in diabetes have not yet been elucidated. Therefore, by measuring the markers of M1 and M2 macrophages and the changes in inflammatory cytokines, we explored the regulatory effect of P-4HC on macrophage polarization. Combined with transcriptome analysis, experimental studies helped identify the potential mechanisms by which P-4HC regulates macrophage polarization.

In the inflammatory phase, the plasticity of macrophages is crucial for wound healing and remodeling. Macrophages induce the inflammatory response and tissue damage in the early stages of inflammation, characterized by increased levels of inflammatory cytokines and pathogen-killing capabilities. In the late phase of inflammation, macrophages differentiate into the M2 phenotype and enter the proliferative phase, producing anti-inflammatory cytokines [42]. Different phenotypes of macrophages dominate at specific time points during wound healing. After skin injury, the macrophages infiltrating the wound initially polarize to the M1 phenotype, exhibiting pro-inflammatory characteristics. They can phagocytose all cellular fragments of microorganisms, matrix, and platelets, and secrete pro-inflammatory factors [43]. In the later stage, they polarize into M2 macrophages, secreting anti-inflammatory factors. They also release angiogenic factors, which promote the formation of new blood vessels and granulation tissue, and activate fibroblasts to promote collagen deposition and tissue repair, thereby facilitating wound healing [44,45]. Studies have shown that chronic inflammation can lead to the accumulation of ROS and inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6 and TNF-α [46]. A hyperglycemic environment upregulates pro-inflammatory cytokines, which in turn increases the polarization of M1 macrophages, forming a vicious cycle that hinders the transition of inflammation to proliferation [10]. Therefore, promoting the M2 polarization of macrophages is of great significance for regulating the immune microenvironment of diabetic wounds and promoting wound healing. Our study indicated that P-4HC reduces the proportion of M1 macrophages, enhances M2 macrophage polarization, and lowers the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in chronic diabetic wounds, thereby alleviating the inflammatory response and expediting chronic wound healing.

The molecular mechanisms by which P-4HC regulates macrophage polarization and promotes chronic wound healing in diabetes remain unclear. Therefore, we conducted a transcriptomic analysis, which indicated that P-4HC may affect macrophage polarization through the TLR/IL-17/TNF signaling pathway. TLRs are a family of signaling receptors on the cell membrane that play a crucial role in innate immunity, particularly in tissue inflammation [47]. TLR9 is a pro-inflammatory receptor that dose-dependently inhibits M2 polarization of macrophages in RAW264.7 cells. TLR9 deficiency suppresses inflammatory and fibrotic signaling pathways [48,49]. The IL-17 cytokine family includes six structurally related cytokines, IL-17A to IL-17F, five receptors (IL-17RA to E), and one cytoplasmic adapter (CIKS). CIKS contains various functional motifs, particularly in its long N-terminal region, including RNA-binding sites, tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor (TRAF) binding sites, and a U-box with TRAFs E3 ubiquitin ligase activity [50]. By inducing pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, IL-17A is associated with various diseases, including autoimmunity, inflammation, tumors, and infections [51]. Recently, researchers have reported the role of IL-17A in macrophage differentiation. For example, in a mouse model of chronic wound healing, IL-17A-deficient mice showed a significant increase in the abundance of M2 macrophages compared to wild-type mice. Antibody-mediated inhibition of IL-17A improved wound closure 14 days after wound healing, which was associated with an increased abundance of M2 macrophages [52]. Studies have also indicated that macrophages significantly overexpress IL-17RA and IL-17RC in the lesional skin of patients with inflammatory diseases [53]. IL-17RA pairs with IL-17RC to bind IL-17A as a covalent homodimer induction signal [54], suggesting that the pro-inflammatory response of IL-17A relies on IL-17RA and IL-17RC [55]. TRAF1 is associated with Th differentiation in the TNF signaling pathway [56]. Studies have shown that the absence of TRAF1 affects inflammatory cell infiltration, the expression of inflammatory cytokines, and inflammatory signaling pathways [57]. In the myocardium of the TRAF1-KO group, M1 markers and TNF-α were significantly downregulated, and M2 markers were significantly upregulated. In contrast, TRAF1 overexpression upregulated inflammatory factors, indicating that TRAF1 inhibits M2 polarization of macrophages [57]. Studies have shown that TLR9 activators can promote the production of IL-17A, and TLR9 knockout can significantly reduce the plasma levels of IL-17A. Blockade of the IL-17A pathway is beneficial for alleviating excessive inflammation, thereby mitigating organ damage [58]. In the TNF signaling pathway, TRAF1 can bind to IL-17A, forming a strong synergistic effect that amplifies the inflammatory response [59]. Therefore, reducing the expression of TLR9 can inhibit the expression of IL-17A and TRAF1, promote M2 polarization of macrophages, reduce the expression of the inflammatory factor TNF-α, and ameliorate tissue damage. In this study, RT-qPCR and Western blotting showed that P-4HC reduced the expression of TLR9, IL-17A, IL-17RA, IL-17RC, TRAF1, CD86, and iNOS in the wound tissue of db/db mice, while increasing the expression of CD206 and Arg-1. In RAW264.7 cells, activation of either TLR, IL-17, or TNF signaling pathway attenuated the therapeutic effects of P-4HC. Notably, TLR activation upregulated the expression of proteins related to both IL-17 and TNF pathways, while IL-17 activation enhanced the expression of TNF pathway-related proteins. These findings indicate that P-4HC exerts its effects by inhibiting the TLR/IL-17/TNF signaling pathway. These results strongly suggest that P-4HC enhances the M2 polarization of macrophages by inhibiting the TLR/IL-17/TNF signaling pathway, highlighting the potential mechanisms by which P-4HC regulates the inflammatory response in chronic diabetic wounds.

Conclusion

In summary, our P-4HC hydrogel targets TLR9, IL-17A, and TRAF1 to suppress the TLR/IL-17/TNF signaling pathway, thereby shifting macrophage polarization from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype (Fig. 9). This phenotypic shift facilitates the transition from the inflammatory phase to the proliferative and remodeling phases, finally enhancing the healing of chronic diabetic wounds. This study not only presents a novel wound dressing that significantly improves diabetic wound healing but also provides new possibilities for developing effective and safe wound management strategies.
