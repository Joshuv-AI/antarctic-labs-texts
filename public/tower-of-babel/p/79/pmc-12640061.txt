Plasma Cell–Directed Rescue With Bortezomib in Refractory Antiphospholipase A2 Receptor–Positive Membranous Nephropathy
Authors: Fatima Ayub, John Arthur, Md Rajibul Hasan, Luis A. Juncos, Joseph Hunter Holthoff
Published: 2025

Introduction

Membranous nephropathy (MN) is a leading cause of nephrotic syndrome in adults, driven by autoantibodies against glomerular antigens. In primary MN (PMN), antiphospholipase A2 receptor (PLA2R) antibodies are implicated in 50% to 80% of cases.1 Although most patients respond to immunosuppressive therapy, 30% remain resistant to rituximab, cyclophosphamide, or calcineurin inhibitors. In these refractory cases, persistent antibody production may arise from long-lived plasma cells, providing rationale for plasma cell–directed therapies such as bortezomib, a proteasome inhibitor used in multiple myeloma.2

We describe a case of refractory anti-PLA2R–positive MN with a rapid response to bortezomib. To our knowledge, this is the third reported case of nontransplant PLA2R-positive MN treated with bortezomib,3, 4, 5 highlighting its potential role in resistant disease. Case Presentation

A 44-year-old male with newly diagnosed essential hypertension presented to the emergency department with progressive shortness of breath and anasarca over 3 weeks. On presentation, his blood pressure was 161/121 mm Hg with a heart rate of 105 beats/min, bodyweight increased from 151 lbs to 184 lbs and physical examination revealed generalized edema. Laboratory workup showed a serum creatinine of 2.5 mg/dl (baseline 1.2 mg/dl), serum albumin of 0.9 g/dl, and a urine protein-to-creatinine ratio of 12.9 g/g (15 g on 24-hour urine collection). His hemoglobin was 9 g/dl with a low serum transferrin, suggesting iron deficiency anemia as the most likely explanation for his shortness of breath. He also had severe hyperlipidemia with serum cholesterol of 442 mg/dl. Renal ultrasound and chest x-ray were unremarkable.

The anti-PLA2R antibody titers measured by indirect immunofluorescence were 1:2560 IU, supporting the diagnosis of PMN. Renal biopsy confirmed PLA2R-positive MN, with a small focal cellular crescent in 1 of 33 glomeruli on light microscopy, subepithelial deposits on electron microscopy, and positive PLA2R staining on immunofluorescence in the glomeruli. Autoimmune workup, including antineutrophil cytoplasmic autoantibody antibodies was negative.

Due to high-grade proteinuria, elevated anti-PLA2R titers, and worsening renal function (estimated glomerular filtration rate decreased from a baseline of 76 ml/min per 1.73 m2 to 30 ml/min per 1.73 m2), the patient met the criteria for high-risk MN and was started on immunosuppressive therapy with rituximab in addition to losartan. He received 2 doses of i.v. 1 g rituximab infusion, 2 months apart, initially resulting in a slight decline in proteinuria (from 15 to 10 g/d) and anti-PLA2R titer (1:2560 to 1:1280 IU). However, within 8 weeks, both proteinuria and antibody levels rebounded, and renal function continued to deteriorate with estimated glomerular filtration rate at 15 ml/min per 1.73 m2. Despite complete peripheral B-cell depletion (confirmed by undetectable CD19+ cells on flow cytometry), the patient showed no clinical improvement. Given the well-recognized delay of several weeks to months between B-cell depletion and a corresponding decline in anti-PLA2R antibody levels, we opted to continue conservative management for 6 months following the last dose of rituximab.

After extensively discussing alternative therapies, including cyclophosphamide, calcineurin inhibitors, or repeat rituximab, the patient expressed hesitation because of previous treatment failure. Given the lack of response despite complete B-cell depletion, we hypothesized that long-lived plasma cells could be the source of persistent autoantibody production. Based on this hypothesis, we initiated plasma cell–targeted therapy with bortezomib.

Following a shared decision-making process, the patient was started on weekly 1.3 mg/m2 of i.v. bortezomib along with dexamethasone. Remarkably, anti-PLA2R titers declined from 1:2560 to 1:1280 IU after the first dose and proteinuria declined to 4 g/d within 2 weeks. Serum anti-PLA2R titer declined further to 1:640 IU after the second dose and subsequently to 1:320 IU. In addition, his serum albumin improved to 2.1 g/dl with estimated glomerular filtration rate improvement to 23 ml/min per 1.73 m2. He was initially scheduled to receive 4 weekly doses of bortezomib; however, he developed nausea and vomiting before his third dose and declined further treatments. Despite not receiving standard or scheduled dosing of bortezomib, the patient exhibited a significant and rapid clinical response, highlighting the potential efficacy of this off-label therapeutic approach in treatment-refractory PMN (Figure 1). Discussion

PMN is an autoimmune glomerular disease and significant progress has been made in the understanding of its pathogenesis, with anti-PLA2R antibodies implicated in approximately 80% of cases.1 Rituximab, an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, achieves remission in 60% to 80% of cases, yet 30% to 40% remain resistant or relapse despite complete B-cell depletion.6

In cases of treatment refractory PMN, long-lived plasma cells, unaffected by CD20-targeting, are implicated in persistent antibody production. This supports the rationale for proteasome inhibition with bortezomib, which has been applied in transplant and autoimmune settings.7 In non-myeloma patients, its side effect profile is similar to that observed in oncology settings, with peripheral neuropathy being the most common dose-limiting toxicity. Gastrointestinal symptoms (e.g., nausea and diarrhea), cytopenia, and herpes zoster reactivation are also frequently reported. Although serious infections and cardiopulmonary events are less common, they may occur, especially in patients with multiple comorbidities. Despite these risks, short courses in nonmalignant conditions are generally well-tolerated.7 Felzartamab, an anti-CD38 monoclonal antibody, is another plasma cell–directed agent under investigation. In the recent phase 1b/2a M-PLACE trial, felzartamab induced rapid anti-PLA2R antibody reductions in refractory PMN, underscoring the therapeutic promise of plasma cell–targeted approaches.8

To date, only 2 cases3, 4, 5 have been published describing the successful use of bortezomib in refractory PLA2R-positive PMN, each demonstrating meaningful immunologic and clinical improvement (Supplementary Table S1). Although Hartono et al.4 reported a case of favorable response to treatment with bortezomib, that patient did not undergo serological testing for anti-PLA2R antibodies and did not fail conventional therapies before initiation of bortezomib, which limits extrapolation to our case. In the cases by Geara et al.5 and Salhi et al.,3 patients with refractory PMN achieved clinical and immunologic remission following treatment with several cycles of bortezomib.

Our patient exhibited a similar clinical trajectory. Despite achieving complete B-cell depletion with rituximab, he had persistently high anti-PLA2R titers and severe nephrotic-range proteinuria. Upon initiation of bortezomib, his anti-PLA2R titers decreased from 1:2560 to 1:320 IU and his proteinuria decreased from 15 g/d to 4 g/d within 2 weeks. This robust response aligns with the plasma cell–driven pathophysiologic model proposed in previous cases.

What distinguishes our case from previously reported cases is the unusually rapid and robust response to a short, nonstandard course of bortezomib, with anti-PLA2R titers and proteinuria improving within 2 weeks, much faster than previously reported experiences. The biopsy showed minimal interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy and a small focal crescent, a rare but recognized entity in PMN,9 further emphasizing its uniqueness and suggesting that plasma cell–directed therapy may offer benefit in aggressive, treatment-refractory PMN.

This case report is inherently limited by its short-term follow-up, single-patient design and observational nature. We did not measure residual rituximab level at 6 months or direct plasma cell populations. In addition, long-term follow-up is needed to determine the durability of remission and assess the safety profile as well as potential toxicities associated with bortezomib in non-myeloma patients.

Prospective studies are essential to define the role of plasma cell–targeted therapies in refractory PLA2R-positive PMN. Until then, treatment should be guided by personalized risk-benefit assessment, ensuring that novel therapies are reserved for select high-risk patients.
