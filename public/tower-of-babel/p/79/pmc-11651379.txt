Unveiling variability: A systematic review of reproducibility in visual EEG analysis, with focus on seizures
Authors: Eivind Aanestad, Sándor Beniczky, Henning Olberg, Jan Brogger
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveReproducibility is key for diagnostic tests involving subjective evaluation by experts. Our aim was to systematically review the reproducibility of visual analysis in clinical electroencephalogram (EEG). In this paper, we give data on the scope of EEG features found, and detailed reproducibility data for the most studied feature.MethodsWe searched four databases for articles reporting reproducibility in clinical EEG, until June 2023. Two raters screened 24 553 citations, and then 2736 full texts. Quality was assessed according to the GRRAS guidelines.ResultsWe found 275 studies (268 interrater and 20 intrarater), addressing 606 different EEG features. Only 38 EEG features had been studied in >2 studies. Most studies had <50 patients and EEGs. The most often addressed feature was seizure detection (62 papers). Interrater reproducibility of seizure detection was substantial‐to‐almost‐perfect with experienced raters and raw EEG (kappa .62–.88). With experienced raters and transformed EEG, reproducibility was substantial (kappa .63–.70). Inexperienced raters had lower reproducibility. Seizure lateralization reproducibility was moderate to substantial (kappa .58–.77) but lower than for seizure detection.SignificanceMost EEG reproducibility studies are done only once. Intrarater studies are rare. The reproducibility of visual EEG analysis is variable. Interrater reproducibility for seizure detection is substantial‐to‐perfect with experienced raters and raw EEG, less with inexperienced raters or transformed EEG.The results of visual EEG analysis vary within the same rater, and between raters. There is a need for larger collaborative studies, using improved methodology, as well as more intrarater studies of EEG interpretation.Keywords: EEG, interrater variability, intrarater variability, seizure detection, systematic review

INTRODUCTION

Diagnostic tests in medicine are most useful when they provide certain combinations of sensitivity, specificity, and reproducibility. Electroencephalogram (EEG) is a useful diagnostic test in the management of many brain disorders. 1 Clinical EEG today is interpreted 2 using a nomenclature developed and refined in more than 80 years since its introduction. 3 The interrater and intrarater reproducibility of clinical EEG interpretation has been studied since 1954, 4 but variation continues to have a clinical impact. 5 Clinical focus on interrater EEG interpretation variability is not always perceived positively. 6 Artificial intelligence (AI) applications are likely to see increased use in medicine and clinical EEG,7, 8 but results are often not fully explainable, may be brittle, and are effectively a synthetic average of the raters included in the dataset. 6 Variability in diagnostic interpretation is important in other medical fields such as radiology, with radiology having more advanced research designs and more data than clinical neurophysiology. 9

Systematic reviews are the gold standard for summarizing evidence. 10 Systematic reviews are infrequent in clinical neurophysiology. A systematic review has been performed regarding the validity of aEEG for neonatal seizures, 11 and methods for reactivity testing in coma. 12 We have not been able to identify any systematic reviews on the reproducibility of visual analysis of clinical EEG.

Our goal was to perform a systematic review of the reproducibility of visual interpretation of EEG, and in this paper to focus on what EEG features have been studied and give data on the most commonly studied EEG feature, which turned out to be seizure detection. In addition, we present the second‐most data‐rich seizure feature which was seizure lateralization. There was more data than can be presented in a single paper. More data on other EEG features with less data available will be presented in the future.

METHODS

A protocol for this systematic review was developed and registered in PROSPERO (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/display_record.php?RecordID=99318) (ID 99318), a prospective registry for systematic reviews. 13 A librarian assisted in developing search strings with a sensitivity of about 85% for known studies.

Selection criteria

Selection criteria: papers reporting interrater or intrarater reproducibility (agreement/reliability) data on the same human EEG, scored two or more times by one or more human raters with the same modality using a terminology recognizable to a trained EEG reviewer.

Literature search

On June 22, 2023 we searched Pubmed, EMBASE, PsycInfo, and the Cochrane database. The search strings are listed in Table S1. References from the author's own library were included. References were automatically and manually de‐duplicated in EndNote and Rayyan.

Screening

Two raters (EA and JB) independently screened the resulting abstracts in the free online tool Rayyan. 14 Abstract assessment conflicts between the two raters (EA and JB) were resolved by consensus. Full‐text was retrieved and scored again by the two raters (EA and JB). Fulltext assessment conflicts between the two raters were resolved by consensus. Citation tracking was performed. For these included references at this stage, their citation lists, and references citing them were obtained from the Web of Knowledge. These were obtained in full text and screened by one rater. Selection decisions were confirmed by a second rater.

Level of experience.

Self‐reported level of experience per paper was abstracted as expert if the paper referred to the EEG reviewers as expert, experienced if the paper referred to the reviewers as experienced or otherwise suggested experience. For the EEG feature of seizure detection, a more thorough approach was required to illuminate variation. For this feature, experience level was annotated for each reproducibility data point, based on a manual assessment by author JB. Nurses, junior doctors, and others with no or minimal training were assessed as inexperienced, and specialists or self‐reported experts were assessed as experienced. Inexperienced and experienced groups mixed together were considered inexperienced. Author clarifications attempted are listed in Table S1.

EEG features were given the same name if they concerned the same phenomenon and had the same categories. EEG features were further categorized into pre‐defined domains, such as normal variants, global abnormality, EEG in coma, interictal abnormalities, and seizures. Reproducibility metrics were classified into preselected categories as agreement without adjusting for chance, Cohen's kappa for two raters, Fleiss kappa for multiple raters, intraclass correlation coefficients, Gwet's AC1, and others. Chance‐adjusted kappa‐type statistics were re‐scaled from (zero, one) to (zero, hundred) for comparison with unadjusted agreement.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Stata version 17.0. Descriptive statistics per paper and per EEG feature were computed.

For a detailed analysis of seizure detection presence/absence, the following analysis was performed. Quantitative meta‐analysis with effect size estimation was aborted due to substantial heterogeneity. Instead, median agreement statistics were computed for age group, statistic type (kappa‐type or plain agreement), type of EEG (transformed or raw), and experience level. Data points counted as experienced raters involved only experienced or expert raters, while data points involving only inexperienced raters, or a mixture of experienced and inexperienced raters were counted as inexperienced in the interest of data simplification.

Study characteristics

The typical reproducibility study of clinical EEG has about 40 patients and 50 EEGs (Table 1). The vast majority were interrater studies only, with typically three raters. There were only 20 intrarater studies. Most studies concerned epilepsy (36%), coma (24%), and neonates (19%). The typical study had 16 EEG electrodes and used short clips or standard EEG. A third of the papers did not specify the duration of EEG and number of electrodes.

The length of EEG bears particular consideration. The length of EEG studied for reproducibility varied by orders of magnitude—between .02 and 8460 min. Typically, studies with long segments had fewer raters than studies with short segments. A number of these studies recruited a large number of reviewers to assess a single page or less of EEG. It is not always evident whether the reproducibility of a short segment of EEG can be transferred to the assessment of a whole clinical EEG.

About half of the studies were performed in the United States, while only 5% of studies were multinational; 97% of studies were found in the Pubmed database; and 72% of studies were found in the initial database search, while the rest were found in citation tracking.

The rater experience was described in many different ways. An assessment of rater experience level as experienced or expert could not be made in half of the studies, and rater experience in years was missing in 90% of studies. Patient recruitment was consecutive in about half of the studies, and convenience or a synthetic mix or missing in half, showing some potential for selection bias.

Seizure detection

More data were found than expected. The most data‐rich EEG feature was the presence or absence of seizures, which is presented here. There were 55 papers with 186 interrater or intrarater data points on seizure detection. Seven papers with only aggregate values were excluded. An evidence table with some detailed characteristics of each study is given in Table S4. A post hoc analysis of median reproducibility by display modality, experience, and agreement statistic showed that for experienced raters, reproducibility was high for raw EEG analysis and moderate for transformed EEG (Table 4). Inexperienced raters had poorer reproducibility than experienced raters and worse performance on transformed EEG. Experienced raters analyzing raw EEG have substantial to almost perfect kappa statistics of .62–.88. Experienced raters analyzing transformed EEG had substantial kappa of .63–.70. Inexperienced raters using transformed EEG had a fair agreement for neonates (.36) and substantial for pediatric and adults (.62–.7). Inexperienced raters were not studied for seizure detection using raw EEG in neonates or pediatric age groups, but in adults kappa was only fair (.31). Intrarater seizure detection data with confidence interval was only available for neonatal raw EEG with experienced raters from one study, and was almost perfect.

DISCUSSION

We have shown that most reproducibility studies in clinical EEG are small, of moderate quality, and there are few intrarater studies. Missing data on important covariates was common. Some allowance must be given to the coding practice of the authors working with variable descriptions.

Even though a pilot study was performed, this review turned out to have a wide scope with more reproducibility data about EEG features than can be published in a single paper. The large variety of different EEG features studied probably reflects the limited standardization of EEG reporting. It would probably be helpful if future studies were more focused. The International Federation for Clinical Neurophysiology‐endorsed SCORE standard 24 has not yet seen widespread adoption, but the use of even parts of the SCORE nomenclature could reduce the explosion of possibilities when considering how to name, frame, and analyze an EEG phenomenon. Two studies included here are SCORE‐based.8, 25 The substantial effort over time by the American Clinical Neurophysiology Society in developing a standardized critical care EEG terminology is to be commended and emulated, 26 as it has given a large body of reproducibility data for analysis in a later paper.27, 28, 29

EEG names are often written in shorthand, leaving some doubt as to what the exact EEG phenomenon is. A number of studies use “slowing,” with some uncertainty as to whether this is focal and or generalized.30, 31, 32 Time can also interfere with understanding. Volavka's 1973 paper stressing a “focus” (German: “einen Herd”) probably means an interictal epileptiform focus, but we cannot be sure in hindsight 50 years later. 33 It is also not entirely clear whether “paroxysmal abnormalities” are in fact interictal epileptiform discharges.33, 34, 35, 36 This may be an example of “EEG dialect,” where an informal EEG feature name seems to acquire common currency. A clear description of the EEG feature under investigation would make future papers more useful. We suggest that future studies provide at least two raw EEG examples of the EEG feature under study and two raw EEG samples that are not the EEG feature under study. This could perhaps be tagged with the new HED‐SCORE terminology 37 in the EEG‐BIDS data format 38 or EEG‐DICOM data format 39 as a data supplement.

Recruitment was consecutive in a major fraction of studies, though not always prospective. However, the majority of studies were either convenience samples, or synthetic mixes, or did not give enough information about recruitment to assess. There is therefore a potential for selection bias. Future high‐quality studies should strive to encompass a wide stream of clinical EEGs with limited pre‐selection.

An analysis of the most data‐rich EEG feature of seizure presence or absence showed substantial variation. The substantial variation in reproducibility and missing covariates makes a quantitative summary only tentative. Broad summary statistics indicated that experienced raters using raw EEG had substantial reproducibility for seizure detection (median kappa .62–.88), and this was slightly lower for transformed EEG (median kappa .63–.70). Reproducibility was not lower for neonatal EEG with experienced raters when comparing transformed and raw EEG. Results involving inexperienced raters and transformed EEG had similar reproducibility as experienced raters (pediatric and adult .62–.7), except for neonatal seizure detection (kappa .36). Analysis of seizure detection results by experience level was hampered by the limited description of experience in the papers.

Seizure lateralization mostly in presurgical patients with epilepsy had moderate reproducibility, less than for seizure detection, and had the same wide variation. It is unexplained why some rater pairs have as low a reproducibility as a kappa of .18. There was no data on seizure lateralization in critically ill patients.

It is intriguing that intrarater and interrater reproducibility for seizure detection are close in value in the one study that analyzed both. The clinical role of intrarater variability in EEG reporting is likely an understudied and important factor.

There appear to be no easy solutions to reviewer variability except training for the individual, and more research for the EEG field in general. Assessment of EEG reviewer confidence has shown that most EEGers are very confident even in the face of substantial variation. 40 Overconfidence is a common cause of diagnostic error. 41 Breakthroughs in AI and EEG are likely in the future.

The problem of inter‐ and intrarater variability significantly affects medical diagnostic AI. 42 AI‐based solutions provide an average of the several raters it is trained with, and is limited by intra‐ and interrater variability. Proposed solutions are to increase the expertise of graders, increase the number of graders, and have an unbiased disagreement resolution process. More insight into factors that affect reproducibility in EEG could enable higher‐quality diagnosis, with AI or without AI.

Perhaps most promising for future studies is the study of “calibration curves” between multiple raters. 43 For some EEG features, a substantial portion of variation between raters appears to be due to a difference in the sensitivity to call pathology (“undercalling”/“overcalling”). Future studies would benefit also from studying reproducibility and validity together. In daily clinical practice, it is likely that borderline or weak EEG findings contribute most to EEG variation. Reproducibility can likely be improved by adjusting diagnostic thresholds in the direction of clear pathology and increased specificity, but possibly at the expense of sensitivity.

CONCLUSIONS

The reproducibility of visual EEG analysis is variable. Most EEG reproducibility studies are not repeated. Only a limited set of EEG features have reproducibility studies. Incomplete study descriptions are common. Selection bias is possible. Future studies of EEG reproducibility should follow the GRRAS guidelines, or similar. There is a need for larger collaborative studies with well‐characterized EEG features, raters, and patients, as well as more intrarater studies of EEG interpretation. Inspiration should be taken from the established method for diagnostic test evaluation in radiology. 9

The interrater reproducibility of seizure detection is substantial to almost perfect with experienced raters and raw EEG (kappa .62–.88). With experienced raters and transformed EEG, reproducibility is substantial (kappa .63–.70). Inexperienced raters have lower reproducibility. Intrarater and interrater reproducibility were close in the one study that reported both. Seizure lateralization has moderate reproducibility.

For the clinical EEG reader, the take‐home message is that experience in the interpretation of EEG matters: there is clinically important variation between readers and likely within the same reader, which probably lessens with experience. Reproducibility can likely be improved by being conservative in calling pathology, but possibly at the expense of some sensitivity.
