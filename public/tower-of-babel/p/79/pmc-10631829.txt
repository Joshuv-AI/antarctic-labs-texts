Effects of remdesivir on SARS-CoV-2 viral dynamics and mortality in viraemic patients hospitalized for COVID-19
Authors: Karl Hagman, Magnus Hedenstierna, Jacob Widaeus, Emelie Arvidsson, Berit Hammas, Lena Grillner, Jan Jakobsson, Patrik Gille-Johnson, Johan Ursing
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStudies on the antiviral effects of remdesivir have shown conflicting results. SARS-CoV-2 viraemia could identify patients in whom antiviral treatment may be particularly beneficial.ObjectivesTo investigate antiviral effects and clinical outcomes of remdesivir treatment in viraemic patients.MethodsViraemic patients hospitalized for COVID-19 with ratio of arterial oxygen partial pressure to fractional inspired oxygen of ≤300, symptom duration ≤10 days, and estimated glomerular filtration rate ≥30 mL/min were included in a cohort. The rate of serum viral clearance and serum viral load decline, 60 day mortality and in-hospital outcomes were estimated. A subgroup analysis including patients with symptom duration ≤7 days was performed.ResultsA total of 318 viraemic patients were included. Thirty-three percent (105/318) received remdesivir. The rate of serum viral clearance [subhazard risk ratio (SHR) 1.4 (95% CI 0.9–2.0), P = 0.11] and serum viral load decline (P = 0.11) were not significantly different between remdesivir-treated patients and controls. However, the rate of serum viral clearance was non-significantly higher [SHR 1.6 (95% CI 1.0–2.7), P = 0.051] and the viral load decline was faster (P = 0.03) in remdesivir-treated patients with symptom duration ≤7 days at admission. The 60 day mortality [HR 1.0 (95% CI 0.6–1.8), P = 0.97] and adverse in-hospital outcomes [OR 1.4 (95% CI 0.8–2.4), P = 0.31] were not significantly different between remdesivir-treated patients and controls.ConclusionsRemdesivir treatment did not significantly change the duration of SARS-CoV-2 viraemia, decline of serum viral load, 60 day mortality or in-hospital adverse outcomes in patients with ≤10 days of symptoms at admission. Remdesivir appeared to reduce the duration of viraemia in a subgroup of patients with ≤7 days of symptoms at admission.

Introduction

Remdesivir is a prodrug for an adenosine nucleotide analogue that terminates the RNA synthesis of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2).1 Remdesivir treatment reduced time to clinical improvement, and lowered mortality in patients hospitalized with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) but was not beneficial for patients needing mechanical ventilation.2,3 Remdesivir also reduced the risk of hospitalization.4 Treatment was most effective when used early after symptom onset when viral replication is high and in patients with high nasopharyngeal viral loads.3–6

Several studies have explored the antiviral effects of remdesivir in vivo, with conflicting results.4,7–15 Most of the larger, randomized controlled trials reported no effect on upper respiratory tract viral load.4,7,9,10 However, a post hoc modelling of data in one study showed a modest effect on time to upper respiratory tract viral clearance when time from symptom onset to treatment was considered.13 The effect was more pronounced in patients with initially high upper respiratory tract viral loads.

Detectable SARS-CoV-2 RNA in blood (henceforth called viraemia) was associated with both high upper and lower respiratory tract viral load and may indicate poor viral control.16–18 Viraemia was also strongly associated with mortality.19–21 We hypothesized that viraemia at admission indicated ongoing viral replication and thus identified a group of patients in whom antiviral treatment could be particularly beneficial. The primary aim of this cohort study was to investigate if treatment with remdesivir shortened the time to serum viral clearance. Secondary aims included if remdesivir reduced serum viral load and if there was an effect on all-cause 60 day mortality and adverse in-hospital outcomes in a group of viraemic patients.

Patients and methods

Study design, setting and population

This cohort study included patients hospitalized for COVID-19 at Danderyd Hospital, Stockholm, Sweden between 23 March 2020 and 20 April 2021. Inclusion criteria were: (i) a serum sample positive for SARS-CoV-2 RNA with RT–PCR within −1 to 3 days of admission; (ii) symptom duration ≤10 days; (iii) estimated ratio of arterial oxygen partial pressure to fractional inspired oxygen (PaO2/FiO2 ratio) ≤ 300 (corresponding to approximately ≤91% oxygen saturation in room air); and (iv) estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) ≥ 30 mL/min at admission. Exclusion criteria were: substantial missing data; transfer from another hospital; and COVID-19 developed whilst admitted. Patients were divided into a treatment group (defined as receiving at least one dose of remdesivir whilst hospitalized) and a control group that did not receive remdesivir.

The ancestral strain of SARS-CoV-2 dominated until 20 February 2021, whereafter the Alpha Variant of Concern dominated. Corticosteroids were given routinely to patients with respiratory failure from 18 June 2020. Remdesivir was accessible for treatment from 29 June 2020. The availability of remdesivir was limited throughout the study. The inclusion criteria of this study matched the local criteria for remdesivir treatment during the study period. Randomized studies on convalescent plasma treatment and inhaled nitric oxide were performed at the hospital during the study period. Convalescent plasma was also frequently prescribed to viraemic patients admitted to intensive care. Patients receiving convalescent plasma were given one unit (approximately 200 mL) of high-titre plasma daily until they were no longer viraemic. No other investigational medicinal products were used.

Data collection

The study was performed with a dataset containing baseline characteristics, virological and outcome data, extracted by reviewing electronical medical records and laboratory data. The electronical medical records were automatically linked to the Swedish Population Register, enabling correct data on all-cause 60 day mortality. The PaO2/FiO2 ratio was estimated from the peripheral oxygen saturation and the simultaneous level of oxygen treatment at admission. Immunocompromisation was defined as intake of the equivalent of ≥10 mg prednisolone for more than 4 weeks before admission, ongoing chemotherapy and treatment with rituximab, infliximab, adalimumab or similar profoundly immunosuppressive medication.

RT–PCR analyses

Serum samples for the detection of SARS-CoV-2 RNA with RT–PCR were drawn sporadically at the beginning of the study period but drawn routinely during hospitalization from 10 May 2020. They were analysed at Karolinska University Laboratory using either the cobas® 6800 instrument (Roche Molecular Diagnostics, Pleasanton, CA, USA) or an in-house assay described elsewhere.16 The cobas® and in-house RT–PCR have been shown to have similar performance.19 Amplification of at least one gene target was considered a positive test. RT–PCR cycle threshold (CT) values were used as an indicator of viral load with lower CT values indicating a higher viral load.

Statistics

Continuous variables are presented as median (IQR) and categorical variables as number (percentage). Differences in groups were analysed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 15.1 (StataCorp, TX, USA). A P value of <0.05 was considered significant.

Duration of viraemia

Observed serum viral clearance was defined as a RT–PCR-negative serum sample with no positive samples drawn after. The duration of viraemia was defined as the number of days from admission to the day after the last positive serum sample in patients with observed serum viral clearance.

A Fine and Gray competing risk regression was fitted to estimate the rate of serum viral clearance. In-hospital mortality before observed serum viral clearance was treated as a competing risk. Data were censored at discharge if the patient was discharged before serum viral clearance. Covariates [age, sex, immunocompromisation, Charlson comorbidity index, symptom duration, disease severity (estimated PaO2/FiO2), C-reactive protein, lymphocyte count, corticosteroid treatment for COVID-19, and convalescent plasma treatment] according to prior knowledge and a directed acyclical graph were included in a multivariate model. Results are presented as subhazard risk ratio (SHR) with 95% CI and plotted as a cumulative incidence function. A subgroup analysis was performed including only patients with symptom duration of ≤7 days at admission. Sensitivity analyses were performed with time for serum viral clearance defined as the day of the first negative sample with no more positive samples afterwards and excluding immunocompromised patients.

Viral load decline

Median CT values for E-gene targets from both RT–PCR assays were plotted each day after admission. To compare the slopes of serum viral load decline for remdesivir-treated patients and controls an adjusted linear mixed-effects model was fitted allowing individual intercepts for different patients. RT–PCR-negative serum samples between two positive samples were manually excluded for this analysis.

60 Day mortality and in-hospital outcome

Analysis of all-cause 60 day mortality and adverse in-hospital outcome was restricted to patients included after corticosteroid treatment became routine practice following the publication of the RECOVERY study.22

A Kaplan–Meier survival curve was constructed, and a Cox proportional hazards regression fitted, to estimate the risk of 60 day mortality for remdesivir-treated patients and controls. The log-rank test was used to assess differences in the Kaplan–Meier curve. Covariates were included in the multivariable Cox regression in line with the main analysis. Results are presented as adjusted HRs with 95% CI. The assumption of proportional hazards was tested graphically by comparing curves on the log-log plot of survival. Supplementary analyses of 7 and 28 day mortality were also performed.

In-hospital outcome was described using the highest reached level on the WHO ordinal scale of outcome: 3 = hospitalized, no oxygen therapy; 4 = oxygen therapy; 5 = non-invasive ventilation (NIV) or high-flow oxygen therapy (HFO); 6 = mechanical ventilation; 7 = mechanical ventilation and organ support; 8 = death. An adjusted proportional odds model was fitted to assess the odds of reaching a higher level on the WHO ordinal scale. The assumption of proportional odds was tested with the Lipsitz, Hosmer–Lemeshow and Pulkstenis–Robinson tests. Results are presented as OR with 95% CI.

Ethics

The Swedish Ethical Review Authority approved of the study (registration numbers 2020-01770 and 2020-06337). Informed consent was waived.

Results

Study population

A total of 318 viraemic patients were included, of whom 33% (105/318) received at least one dose of remdesivir (Figure 1). Remdesivir was not administered to 67% (213/318), of whom 47 patients were admitted before remdesivir was available. SARS-CoV-2 RNA was found in 71% (767/1087) of collected serum samples. Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. The median age was 65 (IQR 54–76) years and 65% (206/318) were male. Most baseline characteristics were balanced between remdesivir-treated patients and controls. However, remdesivir-treated patients were more frequently immunocompromised (12% versus 6%, P = 0.05), had a lower baseline serum RT–PCR CT value [34.3 (IQR 32.8–36.2) versus 35.2 (33.4–36.6), P = 0.03], a shorter symptom duration [6 (IQR 4–7) versus 8 (IQR 6–9) days, P < 0.01] and a lower estimated PaO2/FiO2 [172 (IQR 93–228) versus 202 (IQR 108–246), P = 0.04] at admission. Remdesivir-treated patients received corticosteroids (96% versus 83%, P < 0.01) and convalescent plasma (45% versus 23%, P < 0.01) more often.

The first dose of remdesivir was given a median of 1 (IQR 0–2) day after admission, corresponding to 6 (IQR 5–8) days after symptom onset. The median duration of remdesivir treatment was 5 (IQR 4–5) days. Remdesivir dosage was 200 mg on Day 1 and 100 mg once daily on subsequent days.

Duration of viraemia and viral load decline

One remdesivir-treated patient cleared viraemia prior to treatment and was therefore assigned to the control group in the analyses of viraemia duration and viral load decline. Serum viral clearance was observed in 52% (54/104) of remdesivir-treated patients and 43% (91/214) of controls (P = 0.12). Serum viral clearance in remdesivir-treated patients was not observed in 17% (18/104), who died, and 31% (32/104), who were discharged before viral clearance was observed. The corresponding numbers for controls were 16% (34/214; P = 0.75) and 42% (89/214; P = 0.07).

There was no significant difference in the rate of serum viral clearance in remdesivir-treated patients compared with controls [SHR 1.4 (95% CI 0.9–2.0), P = 0.11] in the multivariable competing risk model after adjusting for age, sex, immunocompromisation, Charlson comorbidity index, symptom duration, estimated PaO2/FiO2, C-reactive protein, lymphocyte count, corticosteroid and convalescent plasma treatment (Table 2). The cumulative incidence functions for serum viral clearance in remdesivir-treated patients and controls are presented in Figure 2(a). An increased rate of serum viral clearance was found in patients treated with convalescent plasma [SHR 1.7 (95% CI 1.2–2.3), P < 0.01] in the multivariable analysis. The rate of serum viral clearance decreased with higher age (P = 0.02) and increased with longer symptom duration (P = 0.01) and higher lymphocyte count (P < 0.01). There were no significant interactions between remdesivir treatment and symptom duration or convalescent plasma treatment. The results were concordant in a sensitivity analysis where time for observed serum viral clearance was defined as the first negative serum sample (Table S1, available as Supplementary data at JAC Online). Results were also concordant in a sensitivity analysis excluding immunosuppressed patients (Table S2).

The cohort was restricted to patients with symptom duration of ≤7 days at admission in a subgroup analysis. The median symptom duration at admission was 6 (IQR 4–7) days and 45% (81/182) received remdesivir treatment in this subgroup. There was a non-significant trend towards faster serum viral clearance in remdesivir-treated patients [SHR 1.6 (95% CI 1.0–2.7), P = 0.051] (Table S3 and Figure S1).

The daily median CT values of remdesivir-treated patients and controls are shown in Figure 2(b). Serum viral load decline did not differ significantly (P = 0.11) between remdesivir-treated patients and controls in the main cohort in an adjusted linear mixed model. However, serum CT values increased 0.16 units/day (95% CI 0.02–0.31, P = 0.03) faster in remdesivir-treated patients compared with controls in the subgroup analysis including only patients with symptom duration of ≤7 days at admission.

Mortality and in-hospital outcome

There was no loss to follow-up for 60 day mortality. The 60 day mortality in the full cohort was 27% (86/318). The cohort included 272 patients, of whom 39% (105/272) received remdesivir after restricting to when corticosteroids were routinely given. The 60 day mortalities were 30% (32/105) for remdesivir-treated patients and 22% (37/167) for controls in this subgroup. A Kaplan–Meier curve of 60 day mortality is presented in Figure 3(a). There was no significant difference in the risk of 60 day mortality in remdesivir-treated patients compared with controls [HR 1.0 (95% CI 0.6–1.8), P = 0.97] in the multivariable Cox regression (Table 3). Higher age (P < 0.01), lower estimated PaO2/FiO2 (P < 0.01) and shorter symptom duration (P = 0.02) increased the risk of 60 day mortality. The results were consistent in the subgroup analysis including only patients with symptom duration of ≤7 days at admission (Table S4). In-hospital outcomes for remdesivir-treated patients and controls are presented in Figure 3(b). The odds for progression on the WHO ordinal scale were not significantly different for remdesivir-treated patients compared with controls [OR 1.4 (95% CI 0.8–2.4), P = 0.31] (Table S7). Higher odds were seen for patients with higher age (P < 0.01), male sex (P = 0.02), lower PaO2/FiO2 (P < 0.01) and convalescent plasma treatment (P = 0.02). Results were mostly consistent in the subgroup analysis that only included patients with symptom duration of ≤7 days at admission (Table S8).

Discussion

Viraemia at admission may identify a high-risk population with poor viral control for whom effective antiviral treatment could be beneficial. This study aimed to describe the antiviral effects and outcomes of remdesivir treatment in viraemic patients with severe COVID-19.

The rate of serum viral clearance was not significantly different between remdesivir-treated patients and controls. However, there was a non-significant trend indicating an estimated 40% increased likelihood of serum viral clearance in remdesivir-treated patients. A secondary analysis restricted to patients with symptom duration ≤7 days was performed due to previously reported better effect of early remdesivir treatment and because symptom duration was significantly shorter in the remdesivir group of the whole cohort. The trend towards a faster rate of serum viral clearance was strengthened (P = 0.051) and serum viral load decreased significantly faster in remdesivir-treated patients compared with controls with symptom duration ≤7 days. These results suggest an apparent, modest antiviral effect of remdesivir on SARS-CoV-2 viraemia in this cohort.

A more pronounced effect of remdesivir early after symptom onset is in line with previous data showing that viral loads peak early, and a reduced risk of hospitalization seen with early remdesivir treatment.4,23 Treatment with convalescent plasma also appeared to affect serum viral clearance. These data suggest that viral replication was ongoing in the cohort and was inhibited by treatment, at least in patients with ≤7 days symptom duration at admission. Viraemia may thus indicate a systemic infection with replicating virus and poor viral control. This is further supported by previous data showing prolonged shedding of replicating virus in patients with severe disease, visualization of intact virions in plasma, and positive viral cultures from plasma.24–26 However, serum SARS-CoV-2 PCR positivity may also reflect a spill-over from high viral load in other tissues or tissue damage with leakage of viral debris as suggested previously.27

Previous in vivo data on the antiviral effects of remdesivir focused on upper respiratory tract samples with inconsistent results. The non-significant results on viraemia in the primary analysis including patients with ≤10 days symptom duration are consistent with randomized trials reporting no significant difference in respiratory tract viral load.4,7,9,10 On the other hand, the trend of faster serum viral clearance in patients with ≤7 days symptom duration is in line with studies reporting a faster decline of SARS-CoV-2 RNA in the upper respiratory tract.11–13 The, at best, modest effect of remdesivir found in this study is in line with previous inconsistent results from studies on the effect on upper respiratory tract viral load and indicate that the largest effects are likely to be seen with early treatment.

Patients treated with convalescent plasma had 1.7 times higher rate of serum viral clearance in this study. Previous randomized trials on convalescent plasma and monoclonal anti-spike antibodies have shown inconsistent results on viral clearance in the upper respiratory tract.28–32 A randomized study reported no effect on clearance of viraemia with three units of convalescent plasma (n = 60) compared with standard of care (n = 60).33 In contrast, one unit of convalescent plasma was administered daily until serum viral clearance at our study hospital, which may explain a difference between these studies. It is, however, important to note that results from this study were incidental findings and that the study was primarily designed to investigate the effect of remdesivir and not convalescent plasma treatment. There is an ongoing randomized trial on convalescent plasma treatment at the study hospital that will be more suited to analyse the effects of convalescent plasma treatment.34

Remdesivir treatment did not affect 60 day mortality or in-hospital adverse outcomes in this study. Two previous randomized studies (n = 1062 and n = 8275) on hospitalized patients similarly reported no effect on Day 29 and in-hospital mortality, respectively.2,3 However, these two studies reported shorter time to recovery in the treatment group and a small reduction of the risk for in-hospital mortality and/or progression to ventilation in a subgroup of patients not on ventilation when included.2,3 Furthermore, a cohort including 2607 patients showed a reduced risk of 30 day mortality for remdesivir-treated patients, with more pronounced effect if given early (≤6 days) after symptom onset.6 Our study may have been underpowered to detect significant differences in mortality or adverse in-hospital outcomes as clinical effects in previous studies have been small.

This is, to our knowledge, the first study investigating the antiviral effects of remdesivir on viraemic patients hospitalized for COVID-19. Strengths of this study include a reasonably large population sample with detailed baseline characteristics and outcome data, and no loss to follow-up for 60 day mortality. Limitations beyond the retrospective and observational nature of this study include differences in baseline characteristics between treatment groups. The differences are in line with the supply of remdesivir being limited during the study period and prioritized for patients perceived to be more ill. Despite a subgroup analysis on patients with shorter symptom duration and adjustment for potential confounders, there is still a risk of remaining confounding by indication and unmeasured confounding that could attenuate the results. Moreover, serum sampling was not daily on all patients, leading to a considerable amount of censoring for the duration of viraemia and viral load decline analyses. Detecting viraemia using RT–PCR as opposed to actual viral identification through, for example, viral culturing may not correctly reflect antiviral effects. Factors affecting the external validity of the study include that not all hospitalized patients were sampled for SARS-CoV-2 RNA in serum, leading to a selection bias affecting generalizability.

Conclusions

No significant differences were seen in duration of SARS-CoV-2 viraemia, decline of serum viral load or 60 day mortality and in-hospital adverse outcome between remdesivir-treated patients and controls in this cohort study of 318 viraemic patients hospitalized for COVID-19. An apparent trend towards shorter duration of viraemia and a significantly faster decline of serum viral load were seen in patients with symptom duration of ≤7 days at admission, suggesting an effect of remdesivir when given early after symptom onset.
