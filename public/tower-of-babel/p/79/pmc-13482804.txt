Single cell multiomics unravel the transcription networks controlling the different EMT tumor states
Authors: Andrea Pérez-González, Gabriel Windels, Kevin Bévant, Justine Lengrand, Sophie Lemaire, Virginie Moers, Samuel Scozzaro, Ulysse Debroux, Christine Dubois, Sebastiaan Vanuytven, Cédric Blanpain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a dynamic process during which cells lose their epithelial characteristics and acquire mesenchymal traits. In cancer, EMT is closely associated with tumor initiation, progression, invasion, metastasis, and therapy resistance. Rather than being a binary state switch, EMT encompasses a spectrum of tumor states with distinct functional properties. However, the transcription factors (TFs) that govern transitions between these EMT states remain poorly defined. Here, using multi-omic approaches combining single-cell RNA-seq and single-cell ATAC-seq, we delineate the transcriptomic and chromatin landscapes of distinct EMT states in a mouse model of skin squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). Through CRISPR/Cas9-mediated loss-of-function studies coupled with in vitro and in vivo functional assays, we identify TFs regulating specific EMT states. Klf5 and Pitx1 control the early stages of EMT and are essential for metastasis formation. In contrast, Nfatc1 and Creb3l1 act at later stages of EMT. Similar EMT states and regulatory patterns are found in mouse pancreatic adenocarcinoma and human cancers. Altogether, our study defines the transcriptional and chromatin landscape controlling EMT progression in mouse skin SCC, identifies EMT state-specific TFs and highlights their essential roles in regulating metastasis.

Introduction

Epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a cellular process through which epithelial cells lose defining features, such as tight cell–cell junctions and apicobasal polarity, and acquire mesenchymal traits with increased invasiveness. In cancer, EMT is a key driver of cell plasticity and tumor heterogeneity, and is associated with tumor initiation, progression, invasion, metastasis, and resistance to therapy1–3.

Despite traditionally considered as a binary state switch, recent studies demonstrated that EMT tumor cells present intermediate or hybrid states that can transit from one to another while co-expressing epithelial and mesenchymal markers4,5. The epithelial state is considered as the most proliferative, although hybrid EMT states can also exhibit high proliferation5–7. Hybrid EMT states are the most metastatic, whereas late EMT states are more locally invasive4,5,8,9. While EMT states show comparable levels of stemness, they differ in plasticity as well as in spatial distribution of tumor niches, which different composition of immune, endothelial, and lymphatic cells4–6,9. EMT is regulated by transcription factors (TFs) such as SNAI1, TWIST1, ZEB1, and ZEB210. These distinct EMT states have been also identified in human cancers11–13.

However, the intrinsic and extrinsic mechanisms governing EMT states and their transitions remain incompletely understood. The chromatin and transcriptomic landscapes underlying each state, as well as the complete set of TFs and gene regulatory networks (GRNs) regulating their stability and transition, remain elusive.

Here, we perform a multi-omic analysis combining single-cell RNA-seq and ATAC-seq to define, at single-cell resolution, the genes, enhancers, and TFs associated with distinct EMT tumor states. Through CRISPR/Cas9 deletion and functional characterization in vitro and in vivo, we identify Pitx1, Klf5, Nfatc1 and Creb3l1 as key TFs regulating specific tumor state transitions during EMT.

Results

Distinct EMT states identified by single cell RNA-seq in skin SCC

To identify EMT tumor states, single-cell RNA-seq was performed on twelve primary tumors from a genetic mouse model of skin SCC exhibiting spontaneous EMT (Lgr5-CreER; KrasG12D; Trp53cKO; Rosa-YFP)4,14. Tumors presenting different degrees of EMT (1-65% EPCAM + ) were isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). Unsupervised clustering of this single-cell RNA-seq integrated dataset identified four distinct EMT tumor states (Fig. 1a). Tumor cells were distinguished from untransformed keratinocytes based on genetic and transcriptional features, including YFP expression, elevated CNV scores inferred by inferCNV, Trp53 exon 2–10 deletion, and KrasG12D mutation (Supplementary Fig. 2a–f).

The epithelial state (Epcam + , Cdh1 + , Krt14 + , Esrp1 + , S100a14 + ) expressed canonical epithelial markers and lacked mesenchymal gene (Vim) expression. This tumor state included a small more differentiated squamous subpopulation (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). In the early hybrid EMT state, Epcam and Cdh1 expression was lost, while other epithelial markers (Krt14, Krt5, Itga6) were retained, and Vim was upregulated. In the hybrid EMT state, epidermal keratins (Krt14, Krt5) were lost; instead, Krt8 expression was induced, a marker of simple epithelia, and mesenchymal gene expression further increased. In the late EMT tumor state, fibroblast-like genes such as Col3a1, Pdgfrb, Aspn, or Postn were upregulated. Zeb1, one of the master regulators of EMT, was expressed across all EMT states, whereas other EMT TFs (Prrx1, Twist1) showed progressive upregulation from early hybrid towards late EMT (Fig. 1b–f). All tumors, except one, contained all EMT states with different proportions. Some tumors were enriched in epithelial and early hybrid states, whereas others contained equal proportion of each state or were enriched in hybrid and late EMT states (Supplementary Fig. 3c). These expression patterns were reproducible across individual datasets (Supplementary Fig. 4). Cycling cells were observed at similar proportions in epithelial, early hybrid, and hybrid EMT states, but were markedly reduced in late EMT, and no cluster was dominated by cell cycle genes (Fig. 1g).

Lineage trajectory analysis using Slingshot15–17 identified two bifurcating trajectories originating from the epithelial state: one progressing through hybrid states towards late EMT; another ending at the hybrid EMT state (Supplementary Fig. 5a–d). Similar results were obtained by scFates18 (Supplementary Fig. 5e–g).

The existence of distinct EMT states was confirmed at the protein level by in situ immunostaining of mouse tumors, validating epithelial (YFP + , E-cadherin + , KRT14 + , KRT8-, VIM-), early hybrid EMT (YFP + , E-cadherin-, KRT14 + , KRT8-, VIM + ), hybrid EMT (YFP + , E-cadherin-, KRT14-, KRT8 + , VIM + ) and late EMT (YFP + , E-cadherin-, KRT14-, KRT8-, VIM + ) tumor states (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Altogether, these data support the existence of discrete EMT states, and the lineage trajectory analysis is consistent with a progression through intermediate EMT states4,5,19–22. This integrated single-cell RNA-seq dataset provides a robust framework for dissecting the molecular mechanisms, TFs, and GRNs governing EMT tumor state transitions.

TFs regulating the different EMT states predicted by SCENIC analysis

To identify TFs regulating the distinct EMT states, SCENIC (single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering)23 analysis was applied to the integrated single-cell RNA-seq dataset. This computational approach infers TF activity and gene regulatory networks (GRNs) by combining co-expression analysis with the identification of TF binding motifs within 10 kb surrounding the transcription start sites, enabling the identification of regulons - groups of genes likely regulated by the same TF. Regulons are classified as ‘positive’ (transcriptional activator) or ‘negative’ (transcriptional repressor) based on their positive or negative correlation with TF expression.

While TFs governing epithelial (e.g. Grhl1, Grhl2, Ovol1, Ovol2, Trp63) and mesenchymal states (e.g. Snai1, Twist1, Zeb1, Zeb2) have been previously described4,5, the mechanisms regulating transitions across the distinct EMT states remain elusive. Classical positive epithelial regulators (Grhl1, Grhl2 and Ovol2) were active exclusively in the epithelial state, with Grhl2 also predicted to repress mesenchymal genes. Bhlhe41 was predicted to activate the epithelial state while repressing early hybrid EMT. Several TFs, including Klf5, Pitx1 and Trp63, were active in both epithelial and early hybrid EMT states – reflecting the plastic nature of the early hybrid EMT state -, with Klf5 displaying higher activity in early hybrid EMT. Instead, Nfe2l2 activity was predicted to be restricted to early hybrid EMT. Hybrid and late EMT states expressed canonical EMT-TFs as Twist1 and Prrx1, whose expression increased gradually along EMT, whereas Zeb1 was consistently expressed across EMT states. These TFs were predicted to repress epithelial gene expression, while Zeb1 and Twist1 also promoted the expression of late EMT genes, with Twist1 activity emerging already in hybrid EMT. Additional regulators were identified. Hoxc8 and Klf2 were active in hybrid and late EMT states and repressed epithelial and early hybrid programs. Nr2f2, previously described as EMT regulator24, showed activity at the hybrid to late EMT transition and repressed epithelial genes. Cebpb, Nfatc1, Ebf1, and Creb3l1 were predicted to regulate the late EMT state, with their activity initiating in hybrid EMT. Among these, Cebpb and Ebf1 also repressed epithelial genes, whereas Nfatc1 repressed early hybrid EMT (Fig. 2b; Supplementary Fig. 7b; Supplementary Data 1). These patterns were reproducible across individual datasets (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Altogether, SCENIC analysis confirms known epithelial and mesenchymal regulators, refines their temporal activity during EMT progression, and identifies additional candidate TFs associated with specific states.

TFs associated with chromatin remodeling across EMT states

To identify open chromatin regions and associated TFs across EMT states, combined single-cell RNA-seq and single-cell ATAC-seq (10X Multiome; n = 3) was performed on FACS-isolated tumor cells (YFP+, LIN-). The EMT states uncovered by single cell RNA-seq (Fig. 1) were recapitulated, based on defined marker gene expression. Consistently, Zeb1 expression remained stable across EMT states, whereas Prrx1 and Twist1 increased progressively from early hybrid to late EMT (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 9a–f). Two early hybrid EMT states were identified. Early hybrid EMT-I showed higher expression of detoxification (Mt1, Mt2, Gclm, Mgst1, Aldh3a1), lipid metabolism (Acaca, Tkt, Fabp5, Sfxn1), and epithelial adhesion (Dsp, Cdh13, Itga1) genes, whereas early hybrid EMT-II exhibited increased expression of canonical EMT TFs (Zeb1, Twist2, Foxc2), and extracellular matrix components (Mmp14, Ltbp4, Tgfb1, Col23a1). Slingshot trajectory analysis indicated a sequential transition from early hybrid EMT-I to EMT-II before progression to later EMT states. Single cell ATAC-seq data identified differentially accessible chromatin regions associated with EMT states, representing candidate cis-regulatory elements controlling gene expression during EMT tumor state transitions25. Epithelial-specific putative enhancers were accessible only in the epithelial state and associated with genes such as Epcam, Esrp1, and Grhl2, while regions linked to genes like Krt14, Pitx1, and Itga6 remained accessible in both epithelial and early hybrid EMT states (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). In contrast, putative enhancers associated with genes upregulated during EMT (e.g., Klf2, Col3a1, Postn) showed progressively increased accessibility towards hybrid and late EMT states (Supplementary Fig. 10c).

To define TF networks, enrichment of TF-binding motifs in differentially accessible regions was analyzed and integrated with RNA expression to identify active TFs within each family (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 11a–e). In the epithelial state, enriched motifs included ZF-C2H2 (Zeb1), Grh/CP2 (Grhl2), bHLH (Bhlhe41), HMG (High Mobility Group; Sox15), ZF-C2H2 (Klf5), AP-1/bZIP (Creb5/Mafk/Maf) and Homeobox (Pitx1). Although Zeb1 motif was enriched in epithelial chromatin accessible regions, Zeb1 expression was absent, consistent with its repressive function during EMT. In early hybrid EMT, enriched motifs included P53 (Trp63), Homeobox (Pitx1), ZF-C2H2 (Klf5/Klf3), AP-2 (Tfap2c/Tfap2b), bHLH (Hif1a) and T-box (Tbx1). Motif analysis further distinguished the two substates: early hybrid EMT-I was enriched for AP-2 (Tfap2c/b), KLF (Klf3/5), and bHLH (Hif1a) motifs, whereas early hybrid EMT-II showed T-box motif (Tbx1) enrichment. Both shared an enrichment for P53 (Trp63) and Homeobox (Pitx1) motifs, indicating a common regulatory core (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 11a–c).

Hybrid EMT was characterized by enrichment of Runt (Runx2/Runx3), bHLH (Twist1/Twist2), nuclear receptor (NR; Nr2f6/Nr2f2/Nr2f1), AP-1/bZIP (Nfe2l1), ZF-C2H2 (Klf2/Klf6) and RHD (Rel homology domain; Nfatc4/Nfatc2) motifs. Late EMT showed enrichment for Homeobox (Prrx1/Prrx2), bHLH (Twist1/Twist2/Snai1), RHD (Nfatc1/Nfkb1), AP-2 (Tfap2b), NFI (Nfix), nuclear receptor (Nr2f2), Forkhead (Foxo3), IRF (Irf1), AP-1/bZIP (Creb3l1/Cebpb/Cebpd), STAT (Stat1/Stat2) and Collier/Olf1/EBF (COE; Ebf1) (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 11d, e).

Altogether, these analyses define dynamic chromatin remodeling across EMT state progression, identifying cis-regulatory elements and TF networks associated with specific tumor states and their transitions.

SCENIC+ analysis of single cell RNA and ATAC-seq unravels transcription factor activity in distinct EMT states

To further characterize the GRNs underlying distinct EMT states, SCENIC+ analysis26 was applied to combined single-cell RNA-seq and single-cell ATAC-seq data. This computational method infers enhancer-driven regulons (eRegulons) and classifies TFs as transcriptional activators or repressors.

SCENIC+ recovered known epithelial regulators (Grhl2, Grhl3) and regulators of both epithelial and early hybrid EMT (Trp63)4,14, and identified additional positive regulators of the epithelial state, such as Ehf, Bach2, Bhlhe40 and Cux1. TFs including Pitx1, Klf5 and Clock were associated with both epithelial and early hybrid EMT states (Fig. 4a). Pitx1 was predicted to have a dual activity, promoting epithelial and early hybrid EMT while repressing later EMT states. Canonical EMT-TFs (Zeb1, Zeb2, and Snai1) were predicted to repress epithelial programs. Zeb1 expression initiated in early hybrid EMT, whereas Zeb2 and Snai1 were upregulated from hybrid EMT (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Data 2). Additional regulators of later EMT states were identified. Creb3l1, Nfatc1, and Ebf1 were associated with late EMT but were already active in hybrid EMT. Nfe2l1 was linked to hybrid EMT, while Bnc2 and Nfix were specific to late EMT and repressed epithelial states. Etv1 showed activity across EMT, with the highest expression in late EMT (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Data 2).

TF–TF interaction networks derived from SCENIC+ revealed predicted regulatory hierarchies. Epithelial TFs formed interconnected circuits, with Grhl1 activating Ovol1 and Ehf, and reciprocal activation observed among Grhl1 and Ehf, and Grhl2 and Trp63. Trp63 was predicted to activate Klf5, which in turn regulated Nfe2l2 and Pitx1. These early regulators, Trp63, Klf5, and Pitx1, were predicted to be repressed by Zeb1. In later EMT, Prrx1 was linked to the activation of Nfatc1 and Ebf1, promoting mesenchymal programs, including the activation of Creb3l1, while Pitx1 repressed late EMT TFs such as Ebf1, Nfatc1, and Snai1 (Fig. 4b).

Altogether, integration of SCENIC and SCENIC+ analyses confirms known EMT regulators and identifies additional candidate TFs associated with specific EMT states, including epithelial (Ehf, Pitx1, Klf5, Clock), early hybrid EMT (Pitx1, Klf5, Clock), and hybrid and late EMT (Nfatc1, Ebf1, Creb3l1, Nfix, Bnc2).

In silico perturbation analysis uncovers putative regulators of specific EMT states

To predict the importance of candidate TFs in the regulation of EMT states, in silico perturbation analysis was performed on the GRNs inferred by SCENIC + . Simulated loss-of-function effects were evaluated along the principal component axis capturing EMT progression (i.e., PC_0), which reflects the continuum from epithelial to mesenchymal states (Fig. 4c).

Loss of epithelial regulators (e.g., Grhl2) and activators of epithelial/early hybrid EMT states (e.g., Klf5, Trp63, Clock) resulted in a shift towards later EMT states (positive PC0 shift). Similarly, loss of Pitx1—predicted to activate early states while repressing later ones—also shifted cells towards advanced EMT. Conversely, loss of activators of hybrid and late EMT states (e.g., Creb3l1, Ebf1, Nfatc1) induced a shift towards earlier EMT states (negative PC0 shift). Simulated knockdown of repressors of the epithelial state (e.g., Zeb1, Zeb2) maintained epithelial gene expression in tumor cells and shifted profiles towards epithelial and early EMT states. The results of the in silico perturbations were consistent with the regulatory roles assigned by SCENIC+ (Fig. 4c, d).

Pitx1 and Klf5 promote epithelial and early hybrid EMT states

PITX1 has been previously linked to tumor squamous differentiation27, and KLF5 has been associated with EMT in vitro28–30. However, their roles in regulating discrete EMT states remained unknown. SCENIC and SCENIC+ based analyses predicted both TFs as activators of epithelial and early hybrid EMT states. Consistently, immunostaining of primary tumors showed that PITX1 and KLF5 expression was restricted to epithelial (KRT14 + /VIM-) and early hybrid EMT (KRT14 + /VIM + ) states, and absent from hybrid (KRT8 + /VIM + ) and late EMT (KRT8-/VIM + ) states (Supplementary Fig. 12a–d).

To validate computational predictions and define the functional roles of candidate TFs, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated deletion was performed targeting the DNA-binding domains of Pitx1 and Klf5 in a primary mouse skin SCC cell line. Gene deletion was confirmed by PCR and western blot. Residual wild-type Pitx1 was eliminated through single-cell cloning to ensure complete knockout (Supplementary Fig. 13a, b).

To assess EMT dynamics, FACS-isolated EPCAM+ tumor cells from control and KO lines were cultured and treated with TGFβ1, which triggers EMT progression in a synchronized and reproducible manner. Cells were analyzed after 8 days, a time point at which intermediate EMT states predominate in vitro. Under these conditions, control cells largely adopted early hybrid EMT features, characterized by retention of KRT14 expression together with induction of VIM. Although KRT8 is not expressed in epithelial cells in vivo, it was detected in cultured epithelial and early hybrid EMT cells, reflecting adaptation to in vitro conditions (Supplementary Fig. 13c).

Deletion of Pitx1 or Klf5 resulted in a marked redistribution of EMT states, as assessed by immunostaining on cytospin. Both knockouts showed a strong reduction in KRT14+ populations, indicating loss of epithelial and early hybrid identities, and a concomitant increase in KRT8+/VIM+ cells, consistent with enrichment of more advanced EMT states (Fig. 5a, b; Supplementary Fig. 13d, e). TGFβ1 treatment led to a near-complete loss of KRT14+ populations, with a strong decrease in KRT14 + /KRT8 + /VIM- cells (24.4% vs 0.6%) and KRT14 + /KRT8 + /VIM+ cells (58.0% vs 5.6%), and a marked increase in KRT14-/KRT8 + /VIM+ cells (17.5% vs 54.7%) in Pitx1 KO cells (Fig. 5a). A similar redistribution was observed upon Klf5 KO, with consistent reduction of epithelial (24.4% vs 7.9% KRT14 + /KRT8 + /VIM-), and early hybrid (17.5% vs 36.5% KRT14-/KRT8 + /VIM + ) populations and expansion of hybrid EMT cells (17.5% vs 36.5% KRT14-/KRT8 + /VIM + ) (Fig. 5a, b). Similar results were obtained using independent sgRNAs targeting Klf5 (Supplementary Fig. 13f–h). Late EMT (KRT8-/VIM + ) state was not observed in vitro under these culture conditions.

To evaluate these effects in vivo, FACS-isolated EPCAM+ cells were transplanted subcutaneously into NOD/SCID/Il2Rγ-null immunodeficient mice. As only epithelial cells were injected, differences in the distribution of EMT states reflected cellular plasticity rather than initial tumor state composition. Tumors derived from Pitx1 or Klf5 KO cells showed reduced early hybrid EMT (KRT14 + /VIM + : 39.7% control; 10.9% Pitx1 KO; 24.9% Klf5 KO) and a strong increase in hybrid EMT cells (KRT8 + /KRT14-/VIM + : 16.2% control; 49.6% Pitx1 KO; 51.9% Klf5 KO), confirming a shift towards more advanced states upon Pitx1 or Klf5 deletion (Fig. 5c, d; Supplementary Fig. 14a, b).

To assess the impact of Pitx1 and Klf5 deletion on the expression of epithelial or mesenchymal genes, bulk RNA-seq of FACS-isolated EPCAM+ and EPCAM- cells following TGFβ1 treatment was performed. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of the epithelial signature (upregulated genes in control Epcam+ epithelial tumor cells vs. control Epcam- mesenchymal cells) on the differentially regulated genes following gene KO in EPCAM+ cells showed a strong reduction of the epithelial signature in KO EPCAM+ cells, including Ovol2, Krt7, L1cam, S100a14, and Dll1 (Pitx1 KO), and Grhl3, L1cam, Aqp3, Itgb6, and Dll1 (Klf5 KO) (Fig. 5e–h; Supplementary Fig. 15a). In EPCAM- cells, loss of Pitx1 or Klf5 reduced early hybrid markers (e.g., Krt14, Itga6) and increased mesenchymal gene expression, consistent with the enrichment of hybrid EMT cells. Upregulated mesenchymal genes included Pdgfra/b, Col1a1, Col5a2, and Col27a1 (Pitx1 KO), and Col4a5, Col12a1, and Mmp19 (Klf5 KO) (Fig. 5i–p; Supplementary Fig. 15b). Together, these results indicate that Pitx1 and Klf5 regulate both epithelial and mesenchymal transcriptional programs.

Analysis of SCENIC and SCENIC+ predicted target genes supported direct regulatory roles. Pitx1 deletion reduced expression of predicted epithelial targets (Krt14, Krt17, Itga6) (Fig. 5m, n) associated with accessible chromatin regions containing Pitx1 motif binding sites (chr11:100260790-100261835 for Krt14 and Krt17; chr2:71738298-71739233 for Itga6) (Supplementary Fig. 15c, d). Similarly, Klf5 deletion downregulated Krt14, Itga6, Tns4, and Cdh13 (Fig. 5o, p), and Krt14 gene region contained a potential enhancer with a Klf5 binding site (chr11:100261964-100262570), different from the enhancer with a Pitx1 binding site, in a differentially accessible region for epithelial and early hybrid EMT states (chr11:100261964-100262570) (Supplementary Fig. 15c).

Altogether, these data demonstrate that Pitx1 and Klf5 maintain epithelial and early hybrid EMT states by sustaining epithelial gene expression and limiting progression towards advanced EMT. Their loss disrupts this balance, favoring transition towards hybrid EMT states both in vitro and in vivo.

To assess the functional relevance of the identified EMT regulators, Pitx1, Klf5, Nfatc1, and Creb3l1, tumorigenic and metastatic capacities were evaluated in vivo. Secondary tumors were resected after 35 days, and lung metastases were assessed 6 weeks post-transplantation.

Upon transplantation, Pitx1 and Klf5 KO tumors exhibited slightly reduced growth, whereas Nfatc1 and Creb3l1 KO tumors did not differ from controls. TGFβ1 pre-treatment led to more pronounced EMT and predominantly late EMT states in control, Pitx1 KO, and Klf5 KO tumors, while Nfatc1 and Creb3l1 KO tumors retained epithelial and early hybrid EMT features (Supplementary Fig. 17a–c).

EMT transition states and transcriptional regulators in mouse pancreatic cancer

To assess whether the transcriptional regulators identified in distinct EMT states in mouse skin SCC were conserved across tumor types, a published single-cell RNA-seq dataset of primary and metastatic mouse pancreatic adenocarcinoma was analyzed, a model known to exhibit a continuum of EMT states35. Four EMT states were identified based on a gradient of expression of epithelial and mesenchymal genes. The epithelial state expressed Epcam, Cdh1, Muc1, and Krt8. The early hybrid EMT state lost Epcam while retaining Cdh1 and Krt8 and upregulating Vim. The hybrid EMT state further lost Cdh1 but maintained Krt8 and Vim, with additional expression of Sparc. In the late EMT state, epithelial markers were absent, and mesenchymal genes such as Vim, Sparc, Col3a1, and Mmp19 were highly expressed. Expression dynamics of canonical EMT TFs mirrored those observed in skin SCC. Zeb1 expression initiated in early hybrid EMT, Prrx1 was detected from the hybrid EMT state onwards, and Snai1 expression was largely restricted to the late EMT state (Fig. 8a–c).

SCENIC analysis was performed to infer state specific transcriptional regulators. Gata4, Cdx2, and Klf5 were predicted to positively regulate the epithelial state, with Klf5 also being active in early hybrid EMT. Stat1, Cebpa, and Fosl2 were associated with early hybrid EMT, whereas Nfic and Smad3 were predicted to regulate hybrid and late EMT states. Notably, Nfatc1 and Creb3l1 were identified as regulators of the late EMT state. Zeb1 and Prrx1 were predicted to repress epithelial programs while promoting hybrid and late EMT states (Fig. 8d, e).

These data indicate that key aspects of EMT regulatory programs can be conserved across tumor types. Although Pitx1 was not expressed in pancreatic tumors, Klf5 was maintained as a regulator of epithelial and early hybrid EMT states, whereas Nfatc1 and Creb3l1 were conserved as regulators of late EMT.

EMT transition states and transcriptional regulators in human cancer

To assess the human relevance of these findings, EMT signatures derived from the mouse skin SCC mouse model (Fig. 1) were compared with previously defined EMT metaprograms in human cancers12. The human complete EMT program (EMT-I) showed strong overlap with the mouse late EMT state, whereas the human hybrid EMT program (EMT-III) closely matched the mouse hybrid EMT state (Fig. 8f, g; Supplementary Data 3), supporting conservation of EMT transition states across mouse and human cancers. Other human metaprograms (EMT-II, EMT-IV, and MES) showed weaker correlations, likely reflecting tumor-type specificity (Supplementary Fig. 18a).

To further evaluate human conservation at the regulatory level, the specific regulons associated with Pitx1, Klf5, Nfatc1, and Creb3l1 were compared with human EMT metaprograms. For EMT-I (complete EMT), the KLF5 and PITX1 regulons showed a strong negative correlation, whereas CREB3L1 and NFATC1 showed a strong positive correlation, consistent with their respective roles in restraining or promoting late EMT, respectively. For EMT-III (hybrid EMT), KLF5 and PITX1 remained negatively correlated, CREB3L1 showed positive correlation, and NFATC1 displayed minimal association. The negative correlation of KLF5 and PITX1 reflects their activity in epithelial and early hybrid EMT states rather than in more advanced EMT programs (Supplementary Fig. 18b, c).

Overall, these results indicate that key EMT regulatory programs identified in mouse SCC are also conserved in human cancers exhibiting EMT features. Discussion

In this study, a combined single-cell transcriptomic and epigenomic approach, integrated with CRISPR/Cas9 loss-of-function experiments, was used to define the transcriptional and chromatin landscape of EMT states in mouse skin SCC and identify TFs governing their transitions. This framework enabled the identification of Klf5, Pitx1, Nfatc1, and Creb3l1 as key regulators with distinct roles across EMT progression in vitro and in vivo.

Previous work established TFs such as Ovol1, Grhl1, and Trp63 as regulators of epithelial identity, whereas control of early EMT transitions has remained less defined. Here, Klf5 and Pitx1 were identified as regulators of epithelial and early hybrid EMT states, maintaining epithelial gene expression programs and restraining progression towards more advanced EMT states. Functional perturbation of Klf5 and Pitx1 altered the distribution of EMT states and reduced metastatic burden, supporting a role for early EMT programs in tumor plasticity and dissemination capacity. The reduction of metastatic burden upon Klf5 and Pitx1 deletion was associated with diminished epithelial features in metastatic lesions, suggesting that epithelial reprogramming (MET) might contribute to efficient lung colonization and outgrowth. However, a direct role of Klf5 and Pitx1 in the regulation of metastatic initiating cells, their survival in the bloodstream, or their ability to colonize the metastatic sites is also possible. Lineage tracing of different EMT states upon Klf5 and Pitx1 deletion would be required to fully resolve the role of EMT–MET dynamics in the regulation of metastasis formation.

In contrast, Nfatc1 and Creb3l1 were identified as regulators of late EMT states. Loss-of-function experiments indicated that these factors are required for efficient progression into advanced mesenchymal states, but their deletion did not alter metastatic burden. This suggests that late EMT regulators contribute to the stabilization of mesenchymal features rather than driving metastatic dissemination.

Cross-model comparisons indicate that these regulatory programs are not restricted to skin SCC. Klf5 is also associated with epithelial and early hybrid EMT states in pancreatic adenocarcinoma, whereas Nfatc1 and Creb3l1 are conserved in late EMT programs. These findings support a partially conserved regulatory framework underlying EMT progression across cancer types.

Collectively, these results define a hierarchical organization of EMT regulation, in which early factors such as Klf5 and Pitx1 shape initial transcriptional plasticity, while later factors such as Nfatc1 and Creb3l1 maintain mesenchymal states. Rather than representing fixed endpoints, EMT states appear dynamically interconnected, and their transitions can contribute to tumor heterogeneity and metastatic colonization. Future studies should address how these regulatory modules interact with microenvironmental and immune signals to control state plasticity, adaptive resistance, and metastatic progression, potentially revealing therapeutic vulnerabilities.

Methods

Compliance with ethical regulations

This study complies with all relevant ethical regulations. Mouse colonies were housed in an accredited facility and handled in accordance with European regulations. All procedures were approved by the Commission d’Éthique et du Bien-Être Animal (CEBEA, Université Libre de Bruxelles), under the license ID 828 N and 832 N, operating under the European Convention for Vertebrate Animals (ETS No. 123). Mice were monitored daily, and humane endpoints were applied. Animals were euthanized before tumors reached 1 cm diameter (primary skin SCC model) or 2 cm diameter (subcutaneous tumor model), upon ulceration, >20% body weight loss, or signs of distress. No tumors exceeded approved size limits. Housing conditions included 20–24 °C, 55 ± 10% humidity, ad libitum food and water, nesting material, and a 12 h light/dark cycle.

Mouse strains

Rosa26-YFP mice36, Lgr5-creER mice37, KrasLSL-G12D mice38, and Trp53 fl/fl mice39 were imported from the NCI repository and Jackson Laboratories. NOD/SCID and NOD/SCID/Il2Rγ null mice were purchased from Charles River. Both male and female mice with a mixed genetic background were used. No randomization or blinding was performed.

Skin SCC genetically induced skin tumors

The skin SCC mouse model was generated by crossing Rosa26-YFP36, Lgr5-creER37, KrasLSL-G12D38, and Trp53fl/fl39 mice, as previously described40. Tamoxifen was prepared at 25 mg/ml in sunflower oil (Sigma, T5648-1G), 10% ethanol, and administered by four daily intraperitoneal injections (2.5 mg) at postnatal day 28 to activate CreERT2 specifically in LGR5+ hair follicle cells. Cre-lox recombination induced Kras oncogene activation, Trp53 deletion, and YFP expression for lineage tracing of tumor cells. Tumors developed on average 9 weeks after induction and were monitored daily by palpation. Mice were euthanized before tumors reached 1 cm in diameter or upon signs of distress. Tumor size was measured weekly using a precision caliper (0.1 mm resolution) from first detection until endpoint.

Cell culture

Primary FACS-isolated EPCAM+ tumor cells were cultured in Modified Eagle Medium (Capricorn Scientific, SP-2002-500 ml) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, 10437-028), 4 ug/ml hydrocortisone (Sigma, H0888), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (100x) (Capricorn Scientific, PS-B), 2 mM L-glutamine, and 500 ul T3 (Sigma, T6397). Cells were washed with PBS and detached using trypsin (Capricorn Scientific, TRY-2B10). EMT was induced by treatment with TGFβ1 (Peprotech, 100-21) at 0.5 ng/μl for 8 days, with medium refreshed every 2 days under standard culture conditions.

FACS isolation of tumor cells

Skin tumors from Lgr5CreER/KrasLSL-G12D/p53fl/fl/Rosa26-YFP+/+ mice and secondary tumors from subcutaneous grafts in NOD/SCID/Il2Rγ-null mice were dissected, minced, and digested in collagenase I (3.5 mg/ml; Sigma) in HBSS (Gibco) for 1 h at 37 °C under agitation and protected from light. Digestion was stopped with EDTA (5 mM), and cells were washed in PBS with 10% FBS. Suspensions were filtered (70 μm; BD Bioscience) and centrifuged (300 g, 10 min, 4 °C). Cells were resuspended in PBS with 2% FBS. Primary skin tumor cells were incubated with BV711-conjugated anti-EPCAM (rat, clone G8.8, BD Bioscience 563134, 1:100), PE-conjugated anti-CD45 (rat, clone 30-F11, BD, 553081, 1:200) and PE-conjugated anti-CD31 (rat, clone MEC 13.3, BD, 553373, 1:200). Cells coming from secondary tumors from subcutaneous grafts were incubated with APC-Cy7-conjugated anti-EPCAM (rat, clone G8.8, Biolegend 118218, 1:100), APC-conjugated anti-CD45 (rat, clone 30-F11, eBioscience 17-0451, 1:100) and APC-conjugated anti-CD31 (rat, clone 390, eBioscience 17-0311, 1:100). All incubations were performed for 30 min at 4 °C protected from light. After washing with PBS with 2% FBS and centrifugation, cells were resuspended in Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen, H3570, 1:4000) and filtered (40 μm; BD Bioscience). Live single cells were selected based on forward/side scatter, doublet exclusion, and Hoechst exclusion. Tumor cells were identified as YFP+ Lin- (CD45- CD31-), and EPCAM expression was assessed within this population. For secondary tumors, mCherry expression was additionally used.

FACS acquisition and sorting were performed on FACSAria and LSRFortessa using FACSDiva software (v.9.1, BD Bioscience). Sorted cells were collected in culture medium with 50% FBS (transplantation or culture), lysis buffer (RNA extraction, RNeasy Micro Kit - Qiagen) or PBS with 0.04% BSA (single cell sequencing).

For analysis or sorting of cultured cells, cells were trypsinized, washed, and stained with anti-EPCAM (rat, clone G8.8, Biolegend 118218, 1:200) for 30 min at 4 °C. After washing, cells were incubated with 7-AAD (BD, 559925, 1:40) and filtered (40 μm; BD Bioscience). Live single mCherry+ YFP + EPCAM+ or EPCAM− tumor cells were selected using standard gating strategies.

Cloning of dual sgRNA-expression constructs

Dual sgRNA constructs were generated using a one-step cloning strategy41. SgRNAs were ordered as sense and antisense oligos with the appropriate overhangs: sgRNA1–CACC-SENSE, ANTISENSE-CAAA; sgRNA2 CTTGTTT-SENSE-GTTTA, AAA-ANTISENSE-CAAATTCTC. Oligos were annealed and phosphorylated (100 μM each) in T4 ligation buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with T4 PNK (NEB) at 37 °C for 30 min, 95 °C for 5 min, then cooled to 25 °C at 5 °C per min. Annealed sgRNA1 (1:200 dilution) was ligated into 100 ng pLenti_dual-hU6-sgRNA-mU6-sgRNA-EF1a-mCherry-P2A-PuroR backbone using CutSmart buffer (NEB), ATP (Thermo Fisher Scientific), Esp3I (NEB), and T4 ligase (NEB) in 24 cycles (37 °C/16 °C). sgRNA2 (1:200 dilution) and BbsI-HF (NEB) were then added and subjected to a second ligation cycle, followed by 2 h at 37 °C. Constructs were transformed into NEB Stable competent E. coli.

Lentiviral transduction

Lentivirus was produced in HEK 293 T cells (ATCC) by co-transfection of 10 μg plasmid of interest, 10 μg psPAX2, and 5 μg PMD2.G using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in Opti-MEM (Capricorn Scientific). Viral supernatants were collected at 48–72 h and concentrated using 40% PEG-8000 (Sigma) with 1.2 M NaCl. Target cells were transduced in the presence of polybrene (10 μg/ml; Sigma).

All the plasmids used for the CRISPR-KO generation were a gift from Johannes Zuber. First, pRRL-SFFV-rtTA3-IRES-EcoR-PGK-HygroR was introduced into EPCAM + SCC cells, followed by hygromycin-B selection. Cells were then transduced with pLentiv2-TRE3G-Cas9-P2A-BFP and Cas9 expression induced with doxycycline (0.2–0.4 μg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich) for 48 h. BFP+ single cells were sorted to establish single cell clones. Finally, dual sgRNA constructs were introduced, and puromycin selection was applied (14 μg/ml; Gibco).

Knock-out validation by PCR

Cells were pelleted, frozen, and genomic DNA extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen), with proteinase K digestion in 200 μl PBS. PCR was performed on 250 ng DNA using gene-specific primers, and deletions were verified on 2% agarose gels.

Tumor transplantation assays

FACS-isolated YFP+ EPCAM+ tumor cells from cultured control or knockout lines derived from Lgr5CreER/KrasLSL-G12D/p53fl/fl/Rosa26-YFP+/+ mice were collected in 50% medium/50% FBS at 4 °C. Cells were resuspended in 50% FBS/50% Matrigel (E1270, 970 mg/ml; Sigma) and injected subcutaneously into NOD/SCID/Il2Rγ-null mice (1000 cells per injection site). Tumor growth was monitored weekly by palpation and caliper measurement. Mice were euthanized when tumors reached 1 cm or upon signs of distress, and tumors were analyzed by flow cytometry and histology.

Metastasis assay from subcutaneous xenografts

Cultured tumor cells (control or knockout) were treated with TGFβ1 (Peprotech, 100-21; 0.5 ng/μl; every 2 days for 8 days) to induce EMT, then collected directly; or untreated tumor cells were FACS-sorted for YFP+ EPCAM+ cells. Cells were resuspended in 50% FBS/50% Matrigel (Sigma) and injected subcutaneously (1000 cells per site) into NOD/SCID/Il2Rγ-null mice. Tumors were monitored weekly and resected at day 35 (TGFβ1-pre-treated cells) or at 0.8–0.9 cm (EPCAM+ transplants), followed by flow cytometry and histological analysis.

After 6 weeks, lungs were collected, and metastases were quantified on three cryosections per lung based on YFP expression. Metastatic burden was expressed as the number of lesions per lung and the relative metastatic area (ZEN Microscopy Software). EMT states in metastases were assessed by immunofluorescence for KRT14, KRT8, and vimentin.

Cytospin

Cytospin preparations were generated using Cellspin I (Tharmac) with 20,000 cells per sample, following the manufacturer’s instructions (Advanced Cell Diagnostics).

Immunofluorescence

Stainings were performed on frozen sections or cytospins. Tumor tissues and lungs were fixed in 4% PFA (3.5 h), washed in PBS, incubated in 30% sucrose overnight (4 °C), embedded in OCT (Tissue Tek) for cryopreservation, and sectioned (4 μm; Leica CM3050S cryostat). Cytospins were fixed in 4% PFA (10 min). Samples were blocked (PBS, 5% horse serum, 1% BSA, 0.2% Triton X-100; 1 h) and incubated with primary antibodies overnight (4 °C). After PBS washes, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies (1:400; 1 h), stained with Hoechst (Invitrogen, H3570, 1:2000), and mounted (SafeMount, Labonord). Images were acquired using a Zeiss Axio Imager M2 or Axioscan 7 and analyzed with Axiovision or ZEN software.

Antibodies

Primary antibodies: anti-GFP (Abcam, ab6673, 1:500), anti-KRT14 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA5-11599, 1:1000), anti-VIM (Abcam, ab92547, 1:500–1:1000), anti-KRT8 (DSHB, AB_531828, 1:200–1:1000), anti-E-cadherin (Invitrogen, 13-1900, 1:200). Secondary antibodies (1:400): species-specific conjugates (Rhodamine Red-X and Cy5, Jackson ImmunoResearch; Alexa Fluor A488, Invitrogen). Nuclei were stained with Hoechst (Invitrogen, H3570, 1:2000).

Immunoblot

Cells were lysed in SDS buffer, sonicated (Bioruptor Plus, Diagenode), and centrifuged (10,000 g, 15 min, 4 °C). Protein concentration was measured (Pierce 660 nm kit, Thermo Fisher; Bio-Rad iMark microplate). Samples (10–30 μg) were resolved on 4–12% SDS–PAGE (Thermo Fisher) and transferred to nitrocellulose sheets. Membranes were blocked (5% milk, 0.05% BSA) and incubated overnight (4 °C) with primary antibodies: anti-Nfatc1 (Santa Cruz, sc-7294, 1:200), anti-Klf5 (Genetex, GTX103289, 1:1000), anti-Pitx1 (Novus, NBP1-88644, 1:1000), anti-β-actin (Abcam, ab8227, 1:2000). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Sigma Aldrich, 1:5000) and visualized using ECL reagents (Pierce). Imaging was performed on iBright FL1500 (Invitrogen).

Immunohistochemistry

Tumors were fixed in 4% PFA (overnight, 4 °C), washed with PBS, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. Antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (pH 6). Sections were incubated overnight (4 °C) with anti-Pitx1 (Novus, NBP1-88644, 1:100), anti-Klf5 (Bioconnect, GTX103289, 1:400), or anti-Nfatc1 (Santa Cruz, sc-7294, 1:50). Detection was performed using biotinylated secondary antibodies, ABC kit, and DAB (Vector Laboratories). Slides were stained with hematoxylin (Labonord), dehydrated, and mounted (SafeMount). Imaging was performed with ZEISS Axioscan 7.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed on biological replicates (n indicated) using unpaired t-tests in GraphPad Prism (v9.0). Data are presented as mean ± s.e.m.

Single cell RNA and Multiome sequencing

Single-cell RNA library preparation and quality control

Following FACS isolation, primary skin SCC cells were sorted (50% YFP+Lin-, 25% YFP-Lin-, 25% YFP-Lin + ), collected in PBS with 0.04% BSA, centrifuged (250 g, 5 min, 4 °C), resuspended, counted, and loaded onto Chromium Single Cell 3′ chips (10x Genomics). Libraries were prepared using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Kit (V2, 10x Genomics), quality-checked on an Illumina NextSeq 550, and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq.

Integration and analysis of scRNA-seq datasets

Nine single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets and four Multiome samples were integrated. Individual datasets were processed using Scanpy (v1.10.2) 42 with standard parameters. Cells with >700 uniquely expressed genes and <20% mitochondrial and <30% ribosomal counts were retained. Ambient RNA was removed (SoupX v1.6.2)43, and doublets were identified (scDblFinder v3.20.3) 44. After log-normalization, highly variable genes (Seurat method) were used for PCA. Significant principal components were determined by bootstrapping (max 60 PCs). Clustering (Leiden, resolutions 0.1–1.0) was evaluated for stability (clustree v0.5.1)45. Marker genes were identified using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests ( ≥ 25% expression, logFC ≥0.25). Cell types were annotated using Scanpy module scoring and PanglaoDB markers (v27/03/2020)46. Datasets were integrated using scVI (v1.1.4; n_hidden=128, n_latent=50, n_layers=2) and scANVI, correcting for batch effects and technical covariates47. Scanpy functions were utilized to compute a neighborhood graph of observations based on the scANVI latent space (scanpy.pp.neighbors), as well as to perform dimensionality reduction (sc.tl.umap) and Leiden clustering (sc.tl.leiden, resolution=0.5). Tumor cells (YFP⁺, KrasG12D, Trp53 mutant, high CNV by inferCNV v1.22.048) were subclustered to define EMT states. PCA and Harmony (v1.2.1) 49 were applied, with stable clustering at resolution 0.4 (four EMT states) and 0.5 (four EMT states; one differentiated state). Marker genes were again identified using Wilcoxon tests (same thresholds). Gene set enrichment was computed using AUCell (decoupler package) and visualized on UMAP50.

Trajectory inference

Pseudotime was inferred using Slingshot (v2.0.0) on Harmony embeddings, with the epithelial cluster as the root17. Robustness was validated using scFates (v1.1.1) 18. Diffusion maps and multiscale space were computed with Palantir: palantir.utils.run_diffusion_maps(harmony_reduction), palantir.utils.determine_multiscale_space(dm_res, n_eigns = 4) followed by neighbor graph construction (n_neighbors=30) and force-directed layouts. Trajectories were inferred using the EPG algorithm (nodes=50, ppt_nsteps=200), defining the epithelial state as the starting point.

Single cell Multiome: library preparation and quality control

Following FACS isolation, three primary skin SCCs were sorted (YFP + LIN-); 200,000 cells were collected in PBS with 0.04% BSA (low-binding tubes, Eppendorf). Nuclei isolation buffers were prepared following the manufacturer’s protocol (10x Genomics, CG000365 Rev C), including nuclei, wash, and lysis buffers supplemented with DTT and RNase inhibitor. Cells were centrifuged (300 g, 5 min, 4 °C), resuspended in 100 μl lysis buffer, mixed, and incubated on ice (3.5 min). Wash buffer (800 μl) was added, followed by centrifugation (500 g, 5 min, 4°C) and a second wash. Nuclei were resuspended in nuclei buffer, counted, and loaded onto Chromium Next GEM Single Cell Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression chips (10x Genomics). Libraries were prepared using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Kit (V2), quality-checked on an Illumina NextSeq 550, and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq.

Reads were aligned to the mm10 genome and quantified using CellRanger, yielding 1732, 5957, and 11,014 barcodes. Datasets were processed using Seurat (v5.1)51 and Signac (v1.14)25. Gene expression matrices were imported (Read10x_h5), chromatin data (CreateChromatinAssay), and Seurat objects (CreateSeuratObject) were generated. Annotation was based on Ensembl v79. RNA counts were normalized (10,000 reads per cell, log-transformed) and scaled (z-score). Quality control retained nuclei meeting both ATAC and RNA criteria. ATAC filters included 1500–120,000 fragments in peaks, <40% reads in peaks, TSS enrichment >2, blacklist ratio <0.025, and nucleosome signal <4. RNA filters included hybrid_score <0.90 (scds)52, <30% mitochondrial/ribosomal reads, and >800 total counts. Only barcodes passing all filters were retained.

Preliminary clustering and UMAP were performed with Seurat and Signac, followed by removal of low-quality clusters or stromal contaminants based on QC metrics and marker expression. For ATAC analysis, dimensionality reduction used RunTFIDF function and latent semantic indexing (LSI). Peaks were called per sample using CallPeaks (Signac).

Motif enrichment analysis

Differentially accessible peaks were analyzed using HOMER. De novo motif discovery was performed with findMotifsGenome.pl (mm10, size −200, all peaks as background). Enriched motifs were curated by selecting representative motifs per family, and associated TF families were assigned to infer candidate regulators of each tumor state.

Gene regulatory network analysis (SCENIC)

Gene regulatory networks were inferred using pySCENIC (v.0.12.1) with default parameters and the –all_modules flag to include negative regulons23. Input matrices excluded ribosomal, mitochondrial, and lowly expressed genes. To reduce stochasticity, the pipeline was run 10 times, retaining regulons present in ≥30% of runs. Regulon activity (AUC) was averaged across runs. Differentially active regulons were identified using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Significant regulons met the following criteria: TF–AUC correlation ≥0.2, z-score ≥1.5, fold change ≥1.25, and expression in ≥10% of cells.

Gene regulatory network inference (SCENIC + )

Enhancer-gene regulatory networks (eGRNs) were inferred using SCENIC+ (v.1.0.1) on Multiome data26. Topic modeling by Mallet used n_topic = [6,8,10,16,20], n_iter = 500, eta = 0.1; results from 8 topics were used for cisTarget analysis (database v10nr_clust). eGRNs were constructed using create_SCENICPLUS_object() and build_grn(). Significant interactions required Pearson correlation >0.5 between TF expression and gene-based cistrome AUC, and >0.5 between gene- and region-based cistromes.

In silico perturbation analysis

Perturbations were simulated using SCENIC+ on one representative Multiome sample. A Random Forest model predicted gene expression from eGRN activity scores. TF knockouts were simulated by setting TF expression to zero, with effects propagated over five iterations. Perturbed cells were projected onto PCA space derived from gene-based cistromes, and shifts along PC0 (associated with EMT states) were quantified. Trajectory bifurcation was not recovered, likely due to limited hybrid EMT representation and simulation constraints.

Cis-regulatory elements (CRE) and target gene prediction

Results from three Multiome datasets were aggregated using the mean and the absolute maximum scores for the TF2G and R2G metrics. Regulatory links were retained if present in ≥2/3 samples. Target gene signatures were defined using thresholds: TF2G_agg ≥0.060, TF2G_abs_max ≥0.160, and R2G_agg ≥0.035.

RNA extraction

RNA was extracted from FACS-isolated cells or culture lines using RNeasy Micro Kit (QIAGEN) following manufacturer instructions.

Bulk RNA sequencing

RNA quality was assessed using a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent). Indexed libraries were prepared with Ovation Solo RNA-seq (NuGen) following manufacturer instructions. Libraries (11 or 18 pM) were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) using HiSeq PE Cluster Kit v4 and TruSeq SBS Kit v3-HS (250 cycles), generating ~20 M paired-end reads per sample. Quality control was performed with FastQC. Adapters and low-quality bases were trimmed using Trimmomatic. Reads were aligned to mm10 (GRCm38) using STAR, and duplicates were removed with Picard MarkDuplicates. Gene annotations (Ensembl GRCm38.87) were used for quantification. Gene counts were obtained with HTSeq and normalized to 20 M aligned reads. Two biological replicates per condition (n = 2) were averaged and fold changes computed between control (empty vector) and knockout samples in EPCAM+ and EPCAM- fractions. Genes were filtered for ≥2 samples with reads, total count across samples ≥10, ≥5 reads in controls, and removal of pseudogenes, ribosomal genes, and unannotated predicted genes (15,575 genes retained from 50,000).

Differential gene expression (DGE) was assessed using a paired z-score test. DEGs were defined as FC ≥ 2, ≥10 reads in the higher group, and z ≥ 0.90. Ranked gene lists that combined fold changes and z-scores were used for GSEA. Knockout signatures were derived from DEGs, while SCENIC+ signatures were aggregated across three Multiome datasets. Signature enrichment was assessed by (i) overlap analysis ( ≥ 5 intersecting genes, Jaccard ≥0.005, Fisher p ≤ 0.05) and (ii) GSEA using fgsea53 (two-sided, p ≤ 0.05, NES ≥ 0.5).

Mouse pancreatic adenocarcinoma analysis

Processed 10x data (GSE173958) from sample M1 were downloaded, retaining final singlets. Analysis was performed with Scanpy (v1.10.2)42. Cells with >4000 genes and <20% mitochondrial reads were kept (14,452 total cells before QC; 11,919 cells after QC filtering). Data were log-normalized, regressed for cell cycle, library size, and mitochondrial content, then scaled. Highly variable genes were used for PCA. Significant PCs (up to 60) were selected by bootstrapping. Batch correction (Harmony v1.2.1), Leiden clustering, and UMAP were applied. Cluster stability (clustree v0.5.1)45 identified resolution 0.4 as the lowest stable resolution. Marker genes were defined by Wilcoxon test ( ≥ 25% expression, logFC ≥0.25). Clusters were annotated using canonical markers for epithelial (Epcam, Cdh1, Muc1, Krt8), mesenchymal (Vim, Sparc, Prrx1, Snai1, Col3a1, Mmp19), and hybrid states. SCENIC analysis followed the pipeline described above. Gene signatures were scored using AUCell (decoupler)54 and visualized on UMAPs or heatmaps.
