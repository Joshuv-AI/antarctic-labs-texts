A monoclonal antibody-based, time-resolved fluorescence microsphere immunochromatographic testing strip for rapid and sensitive detection of H7 avian influenza viruses
Authors: Han Wu, Ping Wang, Jiamin Fu, Fan Yang, Jing Guo, Linfang Cheng, Fumin Liu, Linwei Zhu, Hangping Yao, Nanping Wu, Lihua Xu, Haibo Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAvian influenza viruses (AIVs), particularly the H7N9 subtype, pose a persistent threat to global health security, having caused 1,687 human infections and 615 deaths to date. The significant socioeconomic impact and pandemic potential of H7N9 underscore the urgent need for rapid, reliable diagnostic tools to facilitate early intervention and control measures.MethodsThis study conducted on 2025 developed and optimized a time-resolved fluorescence microsphere immunochromatographic strip (TRFICS) assay for the rapid detection of H7 AIVs. The assay was constructed using two specific monoclonal antibodies, 2H9 and 1H9. Comprehensive evaluations were conducted to assess the assay’s sensitivity, specificity, reproducibility, and stability. Furthermore, clinical utility was validated by comparing TRFICS results with reverse transcription quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) using field samples (collected from 2013 to 2025).ResultsThe optimized TRFICS assay achieved limits of detection (LOD) of 2–5 hemagglutination units (HAU) for H7N9 in allantoic fluid and 0.01 ng/mL for purified H7N9 hemagglutinin protein within 15 min. The assay demonstrated 100% concordance with RT-qPCR results in field sample testing. High specificity was observed with no cross-reactivity against other prevalent pathogens. Additionally, the assay exhibited excellent repeatability (relative standard deviation < 8%) and maintained stable performance under various storage conditions.ConclusionThe developed TRFICS assay represents a robust, sensitive, and rapid platform for the on-site identification of H7 AIVs. Its superior performance characteristics make it a valuable tool for effective surveillance and timely response to potential H7 AIV pandemics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05447-2.

Introduction

Avian influenza viruses (AIVs) continue to pose a formidable and evolving threat to both animal and human health worldwide. The AIV genome comprises eight gene segments: haemagglutinin (HA), neuraminidase (NA), and six internal genes. Thus far, AIVs bearing the H5, H7, or H9 HA gene have demonstrated widespread dissemination and have been detected across various regions worldwide. This demonstrates the potential of AIVs to infect mammalian species in the future, possibly leading to large-scale epidemics [1].

In contrast to other AIV subtypes that have rarely infected humans, the H7N9 AIV has led to 1,687 human infection cases globally [2]. Depending on pathogenicity, AIVs can be classified into two categories: low pathogenic AIVs (LPAIVs) and highly pathogenic AIVs (HPAIVs). Compared with LPAIVs, HPAIVs can lead to serious illnesses and high mortalities in poultry, resulting in considerable socioeconomic losses. Regardless of their pathogenicity, human H7 AIV infection cases have been sporadic after exposure to infected poultry. In most cases, the patients presented with mild respiratory symptoms. However, during the 2003 H7N7 HPAIV epidemic in the Netherlands, one death was reported [3]. The H7N9 LPAIV emerged in China in February 2013, triggering several outbreaks of zoonotic infections, spanning five distinct epidemic waves that resulted in numerous deaths in humans; as of June 2025, 615 related deaths have been confirmed, resulting in a case-fatality rate of approximately 39% [4]. In early 2017, the H7N9 LPAIV transitioned into the H7N9 HPAIV, leading to a major outbreak in birds; as such, mass poultry immunization campaigns were implemented [5]. However, despite these eradication efforts, H7N9 AIVs remain in circulation, highlighting the need to establish reliable and sensitive detection strategies.

Currently, two approaches are commonly used for AIV detection: enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) [6] and reverse transcription quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) [7]. Despite their outstanding accuracy and sensitivity, these techniques require expensive equipment, complex preprocessing steps and trained personnel; as such, these methods are inapplicable for swift, on-site AIV detection. While our previous work demonstrated the utility of quantum dots in AIV diagnostics [8–10], the current study focuses on time-resolved fluorescence immunochromatographic assay (TRFICA), a novel alternative for rapid AIV detection, in which rare earth elements are used as fluorescent labels. The approach affords high sensitivity, simplicity, and easy visualization, making it suitable for point-of-care testing.

TRFICA has been broadly applied in the detection of pharmaceuticals and toxins, particularly food and environmental residues [11, 12]. Recently, TRFICA has been gradually implemented for the monoclonal antibody (mAb)-driven detection of viruses, such as bovine herpesvirus-1 [13] and SARS-CoV-2 [14], indicating the applicability of the method to infectious disease diagnosis. Time-resolved fluorescent microspheres (TRFMs) are novel signal probes that form the cornerstone of the TRFICA; they possess distinctive fluorescence properties, which intensify signals and elevate assay sensitivity [15]. Compared with other labeling materials, TRFMs exhibit stronger fluorescence intensity with larger stokes shift (> 150 nm) and extended fluorescence life time. As such, these probes delay the measurement time, enabling the removal of nonspecific fluorescence interference [16]. In addition, TRFMs encapsulate thousands of fluorescent molecules within each particle, which substantially boosts labeling efficiency and reduces the marker volume [17]. Moreover, attaching reactive groups, such as carboxyl, to the outer surface of TRFMs can improve the stability of the antibody–TRFM conjugate [18]. In general, the advantages of TRFICA include relatively fast detection, high sensitivity, simplicity, broad standard curve range, strong environmental adaptability, and prolonged storage stability of labeling compounds. Moreover, the ongoing advancements in TRFICA have enabled swift, efficient H7 AIV identification, thereby enhancing surveillance and infection control.

In this study, we developed a time-resolved fluorescence microsphere immunochromatographic testing strip (TRFICS) assay for the rapid and sensitive detection of H7 subtype AIVs. Using two highly specific monoclonal antibodies, 2H9 and 1H9, we established a diagnostic platform designed to address the limitations of conventional rapid tests. The assay’s performance was evaluated in terms of sensitivity, specificity, and stability. Furthermore, its clinical applicability was validated using field samples collected between 2013 and 2025 in comparison with the gold-standard RT-qPCR method. This study aims to provide a robust and portable tool for the large-scale screening and early surveillance of H7 AIVs.

Materials and methods

Viruses, cell lines, and materials

In this study, we included wild-type AIVs identified and isolated at our laboratory between 2013 and 2018 [19]—namely A/Zhejiang/DTID-ZJU01/2013 (H7N9) (ZJU01), A/duck/Zhejiang/DK10/2013 (H7N3), A/chicken/Jiangxi/C25/2014 (H7N7), and A/Guangdong/HP001/2017 (H7N9) (HP001) —along with other viruses maintained in our laboratory, all stored at −80 °C (Table 1). These viruses were propagated in 9-day-old specific pathogen-free (SPF) embryonated chicken eggs, and the virus titers were evaluated using a 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) assay at harvest, as described previously [19]. Highly pathogenic AIVs (H5 and H7) research was conducted under Biosafety Level 3 containment; all other viral studies were performed in BSL-2 facilities. These operations were centralized at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University. SP2/0 mouse myeloma cells and Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cell lines were cultured in our laboratory. For immunization, we procured inactivated AIV vaccines (split virion), containing 30 µg/mL HA from the A/ZJU01/PR8/2013 strain [20], from Zhejiang Tianyuan Bio-Pharmaceutical. Purified recombinant H7N9 (A/Zhejiang/DTID-ZJU01/2013) HA protein was obtained from Sino Biological (Beijing, China). Finally, H7-Re 3, a reassortant strain containing surface genes from A/chicken/Inner Mongolia/SD010/2019 (H7N9) and six internal segments, was derived from the high-yield A/Puerto Rico/8/1934 (H1N1) (PR8) strain. Moreover, H7-Re 4, a reassortant strain, was generated using the HA and NA genes from A/chicken/Yunnan/SD024/2021 (H7N9), combined with the internal genes from the PR8 strain [21].

Viruses included in this study

Murine H7 mAb production and characterization

Five 6-week-old BALB/c mice (20 g each, female) were obtained from Hangzhou Medical College, according to previous research [22]. All procedures involving animals were performed in accordance with the ethical guidelines of Zhejiang University and formally approved by the university’s Animal Ethics Committee. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, we immunized each mouse by intramuscularly administering 200 µL of the H7N9 vaccine (A/ZJU01/PR8/2013) 2 weeks apart. Finally, a booster immunization dose was administered for subsequent cell fusion procedures [19]. In brief, spleen cells from select mice with highest serum antibody concentration were fused with Sp2/0 myeloma cells; then, positive hybridoma cells were isolated using the limiting dilution technique. The Protein-G column (GE Healthcare) was adopted to purify target mAbs from mouse ascites, and the harvested mAbs were preserved at −80 °C. Furthermore, the subtypes and subclasses of the mAbs were assessed using a mouse monoclonal antibody isotyping kit (Bio-Rad), whereas the heavy and light chains of these mAbs were sequenced by SinoBiological (Beijing, China).

Elisa

We coated 96-well plates with purified H7N9 proteins (20ng/well) overnight at 4 °C, as indicated previously [12]. Each well was then supplemented with 100µL of supernatants from selected hybridomas, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti mouse IgG (Novus) for signal detection. Next, the absorbance of each well at 450 nm was measured on a plate reader (Bio-Rad, USA). Similarly, the binding affinities of the mAbs were analyzed using an ELISA protocol detailed previously [23]. The mAbs were first prepared at a concentration of 10 µg/mL and subsequently diluted twofold serially. Each dilution was then analyzed using the aforementioned ELISA methodology.

Hemagglutination assay and hemagglutination inhibition assay

To confirm the presence and titers of the viruses, hemagglutination assays were performed, as described previously [24]. H7 AIVs (50µL), including H7N9, H7N7, and H7N3, were serially diluted twofold; next, 50µL of 1% red blood cells (RBCs) was added, and the suspension was observed. The hemagglutination inhibition (HI) titer represents the reciprocal of the maximum dilution at which an antibody sample fully prevents hemagglutination. We serially diluted our mAbs two-fold in a 25-µL volume, starting from 1 mg/mL. Next, 22 HAU of virus particles were added at 25 µL/well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 20 min. Finally, at 30 min after incubation, 1% chicken RBCs were added at 50 µL/well to assess HI [25].

Microneutralizationassay

We performed a microneutralization (MN) assay, as described previously [19]. Purified H7 mAbs were serially diluted twofold, starting from 100 µg/mL. They were then mixed with equal volumes of virus solution containing 100 TCID50 of H7N9, H7N7, and H7N3 AIVs, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, this virus–antibody mixture was supplemented with TPCK-treated trypsin (Worthington). Next, this mixture was inoculated onto cultured MDCK cells, followed by incubation at 37 °C for another 72 h. Cytopathic effects were monitored visually, and the half-maximal inhibitory concentrations of the mAbs (i.e., antibody concentrations required to inhibit viral replication by 50%) were determined using the Reed and Muench method [26], which interpolates the 50% endpoint between the two dilutions flanking the 50% neutralization mark. Specifically, the proportional distance was calculated and then applied to the log-transformed mAb concentrations to determine the precise half-maximal inhibitory concentrations value.

Immunofluorescence assay

MDCK cells seeded onto 96-well plates at 4 × 104 cells/well were infected with the viruses (multiplicity of infection = 0.5), as described previously [27], followed by cultivation for 24 h and then by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde. Subsequently, 0.5% Triton X-100 was added to permeabilize the cells at 25 °C for 30 min. Next, the mAbs were added to the plates at 10 µg/mL each, with the H1-specific mAb 2B2included as the control [28]. Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti mouse IgG (Abcam) were added as secondary antibodies, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. Finally, fluorescence imaging was performed under a SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica, Germany).

Escape mutation isolation and characterization

MAb escape mutant generation was assessed according to egg-based selection [29]. In brief, a mixture containing 75µL of H7N9 AIV (28 HAU) and an equal volume of mAbs at varying concentrations was introduced into 9-day-old SPF chicken embryos in two rounds. The positive allantoic fluids were extracted and analyzed by sequencing to detect amino acid changes in the HA region, the sequences of the primers used were listed in Table S1. Next, the selected mutant epitopes were represented using Open-Source PyMOL (version 2.5.0, Schrödinger) on the crystal structure retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB).

HA sequence alignment, distribution, and phylogenetic analyses

We downloaded 5,464 complete HA amino acid sequences of H7 AIVs (up to June 2025) from various host species from the GISAID to analyze the amino acid distribution at the escape mutation site. To ensure the robustness of the conservation analysis, we performed a data-cleaning pipeline using RStudio software (version 2025, USA) with the Biostrings package. Subsequently, sequence alignment was performed using MAFFT (version 7.0, Japan) [30] to identify variations at the target position, screened using Jalview (version 2.11.4) [31]. A comprehensive phylogenetic analysis was conducted on representative H7 AIVs’ HA gene sequences, collected over 1988–2025 and retrieved from the GISAID, by using the neighbor-joining method implemented in MEGA (version 11, New Zealand) [32]. Branch reliability was rigorously evaluated using 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Gene segment clusters in the resulting phylogenetic trees were categorized based on a stringent sequence identity cutoff of 95%, ensuring consistency and reliability in lineage designation.

Fabrication of TRFICS based on H7 mAbs

In general, we fabricated TRFICS based on the double antibody sandwich principle [13]. As illustrated in Fig. 1B, the samples (containing H7 AIVs or purified H7 proteins) applied to the sample pad coated with TRFMS-labeled antibodies were allowed to migrate along a test strip via capillary action. The TRFMS-H7 mAb conjugate was captured by the immobilized antibody to form a distinct fluorescent band, referred to as the test (T) line. The overflowing, unbound TRFMS-labeled monoclonal antibodies were captured by goat anti mouse IgG to form the control (C) line. If an H7 component existed within a sample, it combined with the labeled antibodies to emit a detectable fluorescent signal, diminishing the quantity available for capture at the C line. Fluorescence was clearly visible under 365-nm UV light with the naked eye. A fluorescence test strip scanner was also used to detect the captured TRFMs.

To produce TRFM-labeled antibodies, 80µL of 210 nm Eu3+-TRFMs (Microdetection, Nanjing, China) were dissolved in 420 µL of 25 mM 4-morpholineethanesulfonic acid (MES; pH 6.0; Sigma-Aldrich). The carboxyl groups on the microspheres were then activated using 15 µL of 10 mg/mL N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS; Sigma-Aldrich) and 15 µL of 10 mg/mL 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at room temperature for 20 min, followed by centrifugation (15,000 rpm, 10 min). Next, 0.2 mg of 2H9 mAbs were introduced into the mixture to couple to the microspheres in 400 µL of MES buffer, followed by incubation for 2 h. We then added another 400 µL of 10% bovine serum albumin (Sangon Biotechnology) for 2 h to block any active sites and then removed any superfluous chemicals and unreacted antibodies through centrifugation. The precipitate was resuspended in 180µL of MES buffer to prepare it for application onto glass cellulose membranes (Jiening Biotechnology). Subsequently, 1 mg/mL goat anti mouse IgG (Solarbio) at the C line and 3.5 mg/mL 1H9 mAb at the T line were individually dispensed onto a nitrocellulose (NC) membrane (Sartorius) by using the BioDotXYZ platform (Jinbiao Biotechnology) at a flow rate of 1µL/cm. To create the final immunochromatographic testing strip, we layered the treated NC membrane, a sample pad, and an absorbent pad onto a PVC backing plate and then cut the assembly into 3.5 mm-wide strips on an automated cutting machine. These strips were maintained at 4–8 °C until use. Fluorescence signals emitted by the strips were directly observed under 365-nm ultraviolet light and quantitatively analyzed under a fluorescence test strip scanner (Hemai Technology, China).

TRFICS assay optimization and performance assessment

TRFICS assay optimization

The effects of critical factors, including activation buffer pH (0.25 mM MES buffer at pH 5.0, 6.0, and 7.0), TRFM-conjugated mAb quantity (0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 mg), and fluorescence microsphere dosage (60, 70, and 80µL), on the performance of TRFICS assay were systematically assessed to optimize its signal strength and detection sensitivity. To analyze each condition, we performed three repetitions. For each experiment, the T line fluorescence values were measured under the fluorescence test strip scanner. The working conditions were confirmed to be optimal when the fluorescence intensity values were the largest.

TRFICS assay performance assessment

TRFICS assay performance was assessed in terms of sensitivity, specificity, reproducibility, and stability [33].

Sensitivity

TRFICS sensitivity was evaluated by determining the strips’ qualitative and quantitative LODs. In brief, 80 µL of allantoic fluid was serially diluted using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 2− 6 to 23 HAU of H7N9 AIV (A/Zhejiang/DTID-ZJU01/2013), and 0.002–1.25 ng/mL of purified H7 HA protein was added to the TRFICS. Each dilution was tested five times. Each strip was viewed with the naked eye (qualitative) and scanned under the fluorescence test strip scanner (quantitative) after 15 min of exposure. For both observation methods, a positive result at the highest dilution was used to define the strips’ LODs. For the quantitative analysis, the average fluorescence intensity at the highest dilution was also considered the LOD. To plot a H7 AIVs TRFICS standard curve, the fluorescence intensity of purified H7 HA protein at the T line was calculated at various concentrations and added to the y-axis; the corresponding fluorescence intensity values on the T line were added to the x-axis. Finally, the linear fitting equation and linear range were determined using GraphPad Prism (version 10, USA).

Specificity

We assessed the specificity of our TRFICS assay using closely related AIVs (H1N1, H1N2, H1N9, H2N8, H3N2, H3N3, H3N6, H3N8, H4N2, H4N6, H5N1, H5N2, H5N6, H5N8, H6N1, H6N2, H6N6, H7N3, H7N7, H7N9, H9N2, H10N2, H10N3, H10N5, H10N7, H10N8, H11N3, and H11N9), along with other avian viruses, infectious bronchitis virus (IBV; H120 and H52), avian paramyxovirus-4 (APMV-4; ZJ-1), infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV; B87 and NF8), infectious laryngotracheitis virus (ILTV; K317), marek’s disease virus (MDV; FC-126), newcastle disease virus (NDV; Clone 30 and La Sota), avian pox virus (APV; quail-adapted strain)]. Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test to determine the significance of differences between the H7-positive groups and the negative control.

Reproducibility

To verify the reproducibility of the TRFICS assay, each test strip was tested 20 times repeatedly with 80 µL of the aforementioned allantoic fluid containing H7N9 AIVs across a gradient of HA titers (ranging from 2− 4 to 22 HAU) diluted with PBS. The corresponding average absorbance values and relative standard deviation (RSD) were evaluated.

Stability

TRFICS from the same batch were sealed in foil pouches and stored at 4 °C, room temperature, and 37 °C for 1, 7, 14, and 30 days. Then, twofold-diluted allantoic fluid containing H7 AIVs was used to measure sensitivity at each 10th repetition, and the strip stability was evaluated by determining the LOD under each condition [34].

TRFICS assay-based evaluation of real samples

To evaluate the practical performance of our TRFICS assay, 130 field samples (including 40 throat swabs and 90 fecal specimens) were collected from live poultry markets. Among these, 50 samples were spiked with H7 AIVs at various concentrations (2− 6, 2− 5, 2− 4, 2− 3, 2− 2, 2− 1, 20, 21, 22, and 23 HAU per 80µL), while the remaining 80 samples remained unspiked. All samples were then analyzed using both RT-qPCR and the TRFICS assay; here, RT-qPCR results were considered the reference standards for comparison, which was conducted using a commercial influenza A virus detection kit (Liferiver), adhering to established protocols [35]. Before analysis, all field samples were first resuspended in PBS, centrifuged, and then preserved at −80 °C for long-term storage. Each experiment was repeated three times to ensure the robustness of the results. Moreover, the correlation between the TRFICS assay and RT-qPCR results for H7 AIVs was analyzed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient [36] on GraphPad Prism. Of all field samples, 35 were confirmed to be positive, and they were subsequently used for further evaluation of the TRFICS assay. Moreover, a total of 74 human clinical samples, including 63 throat swabs and 11 sputum specimens, were collected for the evaluation. These samples included cases previously confirmed as positive for H7N9 AIVs, other respiratory viruses (such as H1N1, H3N2, influenza B, adenoviruses, rhinoviruses, and SARS-CoV-2), or negative controls, as determined by RT-qPCR. Statistical analysis was carried out to evaluate the performance of TRFICS method with Graphpad.

Results

Characteristics of generated mAbs targeting H7 AIVs

We generated a pair of H7 mAbs (2H9 and 1H9) using the hybridoma method; here, the 2H9 mAb functioned as the capture antibody, whereas the 1H9 mAb acted as the detection antibody. Table 2 presents the results of their isotype identification: both 2H9 and 1H9 mAbs could be classified within the IgG2a subclass. Table 2 also provides a summary of the amino acid sequences of the heavy- and light-chain variable regions of the mAbs. ELISA-based affinity analysis revealed a strong binding affinity of both mAbs to the HA protein of A/Zhejiang/DTID-ZJU01/2013 (H7N9; Table 2). Phylogenetic and mutational antigenic profile

After two rounds of selection, HI-positive allantoic supernatants were collected and subjected to sequencing. Based on H3 hemagglutinin numbering (used throughout the article), escape mutants selected by 2H9 mAbs carried a mutation at N167D residue, whereas those selected by 1H9 mAbs exhibited a single mutation at G132E residue—both located in the receptor-binding domain (RBD). To visually present the results, the protein structure of the H7N9 AIV (A/Shanghai/2/2013) was downloaded from the PDB. Different antigenic epitopes on the HA protein were highlighted using distinct color codes (Fig. 3A). Moreover, to evaluate the genetic diversity and evolutionary relationships of the target viruses, a phylogenetic tree was constructed (Fig. 3B). A global cohort of H7 AIV strains (2013–2025) downloaded from the GISAID was analyzed to characterize the breadth of our antibody candidates. Strains used for validation (red triangles) span the major circulating clades, ensuring high genetic diversity. Of note, the C25 (H7N7) virus exhibited clear phylogenetic separation from the other H7 strains, representing a unique antigenic profile within the study’s validation panel. Referencing the five antigenic regions (A-E) established via the H3 numbering system [38], alignment of representative H7 AIVs (Fig. 3C) identified the G132E and N167D substitutions as residing in antigenic sites A and B, respectively.

Optimized TRFICS assay parameters

During fluorescent microsphere activation, the pH value significantly affects both the labeling efficiency and antibody functionality [33], mainly influencing microsphere dispersionin the buffer [39]. Thus, the pH of the activation buffer (25 mM MES) was systematically adjusted to 5.5, 6.0, and 6.5 using NaOH. As illustrated in Fig. 4A, at pH 6.0, the fluorescence intensity was at an optimal level. As the pH increased further, the intensity subsequently declined; this was because high activation pH led to incomplete fluorescent probe removal. As such, we considered pH 6.0 as the optimal buffer pH for the TRFICS assay.

Optimization of H7 TRFICS assay working conditions with adjustments in A MES buffer activation pH values, B conjugated mAb amounts, and C TRFM probe concentrations (n = 3). Data are presented as means ± SD from three independent experiments

When the quantity of fluorescent microspheres is fixed, insufficient or excessive amounts of conjugated mAbs can compromise probe quality: insufficient amounts of mAbs can reduce labeling efficiency, whereas excessive amounts of mAbs can increase steric hindrance [16]. As shown in Fig. 4B, as the antibody concentration was increased, the fluorescence signal intensified gradually in a dose-dependent manner. As the mAb concentration range increased further, the background signal of the strips increased, indicating that oversized cross-linked microsphere particles blocked the pore size of the membrane, which led to a saturation state and therefore considerable background interference. Thus, we considered 0.2 mg as the optimal mAb concentration for our TRFICS assay.

The amount of fluorescence microspheres can also influence the fluorescence intensity and sensitivity of the TRFICS significantly. At excessive concentrations, fluorescence microspheres can increase mAb wastage, reduce sensitivity, and cause background interference, consequently diminishing detection accuracy [40]. As illustrated in Fig. 4C, the optimal TRFMs probe volume was systematically determined to within 60–80µL because at these volumes the probe concentration was positively correlated with the fluorescence intensity. As such, 80µL was determined to be the optimal working volume of TRFMs for our TRFICS assay.

TRFICS assay performance

Sensitivity

Under optimized experimental conditions, the cutoff value of the TRFICS assay was evaluated using serially diluted allantoic fluid containing H7N9 (ranging from 2− 6 to 23 HAU) and purified H7N9 HA protein across a concentration range of 0.002–1.25 ng/mL. A substantial positive correlation was observed between fluorescence intensity and purified H7 HA protein concentration. The LOD of the aforementioned allantoic fluid was 2− 5 HAU of H7N9 AIVs (Fig. 5A) and 0.01 ng/mL purified H7N9 HA protein (Fig. 5B). Therefore, the LOD of corresponding fluorescence intensity was determined as the average absorbance value at the above detection limitation values, which was 2500 absorbance units. Samples with values exceeding the detection threshold were classified as positive. The linear regression equation for purified H7N9 HA protein detection is y = 4.136e-006x − 0.0005967, with a correlation coefficient (R2) of 0.9954 (Fig. 5C). Moreover, when PBS was applied as the coated antigen to eliminate the nonspecific adsorption, a very low fluorescence signal was observed at the T line. This indicated that the fluorescence intensity changes were primarily due to specific interactions between the immobilized antibodies and the viral antigens.

Specificity

To assess the cross-reactivity of our TRFICS, a panel of AIVs (H1N1, H1N2, H1N9, H2N8, H3N2, H3N3, H3N6, H3N8, H4N2, H4N6, H5N1, H5N2, H5N6, H5N8, H6N1, H6N2, H6N6, H7N3, H7N7, H7N9, H9N2, H10N2, H10N3, H10N5, H10N7, H10N8, H11N3, and H11N9) and other avian respiratory viruses (NDV, IBV, IBDV, APMV-4, MDV, ILTV, and APV) were evaluated on the strips. The typical representative subtypes of each strain were imaged (Fig. 6). Statistical analysis using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test revealed that the fluorescence intensities of the H7-positive groups were significantly higher than those of the 16 non-H7 viruses and the negative control (****P < 0.0001). No cross-reactivity was observed with other respiratory viruses, confirming the high analytical specificity of the developed assay for H7 AIV detection.

Validation of H7 TRFICS assay using real samples

To evaluate the clinical performance of the TRFICS assay, a total of 204 samples were analyzed, consisting of 130 spiked poultry samples (fecal specimens and throat swabs) and 74 naturally infected human clinical samples (throat swabs and sputum specimens). Of all included human samples, 74 (63 throat swab samples and 11 sputum specimens) were confirmed to be positive through RT-qPCR. Specifically, these included 3 samples for H7N9 AIVs, 17 for H1N1, 17 for H3N2, 3 for adenoviruses, 2 for rhinoviruses, 2 for respiratory syncytial viruses, 5 for influenza B, and 25 for SARS-CoV-2 [7, 10]. For the 130 poultry field samples, 35 (20 fecal specimens and 15 throat swabs) were identified to be H7N9-positive through RT-qPCR, with cycle threshold values of 15–32. Viral loads quantified using the TRFICS assay and RT-qPCR were strongly correlated (R2 = 0.9794; Fig. 7). These 35 positive spiked samples, along with three human H7N9-positive samples, were used as the reference standards for comparison with the results of our TRFICS assay to assess the assay’s accuracy. As presented in Table 6, the TRFICS assay showed a 100% coincidence rate with RT-qPCR for both positive and negative results. The calculated sensitivity was 100.0% (38/38; 95% CI: 90.8%−100.00%), and the specificity was 100.0% (166/166; 95% CI: 97.8%−100.00%). The overall accuracy was 100.0% (204/204; 95% CI: 98.2%−100.00%). These results demonstrate that the TRFICS assay is highly reliable for H7 AIV detection across different sample matrices.

Discussion

Given the theoretical risk that H7 AIVs could adapt for human-to-human transmission in immunologically naïve populations [29], the development of sensitive diagnostic tools is essential for mitigating potential public health impacts. Thus far, studies have focused on the rapid H7 AIV detection using various established nucleic acid-based methods: multiplex polymerase chain reaction for real-time detection and differentiation of several influenza viruses [41], reverse transcription loop-mediated isothermal amplification method with sensitivity higher than that of the method recommended by the World Health Organization [42], and a novel CRISPR/Cas13a method combined with RT-qPCR [43]. Although the detection sensitivity, specificity, and speed of these methods are higher than those of traditional methods such as viral isolation and typing, they require large equipment, limiting their in-field application. Another widespread strategy for virus detection is based on protein interaction, mainly involving antibody–antigen reactions, such as mAb-based antigen-capture ELISA [44]. These methods are typically immunochromatographic and have favorable characteristics such as high sensitivity, specificity, and straightforward implementation, making them suitable for on-site, real-time detection of infectious diseases [45].

Here, we developed an immunochromatographic assay based on mAbs generated in-house. Through escape epitope selection, we identified the two mAb binding sites, which were the molecular foundation of our assay. Both sites, 132 (1H9) and 167 (2H9), are part of the RBS, a region considered a potential target for broad neutralizing antibodies [46]. Notably, this antibody pair demonstrated robust HI activity and substantial neutralizing activity against a range of H7 AIV subtypes, indicating its broad-spectrum efficacy. The identification of two mutation sites with > 98% conservation highlights their critical role as stable functional anchors for H7 antigen recognition. Such conservation is pivotal for guiding the design of next-generation antivirals and immunogens. Furthermore, our phylogenetic analysis, spanning a temporal spectrum from early isolates to contemporary Re3 and Re4 strains, substantiates the broad-spectrum applicability of the TRFICS assay. The ability of the selected mAbs to consistently detect these evolutionarily divergent strains indicates their binding to antigenic sites that are refractory to variation. This stability ensures the assay’s enduring efficacy against newly emerging variants, effectively mitigating the threat of diagnostic escape.

By conjugating TRFMs with the labeled mAbs, the detection qualities were improved in sensitivity, with LODs of 2− 5 HAU and 0.01 ng/mL purified H7N9 HA protein using fluorescence scanner—higher than traditional colloidal gold-based immunochromatographic assay reported previously [44, 47], with Lanthanide-doped TRFMs have been widely recognized for their ability to improve assay performance. In contrast to other fluorescent particles [10], TRFMs minimize background fluorescence and instability with enhanced sensitivity, extended fluorescence lifetime, and excellent linearity [48]. Optimal loading of europium (III), a lanthanide complex, into carboxylate-functionalized polystyrene microspheres significantly improves fluorescent labeling efficiency, effectively minimizing background interference [49]. The successful application of time-resolved fluorescence in both H10 [50] and H7 detection (this study) confirms the robustness of TRFMs-based optical signaling for diverse AIV subtypes. Moreover, our mAbs demonstrated no cross-reactivity with a panel of other prevalent viral pathogens, confirming their specificity for H7 AIVs and supporting their use in targeted diagnostic assays. Notably, qualified conformance between the developed method and RT-qPCR, the gold standard technique for AIV detection, confirmed the excellent diagnostic accuracy of our H7 TRFICS assay. Despite the promising performance of the H7 TRFICS assay, several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the limit of detection (LOD) was defined via HAU and purified proteins rather than infectious viral titers (TCID50), as HAU quantifies both viable and non-viable particles, further research is required to determine the assay’s precise sensitivity for active infections. Second, due to the scarcity of commercial H7-specific rapid kits, a head-to-head performance comparison was not feasible. Furthermore, the limited clinical cohort (n = 3) necessitates further large-scale validation against existing diagnostic standards to fully ascertain the assay’s clinical utility and comparative superiority in naturally infected patients. And lastly, although the TRFICS assay demonstrated stability at ambient and 37 °C temperatures for 60 days, long-term viability remains to be fully established.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully developed and characterized the H7 mAbs 2H9 and 1H9. To maximize TRFICS assay performance, key parameters such as activation pH, antibody dosage, and TRFM probe concentration were optimized on lateral flow test strips. Consequently, a rapid, easy-to-use TRFICS assay was established by conjugating TRFMs with H7 mAbs, with LODs of 2− 5 HAU and 0.01 ng/mL. The method demonstrated high specificity, good reproducibility, and excellent stability. Moreover, in real sample analysis, the accuracy of the TRFICS assay closely matched that of RT-qPCR. Therefore, our novel TRFICS platform is a reliable and efficient tool for H7 AIVs detection, enabling early diagnosis and targeted control of influenza outbreaks.
