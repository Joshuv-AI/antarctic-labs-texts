Romosozumab in severe postmenopausal osteoporosis—real-world data from 192 women
Authors: Nicolas Dehne, Felix N. von Brackel, Claudia Günzl, Florian Barvencik, Ralf Oheim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryRomosozumab, used to treat severe osteoporosis, still lacks real-world treatment data. We observed significant gains in spinal and hip bone mineral density, as well as improvements in trabecular microarchitecture. These real-world observations corroborate controlled trials and provide new insights into sequential therapy effects and the management of severe osteoporosis.PurposeOsteoporosis is a common disease that leads to fractures causing immobility and pain. Pharmacological therapy plays a pivotal role in osteoporosis management with a growing number of approved drugs. Romosozumab, an anti-sclerostin antibody, is approved in several countries for the treatment of severe osteoporosis in postmenopausal women. Clinical trials have demonstrated significant gain of areal bone mineral density (aBMD) in these patients over 12 months of treatment; however, real-world data is still limited.MethodsFrom 2020 to 2024, 192 postmenopausal women with severe osteoporosis treated with romosozumab were identified at our institution. Clinical data from a 24-month observation period were analyzed, comprising aBMD by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), bone microstructure by high-resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography (HR-pQCT), and biochemical bone turnover markers.ResultsPatients treated with romosozumab showed significant gains in spinal (L1–L4) and hip aBMD (lumbar spine: 14.47 ± 8.74%, p < 0.001; total hip: 4.15 ± 5.92%, p < 0.001), accompanied by an increase in bone formation parameters after 6 months and an increase in trabecular microarchitectural parameters assessed by HR-pQCT in 12-month follow-up. These changes were significantly lower in patients with previous bone specific treatment. Treatment-naïve (TN) patients benefited significantly more than those previously treated with antiresorptive (AR) medication (lumbar spine aBMD change: TN: 17.74 ± 8.30%, AR: 10.44 ± 7.67%, p < 0.001). This was supported by the dependency of aBMD gain on baseline bone formation markers.ConclusionOur real-world data corroborate the results from controlled studies and provide new insights into microstructural changes as well as biochemical treatment monitoring. To fully evaluate sequential therapy, larger studies and longer observation times are needed in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00198-026-08127-x.

Introduction

As populations in developed countries continue to age, osteoporosis and the need for effective targeted treatments become increasingly important. Osteoporosis is characterized by low bone mass, impaired microarchitecture, an increased risk of fracture, and hence increased morbidity and mortality [1]. As osteoporosis is a systemic disease, systemic medical treatment plays a key role in best clinical practice with different antiresorptive and osteoanabolic treatment options available today.

Romosozumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody neutralizing sclerostin, approved in Europe for the treatment of severe osteoporosis in postmenopausal women. With its dual mechanism of both enhancing bone formation and (partially) inhibiting bone resorption, it is a promising new therapeutic option for osteoporosis therapy, particularly in severe cases. However, apart from the pivotal studies proving romosozumab’s effectiveness [2] [3] [4], real-world treatment data (i.e., routine clinical practice) remain limited [5] [6] [7]. Furthermore, the FRAME study excluded cases of osteoporosis with a previous hip fracture or any severe or more than two moderate vertebral fractures [2]. However, in clinical routine, osteoporosis is often diagnosed after sustaining a major osteoporotic fracture, by which time patients exhibit advanced osteoporosis and poor bone quality before receiving osteoanabolic therapy [8] [9] [10]. Here, we analyzed existing clinical data from women with severe, postmenopausal osteoporosis that were treated with romosozumab (on-label) at our outpatient clinic. We included microarchitectural bone parameters assessed by high-resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography (HR-pQCT), which we routinely perform at our center and where respective data remain very limited. In addition, we investigated potential factors influencing aBMD gain in our patients.

Methods

Participants

In this study, 192 patients with postmenopausal osteoporosis have been retrospectively examined for outcomes of romosozumab treatment at our institution (Department for Osteology and Biomechanics, University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf, Germany) between 2020 and 2024. For this study, only cases of severe postmenopausal osteoporosis were included following the EU label for romosozumab. Severe osteoporosis was defined in line with national and international guidelines by combining BMD T-scores and established risk factors such as (recent) osteoporotic fractures, glucocorticoid treatment, and risk of falls. According to the EU label, patients with previous cardiovascular events were ineligible for romosozumab treatment.

Eligible patients were women who received romosozumab and had DXA scans taken at the beginning and at the end of the 12-month treatment interval. Patients that did not receive the full 12 months of romosozumab were excluded from the statistical analysis. Patients had received various osteospecific medications prior to romosozumab treatment; for clarity, all bisphosphonates were summarized into one group, labeled BP. Patients received regular check-up examinations consisting of DXA scans as well as a biochemical analysis of bone turnover markers at baseline and 6, 12, and 24 months after (M0, M6, M12, M24). Additionally, we performed bone microarchitecture assessment using HR-pQCT. We further included available BMD data from previous DXA scans. This study was approved by the local ethics committee (#2024–300480-WF), and all investigations were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

aBMD assessment

DXA was used to assess the BMD of the lumbar spine and hip. Using a Lunar iDXA device (GE Healthcare, Madison, WI, USA), the scans were performed in accordance with international guidelines [11]. In accordance with international guidelines, all lumbar spine examinations were assessed for relevant degenerative changes or fractures (also via VFA) and the affected vertebrae excluded from aBMD analysis. The pre-treatment period was defined as the time between an existing DXA scan and treatment initiation. To include prior DXA scans from Hologic® Devices, the aBMD values were converted to Lunar iDXA values using the device’s recommended import and conversion option (GE Healthcare, Madison, WI, USA). We assessed the aBMD of the lumbar spine in L1–L4. The lowest spinal aBMD refers to the lowest aBMD measured in two adjacent vertebrae. In the hip, we evaluated total hip aBMD. Influences of treatment response were evaluated based on influence on 12-month aBMD increase in percentage from baseline value. Vertebral fracture analysis (VFA) was performed using the Lunar iDXA device at the initial clinical visit and repeated if patients lost height or reported back pain, to assess new vertebral fractures.

Laboratory assessment

Analyses of bone turnover markers were carried out at each follow-up visit by the local institute of clinical chemistry (Institute of Clinical Chemistry and Laboratory Medicine, University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf, Germany). Serum levels of bone turnover markers, i.e., bone specific alkaline phosphatase (BAP) and osteocalcin (OC), were measured by a Liaison XL device (DiaSorin, Stillwater, MN, USA). Procollagen type 1 n-terminal propeptide (P1NP) as well as β-C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX) were measured by a Cobas E411 (Roche Diagnostics, Rotkreuz, Switzerland). Urinary levels of deoxypyridinoline (DPD) were measured in spot urine by an Immulite-XP System (Siemens, Munich, Germany) and normalized to urinary creatinine. Serum levels of parathyroid hormone (PTH) and urinary creatinine were measured by an Atellica-Solution system (Siemens, Munich, Germany). Reference data were provided by the local laboratory cited above.

Bone microstructure assessment

High-resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography (HR-pQCT) measurements were performed at baseline and after 12 and 24 months of treatment. The non-dominant distal radius and the contralateral distal tibia were scanned if there was no fracture at one of the sites; otherwise, the contralateral side was measured. During the scanning process, the extremity was secured in a specific mold supplied by the manufacturer to reduce motion artifacts [12]. Imaging was conducted using a second-generation HR-pQCT device with an isometric voxel size of 60.7 µm using a 100 W, 68 kVp cone beam (XtremeCT II, Scanco Medical AG, Brüttisellen, Switzerland). The images were analyzed using the software supplied by the manufacturer in compliance with their established guidelines and manuals [13]. We analyzed total (Tt.vBMD), cortical (Ct.vBMD), and trabecular (Tb.vBMD) volumetric bone mineral density as well as further structural and geometrical parameters, specifically trabecular area (Tb.Ar), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), trabecular bone volume per tissue volume (BV/TV), cortical thickness (Ct.Th), cortical area (Ct.Ar), cortical perimeter (Ct.Pm), and cortical porosity (Ct.Po). We have included least significant change (LSC) values from literature [14].

Statistical evaluation

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.3.1 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). All group comparisons were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Unless stated differently, follow-up data were compared using a mixed-effects model. Group comparisons of two groups were performed using a student’s t-test or a Mann-Whitney U-test, depending on the data distribution. Group comparisons involving three or more groups were performed using one-way ANOVA with Šidák’s post-hoc correction for normally distributed data, and the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc test for non-normally distributed data. Due to missing values and non-normally distributed data, Wilcoxon tests using Bonferroni correction were used for bone turnover markers. Due to its data distribution, a ROUT outlier analysis [15] with Q = 1.0% was performed for the analysis of Ct.Po changes. Additionally, two outliers that changed more than three standard deviations over/under mean in multiple parameters were excluded from our 24-month HR-pQCT data. To identify specific predictors for aBMD gain, simple linear regression analysis with various baseline parameters and changes in bone turnover markers was performed. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Patient dropout and new fractures

A total of 46 patients did not complete romosozumab treatment for various reasons, 11 of which reported adverse drug reactions, such as musculoskeletal pain, injection site reactions, or mild nausea. In these cases, an interruption of treatment was not deemed medically necessary, but it was the patient’s request to discontinue. In one case, an 81-year-old woman suffered a subacute thalamic stroke after 4 months of romosozumab treatment, classified as an adverse event. She was a current smoker (50 pack years) and had previously been diagnosed with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) GOLD IV but had no known history of cardiovascular disease or cerebral ischemia. She fully recovered and was immediately switched to denosumab. Nine patients (4.7%) suffered in total ten additional fractures under romosozumab treatment, eight of those patients suffered a fracture in the first 6 months of treatment. Seventy percent of all new fractures were associated with falls. Fractures were located at the distal radius (60%), spine (20%), calcaneus (10%), and pelvis (10%).

Bone mineral density

In the lumbar spine, the follow-up areal bone mineral density (aBMD) showed significant increases 6 and 12 months after initiation of romosozumab treatment. aBMD increased by 9.98 ± 6.94% and 14.47 ± 8.74%, respectively (aBMD: M0: 0.835 ± 0.145 g/cm2, M6: 0.895 ± 0.134 g/cm2, M12: 0.947 ± 0.141 g/cm2; M0 vs. M6: p < 0.001, M6 vs. M12: p < 0.001; Fig. 2a, b). A significant increase was also seen at the lowest aBMD measured in two adjacent vertebrae (lowest aBMD: M0: 0.778 ± 0.142 g/cm2, M6: 0.835 ± 0.135 g/cm2, M12: 0.886 ± 0.136 g/cm2; M0 vs. M6: p < 0.001, M6 vs. M12: p < 0.001; Fig. 2c). The sequential, antiresorptive follow-on treatment with denosumab or bisphosphonates achieved an additional, significant increase of 1.84 ± 5.66% 24 months after romosozumab initiation (aBMD: M12: 0.947 ± 0.141 g/cm2, M24: 0.956 ± 0.142 g/cm2, p = 0.020; Fig. 2a, b). Bone turnover markers

The increase in aBMD was accompanied by a significant increase in the bone formation markers OC, BAP, and P1NP after 6 months of treatment (OC: M0: 19.29 ± 7.04 µg/L, M6: 23.54 ± 7.65 µg/L; p < 0.001; BAP: M0: 13.39 ± 5.44 µg/L, M6: 15.03 ± 5.23 µg/L, p < 0.001; P1NP: M0: 55.04 ± 32.07 µg/L, M6: 67.70 ± 30.86 µg/L, p < 0.001, Fig. 3a–c). Influences on treatment success

We further investigated the impact of several factors potentially influencing spinal aBMD gain. Significantly greater improvements in spinal aBMD were observed in treatment-naïve women compared to individuals with a recent (within 3 months prior to romosozumab treatment initiation) history of bisphosphonates (BP) or denosumab treatment (DMAB). No significant differences were detected between the different drug treatment groups (naïve: 17.74 ± 8.30%, BP: 11.20 ± 7.23%, teriparatide: 10.80 ± 7.61%, DMAB: 5.96 ± 9.18%; naïve vs. BP: p < 0.001, naïve vs. DMAB: p = 0.002, Fig. 5a). There was no significant difference in baseline aBMD between pre-treated and naïve patients (p = 0.191). Additionally, bone gains depended on previous aBMD dynamics. Patients who experienced a substantial loss of aBMD prior to romosozumab treatment (PL) demonstrated a significantly greater increase in aBMD compared to individuals with previously stable (PS) aBMD or those who had gained (PG) aBMD during the pre-treatment period (pre-treatment duration: 19.3 ± 13.1mo; PL: 20.18 ± 7.52%, PS: 11.86 ± 6.88%, PG: 5.34 ± 7.19; PL vs. PN: p = 0.003, PL vs. PG: p < 0.001 Fig. 5b).Fig. 5Influences on spinal aBMD gain: a 12-month aBMD percent change compared by previous osteospecific treatment, b 12-month aBMD percent change compared by aBMD trend prior to romosozumab treatment, c absolute aBMD change from 6 to 12 months depending on 6-month absolute aBMD change (Δ6M), d 12-month aBMD percent change in patients with previous or current methotrexate (MTX) treatment and those without, e 12- to 24-month aBMD percent change compared by consolidation treatment, f linear regression between baseline aBMD and 12-month aBMD gain, g linear regression between baseline osteocalcin and 12-month aBMD percent change, h linear regression between baseline P1NP and 12-month aBMD percent change. We speculated that patients might exhibit different dynamics in aBMD gain. However, 6–12-month aBMD gain was comparable between patients with an aBMD gain above and below the median of 0.080 g/cm2 after 6 months of treatment (0.039 ± 0.028 g/cm2 vs. 0.024 ± 0.041; p = 0.091, Fig. 5c). Also, correlation analysis did not show a significant linear correlation between 0–6- and 6–12-month aBMD gain (R2 = 0.004, p = 0.628). Thus, aBMD gain in the second 6 months of treatment was not significantly dependent on treatment success in the first 6 months.

In addition, other medication, apart from bone specific treatments, also seems to influence treatment outcomes. For instance, patients with methotrexate (MTX)-associated osteopathy or ongoing MTX treatment showed significantly smaller increases in aBMD than those without known MTX exposure (MTX: 8.96 ± 10.63% (n = 10), noMTX: 15.18 ± 8.29%; p = 0.028; Fig. 5d). On the contrary, proton pump inhibitor (PPI) treatment, while being a known risk factor for the development of osteoporosis and fractures [16] [17], did not significantly impact the aBMD gain (PPI: 11.64 ± 11.25% (n = 11), noPPI: 14.75 ± 8.47%, p = 0.262). However, four patients that concomitantly used prednisolone but no MTX (PRED, mean dosage: 5.25 mg/day) did gain the same aBMD like others (PRED: 14.49 ± 6.63% (n = 4) noPRED: 14.72 ± 8.65%, p = 0.958).

After the completion of 12 months of romosozumab injections, patients received antiresorptive follow-on treatment. In the 12 months after completion of romosozumab treatment, patients consolidated with denosumab gained significantly more aBMD than those that were consolidated with bisphosphonates (DMAB: 3.32 ± 4.57%, BP: −0.83 ± 3.89%; p = 0.010; Fig. 5e). Six months of denosumab followed by 6 months of bisphosphonates (D + B) was in between the other two groups numerically but did not differ significantly (D + B: −0.46 ± 5.02%; DMAB vs. D + B: p = 0.176, D + B vs. BP: p = 0.892).

To identify specific factors that might influence treatment success, we performed simple linear regression analyses. There was a significant, negative correlation between baseline spinal aBMD and absolute spinal aBMD gain under romosozumab treatment (Fig. 5f), meaning that patients with a lower baseline aBMD gained significantly more bone mineral density than those with a higher baseline aBMD.

In addition, there was a significant linear correlation between baseline bone formation parameters OC and P1NP and spinal aBMD gain, while there was no significant correlation between baseline resorption parameters and spinal aBMD gain (baseline OC: R2 = 0.124, p < 0.001; Fig. 5g; baseline BAP: R2 = 0.033, p = 0.0515; baseline P1NP: R2 = 0.162, p < 0.001; Fig. 5h; baseline CTX: R2 = 0.026, p = 0.120; baseline DPD: R2 = 0.011, p = 0.263). We further investigated whether increased bone formation parameters after 6 months correlated significantly with the increase in bone mineral density. A significant correlation was observed between OC and P1NP levels after 6 months of romosozumab treatment and increases in spinal aBMD (6M OC: R2 = 0.044, p = 0.025; 6M P1NP: R2 = 0.038, p = 0.046). Again, no significant correlation was found between CTX or DPD levels increase at 6 months and spinal aBMD increase.

Finally, there was no significant difference in spinal aBMD increase between patients that suffered a new fracture under romosozumab treatment (Fx) and those that did not (noFx) (Fx: 16.17 ± 15.34%, noFx: 14.35 ± 8.19%, p = 0.750). Furthermore, there was no significant difference in aBMD gain between patients that suffered a fracture in the year before the start of romosozumab treatment and those that did not, both regarding lumbar spine and total hip aBMD.

Discussion

In this real-world clinical study of 192 women with severe postmenopausal osteoporosis, we demonstrated that romosozumab treatment led to significant increases in bone mineral density in the lumbar spine and hip after 12 months. In parallel, bone formation markers increased as trabecular microstructure improved, confirming the dual anabolic and antiresorptive effects of the treatment.

Romosozumab induced significant aBMD gains, more precisely 14.5% at the lumbar spine and 4.25% at the total hip after treatment. This effect is comparable to previous controlled studies and recent real-world studies. Cosman et al. [2] report a 13.3% and 6.8% increase in the lumbar spine and total hip, respectively; McClung et al. [3] reported 11.3% and 4.1%, and Ishibashi et al. [18] 16.9% and 4.7%. Furthermore, our results exceed other real-world studies by Gielen et al. [5] with 9.2% and 3.0% increase at LS and TH, and Adami et al. [6] reporting a 10.9% and 3.7% aBMD increase providing proof of the efficacy of romosozumab in a real-world setting. Similar to the controlled studies, approximately two-thirds of the total aBMD gain (after 12 months) under romosozumab treatment was achieved after 6 months (70% lumbar spine and 60% total hip). Importantly, the increase in aBMD at 6 months does not correlate with the aBMD gain during the second half of treatment, supporting the 12-month use of romosozumab. However, aBMD gain after 12 months of romosozumab treatment seems to be comparable with an early switch to denosumab after 6 or even 3 months [19] [20]. These aBMD gains of short-term romosozumab treatment and subsequent denosumab treatment compared to 12-month romosozumab treatment may have important implications for healthcare costs and resource allocation. Nevertheless, 30–40% of the total aBMD increase occurred in the second half of treatment (months 6–12), and given the severity of osteoporosis in the included patients, long-term treatment was required, as any additional aBMD gain is clinically important. Thus, further studies are needed to evaluate optimal treatment regimens combining osteoanabolic and antiresorptive therapies. Importantly, we only report total hip measures which reflect averaged changes across a larger region of interest and are less sensitive to positioning variability than femoral neck measurements and thus more robust [21].

Despite their aBMD gains, 4.7% (n = 9) of our patients suffered a new fracture while receiving romosozumab and 1% of patients (n = 2) suffered a new vertebral fracture. While the FRAME study reported a lower 0.5% fracture incidence, patients with a previous hip fracture or any severe or more than two moderate vertebral fractures were excluded [2], contrasting our cohort. However, the ARCH trial with an average baseline T-score of −3.0 and previous fractures reported a risk of 4% for the incidence of new vertebral fractures under romosozumab [4] matching our results. Since the prevalence of vertebral fractures increases the risk of new vertebral fractures [22] [23], these results appear consistent. In accordance with the EU label, our patients were at very high fracture risk due to a prior major osteoporotic fracture. Notably, fracture risk is highest during the first 6 months of treatment, when most fractures are reported [24] [25]. Furthermore, the majority of recorded fractures in our study happened at the distal radius, which indicates an association to trauma, thus does not imply a failure of treatment.

The analysis of bone turnover markers revealed significant increases after 6 months of treatment for BAP, OC, and P1NP. This increase in bone formation involves a significant increase in PTH, indicating an increased need for calcium. Bone resorption markers (CTX) only significantly decreased after 12 months of treatment. This early increase in bone formation and late decrease in bone resorption markers has also been described by others [2] [4] [3]. However, linear regression analysis between various bone turnover markers and aBMD gain in the lumbar spine over 12 months showed only a small correlation between aBMD gain and bone formation markers after 6 months. Instead, baseline bone formation markers showed a stronger correlation with aBMD gain. This led us to conclude that the baseline activity of bone turnover may be more important for treatment success than bone turnover response to treatment. This idea is further supported by the higher responsiveness of treatment-naïve patients to romosozumab treatment compared to those that previously received antiresorptive agents, in line with others [26]. However, 3-month data were not available, which might be of particular interest given the osteoanabolic profile of romosozumab.

Bone microstructure analysis using HR-pQCT revealed a significant increase in trabecular BV/TV in both radius and tibia. Because some of the observed changes were modest in absolute magnitude, we interpreted them in the context of the precision limits of HR-pQCT measurements and compared parameter changes with literature-reported least significant change (LSC) thresholds [14]. At the tibia, total, trabecular and cortical vBMD exceeded the LSC at both 12 and 24 months, whereas at the radius, this was not the case. Notably, cortical porosity significantly increased, approaching LSC at the radius at 12 months. This pattern is consistent with a loading-modulated skeletal response, where mineral deposition preferentially occurs at load-bearing sites while non-loaded regions may transiently contribute to mineral mobilization during early anabolic treatment, as mirrored by transient Ct.Po increase. Notably, most changes remained below literature-based LSC thresholds and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

By trend, cortical area and cortical thickness increased in the tibia. In the tibia, bone mineralization improved in total and cortical vBMD; in the radius, trabecular vBMD improved. The only other study analyzing bone microstructure, yet at limited resolution (300 µm) for microstructural analysis and different measurement sites in 11 patients, reported significant increases in BV/TV in the 12th thoracic vertebral body [27]. Yet, results are not directly comparable due to both anatomical site and imaging technique. While Graeff et al. used a clinical CT system, our analysis employed standardized HR-pQCT, which enables true microstructural assessment at isometric voxel sizes of 60.7 µm at peripheral skeletal sites. Interestingly, many previous studies showed that romosozumab primarily affects the spine, as indicated by the larger aBMD gains at the spine than the hip [2] [3] [18] [28]. The less pronounced bone gain at the peripheral sites, particularly the radius, compared to the strong increases in spinal aBMD suggests a site-specific skeletal response to romosozumab. At non-weight-bearing sites, small increases in trabecular parameters and the observed rise in cortical porosity may reflect transient intracortical remodeling rather than structural deterioration. These findings support the hypothesis that mechanical loading plays an important role in modulating the anabolic response to sclerostin inhibition, as similar results were generated by the sclerostin antibody setrusumab [29].

In addition, the marked discrepancy between axial and peripheral skeletal responses raises the possibility of redistribution of mineral towards load-bearing sites during treatment, potentially limiting detectable gains in peripheral bone. This interpretation is also consistent with the transient increase in PTH at 6 months. A similar pattern has been reported in tumor-induced osteomalacia (TIO), where surgical treatment, correcting FGF23 levels, resulted in marked increases in spinal and hip aBMD while peripheral vBMD initially remained impaired, suggesting a rapid shift in calcium towards the axial bone compartment [30] [31].

There are some limitations to our study. We performed a retrospective analysis of existing clinical data of postmenopausal women with severe osteoporosis receiving romosozumab. Despite this, we analyzed a relatively heterogenous patient cohort, especially regarding specific characteristics such as comedication. However, only this heterogeneity made the analysis of various factors on treatment outcomes possible, such as a reduced treatment outcome in the case of MTX treatment, in line with previous studies [32]. Furthermore, we have experienced a dropout of 46 patients limiting our results of drug efficacy to patients that completed 12 months of treatment, which potentially leads to an attrition bias. In addition, not all patients could receive HR-pQCT measurements at both timepoints. While there is a potential risk of an unintended selection bias, patients were indeed randomly selected based on staff and machine capacity. However, the HR-pQCT subgroup included fewer pre-treated patients (36% vs. 64%, p < 0.001), while age and baseline aBMD were representative of the full cohort which may mirror more of the naïve response than a clinical average. Furthermore, our routine clinical data provide only 12- and 24-month HR-pQCT data, which limits the conclusions drawn with respect to the remodeling dynamics especially within the important anabolic window of the first 3–6 months of romosozumab treatment. Finally, we pooled the results of denosumab and bisphosphonates in our analysis of the 12- to 24-month timepoint, which might confound our results. When comparing patients with denosumab vs. patients with bisphosphonates, the latter did not exhibit further aBMD gains while denosumab-only treated patients exhibited 3.32 ± 4.57% additional increase (p < 0.01).

In conclusion, our data demonstrate the efficacy of romosozumab in a real-world setting. We observed significant increases in aBMD measurement by DXA, as well as a significant increase in trabecular microstructure assessed by HR-pQCT. Analysis of bone turnover parameters revealed a significant increase in bone formation parameters after 6 months of treatment and a significant decrease in the bone resorption marker CTX after 12 months of treatment. However, it was the baseline value of bone formation, rather than its increase, that significantly correlated with aBMD gain. While antiresorptive agents are essential as a sequential follow-on therapy after romosozumab to maintain long-term benefits, prior treatment with antiresorptives was associated with reduced effectiveness of romosozumab. In summary, our data provides new insights into treatment monitoring and potential predictive factors of treatment success possibly influencing clinical decision making in osteoporosis care.
