Radial to Peripheral Intervention: A Paradigm Shift
Authors: Srinivasa Potluri, Mohamad Bader Abo Hajar, Joseph Suhail Jebain
Published: 2025

The evolution of cardiovascular intervention has long been marked by the relentless pursuit of safety, efficacy, and patient comfort. Among the most transformative developments in recent years is the shift from traditional femoral approaches to transradial access (TRA), especially as this technique is now adapted for peripheral vascular interventions (PVI).1 The emergence of radial access in percutaneous coronary interventions was, at first, met with skepticism; however, years of innovation, technical advancement, clinician persistence, and patient satisfaction have transformed radial access into the preferred route for percutaneous coronary interventions in many centers worldwide. The demonstrated reduction in bleeding and vascular complications, enhanced patient comfort, and faster postprocedure ambulation have been undeniable advantages.2

Traditionally, interventions for the iliac, femoral, popliteal, and tibial arteries have relied on femoral or even brachial access. The TRA was preferred over transfemoral access for PVI in cases such as bilateral lower limb lesions treated in 1 procedure, iliac bifurcation stenting, bifurcated aortic grafts with planned pelvic or leg intervention, obesity, pelvic trauma, need for high-dose antithrombotic therapy or anticoagulation, and when the groin is unsuitable due to infection, skin breakdown, or high femoral artery bifurcation.1

Nevertheless, the future of radial to peripheral (R2P) interventions is evolving swiftly and signals a paradigm shift. With an increasing number of physicians using radial access for coronary procedures and increasing equipment made specifically for R2P interventions, this shift is rapidly gaining momentum.

In this issue of JSCAI, Khraisat et al3 present their single-center experience with R2P interventions. The authors retrospectively reviewed 137 patients who underwent TRA for PVI between November 2021 and November 2024. The operators maintained a strong commitment to a “radial-first” approach. Unlike other similar studies, they primarily used left radial access.4 The left radial artery was used in 83.9%, the right in 7.2%, and the left ulnar in 8.7%. The authors provide a clear description of their procedural techniques and equipment choices for aortoiliac, infrainguinal, and infrapopliteal interventions. Notably, 36% of the interventions were for chronic total occlusions (CTOs), with 2/3 of these in the femoropopliteal arteries. Procedural success was achieved in 98.5% of cases, with complications observed in only 3 patients (2.1%).

This high procedural success rate and low complication rate underscore the feasibility and safety of a radial-first approach for PVI, aligning with previous studies; however, this was a single-center retrospective study in the era of significant evolution of technology. Further multicenter prospective trials are needed to validate these findings and to determine their generalizability to broader patient populations.

The principal limitation of this study lies in the ongoing evolution of equipment throughout the study period, which may have affected the consistency and comparability of outcomes over time. Also, it is notable that nearly one-third of patients with CTOs in this single-center study did not require alternative access. In contrast, a prospective study involving 120 patients found that second access was necessary in 25% of cases, most often utilizing pedal access.5 This difference is likely attributable to 2 main factors. First, during the early adoption period of R2P interventions, long-shaft equipment, such as lithotripsy devices or drug-coated balloons for femoropopliteal arteries, was not yet available. Second, in cases involving long CTOs, a retrograde approach is frequently needed for successful recanalization, which often necessitates an alternative access site.

The success of R2P intervention requires proficiency as a TRA operator, alongside a solid understanding of peripheral interventions and a willingness to adapt to a relatively slower learning curve and continually evolving equipment. R2P intervention is more than a technical trend—it is a patient-centered evolution in vascular therapy. By minimizing complications, improving comfort, and supporting outpatient care, it redefines the standard for treating peripheral arterial disease.
