Spider peptides from Brachypelma smithi with slightly different amino acids at their C-terminal loops exert different insecticidal activities
Authors: Herlinda Clement, Lilu Corrales-García, Víctor Carpanta, Galilea Jaimes, Pavel Andrei Montero-Domínguez, Elia Diego-García, Gerardo Corzo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe insecticidal molecules of spiders persistently evolve to ensure rapid paralysis of their prey, and the best molecules are transmitted to their progeny. Here, we cloned two insecticidal peptides, Bs2 and Bs3, from the venom glands of the theraphosid Brachypelma smithi. Bs2 and Bs3 are 90.2% identical, but they exhibit interesting structural differences at their C-termini, including a connecting disulfide bond (residues Cys15-Cys36 for Bs2 and Cys15-Cys30 for Bs3). The genomic origin of Bs2 and Bs3 may be a cause for gene duplication events. Moreover, Bs2 differs in two residues from Tal1 (95.1% identical), an insecticidal peptide, from the tarantula Tliltocatl albopilosus. Likewise, Bs3 is similar to Asp3a from Aphonopelma sp., a peptide that targets mammalian Cav (voltage-dependent Ca2 + channel), but it has not been tested in insects. Bs2 and Bs3 were cloned and recombinantly expressed in bacterial cells, and their paralytic effects were tested on three species of insects. The insecticidal peptide rBs2 with the connecting loop Cys15-Cys36 was significantly more insecticidal than that of rBs3 when affecting Galleria mellonella larvae (Lepidoptera). Yet, the insecticidal peptide rBs3 with the connecting loop Cys15-Cys30 was significantly more insecticidal than that of rBs2 when affecting Acheta domesticus nymph crickets (Orthoptera), and Gromphadorhina portentosa cockroaches (Blattodea). rBs2 and rBs3 structural models show a low-structured C-terminal in rBs3, which correlates with a more flexible amino acid sequence of such C-terminal from residues Tyr30 to Leu42. Since insecticidal spider peptides are constantly evolving for prey capture, they are valuable ion channel antagonists for understanding insect cell receptors, and they are also promising leads for insect control.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00726-026-03521-5.Keywords: Arachnid, Insecticidal, Peptides, Protein expression, Spider, Theraphosid, Venom

Introduction

Spiders feed on insects using prey-capture strategies ranging from ambushing to complex silk traps (Nyffeler and Birkhofer 2017; Ratz et al. 2023). Most spiders rely on their venom to immobilize their prey for feeding because they contain natural insecticides, which in turn are interesting biochemical tools for finding novel insect receptors (Estrada et al. 2007b). Yet, insecticidal peptides could lead us to novel modes of action and biopesticides (Corzo et al. 2000a, 2005). Brachypelma smithi is an endemic theraphosid from Guerrero, Mexico, and this species was first described as Eurypelma smithi (Lyal 2011) and later transferred to the genus Brachypelma (Pocock 1903). Bs1 was one of the first insecticidal peptides obtained from the venom of B. smithi (Corzo et al. 2008), and its primary structure comprises 41 residues and six half-cystines that form three disulfide bridges. The three-dimensional structure of Bs1 was determined and contains three antiparallel beta-sheets. Bs1 is structurally categorized into the ICK (Inhibitor Cystine Knot) family of spider neurotoxins (Corzo et al. 2008). Although the receptor of Bs1 has not yet been described, it is assumed to target insects’ voltage-gated sodium channels (Nav) because of its rapid insecticidal activity, similar to the onset characteristics of other Nav toxins from spider and scorpion venoms (Cestèle et al. 1999; Gordon et al. 1996). Interestingly, the venom of B. smithi contains other well-studied insecticidal motifs, the inhibitor cystine knot (ICK, Bs1) as mentioned, and the disulfide-directed hairpin (DDH, Proteins 1 and 5) (Corzo et al. 2009; Kaiser II, 1994).

Here, we report the molecular cloning, recombinant expression, and insecticidal activities of two similar N-terminal ICK spider peptides, Bs2 and Bs3, with distinctive C-terminal loops from the venom of Brachypelma smithi. Additionally, insecticidal activities and model structures of those two peptides are reported.

Materials and methods

Venom and isolation of insecticidal peptides

The venom from Brachyplema smithi was extracted by electrical stimulation and then lyophilized. The venom represents a pooled sample of six male and female species. It was dissolved (2 mg) in water containing 0.1% of trifluoracetic acid (TFA), and then centrifuged to remove the insoluble material (14,000 g for 5 min). The soluble venom was separated using a reversed-phase analytical C18 column (5C18MS, 4.6 × 250 mm, Vydac, United States) equilibrated in 0.1% TFA, and eluted with 0–60% acetonitrile in 0.1% TFA in a linear gradient, run for 60 min (1 ml/min) (Corzo et al. 2000). The elution fractions were monitored at 230 nm, collected in 1.5 ml vials, and dehydrated under a high vacuum. The dried samples were used to conduct assays in nymph crickets to select fractions with insecticidal activity. The insecticidal toxins were then reduced and alkylated before Edman sequencing, following techniques described by our group (Corzo et al. 2000).

Bacterial strains, enzymes, and plasmids

E. coli XL1 Blue was used for plasmid propagation. The Shuffle E. coli strain was used for the expression of rBs2 and rBs3. The pQE30 vector was used for cloning and protein production with a 6His tag. Restriction enzymes, Taq polymerase, Pfu, Factor Xa, and T4 DNA ligase were purchased from New England Biolabs (Ipswich, MA, USA).

cDNA library construction

Following previous protocols, a cDNA library was constructed using mRNA extracted from a pair of venom glands from one specimen of B. smithi (Satake et al. 2004). RNA was isolated with the “Total RNA Isolation System” from Promega (Madison, WI). Next, a full-length cDNA plasmid library (pDNR-LIB) was prepared using the Creator SMART cDNA Library Construction Kit (Clontech Laboratories, Inc., Palo Alto, CA).

Gene cloning and DNA sequencing

Based on the information obtained from direct peptide sequencing, oligonucleotides were synthesized using a model 391 DNA Synthesizer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA): Bs2CCR5 5’-TGYTGYMGNGARTAYGARTGY-3’; Bs3CCY5 5’-TGYTGYTAYGARTAYGARTGY-3’; and BsCDKC5 5’-TGYGAYAARGAYGAYCC-3’ (M, N, R, and Y represent degenerated nucleotides). These primers were used in a polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with the primer pair CD3/3’. The plasmid library was used as a template for the PCR reaction. The PCR products were purified using a Centricon 100 column (Amicon, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions and ligated into the plasmid vector pGEM (Promega, Madison, WI). The ligation reaction was used to transform competent E. coli DH5 α cells. Positive clones were sequenced from both ends using a model 3100 DNA sequencer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). Briefly, positive colonies (blue/white color) were amplified according to the following protocol: hot start 4 min denaturation at 94 °C; 1 min at 94 °C, 1 min at 50 °C, and 1 min at 72 °C, for 32 cycles; and final step of 5 min at 72 °C.

Plasmid construction

The amino acid sequences of Bs2 and Bs3 were PCR amplified from a cDNA clone obtained from the previously described library. Essentially, each plasmid contained a fusion protein with a 6His-tag, a sequence coding for the amino acids recognized by the protease (Factor Xa), the Bs2 and Bs3 amino acid sequences, and the encoded amino acids of the restriction enzymes. Their constructions were named pQE30rBs2 and pQE30rBs3, and their corresponding protein products were abbreviated rBs2 and rBs3, respectively. Tables S1 and S2 show the oligonucleotides employed for the construction of the Bs2 and Bs3-encoding genes. The peptide sequences of Bs2 and Bs3 contained 41 and 42 amino acid residues, respectively, and sequence analysis and reverse translation were carried out to obtain the corresponding DNA coding sequences. Then, the preferential use of codons was determined because E. coli was used for protein expression. Next, the digestion site for the restriction enzyme BamHI and the recognition site for Factor Xa were added at the 5’-end of the sequence. At the 3’-end, two stop sites and the digestion site for the restriction enzyme PstI were added. This last step was to clone the gene in the plasmid pQE30 (QiaGen®) using the mentioned digestion sites. Briefly, because of their size, the oligos, to flank the coding sequences of Bs2 and Bs3, had to be purified using a polyacrylamide gel (15%) and urea (7 M). After purification, the oligos were quantified, and solutions were prepared at a final concentration of 10 µM. Next, the Bs2 and Bs3 genes were assembled in vitro by “oligonucleotide overlapping extension” using PCR.

For Bs2, megaprimer 1 (Mp1) was obtained in the first PCR by binding Oligo Bs2-Up1 and Oligo Bs2-Lw2 (annealing temperature 59 °C for 40 s, expected size 78 bp; Table S1). Megaprimer 2 (Mp2) was obtained in the second PCR by binding Oligo Bs2-Up3 and Oligo Bs2-Lw4 (annealing temperature 59 °C for 40 s, expected size 103 bp; Table S1). In the third PCR, the Bs2 gene was obtained by binding Mp1 and Mp2 (annealing temperature 60 °C for 50 s, expected size 154 bp). For Bs3, megaprimer 1 (Mp1) was obtained by binding Oligo Bs3-Up1 and Oligo Bs3-Lw2 (annealing temperature 59 °C for 40 s, expected size 78 bp; Table S2). Megaprimer 2 (Mp2) was obtained in the second PCR by ligating Oligo Bs3-Up3 and Oligo Bs2-Lw4 (annealing temperature 59 °C for 40 s, expected size 103 bp; Table S2). Finally, in the third PCR, the Bs3 gene was obtained by binding of Mp1 and Mp2 (annealing temperature 61 °C for 50 s, expected size 157 bp).

Taq and Pfu polymerases were used for the amplification of Bs2 and Bs3. PCR products were run on 1% agarose gels containing GelRed ® dye (Biotium) and visualized under ultraviolet (uv) light (DNA marker from New England Biolabs, NEB). Afterwards, the amplification products were purified from the agarose gel with the PCR Product Purification Kit (Roche®). The presence of the insert in the vector pQE30 was corroborated by colony PCR using the oligos Direct pQE-Fw (5’–GAGGATCCATCGAGGGACGCTGCCTGGGCGAAAACGTGCC–3’) and Reverse pQE-Rev (5’–GGTCATTACTGGATCTAT–3’).

The PCR products and the corresponding plasmids were digested overnight with the corresponding restriction enzymes at 37 °C. Next, PCR products were purified before ligation using a high-purity plasmid isolation kit (Roche). The ligation reaction (20 µl) was carried out using T4 DNA ligase with a 10-fold insert excess over plasmid for 16 h at 16 °C. Then, 10 µl of the ligation reaction was used to transfect competent E. coli XL1Blue cells. Positive clones with the expected insert were grown in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium supplemented with ampicillin. The plasmids of positive colonies were purified using the High Pure Plasmid Isolation Kit (Roche). Plasmid constructs were verified by DNA sequencing from both sites, including the insert boundaries, to confirm the reading frame and the conservation of restriction sites. Shuffle strains were transfected with the corresponding plasmid for 30 s at 42 °C, followed by 5 min in ice and 30 min recovery at 37 °C in LB medium. Plates of LB contained 100 µg/ml of ampicillin.

Production and purification of rBs2 and rBs3

Plasmid-expressing E. coli Shuffle strain cells were grown in LB medium. When the absorbance (600 nm) reached 0.8, cultures were induced with 0.5 mM IPTG (isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside) for 20 h at 16 °C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation (7,871 g for 20 min) using a Beckman centrifuge model JA-14, recovered with washing buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0), and lysed with a One-Shot cell disruptor (Constant Systems®) at a pressure of 30 kpsi. This material was centrifuged again (17,709 g for 20 min), and the supernatant was discarded.

The recombinant peptides were extracted from the inclusion bodies using 6 M guanidinium chloride (GndHCl) in a 50 mM Tris-base buffer, pH 8.0, and centrifuged for 20 min at 17,709 g. The recombinants Bs2 and Bs3 were purified by Ni-NTA (Ni-nitrilotriacetic acid) affinity column chromatography according to the instructions provided by the manufacturer using (denaturing conditions with buffer A: 6 M GndHCl in a 50 mM Tris-base buffer, pH 8.0 and buffer B: 6 M GndHCl in 50 mM Tris-base buffer, containing 400 mM imidazole, pH 8.0; Qiagen, USA). Buffer B was eliminated by a second purification step under reversed-phase HPLC conditions. An analytic C18 reversed-phase column (Vydac 218 TP54) was run from solvent A (0.1% trifluoroacetic acid – TFA- in water) to solvent B (0.1% TFA in acetonitrile). The HPLC system used for this separation was described previously (Rodriguez et al. 2011). The gradient was run for rBs2 from 20 to 70% B in 50 min and rBs3 from 0 to 60% solvent B for 60 min. The rBs2 and rBs3 products were vacuum-dried and reduced using 50 mM DTT in 50 mM Tris-base buffer pH 8.0. Next, 200 µg reduced peptide (rBs2 or rBs3) was allowed to fold under controlled conditions using 300 mM Tris-base buffer pH 8.0 containing 1 mM reduced glutathione (GS)/0.1 mM oxidized glutathione (GSSG), and was separated by RP-HPLC. The identity of the folded recombinant products was confirmed by mass spectrometry analysis using a Finnigan LCQDUO ion trap mass spectrometer (San Jose, CA, USA). These techniques are currently used in the laboratory and are reported elsewhere (Estrada et al. 2007a).

Circular dichroism

The secondary structure content of the native and recombinant peptide was calculated by circular dichroism spectroscopy (CD). Spectra of rBs2 and rBs3 were recorded in quartz cells (1 mm-path) at room temperature (190 to 260 nm) using a spectropolarimeter Jasco J-710 (Jasco, Japan). Data were collected every 1 nm at a speed of 50 nm/min. Each protein was dissolved in 60% trifluoroethanol up to a concentration of 0.6 mg/ml. Trifluoroethanol improves secondary structure. The CD values correspond to the mean of three CD recordings. Finally, percentages of secondary structure were analyzed using the online algorithms at the K2D3 web server (http://cbdm-01.zdv.uni-mainz.de/~andrade/k2d3/).

Biological activity

The protocol for assaying rBs2 and rBs3 in vivo used Galleria mellonella (wax moth larvae), Acheta domesticus (house cricket nymphs), and Gromphadorhina portentosa (cockroaches). G. mellonella and G. portentosa were injected in the pronotum and the metathorax, respectively, with a volume of up to 5 µl at doses of 0, 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300, 350, and 400 µg/g of each recombinant peptide, rBs2 or rBs3, dissolved in distilled water. Crickets were injected intrathoracically between the second and third pair of legs. For G. mellonella, six doses with eight individuals each, A. domesticus, eight doses with three individuals each, and G. portentosa, eight doses with three individuals each were injected, respectively. Insects were monitored for up to 12 h post-injection, and just water was used as a control.

Three-dimensional modeling of Bs2 and Bs3

The three-dimensional models for Bs2 were created by using the algorithms I-Tasser (Zhou et al. 2022), AlphaFold (Abramson et al. 2024), Modeller (Sali 1995), and Rosetta-Fold (Baek et al. 2021). For Bs3, I-Tasser (fold recognition) was used to find a template, and then Modeller created a three-dimensional model. The alignment of Bs3 and its template was done with T-Coffee homology extension (Notredame et al. 2000). The models with the lowest DOPE SCORE were selected.

Statistics

A simple logistic regression was used to calculate the PD50 expressed as mean and standard deviation or as mean with 95% confidence intervals, and a t-test was used to compare the means among groups. Prism v.8.2.1 software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for all statistical methods.

Amino acid sequences of two similar N-terminal ICK spider peptides from B. smithi

The venom of Brachypelma smithi was separated by reversed-phase HPLC. Bs1, Bs2, and Bs3 represent peptide fractions with insecticidal activities (Figure S1). The three insecticidal peptides showed molecular masses of 4,916.6, 4,845.5, and 4,844.5 Da, respectively. The amplification primer AUAP, provided by the commercial 3´RACE Kit (Invitrogen™) and containing a sequence homologous to the adaptor region of the adaptor oligonucleotide (AP), was used as the reverse oligonucleotide 5´- GGC CAC GCG TCG ACT AGT AC −3`. Thus, the B. smithi insecticidal peptide transcripts were obtained. One translated coded sequence was named Bs2; it has seven residues different from Bs1 and is 80.5% identical to Bs1 (Table 1, section A). The Bs2 and Bs3 transcripts were individually cloned into the plasmid pQE30 (pQE30rBs2 or pQE30rBs3, respectively), which contained long pro-peptide structures to help protein expression and purification (Figures S2A and S2B). The final translated coded sequence of Bs2 and Bs3 coded for 57 and 58 residues, respectively. The molecular masses of the products of pQE30rBs2 or pQE30rBs3 are 6,700.5 Da (rBs2) and 6,698.5 Da (rBs3), respectively.

Expression, purification, in vitro folding, and enzymatic cleavage

The E. coli Shuffle strain was transformed with either plasmid pQE30rBs2 or pQE30rBs3. Shuffle cells obtained after induction with IPTG were decanted by centrifugation, resuspended, and lysed with a One-Shot cell disruptor (Constant Systems®) at a pressure of 30 kpsi. The supernatant was purified by affinity column chromatography after solubilization with GndHCl. Figure S3 shows the SDS-PAGE of the pQE30rBs2 (a) and pQE30rBs3 (b) obtained during the growth of Shuffle cells in the presence or absence of IPTG and after affinity column chromatographic purification.

Imidazole eluates from affinity chromatography columns were loaded directly onto a C18 column for HPLC separation (Fig. 1). The rBs2 RP-HPLC profile exhibited multiple fractions. All of them conserve the same molecular mass of 6,700.5 Da (theoretical molecular mass 6,700.4 Da), indicating that they were structural isoforms with scrambled disulfide bonds (Fig. 1A). After testing them for insecticidal activity, only one fraction was active at 150 µg/g; therefore, we selected this fraction for further purification and testing against other insects. Similarly, the rBs3 RP-HPLC profile showed two sharp peaks (one of them with insectidal activity at 150 µg/g) with an expected experimental molecular mass of 6,697.0 Da (theoretical molecular mass 6,698.5 Da) (Fig. 1B). Starting with 1 L of cell culture and after RP-HPLC purification, the yield of the recombinant insecticidal peptides rBs2 and rBs3 was 1.8 and 2.5 mg/L, respectively.

For both biologically active fractions, rBs2 and rBs3, we ruled out distinct disulfide-bonding patterns from the native ones because, among the structural isoforms obtained, only one of them, respectively, retained insecticidal activity, albeit at a level lower than that of the native peptides. So, the weaker activity of rBs2 and rBs3 was more likely due to the extra N-terminal tag or the C-terminal amidation. rBs2 and rBs3 were vacuum-dried, resuspended, and repurified using an analytical biphenyl column (Fig. 1C and D). Repurified rBs2 and rBs3 were used to determine the secondary structure content and insecticidal activities.

Secondary structure

The secondary structures of native Bs1 and the recombinants rBs2 and rBs3 were compared by CD spectroscopy. They showed high absorption for the β-strand secondary structure and relatively low α-helical content (Fig. 2). Deconvolution of the CD data for nBs1, rBs2, and rBs3, respectively, revealed the presence of α-helices (13.2, 12.8, and 20.3%), β-strands (18.2, 30.1, and 11.6%), β-turns (12.9, 14.3, and 14.6%), and other structures (47.5, 40.9, and 45.3%). So far, all ICK spider neurotoxins have large proportions of β-strands, which are characterized by a positive band at 198 nm, and a negative ellipticities at 208–222 nm (Scanu et al. 1969).

Biological activity of rBs2 and rBs3

The recombinant rBs2 and rBs3 were challenged with three insects: Galleria mellonella (Lepidoptera), Acheta domesticus (Orthoptera), and Gromphadorhina. portentosa (Blattodea).

Galleria mellonella larvae

Injections were administered into the pronotum of G. mellonella larvae (Lepidoptera) at the third instar stage, with an average weight of 167 mg. The tested doses were 50, 100, 200, 250, and 300 µg/g insect in 10 µL of rBs2 or rBs3. The rBs2 peptide at a dose of 50 µg induced paralysis in 12.5% of larvae, and this effect persisted for 3 h. At 100 µg, the paralysis increased to 50% of the larvae but was not sustained over the 3 h. At 200, 250 and 300 µg/g, paralysis and death were observed in 100% of the larvae for up to 3 h. The paralyzing dose (PD₅₀) of rBs2 at 3 h post-injection was determined to be 128.9 ± 17.2 µg/g insect. For rBs3, the administration of 50 µg/g induced a maximum paralysis in 37.5% of the larvae at 3 h. With a dose of 100 µg/g, 50% of the larvae were paralyzed 1 h post-injection; however, they recovered by 3 h. At a dose of 200 µg/g, 100% of the larvae exhibited paralysis up to 1.5 h, but the effect gradually decreased, reaching 50% paralysis at 3 h. Finally, at a dose of 250 and 300 µg/g, paralysis and death were observed in 100% up to 3 h. The paralyzing LD₅₀ of rBs3 at 3 h post-injection was 169.4 ± 40.2 µg/g insect. Although rBs3 induced a higher degree of paralysis in the first hour post-injection, its effects were short-lived. In contrast, rBs2 was able to sustain paralysis throughout the experiment, maintaining paralysis signs of insects for 3 h (Fig. 3A).

Acheta domesticus

Acheta domesticus (cricket fifth instar nymphs, Orthoptera) weighing 100 mg were injected between the fifth and sixth abdominal segments. The injected doses were 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, and 300 µg/g in 5 µL of either rBs2 or rBs3. Crickets were monitored for signs of paralysis for 3 h. For rBs2, 50 and 100 µg/g crickets showed no signs of paralysis in 3 h. At a 150 µg/g cricket dose, 33% of individuals showed complete body paralysis after 3 h. At a dose of 200 µg/g, after 3 h, 33% showed complete body paralysis, and at 250 and 300 µg/g, cickets were paralyzed and died at 3 h post-injection. For rBs3, 50 µg/g cricket, no signs of paralysis were observed for 3 h. At a dose of 100 µg/g cricket, 66% of paralysis was observed after injection; however, both crickets recovered. At a dose of 150 µg/g cricket, 66% showed complete body paralysis at 3 h. At a dose of 200, 250, and 300 µg/g from the start of the injection, 100% (i.e., two crickets) showed complete body paralysis and died after 3 h (Fig. 3B).

Gromphadorhina portentosa

Madagascar fourth instar cockroaches of Gromphadorhina portentosa (Blattodea) weighing 100 mg were injected into the second abdominal segment. The injected doses were 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, and 300 µg/g insect in 5 µl of either rBs2 or rBs3. Individuals were monitored for signs of paralysis or death for 3 h. For rBs2, a 50 µg/g dose per cockroach did not show signs of paralysis after 3 h. At a dose of 100 µg/g from the start of the injection, 66% showed complete body paralysis for 3 h. Afterward, one of the cockroaches that had remained paralyzed recovered, and only 33% showed complete body paralysis and died. At a dose of 150 µg/g from the start of the injection, 33% showed complete body paralysis until 3 h. For rBs3, 50 µg/g of cockroach produced 33% paralysis at 1.5 h until 3 h. At a dose of 100 µg/g of cockroach, 33% showed complete body paralysis for 30 min, then one of the paralyzed cockroaches recovered, but 66% showed complete paralysis until 3 h. At a dose of 150 µg/g, 66% showed paralysis, remaining for 30 min, then 33% recovered by 3 h, but 66% showed complete body paralysis for up to 3 h. At higher concentrations of 200 µg/g, all cockroaches were paralyzed and died at 3 h post-injection (Fig. 3C).

A paired t-test was performed to compare the paralysis response between rBs2 and rBs3 in all three insects, G. mellonella, A. domesticus, and G. portentosa. There was a significant difference in paralysis response between rBs2 and rBs3 towards G. mellonella (t(6) = 2.52, p = 0.0453) and A. domesticus (t(7) = 2.376, p = 0.0492). Finally, the paired t-test between rBs2 and rBs3 in G. portentosa showed no significant difference in death response (t(7) = 1, p = 0.3506).

Interestingly, an ordinary one-way ANOVA of rBs2 paralytic activity among G. mellonella, A. domesticus, and G. portentosa showed no significant difference (p = 0.8592). The same result was observed for rBs3 (p = 0.6933).

Three-dimensional modeling of Bs2 and Bs3

I-Tasser, AlphaFold, Modeller, and Rosetta-Fold were used to model Bs2. Rosetta-Fold is an algorithm commonly used for small proteins that lack homologs. This program uses physical and chemical principles to simulate folding without known structural templates. However, this approach was not enough to generate models with the oxidized Cys for Bs2 and Bs3. Only I-Tasser, AlphaFold, and Rosetta-fold programs presented folds with oxidized Cys for Bs2. All three models exhibited the same ICK arrangement of disulfide bridges (Cys1-Cys4, Cys2-Cys5, and Cys3-Cys6) and high structural similarity between them (RMSD < 3 Å). Nevertheless, the 25–30 connecting loop in Bs2 showed the largest structural inaccuracy of all three algorithms because of the lack of experimental data on that region within the construction of each model. The algorithms applied different mathematical methods for modeling Bs2, concluding that similar folding indicated the certainty of the three-dimensional arrangement of such protein (Fig. 4).

Three-dimensional models for Bs2 and Bs3. Alpha-fold (purple) and Rosetta-fold (Cyan) [RMSD: 0.8 Å] (A). Alpha-fold (purple) and I-Tasser (blue) [RMSD: 0.73 Å] (B). The area of most significant uncertainty is enclosed in red (A and C). Disulfide bonds are shown in yellow

Concerning Bs3, none of the algorithms exhibited all oxidized cysteines in their respective models. As a result, molecular homology modeling was performed using the three-dimensional spider structure (PDB ID: 1TYK) by Modeller. A structural model with an RMSD of 0.817 was created based on such 1TYK template, presenting a three-dimensional array of cysteines similar to Bs2 (Cys1-Cys4, Cys2-Cys5, and Cys3-Cys6, Fig. 4C).

The modeled structures of both Bs2 and Bs3 exhibited ICK foldings. They are composed of three anti-parallel beta sheets and share the same three-dimensional arrangement of disulfide bridges. The secondary structure presented in both models agreed with the circular dichroism information exhibited in this study. Despite the high identity between the two toxins (83%), the shift of the last Cys generates a change in the distribution of secondary structures, which are observed in the loops formed by residues 24–33 and 32–41 for Bs2 and Bs3, respectively. These differences correspond to the elongate section in the connecting loop β2-β3, and the C-terminal of both Bs2 and Bs3, respectively (Fig. 5A and C). In those amino acid sequences, a significant RMSD was observed, which might indicate a disordered structure or an undetermined secondary structure. Since it is known that these types of peptides contain a set of cationic amino acids important for biological activity, electrostatic map potentials for each toxin were calculated. Such maps revealed differences in electrostatic distributions for each toxin; that is, the cationic surface is altered because of the spatial rearrangements of residues in each model. This is because the residues Cys1, Lys18, and Lys38 are exposed in the same area of Bs2 when compared to Bs3, which presents the residues Lys38-40 in the opposite direction to that in Bs2 (Fig. 5B and D). The latter can be correlated with the insecticidal activities of Bs2 and Bs3, suggesting better receptor binding. Since the C-terminal of Bs3 does not form a secondary structure, we propose that the unstructured Bs3 C-terminal “tail” could interfere with its receptor or affect an important peptide epitope, making this structural difference an explanation for the difference in insecticidal activity of each toxin.

The structural difference between Bs2 and Bs3 was calculated using Pymol and a high RMSD value of 9 Å was found. Although the identity is 83%, this value is expected taking into account the restructuring of the movement of the last cysteine in Bs3. Maintaining the same folding (three beta sheets) and arrangement of cysteines but presenting disordered zones in opposite regions, the latter being the cause of the high RMSD value.

Discussion

Spiders must adapt to environmental and local prey conditions to survive (Pekar et al. 2018; Pekar and Toft 2015). Most spiders are generalist predators, but some are prey-specialized; therefore, spider venoms must contain prey-specific toxins. Spider venom is unique; it contains acyl-polyamines that produce rapid paralysis and toxins that lead to the flaccid death of their prey (Escoubas et al. 2000). That is, acyl-polyamines have an onset of paralysis that lasts a few minutes, allowing the prey to fight back, unless the prey is wrapped in spider silk, after which peptide toxins kill them (Adams et al. 1989). Spider peptide toxins affect different insect receptors to subdue their prey. These toxin-receptor interactions may be related to the evolution from a generalist to a specialist spider. It is unknown if Brachypelma smithi is a specialist or a generalist spider; however, it has potent insecticidal peptides such as Bs1. Therefore, using oligonucleotides for bioprospecting more insecticidal peptides in its venom, two similar N-terminal ICK spider peptides were found with distinctive C-terminal loops, named Bs2 and Bs3. Nucleotide sequences and molecular evolutionary genetics analysis of Bs2 and Bs3 transcripts disclosed that there are six nucleotide substitutions in Bs3 compared to Bs2, and an additional codon insertion or a substitution in the stop codon was found. Since Bs2 and Bs3 are from the venom of B. smithi, their genomic origin may be the cause of gene duplication events (Lüddecke et al. 2022). That is, the nucleotide substitutions could be due to duplication events on the gene corresponding to the Bs2 (or Bs3) that accumulated mutations throughout its evolutionary history (Schwager et al. 2017). However, the time of duplication is unknown and could not be confirmed, since it would require a more extensive analysis of nucleotide sequences corresponding to the complete genes and the evolutionary rate of theraphosids, of which there is not much information (Wu and Lu 2019). Although there is information comparing it with other species whose evolutionary rate is known, for example, modern spiders, which separated 300 million years ago (Schwager et al. 2017). In the spider cytogenetics database, many chromosomes with n > 40–70 can be observed in the theraphosid group, which is characteristic of species with whole genome duplication (WGD) events in their evolutionary history (Sanggaard et al. 2014).

Although the PD50 values for A. domesticus and G. portentosa were rough estimations (n = 3), the paralytic and killing activity of rBs2 and rBs3 were compared on three insects. The heterologous expression of rBs2 and rBs3 produced fully active peptides, although in low yield, after in vitro folding. The insecticidal spider peptide rBs2 with the connecting loop Cys15-Cys36 was more insecticidal than rBs3 when affecting Galleria mellonella larvae (Lepidoptera). Nevertheless, the insecticidal spider peptide rBs3 with the connecting loop Cys15-Cys30 was more insecticidal than that of rBs2 when affecting Acheta domesticus house crickets (Orthoptera), and Gromphadorhina portentosa cockroaches (Blattodea). Yet, rBs2 prolonged the insect’s paralysis more than that of rBs3 at the same concentrations. Bs2 was isolated in its natural form, but Bs3 was not found in the venom; however, this peptide has been found in the related theraphosid Aphonopelma sp. and named Asp3. Although few 3D structures of American theraphosids exist, the disulfide bridges of Bs2 and Bs3 were assessed only based on the homologous theraphosid toxin GsMTx-4 (PDB: 1TYK) from Grammostola rosea. After in silico modelling, all three have a similar pattern of Cys-Cys. We also found that depending on the structure, they induced significantly different paralysis onset against insects. Multivariate analysis showed significant differences between the two peptides. Structural models of rBs2 and rBs3 show structured C-termini, and they might be the cause of their insecticidal activities in these three different insects. Insecticidal spider peptides are potential novel ion channel modulators for insect cell receptors, and they also represent leads for the biocontrol design of insecticides.

Conclusions

The two toxins exhibit a similar degree of potency, being stronger for rBs2 towards G. mellonella larvae (Lepidoptera), and rBs3 towards A. domesticus house crickets (Orthoptera), and G. portentosa cockroaches (Blattodea). The individual activities of rBs2 and rBs3 towards the three insect species were not differentially affected; that is, rBs2 and rBs3 affect these three species similarly. Peptide toxins have to be in constant evolution; so, the spiders have to produce venom toxin winners towards a specific prey. The insecticidal toxins in Table 1 are a clear example of peptide toxin variability in theraphosid venoms. Much has been described about spider peptide toxins; however, it will be interesting to demonstrate their evolution towards a specific prey. Generalistic spider venoms may also produce specific toxins in significant amounts to subdue their prey. This data supports the hypothesis that the venom of Brachypelma smithi is composed of at least two prey-specific peptides that could be leads for biocontrol.
