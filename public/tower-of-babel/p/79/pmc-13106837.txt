Analysis of a PCORnet® database identifies multi-level predictors of delta hepatitis in a U.S. hotspot
Authors: Lauren Alpert, Xiaotao Zhang, Rachel Smith, Marcia Lange, Tatyana Kushner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Although New York City (NYC) is a hotspot for hepatitis delta virus (HDV) in the United States (US), the epidemiology of HDV remains poorly understood. We aimed to determine HDV testing and positivity rates, calculate prevalence of liver disease progression by HDV status, and assess the association between HDV and community resources.Methods:We utilized the INSIGHT database, which contains data from the five major NYC health systems, to identify adults with laboratory and/or diagnosis code evidence of hepatitis B virus (HBV) from 2010-2023. HDV positivity included HDV RNA ≥ 20 IU/mL and/or positive results for HDV Ab, RNA, and Ag. Prevalence of liver-related complications was calculated. Community deprivation index was used to evaluate socioeconomic disparities by HDV testing and positivity status.Results:Here we show that among 106,210 patients with HBV, 5,388 (5.1%) have received HDV testing; 294 (5.5%) are HDV + . HDV-tested individuals are more likely to be older and Asian, receive comprehensive HBV care, and have more community resources. HDV+ individuals are more likely to be female, White, and have higher prevalence of liver disease progression.Conclusions:HDV is undertested and under-detected, even in a high-prevalence US region. Early linkage to treatment is essential given high rates of liver disease progression. Subject terms: Hepatitis B, Epidemiology, Population screening, Hepatocellular carcinoma, Liver cirrhosis

Introduction

Hepatitis delta virus (HDV) is the most severe form of viral hepatitis, as it is associated with rapid progression to cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and liver transplant1–5. Estimates of HDV prevalence and disease burden vary due to different study methodologies and populations studied, as well as variable HDV testing rates6,7. Most United States (US) studies suggest that the prevalence of HDV positivity among HBV+ individuals is between 2 and 8%3,6–10; many existing analyses are limited by small sample sizes, underrepresentation of high-risk populations, and overrepresentation of select demographic characteristics. Additionally, several US studies have limited access to laboratory data, relying exclusively on diagnosis codes to determine HBV and HDV status and potentially introducing misclassification bias due to coding errors10.

Recent US data have shown that New York State (NYS), and New York City (NYC) in particular, is a geographical “hotspot” for HDV11. NYS has the second-highest prevalence of HDV+ patients residing in urban US ZIP codes10. 1/4 of national HDV+ cases are concentrated in five counties, four of which are in NYS11. The high HDV prevalence in NYC is likely due to its large immigrant population from HBV- and HDV-endemic regions12,13.

The following study analyzes data from the INSIGHT Clinical Research Network (CRN), a robust electronic health record (EHR) database of 13.4 million patients from the five largest academic health systems in NYC, a Patient-centered Clinical Research Network (PCORnet®) partner, and the largest urban clinical network in the nation14. The goal of the study is to determine rates of HDV testing and positivity in a large NYC cohort by leveraging both diagnosis codes and laboratory data. We additionally aim to calculate the prevalence of liver-related disease progression stratified by HDV status and assess the association between HDV testing/positivity and social determinants of health. We incorporate input on study design and analysis from a dedicated community advisory board.

In a diverse patient population in one of the epicenters for HDV in the US, 5.1% of patients with evidence of HBV have received HDV testing; 5.5% of these patients are HDV+. The prevalence of liver-related disease progression, adverse liver-related laboratory outcomes, and liver transplant complications is higher in individuals with HDV. Social deprivation also impacts rates of HDV testing and positivity. Our study points to a lack of provider education on HDV pathogenesis, testing, and management and a need for HDV reflex testing, especially in regions disproportionately impacted by HDV and in low resource communities.

Methods

Data source

INSIGHT is a subset of PCORnet® (https://pcornet.org), a national research network of EHR data. PCORnet® was developed with Patient-centered Outcomes Research Institute funding and is unique in its prioritization of patient-centeredness and engagement; collection of data directly from health systems, rather than insurance claims; and structure that allows healthcare organizations to maintain control over their data, reducing institutional barriers to engagement15. INSIGHT contains deidentified inpatient and outpatient laboratory and diagnosis code EHR data of individuals receiving care at the largest health systems within NYC. It utilizes a common data model that can be queried to evaluate healthcare usage and health outcomes across a large patient cohort representing many insurance types16.

Study sample

This was a longitudinal, retrospective cohort study of patients ≥18 years old in INSIGHT with evidence of HBV from 2010 to 2023. Race and ethnicity were patient-reported and retrieved from the EHR. To enter the cohort, patients were required to have at least one of the following in the EHR: hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) laboratory test with a “positive” or “reactive” qualitative result; HBsAg laboratory test with a quantitative result ≥0.05 IU/mL; or HBV diagnosis code (i.e., international classification of diseases (ICD)-9 or -10 code). HDV testing was defined as the presence of HDV antibody (Ab), RNA, and/or antigen (Ag) laboratory testing at any time during the study period. HDV+ patients included those with HDV Ab, RNA, and/or Ag laboratory testing with a “positive” or “reactive” qualitative result or those with HDV RNA laboratory testing with a quantitative result ≥20 IU/mL17. Selection criteria were determined based on American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) guidelines, which advise risk-based HDV screening in individuals with HBV but also encourage HDV screening in other HBV+ patients at the discretion of the provider18. Patients with HDV laboratory testing (irrespective of HBV status) were identified; individuals with HBV who were never tested for HDV were excluded from statistical analyses (see Supplementary Data 1 and 2 for diagnosis and laboratory codes used).

Exposures and outcomes

Exposures of interest included HDV laboratory testing and positivity. Outcomes of interest included liver-related disease progression stratified by HDV status and community deprivation stratified by HDV testing and positivity.

Statistics and reproducibility

Descriptive statistics were calculated for patients with evidence of HBV and stratified by HDV testing and positivity. Prevalence of HDV testing and positivity was determined. We compared demographic and clinical characteristics using chi-square tests for categorical variables and t-tests for continuous variables. For comparisons of median and interquartile range (IQR), we used Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Utilizing univariate and multivariable logistic regression models, factors associated with HDV testing and positivity were evaluated. Multivariable logistic regression models were adjusted for variables selected a priori and for all variables in the univariate model with p < 0.05. Patients with missing data for the covariates included in multivariable analyses were excluded. The prevalence of liver-related disease and laboratory outcomes was determined in patients with and without HDV. FIB-4 index was calculated using age at cohort entry, maximum alanine aminotransaminase (ALT), maximum aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and minimum platelet count. A survival analysis was also performed to evaluate time from cohort entry (first diagnosis code or laboratory evidence of HBV in INSIGHT) to select liver-related outcomes stratified by HDV status. Using the Nelson–Aalen method for cumulative incidence of liver-related outcomes stratified by HDV status, we generated Kaplan–Meier curves for time to event for each of these outcomes and conducted Gray’s tests. We then performed a multivariable Cox proportion hazard regression model to control for potential confounders. We used a Cox proportional hazards regression to estimate hazard ratios for liver-related outcomes (e.g., liver failure and liver transplant), comparing groups by HDV status while adjusting for covariates. Individuals were censored at the time of last follow-up or at the time of death due to non-liver-related causes. While Cox models assume non-informative censoring and do not account for competing risks, we supplemented these analyses with cumulative incidence functions (CIFs) to estimate the absolute risk over time for each outcome, properly accounting for competing risks (e.g., death or transplant precluding liver failure). We used Gray’s test to compare CIFs between HDV positivity status strata. All statistical tests were two-tailed.

Community deprivation index (CDI), a marker of neighborhood resources based on American Community Survey (ACS) data19, was used to estimate socioeconomic disparities in the study population stratified by HDV testing and positivity. CDI was calculated for each patient with a documented residential US ZIP code based on 2018 census tract-level variables in ACS, as previously described19,20.

All analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (Cary, North Carolina) and R 4.3.2 (Boston, Massachusetts) using the following R packages: ggmap21, pointdensityP22, tigris23, tidycensus24, dplyr25, stringr26, sf27, mapview28, and tmap29. Our study adhered to the STROBE guidelines30.

Community advisory board

We held regular meetings with an HDV community advisory board comprised of HDV + NYC residents and other stakeholders. We provided updates on our study and on progress in the field of HDV research and solicited feedback and input throughout the research process.

Inclusion and ethics

The INSIGHT dataset was created under approval from the BRANY Institutional Review Board (IRB), with reliance from the local IRBs of the following data-contributing sites: Columbia University Irving Medical Center, Montefiore-Einstein, Mount Sinai Health System, NYU Langone, and Weill Cornell Medicine. The data come from routine clinical care and are provided as a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) limited data set (LDS). The IRB granted a waiver of informed consent because the dataset is de-identified at the level allowed for an LDS, the work poses minimal risk, and obtaining consent is not feasible. This type of secondary use of EHR data is permitted under HIPAA and the Affordable Care Act.

This study was deemed exempt human research as defined by the United States Department of Health and Human Services regulations. All research was conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the IRB of the Mount Sinai Health System and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Our study cohort includes 106,210 patients with HBsAg+ laboratory status, an HBV diagnosis code, or both (Supplementary Fig. 1). Seventy-eight thousand two hundred three patients are HBsAg+, and 40,189 patients have an HBV diagnosis code. Individuals with HBV are of median age 41 (IQR: 28, 55); 59.8% female, 49.5% black, 25.3% white, 23.2% Asian, and 31.8% Hispanic.

Prevalence of HDV testing and positivity

A total of 5388 (5.1%) patients have been tested for HDV; 294 (5.5%) are HDV+. Four thousand four hundred seventy-one have received HDV Ab testing, 207 (4.6%) of whom are positive; 250 have received HDV RNA testing, 12 (4.8%) of whom are positive; and 1774 received have HDV Ag testing, 89 (5.0%) of whom are positive. Among 294 HDV+ individuals, 4.1% have RNA-confirmed active infection. 397 patients (7.4%) do not have laboratory evidence of HDV but have an HDV diagnosis code, and 164 (3.0%) are HDV+ based on laboratory testing but do not have an HDV diagnosis code. Out of 294 HDV+ patients, over half (n = 164, 55.8%) do not have an HDV diagnosis code.

Trends in HDV testing and positivity over time

Prevalence of HDV testing and positivity was calculated for each year from 2010 to 2023 (Fig. 1). The lowest HDV screening rate occurred in 2010 (0.6%); the highest occurred in 2023 (51.1%). There is an upward trend in HDV testing from 2019 to 2023. The rate of HDV positivity is lowest in 2012 (2%) and highest in 2018 (9%), with a downward trend in HDV positivity from 2018 to 2023.

Factors associated with HDV testing

HDV tested patients are more likely to be older and Asian; they are less likely to be female, Black, and Hispanic (all p < 0.0001) (Table 1). HDV-tested individuals are more likely to receive the following laboratory tests: HBsAg, hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg), hepatitis B e antibody (HbeAb), either HBeAg or HBeAb, hepatitis B core antibody (HbcAb), and HBV DNA; they also have a higher median HBV DNA (all p < 0.0001). HDV tested patients are less likely to be HBsAg+ and HBcAb+ (p < 0.0001). Additionally, HDV tested individuals are more likely to have the following diagnosis codes: acute HBV, chronic HBV, HBV carrier, HBV/HDV, and other HBV (all p < 0.0001). The mean time from HBV diagnosis to HDV testing is 20.2 months (standard deviation (SD) 33.5 months). Among those with mortality data (N = 271), the mean time from HBV diagnosis to mortality is higher in HDV tested individuals (32.8 vs. 21.9 months, p < 0.0001).

HDV tested patients are more likely to have ALT >2x the upper limit of normal (ULN) (p < 0.0001), an HDV testing criterion per AASLD guidelines18. They are less likely to have HBV DNA <2000 IU/mL (p < 0.0001), though this is also an HDV testing criterion per AASLD recommendations.

HDV tested individuals are more likely to receive hepatitis C virus (HCV) laboratory testing (p < 0.0001) and have an HIV diagnosis code (p = 0.02); they are less likely to have sexually transmitted infection (STI) diagnosis codes, including chlamydia, gonorrhea, human papilloma virus, syphilis, and herpes simplex virus, and less likely receive STI testing (p < 0.0001). The mean CDI is lower for HDV tested compared to untested patients (0.39 vs. 0.46, p < 0.0001), suggesting less resource deprivation among HDV tested patients. In a heat map demonstrating CDI and NYC ZIP code-level rates of HDV testing (Supplementary Fig. 2A), HDV testing appears to occur more often in patients living near flagship hospitals.

On multivariable logistic regression analysis (Table 2), the following are associated with higher odds of HDV testing: HBeAg positivity (odds ratio (OR) = 4.08, 95% confidence interval (CI): 3.61, 4.62), acute HBV diagnosis code (OR = 1.88, 95% CI: 1.68, 2.11), and HCV laboratory testing (OR = 3.16, 95% CI: 2.88, 3.48). The following are associated with lower odds of HDV testing: black (OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.51, 0.65) and Native Hawaiian/other Pacific Islander (OR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.34, 0.83) compared to white race, Hispanic ethnicity (OR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.38, 0.49), HBV DNA <2000 IU/mL (OR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.64, 0.77), HCV (OR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.76, 0.98), HIV (OR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.71, 0.95) and STI (OR = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.44, 0.58) diagnosis codes, and 1 point increase in CDI (OR = 0.09, 95% CI: 0.07, 0.12).

We evaluated rates of HDV testing among patient subgroups with recommended HBV testing, a proposed surrogate of HBV specialty care (Fig. 2). Factors associated with HDV positivity

Patients who tested positive for HDV are more likely to be female (p = 0.005) and white (p < 0.0001); HDV+ individuals are less likely to be Asian (p < 0.0001), black (p < 0.0001), and Hispanic (p = 0.005) (Table 3). Additionally, HDV+ patients are less likely to have a detectable HBV DNA level and chronic HBV diagnosis code (p = 0.04). The median HBV DNA is lower in HDV+ patients (p = 0.001).

With respect to AASLD HDV testing criteria, HDV+ individuals are more likely to have ALT >2x ULN (p < 0.0001), HBV DNA <2000 IU/mL (p = 0.004), and both ALT >2x ULN and HBV DNA <2000 IU/mL (p < 0.0001).

HDV+ individuals with exposure to HDV are also more likely to have an HCV diagnosis code (p < 0.0001), evidence of alcohol use (p = 0.008), and history of cigarette smoking (p = 0.04); they are less likely to have an STI diagnosis code (p = 0.04). The prevalence of select clinical comorbidities, autoimmune diagnoses, and malignancies was calculated in patients with and without HDV (Supplementary Table 1). HDV+ patients are more likely to be diagnosed with hyperlipidemia (p = 0.02), autoimmune hepatitis (p = 0.02), and thyroid cancer (p = 0.006). The mean CDI is lower for HDV+ patients (0.37 vs. 0.39, p = 0.04), suggesting less resource deprivation among HDV+ individuals. In a heat map demonstrating CDI and NYC ZIP code-level rates of HDV positivity (Supplementary Fig. 2B), the residential ZIP codes with the highest HDV positivity rates are in Brooklyn and Queens; these boroughs contain most of the NYC immigrant population31.

On multivariable logistic regression analysis (Table 4 and Supplementary Fig. 3), cirrhosis diagnosis code (OR = 3.05, 95% CI: 2.26, 4.11) and HBV DNA <2000 IU/mL (OR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.10, 2.19) are associated with higher odds of HDV positivity. The following factors are associated with lower odds of HDV positivity: black (OR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.29, 0.68) and Asian (OR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.32, 0.65) compared to white race, Hispanic ethnicity (OR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.24, 0.96), and hyperlipidemia diagnosis code (OR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.39, 0.73).

Liver disease progression

HDV+ patients are more likely to be diagnosed with cirrhosis, ascites, esophageal varices, portal hypertension, liver failure, and HCC (all p < 0.0001); gastric varices (p = 0.01); hepatic encephalopathy (HE) (p = 0.0005); spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (p = 0.02); hepatorenal syndrome (p = 0.03); and portal vein thrombosis (PVT) (p = 0.002) (Supplementary Table 2). They are also more likely to undergo liver transplant (p < 0.0001) and experience transplant complications, including rejection, failure, and infection (p < 0.0001). Mean FIB-4 is higher in HDV+ individuals (p < 0.0001). Compared to patients without RNA-confirmed active infection, those with RNA-confirmed active infection have higher risk of developing liver failure (HR = 5.96, 95% CI: 1.89, 18.83) and HCC (HR = 4.07; 95% CI 1.29, 12.82). Please refer to Supplementary Table 2 for additional liver-related laboratory findings in HDV+ patients.

Kaplan–Meier survival curves demonstrate that time from cohort entry to diagnosis of cirrhosis, HCC, liver failure, and liver transplant is significantly lower in HDV+ patients (all p ≤ 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 4).

On multivariable Cox proportion hazard regression analysis adjusted for age, sex, race, and liver-related comorbidities (Table 5), the following are associated with higher risk of HDV positivity: cirrhosis (hazard ratio (HR) = 1.74, 95% CI: 1.11, 2.44), HCC (HR = 2.08, 95% CI: 1.36, 3.18), liver failure (HR = 2.84, 95% CI: 1.90, 4.24), and liver transplant (HR = 3.04, 95% CI: 1.96, 4.72). A sensitivity analysis was performed excluding diagnosis code-only HBV cases (Supplementary Table 3). Due to reduced sample size, statistical significance is lost for liver-related outcomes that were previously significant (cirrhosis, HCC, liver failure, and liver transplant). However, the direct association between liver-related complications and HDV positivity remains.

Discussion

Our study assesses the prevalence of HDV testing, positivity, and disease progression in one of the epicenters for HDV in the US by leveraging a database with granular EHR-derived sociodemographic, diagnosis, and laboratory data. We demonstrate that there is incomplete correlation between HDV laboratory results and EHR HDV diagnosis codes. Among individuals with evidence of HBV, only 5.1% received HDV testing (though there was an increase in testing after 2019); 5.5% of patients are HDV+. There are significant demographic, clinical, and social differences between patients with and without HDV testing and positivity. Unsurprisingly, the prevalence of liver-related disease progression and adverse liver-related laboratory outcomes is significantly higher in HDV+ patients. Furthermore, HDV+ individuals are more likely to experience liver transplant complications, an association that has not previously been described32. We also demonstrate associations between HDV and hyperlipidemia, autoimmune disease, and thyroid cancer, which should be further investigated. Through a CDI analysis, we found that resource deprivation plays a significant role in both HDV testing and positivity.

To our knowledge, this is the first multicenter study evaluating HDV in NYC. A recent study by Gish et al. estimates a 4.6% prevalence of HDV in the US using a large insurance claims database10. Although this study is unique in its ability to include a large majority of the US insured patient population, defining HDV status only with diagnosis codes may be inaccurate. Among HDV tested patients in INSIGHT, 7% tested negative but still have an HDV diagnosis code in the EHR; 3% tested positive but do not have an HDV diagnosis code. Given the possibility of erroneously classifying HDV status, diagnosis codes alone may be insufficient for estimating the true prevalence of HDV. A strength of INSIGHT is its robust laboratory data, allowing for confirmation of HDV diagnosis codes in the EHR.

Completion of HBV care measures is associated with higher prevalence of HDV testing. Two thousand four hundred forty- six patients received HDV laboratory testing without any evidence of HBV, and nearly one-third of the HDV tested patients received an HDV Ag test, which is not recommended for HDV screening18. This reflects variable provider familiarity with AASLD recommendations18; it may also point to challenges with accessing appropriate HDV tests. The prevalence of HDV Ag testing highlights the need for provider education and increased availability of clinically relevant HDV tests.

Demographic and clinical characteristics of HDV-tested patients differ from characteristics of HDV+ patients, demonstrating provider impact on HDV screening and diagnosis. 42% of HDV tested patients are Asian, but almost half (47%) of HDV+ patients are White. Racial disparities in HDV testing may be attributable to provider racial biases. For instance, providers may over-test Asian individuals for HDV due to an association between HBV and Asian populations, even if a patient’s individual HDV risk is low. Similarly, providers may under-test individuals from other racial backgrounds, even if they are at higher risk for HDV. Additionally, although HDV-tested patients are less likely to have HBV DNA <2000 IU mL, HDV+ individuals are more likely to have HBV DNA <2000 IU/mL. These discrepancies demonstrate the need for provider education. Another potential missed opportunity for HDV screening is during pregnancy, when universal HBV screening is recommended; among patients with HBV and history of pregnancy, less than 5% received HDV testing (Table 1).

HDV positivity is associated with several lifestyle factors. HDV+ patients are more likely to have a history of alcohol use and cigarette smoking. Although previous studies have demonstrated a strong association between HDV and intravenous drug use33, we do not see a statistically significant difference in substance use stratified by HDV status. However, our analysis may be limited by few patients having a documented history of substance use.

To delineate resource scarcity as a contributor to HDV epidemiology, we utilize CDI to evaluate the association between neighborhood-level deprivation and HDV testing and positivity. We found that patients with HDV testing and positivity live in ZIP codes with a lower mean CDI (i.e., more resources), and those screened for HDV lived in close proximity to flagship NYC hospitals. CDI is well-suited to determining resource deprivation in NYC because, unlike other indices, it does not include home or vehicle ownership as a marker of socioeconomic status. Since nearly 70% of NYC residents rent their homes34, home ownership would not accurately reflect regional resource deprivation.

According to recent US Census Bureau data, 36.3% of the NYC population is foreign-born35, which is significantly higher than the national 13.9% prevalence of foreign-born individuals36; 2.7% of the NYC population is estimated to have HBV37. Although NYS and NYC have been identified as national hotspots for HDV in other studies10, the epidemiology of HDV in NYC was previously poorly understood. Our study population has received healthcare in all five boroughs of the city and resides in 180 NYC ZIP codes. 9 ZIP codes with the highest HDV prevalence are in Brooklyn, and 4 are in Queens; these two boroughs account for two-thirds of the NYC immigrant population38. Patients with more resources are more likely to be linked to comprehensive HBV care and receive an HDV diagnosis. This strengthens the argument for HDV reflex testing, which would make the identification and management of HDV more equitable for all patients.

Our study has several limitations. Diagnosis codes are used to identify some cases of HBV and several clinical comorbidities, and there may be a possibility of miscoding and subsequent misclassification bias (as we saw with HDV-positive patients). If patients with HDV are misclassified as having HBV only, subsequent analyses may be biased towards the null. Our definition of HDV positivity includes some HDV Ab+ patients without evidence of HDV RNA positivity; these individuals may not have active HDV infection. We do not have RNA viremia results for every patient due to inadequate HDV RNA testing in our patient population. Despite this, we still see an association between HDV-positive status and many adverse clinical outcomes. Additionally, there may be selection bias with respect to the HDV positivity rate, as only those who received HDV testing are included in subsequent statistical analyses. Patients with increased engagement in the healthcare system may be healthier, potentially biasing towards the null when reporting outcomes. There may also be some degree of socioeconomic selection bias, since INSIGHT only includes individuals with access to healthcare. Although there is some inherent degree of bias in our study, the magnitude is unlikely to change the directions of the associations found. Additionally, INSIGHT does not contain data on country of origin or languages spoken, making it difficult to pinpoint the immigrant populations at highest risk for HDV. Furthermore, alcohol and tobacco use are identified based on diagnosis codes alone, so it is not possible to evaluate for a dose-response relationship between substance use and HDV positivity. The large immigrant population of NYC may reduce the generalizability of our study’s findings to other regions of the US. Lastly, while CDI accounts for multiple markers of neighborhood resources to provide a more balanced perspective, it is subject to confounding. Specifically, it does not account for cultural differences in views on Western healthcare or language barriers that may prevent a person from seeking proactive care. Geographic distance to healthcare facilities is also not included.

Within a diverse patient cohort in one of the US epicenters for HDV, we found clear demographic, clinical, and socioeconomic differences between patients with and without HDV testing and positivity. Although there was a sharp increase in HDV testing starting in 2019, likely related to updated AASLD HBV guidelines18, still only about half of patients with HBV received HDV testing in 2023. We also demonstrate that diagnosis codes may be an inaccurate tool for quantifying the prevalence of HDV. Our findings highlight the importance of prioritizing provider education on HDV pathogenesis, testing, and management and the benefits of implementing HDV reflex testing, particularly in regions disproportionately affected by HDV and in lower resource communities. Given the well-established morbidity and mortality associated with HDV infection, and confirmed in our study, early detection and treatment of HDV in these populations is of paramount importance. Implementation of reflex testing in the US, as has already been done in our health system39, will improve efficiency and reduce gaps in HDV screening, simplify HDV testing for providers, and facilitate more frequent HDV screening and linkage to care for patients. Since the CDC recommends one-time HBV screening for all adults in the US, this would be an optimal time to perform reflex testing and integrate HDV screening into existing clinical workflows40. Furthermore, with recent approval of HDV therapeutics internationally and hopefully imminent approval in the US, earlier detection will be necessary to optimize the disease course in HDV+ patients.
