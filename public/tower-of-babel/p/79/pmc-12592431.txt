ResNet-EfficientNet powered framework for high-precision cough-based classification of infectious diseases
Authors: Dhana Sony Johnson, Paramasivam Alagumariappan, Malathy Sathyamoorthy, Md Shohel Sayeed, Parag Ravikant Kaveri, Pittu Pavan Sai Kiran Reddy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCOVID-19 is a extremely contagious disease triggered by the SARS-CoV-2 virus which mostly affects the human breathing system. Furthermore, the COVID-19 was emerged in late 2019 and escalated rapidly into a global pandemic which impacted health and economic challenges across globe. Similar to other infectious diseases, it transmits through respiratory droplets and the rapid diagnosis is more important in controlling transmission and managing patient health care. In this work, a deep learning framework for COVID-19 classification using cough sounds has been proposed. Furthermore, the various deep learning models such as one-dimensional Convolutional Neural Network (1D-CNN), Depth-wise Convolutional Neural Network (DS-CNN), EfficientNet v2 and ResNet are utilized for the identification of normal and cough sounds produced by COVID-19. Also, the performances of all the deep learning models are analyzed using performance metrics such as accuracy, recall, precision, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), F1_Score and False Positive Rate (FPR). Results demonstrate that the performance of pre-trained models namely EfficientNet v2 and ResNet is better when compared to existing Deep Learning (DL) models. Additionally, the accuracy, precision, recall, F1_Score, MCC and false positive rate of ResNet is 98.5%, 98.99, 98, 0.9849, 0.9699 and 0.01 respectively shows that the ResNet is superior to the other models. Introduction

Infectious diseases cause widespread illness, high mortality, and significant economic disruption, and also continuously presented significant risks to public health throughout human history1. The methods in the existing systems such as PCR testing and clinical assessments, continue to fall short in providing quick, scalable, and easily accessible answers2. Even though these conventional techniques are frequently accurate, they typically need specialized labs, expensive equipment, and skilled workers and resources, which is not always accessible, particularly in underdeveloped areas.

However, there has been a notable change in the past decade, mostly due to the growth of Machine Learning (ML) and Artificial Intelligence (AI) applications in the healthcare industry3. By providing methods to automate intricate diagnostic procedures and lessen reliance on physical infrastructure, these technologies have created new opportunities. The analysis of cough sounds is an intriguing and comparatively unexplored domain, which needs attention. One of the most prevalent symptoms of a variety of respiratory conditions is coughing. Interestingly, coughs have rich acoustic signatures that can reveal subtle physiological changes in the respiratory system, despite which is being ignored in the existing systems and techniques4. They are highly desirable, non-invasive, and widely accessible biomarker for prompt and early disease detection.

Researchers initially using simple machine learning methods that employed manually created features taken from audio recordings5. These early models have drawbacks, particularly when faced with the complexity of real-world audio data, identified with many areas of biomedical signal processing. Performance varies by variables such as patient diversity, recording device variations, and ambient noise6. The COVID-19 pandemic emergency is the major reason for extending interest in this area. A number of projects, including the Coswara Project and the MIT Open Voice Consortium, set out to crowdsource sizable datasets of cough and respiratory sounds in response to the unprecedented testing shortages7. Research has advanced results due to these efforts, but issues with generalizability, data quality, and recording condition standardization have persisted obstinately8.

In the existing systems, deep learning framework developed to categorize infectious diseases from cough recordings. Convolutional and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) are combined in the model architecture to capture the temporal dynamics and spatial patterns present in cough sounds9. The raw audio was converted into time–frequency representations such as Mel-spectrograms and Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs)10 in order to close the gap between laboratory performance and real-world reliability. Aggressive augmentation techniques, such as pitch shifting, noise injection, and time masking11 has been employed, to replicate the chaotic conditions found outside of clinical settings. In order to reduce the computation in the model’s decision-making process, explainability mechanisms has been incorporated using visualization tools namely Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) in a field where trust is crucial12. In clinical applications, transparent reasoning is not merely a benefit; it is a necessary first step in the adoption process. This study bridges the gap between technical innovation and clinical trust by essentially contributing a carefully tailored deep learning model for cough-based disease detection, an evaluation under challenging acoustic scenarios to assess generalizability, and a strong emphasis on explainable AI. Even in the most resource-constrained environments, this work represents a modest but significant step toward the realization of scalable, reasonably priced, and trustworthy diagnostics. Literature survey

Global public health systems are overwhelmed by respiratory illnesses, which have major social and economic impacts in addition to personal suffering. One of the best strategies to stop the spread of these diseases and enhance patient outcomes was the early diagnosis of the disease. However, traditional diagnostic tools are slow, costly, and unavailable to many, especially in underdeveloped areas. This fact has sparked a continuous search for more economical and scalable alternative diagnostic techniques. It is interesting to note that one technique that is gaining traction is the analysis of cough sounds, a straightforward and non-invasive symptom that cuts across demographics, geography, and language. The significant technological turning points, discoveries, and enduring difficulties that characterize the developing field of cough-based disease detection has presented below.

Audio-based illness identification: a new area

Clinicians noticed that some lung conditions produced unique auscultatory sounds since twentieth century, but the formal computational methods have started to develop in the recent days. Traditional signal processing techniques, which relied on feature extraction techniques namely wavelet transforms and spectral analysis, dominated early efforts. De Lima et al.13 successful classification of coughs associated with pneumonia and other respiratory conditions using spectrogram features in conjunction with Support Vector Machines (SVMs) is a noteworthy example. Although these early models provided proof of concept, they mainly relied on manual feature engineering, a laborious procedure prone to bias and inconsistency from humans.

Deep learning models could learn rich representations straight from raw data, eliminating the need for manually created features, in contrast to previous methods. The famous discovery by Rajpurkar et al.14 that a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) could diagnose pneumonia from chest X-rays more accurately than radiologists sparked interest in using similar methods for cough sound analysis. This motivation led to a new surge in interest in audio-driven diagnostics when Zhang et al.15 created a CNN-based classifier that demonstrated exceptional accuracy in identifying COVID-19 infections solely from cough recordings.

Classifying cough sounds using deep learning architectures

CNNs have become the industry standard for processing cough audio data among a variety of deep learning models. They are good at treating cough sounds namely visual patterns because they can recognize spatial hierarchies in spectrogram representations. Taking advantage of this ability, Yamamoto et al.16 successfully used CNNs to categorize respiratory sounds from patients suffering from Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD). In a similar vein, Tian et al.17 investigated COVID-19 coughs and showed that CNNs could accurately identify changes in the disease’s associated cough duration, frequency, and intensity.

CNNs are unable to adequately address the problem of coughs’ temporal nature, or how they evolve and change over time. Researchers are increasingly using RNN, especially Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, to capture these dynamics for the sequential data. By maintaining the temporal relationships within respiratory sounds, RNNs can enhance classification performance, as demonstrated by Hershey et al.18. Given that CNNs and RNNs have complementary strengths, hybrid models that combine the two architectures have demonstrated even more promise. The potential of combining spatial and temporal feature extraction was demonstrated by Liu et al.19, who created a CNN-RNN hybrid that demonstrated remarkable accuracy in differentiating between influenza and COVID-19 coughs.

Present difficulties in analyzing cough sounds

A number of significant obstacles still impede the development of cough-based diagnostic tools. Dataset variability is a significant obstacle. Depending on the device type (smartphone, laptop, professional microphone), background noise levels, patient demographics (age, gender, smoking history), and even cultural variations in coughing behavior, cough recordings can vary significantly. A lack of generalizability is highlighted by the fact that models trained in one environment frequently perform poorly when tested in another. Researchers have looked into data augmentation techniques namely SpecAugment20, which purposefully warp training data to more accurately represent variability in the real world, in an effort to address this problem. SpecAugment was initially created for speech recognition, but it has demonstrated potential when modified for cough classification tasks.

The lack of sizable, varied, and carefully selected datasets is equally urgent. Important contributions namely Coswara are among the many existing repositories that are heavily biased toward particular diseases (most notably COVID-19) and frequently do not represent a wide range of illnesses and patient profiles. Furthermore, inconsistent recording methods—different equipment, varying noise levels, or even different patient instructions—introduce additional noise into the datasets, which hinders the ability of models to generalize to new populations.

The opacity of the majority of deep learning models is arguably the most worrisome problem, particularly from a clinical perspective. Even with their exceptional ability to predict outcomes, many models act as mysterious “black boxes,” providing little information about the decision-making process. In the healthcare industry, where trust, accountability, and the capacity to justify clinical judgments are critical, this lack of transparency presents a significant obstacle to adoption. Thankfully, there is hope thanks to recent developments in Explainable AI (XAI). Researchers and clinicians can see inside the model and determine which features most affected a given decision by using visualization techniques namely Grad-CAM and SHAP. A significant step was taken towards more interpretable systems, whereas Jain et al.21 successfully highlighted important acoustic features associated with various diseases by applying Grad-CAM to cough classification models. Using models such as ResNet and EfficientNet, recent research has made significant progress in applying deep learning to cough-based disease classification. A thorough analysis of deep learning techniques for COVID-19 cough detection was presented by Nguyen et al.22,who also highlighted important developments in feature selection and model design. In order to increase accuracy, Singh et al.23 presented a CNN-based framework that was improved with spectrogram augmentation and attention mechanisms. A hybrid EfficientNet model with multi-scale feature learning was proposed by Gao et al.24 and showed excellent cough recognition performance. Similar to this, Li et al.25 improved detection accuracy by combining Transformer modules with ResNet to capture spectral and temporal patterns in audio signals. Hossain et al.26 provides a CNN-ResNet hybrid system designed for respiratory disease classification.

In their comparison of the EfficientNet and DenseNet architectures, Kapoor and Singh27 found that EfficientNet performed well in the analysis of cough sounds. Alkhodair et al.28 developed an EfficientNet model for respiratory sound classification with an improved accuracy. Mehmood et al.29 used transfer learning on the Coswara dataset for effective classification, while Park et al.30 extended the input modality by incorporating cough and breathing signals into a multi-modal learning framework. In order to improve the transparency of CNN-based cough classifiers, Zargari and El-Khatib31 used explainable AI techniques. In order to increase detection consistency across a variety of data samples, Yang et al.32 investigated deep ensemble learning techniques. Prasad and Dutta33 showed how EfficientNet-B3 and spectrogram fusion work well together to increase classification accuracy.

Scalable diagnosis became possible by Chowdhury et al.’s adaptation of a ResNet101 model for mobile-collected cough data34. ElTantawy et al.35 employed CNN for cough analysis in the detection of flu and pneumonia. Using cough characteristics, Arora et al.36 demonstrated an attention-based model that can distinguish between COVID-19 and asthma. Fong and Dey37 designed ResNet-based ensemble mechanism incorporated with spectrogram features provides the accurate classification of respiratory diseases. Collectively, the studies38–40 highlight the developing possibilities of hybrid and deep convolutional architectures in high-precision disease detection systems.

A number of important obstacles still stand in the way of these technologies’ widespread clinical adoption, even though considerable results has been achieved in applying DL techniques to cough-based disease classification. One of the main obstacles to adoption is still the discrepancy between experimental success and real-world dependability. When taken as a whole, these difficulties show how urgent it is that future studies go beyond basic performance indicators. A model’s accuracy under ideal circumstances is insufficient. Diagnostic frameworks need to be sensitive to uncommon conditions, resilient to real-world variability, and, most importantly, transparent enough to gain the medical community’s trust. If cough-based disease classification is to be a reliable tool in routine clinical practice, systems that satisfy these high standards must be developed.

Proposed methodology

Architecture of the proposed framework

In this work, a normal and COVID-19 cough sounds are collected from the Kaggle COVID-19 cough sound database41 and these datasets was created from the samples collected from COSWARA7. The COSWARA project followed a standard protocol in which both COVID-19 positive and normal individuals are explicitly instructed to produce coughs through smartphone or laptop microphone in a controlled environment especially quiet ambiance. The COVID-19 positive status was primarily self-reported and in most of the cases based on PCR/antigen test confirmation at the time of recording. Also, the dataset documentation indicates that participants typically provided multiple cough sound repetitions during recording sessions. Additionally, the metadata does not suggest a systematic imbalance between extracted vs. spontaneous cough sounds across COVID-19 positive and healthy groups. Further, the collected cough sounds are preprocessed using two different techniques namely resampling (to 48 kHz) and normalization. The resampled and normalized cough sounds were augmented using waveform-level and spectrogram-level augmentation techniques. Once the preprocessing and augmentation is done, all the dataset splits are carried out at the subject level. Also, it is ensured that the recordings from a given individual are confined to a single subset includes training, validation and test. Specifically, 40% of the original and non-augmented cough samples are reserved as a visualized test dataset. Also, the COVID-19 positive vs. healthy ratio are preserved. Furthermore, the rest of the 60% of original samples together with their augmented peers are used to form the training/validation set. Within this set, an 80%/20% subject-wise split are followed to produce the training-validation sets. Additionally, the augmented versions of the original signals are included only in training-validation sets and not leaked into the test set of various DL models. Also, the model interpretability techniques were utilized to investigate model’s prediction for a specific class. Additionally, the model testing was carried out and the performance were analyzed using various performance metrices through classification output.

Preprocessing

Raw respiratory audio clips were first resampled to a uniform rate at 48 kilo Hertz (kHz) and normalized in length. To minimize the impact of sensitivity and distance of recording device and cough effort, each audio utterance was normalized using amplitude normalization. Additionally, after resampling and normalization, the RMS-based loudness normalization is applied to change all the recordings to a consistent perceptual level which ensures that the classification decisions could not be negligibly based on overall energy. Short recordings were zero-padded and longer ones randomly clipped to a fixed duration (e.g., 7 s) to ensure consistent input length. Signal augmentation

In signal augmentation, the two different techniques namely waveform-level and spectrogram-level augmentation were applied to increase robustness and reduce overfitting. On the raw audio, random perturbations such as additive Gaussian noise, random gain namely volume change, and small pitch shifts were applied. For example, Gaussian noise with a randomly chosen Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) was added to simulate background noise, and the audio amplitude was scaled by a random dB gain in a specified range. On the extracted mel-spectrograms, a form of SpecAugment was employed by randomly masking out contiguous frequency bands and time frames: for each spectrogram, a random frequency band up to 15% of the mel bins was selected and zeroed it out, and similarly a random span of time frames up to, say, 25% of the temporal dimension was masked21,42–44. This spectral masking forces the network to rely on distributed features and improves invariance to occluded or noisy frequencies. Through these augmentations, each original recording yields multiple modified versions, effectively enlarging the training set and helping the models generalize to variations in recording conditions.

Data splitting

The original and augmented cough sounds were split into training, validation, and test sets with careful stratification. Specifically, a fixed percentage of 40% original non-augmented COVID-positive and healthy cough sounds was set aside as an unseen test set, preserving the class ratio. The remaining 60% of the original cough sounds were combined with all augmented samples to form the pool for model fitting. This pool was then split typically 80%/20% into a training set and a held-out validation set, again stratified so that each subset maintains the original balance of COVID vs. non-COVID labels7,43,44. Importantly, augmented copies of a given original sample were kept in the training/validation pool but not leaked into the test set. Class-imbalance was addressed by using weighted sampling or by assigning class weights inversely proportional to class frequency during training. This splitting protocol ensures that evaluation metrics (on the untouched test set) reflect true generalization, while the training set is enriched by data augmentation.

Model interpretability with Grad-CAM and Eigen-CAM

A preliminary interpretability study using two popular visualization techniques: Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) and Eigen-CAM has been conducted before proceeding to the full-scale training of deep learning models. The primary objective of this investigation was to ensure that the models learned meaningful signal characteristics, rather than overfitting to irrelevant artifacts such as silent regions, background noise, or augmented artifacts45–47. To achieve this, a lightweight ResNet-18 model was trained for a few epochs on a reduced subset of approximately 10% of the training dataset. After obtaining preliminary convergence, interpretability maps were generated for randomly selected validation samples using both Grad-CAM and Eigen-CAM.

EfficientNetV2-S begins processing the input through Fused-MBConv blocks, where early layers use normal convolution operations to speed up training, and later layers use Mobile Inverted Bottleneck Convolutions (MBConv) to extract more abstract representations. Initially, the model extracts simple spectro-temporal features namely energy shifts and spectral ridges common in coughs. As the network deepens, it learns more abstract patterns such as the roughness, pitch dynamics, and other nuanced characteristics of pathological coughs51,52. The use of squeeze-and-excitation (SE) mechanisms inside MBConv blocks allows the model to perform channel-wise attention, adapting to important frequency bands that characterize cough events. The final output layers involve global average pooling and a dense classifier, converting the feature map into class probabilities.

Conclusion and future scope

Rapid screening plays crucial role in preventing the transmission of COVID-19 and managing patient care. In this work, a deep learning framework for the automated classification of COVID-19 using cough sounds were proposed for the rapid screening of COVID-19 disease. Additionally, the four different deep learning models were utilized and the performance metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1_Score, MCC and FPR were evaluated. The model interpretability techniques namely as Grad-CAM and Eigen-CAM were utilized to investigate the model’s prediction through meaningful signal characteristics. Results demonstrate that the ResNet-18 has the highest precision of 98.99% exhibiting its superior ability in identifying positive COVID-19 cases with minimal false positives ranging 0.01. Also, the stability and consistency of model performance is demonstrated from the whisker’s plot; it is observed that the ResNet-18 has higher median accuracy of 96% and the EfficientNet v2 exhibits second-highest median accuracy of around 94–95% which is nearly comparable to the performance of ResNet-18. Overall results show the dominance of ResNet-18 model in terms of both accuracy and precision shows its robustness and reliability for diagnosis of COVID-19 using cough sounds. Finally, the ResNet-18 model is found to be an effective model for real-world deployment in early detection of COVID-19. Future work shall be extended to multiple respiratory diseases classification including bronchitis etc. alongside COVID-19, enabling a comprehensive respiratory screening system. Also, the dataset shall be expanded in near future with greater diversity and investigated with lightweight architectures namely MobileNet, EfficientNet-lite to validate the model generalizability and to ensure practical deployment.
