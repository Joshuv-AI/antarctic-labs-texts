A Physics-Informed Neural Network Framework Integrating Soft and Hard Constraints for Predicting Biomass Gasification Syngas Compositions
Authors: Qilin Zou, He Huang, Xing Liu, Jiongming Ding, Shaojun Ren, Fengqi Si
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Machine learning methods have demonstrated promising applications in biomass gasification modeling. However, conventional machine learning models primarily rely on experimental data and do not account for the reaction mechanisms of gasification. When data samples are insufficient, the correlations learned by the model can deviate substantially from mechanistic laws. In this study, a method for predicting biomass gasification product distribution based on physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) was proposed to apply on a biomass gasification problem with a small data size. This method seamlessly integrates real experimental data with prior mechanistic knowledge by embedding boundary constraints and monotonic relationships among key variables into an artificial neural network (ANN). Hard constraints were enforced via a normalized output layer, while soft constraints were applied as a monotonicity penalty in the loss function. Results show that the proposed PINN model achieves a coefficient of determination (R 2) greater than 0.89 and a root-mean-square error (RMSE) less than 4%. The overall predictive accuracy is superior to that of other three purely data-fitting machine-learning modelsrandom forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), and ANN. Furthermore, the PINN model strictly adheres to boundary constraints and prior mechanistic monotonic relationships, exhibiting better interpretability and generalization capabilities.

Introduction

With the continuous rise in global energy demand and escalating concerns over climate change, establishing a diversified and sustainable energy production system has become increasingly critical. , As the only renewable carbon source, biomass presents a viable alternative to fossil fuels and offers the potential to reduce greenhouse gas emissions, positioning it as a key component in future net-zero-energy systems. , Biomass gasification, a well-established thermochemical process, converts biomass waste into value-added syngas, primarily composed of H2, CO, and CH4. This syngas can be directly used as a gaseous fuel for heat and power generation or further upgraded into biofuels and high-value chemicals, thereby enhancing the strategic role of biomass in energy transition.

However, different application scenarios impose different requirements on the syngas composition. For example, when syngas is used for heat or electricity generation via combustion, a high yield of H2, CO, and CH4 is preferred. In contrast, a proper H2/CO ratio is crucial for efficient conversion in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. To meet these diverse demands, researchers have developed various strategies, including optimizing reactor structures, selecting appropriate gasification agents, and screening catalytic materials. Moreover, syngas composition and related parameterssuch as the H2/CO ratio, cold gas efficiency, and heating valuealso depend on operating conditions and feedstock properties. Therefore, precise regulation of operating variables is essential to optimize product distribution and improve gasification efficiency. , This necessity for precise control has propelled the development of dedicated optimization methodologies, making it a significant research focus.

To address this need for control, researchers have increasingly relied on numerical modeling, including two main paradigms: mechanism-based models and machine learning (ML)-based models. Mechanism-based models describe the physicochemical phenomena in biomass gasifiers, such as chemical reactions, heat and mass transfer, and gas–solid flow. These models can be categorized into three approaches: thermodynamic equilibrium models, kinetic models, and computational fluid dynamics (CFD) models. Thermodynamic equilibrium models assume ideal equilibrium conditions and simplify the gasifier structure and reaction times, making them computationally simple and fast. However, such models do not provide general empirical expressions that cover broad operating conditions and often suffer significant prediction errors outside calibrated ranges. Kinetic models incorporate a more detailed understanding of the gasifier structure, reaction kinetics, and transport processes. , With advances in computational power, CFD-based kinetic modeling has also been applied to biomass gasification. Nevertheless, kinetic models require detailed chemical kinetics and transport parameters that are difficult to obtain directly and often must be determined empirically through iterative trial and error. , The complex and partially understood nature of the biomass gasification reaction presents a significant challenge for mechanism-based models to achieve both high prediction accuracy and low computational cost. ,

Alternatively, ML-based models are constructed by mining and learning from experimental data, implicitly capturing the underlying reaction mechanisms of biomass gasification. ML methods can learn complex processes and offer advantages such as superior fitting accuracy, lower modeling cost, and flexible model forms. Predictive modeling of biomass gasification using ML approaches has attracted widespread interest. For example, artificial neural networks (ANNs) and support vector machines (SVMs) have been applied to gasification modeling, demonstrating good predictive performance. , However, experimental data are the primary source of knowledge for ML models, and a reliable model typically requires a sufficiently large training data set. Biomass gasification involves many influencing factors, making it difficult to provide training data with adequate density and coverage, even though researchers have conducted numerous experiments and accumulated substantial data. Insufficiently trained ML models often suffer from overfitting, underfitting, poor extrapolation, and a mismatch with underlying physical laws. , Consequently, many researchers have turned to decision-tree-based ensemble learning methods for predictive modeling. Ensemble methods such as random forest (RF) and gradient-boosted regression (GBR) have demonstrated strong predictive performance and generalization capabilities in various fields. − Although these ensemble models show high fitting accuracy, ensuring appropriate predictive behavior outside the training domain remains a challenge.

In response to limited experimental data and the risk of physically inconsistent predictions, researchers have turned to physics-informed machine learning (PIML) to improve model robustness and interpretability. PIML leverages prior physics knowledge to mitigate the challenges posed by limited domain expertise, thereby enhancing model interpretability and generalization. Researchers have demonstrated that PIML can achieve robust performance even with limited training data, and it has been successfully applied in fields such as computational fluid dynamics and the solution of partial differential equations. Among various PIML paradigms, physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) typically incorporate physical knowledge via soft constraints (penalty-based terms in the loss function). , In contrast, hard constraints refer to hard enforcement. Conventional loss-based PINNs provide no formal guarantee of hard enforcement, so predictions may violate physical laws when extrapolated beyond the training domain. Therefore, this loss-based formulation treats the governing physics as soft constraints, offering no formal guarantee of strictly satisfying them. Even if these constraints are well-satisfied on the training data, a model’s predictions may violate physical laws when extrapolated beyond the training domain. Consequently, researchers have explored strategies to enforce constraints more rigorously in PINNs. Lu et al. proposed a physics-informed neural network with hard constraints (hPINN) for inverse design, leveraging penalty and augmented Lagrangian techniques to ensure strict satisfaction of governing PDE constraints. Ma et al. implemented a Lagrange multiplier-based correction approach to strictly impose elemental mass and energy balance constraints in reduced-order model outputs. Chen et al. introduced a KKT-hPINN architecture that employs KKT-derived projection layers to guarantee that linear equality constraints are exactly satisfied by the neural network predictions.

In biomass gasification modeling, research has primarily focused on embedding two key forms of prior knowledge: physical boundary conditions, such as non-negativity and unit-sum constraints, to ensure feasible predictions and monotonic relationships with respect to critical operating parameters like temperature, equivalence ratio, and moisture content. One line of work adopts soft constraints, incorporating monotonicity priors as penalty terms in the loss function to guide model trends under data-limited conditions. Another strategy employs hard constraints, specifically through a constrained particle swarm optimization algorithm, to hard-enforce predefined monotonicity relationships while maintaining alignment with experimental observations. However, both of these methods address only monotonicity without integrating physical boundary constraints. A subsequent approach seeks to coembed both boundary conditions and monotonicity; however, it implements boundary constraints in a soft manner, failing to guarantee hard enforcement of physical feasibility. Separately, representation learning techniques have been developed to improve generalization across diverse types of feedstock by constructing latent representations of biomass properties and imposing monotonicity via evolutionary algorithms with inequality constraints. While this enhances model adaptability, it introduces significant pipeline complexity through additional training stages and auxiliary modules, thereby hindering simplicity and practical reproducibility. Collectively, these strategies highlight two pertinent limitations: the absence of a unified framework that can strictly enforce physical boundary conditions while simultaneously incorporating monotonicity priors and the reliance on complex proceduressuch as multistage training or heuristic searchthat compromise methodological elegance and straightforward replication.

Therefore, this study proposes a physics-informed neural network framework integrating soft and hard constraints for predicting the syngas compositions (H2, CO, CO2, CH4, and N2) in biomass gasification. The main novelty of this work is the structural integration of physical constraints within a unified trainable framework. Specifically, physical boundary conditions are satisfied by construction through output parametrization (hard constraint), while monotonicity knowledge is incorporated as a gradient-based soft prior during training. This unified design ensures boundary feasibility and monotonic physical behavior within a single end-to-end model, rather than treating these constraints as independent or purely penalty-based components. Boundary Constraints

To strictly enforce the physical boundary conditions of gas product volume fractionsnamely, non-negativity of each component and a unity-sum constraint, which is equivalent to 100% when compositions are reported in vol %we impose these constraints at the model’s output layer as a hard constraint.

The particular-solution u part(x) branch is implemented as a neural network and trained using the same form of loss function as that for the residual-correction branch. It serves as a baseline predictive model that provides a reasonable level of prediction accuracy and physical consistency under the hard boundary framework. Building upon this baseline, the residual correction branch is subsequently employed to compensate for the remaining modeling error, thereby further enhancing both the physical consistency and the predictive accuracy of the proposed model.

The distance-based gate is defined as 7 D(x)=∏m=1M[1−exp(−(xm−cm)2sm)] where x m denotes the m-th normalized input feature and s m and c m are the corresponding center and scale statistics estimated from the training set, respectively. It should be emphasized that D(x) is not responsible for enforcing the boundary constraints themselves; instead, its role is to regulate the strength of the residual correction. PINN Modeling Method Incorporating Monotonicity and Boundary Constraints

To strengthen the model’s adherence to physical priors, we incorporate two complementary mechanisms into the proposed PINN framework: boundary-based hard constraints that are enforced by construction through the network output parametrization and monotonicity-based soft constraints that are enforced during training through an additional loss term. In this way, the model can satisfy essential physical boundary requirements inherently while still being guided toward physically consistent trends under different operating conditions.

The predicted syngas volume fractions are constrained to be non-negative and to sum to unity by applying a softmax mapping at the output layer. Specifically, the network output is formulated as the combination of a particular solution and a modulated residual network, followed by the softmax transformation. Because this constraint is embedded in the output formulation, the predicted compositions strictly satisfy the boundary conditions by design, rather than being approximated via penalty terms in the loss function.

With the hard constraints guaranteed structurally, the training objective focuses on fitting the experimental data and incorporating additional physical priors that are not strictly enforceable by parametrization alone. Accordingly, the total loss consists of three components: the regression loss E R , the structural regularization loss E S , and the monotonicity loss E M , as summarized in eq . Construction of Synthetic Samples

Unlike actual experimental samples, each synthetic sample that is used to enforce monotonicity relationships into the model consists of two components: (1) the model’s input parameters and (2) the formalized representation of the mechanistic constraints. Taking the monotonicity constraint between input parameter A and output parameter B as an example, this formalized representation is its monotonicity factor, where a value of 1 is assigned for monotonic increase and −1 for monotonic decrease. Thus, synthetic samples do not include the model’s output values and can therefore be directly generated based on mechanistic constraints without incurring experimental costs.

The process of generating synthetic samples includes the following steps: (1) Identify n 1 = 54 biomass feedstock categories and n 2 = 18 gasification process configurations across the 200 experimental samplesspecifically, the operating mode, reactor type, gasifying agent, catalyst usage, bed material, and system scale; (2) within reasonable variation ranges, sample n 3 = 6 and n 4 = 9 times for gasification temperature (550–1050 °C) and equivalence ratio (ER, 0.1–0.9); and (3) through the cross-combination of biomass different types of feedstock, gasification modes, and operating variables (gasification temperature and ER), generate N syn = n 1×n 2×n 3×n 4 = 54 × 18 × 6 × 9 = 52488 synthetic samples. By minimizing the monotonicity loss, the space of the neural network weights and bias are restricted when using these synthetic samples as part of the training samples. It should be noted that synthetic samples are not involved in the calculation of regression loss.

Training of the PINN Model

This section describes the training strategy for the proposed PINN, including the preparation of real and synthetic samples, the construction of physics-consistent objectives, and the gradient-based optimization procedures. The training workflow is organized as a two-stage procedure, in which the particular-solution branch first provides a physically feasible baseline prediction and the residual-correction branch is subsequently used to further refine the model output. Figure provides a high-level overview of the end-to-end training workflow and the interaction between data-driven learning and physics guidance.

PINN model with embedded monotonicity and boundary constraints.

Figure shows the schematic overview of the proposed PINN training workflow integrating synthetic sample construction and physics-guided learning with boundary (hard) and monotonicity (soft) constraints for syngas composition prediction.

Let θ = {W,b} denote the collection of neural network weights and biases. During training, these parameters are optimized to minimize the total loss defined in Section 2.3. Forward propagation yields the predicted syngas compositions, while the required loss-term derivatives are obtained via PyTorch’s automatic differentiation (backpropagation). Parameter updates use the Adam optimizer (a stochastic gradient descent method with adaptive moment estimation). At each iteration, gradients ∇θ E are used to adjust θ, thereby decreasing the objective. This gradient-based loop continues until convergence criteria are met (e.g., a maximum number of epochs or loss stagnation).

In addition, monotonicity regularization is incorporated into the same differentiable computational graph as the data-fitting objective. Specifically, the required partial derivatives of the normalized outputs with respect to the selected inputs are evaluated automatically through the autograd engine, and the resulting monotonicity penalty is backpropagated jointly with the regression and structural regularization terms. Therefore, the soft monotonicity regularization is optimized within the same gradient-based framework as the hard-constrained output mapping and data-fitting objective. Step 7 Parameter Update

The network parameters were adjusted using the Adam optimizer (a gradient descent method with adaptive momentum) to minimize E. The training epoch count was incremented, and Steps 4–7 were repeated for subsequent epochs. Iterating was continued until a convergence criterion is met.

After the particular solution branch had been trained to provide a physically feasible baseline prediction, its parameters were fixed. The residual correction branch was then further optimized under a similar gradient-based training framework to compensate for the remaining prediction error and to further improve both the predictive accuracy and the physical consistency of the model.

Step 9 Model Selection and Evaluation

After convergence, we evaluated the final model on the testing set to report its predictive accuracy (R2 and RMSE) and verify its physical consistency (satisfaction of boundary conditions and monotonic trends).

Notably, compared with classical residual-based PINNs that typically rely on dense collocation sampling and repeated derivative evaluations, the physics-guided enhancement in the present framework is relatively lightweight. The output parametrization inherently ensures boundary feasibility by construction, so no separate boundary-penalty term is required for boundary enforcement. In addition, the monotonicity prior is enforced through first-order gradient regularization on synthetic samples, without external equation solvers or dense collocation sampling. Therefore, the additional computational overhead in the present framework is moderate and problem-dependent. −

Experimental Data and Evaluation Metrics

Experimental Data

This study considers the diversity of types of biomass feedstock, which include hardwood, softwood, herbaceous plants, and sludge. The distribution of biomass gasification products is primarily influenced by the characteristics of the biomass feedstock and the process parameters during the pyrolysis and gasification. The biomass feedstock characteristics are mainly represented by proximate analysis, elemental analysis, and lower heating value (LHV), which reflect the diversity and intrinsic properties of the biomass.

The process parameters during pyrolysis and gasification primarily include ER, gasification temperature, and gasification pressure.

The experimental data used in this study for biomass gasification were sourced from the literature. Rather than representing a narrow single-reactor or single-feedstock data set, the resulting data set is a curated literature-derived collection of biomass gasification experiments covering diverse feedstock categories and operating conditions. This data set consists of biomass elemental analysis, proximate analysis, lower heating value, process parameters during gasification, and the volumetric fractions of various syngas components. Due to missing data in the original data set, such as the retention time, catalyst type, and parameters like the three components in many experimental samples, some correlations between certain data columns were relatively high. Including these highly correlated columns as input parameters could cause multicollinearity, affecting the model’s accuracy, such as the correlation between fixed carbon, volatile matter, and elemental analysis (carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen mass fractions).

To address this issue, the original data set was preprocessed by removing incomplete data entries, resulting in 200 valid experimental samples. Table provides the detailed statistical information on this data set. These 200 samples span multiple biomass categories, including hardwood, softwood, herbaceous plants, and sludge, and therefore provide a heterogeneous validation basis for the proposed model. In general, the input parameters were divided into two parts: biomass feedstock characteristics and process parameters during pyrolysis and gasification. The outputs consist of the volumetric fractions of the syngas components produced during biomass gasification. The biomass feedstock characteristics include mass fractions of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, LHV, particle diameter, ash content, moisture, and so on. The process parameters during pyrolysis and gasification include the gasification temperature, operational mode, experimental scale, equivalence ratio, catalyst usage, oxidizing agent, gasifier type, and bed material. Results and Discussion

The performance of the proposed model is evaluated from two perspectives and compared to those of three other representative machine learning models: RF, SVM, and ANN. This comparison is intended to provide a representative baseline study (tree-based, kernel-based, and neural-network paradigms) and to isolate the effect of the proposed physics-guided constraints on the present literature-derived tabular data set, rather than to benchmark all recent AI architectures. First, the prediction performance of the PINN model for syngas components is assessed using the R2 and RMSE metrics. Second, the model interpretability is evaluated using the PCD and partial dependence analysis (PDA).

Parameter tuning was performed for all models to ensure fair comparison. The single-hidden-layer ANN model was configured with 384 hidden nodes. For the RF model, the number of trees was set to 260, the maximum tree depth was 20, and the minimum number of samples required to split a leaf node was set to 2. For the SVM model, the penalty coefficient was optimized to 5.7, and the epsilon parameter was set to 1 × 10–2 using the Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) algorithm. The PINN model adopted a single-hidden-layer ANN structure with 384 hidden nodes, and the weighting coefficients in the loss function were selected based on a sensitivity analysis to balance predictive accuracy and physical consistency, with coefficients denoted as λ S = 0.01, λ M = 100.

Model Accuracy

The data set comprising 200 experimental samples was randomly split into a training set (85%) and a testing set (15%). It should be noted that these samples were not drawn from a narrow single-system data set but from a curated literature-derived data set covering diverse biomass categories and gasification conditions. Therefore, the present evaluation reflects model performance on a heterogeneous tabular data set rather than on a narrowly defined case study. Although R 2 and RMSE are the most widely adopted metrics for evaluating prediction models, they only reflect the model’s performance on a limited number of testing samples and lack the ability to assess model behavior in regions without data; in practice, the performance of conventional machine learning models is highly dependent on the coverage of the training samples. When the sample coverage is insufficient, the relationships captured by the model may significantly deviate from the actual physical mechanisms, leading to poor interpretability. Therefore, it is necessary to further evaluate and analyze the interpretability of the model.

Model Interpretability

Interpretability analysis is a necessary step in validating the effectiveness of machine learning models. It allows for the evaluation of a model’s feasibility under broader simulation scenarios and is crucial for real-world applications. In this study, the PCD is used to assess the deviation between the machine learning models and the underlying physical laws. Specifically, the partial derivatives between model outputs and inputs are calculated for both training and testing sets, and the PCD metric is applied to quantify the proportion of samples that satisfy predefined monotonicity constraints. In addition, partial dependence analysis (PDA) is employed to visualize and analyze the parameter correlations learned by the models.

Sensitivity Analysis for Hyperparameters

To evaluate the effects of hyperparameters on model training, a sensitivity analysis was performed using the 170 training samples in this study. Following a procedure consistent with the main training workflow, the weight coefficients λ S and λ M , corresponding to the structural regularization term E S and the monotonicity term E M , respectively, were varied over a set of representative values. The resulting values of the regression loss E R and monotonicity loss E M are shown in Figure . To ensure a fair comparison, all runs used the same network architecture and optimization settings, and only λ S and λ M were varied.

Loss trends under varying hyperparameters (λ s and λ M ): the weight coefficients of structural regularization loss E S and monotonicity loss E M .

As shown in Figure a, E R is jointly affected by λ S and λ M . In general, λ S mainly controls the fitting capability of the model: a proper structural regularization term helps stabilize training, whereas an excessively large λ S increases the competition with the regression objective and thus raises E R . Meanwhile, the effect of λ M on E R is nonmonotonic. A small monotonicity weight can slightly reduce E R , indicating that an appropriate monotonicity constraint helps guide optimization. However, when λ M becomes excessively large, E R increases again. This increase can be attributed to the intense competition between the λ M -weighted monotonicity term and the other weighted loss terms, which leads to a larger E R .

Figure b shows the effects of λ S and λ M on the monotonicity loss E M . When λ M is small, E M remains relatively high. As λ M increases, E M decreases rapidly by several orders of magnitude. In particular, when λ M reaches 100, E M becomes essentially zero for the tested cases, indicating that the monotonicity constraints are effectively enforced. Further increasing λ M beyond 100 does not provide a meaningful additional reduction in E M . These results indicate that E M is highly sensitive to λ M , whereas the influence of λ S on E M becomes relatively weak once λ M is sufficiently large.

Based on these results, λ S = 0.01 and λ M = 100 were selected as the final hyperparameters. This combination provides the best trade-off between predictive accuracy and physical consistency: it maintains a relatively low E R while reducing E M to a near-zero level. Therefore, a moderate structural regularization weight and a sufficiently large monotonicity weight are both necessary for achieving accurate and physically consistent predictions in the proposed PINN framework.

Conclusion

This work presents a novel physics-informed neural network (PINN) framework that integrates both hard and soft constraints for accurate prediction of biomass gasification syngas compositions. The framework embeds boundary conditions as hard constraints and encodes monotonic input–output relationships as soft constraints, enabling the model parameter space to adhere to physical laws. Trained on a small experimental data set (200 samples), the PINN significantly outperforms conventional machine-learning models (RF, SVM, and ANN), achieving an R2 above 0.89 and an RMSE below 4%. Beyond statistical accuracy, the proposed physics-aware design yields markedly improved physical consistency: the predicted syngas components follow the expected monotonic trends with respect to key operating variables and the hard boundary embedding guarantees physically feasible compositions (non-negativity and unity-sum) for every prediction. These properties enhance the model’s robustness and reliability, particularly in data-sparse regimes. Future work will focus on further improving physical consistency by incorporating additional conservation laws (mass, energy, elemental balances) and refining the synthetic sample generation mechanism to better balance domain knowledge with the error distribution observed in experimental data.
