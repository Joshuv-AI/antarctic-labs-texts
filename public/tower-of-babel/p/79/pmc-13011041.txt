Survey data on citizens’ perceptions of avian and their ecosystem services in Zhoushan
Authors: Weiwei Qian, Xin Ma, Yongbo Fu, Qi Chen, Tanhui Shen, Jia Chen, Feng Gui, Huiwen Cai, Sheng Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBirds are important indicators of biodiversity and providers of ecosystem services, particularly in urbanizing coastal regions. However, there is limited information on public awareness of avian ecosystem services and disservices in China. This article presents a dataset derived from a structured, face-to-face questionnaire survey conducted among residents of Zhoushan, a rapidly developing island city. The questionnaire covered six major sections: sociodemographic information, frequency of engagement in nature-related activities, bird species visibility and knowledge, perceptions of avian ecosystem services and disservices, nature connectedness, and conservation attitudes. Data were collected between April and July 2024 from 1,500 respondents across four districts, of which 1,430 valid responses were retained after data cleaning. The dataset provides detailed information on multiple dimensions of public perceptions of birds and their ecological roles and is suitable for descriptive statistics as well as advanced inferential analyses, such as regression and structural equation modeling.Keywords: Urban birds, Ecosystem services, Disservices, Citizens’ Perceptions, Nature relatedness

Background

Understanding public perceptions of biodiversity is crucial for devising effective conservation strategies in coupled human–ecological systems. Due to their prominent ecological roles and frequent human encounters, birds provide both ecosystem services and disservices [1]; therefore, they constitute an apt taxon for assessing public attitudes relevant to conservation policy.

Previous studies in China and elsewhere have investigated public attitudes toward biodiversity, birds, or ecosystem services, often focusing on general environmental awareness, urban green spaces, or single dimensions of ecosystem services [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. However, many of these studies are constrained by relatively small sample sizes, reliance on subjective self-reported knowledge, or a lack of openly accessible datasets suitable for secondary analysis and comparative research. Moreover, few existing datasets simultaneously integrate species-level recognition, perceptions of both ecosystem services and disservices, and psychological constructs such as nature connectedness within a unified and reusable survey framework.

Zhoushan, located in Zhejiang Province, represents a unique and strategic case study. As China’s largest archipelago city, it lies along the East Asian–Australasian Flyway [6], one of the world’s most critical bird migration corridors, hosting numerous resident and migratory species that depend on its coastal wetlands, islands, and marine ecosystems. At the same time, Zhoushan is undergoing rapid urbanization [7], creating pressures on habitats and altering daily human–bird interactions [8]. These island and coastal characteristics directly informed the questionnaire design in this study. Specifically, the bird species included in the visual identification task were selected based on their common occurrence and high visibility in Zhoushan’s coastal and island environments, ensuring that species recognition reflected realistic exposure in residents’ daily lives. Likewise, the ecosystem service and disservice statements emphasized functions and impacts that are particularly salient in coastal urban settings, such as pest regulation, cultural and recreational values, and potential nuisances associated with high bird densities near residential and waterfront areas. Despite its ecological importance and distinctive island geography, systematic evidence on how local citizens perceive birds and their ecological roles remains limited. This dataset addresses this gap by providing large-scale, structured survey evidence. It integrates bird visibility, objectively assessed bird species knowledge based on visual identification, perceptions of avian ecosystem services and disservices structured under the CICES framework, nature connectedness, and conservation attitudes within a single unified dataset. As such, the dataset offers a valuable resource for biodiversity conservation planning, environmental education, and comparative socio-ecological research in coastal and island cities, particularly those located along major migratory flyways.

Data Description

The dataset is divided into six sections, reflecting key dimensions of citizens’ perceptions of birds and their ecological roles in Zhoushan, China.

Sociodemographic characteristics

The first section records sociodemographic information, including gender, age, education level, occupation, and annual household income (Q1–Q5). No demographic quotas were imposed during sampling. Instead of assuming representativeness, a post hoc comparison between the survey sample and the 2020 Zhoushan census data was conducted to assess demographic consistency (Table S1). In total, 1,500 respondents were surveyed, and their demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Women comprised a slight majority (53.1%). Age distribution was relatively balanced, with respondents aged 19–60 years accounting for 82.7% of the sample, while those under 18 (8.4%) and over 60 (8.9%) were less represented. Regarding education, 69.3% of respondents held at least a high school diploma, including 17.9% with a bachelor’s degree or higher. Occupational categories were diverse, with private-sector employees, students, and migrant workers forming the largest groups; other occupations collectively represented 36.5%. In terms of household income, 69.7% reported an annual income between 30,000 and 250,000 CNY. A small proportion reported incomes exceeding 400,000 CNY annually.

Nature-related activities and experiences

Respondents reported the frequency of participation in four types of activities: gardening, nature-based recreation (e.g., birdwatching, hiking), watching nature-related films or documentaries, and reading nature-related materials (Q6). This section was designed to assess the extent of interaction with the natural environment.

Participation varied considerably across activity types (Table 2). About one-third of respondents reported never engaging in gardening, while 22.0% did so once or twice per year, and others reported more frequent engagement ranging from monthly to weekly. For nature-based recreation, 23.6% reported never participating, whereas 25.4% engaged once or twice annually. Watching nature-related films or documentaries was more common, with 27.2% watching once or twice per year and 12.6% watching weekly, though 26.4% never engaged in this activity. Reading nature-related books, magazines, or websites was the least frequent activity: 36.3% never read such materials, while only 9.8% reported doing so weekly or more often.

Bird visibility and species knowledge

Nine common bird species were selected to represent different ecological habitats and levels of detectability in Zhoushan (Fig. 1). The selection criteria included: (i) species frequently encountered in residents’ daily lives (e.g., Chinese Blackbird, Eurasian Tree Sparrow, Little Egret); (ii) species representing diverse habitats, such as waterbirds, woodland birds, and urban-adapted species; and (iii) two less frequently observed species (Long-tailed Shrike and Eurasian Hoopoe), which were included to assess attentiveness toward less familiar birds beyond the most visible coastal and urban species. All photographs were obtained from Wikimedia Commons under Creative Commons licenses or Public Domain, and their taxonomic identities were verified against the Checklist of the Birds of China [9]. Bird visibility was assessed by whether respondents had seen each species (Q7), while bird species knowledge was measured by the number of species correctly identified (Q8).

Perceptions of positive and negative statements of birds

A total of 14 statements were included to assess perceptions of birds, comprising nine positive items representing ecosystem services and five negative items reflecting potential disservices (Q9-Q10). The items were adapted from previous studies and classified according to the Common International Classification of Ecosystem Services (CICES v5.1), with particular emphasis on services and disservices that are salient in coastal and island urban environments, such as cultural appreciation, pest regulation, and potential nuisances associated with bird presence in residential and waterfront areas. Responses were measured on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree). Reliability analysis showed high internal consistency for both scales, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.882 for the avian ecosystem services scale and 0.821 for the avian ecosystem disservices scale, indicating that the items within each dimension consistently measured the intended constructs.

Overall, respondents expressed predominantly positive attitudes toward the ecosystem services provided by birds, with only a small proportion disagreeing. The highest levels of agreement were observed for emotional value (74.4%) and aesthetic value (79.6%), both of which are closely linked to frequent human–bird interactions in coastal and island cities. Recognition of regulating and maintenance services was also strong, with higher agreement for pest control (72.8%) and seed dispersal (70.7%) than for nutrient cycling (63.8%). In contrast, relatively few respondents endorsed the negative statements. For instance, 68.0% disagreed with the claim that “seeing birds makes me unhappy,” consistent with their positive views on aesthetic value. Nevertheless, a notable minority acknowledged risks associated with disease transmission (14.9%) and noise disturbance (16.3%), which are common concerns in high-density coastal settlements (Table 5).

Nature relatedness

Respondents’ sense of nature connectedness was assessed using a modified 14-item version of the Nature Relatedness Scale (NR-21) [10], adapted to reflect everyday interactions with nature in an urban and island context. The selected items retained the three conceptual dimensions of the original scale (Self, Perspective, and Experience), while prioritizing statements applicable to daily life in coastal cities rather than wilderness-oriented or strongly normative scenarios. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), and the mean score was calculated to represent overall connectedness (Q11). The initial reliability analysis yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.601, which is close to the lower acceptable threshold commonly reported in social science studies. To improve internal consistency, item–total correlations and the change in alpha if item deleted were examined. Five items (Items 1, 2, 6, 7, and 10) that showed weak contributions to overall reliability were removed. After refinement, the reduced 9-item version achieved an improved Cronbach’s alpha of 0.693, indicating acceptable internal consistency. Both the original 14-item version and the refined 9-item version are retained in the dataset to allow flexibility for secondary analyses. The results show generally high levels of agreement with statements reflecting emotional and cognitive ties to nature, while items related to resource use and personal efficacy revealed more mixed responses (Table 6).

Conservation attitudes

Conservation attitudes were assessed through five single-choice questions (Q12–Q16) covering respondents’ knowledge of the term “biodiversity,” perceived conflict between urban development and bird survival, attitudes toward bird conservation, preferred approaches to ecological protection, and self-reported conservation behaviors. Together, these five items were designed to capture a progressive structure of conservation-related cognition and behavior, ranging from conceptual awareness and value judgment to governance preferences and actual conservation actions. This item-based design allows flexible reuse of the dataset, enabling researchers to analyze each dimension independently or to explore pathways linking environmental knowledge, attitudes, and conservation behaviors.

The results indicate limited public understanding of biodiversity, with only 27.1% reporting clear familiarity with the term. While 75.6% agreed that birds should be protected, 35.6% perceived a conflict between urban development and bird survival. Respondents most frequently favored legal (30.5%) and educational (28.1%) approaches to ecological protection, whereas 31.0% emphasized voluntary individual action. Actual participation in conservation practices was relatively low, with 43.1% reporting that they rarely or never engaged in actions to protect local avian biodiversity (Table 7).

The dataset is accompanied by a complete data dictionary, which documents variable names, questionnaire items, descriptions, coding schemes, data types, and measurement scales, facilitating transparency and reuse. Together, these components make the dataset a comprehensive resource for examining public perceptions of avian ecosystem services, disservices, knowledge, and conservation attitudes in Zhoushan. The dataset is suitable for both descriptive analyses and inferential modeling, including regression analysis and structural equation modeling. For example, bird visibility and objectively assessed bird species knowledge may be used as key independent variables, nature relatedness as a potential mediating construct, and conservation attitudes or self-reported conservation behaviors as outcome variables, enabling the exploration of pathways linking biodiversity awareness, value perceptions, and pro-conservation actions.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The dataset was generated from a structured questionnaire survey conducted in Zhoushan, China, between April 1 and July 15, 2024. The survey adopted a stratified intercept sampling strategy. Data were collected face-to-face across four administrative regions: Dinghai District (43.35%), Putuo District (32.91%), Daishan County (18.19%), and Shengsi County (5.54%) (Fig. 2). Within these regions, sampling locations were selected according to population density and residential distribution, covering urban streets, rural areas, townships, and inhabited islands. These public residential spaces served as the practical sampling frame due to the absence of an accessible household registry for research purposes.

Survey location.

Within each selected location, respondents were approached using an on-site random intercept method. Interviewers invited passersby encountered in public spaces (e.g., streets, markets, residential areas) to participate in the survey on a voluntary basis, regardless of age or gender, in order to reduce selection bias.

A total of 1,500 residents participated, and the questionnaire required approximately five minutes to complete. Informed consent was obtained from all respondents prior to participation. During data preprocessing, an additional 70 questionnaires were excluded due to evidence of careless or random responding, such as inconsistent or illogical answers, indicating insufficient attention to the survey items. This resulted in a final valid sample of 1,430 respondents corresponding to an effective response rate of 95.3%. Although no demographic quotas were imposed, a post hoc comparison with census data was conducted to evaluate the demographic representativeness of the sample (Table S1).

All responses were collected in Chinese. Although dialect differences occasionally created communication challenges, assistance from accompanying residents ensured the accuracy and completeness of responses. The completed paper questionnaires were manually entered into the Microsoft forms and subsequently analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) for descriptive analysis. For data publication and international reuse, all variable names, labels, and value descriptions in the dataset have been translated into English. A complete data dictionary is provided to link each English variable to its original Chinese questionnaire item, ensuring semantic consistency.

Limitations

The dataset has several limitations. Bird species knowledge was assessed using only nine common species, which may not capture the full scope of residents’ ornithological knowledge. The intercept survey design may have introduced participation bias, as more cooperative individuals were more likely to respond. It should be noted, however, that no prior recruitment, public announcements, reminders, or incentives were used in this study. Participation was entirely voluntary and based on on-site face-to-face invitation, which helps minimize external motivation effects but does not fully eliminate self-selection bias. In addition, a relatively high proportion of private-sector workers was included due to the sampling approach in urban areas, which may affect the generalizability of the dataset. Although a post hoc comparison with the 2020 census data indicates that the sample is broadly representative in terms of gender and regional distribution, younger and more highly educated respondents are moderately overrepresented, while older adults are underrepresented. These demographic differences should be considered when interpreting results related to age- or education-specific perceptions. The dataset has also been used for further analytical studies employing structural equation model, which are reported separately.

Ethics Statement

This survey involved human participants but collected only anonymous, non-identifiable information. Informed consent was obtained from all respondents prior to participation, and parents or guardians provided consent for underage participants. The study followed the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. According to institutional ethical guidelines, the study protocol was reviewed and deemed exempt from formal ethical review and approval due to the anonymous and non-invasive nature of the survey.
