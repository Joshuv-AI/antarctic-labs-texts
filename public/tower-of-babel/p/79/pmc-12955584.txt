Highlighting the molecular refinement of NSMP endometrial cancer in a case of mesonephric-like adenocarcinoma

Published: 2026

Introduction

Based on The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), endometrial cancer (EC) can be classified into four molecular subtypes with distinct risk profiles: (i) POLE-ultramutated tumors with exonuclease domain mutations in the POLE gene (5–10% of ECs); (ii) microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) tumors reflecting mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR; 25–30%); (iii) TP53-abnormal tumors, corresponding to the copy-number high (serous-like) subtype (15–20%); and (iv) tumors with no specific molecular profile (NSMP; 40–50%); representing a diagnosis of exclusion, which lacks alterations in POLE, MMR, and TP53 (Weinstein et al., 2013). These prognostically distinct molecular subtypes were incorporated into the 5th edition of the WHO Classification of Tumors as well as into clinical risk assessment and form the basis of the updated 2025 ESGO/ESTRO/ESP guidelines, which define the current standard of care for EC (Concin et al., 2025). Except for the POLE mutant subtype, the outcome is furthermore affected by adverse clinicopathological factors, particularly stage, grade, and LVSI.

The NSMP group represents the numerically largest category of EC but is characterized by considerable molecular and clinical heterogeneity. In the absence of defining driver mutations, these tumors present a significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenge, as their biological behavior ranges from indolent to highly aggressive. Ongoing efforts aim to refine the stratification of this subgroup, identifying additional biomarkers that can specify precise prognosis and guide personalized treatment strategies (Momeni-Boroujeni et al., 2022). The 2025 ESGO/ESTRO/ESP guidelines since recently have incorporated estrogen receptor (ER) status as a key discriminator (Concin et al., 2025). ER-positive NSMP tumors, particularly when low-grade, are associated with favorable prognosis and considered low-risk. In contrast, ER-negative and/or high-grade NSMP tumors revealed to display a particular aggressive behavior, should be regarded as high-risk and are linked to devastating outcomes. This ER-based subclassification significantly enhances prognostic accuracy and supports more individualized therapeutic approaches (Jamieson et al., 2023, Marchetti et al., 2024). Recent studies have proposed additional biomarkers to refine the stratification of NSMP cancers. L1CAM is also able to stratify NSMP carcinomas according to prognosis but was not adopted by the 2025 ESGO/ESTRO/ESP guidelines (Kommoss et al., 2018). Chromosome 1 copy number alterations, particularly gains in the q-arm, have been associated with poor outcomes in independent cohorts, suggesting their potential as extended prognostic markers (Depreeuw et al., 2017).

We herein present a rare case of a low-grade, ER-negative, highly L1CAM positive NSMP cancer, which emerged as a mesonephric-like carcinoma in histopathologic second opinion. This case illustrates how extended molecular profiling, including assessment of L1CAM, ER, KRAS and copy number alterations in chromosome 1 can provide critical diagnostic and prognostic information

Case description

A 71-year-old female patient was admitted to hospital with abnormal postmenopausal uterine bleeding. The patient exhibited no other symptoms and had no significant medical history. A preliminary diagnostic hysteroscopy with curettage was conducted at an external academic medical center, which revealed the presence of malignant endometrial cells. She subsequently underwent a laparoscopic total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy and sentinel lymph node biopsy. Following the surgical procedure, the patient was referred to the Department of Gynecologic Oncology at the Medical University of Innsbruck in Austria for further evaluation and multidisciplinary management.

Histopathological evaluation of the surgical specimen revealed an adenocarcinoma with villoglandular and papillary architecture but high mitotic activity. Immunohistochemistry revealed negativity for estrogen (ER) and progesterone (PR) receptors, a wild-type pattern for p53, proficiency for the mismatch repair (MMR) proteins MLH1, PMS2, MSH2 and MSH6, positivity for GATA3, CD10 and calretinin, a heterogeneous (patchy) staining for p16, and negativity for TTF1 (Fig. 1A and B).Fig. 1A-B. This immunoreactive profile supported the histological diagnosis of mesonephric-like carcinoma of the endometrium. The tumor demonstrated extensive myometrial invasion, approaching the uterine serosa with only 3 mm of uninvolved myometrium remaining. Involvement of lymphovascular spaces or invasion of blood vessels was not identified, and both the sentinel lymph nodes and peritoneal cytology were free of malignancy. According to the FIGO 2009 classification, which was standard at the time of diagnosis, the tumor was staged as IB. Molecular profiling via next-generation sequencing (NGS) (Talhouk et al., 2015, Talhouk et al., 2017), confirmed microsatellite stability, POLE wild-type status, and a low tumor mutational burden (TMB) of 4 mutations per megabase. Furthermore, testing for homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) revealed a proficient status. In consideration of the findings, the tumor was initially classified as exhibiting low somatic copy number, thus aligning with the NSMP subgroup as delineated by the TCGA. Extended molecular profiling revealed a KRAS exon 2 mutation (COSM521), corresponding to a G12D amino acid substitution, while no pathogenic alterations were detected in PIK3R1, PTEN, PIK3CA, or other commonly mutated genes. These findings are consistent with the extended molecular profiling framework proposed by Momeni-Boroujeni et al, as well as the detected high-level gain of chromosome 1 at the q-arm (Momeni-Boroujeni et al., 2022) (Fig. 2A and B). Given the aggressive clinical behavior typically associated with mesonephric-like adenocarcinoma including its known tendency for early pulmonary metastases, the tumor board adapted its initial treatment recommendation of adjuvant vaginal brachytherapy only. Based on the new diagnostic insights the patient received six cycles of adjuvant carboplatin and paclitaxel chemotherapy. Due to cumulative toxicities such as fatigue, nausea, anemia, thrombocytopenia, and leukopenia, the chemotherapy dose was reduced to 80% from the third cycle onwards. Following the completion of chemotherapy, adjuvant vaginal brachytherapy was administered in three fractions of 7 Gray. The patient tolerated the treatment well and has remained disease-free during five years of follow-up.

Discussion

We consider two aspects of our case as valuable for being reported: the application of the molecular classification of endometrial carcinoma on rare/unusual histological types and the subcategorization of NSMP carcinomas in low-risk and high-risk groups.

Mesonephric-like adenocarcinoma of the endometrium, which has been increasingly recognized as a high-risk histological type may show a molecular and phenotypic overlap. This carcinoma shows at least partially a well differentiated architecture, typically lacks ER/PR expression as well as PTEN or PIK3CA mutations, demonstrates often strong L1CAM positivity, and harbors KRAS mutations (Mirkovic et al., 2018, McCluggage, 2022). Accurate diagnosis relies on recognition of the distinct histologic features. Despite being classified as copy-number low in TCGA, the clinical behavior contrasts with the traditionally intermediate prognosis of other NSMP tumors. Mesonephric-like adenocarcinoma behaves aggressively, tends to present at higher FIGO stages (II to IV), is associated with poor outcomes, and tends to show a predilection for pulmonary metastases, even when diagnosed at apparently early stage (Euscher et al., 2020). In a series reported by Euscher et al., the disease-free survival for mesonephric-like adenocarcinomas patients was significantly shorter compared to other grade-matched ECs. Given this aggressive nature, adjuvant therapy − including chemotherapy and radiation − is commonly recommended, even in the absence of conventional high-risk features (Euscher et al., 2020).

The subclassification of the NSMP category was not implemented yet as a high-risk factor in clinical recommendations at the time of initial treatment of this patient. Since then, ER status has emerged as a significant prognostic discriminator within the NSMP subgroup, dividing these tumors into two clinically distinct categories. ER-positive tumors, particularly those with low-grade morphology, which are generally associated with favorable outcomes and may be managed with less intensive therapy (Jamieson et al., 2023). In contrast, ER-negative and/or high-grade NSMP tumors display aggressive clinical behavior and are frequently linked to additional high-risk features such as L1CAM overexpression and more advanced stage (Marchetti et al., 2024).

A recent study by Momeni-Boroujeni et al. proposed a stratification of NSMP tumors into three genomic clusters. Cluster 1 is enriched for PTEN and PIK3R1 mutations and is associated with favorable clinical outcomes. Cluster 2 is characterized by co-occurring PTEN and PIK3CA mutations. Cluster 3, by contrast, exhibits high-level gain of chromosome 1 q-arm, absence of PTEN mutations, frequent KRAS alterations, and is commonly ER-negative. These tumors tend to present at higher FIGO grades and stages and demonstrate the poorest overall survival within the NSMP category (Momeni-Boroujeni et al., 2022). However, the evidence for the molecular subcategorization of NSMP carcinomas is limited and NGS is not globally available and, therefore, ER and histological grade are currently recommended for stratification of NSMP carcinomas.

The present case shows further parameters of adverse prognosis, particularly L1CAM overexpression and the Cluster 3 profile described by Momeni-Boroujeni et al., on the molecular level characterized by absence of PTEN mutations, frequent KRAS mutations, and chromosome 1q gain (Momeni-Boroujeni et al., 2022). L1CAM has emerged as a particularly relevant biomarker in this context. While it is overexpressed in more than 80% of p53 abnormal carcinomas, it is also detected in approximately 8–10% of NSMP cancers, such as in our patient, where it is then associated with worse prognosis (Kommoss et al., 2018). Large multicenter evaluations in early-stage EC confirmed that L1CAM positivity independently predicts poor outcomes, even in otherwise low-risk cases (Zeimet et al., 2013). Reflecting this, the German S3 guideline (AWMF Registry Number 032/034-OL) lists L1CAM testing as an optional tool alongside p53 and MMR status to refine risk assessment (Emons et al., 2022). L1CAM overexpression, which is common in cluster 3 subset of tumors, has been linked to the loss of hormone receptors and E-cadherin, a hallmark of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and an indicator of aggressive tumor biology. This is a process associated with enhanced invasion, metastatic potential, and treatment resistance (Huszar et al., 2010, Kim et al., 2023). Moreover, amplification of chromosome 1q32.1 has been identified as a crucial prognostic marker within NSMP tumors, associated with significantly worse relapse-free survival in independent cohorts, thereby supporting its classification as a biomarker of high-risk disease requiring intensified adjuvant treatment (Depreeuw et al., 2017).

Similarly, KRAS mutations, another hallmark of Cluster 3 tumors, may contribute to this aggressive phenotype through activation of the MAPK or PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, promoting tumor cell proliferation and survival (Banno et al., 2014). The co-occurrence of L1CAM positivity and KRAS mutation, particularly in the absence of PTEN and PIK3CA alterations, may therefore define a molecularly distinct and biologically aggressive NSMP subgroup with high metastatic potential and poor prognosis (Momeni-Boroujeni et al., 2022, Euscher et al., 2020).

In the context of contemporary molecularly guided treatment allocation, as evaluated in trials such as PORTEC-4a, molecular classification has already been successfully integrated into adjuvant decision-making for early-stage endometrial carcinoma (van den Heerik et al., 2020). However, within these risk-adapted frameworks, NSMP tumors are largely managed as a single intermediate-risk category. The present case illustrates that biologically aggressive cancers may exist within the NSMP subgroup that are not fully captured by current stratification models. In particular, low-grade tumors with ER-negativity represent a biologically uncommon constellation and should prompt additive risk stratification, including L1CAM assessment and careful pathological re-evaluation. In the presence of additional adverse features, extended molecular profiling − including analysis of KRAS alterations and chromosome 1q gain − may provide clinically relevant prognostic information beyond the standard ProMisE criteria. Given the variability in access to comprehensive molecular diagnostics, routine NGS for all NSMP carcinomas cannot currently be considered universally feasible. However, a risk-adapted approach in rare histological subtypes or immunophenotypically high-risk NSMP tumors appears justified.

The 2025 ESGO/ESTRO/ESP guideline, likewise, emphasizes the integration of molecular features into clinical decision-making for EC and, for the first time, recommends a subdivision of NSMP tumors according to ER status and histological grading (Concin et al., 2025). While NSMP tumors are generally associated with an intermediate prognosis, this refinement recognizes that this specific subgroup, namely ER-negative and/or high-grade NSMP cancers, may benefit from intensified adjuvant therapy (Jamieson et al., 2023, Galant et al., 2024, Vermij et al., 2023). The herein presented case strongly supports this recent adaptation of NSMP risk stratification by ESGO/ESTRO/ESP. Nevertheless, even though the subgroup has become more streamlined through recent refinements, NSMP tumors continue to resemble a “catch-all category” of tumor biology − diverse, ill-defined, and in urgent need of further molecular resolution. The heterogeneity of the large NSMP group underscores the need for more molecular granularity to enable clinically meaningful subclassifications. Identifying additional biomarkers and integrating extended molecular profiling into routine practice will be essential to refine risk assessment and ultimately improve patient outcomes.

Conclusion

Molecular classification according to actual guidelines and standards needs to be performed on all histological types of endometrial carcinoma. Especially in NSMP ECs, further refinement of immunohistochemical algorithms and a deeper understanding of the molecular landscape are essential to improve risk stratification and individualized treatment strategies. As emphasized in recent literature, emerging biomarkers − such as ER status, L1CAM expression, and specific genomic alterations beyond ProMisE criteria can add more granularity with substantial clinical relevance in this heterogeneous NSMP subgroup. Mesonephric-like adenocarcinomas should be considered in the differential diagnosis of architecturally well differentiated carcinomas with high mitotic activity, as their deceptively bland morphology represents a frequent diagnostic pitfall, masking an aggressive biological behavior characterized by ER-negativity, strong L1CAM expression, KRAS mutations, and potentially chromosome 1q gain.
