mir-31 mediated control of bacteriome size in tsetse flies
Authors: Mason H. Lee, Rachel A. Morris, Ryan Phillips, Rita V.M. Rio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Symbiont-harboring bacteriomes increase in width during tsetse pregnancy.•miR-31 disruption enlarges bacteriomes in virgins, as seen in mated females.•Changes in bacteriome size likely accommodate dynamic nutritional symbiosis demands.

Introduction

African trypanosomiasis, a parasitic disease caused by infection with African trypanosomes (Trypanosoma spp.), is a significant public and veterinary health issue in afflicted areas (Abro et al. 2021; Fèvre et al., 2008; Franco et al. 2022; Mwiinde et al. 2017; Sutherland et al. 2017, 2015). The primary biological vector of African trypanosomes is the tsetse fly (Family: Glossinidae) with both sexes being strict blood feeders, and thus, capable of parasite transmission. Due to their limited diet, tsetse flies depend on an endogenous population of an obligate mutualistic Gammaproteobacterium, known as Wigglesworthia glossinidia (Aksoy 1995) (hereafter Wigglesworthia) for nutritional supplementation. These symbionts are found within specialized tsetse cells known as bacteriocytes, which collectively form a bacteriome. Wigglesworthia synthesizes and supplies nutrients, including various B vitamins (Akman et al. 2002; Michalkova et al. 2014; Snyder and Rio 2013, 2015), to tsetse and other members of the microbiota (Snyder et al. 2010; Wang et al. 2013). The Wigglesworthia-tsetse symbiosis is essential for various facets of tsetse biology, including morphological development, the maturation of immunity, reproduction, and vector competence (Akman et al. 2002; Bing et al. 2017; Medina Munoz et al. 2017; Michalkova et al. 2014; Rio et al. 2012, 2019). Thus, perturbation of this symbiosis offers a promising target for tsetse fly population suppression.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are emerging candidates for regulating insect-bacteria symbioses (Feng et al. 2018, 2019; Hussain et al. 2011). These small non-coding RNAs typically regulate gene expression by binding to complementary sequences on target mRNAs, leading to mRNA degradation and blocking the initiation of translation. We have previously determined through in silico analyses that a tsetse miRNA, miR-31, may regulate a fatty acyl-CoA reductase (FAR; GMOY009401) by binding at its 3′ UTR and that this interaction appears to be evolutionarily conserved through the tsetse lineage (Lee et al. 2023). During tsetse fly pregnancy, far expression increases in bacteriomes, while the abundance of miR-31 transcripts decreases.

Using a phylogenetic framework, GMOY009401, appears to be evolutionary ‘stable’ as evidenced by the absence of gene duplication events in the various Glossinidae genomes (Attardo et al. 2019; International Glossina Genome Initiative 2014). Orthologs of ‘stable’ FAR genes may contribute to the production of precursors for wax monoesters that coat epithelial cells, the primary cell type composing the bacteriome (Ding et al. 2022; Finet et al. 2019; Jaspers et al. 2014; Reinhardt et al. 1972; Wigglesworth 1929), whereas ‘unstable’ FAR genes are more commonly associated with lineage-specific expansion and diversification, such as in pheromone production (Tupec et al. 2019). Since the tsetse bacteriome is a relatively fixed organ comprised of epithelium modified by the presence of Wigglesworthia, we were intrigued by this apparent dichotomy, as this organ must also meet the dynamic, increased nutrient demands during pregnancy when the female is providing for two, herself and an ‘in utero’ larva (Pais et al. 2008).

Here, we examine whether the Glossina far ortholog (GMOY009401), potentially regulated by miR-31, is crucial for shaping bacteriome traits. To accomplish this, we investigated the expression profile of far relative to tsetse biological sex and mating status and validated its regulation by miR-31. Given that far may be involved in epithelial cell production, we further examined the role of miR-31 regulated far expression in the symbiosis by comparing the bacteriome architecture of age-matched mated and virgin tsetse females We report significant size differences in bacteriomes relative to female mating status. Lastly, we describe the impact of disrupting far expression towards bacteriome size and tsetse fitness. We report a significant impact of miR-31 antagomir treatment, but not the inhibition of far expression through RNAi, on bacteriome size. Therefore, we believe an unknown miR-31 regulated gene(s), independent of far, may be essential for regulating bacteriome growth, potentially to accommodate higher symbiosis demands during gestation.

Materials and methods

Insect rearing

Glossina morsitans pupae were obtained from the International Atomic Energy Agency (Austria). Tsetse flies are maintained at the West Virginia University Department of Biology insectary with a 12:12 h L:D photoperiod cycle at 24 ± 1 °C. Flies are fed with defibrinated bovine blood (Hemostat, CA) on an artificial membrane every 48 h (Moloo 1971). To ensure the virgin status of females, pupae were reared within individual tubes and females were maintained in single-sex cages following adult emergence.

Dissection, RNA extraction, and cDNA synthesis

Flies were immobilized on ice and the bacteriomes were collected and pooled (n = 5 bacteriomes per individual sample) from 2-week-old flies (males, pregnant females, or virgin females) for the analysis of expression of far, gapDH (glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase), and alpha-tubulin (primers provided in S. Table 1). Dissected bacteriomes were stored in RNAlater Stabilization Solution (Invitrogen) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. All bacteriomes were homogenized with RNAse-free mechanical pestles and RNA was isolated following the “Purifying RNA from Animal Tissues” protocol using the PureLink RNA Mini Kit (Ambion) followed by a Turbo DNAse I (Invitrogen) treatment to remove any residual DNA. cDNA synthesis was performed via Superscript II reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen) using 3′ primers for tsetse gapDH, alpha-tubulin and far with 500 ng of RNA as template. Second-strand cDNA synthesis was performed with 1 μL of cDNA as template per 50 μL reaction. The expression of either tsetse housekeeping gene, gapDH or alpha-tubulin, was used to assess RNA quality and as a loading control.

Measurements of bacteriome organs

Intact gut sections spanning from the proventriculus (cardia) to the bacteriome were dissected from 2-week-old pregnant and virgin female flies into 1x PBS solution on ice. These were immediately viewed on a Zeiss Stereo Discovery microscope and photographed using the Zen Blue v3.8 software. Morphological measurements were then taken to determine (1) the distance from the proventriculus to the start of the bacteriome, demarcated by the transition from translucence to opacity, (2) the length of the bacteriome at the bulge region and (3) the distance across the anterior, median, and posterior of the bacteriome using the Graphics>Distance>Curve (Spline) tool in ZenLite. At least six images were taken from each sample for spatial analyses. Subsequent experiments were conducted to determine the impact of either dsfar or miR-31a (ant-31a) antagomir distribution on the bacteriome structure of mated and virgin flies, respectively, using the same procedures as above. Flies were collected at two weeks of age for spatial measurements of bacteriomes.

RNAi

Specific primers for G. morsitans far were designed to contain the 5′ end T7 promoter sequences for dsRNA synthesis (S. Table 2). dsRNA was produced by the ‘in vitro’ transcription of a PCR-generated DNA template containing the T7 promoter sequence on both strands using the MEGAScript T7 kit (Ambion) protocol and purified with the Qiagen PCR purification protocol. The green fluorescence protein (GFP) gene amplified from the pFA6a-GFP(S65T)-His3MX6 plasmid (Wach et al. 1997) (see S. Table 2 for primers) was used to control for non-target ‘in vivo’ effects of dsRNA microinjection. dsRNA products were analyzed on 1 % TBE agarose gels to determine size and verify integrity and sequenced to confirm their identity. The dsRNA concentrations were determined spectrophotometrically on a NanoDrop ND-1000 Spectrophotometer at OD260.

A standard protocol was followed to introduce dsRNA into tsetse flies via incorporation into bloodmeals (Walshe et al. 2009). One-week-old virgin females were fed 500 ng of dsRNA, and their abdomens were visually inspected following feeding. Flies that lacked evidence of blood consumption were removed from downstream analyses. Flies that took in a dsRNA bloodmeal were allowed to rest for 24 hrs prior to being mated. Dissections occurred one week after dsRNA treatments to assess the extent of gene silencing.

Real-time reverse transcription quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR)

qRT-PCR analysis was performed on bacteriome cDNA from the dsfar- and dsgfp-treated flies on a CFX Connect Real-Time System (BioRad) using SsoAdvanced™ Universal SYBR® Green Supermix in a 96-well plate. Three biological replicates were assessed per treatment, with three technical replicates performed for each cohort. The relative expression of far between dsgfp- and dsfar-treated flies was quantified to determine whether significant knockdown occurred, with the minimum cycle threshold (Ct) value set to 1. Normalization was performed using maximum absolute scaling (Lima and Souza 2023).

Antagomir administration

miR-31a antagomirs (ant-31a) and missense-31 (scrambled) antagomirs were synthesized at the W.M. Keck Oligonucleotide Synthesis Facility (Yale University School of Medicine, S. Table 1). Antagomirs were resuspended to a concentration of 100 μM in sterile dH20 and provided to pregnant flies (100 μL of antagomir per 500 μL of blood, corresponding to approximately 0.3 nmol per fly of antagomir, assuming a 20 μL blood meal). Mated females received either missense-31a or ant-31a, supplemented in blood, a method shown to be effective for other miRNA delivery in tsetse (Aksoy et al. 2016), and were dissected after 24 h to assess impact on far expression (Fig. 2A). Bacteriomes were removed from flies and pooled (n = 5 per individual sample) for RNA isolation and cDNA synthesis to assess impact on far expression.

Fecundity and long-term survival measurements

Flies fed dsgfp or dsfar RNA were observed for 60 days after RNAi treatment with deaths, time to larviposition, pupal eclosion duration, pupal eclosion rate, and pupal weight recorded. Pupal weight was recorded on the same day as deposition and compared to that of control flies.

Statistics

For comparisons among three or more groups, the Shapiro-Wilk test was used to confirm normal distribution. If data were normally distributed, a one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) with a built-in Brown-Forsythe test for variance was performed, followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. If normality was not met, the nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test was used. For pairwise comparisons, F-tests for equality of variance were conducted prior to pooled t-tests to assess statistical significance. Survival analyses were evaluated using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. The Fisher’s Exact Test was used to compare eclosion rate between treatment groups. All statistical analyses were carried out using R software version 2023.03.1 and GraphPad Prism version 9.0.0.121, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Expression of far in age-matched tsetse and regulation by miR-31

Our previous in silico work (Lee et al. 2023) using RNAseq and concurrent small RNA sequencing, demonstrated that far expression is high in pregnant flies of various ages carrying 1st to 3rd larval instars. In this study, maternal age and gonotrophic cycle (i.e. first gonotrophic cycle) were controlled. At two weeks of age, far was expressed in the bacteriomes of mated female flies, while not detectable in age-matched males and virgin female flies (Fig. 1A). Orally administered antagomirs provided through bloodmeal distribution confirmed the interaction of miR-31 and far, with inhibited miR-31 resulting in increased levels of far expression within the bacteriomes of pregnant flies (Fig. 1B). This result contrasts with age-matched pregnant flies that received either missense antagomirs or blood only (normal feeding), which showed no change in far expression within their bacteriomes (Fig. 1B). Analysis of bacteriome size relative to mating

Given the increased metabolic demands on the Wigglesworthia symbiosis during adenotrophic viviparity (Attardo et al. 2023; Snyder et al. 2010, 2012; Snyder and Rio 2015) and, the putative role of far contributing to epithelial cell structuring, we sought to determine whether early pregnancy is associated with changes in bacteriome features (Fig. 2D). Morphological measurements obtained from age-matched virgin and mated females showed no significant difference in either the average distance between the end of the proventriculus and the start of the bacteriome (reported as mean ± SEM: 1589 ± 76.61 µm vs. 1833 ± 115.0 µm, respectively, unpaired t-test, t(13), P = 0.11; Fig. 2A) or the total length of the bacteriome at the bulge region (1748 ± 150.8 µm vs. 1863 ± 72.51 µm, respectively; unpaired t-test, t(13), P = 0.46; Fig. 2B). Impact of RNAi on far expression and fly phenotype

Because of the potential impact of dsRNA injection towards tsetse reproduction (Walshe et al. 2009), we decided to introduce dsRNA into tsetse through oral administration. Mated dsgfp- and dsfar-fed tsetse flies showed no significant mortality differences in the eight days prior to dissection (Fig. 3A). Significant differences in far expression between dsgfp and dsfar-fed flies were verified through RT-PCR and RT-qPCR (Figs. 3B and 3C, one-tailed t-test; P = 0.0369). The relative abundance of far transcripts in dsgfp-treated flies (0.7888 ± 0.1075) was approximately 2-fold higher than that of the dsfar cohort (0.4341 ± 0.1009) (Fig. 3C). Phenotypic impacts of far and miR-31 disruption towards bacteriome integrity

Following the confirmation of significant differences in bacteriome width of mated females (Fig. 2C), we investigated the effects of the knockdown of far transcript abundance via RNAi or interference in miR-31::far binding through the administration of antagomirs (Figs. 4A and 4B) on bacteriome width. Impact of far knockdown on the tsetse fecundity

Pupal weight has previously been established as a robust indicator of tsetse larval health (Adden et al. 2023). No significant differences (Kruskal-Wallis test, P = 1.0) in the weight of pupae deposited by either the control group or flies fed dsgfp or dsfar (Fig. 5A) were observed. Additional fecundity measurements similarly revealed that the disruption of far transcript abundance has no impact on larval deposition time (Fig. 5B, two-tailed t-test, t(34), P = 0.6878) or the average time between larval deposition and adult eclosion (i.e. pupa to adult emergence) (Fig. 5C, one-way ANOVA, f(2,49)= 3.073,P = 0.0553). Discussion

Tsetse miRNA expression is variable, yet regulated, with specific expression patterns associated with sex, tissue, and developmental stage (Lee et al. 2023; Meki et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2021). In the guts of various insects, including aphids (Feng et al. 2018, 2019), whiteflies (Sun et al. 2022), broad-headed bugs (Ferguson et al. 2018) and fruit flies (Broderick et al. 2014; Xie et al. 2023), miRNA expression is impacted by members of the host microbiota. In these insects, as in tsetse, these miRNAs are likely preventing dysbiosis and its subsequent health implications. For example, miRNAs have been identified as potential gene regulators in the obligate nutritional endosymbiosis of aphids (Feng et al. 2018, 2019), including of the orphan bacteriocyte-expressed secreted protein 1 (SP1), which is regulated by miR-92a (Feng et al. 2019). Our previous research suggests that post-transcriptional regulation by miRNAs also occurs within the analogous tsetse-Wigglesworthia symbiosis (Lee et al. 2023). miR-31 impacts fatty acyl-CoA (far) transcript abundance within the bacteriomes of pregnant tsetse, suggesting a modulatory role in the tsetse-Wigglesworthia symbiosis during reproduction. The in silico prediction of the far::miR-31 interaction (Lee et al. 2023) was empirically validated through antagomir targeting of miR-31. With the administration of miR-31 antagomirs, both far expression and bacteriome width increased. The wider bacteriome phenotype arising from antagomir-treated virgin flies, however, appears to be more severe, as it is significantly wider than that of control and missense-treated flies. Overall, our results support miR-31 abundance playing a role in bacteriome restructuring, which may be related to increased dependence on bacteriome populations of Wigglesworthia for nutrient provisioning during fecundity (Pais et al. 2008). In other animals, miRNAs are similarly involved in targeting genes involved in cell growth and proliferation, including miR-8 and miR-200, which impact phosphoinositide-3 kinase (PI3K) activity and insulin signaling in Drosophila and humans, respectively (Hyun et al. 2009; Jin et al. 2012). Similarly, cardiac overexpression of miR-195 in transgenic mice induces cardiac hypertrophy by promoting cardiomyocyte growth, ultimately leading to heart failure (van Rooij et al. 2006).

Despite this role for miR-31 in bacteriome width, the direct interaction of far::miR-31 as the primary driver of bacteriome width was not supported. RNAi of far within pregnant females neither leads to significant changes in bacteriome width nor significant fitness consequences in tsetse progeny. These results indicate that, rather than far, another target(s) of miR-31 is likely involved in bacteriome morphology changes during pregnancy. Previously identified miR-31-regulated genes with significant differences in expression between mated and age-matched virgin females include the folate-dependent mitochondrial gene amt and a solute carrier anion transporter (OAT) (Giraldo-Calderón et al. 2015; Lee et al. 2023), both of which have not been characterized in tsetse.

Prior to the implication of these genes in this structural change, however, there are several limitations to the current study that must be addressed. While we found far expression changes because of dsRNA treatment, we cannot be certain this is sufficient to result in an observable phenotype. Additionally, we cannot rule out that another of the numerous fatty acyl-CoAs lacking characterization in the tsetse fly (Attardo et al. 2019; International Glossina Genome Initiative 2014; Lee et al. 2023) may be able to compensate for the disruption of this gene. It is also possible that miR-31 has a stronger impact on far than our dsRNA treatment, or that there may be intergenic interactions affecting far with miR-31 not captured in this study. Furthermore, we did not examine the impact of miR-31 antagomir distribution on pregnant flies. Mir-31 has multiple predicted targets in the tsetse genome, even if not all are conserved across species (Lee et al. 2023), so caution must be taken before pursuing such a study. Lastly, miR-31::far interaction was studied due to its conservation in G. morsitans and G. brevipalpis (Lee et al. 2023). Difficulties in procuring and maintaining G. brevipalpis did not allow us to observe whether the impacts described towards bacteriomes are also conserved between these two species.

Continued characterization and an increased understanding of the regulatory networks supporting the Wigglesworthia-tsetse symbiosis offer several benefits. The most obvious being that disruption of this symbiosis could eventually serve as a focal point for vector control mechanisms, but there are other benefits in enhanced understanding of the tsetse-Wigglesworthia model (Doudoumis et al. 2013; Pais et al. 2008). This symbiosis is ancient and conserved across all tsetse species, suggesting significant potential for uncovering additional miRNA-mediated pathways of microbiome regulation. Moreover, the tsetse-Wigglesworthia symbiosis shares characteristics with the obligate mutualisms of many other economically impactful pests. Thus, this line of research could inform the development of control methods applicable for other host species (Hosokawa et al. 2010; Husnik and McCutcheon 2016; Tsuchida et al. 2002; Toju et al. 2013) and provide a foundation for understanding inter-domain interactions between hosts and their gut microbiota.
