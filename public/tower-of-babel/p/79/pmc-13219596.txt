Automated quantification of coronal balance in spinal deformity: a safety-aware clinical workflow
Authors: Zexi Wang, Yuan Zhang, Feng Xue, Abulikemu Maimaiti, Yingting Li, Jie Zhang, Wen Zhao, Yi Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe assessment of coronal balance is critical for cosmetic appearance and quality of life in patients with adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS), yet its manual quantification remains constrained by substantial workflow demands and inherent subjectivity. While artificial intelligence has shown promise in automated Cobb angle measurement, robust and clinically safety-aware solutions for coronal balance parameters are still lacking. In this study, we developed and validated a robust HRNet-based automated system for quantifying Shoulder Height Difference (SHD), Pelvic Obliquity (PO), and C7 Plumb Line Offset (C7 offset) using a dataset of 847 full-spine radiographs. Beyond anatomically driven topological constraints, we integrated a novel Clinical Safety Gating Mechanism that proactively suppresses unreliable outputs in cases of anatomical ambiguity, functioning as a safety gating by design. The system achieved millimeter-level precision (mean absolute error: 0.86–2.45 mm) and demonstrated strong agreement with manual reference measurements (Pearson r > 0.95), with overall performance approximating the inter-rater variability among senior spinal surgeons. Importantly, the deterministic algorithmic consistency of the system mitigates human fatigue and subjective fluctuation, offering theoretical advantages for future longitudinal assessments. Collectively, this work positions the automated coronal balance system not merely as a measurement tool, but as a safety-aware quality safety gating mechanism. Introduction

Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) is a three-dimensional deformity that persistently impacts cosmetic appearance and quality of life throughout skeletal development1,2. While the Cobb angle has long served as the primary clinical metric for severity, it predominantly quantifies the magnitude of local curvature and may not fully capture the global alignment and trunk symmetry that drive patient self-perception3. In the context of follow-up and treatment decision-making, global coronal balance correlates more strongly with cosmetic deformity, self-image, and patient satisfaction4,5. Issues such as shoulder imbalance, pelvic obliquity, and trunk shift often cause greater anxiety among patients and their families than minor fluctuations in the Cobb angle6,7. Consequently, coronal balance parameters—specifically Shoulder Height Difference (SHD), Pelvic Obliquity (PO), and C7 Plumb Line Offset (C7 offset)—are critical for preoperative assessment, the verification of postoperative alignment goals, and the evaluation of global posture during long-term follow-up.

However, in routine practice, these metrics are not quantified with the same consistency and standardization as the Cobb angle, a discrepancy largely attributable to the practical challenge of sustaining such measurements within a demanding clinical workflow. Full-spine standing radiographs are characterized by high information density and labor-intensive measurement protocols; simultaneous assessment of multiple parameters significantly increases the workload for both clinicians and technicians8. Under the constraints of high-volume outpatient clinics and ward rounds, maintaining a rigorous, consistent measurement workflow for every image is often practically unfeasible. More critically, manual measurement is inherently susceptible to subjectivity; the precise selection of anatomical landmarks is prone to drift influenced by clinician experience, visual parallax, and fatigue9,10. Such variations inevitably introduce discrepancies between different observers—and even within repeated assessments by the same clinician11. For parameters such as PO and C7 Offset—which are hypersensitive to patient positioning, axial rotation, and anatomical occlusion—measurement error and variability are notably amplified12,13. This instability risks confounding clinical judgments regarding disease progression, therapeutic efficacy, and realignment strategies14.

Recent advances in automated image analysis have enabled the extraction of quantitative metrics from spinal imaging. Overall, however, existing literature has predominantly focused on the Cobb angle, driven both by its status as the traditional “gold standard” and by the relative maturity of its annotation protocols and validation frameworks15–18. While a few recent pioneering studies have begun to explore the automated quantification of coronal balance, clavicle angles, and pelvic obliquity19,20, the systematic automated assessment of these global balance parameters in AIS remains relatively underrepresented. Furthermore, existing AI approaches typically function as black-box models that force predictions even under severe anatomical ambiguity. Traditional non-machine learning (non-ML) image processing techniques, such as edge detection, are even less viable, as they rely on low-level pixel gradients and consistently fail when faced with complex overlapping structures (e.g., clavicles overlapping with ribs, or iliac crests obscured by bowel gas). ML-based models, by contrast, possess the capacity to learn global semantic topology to overcome these artifacts. However, applying ML to complex radiographs without quality control poses significant clinical risks. A frequently overlooked yet pervasive challenge within clinical datasets is that clinical datasets are often imperfect in practice: manual annotations are prone to errors such as laterality confusion, anatomical inconsistencies, or grossly implausible keypoint localization. Such label noise not only compromises measurement consistency but also risks introducing systematic bias during model training and evaluation, thereby undermining the reliability of result interpretation21. A more pragmatic clinical requirement is for data-driven tools to go beyond mere measurement accuracy; they must exhibit robustness against inconsistent or suspect annotations and proactively flag potential anomalies, rather than generating deceptively precise yet unreliable outputs under conditions of uncertainty22.

Against this backdrop, the present study aims to develop and validate an automated quantification workflow for coronal balance parameters—specifically SHD, PO, and C7 offset—utilizing full-spine standing radiographs. We leveraged a High-Resolution Network (HRNet) for the detection of key anatomical landmarks23, which was trained and internally validated on a large-scale AIS dataset; consistency was further assessed by benchmarking the automated results against manual clinical measurements. Furthermore, we centered our investigation on the inherent challenge of heterogeneous annotation quality in real-world datasets: beyond merely quantifying error margins, we systematically examined the model’s performance and adaptive strategies when encountering inconsistent labeling, laterality confusion, or anatomical anomalies. Our goal is for this workflow to serve both as a standardized measurement tool and as a safety gating mechanism that identifies and flags problematic manual annotations or measurements in large-scale clinical databases, thereby providing a more robust foundation for longitudinal follow-up, data curation, and downstream research24. This approach positions automated measurement not merely as a replacement for manual quantification, but as an integral component of quality control in longitudinal scoliosis care.

Methods

Study population and data curation

This retrospective study was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University and received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB No.: K202509-69). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations (e.g., the Declaration of Helsinki)25. Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived. Initially, 875 consecutive posteroanterior (PA) standing full-spine radiographs of patients with AIS were retrieved from the institutional PACS database between August 2024 and November 2025. To ensure a high-quality “gold standard” for model training and evaluation, standardized exclusion criteria were applied, including: (1) invisibility of key anatomical landmarks due to incomplete anatomical coverage; (2) poor image quality resulting from severe motion artifacts or occlusion; and (3) presence of metallic implants severely interfering with landmark identification. Following this screening, a final cohort of 847 patients was included for analysis. To prevent data leakage, the dataset was randomly partitioned at the patient level into a training set (n = 592, 70%), a validation set (n = 85, 10%), and a test set (n = 170, 20%).

Image acquisition and standardization

All radiographs were acquired using a standard digital radiography (DR) system. Patients followed a standardized standing positioning protocol, which required keeping the knees straight and hips in a neutral position, with hands placed on the clavicles or hanging naturally at the sides. To achieve precise millimeter-level quantification, we extracted the Pixel Spacing (0028,0030) attribute from each patient’s original DICOM metadata. This allowed for the conversion of image pixel coordinates into physical distances (mm). All images were resized to a standardized resolution of 512 × 512 pixels, followed by the application of Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) to enhance the visibility of bony structures, thereby simulating an authentic “end-to-end” clinical processing workflow26.

Automated anatomical localization system

We developed a fully automated deep learning pipeline based on the High-Resolution Network (HRNet-W32) architecture23; the overall framework and processing pipeline are illustrated in Fig. 1. HRNet was selected for its exceptional capacity to maintain high-resolution spatial information throughout the network, a feature that is paramount for precise landmark localization in medical imaging. Unlike traditional encoder-decoder architectures (e.g., U-Net) that attempt to recover spatial details from heavily downsampled representations, HRNet maintains high-resolution representations continuously. It achieves this by connecting high-to-low resolution subnetworks in parallel rather than in series. Throughout the network, repeated multi-scale fusions are performed: high-resolution branches pass precise spatial details to lower-resolution branches, while lower-resolution branches return rich semantic context to refine the high-resolution output. This parallel architecture prevents the loss of subtle spatial precision, ensuring the high-fidelity heatmap predictions essential for millimeter-level keypoint localization in complex spinal deformities (the architectural block diagram is illustrated in Fig. 2). To optimize the model for the specific challenges of scoliosis, we implemented three clinically motivated enhancements. First, to mitigate the common risk of laterality confusion in scoliosis imaging, we introduced an anatomically driven constraint during training. This mechanism enforces the topological consistency of human anatomy (e.g., ensuring the right shoulder remains spatially to the right of the left shoulder), thereby suppressing anatomically implausible predictions at the algorithmic level. Second, we adopted a foreground-weighted attention strategy via weighted heatmap regression27, designed to sharpen the model’s focus on subtle anatomical landmarks (such as the iliac crests) while simultaneously suppressing background noise. Finally, during the inference phase, we implemented a robust inference strategy known as Test-Time Augmentation (TTA)28. The system integrates predictions from both the original and horizontally flipped images. This process, analogous to a clinical “double-check,” significantly mitigates localization jitter and enhances the model’s robustness across diverse patient positionings.

Clinical safety gating mechanism

To address the challenges posed by heterogeneous annotation quality and severe anatomical occlusion in real-world data, the system integrates a quantitative “Auto-Quality Control (Auto-QC)” module, executing a dual-layered gating logic based on algorithmic certainty and dynamic anatomical constraints. It provides the optimal dynamic range to maximize the discriminative margin between confident localizations and ambiguous anatomical noise, preventing the scores from becoming either overly diffuse or indiscriminately confident. Following established uncertainty estimation protocols in medical imaging29, the optimal static threshold was determined through a systematic grid-search calibration on our internal validation set. By analyzing the coverage-reliability trade-off trajectory, the value of 0.15 was identified as the critical inflection point that effectively isolates heavy-tailed outliers. The system therefore suppresses any landmark prediction where 0.15. Second, the system enforces a dynamic anatomical constraint. The algorithm calculates the absolute vertical difference between symmetric landmarks (e.g., bilateral shoulders or iliac crests). If this difference exceeds a dynamically scaled threshold—defined as 20% of the individual radiograph’s total pixel height (0.20×h0) —the prediction is deemed anatomically implausible. If either the static certainty threshold or the dynamic anatomical constraint is violated, the system triggers the safety gating mechanism, proactively suppressing the landmark’s output (returning a null value) and flagging the specific case for prioritized manual expert review. This design ensures that the AI functions as a robust safety gating mechanism rather than generating deceptively precise yet unreliable metrics under conditions of uncertainty.

Definition of coronal balance parameters

Based on five automatically localized anatomical landmarks—the center of the C7 vertebral body, the superior borders of the bilateral clavicles, and the highest points of the bilateral iliac crests—the system automatically calculates three coronal balance parameters according to standard radiological definitions30. Shoulder Height Difference (SHD) is defined as the absolute vertical height difference between the tangents of the superior borders of the bilateral clavicles. Pelvic Obliquity (PO) is calculated as the vertical height difference between the highest points of the bilateral iliac crests. C7 offset is defined as the horizontal distance from the center of the C7 vertebral body to the central sacral vertical line (CSVL). In the present study, given that sacral anatomical landmarks are occasionally obscured or invisible in certain full-spine radiographs, the vertical bisector of the line connecting the bilateral iliac crests was employed as a functional equivalent for the CSVL. This approach represents a well-established practice in prior spinal imaging research30.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python-based statistical libraries (PyTorch, SciPy and Pandas)31. We employed the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC, two-way random, absolute agreement)32 and the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) to assess the consistency between the AI-derived measurements and the manual gold standard. Systematic bias and the 95% limits of agreement (LoA) were analyzed using Bland-Altman plots33. To establish a clinical benchmark, 40 samples were randomly selected from the test set and independently measured by a second spinal specialist to determine inter-rater reliability. Localization error was defined as the Euclidean distance between the automatically predicted landmarks and the manual gold-standard annotations. We established an empirical threshold of 10 mm to define localization failure, referencing the clinical consensus on acceptable error ranges in spinal imaging research and accounting for the spatial resolution constraints inherent in full-spine radiographs, and consistent with thresholds reported in prior spinal landmark localization studies34. Cases with a localization error exceeding 10 mm were categorized as “outliers” and underwent a qualitative review to identify potential causes (e.g., inconsistent manual annotations or laterality confusion). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

A total of 847 patients with AIS were ultimately enrolled in this study. The dataset was partitioned at the patient level into a training set (n = 592), a validation set (n = 85), and a test set (n = 170). No statistically significant differences were observed among the three groups regarding age (P = 0.383) or sex distribution (P = 0.710), indicating a well-balanced data split. The mean age of the overall cohort was 13.9 ± 2.4 years, with a female predominance (61.4%) (Table 1).

Keypoint Localization Performance

In the test set, the automatic localization error for the five anatomical landmarks generally remained within the millimeter range. The center of the C7 vertebral body achieved the lowest localization error (median: 1.1 mm; 95th percentile: 7.3 mm), with a failure rate (error > 10 mm) of only 2.9%. The median errors for the clavicular and iliac crest landmarks ranged from approximately 2.0 to 3.0 mm, although a small number of outliers exceeding 10 mm were observed (Fig. 3; Table 2). For these outlier cases, a retrospective qualitative review was conducted to analyze potential causes (see the “Robustness and Quality Control” section for details). Due to image cropping or the obscuration of specific anatomical regions, the evaluable sample size (N) varied across different landmarks. To further characterize the heavy-tailed distribution of localization errors and identify the sources of outliers, we conducted a stratified performance analysis based on curve magnitude (mild < 25°, moderate 25°–40°, severe > 40°) alongside a cumulative error distribution analysis (Supplementary Table S1, Supplementary Figures S2 and S3). The overall cumulative distribution confirmed that the vast majority of predictions were highly accurate (e.g., the overall 95th percentile error for SHD was 2.4 mm). However, the stratified analysis revealed that while median errors remained remarkably stable across all severity groups, the 95th percentile errors widened considerably in the severe cohort (e.g., the 95th percentile error for C7 offset increased from 6.25 mm in mild cases to 9.29 mm in severe cases). This structured characterization corroborates that the extreme outliers driving the large standard deviations are not indicative of general algorithmic instability. Rather, they are predominantly localized within severe deformities, where profound axial rotation and overlapping projection artifacts severely compromise the visibility of anatomical landmarks such as the iliac crests. It should be noted that the reported failure rates (2.9%–9.8%) reflect the residual errors on the evaluable subset after the safety gating mechanism was applied. While the safety gating mechanism effectively eliminates catastrophic outliers—as evidenced by the drastic error reduction shown in Supplementary Table S2—a small fraction of highly confident but inaccurate localizations inevitably bypasses the threshold.

Correlation with manual measurements

The AI-derived coronal balance parameters exhibited strong correlations with the manual gold-standard measurements (Fig. 4). Specifically, the Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were 0.965 for SHD (N = 138), 0.953 for PO (N = 115), and 0.981 for C7 offset (N = 115). The mean absolute errors (MAE) were 0.86 mm for SHD, 1.16 mm for PO, and 2.45 mm for C7 offset (Table 3). Due to the activation of the clinical safety gating mechanism, certain low-confidence predictions were automatically suppressed to prevent erroneous outputs; consequently, the effective sample size for comparison was smaller than the total number of cases in the test set.

Agreement and bias analysis

Bland-Altman analysis revealed only minimal systematic bias between the AI system and manual measurements across all three parameters. The mean bias was − 0.10 mm (95% LoA: -3.87 to 3.67 mm) for SHD, 0.13 mm (95% LoA: -4.50 to 4.76 mm) for PO, and 0.04 mm (95% LoA: -6.97 to 7.06 mm) for C7 offset. The data distribution indicated that the differences between automated and manual measurements remained stable across the entire measurement range, with no evident proportional bias (Fig. 5; Table 3).

Inter-rater reliability benchmark

To establish a clinical benchmark for measurement variability, 40 samples were randomly selected from the test set and independently measured by a second spinal surgeon for inter-rater reliability analysis. The results (Supplementary Figure S1) showed that the mean inter-rater biases were − 0.19 mm (95% LoA: -5.72 to 5.34 mm) for SHD, 0.13 mm (95% LoA: -0.84 to 1.09 mm) for PO, and − 0.26 mm (95% LoA: -3.72 to 3.19 mm) for C7 offset. These benchmark data confirm that the overall performance of the automated system closely approximates the inherent variability observed among human experts (Table 3).

Robustness and quality control

A retrospective qualitative evaluation was conducted on the outlier cases identified during the keypoint localization analysis (Fig. 3). The results (Fig. 6) demonstrated that the system maintained anatomical topological consistency even in cases where manual annotations exhibited laterality confusion, showcasing its robustness against inconsistent labeling. Furthermore, in cases characterized by annotation conflicts or ambiguous anatomical structures, the system successfully triggered the safety gating mechanism, automatically suppressing low-confidence outputs and flagging them for manual review. This effectively prevented the generation of unreliable measurement results.

Quantitative evaluation of the safety gating mechanism

To quantitatively substantiate the impact of the proposed safety gating design and to explicitly demonstrate the coverage-reliability trade-off dynamics, we conducted an ablation analysis comparing system performance with and without the safety gating mechanism (Supplementary Table S2). When the gating mechanism was disabled, the system forcibly generated predictions for all test cases (coverage = 100%, 170/170). However, this lack of quality control resulted in the inclusion of severe outliers driven by anatomical ambiguity, substantially increasing the measurement errors (e.g., the 95th percentile error for C7 offset increased to 7.86 mm, and the MAE for Pelvic Obliquity surged to 17.42 mm due to extreme occlusions).

Conversely, activating the dual-layered safety gating mechanism resulted in a controlled reduction in sample coverage (filtering out approximately 19% to 32% of low-confidence cases depending on the specific parameter). In exchange for this coverage trade-off, the reliability of the retained measurements improved dramatically, with the 95th percentile error for C7 offset dropping to 6.5 mm, and the MAE remaining highly stable. This quantitative comparison explicitly confirms the effectiveness of the safety gating mechanism: it successfully isolates the heavy-tailed outliers, ensuring that downstream clinical data remain highly reliable, which is essential for safe algorithmic deployment.

Discussion

In this study, we show that automated coronal balance assessment can be designed not only for numerical measurement, but also with explicit attention to clinical safety. Utilizing a large-scale, single-center dataset of full-spine radiographs, we developed and comprehensively validated an automated system for quantifying key coronal balance parameters, including SHD, PO, and C7-CSVL offset. Our findings demonstrate that the system achieves measurement accuracy that closely approximates the variability observed among human experts, while also addressing limitations of deep learning models that focus solely on geometric precision18. In contrast to many black-box models, which tend to generate outputs even under uncertain conditions, our system integrates a clinically oriented safety gating mechanism24. This design effectively suppresses low-confidence predictions in scenarios involving ambiguous anatomical structures or poor image quality, thereby serving as an additional QC layer within the clinical workflow22.

Although comparing the proposed model against other state-of-the-art AI algorithms would be methodologically interesting, such a direct evaluation is currently unfeasible. Recent tools assessing coronal balance and pelvic parameters are predominantly proprietary commercial software or rely on closed-source algorithms. Without a standardized public benchmark dataset for these specific landmarks in AIS, direct numerical comparisons across different studies are scientifically unreliable due to inherent variations in patient demographics, disease severity, and image acquisition protocols. Therefore, we evaluated the AI’s performance strictly within the framework of human inter-rater variability. Manual measurement is not an absolute zero-error reference; therefore, evaluating AI performance within the framework of inter-rater variability is clinically more meaningful10. As shown in Table 3, the limits of agreement (LoA) between the AI system and manual measurements are largely consistent with the variability bandwidth observed between two senior spinal surgeons. However, we must acknowledge that AI performance is not strictly equivalent to expert inter-rater agreement across all parameters. Notably, the AI-manual limits of agreement (LoA) for PO (-4.50 to 4.76 mm) were substantially wider than the extremely tight inter-rater LoA (r = 0.997). This reflects more than just the complexity of the test samples; rather, it likely reveals the “intrinsic ambiguity” of defining three-dimensional pelvic rotation on two-dimensional radiographs35. In the presence of axial rotation12, the projection of the highest points of the iliac crests is highly susceptible to the observer’s subjective judgment. Therefore, the performance of the AI model essentially reflects a “smoothed expert consensus,” which may offer greater stability than single-instance manual measurements, thereby highlighting the fundamental limitations of using two-dimensional imaging to evaluate complex three-dimensional deformities13.

Clinical data collected in routine practice are rarely perfect and often include various sources of noise; this system demonstrates how a predictive model can be adapted to support safety-aware quality control in real-world clinical data. Our retrospective analysis of outlier cases (Fig. 6) revealed that the system maintained topological consistency through anatomical constraints even in cases with significant manual annotation inconsistencies (e.g., global label permutation), demonstrating its robustness against noisy data. This behavior supports the robustness of the model and indicates that it could also be used as an automated data audit tool. In large-scale retrospective clinical studies, this system can be utilized to pre-scan and flag annotation errors or anomalies within historical data, thereby facilitating the curation of higher-quality research cohorts—a capability that extends far beyond the capabilities of conventional measurement tools21. More importantly from a clinical perspective, in scenarios where anatomical features are conflicting, the system triggers the safety gating mechanism rather than forcing an erroneous output, ensuring that potentially unreliable measurements are promptly flagged for review22.

The introduction of a clinical safety gating mechanism essentially entails a trade-off between coverage (sample size) and reliability. While the activation of the gating mechanism leads to a slight reduction in the effective sample size within the test set, this trade-off is entirely justifiable within a clinical context29. In practice, this trade-off can be adjusted depending on the clinical use case. In future clinical deployment, clinicians can calibrate the confidence threshold to suit specific application scenarios: lowering the threshold to maximize sensitivity for large-scale screening, or raising it to ensure high specificity for diagnostic assistance and the establishment of gold-standard references. In high-throughput environments, a “null” result that prompts prioritized manual review is significantly safer than a seemingly plausible but fundamentally erroneous measurement. This design philosophy aligns with the core medical ethical principle of “Primum non nocere” (First, do no harm)22. Furthermore, the clinical significance of measurement error is inherently parameter-dependent. While a 10 mm variance in the C7 offset may fall within the acceptable physiological range of natural postural sway, a similar 10 mm error in Pelvic Obliquity or Shoulder Height Difference is clinically profound and could erroneously alter decision-making regarding conservative shoe lifts or surgical leveling strategies. Therefore, the 10 mm threshold used for keypoint evaluation in this study serves strictly as a 2D image-processing benchmark rather than a universal clinical tolerance. The true clinical viability of the system is demonstrated by its final derived parameters: the safety gating mechanism successfully constrained the 95th percentile errors for SHD and PO to 2.4 mm and 2.9 mm, respectively—well within strict clinical safety margins.

While extensive research has been dedicated to the automated measurement of the Cobb angle, the quantification of coronal balance parameters has received relatively less attention18. Unlike previous studies that focused primarily on optimizing geometric precision using curated datasets, this study provides a comprehensive validation chain that includes a rigorous evaluation of clinical robustness. Our work complements existing Cobb angle algorithms by providing a more comprehensive evaluation scheme. From a theoretical standpoint, the inherent “algorithmic consistency” of the AI system provides a deterministic output, which conceptually minimizes the measurement noise often associated with manual assessments11. While this stability hypothetically offers advantages for longitudinal monitoring, whether it translates to superior detection of subtle disease progression remains to be clinically validated. Future prospective studies incorporating patient-reported outcomes are required to confirm its actual impact on the long-term management of patient aesthetics and quality of life36.

It is important to contextualize the practical value of this system within real-world clinical environments. For highly experienced spine surgeons, manual assessment of coronal parameters (such as SHD, PO, and C7 offset) is arguably straightforward. However, in high-throughput settings such as large teaching hospitals in developing countries, performing these repetitive measurements on a massive daily scale is physically exhausting and inevitably induces visual fatigue. Furthermore, for medical students, junior orthopedists, or primary care physicians, accurately identifying specific anatomical landmarks—such as the superior margins of the iliac crests—on radiographs obscured by pelvic rotation or severe vertebral deformity remains a substantial challenge due to a steeper learning curve in complex spinal anatomy. Consequently, the primary objective of this AI system is not to outperform top-tier experts. Instead, it serves to digitize and standardize expert-level experience. Even with a 19–32% exclusion rate triggered by the safety gating mechanism, the system effectively reduces the overall manual measurement burden by approximately 70%. Rather than increasing workload, it functions as an intelligent triage tool: autonomously processing the majority of standard cases while precisely directing the clinician’s limited attention to the minority of structurally ambiguous deformities that genuinely require expert manual review. Ultimately, this human-in-the-loop approach liberates surgeons from mechanical labor and facilitates homogeneous care across different tiers of healthcare facilities.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged, which point toward directions for future optimization. First, regarding external validity and generalizability: our dataset was derived from a single center and a uniform imaging workflow. While this approach ensured high internal consistency in annotation standards, it inherently limits the broader applicability of our findings. We acknowledge that no multi-vendor testing was performed, nor was the system’s robustness evaluated under varying radiographic acquisition parameters. Consequently, we must explicitly emphasize that rigorous multi-center external validation remains strictly necessary before this automated system can be considered ready for broader clinical deployment37. Furthermore, the study cohort primarily consisted of patients with AIS. Future work should encompass a broader range of spinal deformities, such as congenital or degenerative scoliosis, to evaluate the system’s applicability across more complex pathological features.

Second, regarding the inherent constraints of two-dimensional (2D) imaging and the transition to 3D analysis: Measurements derived from single AP or PA radiographs are inherently confounded by projectional artifacts, such as patient positioning and pelvic rotation, which fail to capture the true 3D spatial architecture of the deformity35. For instance, our reliance on the iliac crest bisector as a practical 2D proxy for the true Central Sacral Vertical Line (CSVL) introduces inherent geometric errors. In patients with pronounced pelvic axial rotation or translation, the 2D projectional distortion alters the perceived symmetry of the iliac crests. This distortion forces the derived vertical bisector to shift laterally relative to the true anatomical center of the S1 vertebra, thereby introducing a systematic offset in the absolute quantification of the C7 plumb line. In fact, our team actively attempted to utilize deep learning and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to reconstruct 3D models from standard biplanar (PA and lateral) radiographs. However, severe anatomical occlusions (e.g., overlapping ribs, scapulae, and pelvic structures) and the profound difficulty in establishing stable cross-planar landmark correspondences severely compromised the GNNs, whose 3D predictions failed to meet clinical safety standards. While our integrated clinical safety gating mechanism partially mitigates this risk by suppressing unreliable outputs rather than generating erroneous data, extracting high-precision 3D measurements from uncalibrated 2D radiographs remains an unresolved challenge. Overcoming these matching and occlusion hurdles represents the definitive breakthrough target for our future research.

Finally, regarding the extension of clinical value: this study primarily focuses on the measurement accuracy and consistency of radiographic parameters. While coronal balance is closely associated with aesthetic deformity, the current analysis has not yet directly incorporated patient-reported clinical outcomes (such as SRS-22 questionnaires or pain scores)36. In fact, the high-throughput automated tool developed in this study is a necessary prerequisite for conducting such large-scale correlation analyses. Future research will focus on leveraging this tool to uncover deep-seated associations between radiographic parameters and patient quality of life within longitudinal cohorts, thereby validating its practical utility in optimizing clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed and internally validated an automated workflow for the multi-parameter quantification of coronal balance in patients with scoliosis. By integrating anatomical constraints with a safety-oriented safety gating mechanism, the system achieves measurement agreement approximating the inter-rater variability among senior spinal surgeons. Quantitatively, the framework demonstrated high precision with mean absolute errors of 0.86–2.45 mm across key coronal parameters, reliably reducing the manual measurement workload by approximately 70%. Unlike conventional “black-box” models, this system provides a high degree of algorithmic consistency and ensures robust inference by effectively flagging clinically ambiguous cases through its safety gating design. These findings validate the technical feasibility and internal safety of the proposed framework, laying a reliable foundation for future prospective studies to investigate its true impact on longitudinal clinical outcomes and decision-making.
