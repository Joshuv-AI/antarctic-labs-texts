Similarities and differences of regional brain activity between first-episode drug-naïve anxious and nonanxious MDD patients
Authors: Guanghui Liu, Zijia Sun, Chunpeng Zhou, Xiangxiang Wang, Min Dai, Jinping Zhang, Rende Zhang, Hong Xu, Gangzhong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAnxiety is one of the most common symptoms in major depressive disorder (MDD), and anxious MDD patients exhibit more complicated and serious symptoms compared with nonanxious MDD patients. However, the differences of pathological mechanisms underlying anxious and nonanxious MDD are unclear. The present study aimed to explore the differences of central neural correlates between anxious and nonanxious MDD patients.MethodsResting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging data were collected form 42 first-episode drug-naïve anxious MDD, 42 nonanxious MDD patients and 45 healthy controls (HCs). Regional homogeneity (ReHo) values were calculated and compared between groups using the gray matter volume as covariates. Relationships between ReHo values and emotional scale scores were evaluated. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to determine the suitability of altered brain region for distinguishing anxious MDD from nonanxious MDD.ResultsIn comparison with HCs, anxious MDD patients showed decreased ReHo values in the inferior temporal gyrus, precuneus, supplementary motor area and putamen. Apart from the above abnormal brain regions, nonanxious MDD patients additionally demonstrated decreased ReHo values in the middle frontal gyrus when compared with HCs. Decreased ReHo values were identified in the right parahippocampal gyrus of anxious MDD patients when compared with nonanxious MDD patients. Moreover, negative relationship was identified between ReHo values of the right supplementary motor area and 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale scores. Finally, ROC analysis revealed that the right parahippocampal gyrus might be helpful for distinguishing anxious MDD from nonanxious MDD.ConclusionThese findings suggested that the central pathological mechanisms of both anxious and nonanxious MDD might be associated with decreased spontaneous brain activities. Moreover, broader brain regions would exhibit decreased activation when accompanied by comorbid anxiety and the right parahippocampal gyrus might be a potential neuroimaging marker for distinguishing anxious MDD patients from those without anxiety.Keywords: Major depressive disorder, Anxiety, Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging, Spontaneous brain activities, Regional homogeneity

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is ranked as one of the leading causes of disability [1], and comorbid anxiety is common in MDD patients [2, 3]. The combination of MDD and anxiety is associated with greater morbidity [4–8], which were found in anxious MDD patients [8, 9]. Comorbidity of MDD and anxiety refers to the situation where an individual simultaneously experiences symptoms of MDD and anxiety during the same time period, which meets both the diagnostic criteria for MDD and anxiety disorders [10]. Neurotransmitters in the brain, such as serotonin and norepinephrine, play a crucial role in regulating emotions [11]. When the function of these neurotransmitters is abnormal, it may lead to the onset of symptoms of MDD and anxiety [12]. Patients with MDD and anxiety often have negative cognitive patterns, which make them more prone to negative emotions, resulting in symptoms of both MDD and anxiety [13]. The severity and impact of anxiety and MDD symptoms were often assessed through interviews and questionnaires, lacking objectivity. Antidepressants such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) can be used to treat MDD and also have some therapeutic effects on anxiety disorders [14]. The central mechanisms underlying the comorbidity of MDD and anxiety are very complex. 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) plays a crucial role in emotion regulation. When the concentration of 5-HT in the synaptic cleft decreases, it triggers both anxious rumination and loss of pleasure, leading to the onset of MDD and anxiety symptoms [15]. Norepinephrine (NE) is closely related to emotions, attention, and wakefulness. Patients with MDD and anxiety often have abnormal levels of NE, which may lead to depressive symptoms, while excessive activation of NE may trigger anxiety [16]. Dopamine (DA) is associated with reward, motivation, and pleasure. The imbalance of DA levels may affect individuals’ perception and motivation of rewards, leading to depressive symptoms, and may also be related to the generation of anxiety emotions [17].

The amygdala is an important area for emotional perception and processing, closely related to anxiety [18]. The hippocampus is related to memory and emotion regulation [19]. The impaired hippocampus is related to anxiety [20]. The prefrontal cortex is responsible for emotion regulation, decision-making, and cognitive control. Patients with MDD show lower activity in this area, indicating that this may be related to depressive symptoms [21]. Anxiety disorders may exhibit increased activity in this area, which is related to anxiety [22]. The cingulated gyrus is related to emotional regulation, cognitive control, and self-reflection [23]. Patients with MDD and anxiety may exhibit excessive activity in the cingulate gyrus, leading to disorders in emotional regulation and cognitive function [24]. The amygdala-hippocampus-medial prefrontal cortex circuit plays a crucial role in emotion regulation [25]. Anxiety and MDD patients have functional abnormalities in this circuit, such as overactivation of the amygdala, hippocampal atrophy, and abnormal activity of the medial prefrontal cortex, which may lead to emotional regulation disorders and impaired cognitive function [26]. The default mode network is responsible for self-referential thinking, and the connection strength between the default mode network of comorbid patients and the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex is higher than that of healthy individuals [27]. This vicious cycle of “thinking rumination emotion regulation” is the key mechanism for the comorbidity of MDD and anxiety.

In view of the considerable comorbidity between MDD and anxiety, these two common psychiatric conditions might have certain pathophysiological overlap. It was suggested that MDD and anxiety might share certain similar central pathological mechanisms [28]. In addition, considering the discrepancy of the clinical characteristics between nonanxious and anxious MDD, we speculated that MDD and anxiety patients might also exhibit certain different pathological substrates in the brain. However, the common and different pathological mechanisms underlying these two conditions were unclear, especially the distinct brain mechanisms separating these two disorders. Comorbidity with anxiety is common in MDD patients, however, the shared and distinct central pathological mechanisms have not been fully identified. The prefrontal, temporal and parietal cortex were consistently functionally impaired in MDD and anxiety [29–37]. Considering the effects of antidepressant treatment and MDD relapse on the spontaneous brain activities [38–43], it would be useful to recruit first-episode drug-naïve MDD patients to better evaluate the common and special pathological mechanisms between anxious, nonanxious patients.

MDD and anxiety might have overlapping and distinct neural correlates in the brain [44–47]. However, previous neuroimaging studies focused on exploring the differences of central pathological mechanisms underlying MDD or anxiety alone rather than MDD comorbid anxiety. MDD patients with anxiety had decreased spontaneous brain activity intensity in the anterior cingulate cortex and insula when compared with those without anxiety [48]. However, only activities of the anterior cingulate cortex and insula were assessed. Moreover, the effects of disease frequency and treatment on the brain activity could not be excluded.

In recent years, the non-invasive technique of resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) has been widely used to explore the central pathological mechanisms of psychiatric disorders [49–52]. The amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF) or fractional ALFF (fALFF) reflecting the intensity of regional spontaneous brain activity and regional homogeneity (ReHo) assessing the synchronization of regional spontaneous brain activity, are two common measures applied [53, 54]. ReHo can directly reflect the synchronous activity of neurons and is an objective quantitative indicator. It has demonstrated significant value in the study of numerous neurological and psychiatric disorders. By comparing the ReHo values of patients with different diseases and healthy control groups, abnormal changes in brain activity under disease conditions can be discovered, providing clues for the disease pathogenesis. ReHo can detect small changes in brain activity and has high sensitivity for some early or mild brain dysfunction. In addition, ReHo is based on rs-fMRI, which does not require special stimulation or intervention to the subjects, making it non-invasive and suitable for various populations. This enables the comparison and integration of results between different studies, providing assurance for the reliability and stability of the research.

MDD patients showed increased ReHo values in the left superior temporal gyrus, middle temporal gyrus, supramarginal and bilateral precuneus, and decreased ReHo values in the right middle temporal gyrus, superior cerebellum, the lateral middle occipital gyrus and cuneus [55]. In patients with generalized anxiety disorder, lower ReHo values were detected in the right precentral, right postcentral and left precentral gyrus while higher ReHo values were identified in the left posterior cingulate cortex when compared to healthy individuals [56]. Accordingly, MDD patients and anxiety patients may have similar and different changes of regional brain activity, which may be the central mechanisms underlying the conditions of depression and anxiety, respectively. MDD patients showed smaller gray matter volume in the right parahippocampal gyrus while anxious patients exhibited lower gray matter volume in the right medial frontal gyrus [57]. In addition, brain regions with lower gray matter volume were related to the negative emotional processes of anxious patients [57]. These shared and unique structural changes of brain regions were involved in the regulation of emotion and cognition. These findings suggested that MDD patients and anxiety patients might also have common and different abnormalities in the regional spontaneous brain activity. Specific functional connectivity changes were found in the default mode network (DMN), salience network, limbic and sensorimotor networks between patients with MDD and anxiety while no common functional connectivity changes were identified between these two disorders [44]. Depression-related functional changes were identified the limbic regions while anxiety-related functional abnormalities were revealed in the paralimbic regions during rest [26]. To date, most previous neuroimaging studies have focused on MDD or anxiety patients alone rather than those with both conditions. Moreover, the differences of regional spontaneous brain activity between anxious and nonanxious MDD were unclear. However, several previous neuroimaging studies had used the method of rs-fMRI for exploring the differences of central neural correlates between anxious and nonanxious MDD patients. In the first rs-fMRI study, MDD patients formally diagnosed co-morbid anxiety disorders showed dysfunctional connectivity between the amygdala and other brain networks [58]. The findings suggested that abnormal functional connectivity might play an underappreciated role in MDD with co-morbid anxiety. In the second rs-fMRI study, it had demonstrated that MDD with anxiety decreased fALFF values within the anterior cingulate cortex and insula when compared to MDD without anxiety, especially in women, which suggested that the fALFF values can be potential biomarkers for severe MDD phenotype [48]. Based on the above neuroimaging studies of anxious MDD, we had evidence above that rs-fMRI could be used for exploring the differences in the regional spontaneous brain activity between nonanxious and anxious MDD.

The present study aimed to explore the differences of central neural correlates between anxious and nonanxious MDD patients. Based on the findings of aforementioned studies, we hypothesized that nonanxious and anxious MDD might exhibit both common and special changes in regional spontaneous activity in brain regions involved in the processing of emotion. Therefore, first-episode drug-naïve anxious, nonanxious MDD patients and healthy controls (HCs) were enrolled in this study. The data of depression and anxiety scales, as well as rs-MRI data, were collected from all participants. Finally, rs-MRI data were preprocessed and ReHo values were calculated and compared to explore the shared and different changes of spontaneous brain activities between groups. In addition, relationships between ReHo values and HAMD-17, HAMA scores were evaluated. Moreover, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to determine the suitability of altered brain region as potential neuroimaging biomarker for distinguishing anxious MDD from nonanxious MDD. This study focused on exploring the central pathological mechanism underlying MDD comorbid anxiety rather than MDD or anxiety alone. Moreover, the effects of disease frequency and treatment on the brain activity were be excluded in this study.

Materials and methods

Participants

A total of 42 first-episode drug-naïve anxious MDD and 42 nonanxious MDD patients were enrolled in this study. The diagnosis of MDD was based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria. Additionally, 45 demographically-matched HCs were recruited from the local community via advertisements. This study was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of Zhengzhou Seventh People’s Hospital. Prior to participating in this study, all subjects had provided written informed consents. In addition, this study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

In this study, the severity of depressive symptoms was evaluated using the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17), which was designed by Hamilton in 1960 [59]. Total scores were categorized as follows: (1) < 7 points indicated no depression; (2) 7–17 points indicated mild depression, (3)18–24 points indicated moderate depression; (4) > 24 points indicated severe depression. In addition, the anxiety levels were assessed by the Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA) with 14 items rating from 0 to 4, which was designed by Hamilton in 1959 [60]. Total scores were categorized as follows: (1) < 7 points indicated no anxiety; (2) 7–14 points indicated possible anxiety; (3) 14–21 points indicated definite anxiety; (4) 21–29 points indicated obvious anxiety; (5) > 29 points indicated severe anxiety. Participants were eligible if they met al.l of the following inclusion criteria: (1) Han ethnicity (the predominant ethnic group in China); (2) right-handed; (3) aged between 20 and 45 years; (4) more than 9 years of education. In addition, all patients should also meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) first depressive episode with no history of any psychiatric disorders; (2) no more than 12 months of disease duration; (3) HAMD-17 scores ≥ 17; (4) had no history of antidepressants, antipsychotics or any other medical therapy. Moreover, patients were categorized into the group of anxious MDD if HAMA scores ≥ 14 while patients were categorized into the group of nonanxious MDD if HAMA scores < 14 [60, 61]. HCs had no current and history of any psychiatric disorder screened according to the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Nonpatient Edition (SCID-5, Chinese version). All HCs had HAMD-17 scores < 7 and HAMA scores < 7.

Subjects were excluded if they met any one or more of the following exclusion criteria: (1) secondary depression caused by drugs or organic diseases; (2) combined with other psychiatric disorders; (3) history of serious medical conditions that might affect brain function or structure (e.g. neurological diseases; head trauma or loss of consciousness); (4) history of alcohol or substance abuse or dependence; (5) structural brain abnormalities identified by routine MRI; (6) female subjects in lactating, menstruating or pregnant stage; (7) any other contraindication for MRI scanning.

MRI data acquisition and preprocessing

MRI images were acquired using a 3.0 Tesla scanner (750w, GE). All subjects were instructed to keep eyes closed, stay relaxed and awake, think of nothing in particular during MRI scanning. T1-weighted structural images were acquired with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 7.7ms; echo time (TE) = 3.1ms; slice thickness = 1 mm; field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256mm2; matrix = 256 × 256; flip angle (FA) = 12°; slices = 160. Rs-MRI images were obtained with the following parameters: TR = 2000ms; TE = 30ms; slice thickness = 3.5 mm; FOV = 224 × 224mm2; matrix = 80 × 80; FA = 90°; slices = 33; volumes = 240. Participants were excluded if structural abnormalities or artifacts were identified by structural MRI scans.

MRI data were preprocessed using Data Processing Assistant for Resting-State fMRI (DPARSF, 4.1 Advanced Edition) [62] based on MATLAB (R2009a) platform. The main preprocessing steps were listed as follows: (1) the first 10 volumes were discarded to reduce the instability of magnetic field signal during the initial scanning; (2) the remaining functional images were corrected for the inter-slice acquisition time delay by slice timing correction; (3) realignment for head motion correction and participants were excluded if the maximal translation was more than 2 mm or the maximal rotation more than 2°; (4) spatially normalized into the standard Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template space and resampled to 3 × 3 × 3mm3 voxels. In this study, no subject was removed due to head motion, abnormalities or artifacts in T1-weighted structural image.

Calculation of ReHo values

ReHo values were used to evaluate the similarity of time series. Higher ReHo values imply higher synchronization of resting-state time series while lower ReHo values indicate lower synchronization of the voxel with its adjacent voxels. Before the calculations of ReHo values, the pre-processed MRI data were further processed by the following steps: (1) signal linear detrending; (2) temporal bandpass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz); (3) regressing out nuisance covariates including Friston 24 head motion parameters, white matter signals, as well as cerebrospinal fluid signals. The values of ReHo were calculated in the whole brain based on the Anatomical Automatic Labeling (AAL) template [63] by DPARSF (4.1 Advanced Edition) [62] in MATLAB (R2009a) platform. ReHo maps of all subjects were generated by calculating Kendall’s coefficients of concordance (KCC, also called ReHo values) of the time series of a given voxel with its surrounding 26 voxels on a voxel-wise basis. All zReHo values were smoothed. In addition, to improve the normality of data, ReHo values were converted into z-values using Fisher’s r-to-z transformation for all subjects.

Statistical analysis

Differences of demographic and clinical data between groups were determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and post hoc contrasts with least-significant difference (LSD) or Pearson chi-square test using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (IBM SPSS Statistics 23). Adjusted P values less than 0.05 after multiple comparison correction using the FDR method were considered statistically significant. Moreover, differences of ReHo values between groups were evaluated by two sample t-tests (two tailed) using the gray matter volume as the covariate based on the software of Resting-State fMRI Data Analysis Toolkit (REST, 1.8) [64]. The significance threshold was set at voxel-level P < 0.001 and cluster-level P < 0.05 (two tailed), and the results were corrected for multiple comparisons using Gaussian Random Field (GRF) theory. The relationships between ReHo values of altered brain regions and HAMD-17, HAMA scores were evaluated by Pearson correlation analysis. In addition, ROC analysis was constructed to evaluate the accuracy of altered brain region for discriminating anxious MDD from nonanxious MDD. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study, first-episode drug-naïve MDD patients were recruited to evaluate the common and different pathological mechanisms underlying comorbidity of MDD with anxiety compared with those without anxiety. To the best of our knowledge, this was the first study that explored the differences of spontaneous brain activities between first-episode drug-naïve anxious, nonanxious MDD patients and HCs during rest. With the measure of ReHo, decreased brain activities were found in the frontal, temporal, parietal and putamen of MDD patients when compared with HCs, and the affected brain area was larger in anxious MDD patients than those without anxiety. In addition, decreased activation of the right parahippocampal gyrus might be related to the occurrence of anxiety in first-episode drug-naïve MDD patients. Moreover, negative relationship was identified between ReHo values of the right supplementary motor area and HAMD-17 scores. The right parahippocampal gyrus might be helpful for distinguishing anxious MDD from nonanxious MDD.

Decreased functional connectivity was found in the prefrontal cortex of MDD patients while decreased functional connectivity was identified in the putamen of anxiety patients, which implied that dysfunction of the frontal regions might be involved in the impaired executive function and emotion regulation of MDD patients whereas abnormal activity of putamen might cause impaired integration of emotional experience and uncontrollable worry, leading to anxiety [65]. The right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex is implicated in negative emotional evaluation, which is regulated by anxiety during negative emotional processing, whereas the left ventrolateral prefrontal cortex is involved in positive emotional evaluation and inhibiting the activation of left orbitofrontal cortex leading negative emotion including depression and anxiety [66]. These evaluative processing were associated with the function and structure of parahippocampal gyrus and amygdala [66]. These brain regions with altered activity might be the common neuropathological mechanisms in MDD with anxiety and those without anxiety when compared with HCs. In addition, the co-occurring anxiety might lead to more serious level of altered brain activation in MDD patients. In comparison with HCs, both anxious and nonanxious MDD patients showed decreased ReHo values in the left rolandic operculum, putamen, right precuneus, supplementary motor area, as well as increased ReHo values in the right rectus gyrus. These brain regions might be abnormal in both depressive patients and anxious patients [36, 44, 47, 55]. In anxious MDD patients, the unique abnormal brain regions were located in the left supplementary motor area, right inferior temporal gyrus, putamen (decreased ReHo) and in the right angular gyrus (increased ReHo). The supplementary motor area plays a crucial role in coordinating and controlling the execution of complex actions [67]. It works in conjunction with the main motor cortex to regulate the sequence and execution of movements. In addition, the supplementary motor area is a key area for initiating voluntary movement, especially playing an important role in stimulating movement. The inferior temporal gyrus is a part of the temporal lobe of the brain, whose main functions include processing visual information, participating in object recognition and visual cognitive tasks [68]. It is also associated with higher-level visual processing, receiving visual information from the occipital lobe and collaborating with the middle and upper temporal gyrus to participate in the integration and interpretation of visual information. The putamen is an important component of the basal ganglia, primarily responsible for regulating motor function and influencing various types of learning [69]. It performs its function by utilizing dopamine, which is based on the neurotransmitter dopamine circuit. The putamen and caudate nucleus together form the new striatum, which participates in the control of human movement. The angular gyrus is an important area of the brain that primarily processes visual and auditory information and integrates it for language comprehension and production [70]. These brain regions might be anxiety-related in MDD patients [36, 44, 47, 55]. In nonanxious MDD patients, the distinctive abnormal brain regions were the left middle frontal gyrus, middle temporal gyrus, lingual gyrus, which all exhibited decreased ReHo values. These brain regions might be depression-related [36, 44, 47, 55]. More importantly, decreased ReHo values were identified in the right parahippocampal gyrus of anxious MDD patients when compared with nonanxious MDD patients, which suggested that the right parahippocampal gyrus might be an effective biomarker for distinguishing anxious MDD patients from those without anxiety. Therefore, we speculated that these common regions altered brain activity might be the special mechanism underlying MDD. The unique abnormal brain regions of anxious MDD patients might play a key role in the occurrence of anxiety when these individuals developed MDD.

In addition, decreased activation of the right parahippocampal gyrus (a brain region associated with autobiographical memory [71–73]) might be the disorder related functional changes of MDD with anxiety when compared to those without anxiety in this study. The depressive symptom evaluated by the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21) [74], was found to be moderated by the functional connectivity [75] and atrophy severity of this region was found to be associated with the severity of depressive symptom in patients with Alzheimer’s disease [76]. In addition, relative to HCs, anxiety patients exhibited hypoconnectivity between the parahippocampal gyrus and amygdala [77]. The fALFF values of parahippocampal gyrus were correlated with anxiety, which might be mediated by the emotional intelligence [78]. Decreased functional connectivity was detected between the right parahippocampal gyrus and left medial prefrontal cortex of anxiety patients, which suggested that functional deficits of the right parahippocampal gyrus might contribute to the development of anxiety in MDD patients based on the decreased activities in cortical regions of MDD patients [79]. Both MDD and anxiety patients have similar negative cognition patterns including ruminations and concerns, which suggested that these two psychiatric disorders might have common neuropathogenesis [80]. The structural abnormalities in the right parahippocampal gyrus have been found to be related to high comorbidity rate of MDD and anxiety [81].

The parahippocampal gyrus, a region associated with autobiographical memory, belongs to the limbic regions in the brain, which have been found to be involved in MDD pathogenesis [82]. The parahippocampal gyrus was more likely to be recruited during recall or imagination of personal events compared with recognition tasks [83]. In this study, the right parahippocampal gyrus was a characteristic feature for distinguishing depressive patients with anxiety from those without anxiety. The parahippocampal gyrus is the main cortical input area of the hippocampus, which participates in emotional and cognitive processes such as emotional learning and memory consolidation together with the hippocampus. In depression and anxiety disorders, decreased activity in the parahippocampal gyrus might be associated with abnormal memory processing and emotional regulation in these disorders. Patients might exhibit excessive attention and repetitive thinking towards negative information when experiencing negative events, which might be related to abnormal activity in the parahippocampal gyrus. In addition, the parahippocampal gyrus is closely related to emotional regulation and plays a role in handling anxious responses. In anxiety disorders, abnormal activity in the parahippocampal gyrus might lead to increased sensitivity to threats and fear responses. Moreover, the parahippocampal gyrus is particularly associated with rumination leading to intensified emotions and sustained psychological stress, which is one of the common features of depression and anxiety disorders.

Somatic symptoms are closely related to anxiety and depressive symptoms. Anxious and depressive patients often experienced somatic symptoms. In addition, somatic symptoms mediated the association between anxiety and depression, which might be achieved by functional connectivity involving the right medial superior frontal gyrus, left thalamus, triangular part of left inferior frontal gyrus, bilateral insula, precentral gyri, postcentral gyrus and cingulate gyrus in the brain [84]. The right medial superior frontal gyrus was involved in depressive and somatic symptoms. The thalamus was related to sensory processing, which might also play a role in somatic symptoms. Patients with anxiety often suffer from excessive anxious responses leading to avoidance behavior. Altered functional connectivity was found within the amygdala of patients with anxiety [85]. During the emotional task, patients with anxiety exhibited hyperactivation in the amygdala, as well as decreased functional coupling of the left amygdala with the medial orbitofrontal cortex and the posterior cingulate cortex/precuneus. The strength of this functional connectivity was negatively associated with the severity of anxiety. In addition, further reduced functional connectivity and marked functional separation between the medial orbitofrontal and anterior cingulate cortices were identified in patients with anxiety. Combined with the hyperactivation of amygdala the findings corroborated the proposed dysfunction of the fronto-amygdalar inhibition in anxious patients and indicated modulatory influences of the anterior and posterior cingulate cortices on anxiety perception and processing. In human patients, anxiety and depression-like behaviours might be associated increased functional connectivity in the cingulate cortex with regions in the limbic system, resulting in deficits in moderating and regulating the processing of emotion in mood disorders [86]. In young adult rats, anxiety and depression-like behaviours might be associated with the decreased functional connectivity within the salience and interoceptive networks, and hyperconnectivity of multiple regions to the cingulate cortex [87]. The above studies mainly showed abnormal brain functional connections related to depression or anxiety, and explained the similarities and differences in the central pathological mechanisms of the two diseases from the perspective of the strength of connections between different regions of the brain. However, this study further elucidated the similarities and differences in this mechanism from another perspective, the consistency of activity in local brain regions. This further deepened the understanding of the independent brain regions associated with the occurrence of the two diseases.

Changes in brain activity have important values in MDD and anxiety, including symptom recognition, severity assessment, diagnosis and differential diagnosis, drug selection, efficacy prediction, efficacy evaluation, as follows: (1) recognition of potential symptoms of MDD and anxiety. MDD and anxiety may only have slight changes in brain function in the early stages. Through rs-fMRI, these subtle changes can be captured, allowing for the detection of potential MDD and anxiety before symptoms become apparent. (2) Assisting in determining the source of MDD and anxiety: When MDD and anxiety patients experience multiple complex symptoms, changes in brain function can help determine whether the symptoms are caused by brain lesions or other systemic issues. (3) Severity assessment of MDD and anxiety. The rs-fMRI can provide quantitative indicators to evaluate the severity of MDD and anxiety, providing a basis for developing treatment plans and predicting prognosis. (4) Monitoring the progression of MDD and anxiety. During the treatment process, regular rs-fMRI can dynamically monitor changes in brain function to understand whether MDD and anxiety is improving, stable, or worsening. (5) Diagnosis and differential diagnosis of MDD and anxiety. MDD and anxiety have different characteristics of brain function changes. By combining rs-fMRI with clinical symptoms, medical history, etc., the type of MDD and anxiety can be accurately diagnosed.

The findings of this study were of great significance for the understanding and treatment of both depression and anxiety, especially for MDD comorbid with anxiety. Abnormal brain regions could help understanding the impaired physiological functions and psychological states of the brain, which could gain deeper understanding of the neurobiological basis of these two common mental disorders. In addition, these abnormal brain regions might be helpful for developing more effective diagnostic tools and treatment methods for MDD comorbid with anxiety. With the advancement of technology and innovation in research methods, brain function research would continue to provide new perspectives for understanding depression and anxiety. Future research directions might include utilizing more sophisticated neuroimaging techniques to observe subtle changes in brain activity, as well as developing individualized treatment plans based on the brain activity. Moreover, the application of multimodal neuroimaging techniques, such as the combination of electroencephalography and fMRI, would further improve the accuracy of diagnosis and the effectiveness of treatment. In summary, these findings might be not only help for understanding the neurobiological basis of depression and anxiety, but also for providing scientific basis for developing new treatment methods and diagnostic tools.

However, there were several limitations in this study. Firstly, this was a cross-sectional study with a relatively small sample size, which might limit the robustness of the findings. Secondly, since MDD and anxiety were complex diseases associated with multiple clinical and pathological factors, more demographic and clinical characteristics should be collected to comprehensively explore the different and common mechanisms underlying these two disorders. Thirdly, considering that brain structure and function were closely related and brain structure served as the foundation for brain function, therefore, changes of brain activity should be further confirmed by structural MRI. In the future, large sample size studies were needed to explore the differences of central pathological mechanisms between anxious and nonanxious MDD patients based on multimodal MRI data. Finally, considering that the duration of illness could significantly impact brain structure and function, it was necessary to compare the brain function and structure of patients with different disease courses, and conduct longitudinal follow-up studies to track the changes of brain function and structure as the prolonging of course of disease.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this was the first rs-fMRI study exploring the differences of spontaneous brain activities between first-episode drug-naïve anxious and nonanxious MDD patients with the measure of ReHo. Both anxious and nonanxious MDD patients showed decreased spontaneous brain activities in the frontal, temporal, parietal and putamen, and patients comorbid with anxiety exhibited more serious decreases of activation in these regions. Moreover, decreased activities of the right parahippocampal gyrus might be implicated in the occurrence and development of anxiety in MDD patients. These findings revealed the common and distinct central mechanisms underlying anxious and nonanxious MDD, which provided potential neuroimaging markers for discriminating anxious MDD from those without anxiety.
