Validity and reliability of the Chinese version of the Watson Caritas Patient Score
Authors: Yen-Fang Chou, Shu-Fen Chi, Chiu-Tzu Lin, Shu-Ling Yeh, Hsing-Ju Lu, Mei-Chu Tsai, Yi-Ping Tseng, Wen-Pin Yu, Ming-Chu Chiang, Chin-Yen Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWatson’s Theory of Human Caring emphasizes ten Caritas Processes®, providing a theoretical foundation for holistic nursing care. The Watson Caritas Patient Score (WCPS) has been validated internationally. However, to date, no culturally adapted Chinese version exists. The aim of this study was to translate and culturally adapt the WCPS into a Chinese version (WCPS-C), and examine its psychometric properties and convergent validity using the Patient Satisfaction with Nursing Care Scale.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in two phases: (1) translation and cultural adaptation of the WCPS into Chinese, and (2) psychometric testing using two independent samples. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA, n = 104) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA, n = 221) were also performed. Data were collected from March 1 to September 8, 2025, at five hospitals in Taiwan. Descriptive statistics, Cronbach’s α for reliability, Pearson correlation for convergent validity, EFA with principal axis factoring and Promax rotation, and CFA using AMOS were conducted to evaluate construct validity and model fit.ResultsThe participants’ demographics did not differ significantly between the EFA and CFA samples. EFA supported a one-factor structure (KMO = 0.813, Bartlett’s p < 0.001), explaining 82.34% of variance, with factor loadings ranging from 0.825 to 0.937. CFA confirmed good fit indices: χ²/df = 0.50, CFI = 1.000, RMSEA = 0.000, SRMR = 0.004; standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.825 to 0.937. Internal consistency was high (Cronbach’s α = 0.935). The WCPS-C scores showed a positive correlation with patient satisfaction (Spearman’s rho = 0.628–0.764, p < 0.001).ConclusionsThe WCPS-C is a culturally relevant psychometric tool for assessing patients’ perceptions of nursing care in Taiwan. It captures the holistic dimensions of nursing care beyond traditional satisfaction measures. It can be applied in clinical settings to evaluate nurses’ caring behaviors, enhance caring competence, and guide quality improvement in nursing practice.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04605-z.Keywords: Watson Caritas Patient Score, Exploratory factor analysis, Confirmatory factor analysis, Internal consistency

Background

Caring is a core value of nursing. It encompasses clinical skills, judgment, and commitment to ensuring patients’ dignity, emotional well-being, and holistic health [1]. In the post COVID-19 pandemic era, the global health care system faced shortages in the nursing workforce. This highlights the need for person-centered caring and healing nurse-patient relationships [2, 3]. The Theory of Human Caring includes 10 Caritas Processes®. The theory guides nurses in delivering care that fosters trust, preserves human dignity, creates healing environments, and respects patients’ beliefs and hopes [1, 4–7]. These processes affirm that patients’ experiences of care extend beyond clinical outcomes and include emotional, spiritual, and relational dimensions [7]. The Theory of Human Caring helps to enhance the professional identity of nurses. It also focuses on the ethical and humanistic dimensions that are often overlooked in high-tech healthcare environments [8–10].

In modern technology-driven health care systems, nurses often face time constraints and this leads to reduced interpersonal contact with patients [11]. Studies have shown that caring behaviors are positively correlated with patient satisfaction, trust, and treatment adherence [12, 13]. Watson et al. developed the Watson Caritas Patient Score (WCPS) to operationalize the assessment of caring behaviors from a patient’s perspective. When nurses form authentic human connections, their actions affect the surrounding energy field, awakening compassion and fostering the development of Caritas consciousness in clinical practice. This perspective enables nurses to encounter others with a loving heart, which is a core concept underpinning the use of the WCPS to measure caring behaviors [7, 14].

The WCPS is a unidimensional five-item instrument, in which each item reflects a core caring domain derived from the 10 Caritas Processes® [9]. Although conceptually grounded in multiple caring domains, psychometric validation studies have consistently supported a single-factor structure, thus indicating that these domains collectively represent an overarching caring construct [9, 15]. The original WCPS demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.90) and construct validity [9]. Existing studies have confirmed its reliability and unidimensional structure across different cultural contexts, including Korean, Italian, and Spanish [14–16]. These findings indicate that the WCPS is a theoretically grounded yet pragmatic tool for capturing patients’ perceptions of caring behaviors in clinical settings.

In Taiwan, patient satisfaction surveys are routinely employed as key indicators for evaluating the quality of nursing care. Patient satisfaction captures patients’ perceptions of care, communication, emotional support, and overall experience. Higher patient satisfaction is closely associated with adherence to care plans, patient loyalty, and health-promoting behaviors [17–19]. However, existing tools emphasize transactional service dimensions and often fail to measure the relational and emotional aspects central to nursing care. This is particularly concerning in fast-paced, technology-driven clinical settings in Taiwan, where opportunities for human connection may diminish. Research has demonstrated that nurse–patient caring relationships significantly affect patient satisfaction and perceived care quality [18, 20]. Thus, there is a crucial need for culturally adapted instruments that capture the core values of nursing care.

The WCPS has been translated and validated in multiple languages and countries [14–16]. However, a validated Chinese version of this scale is not available yet. This limits the ability of health care organizations in Taiwan to measure and improve the caring dimensions of nursing from the patient’s perspective. Without using this tool, it is possible that quality improvement efforts neglect one of the most meaningful aspects of patient-centered care. Additionally, no studies compared the WCPS with general patient satisfaction tools in Taiwan, thus leaving a gap in understanding how caring-based metrics align with traditional satisfaction outcomes.

Methods

Aims

This study aimed to (1) translate and culturally adapt the WCPS and develop a Chinese version (WCPS-C); (2) examine the psychometric properties of the WCPS-C, including reliability and validity; and (3) compare the WCPS-C with a commonly used patient satisfaction scale to evaluate its convergent validity and potential utility in health care settings in Taiwan.

Study design and participant selection

This study employed a cross-sectional design. In the first phase, the Chinese version of the Watson Caritas Patient Score questionnaire was developed through translation and cultural adaptation. Forward–backward translation and cognitive interviews were conducted to assess participants’ comprehension of the tool [21, 22]. The WCPS was first translated into Chinese by a translator fluent in English and knowledgeable in nursing practices to ensure conceptual equivalence and contextual appropriateness. The translator also rated the difficulty of each item on a scale of 0–10, with 0 indicating no difficulty and 10 indicating extreme difficulty. After translation, a panel of five nursing experts assessed the validity of the translated questionnaire. To ensure a unified and culturally adapted version, differences in wording were discussed to optimize readability and reach a preliminary consensus. Each expert rated the “appropriateness” and “clarity” of the items using a 4-point Likert scale, where 4 = highly relevant (no revision required and should not be omitted), 3 = relevant (retain with minor revisions), 2 = relevant with major revision required, and 1 = not relevant (requires complete revision or deletion). Items with a content validity index (CVI) below 0.80 were deleted or revised based on expert consensus. The CVI for the questionnaire was calculated as the proportion of items rated 3 or 4 divided by the total number of items, with a CVI of ≥ 0.80 indicating acceptable content validity [23]. After confirming the Chinese translation, back-translation was performed. A bilingual translator, who was a native English speaker fluent in Chinese, translated the Chinese version back into English. A comparison of the original English WCPS and the back-translated version revealed no significant discrepancies, thus confirming the accuracy of the translated version.

For the pretesting phase, 10 hospitalized adult patients were randomly selected to review the WCPS-C for comprehensibility. The inclusion criteria were (1) age ≥ 18 years, (2) current hospitalization in a medical or surgical unit, (3) ability to verbally communicate in Mandarin Chinese, and (4) willingness to provide feedback on questionnaire clarity. Patients with documented cognitive impairment, severe illness limiting communication, or inability to complete the review were excluded. Further, the pretesting sample included both male and female patients across a range of adult ages and hospital units, thus providing contextual diversity for assessing item clarity. Participants reported that the questionnaire was clear and easy to understand. Comprehension was assessed using three methods: (1) a 3-point ordinal scale (1 = clear and easy to understand, 2 = somewhat difficult to understand, and 3 = difficult to understand); (2) probing questions; and (3) paraphrasing, in which the participants expressed their understanding of the items. Overall, comprehension ratings were favorable and no issues were reported. All participants considered the questionnaire to be clear and understandable. The study procedure is depicted in Fig. 1.

Study procedure

In the second phase (psychometric analysis), exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) were performed [24]. The following were the inclusion criteria for this phase: (1) male or female inpatients at the participating hospital, (2) willingness to complete the questionnaire, and (3) age ≥ 18 years. The exclusion criteria included (1) pregnancy or breastfeeding and (2) unwillingness to participate in the survey. With regard to sample size estimation, methodological guidelines recommend a minimum of 5–10 participants per item and at least 100 total participants for EFA to achieve stable factor solutions [25]. For CFA, a minimum sample size of 200 is generally recommended to support reliable parameter estimation and model fit evaluation [26]. Considering an anticipated nonresponse rate of 10%, 275–330 participants were targeted for data collection.

Instruments

Watson Caritas Patient Score (WCPS)

The WCPS is a concise and reliable measure of human care in clinical practice. The WCPS was developed based on the concepts of Carative factors and Caritas Processes® [9]. It is a five-item, patient-reported instrument designed to measure caring behaviors among health care providers. The WCPS consists of five items, with each item representing a core caring domain: (1) providing care with loving kindness; (2) meeting human needs with dignity; (3) fostering trusting relationships; (4) creating a caring-healing environment; and (5) honoring personal beliefs, faith, and hopes [9]. However, psychometric evidence supports a single underlying caring construct. Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = not at all to 7 = very much), with total scores ranging from 5 to 35. Higher scores indicate greater perceived quality of care. The original tool reported good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.90 [9]. One study found that the Korean version of the WCPS had good psychometric properties, including high internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.94, explained variance of 81.36%, and factor loadings ranging between 0.86 and 0.93 [15].

Taiwanese Inpatient Nursing Care Satisfaction Scale (TINCSS)

The TINCSS was developed by the Taiwan Nurses Association to measure patient satisfaction with nurses’ attitudes, competence, respect and caring, and environmental management during hospitalization. The instrument comprises 20 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale. It includes two subscales: professional competence (15 items; Cronbach’s α = 0.97) and environmental control (5 items; Cronbach’s α = 0.94). The overall internal consistency reliability of the scale has a Cronbach’s α value of 0.97 [27]. In this study, the TINCSS was used to examine the criterion-related validity of the WCPS.

Demographic variables

The demographic variables utilized in this study included participants’ age, sex, marital status, educational level, religion, occupational status, hospitalization experience, number of hospitalizations, hospitalization department, and length of hospitalization.

Data collection

Convenience sampling was utilized for this study. Eligible participants were recruited from five private hospitals in Taiwan. Recruitment was conducted by trained nurses or research assistants at each participating hospital. Prior to data collection, all recruiters received standardized instructions regarding eligibility screening, informed consent procedures, and participant communication to ensure consistency across sites. Participants or their family members were invited to participate via posters that displayed a QR code that linked to an online questionnaire hosted on Google Forms. The recruitment approach emphasized voluntary participation, anonymity, and the absence of any impact on clinical care. In addition, the online questionnaire included an electronic informed consent form, which participants were required to review and agree to before proceeding. Data were collected from March 1, 2025, to September 8, 2025.

During the data collection period, participants were allocated to the EFA and CFA samples using a sequential allocation strategy based on enrollment order. A priori target sample sizes were predefined as ≥ 100 participants for EFA and ≥ 200 participants for CFA [25, 26]. A contingency recruitment plan was implemented to ensure statistical adequacy. If the EFA sample had not reached 100 participants, recruitment would have continued prior to conducting factor extraction. Similarly, if the CFA sample had not reached 200 participants, additional recruitment would been undertaken before model testing to ensure model stability and adequate statistical power. To ensure statistical power and model stability, the research team monitored enrollment on a weekly basis.

In the study, the first 104 eligible participants were assigned to the EFA sample after exceeding the predefined minimum. Recruitment then continued, and the remaining 221 participants were assigned to the CFA sample. This approach ensured that EFA and CFA were conducted on independent samples, thereby reducing the risk of overfitting and strengthening construct validation. Due to efficient recruitment across the participating sites, the target sample sizes were reached within the original timeframe without the need for extensions.

Data analysis

All questionnaire responses were saved on Google Forms and downloaded to a Microsoft Excel 2016 database after data collection was completed. Data accuracy and completeness were verified prior to analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Assumption testing included normality, linearity, outliers, and multicollinearity. Descriptive statistics were utilized to present the participants’ sociodemographic characteristics and the distribution of scales in terms of value, percentage, mean, median, and standard deviation. Reliability analysis was performed to assess the internal consistency of the WCPS. Further, principal-axis factoring analysis was conducted with Promax rotation to determine validity based on initial eigenvalues (≥ 1) and factor loadings (> 0.40). The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) and Bartlett tests of sphericity were used as sampling adequacy indices [28]. Kruskal–Wallis H tests were conducted for ordinal or categorical variables in more than two groups. The Mann–Whitney U test was utilized for binary categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at < 0.05. Pearson’s correlation was employed to analyze the relationships among the factors of the WCPS.

The software package called AMOS v.18.0.0 (IBM Corporation, Wexford, PA, USA) was used to perform CFA. Different domains and items obtained from the previous EFA were included as elements. To assess the fit of the model, the following indices were selected: (1) the chi-square probability setting as appropriate for non-significant values (p > 0.05), (2) root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) < 0.08, and (3) comparative fit index (CFI) > 0.90 [29]. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were calculated using the instrument’s reliability index. Composite reliability was also computed. Cronbach’s alpha was interpreted in the following manner: < 0.5, unacceptable; ≥ 0.5 to < 0.6, poor; ≥ 0.6 to < 0.7, questionable; ≥ 0.7 to < 0.8, acceptable; ≥ 0.8 to < 0.9, good; and > 0.9, excellent [30, 31].

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Chang Gung Hospital (IRB No. 202401720B0). Participants were informed of the purpose and procedures of the study. Participation was voluntary, with the freedom to withdraw at any time without consequence. The participants were informed that their right to treatment would not influence their participation in the study. Confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained, ensuring that no individual could be identified in any publication or report.

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA)

CFA was conducted to evaluate the measurement model of the “Caring” construct, consisting of five observed indicators (Caring1 to Caring5). The model demonstrated an excellent fit to the data, with the following fit indices: χ² (5) = 2.498, p = 0.777, χ²/df = 0.500, GFI = 0.995, AGFI = 0.986, CFI = 1.000, and RMSEA = 0.000. These values indicated a well-fitting model, with no significant discrepancies between the hypothesized structure and the observed data (Fig. 2).

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) of WCPS-C

Further, all factor loadings of the observed variables in the latent construct “Caring” were statistically significant (p < 0.001), with standardized regression weights ranging from 0.813 (Caring5) to 0.936 (Caring2), suggesting strong convergent validity. Specifically, the following are the standardized estimates: Caring1 = 0.928, Caring2 = 0.936, Caring3 = 0.884, Caring4 = 0.866, and Caring5 = 0.813. The variance of the latent construct “Caring” was significant (Estimate = 0.366, SE = 0.041, CR = 9.023, p < 0.001), and all measurement errors (e1 to e5) showed significant variance (p < 0.001), thus supporting the reliability of the indicators. Overall, the CFA results confirmed the unidimensional structure of the “Caring” construct, with a good model fit and robust factor loadings across all indicators.

Discussion

Main findings

This study validated the Chinese version of the WCPS, confirming its psychometric soundness and cultural relevance. The findings extend previous research on caring measurements and contribute to the international literature on the operationalization of Watson’s Theory of Human Caring. The tool revealed good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.935) and a unidimensional factor structure that explained 82.3% of the variance. These results are consistent with the Korean validation of the WCPS, which reported similar reliability (α = 0.94) and unidimensionality. Furthermore, convergent validity was supported by significant correlations between the WCPS and patient satisfaction dimensions (r = 0.628–0.746, p < 0.001), thereby reinforcing the scale’s ability to capture holistic perceptions of caring. These findings provide evidence that the WCPS is a robust tool across diverse cultural settings, which is in line with its intended use as a concise and patient-centered measure of caring science. CFA results confirmed the one-factor model, with superior fit indices (χ²/df = 0.50, GFI = 0.995, AGFI = 0.986, RMSEA = 0.000) and strong loadings (0.94–1.12). Convergent validity with patient satisfaction further reinforced the tool’s clinical utility. These findings align with validations of the scale in other languages that have confirmed a one-factor model with high reliability and convergent validity [14–16].

Cross-cultural adaptation

The translation and cultural adaptation of the WCPS followed established guidelines, including forward–backward translation, expert panel review, and pretesting with hospitalized patients [22, 32, 33]. Attention was paid to linguistic clarity and cultural relevance in Taiwanese clinical nursing contexts. During expert review, minor wording adjustments were made to ensure that expressions related to caring, dignity, and nurse–patient interaction reflected the commonly used and culturally appropriate language in Taiwan.

The adaptation process emphasized a respectful tone consistent with Taiwanese cultural norms, in which caring is often expressed through politeness, attentiveness, and interpersonal harmony rather than overt emotional expression, which is in line with the relational and humanistic principles of caring science [9]. Concepts related to dignity were framed to reflect being treated with respect and consideration, both of which are central to patient expectations in Taiwanese health care settings. Similarly, wording related to nurse–patient interaction was reviewed to ensure it aligned with culturally appropriate communication styles characterized by respect, sensitivity, and relational balance, which is consistent with prior cross-cultural applications of the WCPS in Asian health care settings [15]. Further, pretesting with hospitalized patients confirmed that the WCPS-C items were understandable and culturally resonant. Participants reported that the wording accurately reflected their experiences of nursing care without ambiguity. These steps strengthened the content validity of the WCPS-C and reduced the risk of cultural misinterpretation.

Theoretical integration

The psychometric findings of this study provide empirical support for Watson’s Theory of Human Caring, particularly its emphasis on transpersonal relationships and holistic care [4]. The unidimensionality found in this study reflects the coherence of Watson’s Caritas Processes, which have been widely applied in clinical, educational, and organizational contexts [34]. The WCPS captures these constructs from the patient’s perspective, reinforcing both its theoretical foundation and philosophical underpinnings. The results affirm the transpersonal dimension of nurse–patient relationships, positioning caring not only as an observable behavior but also as a moral and relational commitment. Watson’s theory has been recognized as advancing ethical, compassionate, and human-centered nursing practices [35]. In practice, the WCPS measures care behaviors that align with the Caritas framework, providing a patient-centered tool for evaluating care. Studies further support the idea that patients’ perceptions of care behaviors are closely associated with trust and overall satisfaction [18, 20], thereby reinforcing the premise that caring is both measurable and philosophical. These findings position the WCPS as a valid assessment tool and a bridge between theory and practice. This instrument’s application in diverse cultural contexts strengthens the global relevance and significance of Watson’s caring sciences.

Implications for clinical practice

The validated WCPS-C is a culturally appropriate, theory-based instrument for evaluating patients’ perceptions of nursing care in Taiwan. The instrument’s ease of administration and strong psychometric properties make it suitable for routine use [15, 36]. As a patient-reported outcome measure, the WCPS-C can be administered in either paper-based or electronic formats to clinically stable patients who are able to independently complete the questionnaire, which is consistent with prior applications of the WCPS in hospital environments [9]. From a clinical practice perspective, the WCPS-C could serve as a reflective tool for nurses that enables them to examine how their caring behaviors are perceived by patients and to align daily practice with caring science. By incorporating patients’ perspectives, nurses can obtain meaningful feedback on interpersonal communication, respect for dignity, and the establishment of caring and healing relationships. For nurse managers, the WCPS-C results can provide actionable insights into unit-level caring practices, thereby enabling managers to identify strengths, identify areas for improvement, and design targeted interventions that encourage the delivery of caring-centered care. Previous validation studies have demonstrated the practical utility of the WCPS in diverse clinical contexts and supported its role as a feasible and meaningful indicator of caring behaviors from the patient’s viewpoint [15, 36].

In the context of quality improvement, the WCPS-C complements traditional patient satisfaction surveys by focusing on the caring dimension of nursing practice rather than service efficiency or environmental factors alone. Because the WCPS-C reflects a unidimensional caring construct, its scores should be interpreted as overall indicators of perceived caring quality rather than as discrete domain scores. This holistic interpretation aligns with the theoretical premise that caring is an integrated, relational process rather than a collection of isolated behaviors [9]. When utilized longitudinally, WCPS-C data may support continuous quality improvement initiatives by monitoring changes in caring perceptions following educational interventions, staffing adjustments, or organizational changes. Therefore, the WCPS-C offers a practical and theoretically grounded means of translating caring science into measurable clinical outcomes. Its integration into routine practice and quality improvement frameworks could strengthen the visibility of caring behaviors, support reflective and relational nursing practice, and contribute to improved patient experiences and satisfaction.

Implications for nursing leadership

The WCPS-C has important implications for nursing leadership. Prior research has revealed that care-based leadership can significantly enhance organizational commitment and job satisfaction among nurses [37]. By integrating the WCPS-C results, nurse-leaders can gain deeper insight into how caring behaviors affect patients and nurses. These empirical findings can inform strategic initiatives to enhance workforce well-being, professional identity, and workforce retention. In addition, the focus on care-based leadership is congruent with the existing literature on the subject, which encourages leaders to foster ethical and healing work environments in which compassion and relational integrity are valued as core organizational assets [37, 38]. This human-centered leadership approach is pertinent in highly technological and AI-based healthcare systems, where emotional connection and the creation of meaning are critical to sustaining high-quality, patient-centered care [39]. By embedding the WCPS-C into leadership development, organizations can bridge the gap between empirical measurement and ethical practice, thereby cultivating leaders who manifest caring consciousness across decision-making and organizational culture.

Implications for nursing education

In nursing education, the WCPS-C provides the opportunity to integrate the caring science theory into measurable outcomes among students with a Chinese background. Specifically, applying the WCPS-C to nursing curricula will enable the alignment of educational experiences with Watson’s 10 Caritas Processes, facilitating students’ recognition of relational caring behaviors. Literature suggests that caring science can serve as a discipline-specific organizing foundation of the core nursing curriculum and enable the fostering of presence and compassion [38, 40]. By utilizing the WCPS-C in undergraduate and continuing education programs, nurse educators can guide learners to reflect on their caring practices and develop care-centered values from the onset of their training. This strategy can strengthen professional identity, ethical awareness, and the capacity to provide holistic care.

Limitations

This study was conducted in five hospitals, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Additionally, although convergent validity was established, further testing with longitudinal outcomes, such as patient safety and nurse engagement, is warranted. Future research should explore WCPS across different healthcare contexts and populations, building on global initiatives that apply care science to reduce disparities. Longitudinal studies may clarify how patient-reported care relates to nurses’ well-being, resilience, and organizational outcomes.

Conclusion

This study aimed to develop and validate the WCPS-C. The translated instrument demonstrated strong reliability, excellent one-factor model fit, and evidence of convergent validity. However, because the present validation excluded pediatric, obstetric, and outpatient populations, its generalizability remains limited. Further testing is warranted to confirm whether the 7-point rating scheme of the WCPS-C is applicable to diverse patient groups in Taiwan. Within the current healthcare contexts, which are dominated by evidence-based practice and traditional performance evaluation frameworks, expressing and evaluating moments of care remain a challenge. The WCPS-C is a culturally adapted and psychometrically sound tool that effectively captures hospitalized patients’ perceptions of nursing care in Taiwan. This instrument addresses the gaps left by conventional satisfaction surveys in reflecting the holistic and humanistic dimensions of care, and provides an important basis for initiatives aimed at improving hospital quality.
