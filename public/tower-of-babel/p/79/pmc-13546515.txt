Bioinspired layer-by-layer nanoengineering for dental hard tissue regeneration and caries prevention
Authors: Yuyan Li, Jue Lan, Lingshan Ran, Yulu Xie, Jinmei Yu, Yunxin Yang, Xiaofeng Tan, Xiaodong Qu, Hongxia Wei, Jun Luo, Xiaoyu Huang, Rongmin Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDental caries, a chronic disease resulting from bacterial demineralization of dental hard tissues, poses a significant global health challenge. Remineralization therapy presents a promising strategy to arrest caries progression and restore tissue integrity. However, simultaneously achieving stable, efficient remineralization and effectively combating plaque biofilm formation remains a critical hurdle. Inspired by the hierarchical “brick-and-mortar” architecture of nacre and the adhesive functionality of mussel foot proteins (Mfps), a novel nanoarchitectonic system was developed via a layer-by-layer biomimetic assembly. This multifunctional system comprises amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) as a mineral ion reservoir, tannic acid (TA) as a bridging and crystallization-regulating layer mimicking the catechol chemistry of Mfps, and hydroxypropyltrimethyl ammonium chloride chitosan (HACC) as a functional antibacterial outer layer. This bioinspired design yields a stable platform capable of sustained release of calcium and phosphate ions for remineralization while providing long-lasting antibacterial activity against cariogenic biofilms. The system not only forms a durable protective barrier on the enamel surface but also facilitates effective biomimetic remineralization of early carious lesions. Introduction

Dental caries is a chronic, progressive disease affecting the hard tissues of teeth, driven primarily by bacterial activity, and significantly impacts both oral and overall health [1, 2]. The formation of carious lesions involves demineralization of inorganic substances and the breakdown of organic matter. Cariogenic bacteria, such as Streptococcus mutans (S. mutans), produce acid through fermentation, lowering the local pH below a critical threshold. This leads to the dissolution of hydroxyapatite (HAp), the primary component of tooth structure, and the release of calcium and phosphate ions—a process known as demineralization [3, 4]. Continuous demineralization eventually results in tooth structure loss, causing sensitivity, and in severe cases, pulpitis or periapical periodontitis. However, caries formation is not solely a process of demineralization but an alternating cycle of demineralization and remineralization [5]. In the absence of key factors like local plaque microorganisms, caries progression halts, and under favorable conditions, demineralized tissue can undergo limited repair [6]. Yet, without external intervention, this self-repair is minimal. Thus, it is crucial to both combat cariogenic bacteria and repair defects in dental hard tissues.

In recent years, biomimetic remineralization, which uses nanotechnology and tissue engineering to mimic natural mineralization processes, has emerged as a promising focus in oral treatment research for halting caries and repairing damaged dental tissues. Biomimetic mineralization materials can be classified into two categories based on their composition. The first category relies on calcium and phosphate ions from saliva for mineralization, using high-affinity surfaces to attract these ions and form a mineralized layer. Examples include catechol compounds [7, 8], biomimetic polypeptide P11−4 [9, 10], and polyamidoamine dendrimers (PAMAM) [11]. However, this approach depends on external ions, which limits its effectiveness and fails to achieve optimal results. To overcome this limitation, researchers have developed mineralization systems that carry their own mineral ions, such as agarose hydrogel biomimetic mineralization models [12] and the use of small-molecule triethylamine to stabilize calcium phosphate ion clusters [13].

Despite advancements, pure remineralization therapies face challenges. The extended treatment duration allows plaque biofilm or newly colonized bacteria to produce acid, disrupting the remineralization process and potentially leading to further demineralization [14]. Effectively inhibiting bacteria and biofilms involved in dental caries is essential for promoting sustained remineralization. In response, researchers have incorporated antibacterial agents, such as quaternary ammonium monomers and amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP), into restorative resins and adhesives [15, 16], or developed nanomaterials combining ACP with antibacterial zwitterionic polycarboxybetaine acrylamide (PCBAA) [17]. Although existing studies have considered the impact of microbial environments on remineralization, they still fall short in achieving optimal remineralization, particularly regarding the depth of dentin tubule occlusion. Given that teeth experience an average wear of approximately 15 μm per year [18], insufficient occlusion depth increases the risk of dentin tubule exposure. Therefore, an ideal treatment should not only promote antibacterial remineralization but also achieve satisfactory dentin tubule occlusion depth and mineral layer thickness, ensuring long-term effectiveness and reducing the risk of dentin exposure.

Inspired by the adhesive chemistry of mussel foot proteins (Mfps) and the “brick-and-mortar” composite architecture of nacre, this study proposes a bioinspired nanoarchitectonics strategy based on hierarchical assembly. The system mimics the biomineralization process by constructing a triple-layered core–shell nanostructure, with amorphous calcium phosphate (ACP) as the mineralizing core (“brick”), tannic acid (TA) as the adhesive and stabilizing interlayer (“mortar” and mussel‑inspired adhesive), and hydroxypropyltrimethyl ammonium chloride chitosan (HACC) as the outermost functional coating (Fig. 1). In this design, the ACP core acts as a biomimetic calcium‑phosphate reservoir, providing sustained nucleation sites for remineralization [19–22]. The TA interlayer not only stabilizes ACP by chelating calcium ions and regulating crystallization kinetics but also replicates the interfacial adhesion of mussel proteins through its catechol groups [23, 24], which are the core functional structure of DOPA (3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine) [25], the key adhesive component in Mfps. The outermost HACC is selected as the antibacterial shell because its cationic quaternary ammonium groups enable surface‑anchored contact killing without uncontrolled release. Additionally, HACC adheres to the TA interlayer through electrostatic and hydrogen‑bonding interactions, while its hydrophilic backbone does not chelate Ca²⁺, thus preserving the sustained Ca²⁺/PO₄³⁻ release from the ACP core [26–30]. Experimental results demonstrate that this biomimetic assembly system not only meets the core requirements of antibacterial activity and remineralization for caries treatment but also exhibits effective caries-preventive properties and excellent biocompatibility. This biomimetic self-assembled nanoarchitectonics shows promising potential for clinical translation, offering a novel and nature-inspired strategy for the prevention and treatment of dental caries.

Experimental

Materials

Chitosan quaternary ammonium salt was sourced from Macklin (China), and tannic acid from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Calcein-AM came from Aladdin (China), while ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), calcium chloride dihydrate (CaCl₂·2 H₂O), disodium hydrogen phosphate (Na₂HPO₄), and other salts were obtained from Chron Chemicals (China). Fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin-streptomycin (P/S), and Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) were supplied by Gibco (USA). The cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) was purchased from MedChemExpress (USA), and fluorescein diacetate/propidium iodide (FDA/PI) from Solarbio (China). Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH = 7.4, without calcium and magnesium), immunostaining permeabilization buffer with Triton X-100, FITC-Phalloidin, and 2-(4-Amidinophenyl)−6-indolecarbamidine dihydrochloride (DAPI) were procured from Biosharp (China).

Assembly and fluorescence labeling of nano-assemblies

The reaction was conducted at 4 ℃. First, a 0.004 M Na₂HPO₄ solution (30 mL) was quickly mixed with a 0.006 M CaCl₂ solution (30 mL) and stirred for 2 min to form an ACP suspension. Next, 120 µL of TA solution (8 mg/mL, pH = 8) was added to the ACP suspension and stirred for 5 min to produce an ACP@TA suspension. Subsequently, 800 µL of HACC solution (8 mg/mL) was added to the ACP@TA suspension every 5 min, for a total of 5 additions. The naming convention and assembly ratios of these nano-assemblies are detailed in Table S1.

To assess nano-assembly infiltration into dentin tubules and the mineralization effect, fluorescence labeling was applied. For this, 60 µL of Calcein-AM (1 mg/mL) was added to 60 mL of the synthesized ACP suspension and stirred for 30 min. The suspension was then dialyzed against Milli-Q water within a 2 kDa dialysis bag (Biosharp, USA) for 4 h to remove unbound compounds. The labeled ACP@TH nano-assemblies were then assembled following the previously described steps.

Characterization of nano-assemblies

The surface zeta potentials of nano-assemblies were measured (Malvern, Z90, UK) to preliminarily verify the sequential assembly of TA and HACC on ACP surfaces.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR, Nicolet 6700, Thermo Scientific, USA) was conducted to analyze the chemical composition and functional groups, with spectra recorded in the wavenumber range of 4000 to 500 cm⁻¹. For sample preparation, the nano-assembly solution was first centrifuged and freeze-dried, then the freeze-dried material was ground together with potassium bromide (KBr) powder to form tablets for testing.

X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) was used to examine the surface chemical composition and elemental states of the nano-assembly materials. The measurements were performed using an XPS instrument (K-α, Thermo Scientific, USA) equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (1486.6 eV). Spectra were acquired under ultra-high vacuum conditions, and the binding energies were calibrated using the C1s peak at 284.8 eV as the reference. Surface elemental composition and chemical bonding states were analyzed, with particular focus on calcium, carbon, and nitrogen.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed using a thermogravimetric analyzer (NETZSCH STA 449 F3, Germany) to assess thermal stability, with samples heated from room temperature to 800 °C at a rate of 20 °C/min in a nitrogen atmosphere. The weight loss as a function of temperature was recorded, and the data were used to determine the decomposition temperatures and the relative amount of organic and inorganic components in the nano-assembly.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) was employed to identify the crystalline phases of the nano-assemblies and assess their degree of crystallinity. The XRD patterns were recorded using an X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku Ultima IV, Tokyo, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at 40 kV and 30 mA, scanning a 2θ range of 10° to 60° with a step size of 0.02°.

The nano-assemblies were stored in sealed glass vials at 4 °C. And the particle morphology and structure of these nano-assemblies were characterized using Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM, JEOL, JEM-2100 F, Japan) operated at an acceleration voltage of 110 kV at four time points: immediately after synthesis, and on days 1, 3, and 5. And the elemental compositions of the nano-system were detected via Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS, Aztec XMax80, Oxford, UK). For sample preparation, the nano-assembly solutions were subjected to ultrasonic treatment to achieve a uniform dispersion. A volume of 5 µL of the treated solution was then placed onto a carbon-coated copper grid for 30 s. Then, excess liquid was carefully absorbed using filter paper, and the grid was left to air dry prior to analysis. Selected area electron diffraction (SAED) was performed in conjunction with TEM to investigate the morphological changes and crystallization behavior of each nano-assembly.

Subsequently, the in vitro ion release behavior of ACP, ACP@TA, and ACP@TH was investigated. Five milliliters of each sample (n = 3) were placed in dialysis bags (MW = 3500 Da) and sealed. The dialysis bags were then immersed in centrifuge tubes containing 20 mL of Milli-Q water. At predetermined time points (days 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, 12, 14, and 20), 500 µL of the solution was collected, and 500 µL of fresh Milli-Q water was added. The calcium and phosphate release profiles were analyzed and plotted using calcium and phosphate assay kits according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

In vitro antibacterial and anti-biofilm analyses

The typical cariogenic bacterium S. mutans (UA159) [31, 32] was used for antibacterial and anti-biofilm characterization. The bacteria were revived and incubated in brain heart infusion (BHI) medium at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. To minimize variations in dental radiographs caused by differences in thickness and shape, HAp tablets were used to simulate tooth surfaces. These tablets were placed in 24-well plates, and 500 µL of bacterial suspensions (10⁶ CFU/mL) were added to each well, followed by an equal volume of sample solution (Milli-Q water, ACP, ACP@TA, ACP@TH, n = 3). After thorough mixing, the plates were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 4 h. The supernatants from each group were then diluted and plated for bacterial enumeration. The antibacterial performance (R) of the different samples was calculated using the formula:

R% = (Nc – N)/Nc ×100%.

where Nc represents the colony count of the control group, and N represents the bacterial count of the experimental group.

Afterward, the HAp tablets were gently rinsed with PBS to remove non-adherent bacteria. The tablets were then fixed overnight in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C, followed by gradient dehydration in ethanol solutions (100%, 90%, 75%, 50%, and 25% ethanol, each for 15 min). Once dehydrated, the samples were dried, sputter-coated with gold, and analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Apreo S, Thermo Scientific, USA) to observe morphological changes in S. mutans.

For the anti-biofilm experiment, 1% sucrose was added to the BHI medium to promote biofilm formation. Sterile HAp slices were placed in 24-well plates, and 500 µL of bacterial suspension (10⁶ CFU/mL) was added to each well. After incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24 h, the HAp slices were rinsed with PBS three times and transferred to fresh 24-well plates. Next, 500 µL of BHI medium with 1% sucrose and 500 µL of sample solution (Milli-Q water, ACP, ACP@TA, ACP@TH, n = 3) were added to each well for an additional 4 h. After rinsing with PBS three times, the HAp slices were stained using a LIVE/DEAD BacLight Viability Kit and imaged with a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, A1R+, Nikon, Japan) to visualize the biofilm.

Preparation of human dental slices

Healthy premolars and third molars, free of caries, cracks, or defects, were selected from the Hospital of Stomatology, Guangxi Medical University. Dentin and enamel slices, each measuring 4 × 3 × 1 mm³, were prepared using a cutting machine (DTQ-5, Weiyi, Laizhou, China) and a grinding machine (M-1, Weiyi, Laizhou, China), then polished with 600- to 5000-grit SiC papers under running water. Demineralization models were created by etching dentin slices with 17% EDTA (pH = 7) for 8 min [33, 34] and enamel slices with 37% phosphoric acid for 30 s [17]. The etched slices were sonicated in Milli-Q water for 10 min and stored in 0.05 wt% thymol solution at 4 °C. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Guangxi Medical University (No. KY0127).

Mineralization treatment and characterization of human dental slices

The etched enamel and dentin slices were immersed in 5 mL of nano-assembly suspension (ACP, ACP@TA, or ACP@TH), fluoride solution (NaF, 2000 ppm, a clinically established anticaries concentration [17, 35, 36]), or Milli-Q water for 5 min. Following this, the slices were incubated at 37 °C in 10 mL of 1.5 × simulated body fluid [37] (SBF, pH = 7.4; containing 137.5 mM NaCl, 4.2 mM NaHCO₃, 3 mM KCl, 1.5 mM K₂HPO₄·3 H₂O, 1.5 mM MgCl₂·6 H₂O, 3.75 mM CaCl₂, and 0.5 mM Na₂SO₄) for 7 d. The SBF was refreshed every 24 h. After 7 d of mineralization, the slices were sonicated in Milli-Q water for 30 s to remove deposited minerals and air-dried prior to characterization. In order to test the permeability of dentin slices treated with ACP@TH nano-assemblies, another experiment was conducted. Dentin slices were treated with calcein-AM-labeled ACP@TH nano-assemblies for 5 min (n = 3), followed by analysis using CLSM to assess the depth of nano-assembly penetration into the dentin tubules.

Upon completion of the mineralization experiments, Vickers hardness testing (TMVS-1, Times High Tech, China) was first conducted on the enamel and dentin slices. Hardness was measured at five random positions on each dental slices [38] with a 25-gf load applied for 10 s (n = 3). Following the hardness testing, XRD testing and SEM observation were promptly conducted on the same dental slices. XRD (Ultima IV, Japan) analysis was used to determine the crystallographic phases of the minerals on the surface of the dental slices, while SEM characterized the mineralization on the surfaces of the enamel and dentin slices as well as inside the dentin tubules. And EDS was employed to determine the elemental composition and distribution of the formed minerals.

Additionally, to better observe the changes in the ACP@TH group tooth slices before and after mineralization, half of the enamel/dentin slice was immersed in acid‑resistant nail varnish to achieve uniform coverage of one half of the sample before the mineralization experiment. After air‑drying, the mineralization experiment was performed according to the standard procedure. Upon completion of mineralization, the nail varnish was completely removed with acetone, and the specimens were subjected to SEM observation.

Airtightness test

To evaluate the sealing and mineralizing effects of nano-assemblies on dentin tubules, an airtightness test was conducted [34] (Control, ACP, ACP@TA, ACP@TH, and NaF groups; n = 3). Dentin slices were placed between O-shaped rubber rings in a sealed chamber, and the critical air pressure, which indicates the sealing efficiency, was measured as the pressure at which a water droplet in the pipeline began to move.

To simulate mechanical brushing and acid challenges, the mineralized dentin slices underwent continuous brushing for 20 min, followed by immersion in 2% EDTA for 1 min [39]. After thorough rinsing with Milli-Q water and air-drying, the airtightness was reassessed. Surface mineral changes were also analyzed using SEM.

Cell compatibility of nano-assembly

Human dental pulp cells (HDPCs) were serviced for the evaluation of cell compatibility, and DMEM medium (consisting of 10% FBS, 1% P/S, and 89% DMEM) was used for the recovery and culture of HDPCs (No. KY0127). The cytotoxicity of nano-assemblies was assessed using a CCK-8 kit. The experiment was conducted with four groups (n = 6): Control, ACP, ACP@TA, and ACP@TH. HDPCs were seeded in a 48-well plate at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well, and incubated for 24 h. After removing the original DMEM medium, 290 µL of fresh DMEM medium and 10 µL of sample solution were added to each well. Following another 24 h of incubation, DMEM medium containing 10% CCK-8 Kit was replaced to each well and cultured for 2 h. Subsequently, 100 µL of supernatant from each well was transferred to a 96-well plate and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a spectrophotometer (KHB ST-360, Kehua Bio-Engineering, China).

Additionally, FDA/PI or TITC-phalloidin/DAPI could be used to stain HDPCs after 24-h treatment with sample solution to evaluate the cell viability and morphological characteristics. And the staining cells were imaged by a fluorescence microscopy (Olympus IX71, Japan).

Evaluation of remineralization potential using a semi-in vivo rat model

All experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guangxi Medical University (No. 202405025).

Eight -week-old male SPF-grade SD rats weighing 220–270 g were selected, with 5 rats in each group. Pre-deminearlized human enamel/dentin slices were prepared, ground into an oval shape, and drilled with two holes to facilitate subsequent fixation in the rat oral cavity. The enamel/dentin slices were treated with freshly prepared ACP@TH, NaF, or Milli-Q water for 5 min. Then, the slices were fixed onto the rat palate using a 0.5 mm diameter stainless steel ligature wire passed through the two holes. One end of the wire was looped around the interdental space between the first and second molars, while the other end was threaded through the anterior teeth. Postoperatively, the rats were fed a soft diet to avoid interference with the slices. The stability of the slices was checked daily, and any dislodged samples were immediately repositioned. After 7 d, the slices were collected for SEM, EDS, and Vickers hardness testing.

Construction of standardized rat dental caries model

Twenty 3-week-old male SPF-grade SD rats (weighing 80 ± 10 g) were randomly assigned into four groups of five rats each (No. 202405025): Control group (blank control), H₂O group (negative control), NaF group (positive control), and ACP@TH group (experimental group). Prior to the experiment, all rats were administered antibiotic water containing penicillin potassium (0.2 g/L) and streptomycin sulfate (1.5 g/L) for 3 d to suppress the indigenous oral flora. Thereafter, except for the Control group, all rats were inoculated daily by evenly swabbing the oral cavity with a sterile cotton swab soaked in a S. mutans suspension (100 µL, 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) for 3 consecutive days. Following each inoculation, food and water were withheld for 2 h. Successful colonization of S. mutans was verified by culturing on MSB agar plates. Throughout the experiment, except for the Control group which received standard chow, all other groups were fed a cariogenic Diet 2000 and 5% sucrose water, while other housing conditions remained identical.

After bacterial inoculation, rats were anesthetized using isoflurane inhalation, and 100 µL of freshly prepared ACP@TH, NaF, or H₂O was uniformly applied to the occlusal surfaces of all molars using a sterile applicator stick for 1 min. Food and water were subsequently withheld for 10 min to prolong the contact time of the agents. This intervention was performed once daily for a total duration of 4 weeks.

At the end of the experimental period, quantitative assessment of caries lesions in rat molars was performed using a modified Keyes staining-scoring system. The collected rat jaw specimens from each group were immersed in 0.4% ammonium purpurate staining solution and stained at a constant temperature of 37℃ in the dark for 12 h. Subsequently, the specimens were rinsed with Milli-Q water until the eluate became colorless, followed by drying in a light-protected drying oven for 24 h. The rat jaws were fixed using a customized three-dimensional positioning base to ensure that the mesiodistal orientation of the molars was perpendicular to the cutting plane. Sagittal sections of the molars with uniform thickness were precisely obtained using a hard tissue cutting system (300cp, Exakt, Japan). The caries severity in the molars of each group was then evaluated under a stereomicroscope. According to Larson’s modified Keyes scoring criteria, carious lesions appeared as characteristic purplish-red areas and were classified into four levels based on the depth of involvement: E (Limited to the enamel layer), Ds (Extending to the enamel-dentin junction and the outer one-fourth of the dentin), Dm (Involving the middle one-fourth to three-fourths of the dentin), and Dx (Extending through the entire dentin layer). The caries evaluation was conducted in a double-blind manner by three uniformly trained evaluators who had passed a standardized consistency test (Kappa > 0.85). The final score for each specimen was determined by averaging the scores from the three evaluators.

In parallel, high-resolution Micro-CT scanning was performed on the molar samples from each group of rats, and three-dimensional reconstruction of the scanned images was carried out using Mimics Medical 21.0 software. Meanwhile, to accurately evaluate the progression of caries lesions in each group, two-dimensional sagittal sectional images were obtained along the long axis of the molars to observe the locations of the caries lesions.

Finally, biocompatibility testing was conducted. One milliliter of blood was collected from each rat via the abdominal aorta for a complete blood count analysis, including white blood cell count (WBC), red blood cell count (RBC), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), platelet count (PLT), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), hemoglobin concentration (HGB), hematocrit (HCT), and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC). Tissues from the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys, as well as oral tissues from the cheek, tongue, and palate were collected for subsequent hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to evaluate the biocompatibility of the ACP@TH nano-assemblies.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 23 (IBM Corporation, USA) and Origin 2018 (OriginLab, USA). Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Group comparisons were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with a p-value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Successful construction and structural validation of the bioinspired hierarchical assembly

Inspired by the adhesive chemistry of mussel proteins and the “brick-and-mortar” structure of nacre, a triple-layered core–shell nanostructure (denoted as ACP@TH) was designed and constructed. This assembly follows a sequential process: the ACP core serves as the biomineralization “brick”; the TA coating acts as both a stabilizer (the “mortar”) and a bioadhesive via its catechol groups [40, 41]; and an outermost HACC layer forms a positively charged [42], functional shell (Fig. 1).

The successful stepwise assembly was first and most directly evidenced by a systematic reversal of surface charge. As shown in Fig. 2a, the zeta potential shifted predictably from mildly positive (+1.33 mV) for bare ACP cores to strongly negative (−17.1 mV) after TA coating, and finally to strongly positive (+30.2 mV) following HACC encapsulation. This sequence provides unambiguous, quantitative proof of the sequential electrostatic-driven deposition of each layer, confirming the intended core-shell architecture.

Peak a: 1458 cm− 1, Peak b: 1351 cm− 1, Peak c: 1498 cm− 1. (c) TGA curves of ACP, ACP@TA, and ACP@TH powders under heating. (d) TEM images of ACP, ACP@TA, and ACP@TH (insets: SAED patterns). (e) TEM images of ACP, ACP@TA, and ACP@TH after 1 d of storage (insets: SAED patterns)

Chemical characterization further confirmed the integration of each functional component. FTIR spectra (Fig. 2b) revealed the sequential appearance of signature peaks: vibrations corresponding to the phenyl ring of TA (C-O and C-H) emerged [43] after the intermediate coating step, with characteristic peaks at 1458 cm⁻¹ (Peak a) and 1351 cm⁻¹ (Peak b). While the final HACC coating introduced a characteristic amine group peak at 1498 cm⁻¹ (Peak c) [44]. In XPS analysis (Figure S1), a significant enhancement of C-O (286.3 eV) and C = O (288.6 eV) signals in the C 1 s spectrum of ACP@TA confirmed the TA layer [45]. Crucially, the subsequent appearance of a distinct N 1 s signal for ACP@TH [46], with contributions from -N⁺ (402.6 eV) and -NH₂ (399.5 eV) species, provided direct evidence for the successful integration of the quaternary ammonium-functionalized HACC outer shell. TGA data (Fig. 2c) complemented these findings by showing progressively greater organic content from ACP to ACP@TA to ACP@TH, which correspondingly led to increased weight loss at higher temperatures and physically validated the incremental build-up of organic layers.

The assembled nanostructure exhibited the desired spherical morphology and a critically important amorphous state. TEM imaging (Fig. 2d) showed that all assemblies were spherical with a uniform diameter of approximately 60 nm, indicating that the coating processes did not induce aggregation. This was corroborated by the stable colloidal nature of the ACP@TH suspension, as demonstrated by a clear Tyndall effect (Figure S2). Both SAED patterns (insets in Fig. 2d) and XRD (Figure S3) consistently confirmed the amorphous nature of the initial assemblies, showing only diffuse halos and a broad hump, with no sharp diffraction peaks corresponding to crystalline hydroxyapatite (HAp) or other CaP crystalline phases [47, 48]. Furthermore, EDS analysis of ACP@TH reveals that the spatial distribution of N corresponds well with both the particle morphology and the distributions of Ca, P, and O, confirming that the ACP core is continuously coated by HACC (Figure S4).

Most importantly, the hierarchical design conferred exceptional structural stability to the ACP core. Stability monitoring via TEM over time (Figs. 2e, S5) revealed that bare ACP and the ACP@TA intermediate began transforming into needle-like HAp crystals [49, 50] within 1 d. In stark contrast, the fully assembled ACP@TH maintained its spherical, amorphous morphology for up to 5 d. This remarkable stability underscores the synergistic role of the TA and HACC layers: TA chelates calcium ions to kinetically retard crystallization, while the HACC outer shell likely provides an additional physicochemical barrier, collectively preserving the metastable ACP “reservoir”. This can also be observed from the calcium and phosphate release curves (Figure S6): ACP@TH is capable of self‑supply, continuously releasing mineral ions, and exhibits a higher release amount than ACP and ACP@TA.

In conclusion, through a combination of zeta potential, spectroscopic, thermal, and microscopic analyses, the successful fabrication of the proposed bioinspired ACP@TA@HACC nanoarchitecture was unequivocally validated. This hierarchical assembly not only replicates the conceptual “brick-and-mortar-adhesive” design but also translates it into a stable, amorphous nanoplatform with built-in functional sequences for subsequent antibacterial and remineralizing actions.

In vitro antibacterial and anti-biofilm properties of ACP@TH nano-assembly

The hierarchical design of the ACP@TH nanoarchitecture directly translates into its biological function, particularly in combating cariogenic biofilms, a key etiological factor in dental caries. The outermost HACC shell plays a pivotal role as the first line of defense. Its strong positive charge enables electrostatic attraction to negatively charged bacterial membranes, leading to membrane disruption and cell death [28]. To evaluate the antibacterial performance of the nano-assembly, S. mutans, a common oral cariogenic bacterium, was chosen as the target for antibacterial and anti-biofilm assays (Fig. 3a). Quantitative plate counting revealed that ACP@TH achieved a bacterial inhibition rate of 97.28%, demonstrating potent bactericidal activity (Figs. 3b, c), significantly exceeding the bactericidal rates of the ACP group (30.6%) and the ACP@TA group (51.6%) with statistically significant differences (p < 0.001). SEM observations further corroborated this effect, showing clear morphological damage, including shrinkage and deformation of bacterial cells treated with ACP@TH, unlike the intact cells in the control and ACP groups (Fig. 3d).

Evaluation of antibacterial and anti-biofilm efficacy of ACP@TH nano-assembly. (a) Schematic diagram of antibacterial and anti-biofilm experiments. (b) Antibacterial rates of each group after co-culturing with S. mutans (***p < 0.001). (c) Exemplary visualization of colony-forming units (CFUs) pertaining to S. mutans. (d) SEM images characterized the bacterial morphology during the co-culturing of bacteria. (e) CLSM imagery and (f) stereoscopic 3D reconstructions, offering distinct perspectives on the prefabricated biofilm architecture of S. mutans subjected to varied interventions

Beyond targeting planktonic bacteria, the true clinical challenge lies in eradicating structured biofilms that persistently colonize demineralized tooth surfaces [51]. The anti-biofilm efficacy was therefore critically evaluated using a validated S. mutans biofilm model and CLSM. Specifically, biofilms were allowed to form for 24 h, followed by a 4‑h treatment period with the nano-assemblies. The results vividly illustrated a performance gradient that correlates directly with the nanoarchitecture’s complexity (Figs. 3e, f). While control and ACP groups were densely covered with viable (green) bacteria, the ACP@TA group showed a mixed population of live and dead cells (yellow), suggesting a moderate effect likely attributable to TA’s inherent mild antimicrobial properties [52]. Most significantly, the ACP@TH group displayed predominantly red fluorescence, indicating extensive bacterial death within the biofilm. This superior anti-biofilm performance underscores a synergistic outcome of the layered design: the cationic HACC shell actively attacks and permeabilizes the biofilm matrix and bacterial membranes, while the underlying TA layer may enhance local retention and interaction via its adhesive properties, ensuring sustained antibacterial action at the interface.

In summary, the antibacterial results validate the functional success of the outermost HACC layer within the biomimetic assembly. More importantly, they demonstrate that the integrated nanoarchitecture achieves exceptional biofilm disruption, which is a critical prerequisite for creating a clean substrate conducive to the subsequent remineralization process facilitated by the ACP core.

In vitro enamel remineralization induced by ACP@TH nano-assembly

Enamel, the highly mineralized tissue covering the tooth crown, serves as the first line of defense against external factors and is the earliest to be affected by dental caries. The phosphoric acid-etched enamel surface exhibited a distinctive “fish-scale” morphology [53] with exposed enamel prism structures (Figure S7), creating a model for evaluating remineralization efficacy.

After a 7-day remineralization cycle, SEM analysis revealed a clear performance gradient among groups (Fig. 4a and b). While only a thin mineral layer formed in the control group, treatments with ACP, ACP@TA, and the positive control NaF effectively filled the gaps between enamel prisms. Notably, the ACP@TH group demonstrated the most obvious and organized mineral deposition, nearly completely restoring the surface contour (Fig. 4b and c). And the Ca/P ratio of the newly formed minerals was 1.74 (Figure S8), closely matching the ideal HAp standard value of 1.67 [54]. This superior outcome is attributed not merely to the presence of the ACP core [55] but to the integrated function of the nano-assembly. The outermost cationic HACC shell facilitates initial electrostatic attraction to the negatively charged enamel surface [56]. More importantly, the intermediate TA layer, through its catechol groups [57], provides strong, versatile adhesion that enhances the retention and localized concentration of the nano-assemblies at the demineralized site, enabling sustained mineral delivery.

Assessment of ACP@TH nano-assembly’s effect on enamel mineralization. (a) Low-magnification and (b) high-magnification SEM images of the mineralized enamel in each group. (c) SEM visuals of a partially mineralized enamel slice induced by ACP@TH. (d) XRD patterns of enamel surface after mineralization period. (e) Vickers hardness of enamel after 7-day incubation (*p < 0.05, ***p < 0.001)

In the XRD spectra, characteristic peaks of HAp can be identified around 2θ values of approximately 25.9°, 31.8°, 32.9°, and 34.1°, which correspond to the (002), (211), (112), and (300) crystallographic planes of HAp [49, 50]. These peaks are prominent in the samples treated with ACP, ACP@TA, ACP@TH and NaF (Fig. 4d), indicating that the primary component of the new mineral layers in these groups was HAp, which was similar to the inorganic material found in healthy enamel.

The ultimate validation of effective remineralization is the restoration of mechanical integrity. Nanoindentation tests confirmed that the improved mineralization directly translated into enhanced enamel hardness (Fig. 4e). The ACP@TH treatment significantly increased the surface hardness of acid-etched enamel from 0.69 ± 0.033 GPa to 2.14 ± 0.037 GPa, a recovery performance that far surpassed all other groups, including the NaF positive control (0.92 ± 0.050 GPa). This improvement is likely due to ACP@TH’s ability to induce more organized enamel regrowth, creating a stronger protective enamel layer [58, 59].

In vitro dentin occlusion accelerated by ACP@TH nano-assembly

The progression of caries into dentin poses a greater challenge due to its permeable tubular structure (Figure S9), which accelerates lesion spread. As the protective layer over the pulp [60], demineralized dentin leads to sensitivity and pulpitis. Therefore, occluding these tubules through remineralization is essential to reduce external stimulus exposure and alleviate hypersensitivity [61].

Given that the diameter of dentin tubules is approximately 3 μm [62], ACP@TH nano-assembly, with a size of around 60 nm, can easily penetrate into the dentin tubules. Additionally, the strong positive surface charge not only provides an initial electrostatic attraction to the tubule walls [63], but also exerts electrostatic repulsion among the unabsorbed nanoparticles, preventing orifice blockage and facilitating deeper penetration into the dentinal tubules. CLSM confirmed that within 5 min, ACP@TH uniformly coated the dentin surface and infiltrated tubules to a depth of approximately 20 μm (Fig. 5a), demonstrating rapid delivery to the demineralized sites.

Mineralization frontiers and mature minerals formed on the dentin surface. (a) CLSM images showing the cross-sectional and surface views of dentin immersed in ACP@TH for 5 min. (b) SEM images of remineralized dentin surfaces before and after mechanical friction and EDTA-erosion

After a 7-day remineralization cycle, SEM surface analysis revealed a distinct performance hierarchy (Figs. 5b, S10). While ACP and ACP@TA groups showed mineral deposits but incomplete tubule occlusion, the ACP@TH group achieved complete surface sealing, comparable to the NaF positive control. EDS analysis confirmed that the Ca/P ratio of the newly formed surface mineral in the ACP@TH-treated dentin slices closely matched that of stoichiometric HAp (Figure S11), and XRD patterns verified its crystalline phase as HAp (Fig. 6e).

Thorough assessment of the mineralization capability in dentin. (a) Cross-sectional SEM views of dentin after 7-day incubation. (b) Large-scale SEM image of ACP@TH-treated dentin; dense minerals occluded the dentin tubules (highlighted in yellow) (c) Schematic of airtightness testing setup. (d) Airtightness outcomes of dentin samples before and after friction & erosion (n.s.>0.05, ***p < 0.001). (e) XRD patterns of dentin after 7-day incubation. (f) Vickers hardness of dentin after 7-day incubation (*p < 0.05, ***p < 0.001)

However, surface-level mineralization alone is insufficient to alleviate tooth sensitivity effectively, as daily activities such as eating, brushing, or exposure to external acids [64, 65] can easily damage the superficial mineral layer, diminishing the treatment’s effectiveness. The stability of the mineralized layer was evaluated by subjecting the mineralized dentin slices to mechanical friction and EDTA erosion. Post-challenge SEM showed that only the ACP@TH group maintained intact tubule sealing, while surface minerals in other groups were removed (Fig. 5b). Crucially, cross-sectional SEM of fractured dentin revealed that only the ACP@TH group formed dense, continuous mineral “plugs” within the tubules, achieving an exceptional occlusion depth of ~70 μm (Fig. 6a and b, S12). The Ca/P ratio of this intratubular mineral also corresponded to HAp (Figure S13). This deep intratubular mineralization, which far exceeds the initial 20 μm penetration depth, suggests a sustained mineral delivery and deposition process driven by the stable retention of the nano-assemblies within the tubules, likely mediated by the adhesive TA interlayer.

In addition to direct SEM observations, airtightness tests were conducted (Fig. 6c) to evaluate the sealing efficiency of dentinal tubules on a macroscopic level. Following dual challenges of simulated toothbrushing and acid etching, only the ACP@TH group retained its original high critical air pressure (~132 kPa). This demonstrates a durable barrier against external stimuli, which is a key requirement for treating dentin hypersensitivity.

The functional repair was further evidenced by the restoration of mechanical integrity. Nanoindentation tests showed that demineralized dentin hardness plummeted to 0.14 ± 0.004 GPa. While all treatment groups improved hardness, the ACP@TH group achieved the most significant recovery to 0.59 ± 0.030 GPa, closely approximating healthy dentin (0.64 ± 0.060 GPa) (Fig. 6f). This indicates that the deep, intratubular mineralization contributed substantially to restoring the bulk mechanical function of dentin.

Cell compatibility of ACP@TH nano-assembly

A comprehensive cell biocompatibility evaluation, encompassing live/dead staining, cell morphology observation, and CCK-8 viability assays [23], confirmed that the ACP@TH nano-assembly is non-cytotoxic and supports normal cell viability.

The excellent biocompatibility was first visualized through live/dead staining, which showed a vast majority of viable cells with minimal red fluorescence signaling cell death in the ACP@TH group, comparable to the control (Figure S14a). Morphological analysis revealed uniform cell morphology, with cells exhibiting healthy, elongated shapes and consistent spreading across the samples (Figure S14b). Quantitatively, the CCK-8 assay demonstrated that cell viability in the ACP@TH group was high and showed no significant difference from the control group (Figure S14c), confirming that the nano-assembly does not inhibit metabolic activity. These collective findings robustly demonstrate the favorable biocompatibility of the ACP@TH nano-assembly, which can be attributed to its biomimetic and organic-rich composition.

Assessment of remineralization in a semi-in vivo animal model

To evaluate performance under conditions closer to the complex oral environment which is characterized by saliva, pH fluctuations, and microbial activity [66, 67], the remineralization efficacy of ACP@TH was verified using a semi-in vivo rat model.

SEM analysis revealed distinct outcomes among groups (Fig. 7a). While the control group showed no significant mineral deposition, both ACP@TH and the NaF positive control induced new mineral formation. Notably, the ACP@TH group produced a more compact and homogeneous mineral layer. EDS analysis confirmed that the Ca/P ratio (1.78) of this new mineral in the ACP@TH group closely matched stoichiometric HAp (Figure S15). Importantly, Vickers hardness testing demonstrated that although the hardness of ACP@TH-treated enamel did not fully recover to the level of sound enamel, it was significantly higher than that of both the control and NaF groups (Fig. 7c). This indicates a superior ability to restore functional microhardness under bioenvironmental challenges.

(a) Low- and high-magnification SEM images of the surfaces of human enamel slices from each group after 7 days of intra-oral mineralization in rats. (b) Cross-sectional and longitudinal SEM images of human dentin slices from each group after 7 days of intra-oral mineralization in rats. (c) Vickers hardness measurements of remineralized enamel for each group (n.s.>0.05, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01). (d) Large-scale longitudinal SEM image of remineralized dentin in the ACP@TH group. (e) Hardness measurements of remineralized dentin for each group (n.s.>0.05, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01)

ACP@TH also demonstrated a robust capacity to seal dentin. SEM images showed that it effectively occluded tubule openings and achieved intratubular mineralization to a depth of approximately 40 μm (Fig. 7b and d), which helps reduce dentin hypersensitivity and inhibit bacterial invasion [68, 69]. The mineral formed within the tubules also exhibited a biomimetic Ca/P ratio of 1.74 (Figure S16). Although the occlusion depth was somewhat less than that observed in ideal in vitro settings, likely due to salivary clearance and the dynamic oral environment [70], the functional recovery was pronounced. The Vickers hardness of ACP@TH-treated dentin recovered to a level statistically comparable to that of healthy dentin (Fig. 7e), underscoring its clinical relevance for alleviating hypersensitivity and restoring biomechanical integrity.

Further assess anti-caries function of ACP@TH using a standardized rat caries model

To rigorously evaluate the anti-caries efficacy and safety under highly relevant biological conditions, a standard rat caries model was employed. This model is particularly suitable as the rat oral environment shares key enzymatic and ionic similarities with humans [71], and the mechanical challenges of mastication provide a stringent test for the stability of any therapeutic mineralization [34].

Evaluation based on Keyes scoring and sagittal sections of the molars provided clear histopathological evidence (Fig. 8a and b). As expected, the Control group (no cariogenic challenge) showed no lesions across all severity levels (E: 0, Ds: 0, Dm: 0, Dx: 0). The H₂O group (cariogenic challenge only) developed the most severe lesions, with Keyes scores of 12 (E), 8.3 (Ds), 6 (Dm), and 4 (DX), indicating caries extension into deep dentin. The NaF positive control group exhibited reduced lesion severity (E: 8, Ds: 5.3, Dm: 1.6, DX: 3), although some samples still showed deep dentinal caries. In striking contrast, the ACP@TH group achieved markedly lower scores (E: 6, Ds: 4.7, Dm: 0, DX: 0), with lesions consistently confined to enamel or shallow dentin and no involvement of middle or deep dentin. This demonstrates a significant advantage of ACP@TH in arresting the progression of caries, likely through a combination of inhibiting demineralization and promoting early remineralization at the lesion front.

Comprehensive evaluation of topical ACP@TH nano-assembly treatment against tooth decay in vivo. (a) Caries scores for rat molars in each group (n.s.>0.05, *p < 0.05). (b) Stereomicroscopic assessment of rat molars from each group. (c) Micro-CT evaluation of caries severity in rat molars from each group

Micro-CT analysis provided three-dimensional, quantitative confirmation of these findings (Fig. 8c). Consistent with the histological scores, the ACP@TH group exhibited the least severe enamel caries lesions, with caries limited to shallow dentin. In contrast, the H₂O group showed extensive “moth-eaten” enamel defects, with some samples showing caries tracts penetrating into the pulp chamber. Although the NaF group showed reduced caries severity compared to the H₂O group, certain samples still exhibited deep dentinal caries, occasionally communicating with the pulp chamber. The superior morphological preservation in the ACP@TH group suggests that the nano-assembly forms a stable, protective interface on the enamel surface. This barrier likely prevents the coalescence and expansion of micro-porosities under cariogenic attack.

The in vivo results robustly validate the therapeutic potential of ACP@TH. Its ability to confine lesions to superficial tooth structures represents a shift from merely reducing lesion severity to actively modulating the disease progression. This superior performance can be attributed to the multifunctional synergy of its design: the sustained mineral supply and biomimetic deposition from the ACP core, combined with the biofilm-disrupting action of the HACC shell and the interfacial stability provided by the TA adhesive layer. This integrated action effectively creates a dynamic barrier that counteracts the pathogenic challenges of the oral environment, highlighting its promise for clinical caries management.

Biocompatibility of ACP@TH nano-assembly

The systemic biosafety of ACP@TH, a critical prerequisite for clinical translation, was preliminarily evaluated through histopathological examination and routine hematological profiling in the rat model.

H&E staining of both local oral tissues and major systemic organs revealed no signs of overt toxicity across all groups (Figures S17, S18). Tissues from animals treated with ACP@TH displayed normal cellular morphology, well-defined tissue architecture, and an absence of pathological features such as inflammatory infiltration, abnormal interstitial deposits, or cellular necrosis. Within the observation window, these findings suggest that the nano-assembly does not induce obvious local or systemic tissue damage. Routine hematological analysis further provided supportive evidence for the preliminary safety profile (Figure S19). All blood routine parameters (e.g., red and white blood cell counts, platelet counts, hemoglobin levels) fell within normal physiological ranges for all groups (p > 0.05), indicating that the treatment did not cause acute disruption of systemic homeostasis. More comprehensive systemic biosafety assessments including serum biochemistry and functional evaluations of major organs will be conducted in future clinical trials. The ACP@TH nano-assembly, possessing both remineralization and antibiofilm efficacy, is expected to be a safe and effective approach for the prevention of dental caries.

Limitations and prospects

The bioinspired ACP@TH nano-assembly shows compelling anti-caries and remineralization efficacy in experimental models, and offers several practical advantages, including the use of commercially available, biocompatible components and a straightforward self‑assembly process that enables easy clinical application without specialized equipment. Yet several aspects warrant attention before clinical translation can be realized. First, the long‑term stability of ACP@TH in aqueous formulations remains a challenge, and future investigation into its use as a lyophilized powder is needed. Second, while the rat caries model demonstrates lesion reduction, it does not directly resolve the relative contributions of antimicrobial action, surface barrier formation, and mineral repair to the observed in vivo protection. Third, while a synergistic “antimicrobial-remineralization” mechanism has been proposed, deeper molecular-level insights, such as the precise interaction of TA with dentin collagen and the detailed antibiofilm mode of action of HACC-would further clarify the underlying mechanisms.

Future research should therefore aim to bridge these gaps. Specifically, in vivo studies incorporating plaque microbiota analysis (e.g., 16 S rRNA sequencing) and quantification of cariogenic pathogens will help disentangle the antimicrobial versus mineral‑based contributions to caries protection. Enhancing environmental adaptability through stimuli-responsive designs (e.g., pH- or enzyme-triggered release) could enable more targeted and durable therapy. Beyond coronal caries, the core biomimetic adhesion and mineralization strategy may also hold potential for applications in root caries, enamel erosion, and even bone regeneration. With continued refinement, the ACP@TH nano-assembly represents a promising integrated platform for comprehensive caries management and dental tissue repair.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study presents a nacre-inspired ACP/TA/HACC layered nanoarchitecture that successfully unites hierarchical biomimetic design with a multifunctional mechanism for caries intervention. By emulating natural biomineralization pathways, the system synergistically integrates sustained antibacterial activity, enamel remineralization, and deep dentinal tubule occlusion into a coherent preventive and reparative strategy. This bioinspired approach demonstrates considerable promise as a dual‑functional therapeutic platform for regeneration of dental hard tissues and comprehensive management of dental caries.
