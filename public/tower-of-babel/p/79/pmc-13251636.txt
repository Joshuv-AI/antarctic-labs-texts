Impact of Preoperative Osteoporotic Medications on Total Hip Arthroplasty Outcomes
Authors: Sophia S. Antonioli, Garrett Ruff, Nicole Leung, Amy Patel, Ran Schwarzkopf, Anna Cohen-Rosenblum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteoporosis (OP) is a common comorbidity in patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty (THA) and is a known risk factor for poor postoperative outcomes such as periprosthetic fracture (PPF). The impact of preoperative OP medications in patients with OP undergoing THA remains unclear. This study aimed to compare THA outcomes by OP diagnosis and preoperative bone strengthening medication usage.MethodsThis was a retrospective review of primary elective THAs from June 2011-January 2024. Patients were stratified by OP diagnosis and OP medication usage, then propensity matched in a 1:2:3 ratio by age, sex, body mass index, and comorbidities. The resulting cohorts: (1) OP and medication usage for at least 1 year preoperatively and within 7 years of the procedure (n = 296), (2) OP and no medication usage (n = 592), and (3) no diagnosis of OP and no medication usage (n = 888) were then compared for postoperative outcomes.ResultsPatients in cohort 1 were more likely to visit the emergency department (P = .043) or be readmitted for a surgical complication (P = .007) within 90 days. They were more likely to undergo posterior approach than cohorts 2 and 3 (P = .016), had the highest occurrence of cemented femoral stems (38.2%, P < .001), and had a higher incidence of revision for PPF (3.4%, vs 0.8% [cohort 2] and 1.2% [cohort 3], P = .009). Of the 12 revisions due to periprosthetic femoral fracture in cohorts 1 and 2 combined, 11 (91.7%) occurred around an uncemented implant.ConclusionsOsteoporotic patients on OP medications did not have improved outcomes after THA compared with nonmedicated osteoporotic patients or those without a diagnosis of OP. PPF in osteoporotic patients overwhelmingly occurred around uncemented femoral implants. Surgeons should use caution when operating on osteoporotic patients, regardless of utilization of preoperative medications, and strongly consider using cemented femoral implants to decrease the risk of PPF.

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) for the treatment of end-stage hip osteoarthritis is a common procedure with high patient satisfaction [1,2]. However, patients undergoing THA in the United States are increasingly older with a greater comorbidity burden [3,4]. Rates of osteoporosis (OP) in particular are increasing, with 4.3% and 15.4% of all males and females aged 50 years and older, respectively, having OP in 2010, with a projected 32% increase in the prevalence of OP by 2030 [5]. The prevalence of OP in the US THA patient population was previously reported at 28% in 2007, with the prevalence of low bone density, defined as osteopenia or OP, in total joint arthroplasty patients being 86% in 2019 [6,7].

OP is a significant risk factor for multiple complications after THA, particularly periprosthetic fractures (PPFs) and aseptic loosening [8,9]. Many patients with OP are treated with osteoporotic medications, including antiresorptive agents—most commonly bisphosphonates—and anabolic agents [[8], [9], [10]]. Many studies have investigated the postoperative use of OP bone-strengthening medications, particularly bisphosphonates, and have consistently found reductions in postoperative bone loss across multiple follow-up periods, with studies reporting approximately 5% loss of bone mineral density (BMD) with placebo compared to approximately 5% gain in BMD with bisphosphonate usage [[11], [12], [13]]. This correlates with reduced rates of PPF and aseptic loosening, with recent studies reporting aseptic revision rates with bisphosphonate usage of one-half to one-third compared to those of patients with OP not taking medications [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. However, research into the effects of preoperative OP treatment on THA patient outcomes remains limited, and outcomes are mixed, particularly given the concern for atypical femur fractures secondary to trabecular network changes with bisphosphonate usage [15]. Some studies have found an increased risk of PPF after total knee arthroplasty and THA with bisphosphonate usage, particularly in patients under the age of 65 [12,13]. Recent US national database studies have found that preoperative bisphosphonate usage is associated with greater risks of PPF and revisions [[16], [17], [18]]. However, the inherent nature of retrospective, national database studies make these studies susceptible to significant selection bias, where patients with more severe OP are more likely prescribed bisphosphonates, and the lack of granularity in national databases limits these studies’ conclusions.

While the postoperative usage of osteoporotic medications is relatively well-established and supported, there is a paucity of evidence investigating preoperative medication use in osteoporotic patients undergoing THA. Therefore, our study aims to compare THA outcomes, stratifying patients by OP diagnosis and preoperative medication prescriptions. We hypothesized that patients with preoperative osteoporotic medications would have similar outcomes to patients with OP without prior medication usage, and all OP patients would have worse outcomes compared to THA patients without OP.

Material and methods

Study design and cohort selection

This study aimed to evaluate postoperative outcomes of patients with and without OP, and patients with OP who did or did not utilize preoperative OP medications. A retrospective review of all primary, elective THA procedures between June 2011 and January 2024 was performed. Patients aged 18 years and older undergoing a unilateral THA procedure with at least 1 year of follow-up were included. Patients who underwent bilateral procedures, revision THA, or nonelective procedures, for example THA due to femoral neck fracture, were excluded. Institutional review board approval was obtained (i17-01223) prior to the conduction of study activities.

The 10th Revision of the International Classification of Diseases codes was used to stratify patients by preoperative diagnosis of OP, and the electronic medical record (EMR) was queried for osteoporotic medication prescriptions. Medications considered were antiresorptive agents (oral bisphosphonates, zoledronic acid, denosumab), anabolic agents (teriparatide and abaloparatide), and mixed agents (romosozumab). The timeline for medication usage of cohort 1 was defined as dosing for at least 1 year within 7 years preoperatively. Seven years was chosen in order to capture patients who may have been of bone-strengthening medications, but with the potential for residual effects of treatment.

Patients on bisphosphonates are often treated for 3-5 years and then transitioned to a drug holiday for 2-4 years, after which they restarted the medication [19,20].

Patients were propensity-score matched to cohort 1 in a 1:2:3 ratio based on age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression, and pairwise matching of the 3 cohorts was performed using the nearest-neighbor method, minimizing the absolute differences in propensity scores between matched pairs and ensuring that the cohorts were similar with respect to the key covariates: age, sex, BMI, and CCI. This resulted in 296, 592, and 888 patients in cohorts 1, 2, and 3, respectively.

Data collection and outcome measures

Patient characteristics, including sex, age, BMI, race, smoking status, American Society of Anesthesiologists status, and CCI, were collected from the EMR (EPIC, Verona, Wisconsin, USA). Perioperative characteristics were also collected, including type of anesthesia, surgical approach, fixation, operative time, length of stay (LOS), and discharge disposition. Postoperative outcomes were captured including emergency department (ED) visits and readmission within the 90-day postoperative period, and any reoperations or revisions within the follow-up interval.

Data analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using Excel software (Microsoft Corporation, Richmond, WA, USA) and Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) (Version 28; IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were analyzed using independent sample t-tests when comparing 2 cohorts and one-way analysis of variance when comparing 3 groups, then reported as means and ranges. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square analyses, then expressed as counts and percentages. Our study, with a total sample size of 1776 patients across 3 cohorts, demonstrated adequate power to detect the observed differences in PPF rates (approximately 82%) and all-cause revision rates (approximately 97%) using chi-square analyses with an α of 0.05. This suggests that the analyses were sufficiently powered to detect statistically significant differences in these outcomes, if present. P values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Propensity matching ensured no significant differences existed between the cohorts regarding age (P = .191), sex (P = .485), BMI (P = .332), and CCI (P = .250). There was no significant difference in smoking status when comparing the 3 cohorts (P = .190). There was a significant difference in race (P = .002) with cohort 1 having more white patients than either cohort 2 or cohort 3. The cohorts demonstrated similar comorbidity burdens, as measured by the average CCI (4.4 [cohort 1], 4.3 [cohort 2], 4.5 [cohort 3], P = .250). Short-term clinical outcomes

When comparing the 3 cohorts, there was no difference in frequency of all-cause 90-day ED visits. There was, however, a difference in 90-day ED visits due to surgical complications, with cohort 1 more likely to return to the ED for this reason (3.4% [cohort 1], 1.5% [cohort 2], 1.2% [cohort 3], P = .043). Further analysis revealed this difference persisted when specifically comparing cohorts 1 vs 3 (P = .016). When comparing the rate of 90-day readmission between the cohorts, we found a similar trend. Cohort 1 had a significantly higher rate of postoperative surgical complications (5.4% [cohort 1], 1.7% [cohort 2], 2.7% [cohort 3], P = .007). This difference persisted when comparing cohort 1 vs 2 (P = .002) and cohort 1 vs 3 (P = .026). There were no significant differences in postoperative complications between cohorts 2 and 3 (P = .202). There were no significant differences in the frequencies of complications such as periprosthetic joint infections, dislocations, PPF, deep vein thrombosis, and wound complications that led to 90-day ED visits or readmissions. Revisions and periprosthetic fractures

All patients included in the study had a minimum follow-up of 1 year. The average follow-up was 2.2 years for cohort 1, 2.5 years for cohort 2, and 2.7 years for cohort 3 (P = .071). Revision procedures were therefore evaluated at 2 time points—revisions within 1 year postoperatively and revisions at the latest follow-up. A significant difference was found between the rate of revision within 1 year (P = .047), with the highest revision rate within cohort 1 (5.1%), the second highest revision rate within cohort 3 (2.4%), and the lowest revision rate within cohort 2 (2.0%). Post hoc analysis comparing groups 1-on-1 revealed the significance of these differences in rates persisted when comparing cohort 1 vs 2 (P = .013) and cohort 1 vs 3 (P = .019), but not when comparing cohort 2 vs 3 (P = .666). When evaluating the indications for revision, cohort 1 had a significantly higher rate of revision due to PPF (2.3% [cohort 1], 0.5% [cohort 2], 1.1% [cohort 3], P = .047). Further investigation comparing the cohorts 1-on-1 reported a significant difference between the rate of revision within 1 year due to PPF when comparing cohort 1 vs 2 (P = .013), indicating that, of the patients who had OP, the patients who had OP and took OP medications had a higher rate of revision due to PPF than those who did not take OP medications. There were no reports of intraoperative fracture across all patient cohorts. There were no significant differences in rate of revision due to other indications such as periprosthetic joint infection (P = .407), mechanical loosening (P = .136), or instability/dislocation (P = .520). There was no significant difference between the mean times to revision within 1 year when comparing the 3 groups by analysis of variance analysis (P = .082).

There was a difference in all-time revision rate between the cohorts with cohort 1 at 6.1%, cohort 2 at 2.5%, and cohort 3 at 3.4% (P = .025). Post hoc analysis showed persistent significance when comparing cohort 1 vs 2 (P = .008) and cohort 1 vs 3 (P = .041), but no significance between cohort 2 vs 3 (P = .597). We also found significant differences when comparing revision due to PPF at latest follow-up (P = .009) with cohort 1 experienced 10 revisions (3.4%) due to PPF, cohort 2 experienced 5 (0.8%), and cohort 3 experienced 11 (1.2%). Comparing the groups 1-to-1 showed a significant difference between cohorts 1 vs 2 (P = .006) and cohort 1 vs 3 (P = .016). There was no significant difference in time to revision at latest follow-up interval (P = .694). When further investigating the revisions, we found that of the 10 patients in cohort 1 who underwent revision due to PPF, 7 experienced femur fractures and 3 experienced acetabular fractures. Of the 7 femur fractures, 6 (85.7%) had an uncemented femoral implant. Within cohort 2, all 5 of the patients who underwent revision due to PPF were proximal femur fractures around an uncemented implant. This theme was consistent within cohort 3, as well, where 11 fractures led to revision THA, 10 of which were femoral fractures, and all 10 of these femoral fractures (100%) occurred around an uncemented stem. Of the periprosthetic femur fractures occurring around uncemented stems across all cohorts by latest follow up (n = 22), 50% (n = 11) occurred around a single-wedge type femoral implant. Perioperative information

We found a significant difference in surgical approach when comparing the cohorts, with the OP patients on bone-strengthening medications (cohort 1) undergoing posterior approach THA more frequently (and anterior approach less frequently) than patients with OP not on medications (cohort 2) and non-OP patients (cohort 3) (P = .016). Post hoc analysis revealed a significant difference in this trend when comparing cohort 1 vs 2 (P = .005) and cohort 2 vs 3 (P = .035), but not when comparing cohort 1 vs 3 (P = .318). Additionally, we found a difference in the type of femoral fixation used between cohorts. Press-fit femoral implants were less commonly used in cohort 1 than cohort 2, and less commonly used in cohort 2 than cohort 3. These findings were significant in all variations of post hoc analysis including cohort 1 vs cohort 2 (P < .001), cohort 1 vs 3 (P < .001), cohort 2 vs 3 (P = .015), and cohorts 1 and 2 vs 3 (P < .001). The average surgical time was longest in cohort 1 (108.2 minutes), followed by cohort 2 (99.6 minutes), and cohort 3 (94.5 minutes) (P < .001). This trend in average operative time was consistent with the trend of cemented femoral fixation. Average LOS was significantly shorter in cohort 2 than cohort 3 (46.7 hours [cohort 2], 53.9 hours [cohort 3], P = .001). Our investigation found no other significant differences between the cohorts in regard to LOS. Lastly, frequency of home discharge favored cohort 2 over cohort 1 (85.5% [cohort 1], 90.0% [cohort 2], P = .038), and cohort 2 over cohort 3 (90.0% [cohort 2], 84.9% [cohort 3], P = .012).

Cohort 1 medications subanalysis

Within cohort 1, there was an unequal distribution of patients between varying classes of bone-strengthening medications. Bisphosphonates were used with the most frequency, with 71.3% of the cohort utilizing this class of OP medications. The remaining patients utilized denosumab (22.3%), anabolic medications such as teriparatide and abaloparatide (6.1%), and the antiresorptive and anabolic medication romosozumab (0.3%). Analysis of the frequencies of PPFs and revisions stratified by medication taken found no significant differences between complication rates of the medication classes. Comparing PPF and revision rate in patients on antiresorptive medications vs patients on anabolic medications revealed no significant differences (P = .600 and P = .360, respectively). Discussion

In this propensity-matched study evaluating the impact of preoperative OP medications on THA outcomes, we found that OP patients on OP therapies did not experience improved clinical outcomes compared to their untreated counterparts. OP patients on bone-strengthening medications experienced higher rates of 90-day ED visits and readmissions due to surgical complications when compared to both OP patients not receiving medications and nonosteoporotic patients. OP patients on bone-strengthening medications had a higher incidence of revision due to PPF. Notably, the vast majority of PPFs across all OP patients, regardless of bone-strengthening medication usage, occurred in the setting of uncemented femoral implants.

Our investigation of short-term clinical outcomes aligns with the findings of Emara et al., who prospectively demonstrated that a preoperative diagnosis of OP, regardless of medication status, was associated with increased healthcare utilization, worse patient-reported outcomes, and higher reoperation rates following THA [21]. In their cohort, patients with OP had higher odds of nonhome discharge, prolonged LOS, and failure to achieve clinically significant improvements in pain and physical function when compared to THA patients without an OP diagnosis. While the present study focused on surgical complications and revisions, these 2 studies broaden the general understanding of OP’s systemic impact on THA recovery.

Recent literature has questioned the presumed benefits of preoperative bisphosphonate use in reducing THA complications. In a national database study, Torres et al. found that preoperative bisphosphonate use was associated with significantly higher odds of revision for PPF at 1 year when compared to non-bisphosphonates users [16]. These findings closely mirror our own results, wherein OP patients who received bone-strengthening medications had significantly higher rates of revision for PPF compared to both OP patients who did not take medications and those without an OP diagnosis. The consistency between our single institutional dataset and the broader national analysis by Torres et al. reinforces the growing concern that preoperative antiresorptive therapy may not mitigate fracture risk following THA.

The randomized controlled trial by Aro et al. offers important mechanistic insights into the effects of bisphosphonates on periprosthetic bone remodeling and implant stability. In this study of postmenopausal women undergoing cementless THA, a single infusion of zoledronic acid was shown to preserve periprosthetic BMD over 4 years but did not reduce early femoral stem migration or improve initial implant stability [22]. These findings are highly relevant to the present study, which also suggests that bisphosphonate-treated OP patients may still be at elevated risk for PPF, regardless of improvements in bone density. Aro et al.’s results highlight a crucial disconnect between maintaining bone density and achieving immediate mechanical stability, particularly in uncemented femoral implants, where inadequate bone quality can jeopardize osseointegration. Our observation that the vast majority of PPFs across all cohorts occurred in patients with uncemented stems, including those on long-term bisphosphonates, supports this interpretation and raises concern that bisphosphonate, and other OP medication classes, treatment alone is insufficient to overcome the mechanical demands placed on compromised bone during uncemented hip arthroplasty.

The American Joint Replacement Registry 2024 Annual Report found that when “adjusting for age, sex, and CCI, cemented fixation showed a statistically significant reduction in early revision due to PPF compared to cementless fixation in elective primary THA for patients ≥65 years of age” [23]. Additional studies have found increased odds of PPF in patients >65 years of age when cementless fixation is used [24] and it is therefore recommended to increase the use of cemented fixation in older patient populations due to their worse bone quality, a recommendation that has also been made specifically for OP patients due to their compromised bone quality [9]. Our findings are consistent with reports that cementless fixation is associated with increased rates of fracture in at-risk populations, finding that 11 of the 12 revisions due to periprosthetic femur fracture (PPFF) in OP patients occurred around press-fit femoral stems. Nonetheless, only 30.7% of OP patients in our study received cemented stems. The American Joint Replacement Registry 2024 Annual report notes that only 5.1% of patients that underwent primary elective THA in 2023 were implanted with cemented femoral stems and notes these rates are much lower than found in other international registries [23]. Furthermore, Garvin et al. reported that within all patients who underwent primary THA by surgeons seeking initial board certification or recertification through the American Board of Orthopaedic Surgery in 2022 or 2023, only 30% of patients above the age of 80 underwent cemented fixation of the femoral stem [25]. In conjunction, these findings highlight that cemented stems are generally underutilized in populations at an increased risk for PPFF. A focus on promoting more consistent adoption of cemented fixation in OP patients may help reduce the incidence of PPFF and improve outcomes of this high-risk patient population.

Our findings challenge prior studies that have found a protective influence of OP medications in the postoperative setting, particularly when investigating the risk of PPF and aseptic loosening through BMD maintenance. While studies have supported postoperative bisphosphonate use for preserving periprosthetic BMD and reducing implant migration [22,[26], [27], [28]], our study adds to the growing body of evidence suggesting that preoperative OP treatment may not confer similar benefits and may even be associated with higher complication rates. This discrepancy may reflect differences in the timing, duration, or bone quality responsiveness to pharmacologic intervention, or may indicate underlying differences in disease severity of patients on osteoporotic medications.

Limitations

A notable limitation of our study is the lack of control for patients’ BMD. While we found increased rates of PPF in the cohort of OP patients on bone-strengthening medication, we cannot claim that the medications contributed to increased risk of fracture, since those on OP medications were likely to have more severe OP. We can, however, conclude that OP medications did not decrease the risk of PPF to rates comparable to those of untreated OP patients or patients without an OP diagnosis.

Another limitation of this study concerns the timing and duration of OP medication use. In cohort 1, we included patients who received OP medications for at least 1 year preoperatively and within 7 years prior to THA. These cutoffs were based on typical duration of treatment and drug holidays, but they may not have captured the optimal treatment window for reducing postoperative complications for medically treated OP patients.

An additional limitation is the retrospective nature of this study, which relied on EMR data to determine OP diagnosis and medication use. While we could confirm that patients were prescribed OP medications, we could not verify adherence to the prescriptions. If a patient was prescribed these medications by a physician outside of our medical system and it was not recorded in our EMR, we would not be able to gather this information.

Conclusions

Osteoporotic patients on preoperative OP medications did not see an improvement in postoperative THA outcomes compared to untreated OP patients and patients without OP. OP patients on bone-strengthening medications had a higher incidence of revision THA due to PPF, as well as higher rates of postoperative ED visits and readmissions. The vast majority of PPFs across all OP patients, regardless of medication usage, occurred in the setting of uncemented femoral implants. Surgeons performing elective primary THA on patients with OP should highly consider using cemented femoral implants, especially in elderly female patients who are being treated with bone-strengthening medications.
