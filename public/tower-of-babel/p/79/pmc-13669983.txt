Clinical characteristics and in‐hospital mortality among insulin pump users with acute coronary syndrome in the predominantly open‐loop era: A UK‐wide MINAP registry analysis
Authors: Harshal Deshmukh, Moosa Shaikh, Emma G. Wilmot, Eric Kilpatrick, Sudipta Chattopadhyay, Thozhukat Sathyapalan, Joseph John
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe compared characteristics of adults with insulin‐treated diabetes using continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII) versus multiple daily insulin injections (MDI) during admission with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and identified factors associated with in‐hospital mortality among CSII users.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used the Myocardial Ischaemia National Audit Project, a registry of ACS admissions in England and Wales. Adults aged ≥18 years with confirmed ACS and insulin‐treated diabetes were classified as CSII or MDI users. Baseline characteristics were compared. Gradient boosting machine (GBM) modelling and multivariable logistic regression evaluated mortality predictors among CSII users.ResultsThe cohort included 3321 CSII users and 30,593 MDI users. In‐hospital mortality was higher among CSII users (11.4% vs. 5.1%; p < 0.001). CSII users had higher rates of cardiogenic shock (4.8% vs. 1.3%), pulmonary oedema (12.1% vs. 8.3%), poor left ventricular systolic function (23.5% vs. 19.7%), and STEMI (32.2% vs. 20.5%). Cardiogenic shock was the strongest predictor of mortality (adjusted OR 10.46, 95% CI 6.95–15.81), followed by poor left ventricular function (OR 4.15, 95% CI 2.69–6.53), basal crepitations (OR 1.94, 95% CI 1.40–2.67) and pulmonary oedema (OR 1.56, 95% CI 1.05–2.28). Higher systolic blood pressure was associated with lower mortality (OR 0.86 per 10 mmHg, 95% CI 0.82–0.90). The GBM model achieved an AUC of 0.85 and a Brier score of 0.088.ConclusionsCSII users admitted with ACS represented a more complex group with haemodynamic compromise and higher unadjusted mortality. Findings predominantly reflect the open‐loop CSII era and may not generalise to contemporary hybrid closed‐loop systems.

INTRODUCTION

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) remains a leading cause of hospitalisation and death among individuals with diabetes mellitus, accounting for a disproportionately higher burden of cardiovascular morbidity in this population. In the United Kingdom, diabetes affects approximately 8.6% of the population with approximately 5.8 million people living with diabetes. 1 Diabetes is one of the most important causes of cardiovascular disease with approximately 50% of all CVD attributed to diabetes. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Insulin therapy is commonly required in individuals with longstanding or poorly controlled diabetes, and its use is often a surrogate marker for advanced disease and increased cardiovascular risk. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10

Insulin delivery modalities vary widely, with continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII), or CSII therapy, increasingly utilised in the management of type 1 diabetes and selected cases of insulin‐requiring type 2 diabetes. Evidence suggests that CSII may offer superior glycaemic control, reduced glycaemic variability and lower risk of severe hypoglycaemia compared to multiple daily injections (MDI). 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 However, despite its widespread use, there is a paucity of data examining whether patients on CSII differ in clinical characteristics or outcomes during acute cardiovascular events.

In the United Kingdom, access to CSII therapy within the National Health Service (NHS) is regulated by specific criteria outlined by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE). Between 2008 and 2023, CSII therapy was offered primarily to individuals with type 1 diabetes who experienced disabling hypoglycaemia or those who were unable to achieve an HbA1c of <69 mmol/mol with MDI. As a result, individuals on CSII therapy may represent a highly selected population with poor baseline glycaemic control, a long duration of diabetes, increased risk and excess exposure to hypoglycaemia with hypoglycaemia unawareness. 17 These factors may influence their cardiovascular risk profiles, presentation patterns and inpatient outcomes during ACS.

Hence the objective of this study is to compare the clinical characteristics of patients admitted with ACS who were on CSII versus those using MDI and to identify factors associated with in‐hospital mortality in ACS in those using CSII. However, it is important to recognise that during the majority of the study period, CSII within the UK National Health Service primarily consisted of open‐loop systems. Hybrid closed‐loop (HCL) systems, which integrate continuous glucose monitoring with automated insulin delivery, were only introduced into routine clinical practice in the later years of this study. Therefore, the findings of this analysis largely reflect outcomes associated with open‐loop CSII and may not be directly applicable to contemporary populations using HCL systems.

METHODS

Study design and data source

This was a retrospective cohort study using data from the Myocardial Ischaemia National Audit Project (MINAP), a national registry capturing detailed information on all patients admitted with suspected ACS to hospitals in England and Wales in the United Kingdom between 2002 and 2024. It is important to note that during this study period, NICE guidance on CSII therapy referred exclusively to open‐loop CSII, with eligibility restricted to individuals with disabling hypoglycaemia or persistent HbA1c ≥69 mmol/mol despite MDI. Automated insulin delivery (hybrid closed‐loop) systems were not widely available during this timeframe and were only incorporated into NICE recommendations in December 2023, near the end of our study period.

Data collection for MINAP began in October 2000, and by mid‐2002, all acute hospitals in England and Wales were participating in the registry. MINAP contains prospectively collected, standardised clinical, demographic and treatment data and is managed by the National Institute for Cardiovascular Outcomes Research (NICOR).

Study population

We included adult patients (aged ≥18 years) with a confirmed diagnosis of ACS—encompassing ST‐elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), non‐ST‐elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) and unstable angina—who had a documented history of diabetes mellitus treated with insulin. Patients were stratified based on the method of insulin delivery at the time of admission: CSII or MDI.

Variables and outcomes

Baseline characteristics collected included age, sex, ethnicity, diabetes type, diabetes duration, body mass index (BMI), smoking status and co‐morbidities (including hypertension, chronic kidney disease (CKD), prior myocardial infarction, stroke, peripheral vascular disease). Clinical variables at admission included Killip class, systolic blood pressure, heart rate, serum creatinine, haemoglobin and admission glucose. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and treatment details (e.g., coronary intervention, medications) were also recorded. The primary outcome was in‐hospital mortality, defined as all‐cause death during the index hospital admission for ACS. Left ventricular systolic function was categorised according to left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) in line with contemporary clinical practice as preserved (≥50%), mildly impaired (40%–49%), moderately impaired (30%–39%) and severely impaired (<30%), representing clinically meaningful strata of ventricular dysfunction commonly used in cardiovascular outcome studies. All predictor variables, including cardiogenic shock, were defined and captured at the time of presentation (i.e., on admission) rather than as evolving events during the hospital stay.

Patients with diabetes were identified using two variables from the MINAP dataset: Diabetes (X2.17) and Management of Hyperglycaemia/Diabetes (X3.41). Individuals were included if they were coded as having insulin‐dependent diabetes and their inpatient management involved insulin therapy, either via multiple daily injections (MDI) or CSII. Patients managed with a combination of insulin and oral agents, dietary therapy, or with unknown/missing treatment data were excluded to avoid misclassification of Type 2 diabetes. To ensure consistency between chronic diagnosis and acute inpatient management, only those with insulin recorded in both variables were retained. A binary variable (on_insulin_pump) was created by scanning treatment fields for text strings indicating CSII use. This approach enabled stratification of a clean insulin‐only cohort, distinguishing those on MDI versus CSII. The final cohort, therefore, predominantly comprised individuals with likely Type 1 diabetes managed with insulin monotherapy. In this dataset, CKD was captured as a binary variable based on physician diagnosis documented in the registry at admission, rather than eGFR‐based KDIGO criteria.

Imputation of missing data

In order to handle missing data, we used the missRanger package in R, which performs imputation using random forest models combined with predictive mean matching (PMM). This approach is particularly advantageous for datasets like MINAP as it contains a mixture of continuous and categorical variables, as it can model complex, non‐linear relationships and interactions without requiring parametric assumptions. Compared to traditional approaches such as mean imputation or regression‐based imputation, missRanger better preserves the underlying data structure and reduces bias. Also, as compared with other machine learning–based methods such as missForest, it is computationally more efficient for datasets. Predictive mean matching (PMM) was used (pmm.k = 3), whereby for each missing value, a small set of candidate donors is identified based on the closest predicted values from the imputation model, and one observed value is randomly selected from these donors. This approach preserves the distribution of the data and avoids implausible imputed values, particularly for skewed variables. However, we acknowledge certain limitations with this imputation. missRanger performs single imputation rather than multiple imputation, which may underestimate variance. Additionally, as with all imputation methods, it assumes that data are missing at random (MAR), which cannot be formally verified in observational datasets. Finally, imputation models may not fully account for unmeasured confounders. Missing data proportions are reported in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise patient characteristics, stratified by insulin delivery modality (pump vs. MDI). Continuous variables were reported as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range) and compared using t‐tests or Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using chi‐squared or Fisher's exact tests. To identify key predictors of in‐hospital mortality among individuals using CSII, we developed a supervised machine learning model using a gradient boosting framework. The dataset was first restricted to patients who were using CSII. All missing values were previously imputed using the missRanger algorithm, which applies a random forest‐based approach to estimate missing entries by incorporating correlations among variables. We employed the gbm package in R to fit a gradient boosting machine (GBM) model, with death_in_hospital as the binary outcome variable. A 10‐fold cross‐validation was implemented to identify the optimal number of boosting iterations (trees), minimising the out‐of‐bag classification error. The optimal number of trees was determined during model tuning using cross‐validation within the caret framework. Based on the tuning grid and cross‐validated performance (optimising the area under the ROC curve), the optimal number of trees selected for the final gradient boosting model was 300 trees, in combination with an interaction depth of 3 and a learning rate (shrinkage) of 0.01. The relative influence of each predictor variable on the outcome was quantified using the built‐in summary.gbm function, which calculates variable importance as a function of the improvement in squared error attributable to each variable across all splits. To minimise overfitting and obtain an unbiased estimate of model performance, the dataset was randomly split into training (70%) and test (30%) subsets using stratified sampling. Model training and hyperparameter tuning were performed exclusively on the training set using 10‐fold cross‐validation. The final model was then evaluated on the independent test set, ensuring that predictions for each individual were generated from a model that had not been trained on their data. In addition to discrimination assessed by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), model calibration was evaluated using the Brier score on the independent test dataset.

Model performance was evaluated using the AUC, generated via the pROC package. The AUC and its 95% confidence interval were calculated to assess discrimination performance. Sensitivity and specificity were computed at the optimal threshold determined using Youden's index. We also conducted multivariable logistic regression to identify the direction of effect obtained for the top 10 variables obtained from the GBM model. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.0).

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted using de‐identified data from the MINAP registry. Ethical approval was not required as the analysis was carried out for the purpose of service evaluation using anonymised national audit data. The study was approved by the MINAP Academic Group in accordance with NICOR data governance policies.

RESULTS

Unadjusted in‐hospital mortality was higher among CSII users than among MDI users (11.4% vs. 5.1%; p < 0.001; SMD 0.232). CSII users were slightly younger (67.10 vs. 68.76 years; p < 0.001; SMD 0.130), while the proportion of women was similar between groups (40.8% vs. 38.0%; p = 0.002; SMD 0.056). Asian ethnicity was more frequent among CSII users (12.7% vs. 10.7%), although the overall difference in ethnicity was small (p < 0.001; SMD 0.086).

Markers of haemodynamic compromise and heart failure were more common among CSII users. Cardiogenic shock occurred in 4.8% of CSII users compared with 1.3% of MDI users, pulmonary oedema in 12.1% versus 8.3% and basal crepitations in 18.4% versus 15.5% (overall p < 0.001; SMD 0.272). Poor left ventricular systolic function was also more common among CSII users (23.5% vs. 19.7%), while moderately impaired LVEF occurred in 43.8% versus 41.5% (overall p < 0.001; SMD 0.136).

CSII users were more likely to present with an anterior infarction (20.7% vs. 16.0%) or inferior infarction (26.9% vs. 21.5%), while an indeterminate infarction site was less common (48.2% vs. 58.6%) (overall p < 0.001; SMD 0.214). STEMI was also more frequent among CSII users (32.2% vs. 20.5%), whereas NSTEMI was less frequent (60.3% vs. 71.6%) (p < 0.001; SMD 0.270).

Current smoking was more common among CSII users (20.5% vs. 17.3%), while ex‐smoking was less common (39.0% vs. 43.8%) (overall p < 0.001; SMD 0.108). CSII users had modestly higher HbA1c levels (78.18 vs. 76.26 mmol/mol; p < 0.001; SMD 0.097) and a higher prevalence of chronic renal failure (32.8% vs. 29.3%; p < 0.001; SMD 0.077), peripheral vascular disease (21.4% vs. 17.2%; p < 0.001; SMD 0.107), hypercholesterolaemia (49.4% vs. 44.8%; p < 0.001; SMD 0.093) and loop diuretic use (44.2% vs. 41.0%; p < 0.001; SMD 0.066).

Several other between‐group differences were statistically significant but small in magnitude. Aldosterone antagonist use was similar between groups (15.3% vs. 14.1%; p = 0.069; SMD 0.033), as were ticagrelor or clopidogrel use (40.7% vs. 42.0%; p = 0.153; SMD 0.027) and aspirin use before admission (38.8% vs. 39.2%; p = 0.675; SMD 0.008).

Figure 1 shows the relative influence of various clinical variables in predicting in‐hospital mortality among CSII users from the GBM model. The Killip class, systolic blood pressure (BP), and age emerged as the most influential predictors, with Killip class being the strongest factor. Other important variables such as heart rate, left ventricular ejection fraction, and cholesterol also contributed significantly to the model's performance. Variables like haemoglobin, creatinine and HbA1c, though less influential, still played a role in determining mortality risk and provide insight into the multifactorial nature of in‐hospital mortality in this patient group. Figure 2 shows the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for an optimised GBM model used to predict in‐hospital mortality among patients with CSII admitted for ACS. The curve, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.85, demonstrates the model's strong performance in distinguishing between survivors and non‐survivors. The Brier score was 0.079, suggesting acceptable overall predictive accuracy. The calibration intercept was −0.168 (95% CI −0.532 to 0.196), the calibration slope was 0.908 (95% CI 0.735–1.081) and calibration‐in‐the‐large was −0.013 (95% CI −0.232 to 0.205). These findings indicate generally good calibration, with no strong evidence of systematic overprediction or underprediction. The calibration plot demonstrated close agreement between predicted and observed risks across most of the risk distribution, although uncertainty increased at higher predicted probabilities.

Increasing age was associated with higher in‐hospital mortality, with a 3% increase in the odds of death per additional year of age (adjusted OR 1.03, 95% CI 1.02–1.05; p < 0.001). In contrast, higher systolic blood pressure was associated with lower mortality, with a 14% reduction in the odds of death per 10 mmHg increase (adjusted OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.82–0.90; p < 0.001).

Left ventricular systolic dysfunction was also independently associated with mortality. Moderately impaired LVEF was associated with increased mortality compared with good LVEF (adjusted OR 1.66, 95% CI 1.07–2.63; p = 0.026), while poor LVEF was associated with a more than fourfold increase in mortality (adjusted OR 4.15, 95% CI 2.69–6.53; p < 0.001).

The logistic regression model demonstrated a Brier score of 0.088.

DISCUSSION

In this large, population‐based study in the United Kingdom, we demonstrate that individuals with diabetes using CSII who present with ACS exhibit more severe clinical profiles—characterised by advanced heart failure, higher rates of cardiogenic shock and a greater likelihood of ST‐elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI)—compared to those managing their diabetes with MDI. This group also demonstrated higher unadjusted in‐hospital mortality; however, this likely reflects differences in baseline clinical characteristics and selection bias rather than an effect of insulin delivery modality.

CSII has been associated with improved glycaemic control, 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 a more favourable metabolic profile, 24 reduced diabetes‐related distress 25 and potential long‐term mortality benefits 26 in people with diabetes. Recent real‐world data 27 from a large retrospective cohort study of over 34,000 individuals with type 1 diabetes has shown that CSII therapy is associated with significantly lower all‐cause mortality compared to multiple daily injections (MDI) over a five‐year period. These analyses include populations from the era of hybrid closed‐loop (AID) systems, which were not part of routine care for the vast majority of patients in our cohort. However, an unexpected and potentially important finding was the increased incidence of ischaemic heart disease (IHD) among CSII users during years 2–5 of follow‐up, despite their lower overall mortality and HbA1c levels. In contrast to improved glycaemic control with CSII shown by several trials, a UK‐based REPOSE randomised controlled trial 28 found no significant difference in HbA1c outcomes between the two modalities when both groups received structured education, although pump therapy improved treatment satisfaction. This highlights that glycaemic benefits may be context‐dependent and influenced by patient selection and education. These findings highlight the complexity of cardiovascular risk and pump therapy for type 1 diabetes.

There is a lack of data evaluating the clinical characteristics of those who are established on CSII who present with ACS. In our cohort, patients using CSII appeared to represent a clinically more complex group, with higher Killip class at presentation, poorer left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and a greater burden of cardiovascular co‐morbidities. This likely reflects the previous eligibility criteria for CSII in the United Kingdom, as outlined by NICE, which recommends CSII for those with higher HbA1c values or problematic hypoglycaemia despite multiple daily injections. Consequently, users often represent a subgroup who experienced chronic high glucose levels and recurrent glucose excursions for the period leading up to CSII therapy—factors known to contribute to increased cardiovascular risk and poorer outcomes.

Given the inherent selection bias, comparing outcomes such as survival between CSII users and non‐users is fraught with residual confounding, even with statistical adjustments. For example, although regression models can adjust for LVEF, these are often limited to broad categories and may not capture the true severity of cardiac dysfunction (e.g., EF of 20% vs. 35%). Moreover, important unmeasured variables such as the frequency of severe hypoglycaemia, glycaemic variability and duration of type 1 diabetes or prior cardiovascular events are often not captured in MINAP and other ACS datasets, making it difficult to fully account for baseline differences. Therefore, we did not attempt to assess whether CSII use is independently associated with in‐hospital survival after adjusting for covariates. Instead, our analysis focused on describing the baseline differences in co‐morbidities and clinical status between the two groups and subsequently identifying predictors of survival within the CSII cohort alone.

Numerous studies have established that key predictors of mortality in the general population presenting with ACS include advancing age, higher Killip class, lower systolic blood pressure, tachycardia, impaired renal function, anaemia, elevated admission glucose and poor left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). These risk factors are widely used in validated risk scoring systems such as the GRACE 29 , 30 and TIMI 31 scores to estimate short‐term and in‐hospital mortality. In our analysis focused specifically on Type 1 diabetes population and those with CSII therapy, a population often excluded or underrepresented in large cardiovascular trials—we identified a similar set of prognostic markers, including Killip class, systolic blood pressure, age and LVEF. Notably, renal function (as measured by creatinine), a strong predictor in the general ACS population, did not significantly predict mortality in our CSII cohort. This may reflect the relatively uniform presence of chronic co‐morbidities in this group, diluting the discriminative value of creatinine. Our findings underscore the importance of tailoring prognostic models to reflect the distinct risk profiles of technologically advanced diabetes subgroups, as their baseline complexity, diabetes‐related complications and prior treatment exposures may uniquely shape their response to acute cardiovascular insults.

These findings have several important clinical and policy implications. First, they underscore the complexity and heightened vulnerability of CSII users who present with ACS, challenging the assumption that technology use always reflects better overall health. In fact, the higher mortality observed in this group likely reflects the severity of underlying co‐morbidities and disease burden, as access to pump therapy in the United Kingdom is generally reserved for those with more problematic type 1 diabetes under current NICE guidelines. This highlights a potential mismatch between the timing of diabetes technology initiation and the window in which it might offer the most benefit. Many individuals in the United Kingdom had lived with diabetes for nearly two decades before commencing CSII therapy, suggesting that advanced technologies like CSII are often introduced late in the disease course—possibly after irreversible complications have developed. 17 This delay may diminish the potential impact of such interventions on long‐term outcomes, underscoring the importance of earlier identification and targeted adoption of diabetes technology in those most likely to benefit. Clinicians and policymakers should consider whether current criteria for technology access inadvertently delay initiation until after significant clinical deterioration has occurred—potentially limiting the preventive benefits of such interventions. It is reassuring that the guidance in the United Kingdom has changed. In December 2023, NICE recommended hybrid closed‐loop (HCL) systems for adults with type 1 diabetes who have an HbA1c of 58 mmol/mol (7.5%) or higher, or who experience disabling hypoglycaemia, for all children and young people under 18 years, as well as for individuals who are pregnant or planning to become pregnant. This includes a plan for 5 year implementation which will hopefully help prevent the previously seen 2 decade delay in access to technology and improved outcomes.

Our study has several limitations. The MINAP registry does not capture specific details on CSII models, whether pumps were sensor‐augmented or low‐glucose suspend, the use of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) at home or during hospitalisation, or the types of basal and prandial insulins used in the MDI group. These limitations restrict our ability to assess how differences in technology use may have influenced outcomes. However, to our knowledge, there is currently no other publicly available dataset that combines such a large‐scale, nationally representative cohort with detailed ACS outcomes and stratification by insulin delivery modality. Another key limitation is that the MINAP registry does not definitively identify individuals with Type 1 diabetes based on confirmatory biomarkers such as autoantibodies or C‐peptide levels. Instead, classification relies on treatment patterns, such as insulin‐only therapy. Although we excluded all participants who received any form of oral glucose‐lowering medication to reduce misclassification, this approach cannot entirely eliminate the possibility that some individuals with Type 2 diabetes were included. Additionally, the retrospective nature of the study limits the ability to establish causality, and the potential for residual confounding remains despite statistical adjustments. For example, important unmeasured variables such as the frequency of severe hypoglycemia, glycemic variability and duration of diabetes were not available in the dataset. These factors could influence both the management strategies and outcomes in patients with CSIIs, making comparisons with MDI users challenging. Furthermore, we were unable to assess hospital‐level variability in CSII management and outcomes, as the dataset lacked structured identifiers and site‐level contextual information. Future research incorporating detailed hospital‐level data is needed to explore this heterogeneity more comprehensively. Our study spans a 22‐year period (2002–2024), during which CSII technology evolved significantly. For most of this period, CSII users were on open‐loop pumps, as hybrid closed‐loop systems only became available and gradually adopted in the latter years of the study. Therefore, our results predominantly reflect outcomes in the open‐loop pump era and are not generalisable to current or future populations using advanced hybrid closed‐loop technologies. Further analyses focusing on the most recent years could help clarify whether outcomes have improved with broader adoption of automated insulin delivery systems. Finally, these findings should be interpreted within the context of the UK healthcare system, where historically strict NICE criteria restricted pump therapy to individuals with disabling hypoglycaemia or persistently high HbA1c. This selection bias resulted in a sicker, more co‐morbid cohort of CSII users compared to pump populations in countries with broader access. Therefore, our results cannot be directly extrapolated to healthcare settings where CSII therapy is initiated earlier and in a wider range of patients. Also, it is important to note that eligibility criteria for CSII therapy vary across healthcare systems; in countries where access to pump therapy is broader and initiated earlier in the disease course, such differences in baseline clinical complexity may be less pronounced, and our findings may therefore not be directly generalisable. Finally, adjunct therapies (e.g., SGLT2 inhibitors) in type 1 diabetes were not captured and may represent unmeasured confounding, particularly given their selective use during the study period.

In conclusion, this study highlights the complexity and increased cardiovascular risk among CSII users admitted for ACS. While CSII offers significant benefits in glycemic control for many patients with diabetes, those using CSII in the context of ACS appear to represent a more clinically complicated group with greater co‐morbidity burden, advanced heart failure and higher mortality rates. These findings suggest that CSII therapy is often used in individuals with higher HbA1c levels and longstanding diabetes, which may contribute to poorer ACS outcomes. Further research is needed to better understand the impact of CSII on ACS outcomes, accounting for the unique characteristics and risk factors of this patient group. Additionally, more equitable and earlier access to advanced diabetes technologies could potentially improve early intervention and outcomes for individuals at high cardiovascular risk.
