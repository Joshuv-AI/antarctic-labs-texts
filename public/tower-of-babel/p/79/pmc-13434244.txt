Load state identification of rock bolts based on energy and multiscale permutation entropy of ultrasonic signals
Authors: Hongyao Zhang, Long Wang, Liuyu Zhang, Di Mo, Zaitie Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Monitoring the load state of rock bolts is of great importance for ensuring the safe operation of geotechnical and underground structures. In view of the pronounced stage-dependent variation of ultrasonic response characteristics under complex contact interface conditions at the anchorage end, this study proposes a stage-wise identification method for rock bolt load states based on wave energy and multiscale permutation entropy (MPE). Starting from the mechanical evolution of the anchorage interface, wavelet packet energy is employed in the low-load stage to characterize the global response dominated by the average contact stiffness, while MPE is introduced in the high-load stage to capture the enhanced interface nonlinearity and the evolution of signal complexity. In this way, stable identification of the rock bolt load state over the entire loading range is achieved. To validate the proposed method, a multi-path piezoelectric (PZT) excitation-reception experimental system was established, and ultrasonic reflection signals were collected under different working conditions and loading levels. The experimental results indicate that wavelet packet energy exhibits high sensitivity to variations in bolt load during the low-load stage, whereas MPE shows a stable and monotonic evolution with increasing load in the high-load range. Owing to the complementary characteristics of the two features across different loading stages, the proposed stage-wise identification strategy enables full-range characterization of the rock bolt load state. The findings provide a physically interpretable ultrasonic monitoring approach for assessing the load state of rock bolts under complex contact interface conditions.

1. Introduction

As an important supporting component widely used in geotechnical and underground structures, the load state of rock bolts is directly related to surrounding rock stability, stress distribution in linings, and the overall safety of support systems.1,2 Since rock bolts are typically embedded within grout and rock masses, their load states are invisible and conventional sensors are difficult to deploy. Achieving non-destructive and long-term monitoring of rock bolt axial force has therefore long been recognized as a key challenge in the field of structural health monitoring.3,4

Traditional rock bolt monitoring methods mainly include strain gauges, hydraulic load cells, and fiber optic sensing techniques. Although strain gauges and fiber optic sensors can provide relatively high accuracy under laboratory conditions, their practical applications are limited by complex installation procedures, poor resistance to environmental interference, difficulty in long-term service, and infeasibility of deployment within grout bodies.5,6

In recent years, response-signal-based non-destructive testing techniques have attracted increasing attention, particularly methods based on ultrasonic guided waves, frequency response analysis, and signal energy variations. 7 These approaches are sensitive to internal stress changes in rock bolts and allow flexible sensor deployment. Representative studies include methods based on coda wave interferometry, 8 instantaneous phase difference, 9 electromagnetic wave propagation characteristics, 10 linear frequency-modulated guided waves, 11 and low-cost guided wave systems, 12 which have achieved promising results in anchorage defect identification, integrity assessment, and preload variation analysis. However, such studies mostly rely on information such as the propagation time, wave velocity, or reflection location of ultrasonic longitudinal waves or guided waves to infer the axial force state of anchor bolts. The applicability of these methods is usually based on the assumption of a clearly defined propagation path and a relatively homogeneous medium. However, practical anchor systems are often composed of multiple media, including the anchor rod, grout, and surrounding rock or concrete. The load distribution along the anchorage length is markedly non-uniform and is accompanied by complex interface contact and bonding behavior, which imposes certain limitations on distance-based measurement methods in engineering applications.

In this context, active ultrasonic sensing methods based on piezoelectric (PZT) ceramics have received growing attention due to their ability to be deployed at accessible ends (e.g., bearing plates or nut surfaces), low cost, and high sensitivity to interface contact conditions. By analyzing characteristics such as wave energy propagation,13,14 echo energy ratio, 15 energy attenuation,16–18 and frequency response, 19 load-related diagnostic indices can be constructed. Among these, energy-related features, as intuitive reflections of the response, are often used to construct diagnostic indicators related to load state due to their advantages of clear physical meaning and simple calculation. Relevant studies usually explain these phenomena from the perspective of contact mechanics, where changes in reflected wave energy with increasing load are attributed mainly to the increase in real contact area and contact stiffness at the anchorage interface.20,21

However, existing studies have also shown that when the anchorage region reaches a high prestress level or enters a fully compacted interface state, the growth rates of real contact area and equivalent contact stiffness decrease significantly. 22 Under such conditions, traditional ultrasonic features based on energy, amplitude, or equivalent impedance tend to exhibit reduced sensitivity or even saturation behavior,23–26 which limits their discrimination capability for rock bolt load states in the high-load range. This “feature saturation” phenomenon is common in active ultrasonic monitoring methods based on local interface contact, yet its physical interpretation is often limited to average contact area or equivalent parameters, making it difficult to fully reflect the localized nonlinear evolution of the interface at high load levels.

On the other hand, once interface contact enters a pronounced nonlinear stage, multiscale mechanisms such as elastoplastic deformation of asperities, local slip, and reconstruction of scattering paths significantly enhance the non-stationarity and temporal complexity of ultrasonic response signals. Unlike traditional energy-based features, which mainly focus on characterizing the overall amplitude or energy variation of a signal, entropy-based complexity measures describe the internal structure of a time series by capturing the ordering patterns of the signal and their dynamic evolution. As a result, they exhibit higher sensitivity to nonlinear and stochastic variations. As a representative class of nonlinear dynamic indicators, entropy-based methods have been widely applied in signal classification, state monitoring, and structural health monitoring. 27

Since the introduction of information entropy, methods such as approximate entropy, sample entropy, and permutation entropy (PE) have been widely used in nonlinear time series analysis.28–30 Among these, PE is widely applied in engineering fault diagnosis due to its high computational efficiency, threshold-free formulation, and good robustness to noise.30–33 However, as a typical ordinal-based method, PE focuses only on the ordering structure of the signal and ignores amplitude information, which leads to certain limitations in complexity characterization.33,34 To address this issue, researchers have proposed improved approaches, such as dispersion entropy (DE) and weighted permutation entropy, aiming to incorporate amplitude information or enhance pattern discrimination capability.28,29,34

In addition, single-scale entropy methods have difficulty capturing the dynamical characteristics of signals across different time scales. To address this issue, the concept of multiscale entropy was proposed and has been extensively developed, leading to methods such as multiscale sample entropy (MSE), multiscale dispersion entropy (MDE), and multiscale permutation entropy (MPE).27,31,32,35–37 Among these, MPE computes permutation entropy over multiple time scales through a multiscale coarse-graining process, enabling a more comprehensive characterization of nonstationary and multiscale nonlinear processes. Consequently, MPE has been widely applied in state monitoring and fault diagnosis of structures such as rolling bearings, gears, and bolts.27,29,31,32,38

Although MPE demonstrates good discriminative capability in complexity analysis, existing studies have pointed out that it still has certain limitations in engineering applications. On the one hand, because MPE relies on a multiscale coarse-graining process, local dynamic information and high-frequency components in the original time series may be weakened or even lost as the scale factor increases, which can affect the accurate extraction of nonlinear features.27,29,31 On the other hand, MPE is essentially an ordinal-based method and is insensitive to signal amplitude variations, which imposes certain constraints on its applicability to continuous physical quantity inversion problems.29,34 In addition, previous studies have shown that the stability and monotonicity of MPE features may also be influenced by the selection of parameters such as embedding dimension, time delay, and scale factor, with these effects being particularly pronounced under high-noise or large-scale conditions.29,31

To address the above limitations, various improvement strategies and novel entropy-based indicators have been proposed. For example, hierarchical or composite approaches have been introduced to simultaneously extract low- and high-frequency information, while weighting schemes and multivariate extensions have been employed to enhance the stability of entropy measures.29,31 In addition, new complexity indicators such as rating entropy and its multivariate extensions, as well as geometric entropy, have been developed to compensate for the limitations of traditional entropy methods in characterizing amplitude information or phase-space geometric properties, and have demonstrated good performance in specific applications such as rotating machinery fault diagnosis.39,40 However, different entropy measures are usually developed for different signal assumptions, parameter settings, and application objectives. As a result, they exhibit distinct strengths in complexity characterization and are suitable for different types of problems. In practical engineering signals, complexity features are often accompanied by pronounced nonstationarity and multiple frequency components. When a single entropy measure is directly applied to the raw signal, it is susceptible to spectral mixing and the masking of local dynamics, which may weaken its ability to characterize key physical processes.

A large body of existing research has shown that combining time–frequency decomposition techniques with entropy-based complexity measures is an effective approach for analyzing nonstationary signals and extracting discriminative features. By using time–frequency decomposition methods such as wavelet packet transform and flexible analytic wavelet transform, the original signal can be decomposed into multiple sub-signals with distinct frequency-band characteristics, thereby alleviating the effects of spectral aliasing and nonstationarity. On this basis, introducing permutation entropy or its extended forms helps to further quantify the complexity characteristics of signals in different frequency bands.41–45 This feature construction strategy has been validated in various applications, including mechanical fault diagnosis, structural damage identification, and building energy consumption prediction, demonstrating its effectiveness and robustness.43,44,46

It should be noted that, although the effectiveness of combining decomposition methods with entropy-based indicators has been well validated in the aforementioned studies, most existing work focuses on state classification or fault identification problems. Relatively little attention has been paid to systematically analyzing the applicability of different features across various load intervals from the perspective of continuous load evolution and stage-wise changes in physical mechanisms. In particular, in anchor force monitoring, the rough contact interface at the anchorage end undergoes a continuous evolution process during loading. The dominant mechanical mechanisms differ significantly across these load stages, making it difficult for a single feature to maintain stable discriminative capability over the entire load range.

Based on the above considerations, this study builds upon existing research and, in combination with the mechanical evolution mechanism of the rough contact interface at the anchorage end, proposes a stage-wise anchor force state identification method based on wavelet packet energy and MPE. By introducing energy features that are highly sensitive to variations in average contact stiffness at low load levels, and incorporating MPE with stable responses to the evolution of interface nonlinear complexity at high load levels, stable identification of anchor force states over the entire load range is achieved.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 1 presents the introduction and research background. Section 2 establishes the theoretical and methodological framework for load state identification, focusing on anchorage interface contact mechanisms and signal complexity evolution. Section 3 validates the proposed method through experimental investigations. Section 4 discusses and analyzes the experimental results. Section 5 summarizes the main conclusions and outlines directions for future research.

2. Methodology

This section starts from the mechanical mechanism of the rough contact interface at the anchorage end and establishes an analytical framework linking contact pressure, interface stiffness, reflected wave energy, and signal statistical features. On this basis, a rock bolt load identification method is proposed by combining wave energy features with MPE.

2.1. Principle of interface wave propagation and load state identification of rock bolts based on piezoelectric sensing

2.1.1. Mechanical model of the rough contact interface at the anchorage end

Rock bolts, as critical load-bearing components, are widely used in civil and mining engineering to reinforce slopes, tunnel surrounding rock, and underground structures. A typical rock bolt is equipped with a threaded end to allow the installation of a bearing plate and nut, thereby achieving effective anchorage, as illustrated in Figure 1(a). In practical engineering applications, the axial force of a rock bolt is primarily applied and maintained by the anchorage-end nut, and variations in the anchorage-end force can directly reflect the evolution of the rock bolt load state.Figure 1.(a) Anchor bolt structure and macroscopic force; (b) Microscopic interface energy transfer.

(a) Anchor bolt structure and macroscopic force; (b) Microscopic interface energy transfer.

In this study, an active sensing approach based on piezoelectric transducers (PZTs) is employed for monitoring. Specifically, PZT elements are bonded to the surface of the anchorage washer, as shown in Figure 1(b). During operation, PZT 1 acts as an actuator to excite ultrasonic waves propagating along the longitudinal direction of the rock bolt. When the wave passes through the anchorage zone, its propagation characteristics are significantly influenced by the interface contact condition. Part of the wave energy is transmitted into the anchoring body, while the remaining part is reflected and scattered due to interface discontinuities, and finally received by PZT 2. It is well known that ultrasonic wave propagation characteristics (including transmission and reflection) are closely related to preload and real contact area. 22 As illustrated in Figure 1(b), the connection interface exhibits irregular rough morphology at the microscopic scale, and the real contact regions are distributed only among asperities.

To describe the influence of contact pressure on the mechanical properties of the interface, classical rough surface contact mechanics theory is adopted to model the normal stiffness of the interface, as shown in Figure 2.Figure 2.(a) Simplified contact plane; (b) Asperity mechanical analysis; (c) Schematic diagram of microscopic contact on the interface.

The contact interface among the nut, washer, and bearing plate at the anchorage end represents a typical rough surface contact problem. By simplifying the complex contact morphology, the interface is modeled as the interaction between a rigid flat surface and an equivalent rough surface, as illustrated in Figure 2(a).

Next, the mechanical response of a single asperity is analyzed to capture the fundamental contact mechanics behavior, as shown in Figure 2(b). Under normal loading conditions, the equivalent normal stiffness of the interface is defined as the derivative of the interface normal force with respect to the macroscopic normal deformation, i.e., (1) k¯=dFdδ where F denotes the total normal load acting on the interface, and δ represents the macroscopic indentation between the two contacting surfaces.

Finally, the behavior of a large number of asperities is statistically integrated to obtain macroscopic contact parameters, as illustrated in Figure 2(c). According to the Greenwood-Williamson (GW) model, 47 a rough surface consists of numerous asperities with radius R, whose heights z follow a probability density function ϕ(z) . The asperity density per unit area is denoted by η . 2.2. Evolution analysis of multiscale permutation entropy under contact pressure

Multiscale permutation entropy (MPE) is employed to characterize the complexity of time series at different temporal scales. Essentially, MPE is a statistical measure of the probability distribution of ordinal patterns in the reconstructed phase space of a signal. Due to the pronounced nonlinearity and randomness exhibited by the rough contact interface during loading, it remains difficult to establish an explicit analytical relationship between contact pressure and MPE. Therefore, from the perspective of contact nonlinearity, this study analyzes the evolution of MPE during loading and its physical interpretability.

2.2.1. Influence of nonlinear modulation on ordinal pattern distribution

In MPE analysis, the original ultrasonic signal sequence is denoted as {x1,x2,...,xn} . For a given scale factor s, a time series yu(s) at scale s is constructed through a coarse-graining operation. The coarse-graining process (as shown in Figure 3)) can be expressed as: (19) yu(s)=1s∑i=(u−1)s+1usxi,u=1,2,...,⌊ns⌋ where yu(s) denotes the new sequence obtained by segmenting and averaging the original signal sequence with a scale of s, n represents the total length of the original signal, and ⌊·⌋ denotes the floor operator. It can be seen that different scale factors s correspond to coarse-grained time series with different lengths, thereby forming a set of multiscale time series used to characterize the dynamic properties of the signal at different time scales.Figure 3.The processing flow from the original signal to the multi-scale coarse-grained signal.

The processing flow from the original signal to the multi-scale coarse-grained signal.

At a given scale s, phase-space reconstruction is performed on the coarse-grained sequence yu(s) . Given the embedding dimension m and the time delay τ , the embedding vectors can be constructed as: (20) Yq(s)={yq(s),yq+τ(s),...,yq+(m−1)τ(s)},q=1,2,..,N where N=⌊n/s⌋−(m−1)τ , denotes the total number of embedding vectors that can be formed at scale s.

For each embedding vector Yq(s) , the corresponding ordinal pattern is determined according to the relative magnitudes of its components. When the embedding dimension is m, there are theoretically m! different ordinal patterns. Let the probability of occurrence of the j-th ordinal pattern πj at scale s be denoted as p(s)(πj) , with j=1,2,...,m! . The probability is calculated as: (21) p(s)(πj)=Nj(s)N where Nj(s) is the number of occurrences of the j-th ordinal pattern πj at scale s, and N is the total number of embedding vectors.

As the contact pressure increases, nonlinear modulation becomes more pronounced, inducing more frequent small perturbations in local temporal ordering relationships. Previous studies have shown that permutation entropy is highly sensitive to such subtle temporal order variations. Even when the overall shape of the ordinal pattern distribution does not undergo significant reconstruction, changes in its fine probability structure can still be captured by entropy values.49,50

Accordingly, with increasing contact pressure, the ordinal pattern probability distribution tends to evolve “from biased toward relatively uniform”, i.e., (22) p(s)(πj)→1m!

This expression emphasizes a statistical evolution trend rather than a strictly uniform distribution.

2.2.2. Monotonic evolution of multiscale permutation entropy

By counting the probabilities p(s)(πj) of all ordinal patterns at scale s, the permutation entropy at scale s can be calculated as: (23) PE(s)=−∑j=1m!p(s)(πj)ln p(s)(πj)

When the ordinal pattern probability distribution approaches uniformity, the entropy value reaches a higher level. Combining Eqs (21). - (23), a monotonic mapping can be established: increasing pressure p enhances nonlinear modulation, promotes uniformization of p(s)(πj) , and consequently increases permutation entropy PE(s) .

To eliminate the influence of the embedding dimension on the magnitude of the entropy value, a normalized form is usually adopted: (24) NPE(s)=PE(s)ln m! By repeating the above procedure for different scale factors s, under the condition of a maximum scale smax , if the mean value of multiscale permutation entropy is taken as the final feature indicator, it is defined as: (25) MPE=1smax∑s=1smaxNPE(s)

Therefore, contact pressure enhances local stiffness fluctuations and nonlinear scattering intensity at the interface in the high contact pressure stage, indirectly altering the ordinal pattern probability distribution of the reflected signal, which leads to a continuous increase in MPE. This theoretical framework provides a clear physical basis for entropy-based indices to enable full-range identification of rock bolt mechanical states.

3. Experimental validation

3.1. Experimental setup and testing scheme

To verify the effectiveness of the proposed method, a series of rock bolt load monitoring experiments were conducted. The experimental setup is illustrated in Figure 5(a). The testing system mainly consists of a loading device, a fixing assembly, piezoelectric sensing system and a signal acquisition system. The loading and fixing assembly includes a fixing frame composed of two steel plates with dimensions of 30 cm × 30 cm and a thickness of 4 cm, together with four high-strength threaded rods with a diameter of 30 mm, which are used to apply and constrain the axial load of the rock bolt.Figure 5.Experimental setup: (a) loading system; (b) PZT arrangement and signal acquisition system.

Experimental setup: (a) loading system; (b) PZT arrangement and signal acquisition system.

The rock bolt used in the experiments was an HRB500-grade anchor bolt selected in accordance with the Chinese national standard GB 1499.2–2024, with a nominal diameter of 20 mm. The right end of the bolt was anchored to the steel plate through a threaded connection. The loading system employed a hydraulic jack with a rated capacity of 20 tons, and the applied load was monitored and controlled in real time using a 30 tons spoke-type load cell coupled with a digital display unit.

The piezoelectric sensing system comprised four PZT patches, each with a thickness of 1 mm, labeled PZT A, PZT B, PZT C, and PZT D. These patches were uniformly bonded to the surface of the washer, as illustrated in Figure 5(b). PZT A functions as the exciter, while PZT B, PZT C, and PZT D serve as sensors, creating three signal propagation paths: AB, AC, and AD, as detailed in Table 1. The test loading was conducted in a staged manner. Before the formal test, an initial preload was applied to the anchor bolt to eliminate the effects of assembly gaps and initial interface loosening on the test results. Subsequently, starting from 0 kN, the load was increased in increments of approximately 10 kN until reaching a maximum load of 120 kN. After each target load was reached and stabilized, an ultrasonic response signal was recorded. To assess the repeatability of the test results, three loading tests were performed without altering the sensor layout, adhesion state, or acquisition parameters. After each loading, the load was fully released to the initial state before the next loading. 4.2. Energy calculation results and determination of saturated load

To reveal the relationship between signal energy and the load, the wavelet packet energy along different propagation paths was calculated, as shown in Figure 8. Overall, the signal energy varies with the anchor bolt load in a distinctly nonlinear manner, which can be divided into two stages. In the first stage, as the anchoring interface gradually compacts, the actual contact area increases, enhancing the interface’s transmission capability. This results in a decrease in reflected energy and a rapid decline in the received signal energy. In the second stage, as the load continues to increase, the rate of energy decline gradually diminishes and stabilizes, indicating that the interface contact state is approaching saturation and that further loading has a diminished effect on the wave propagation characteristics.Figure 8.Variation of wavelet-packet energy with load for different sensing paths.

Variation of wavelet-packet energy with load for different sensing paths.

To quantitatively assess the saturation behavior of energy characteristics, this paper calculates the energy decline gradient based on the original energy changes between adjacent load points to characterize the sensitivity of energy indicators to load variations. The energy decline gradient is defined as Gi=|ΔEi/ΔLi| , where ΔEi represents the change in wavelet packet energy between two adjacent load levels, and ΔLi denotes the corresponding load increment. A larger gradient value indicates greater sensitivity of the energy characteristics to load variations; conversely, when the gradient value approaches zero, it signifies that further increases in load cause minimal energy change, and the energy characteristics gradually reach a saturation state. Using Gi as the vertical coordinate and Lg,i=(Li+Li+1)/2 as the horizontal coordinate, the energy decline gradient with respect to load variation is shown in Figure 9. When the gradient approaches zero, the corresponding load is defined as the saturated load. The calculation results are presented in Table 4. From Figure 9 and Table 4, it is evident that for the AB path, the gradient values tend toward zero after the load exceeds approximately 50–60 kN. The saturated loads identified by the three loading tests are 51.40 kN, 58.25 kN, and 56.40 kN, with a mean ± standard deviation of 55.35 ± 3.54 kN. For the AC path, the saturated loads are 54.85 kN, 52.75 kN, and 54.15 kN, with a mean ± standard deviation of 53.92 ± 1.07 kN. For the AD path, the saturated loads are 60.95 kN, 58.65 kN, and 56.00 kN, with a mean ± standard deviation of 58.53 ± 2.48 kN. 4.3. Results of multiscale permutation entropy calculation

To enhance the identification capability of the rock bolt load state under high-load conditions, MPE was introduced to analyze the ultrasonic signals. According to the experimental setup described in Section 3, the length of each signal is approximately 95,000 sampling points (10.64 ms × 8.93 MHz), which satisfies the basic data length requirement for permutation entropy analysis. By fixing the embedding dimension m = 3 and the time delay at τ = 1, the influence of the scale factor s on the normalized permutation entropy (NPE) was investigated. The results are shown in Figure 10. It can be observed that, under different load levels, the overall trend of NPE with increasing scale factor is generally consistent. As the scale factor s increases, the NPE value gradually increases; when s > 5, the growth of NPE enters a stable plateau region. It should be noted that an excessively large scale factor leads to over-coarse-graining of the signal, which significantly shortens the effective sample length of the coarse-grained sequence and consequently affects the reliability of ordinal pattern statistics. Therefore, in the subsequent parameter sensitivity analysis, only scales within the plateau region, namely s = 5, 6, 7, and 8, were selected to further investigate the influence of the embedding dimension m and time delay τ on the monotonicity of permutation entropy features.Figure 10.The variation law of NPE with the scale factor.

The variation law of NPE with the scale factor.

To quantitatively evaluate the monotonic correlation between permutation entropy features and load level, the Spearman rank correlation coefficient was adopted as the evaluation index. For a given parameter combination (s, m, τ ), the obtained permutation entropy feature sequence is denoted as {NPEi}i=1n , and the corresponding load sequence is denoted as {Li}i=1n . First, rank transformation is performed on the two data sets to obtain the rank sequences Ri(NPE)=rank(NPEi) and Ri(L)=rank(Li) , respectively. The Spearman correlation coefficient is then defined as the Pearson correlation coefficient between the two rank sequences: (33) ρs=∑i=1n(Ri(PE)−R(PE))(Ri(L)−R(L))∑i=1n(Ri(PE)−R(PE))2∑i=1n(Ri(L)−R(L))2 where R(PE) and R(L) denote the mean values of the corresponding rank sequences. A larger absolute value of |ρs| indicates a more significant monotonic dependence between the permutation entropy feature and the load level.

Based on Equation (33), independent parameter sensitivity analyses were conducted for the three transmission paths (AB, AC, and AD). The Spearman correlation coefficients between the MPE and the load were systematically compared across different embedding dimensions (m = 3, 4, 5, 6, 7), time delays (τ = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7), and scale factors (s = 5, 6, 7, 8) to identify the optimal parameter combinations for each path. The resulting parameter sensitivity analysis heatmap is shown in Figure 11. Different colors in the heatmap represent the magnitude of ρ: red indicates a strong positive correlation (ρ = 1), blue indicates a strong negative correlation (ρ = -1), and green, close to zero, indicates a weak correlation. The results show that the maximum Spearman correlation coefficients for all three paths occurred with the same parameter combination: m = 3, τ = 1, s = 5, with correlation coefficients of 0.972, 0.952, and 0.964, respectively. Therefore, this paper adopts the parameter combination m = 3, τ = 1, s = 5 uniformly for the subsequent analysis.Figure 11.Parameter sensitivity analysis heat map.

Parameter sensitivity analysis heat map.

In summary, this study proposes a rock bolt load state identification method based on ultrasonic signal energy and MPE. The research findings indicate that this method holds significant engineering application potential for monitoring the force state of rock bolts and structural health. Unlike existing studies that rely solely on energy-based or entropy-based indicators, this approach establishes a phased feature recognition framework grounded in the ultrasonic response mechanism of the anchoring interface under varying loads. During the low-load stage, wavelet packet energy more effectively reflects the evolution of the contact state at the anchoring end interface. In the high-load stage, MPE further characterizes the nonlinear changes in the complexity of the time structure of the received signal. Consequently, this method creates a physical link between the feature indicators and the load state of the anchor bolt, enhancing the stability of identification results across the entire load range. Additionally, the multi-path PZT excitation and reception system mitigates the impact of local anomalies in any single path on the identification outcomes.

It should be noted that this method is better suited as a recalibratable monitoring framework rather than a universal empirical formula applicable to all types of anchor bolts and construction conditions. Changes in anchor bolt specifications, grouting materials, washer geometric dimensions, boundary conditions, or sensor layout can alter the ultrasonic propagation path, interface contact state, and energy dissipation mechanisms. Nevertheless, the phased identification approach established in this paper, along with the corresponding physical mechanism analysis framework, retains a degree of universality across different anchoring systems. For new working conditions, by collecting a small number of benchmark samples or known load conditions, the characteristic-load relationship can be re-established, and the phased determination thresholds can be updated, thereby enabling the re-calibration of the method’s parameters. Additionally, sensor degradation, aging of the bonding layer, variations in temperature and humidity, field noise, and changes in coupling conditions during long-term service may affect the stability of energy and entropy characteristics. Further research should verify the long-term applicability of this method by integrating sensor state self-diagnosis, temperature compensation, periodic benchmark updates, and multi-condition field testing.

5. Conclusions

This study addresses the problem of reliable identification of rock bolt load states and proposes a stage-wise load state identification method based on ultrasonic signal energy and MPE. The feasibility and stability of the proposed method were verified experimentally. The main conclusions are as follows:(1) A rock bolt load monitoring experimental platform was established, and a multi-path PZT-based excitation-reception system was constructed to achieve full-process ultrasonic monitoring of the anchorage interface load state. Experimental results demonstrate consistent signal evolution trends across different sensing paths, confirming the insensitivity of the proposed method to sensing path variations and its good engineering applicability.(2) In the low-load stage, ultrasonic signal energy extracted using wavelet packet decomposition exhibits high sensitivity to variations in rock bolt load and effectively reflects the overall evolution of the anchorage interface contact state, making it suitable for the identification of early-stage load conditions.(3) MPE characterizes the nonlinear evolution of ultrasonic signal temporal complexity with increasing load. In the high-load region, MPE exhibits a stable monotonic trend with load, indicating its strong capability to represent enhanced interface nonlinearity and randomness under high-load conditions.(4) Based on the complementary characteristics of energy-based and entropy-based features in different load intervals, a stage-wise load identification model was established, incorporating a discrimination coefficient to enable adaptive determination of load intervals. Blind test results show that the proposed model can achieve stable inversion of rock bolt load states under different test conditions, with good agreement between estimated and true loads, thereby validating the effectiveness and robustness of the method.(5) It should be noted that the core contribution of this paper lies in proposing a phased recognition framework and a feature selection strategy based on physical mechanisms, rather than establishing a fixed empirical model applicable to all operating conditions. This framework improves the stability of identification results across the entire load range by leveraging the sensitivity of energy characteristics to the signal during the low-load stage and the sensitivity of entropy characteristics to changes in signal complexity during the high-load stage. For different anchor bolt specifications, grouting materials, washer geometric dimensions, and sensor layout conditions, the feature-load relationship and phased thresholds still require recalibration. Future research will further consider the effects of sensor degradation, aging of the bonding layer, temperature and humidity variations, on-site noise, and long-term service conditions. It will also integrate temperature compensation, self-diagnosis of sensor status, and on-site multi-condition testing to further evaluate and improve the engineering applicability of this method.
