Key factors in mandibular third molar surgery: a comprehensive view of their prevalence and interrelationships
Authors: Mostafa Sorooshzadeh, Yousef Hoseyni, Fereshteh Goudarzi, Ali Jamali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study comprehensively assessed the factors affecting the complexity of wisdom tooth surgery, the prevalence of each factor class, and the relationships between them. This retrospective cross-sectional study analyzed 526 Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) scans of wisdom teeth. Variables examined included impaction positions using Winter’s and Pell and Gregory’s classifications, root counts, and frequency of contact with the inferior alveolar canal. The statistical analysis was performed using Pearson's chi-square test. The vertical type was the most prevalent impaction position (n = 189, 35.9%), followed by the mesioangular type. According to Pell and Gregory’s classification, the most frequent impactions were in positions A and 1. Of the cases studied, 234 involved mesial root contact, 239 had distal root contact, and 158 showed contact with both roots. In total, 345 cases (65.6%) exhibited contact between the third molar and the inferior alveolar nerve, most frequently in the apical third of the root (62.7%). The canal was most often positioned apical to the third molar (n = 415, 78.9%), and the highest ridge position was lingual (N = 234, 44.5%). These findings underscore the high prevalence of contact between impacted mandibular third molars and the inferior alveolar nerve, emphasizing the importance of meticulous preoperative planning to minimize the risk of nerve injury.

Introduction

Surgical removal of the impacted mandibular third molar is a common procedure in dental practice. The most serious potential complication associated with this surgery is injury to the inferior alveolar nerve (El Hadidi et al. 2024; He and Ruan 2024). The main risk factors for nerve damage include advanced age, depth of tooth embedding, angulation of tooth impaction, need for bone removal, distance of the tooth from the canal, and the position of the canal (lingual, buccal, between the roots) as mentioned in various studies (Kubota et al. 2023; Maqbool et al. 2024). Previous studies have reported that the incidence of nerve-related complications during lower third molar surgery ranges from 0.6% to 5.3%, with approximately 1% of patients experiencing permanent deficits (Leung et al. 2023).

Radiographic imaging is crucial for surgical assessment and planning as it provides valuable information about tooth location, root number and morphology, and the relationship of the tooth to adjacent structures. Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) images are preferred over panoramic imaging when planning mandibular third molar surgery due to their superior accuracy and enhanced ability to provide detailed three-dimensional views. This advanced imaging technique enables a more comprehensive assessment of the anatomy and potential complications, thereby improving surgical outcomes and patient safety (Issrani et al. 2021; Patel et al. 2020; Sklavos et al. 2021).

Accurate determination of the mandibular canal’s position in relation to the mandibular third molar across various demographic groups is crucial. This identification is essential for minimizing potential complications during surgical procedures and ensuring better patient outcomes. (Kubota et al. 2023; Leung et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2023). The placement and curvature of the inferior alveolar canal are associated with various patterns of mandibular growth and rotation. These variations are important considerations in preoperative planning for mandibular third molar extraction (Gu et al. 2018; Hashemipour et al. 2013).

Knowledge Gap: Although numerous studies have investigated individual anatomical or radiographic factors associated with mandibular third molar surgery, they have often examined these variables in isolation. There is a lack of comprehensive studies that assess the relationship among multiple factors, such as tooth position, angulation, depth, and the position of the inferior alveolar canal. Understanding how these variables interact is critical for improving risk prediction, preventing nerve injury, and enhancing surgical planning. Moreover, large-sample studies that explore these associations in an integrated manner are scarce. This study aims to address this gap by evaluating a broad range of related factors and investigating their interrelationships, with particular focus on how the position of the third molar correlates with the course of the inferior alveolar canal.

Method

This retrospective, cross-sectional study evaluated 526 CBCT scans of impacted mandibular third molars obtained over a two-year period from a private maxillofacial radiology center. All scans were obtained upon referral from surgeons and dentists, and no additional radiation exposure was administered for the purpose of this investigation. The Ethics Committee of Bandar Abbas University of Medical Sciences approved the study (IR.HUMS.REC.1403.065). The sample size was determined using established statistical procedures to ensure both the validity and reliability of the results. Established statistical methods, including a priori power analysis, were employed to estimate the necessary sample size. This involved evaluating the expected effect size and calculating the statistical power needed to confidently detect any significant differences. These steps reduced the risk of Type I and Type II errors, thereby strengthening the validity of the findings. The sample size was sufficient to support the conclusions drawn, contributing to the overall robustness of the study. The study included records of patients with CBCT images of impacted mandibular third molars, as well as images of the surrounding areas, such as the anterior border of the ramus and the entire buccolingual width of the alveolar ridge in the mandibular third molar area. Cases were excluded if there was evidence of a pathological lesion near the mandibular third molar, if the image quality was inadequate or affected by artifacts, or if there was a prior history of surgery or other pathology in the area under investigation. The patient's age and sex were recorded using software (On-Demand) information. all three-dimensional plans including axial plan, panoramic-like view (sagittal plan), and cross-sectional view were examined in all patients.

The statistical data was analyzed using SPSS 27.0 software. The chi-squared and Fisher's exact test were utilized in the study to examine the relationship between variables. CBCT 8200 Carestream imaging equipment from Carestream Dental LLC in Atlanta, Georgia, was used to obtain images. Results

In this study, 526 mandibular third molars were assessed: 331 (62.9%) from female patients and 195 (37.1%) from male patients. Participants’ ages ranged from 17 to 65 years, with a mean age of 28.9 year.

Types of impaction positions

Regarding the angulation of the impacted third molar, the most prevalent type was vertical, with 189 cases (35.9%), followed by the mesioangular type, with 180 cases (34.2%). The least prevalent type was the inverted, with only 2 cases (0.4%).

Prevalence in Pell&Gregory classification

In Pell and Gregory's classification, the most common impactions were associated with teeth in positions A and 1, while the least common were associated with positions C and 3. The highest occurrence of impaction position and Pell and Gregory classification was observed in teeth with simultaneous vertical and A1 positioning, while the lowest occurrence was associated with inverted and A1 positions. There was a significant association between the impaction position of teeth and both the Pell and Gregory categories (Chi-square p-value = 0.000)(Fisher exact: p-value = 0.000).

Developed and undeveloped roots and their contact with the inferior alveolar nerve

Out of 397 teeth with developed roots, 280 of them (72.3%) were in contact with the inferior-alveolar nerve. Additionally, 129 teeth (24.5% of all examined teeth) with undeveloped roots, and 65 of these (approximately 50%) were found to be in contact with the nerve. A statistically significant association was observed between root development and nerve contact(p-value of 0.000).

The number of roots and their contact with the inferior alveolar canal

In the survey, it was observed that 51 single-rooted teeth (9.7%), 459 two-rooted teeth (87.3%), 12 three-rooted teeth (2.3%), and 4 four-rooted teeth (0.8%) were present. Nerve contact was observed in 58.8% of single-rooted teeth, 65.8% of two-rooted teeth, 83.3% of three-rooted teeth, and 75% of four-rooted teeth.

Tooth contact with root type

315 nerve contacts were observed involving two or more rooted teeth. Of these, 234 contacts involved mesial roots, and 239 involved distal roots. In 158 cases, the nerve had contact with both mesial and distal roots. The inferior-alveolar nerve had the most contact with a tooth exhibiting mesioangular impaction with the mesial root (106 cases), followed by contact with a tooth exhibiting mesioangular position with the distal root (101 cases). A significant relationship has been found between the position of the impacted mandibular third molar and the nerve's contact with the root(s) (mesial or distal) (p-value(mesial) = 0.009, p-value(distal) = 0.015, p-value(both) = 0.013).

Level of the contact position of the inferior alveolar nerve in relation to the tooth

345 cases (65.6%) of nerve contact with the third molar were observed. The highest prevalence was contact with the 1/3 apical region of the tooth (62.7%), followed by the 1/3 middle region (2.7%) and 1/3 cervical region (0.2%).

The position of the inferior-alveolar nerve relative to the tooth

Most canal positions were apical to the third molar teeth, with 415 cases (78.9%). This was followed by buccal (13.5%), lingual (6.7%), and positions between the roots (1%).

The position of the inferior-alveolar canal relative to the ridge

The canal's highest position in terms of the ridge was lingual in 234 cases (44.5%), with 205 cases (39%) of the 1/3 middle type and 87 cases (16.5%) of the buccal type.

Prevalence of accessory nerve canals and their contacts with the inferior alveolar canal

In 88 cases (16.7%), accessory nerve canals were observed, of which 33 cases (37.5% of cases with secondary roots) were in contact with the third mandibular molar.

The significance of demographic variables (age and sex) with study variables

There was a significant relationship between the Pell and Gregory classification categories and the age of the patients. In all age ranges, the incidence of A and 1 was the highest, while the incidence of C and 3 was the lowest (P-Value = 0.028, P-Value = 0.024). The relationship between age and the growth status of impacted molar (p-value:0.003) and angulation (p-value:0.001) of impacted third molar teeth was found to be significant. In younger age groups, the apex was often incomplete, while in older age groups, the apex was generally closed. The mesioangular position was most common in younger age groups, while the vertical position was more prevalent in older age groups. The inverted position was the least common in all age groups. There was no significant relationship between age groups and the contact of the inferior-alveolar nerve with the root of the tooth (p-value = 0.310). Additionally, no significant relationship was found between the sex of the examined subjects and the state of the eruption of third mandibular molar teeth (P-value = 0.682) or the presence of an accessory nerve canal (p-value = 0.856). Furthermore, there was no significant relationship between sex and Pell and Gregory classification categories (p-value:0.493, p-value:0.077). In this study, a significant relationship was found between sex and tooth-nerve contact. Women had a significantly higher amount of tooth-nerve contact compared to men, with an odds ratio (OR) of 2.18 (95% confidence interval (CI): [1.05, 4.52]). This indicates that women are approximately twice as likely to experience nerve contact associated with impacted mandibular third molars than men (P-Value = 0.029). Additionally, mesioangular positions were more common in men, while distoangular and vertical positions were more common in women. The inverted position was only found in men. The horizontal position was relatively equal in both sexes. The relationship between sex and impaction position was also found to be significant (p-value = 0.029). Furthermore, there was a significant relationship between the sex of the study subjects and the position of the inferior-alveolar canal (according to the tooth) (p-value = 0.001) and the position of the inferior-alveolar canal (according to the ridge) (p-value = 0.000). We found that the apical position of the nerve compared to the tooth was more common in men, while buccal, lingual, and positions between two roots were more common in women. Additionally, the middle and lingual position of the nerve relative to the ridge was more common in men, while the buccal position of the nerve relative to the ridge was more common in women.

Relation between different positions of the inferior-alveolar with impaction position

The most common types of tooth positions, based on the level of contact between the nerve and the root, were related to the apical contact of the tooth with the mesioangular position with the inferior-alveolar nerve (123 cases) and the apical contact of the tooth with the vertical position with the nerve (109 cases). Contact with the 1/3 cervical region of the root was observed only in one case of a tooth with horizontal impaction. When the mandibular canal was positioned inferiorly relative to the impacted third molar, the nerve exhibited more contact with the tooth compared to other positional relationships, and this difference was statistically significant(p-value = 0.000). Also, the relationship between the types of position of impaction and the contact location of the inferior-alveolar nerve with the root was not significant. (p-value = 0.702) (Table 1). The correlation between the position of the inferior-alveolar canal in relation to the ridge and its position in relation to the teeth was statistically significant (p-value = 0.000).

Discussion

In surgeries involving impacted mandibular third molars, there is a risk of damaging surrounding structures such as the inferior alveolar canal. Numerous studies have compared the accuracy of panoramic and tomographic radiographs in detecting the proximity or contact of the inferior alveolar nerve with the third molar (He and Ruan 2024; Maqbool et al. 2024; Santos et al. 2022). However, no study has comprehensively examined all the variables related to the position of the tooth and the canal, which are crucial during mandibular third molar surgery. This comprehensive examination is important for increasing surgeons' understanding of the frequency of different anatomic variations. In this study, CBCT images were used to evaluate the variables related to the increased risk of damage to the inferior alveolar nerve due to proximity and contact with the impacted third molar. Several studies investigating these variables have been reviewed. Telles Arauju's systematic review (Araujo et al. 2019) indicates that CBCT imaging does not alter the surgical approach for third molar extraction compared to panoramic radiography. However, CBCT becomes particularly important when panoramic imaging reveals signs that suggest proximity of the roots to the mandibular canal (MC). 3D imaging can serve as a valuable supplementary tool, offering additional insights into the relationship between the lower third molar and the mandibular canal. This extra information can enhance the surgeon's confidence and help prevent any harmful movements during surgery that could lead to neurosensory complications involving the inferior alveolar nerve (IAN).

Impaction positions

Pangnoush, Nyachhyun, Gu, and Chaudhary (Chaudhary et al. 2020; Gu et al. 2018; Nyachhyon et al. 2022; Panjnoosh et al. 2014) studied the angulation of impacted third molars. They found that mesioangular and vertical positions were the most common, while the inverted position was the least common, which is consistent with our study's findings. Similarly, Liu and colleagues also studied the position of impacted third molars and reported that mesioangular and horizontal orientations were more common. The difference in Liu's study could be due to the smaller sample size they examined.

Prevalence of impaction according to Pell&Gregory classification

In this study, the Pell & Gregory classification was evaluated, which is based on the position of the third molar relative to the ramus and the adjacent second molar. A1 impaction was the most common. In epidemiological studies by Jaron and Hashemipour (Hashemipour et al. 2013; Jaroń and Trybek 2021) A2 prevalence was highest, while Santos et al. (Santos et al. 2022) reported the highest prevalence of B2. Our study's results were consistent with the studies of Jaron and Hashemipour in this regard. The differences in the results of the studies may be due to genetic and endemic differences among the people and the amount of data examined.

Prevalence of tooth-nerve contact

In the current study, 65.6% of impacted third molars were in contact with the inferior-alveolar nerve. This percentage varied widely in other studies, ranging from 7.3% to 86.5%. Specifically, Pangnoush, Nyachhyun, Chaudhary, and Liu (Chaudhary et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2023; Nyachhyon et al. 2022; Panjnoosh et al. 2014) reported nerve contact prevalence rates of 81.7%, 77.3%, 86.5%, and 54% respectively. Gu's (Gu et al. 2018) study, however, reported a much lower rate of 7.3% in the Chinese population. The substantial difference between Gu's study and the others could be attributed to genetic variations and differing growth patterns among the populations studied.

The position of the inferior alveolar nerve relative to the tooth

In recent studies by Liu and Gu (Gu et al. 2018; Liu et al. 2023), they investigated the connection between impacted third molars and the inferior alveolar canal. They found that the most common position of the impacted tooth in relation to the canal was apical (below the tooth canal), followed by buccal, lingual, and inter radicular canal positions. Chaudhary and Pangnoush (Chaudhary et al. 2020; Panjnoosh et al. 2014) reported that the highest prevalence of impacted third molar positions with respect to the inferior-alveolar canal was buccal, followed by lingual to the tooth. Nyachhun and colleagues (Nyachhyon et al. 2022) also found that the highest prevalence of the canal position was buccal, followed by apical. The results of studies on the position and location of impacted teeth relative to the canal are relatively consistent. Additionally, Liu et al. (Liu et al. 2023) assessed the position of the inferior-alveolar nerve in relation to the tooth, and found that the highest prevalence was related to the nerve being inferior to a horizontally positioned tooth, and the lowest prevalence was related to the nerve being positioned between the roots of a vertically positioned tooth. In our study, the highest prevalence was related to the nerve being apical to a vertically positioned tooth, and the lowest prevalence was related to the nerve being positioned between the roots of a vertically positioned tooth. They found that the relationship between different tooth positions and the position of the nerve relative to the tooth was not significant, which contradicts the findings of our study.

Level of the contact position of the inferior alveolar nerve in relation to the tooth

The study evaluated the contact level of the inferior alveolar nerve with the apical, middle, and cervical thirds of the root of the impacted third molar, which had not been investigated in other studies. The contact area was found to be significantly in the 1/3 apical part of the root, with a very low probability of nerve contact with the 1/3 cervical region of the root, and this was statistically significant. Additionally, the study investigated the contact with the mesial and distal root(s) or with both teeth with two or more roots, an aspect that previous studies did not assess. In about half of the nerve-to-tooth contact cases, both mesial and distal root(s) were involved, while in the other half, only one root(s) was in contact. The current study also examined the relationship between the sex of the subjects and the position and angulation of the canal, which was found to be statistically significant. This may be due to differences in jaw size between men and women, leading to variations in the available space for the eruption of impacted third molars in the mandible, thereby affecting the position of the impacted tooth relative to the inferior alveolar canal.

In this study, various factors associated with the impacted mandibular third molar and the inferior alveolar nerve were assessed to predict the difficulty of surgical extraction of the mandibular third molar. The relationship between these factors was also assessed. Pell & Gregory's classification and Winter's positions were examined to determine the difficulty of extracting impacted teeth, and a statistically significant relationship between subgroups was found. Additionally, the relationship between Winter's positions and the position of the inferior-alveolar canal in relation to the tooth as a determinant of the risk of nerve damage during preoperative surgery was explored, which was also statistically significant. Most previous studies had focused on these variables individually without determining the significance of their relationship. The present study aimed to comprehensively evaluate various factors related to the relationship between impacted third molars and the inferior alveolar nerve, highlighting the statistical significance of these variables. This demonstrates the significance of the research.

Our study's limitations include that all images were sourced from a private referral imaging center. This sampling approach likely resulted in an overrepresentation of more complex or symptomatic cases, which may not accurately reflect the broader population with impacted mandibular third molars. Consequently, caution should be exercised when interpreting these results, as they might not be universally applicable to all patients with similar conditions. Recognizing this limitation underscores the importance of further research in more diverse settings to validate our findings and explore variations in clinical presentations.

Conclusion

The study found a high prevalence of contact between the impacted mandibular third molar and the inferior alveolar nerve, which is typically positioned below the impacted tooth. The study used CBCT images to perform a three-dimensional evaluation of the types of relationships between the impacted third molar and the inferior alveolar nerve. The goal was to increase surgeons' awareness of the various factors that can affect the difficulty of mandibular third molar surgery and to emphasize the importance of thorough examination before surgery to prevent damage to the vital structures of the inferior alveolar nerve.
