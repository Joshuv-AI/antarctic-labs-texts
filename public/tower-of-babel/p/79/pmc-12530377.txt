Efficacy of non-invasive brain stimulation combined with constraint-induced movement therapy on upper extremity function in patients who had a stroke: protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials
Authors: Chaoran Gao, Han Zhang, Kang He, Li Zhang, Liuxia Wu, Qiang Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionStroke remains a leading cause of death and long-term disability worldwide, with the majority of survivors experiencing functional impairments, particularly affecting the upper extremities (UEs). Although clinically widespread rehabilitation methods, such as physical and occupational therapy, provide some benefits, their efficacy in restoring UE function is often limited. Constraint-induced movement therapy (CIMT) is widely recognised as one of the most effective physical therapy interventions for improving motor outcomes in the paretic UE. Meanwhile, non-invasive brain stimulation (NIBS) has demonstrated promising results in facilitating neuroplasticity and enhancing UE recovery. The combination of NIBS and CIMT (NIBS-CIMT) may offer synergistic benefits, further promoting functional improvements. This meta-analysis aims to evaluate the efficacy of NIBS-CIMT in enhancing UE function in patients who had a stroke based on evidence from randomised controlled trials (RCTs).MethodsPublished RCTs will be included if the experimental group received NIBS-CIMT in adult patients who had a stroke. Five electronic databases—Cochrane Library, Scopus, PubMed, Web of Science and Embase—will be systematically searched from inception to June 2025. The outcome of interest is UE motor recovery. A comprehensive search of grey literature will also be conducted. Two independent reviewers will perform study selection, data extraction and quality assessment. The risk of bias will be evaluated using the Cochrane Collaboration’s Risk of Bias tool. The quality of evidence will be assessed according to the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation framework.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required for this study, as it involves the review of previously published data. Introduction

Background

Stroke is the second leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for approximately 6.5 million deaths annually.1 Among survivors, 75%–80% experience some degrees of functional impairment.2 Beyond its impact on physical health, stroke imposes substantial burdens on families and healthcare systems.

Upper extremity (UE) and hand dysfunction are major contributors to poststroke disability and loss of independence.3 These impairments encompass not only paralysis but also sensory deficits, reduced coordination, increased muscle tone and tremors, all of which hinder the performance of essential daily activities, such as dressing, eating and toileting.4,6 Deficits in fine motor skills, such as difficulty buttoning clothes or writing, further compromise autonomy and quality of life and may result in psychological distress and social isolation.7 In severe cases, patients become highly dependent on caregivers, thereby increasing long-term care demands and associated healthcare costs.8 Although significant advances have been made in stroke rehabilitation, recovery of UE function remains particularly slow and often incomplete. Many patients require prolonged therapy over 6–12 months, with progressively diminishing improvements over time.9

Clinically widespread rehabilitation methods, such as physical therapy (PT) and occupational therapy (OT), often yield limited and variable outcomes, typically requiring substantial time investment while providing only modest functional gains. These limitations underscore the urgent need for more effective and personalised rehabilitation strategies. In recent years, non-invasive brain stimulation (NIBS) and constraint-induced movement therapy (CIMT) have emerged as promising interventions in the field of stroke rehabilitation.

CIMT has been demonstrated to be an effective rehabilitation approach for addressing a range of impairments in stroke survivors.10 11 It is widely regarded as one of the most effective PT interventions for improving motor outcomes in the paretic UE.12 13 The theoretical foundation of CIMT dates back more than a century.14 15 In 1909, Munk observed that non-human primates could use an impaired limb if its use was required. This observation was further supported by Ogden and Franz in 1917, who noted that monkeys with pyramidal tract lesions regained use of the affected limb when the unaffected limb was restrained.12 These early findings suggested that motor deficits might result more from disuse than from true paralysis. The concept of ‘forced use’ was later revisited by Knapp et al,16 who demonstrated that behavioural interventions could overcome learnt non-use, thus laying the groundwork for modern CIMT. Despite its clinical success, the precise neurophysiological mechanisms underlying CIMT-induced improvements remain poorly understood.

Recent advances in technology and neuroscience have enabled the use of NIBS techniques in the treatment of patients who had a stroke. These include transcranial magnetic stimulation, transcranial direct current stimulation, transcranial alternating current stimulation, transcranial ultrasound stimulation, among others.17 NIBS has emerged as a promising intervention for promoting UE motor recovery in individuals following stroke,18,20 as well as for enhancing performance in the activities of daily living.21

Building on the aforementioned background, the integration of NIBS with CIMT—referred to as NIBS-CIMT—holds the potential to further enhance rehabilitation outcomes. In recent years, an increasing number of studies have investigated whether this combined approach could provide a more comprehensive and effective strategy for promoting recovery in patients who had a stroke. However, while comprehensive reviews, such as the meta-analysis by Ahmed et al,19 21 have valuably compared the efficacy of various NIBS techniques combined with a broad range of rehabilitation therapies for UE recovery, a critical evidence gap remains regarding the specific efficacy of NIBS-CIMT, a distinct and highly effective rehabilitation approach with a unique theoretical foundation. To the best of our knowledge, no meta-analysis has yet specifically evaluated the effects of NIBS-CIMT on UE motor rehabilitation. Therefore, the present study aimed to conduct a meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) to assess the efficacy of NIBS-CIMT in improving UE motor function in individuals following stroke.

Objective

Based on this background, the overall objective of this study was to evaluate the efficacy of NIBS-CIMT as a rehabilitation strategy for patients who had a stroke. We hypothesised that a systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs would yield robust and comprehensive evidence supporting the efficacy of NIBS-CIMT in stroke rehabilitation.

Methods

This protocol will be reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) Protocols guidelines and was registered on the PROSPERO platform (CRD420251083201). The study commenced on 29 June 2025 and is expected to be completed by 29 August 2025.

Criteria for inclusion and exclusion

Participants

Participants must meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) adults aged over 18 years; (2) diagnosed with a first-ever stroke; (3) disease duration of less than 12 months and (4) currently in a stable or recovery phase.

Exclusion criteria include patients with major comorbidities, such as malignant tumours or organ failure.

Interventions

CIMT that is combined with NIBS and implemented in adult patients who had a stroke will be included. However, multicomponent interventions—which integrate NIBS and CIMT with other active rehabilitation modalities (ie, robotic therapy and virtual reality)—will be excluded to isolate the specific effect of NIBS-CIMT.

Controls

Comparator will be considered as follows: (1) usual care, including medication management, diet, rehabilitation care and complication prevention; (2) clinically widespread rehabilitation methods (such as PT and OT); (3) NIBS or CIMT alone and (4) Sham therapy.

Outcomes measures

The primary outcome will be UE motor function measured by the Fugl–Meyer Assessment—UE. Secondary outcomes will include other measurements related to UE motor recovery. Specifically, the measurements mainly include: (1) outcomes related to impairments in UE functions and structures, such as UE range of motion (ROM (ie, goniometer), UE muscle strength (ie, manual muscle testing), UE spasticity (ie, modified Ashworth scale) and UE stroke recovery stage (ie, Brunnstrom stages of stroke recovery for UE); (2) outcomes related to activity limitation, such as daily activities (ie, functional independence measure), hand dexterity (ie, box and block test), arm and hand motor ability (ie, action research arm test, wolf motor function test and manual function test) and hand motor ability (ie, Jebsen hand function test) and (3) outcomes related to participation restrictions in life situations, such as quality of life (ie, short-form 36 health survey) or UE use in daily life (ie, motor activity log).

Study type

Only RCTs will be considered for inclusion. Observational studies, case series, qualitative and laboratory studies and uncontrolled trials will be excluded.

Data sources and search strategy

The professional search will use the medical subject headings and free words. The search items will include ‘Stroke’, ‘Apoplexy’, ‘Cerebrovascular Accident’, ‘CVA’, ‘Brain Vascular Accident’, ‘Subarachnoid Hemorrhage’, ‘Non-invasive Brain Stimulation’, ‘Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation’, ‘Transcranial Direct Current Stimulation’, ‘Transcranial Alternating Current Stimulation’, ‘Theta-Burst Stimulation’, and ‘Transcutaneous Vagus Nerve Stimulation’, ‘Transcranial Ultrasound Stimulation’, ‘Constraint Induced Movement Therapy’ and ‘Randomized Controlled Trial’. We will search electronic databases to identify published studies, including the Cochrane Library, Scopus, PubMed, Web of Science and Embase. The retrieval time will be from inception to June 2025. In addition to the database search, the grey literature will be scanned to identify additional eligible studies.

Study selection

Two reviewers will independently conduct the literature search and study selection in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. The selection process involved three stages: (1) removal of duplicate records; (2) screening of titles and abstracts for relevance and (3) full-text assessment of the remaining articles based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Data extraction

A standardised data extraction form will be developed through consensus among all researchers. Two reviewers will then independently and systematically review the full texts of the included studies, extracting relevant data using the standardised form. Extracted information will include authorship, year of publication, sample size, patient characteristics (eg, age and sex), stroke recovery stage, type of NIBS used, intervention frequency and duration as well as outcome measures. Both dichotomous and continuous outcomes will be recorded in a predesigned Excel spreadsheet. All extracted data will be cross checked, and any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion among the reviewers. Dichotomous outcomes will be appropriately coded, while continuous outcomes will be directly entered into the statistical software for analysis without further transformation.

Assessment of risk of bias

The risk of bias in the included trials will be assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration’s Risk of Bias tool, focusing on the following domains: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and healthcare providers, blinding of outcome assessors, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting and other potential sources of bias—such as significant baseline differences between groups or discrepancies in intervention duration.22 Review Manager software (RevMan, V.5.4), developed by The Cochrane Collaboration, will be used for this assessment. The tool provides a structured framework for evaluating each domain, with predefined criteria categorising the risk of bias as low (‘yes’), high (‘no’) or unclear (indicating insufficient information or uncertainty). Two reviewers will independently conduct the risk of bias assessment, and any disagreements will be resolved through discussion with a third reviewer.

Data analysis

Outcomes will be included in the meta-analysis if they are reported in at least three studies. Statistical analyses and forest plot generation for result visualisation will be conducted using Review Manager (RevMan, V.5.4). Relevant statistical data—including means, SDs before and after the intervention and sample sizes—will be extracted and entered into RevMan for analysis. For studies reporting only pre- and postintervention means and SDs, the mean and SD of the change scores will be calculated using the following formulae:

SDchange=Meanchange=Meanpost−MeanpreSDpre2+ SDpost2− 2 × r × SDpre× SDpost

where r is the correlation coefficient between pre- and postintervention values. If the correlation was not available in the original publication, a value of r=0.5 was assumed according to the recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions.23

For studies reporting medians and IQRs, the data were converted to means and SDs using the following methods: (1) the median was treated as the mean and (2) the SD was estimated as IQR divided by 1.35.24 Continuous outcomes were analysed using the mean difference (MD) when the same measurement tool was applied across studies or standardised MD when different instruments were used. All estimates were reported with corresponding 95% CIs. The inverse variance method was employed for the analysis of continuous variables.

Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using the χ2 test and quantified using the I² statistic, with thresholds of 25%, 50% and 75% indicating low, moderate and high heterogeneity, respectively.25 A fixed-effects model was used for meta-analysis when heterogeneity was low. In cases of substantial heterogeneity (I² > 50%), a random-effects model was applied to provide more conservative estimates.

Handling missing and incomplete data

In cases where essential data are missing, the corresponding authors of the primary studies will be contacted to obtain the relevant information, where feasible. If the authors are unreachable or unable to provide the requested data, no imputation will be performed. Instead, the potential impact of missing data on the review findings will be addressed and discussed in the discussion section.

Assessment of publication bias

Egger’s regression test will be employed to assess the potential presence of publication bias. A two-tailed p value of less than 0.05 will be considered indicative of possible publication bias.26 Egger regression test will be performed by Stata 17.

Subgroup analysis

In cases where substantial heterogeneity is detected, subgroup analyses will be conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. These analyses will primarily be structured around the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, and Study design (PICOS) framework.

Sensitivity analysis

Where data allow, sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the robustness of the review findings, particularly in relation to outlier studies with disproportionately high weight. This will involve iteratively rerunning the meta-analysis while excluding individual studies one at a time to evaluate their influence on the overall effect estimate. Additional sensitivity analyses will be performed by excluding studies with a high risk of bias or other methodological limitations. This approach will help determine whether the pooled estimates—and, thus, the review conclusions—are unduly influenced by any single study or subgroup of studies. All sensitivity analyses will be performed using Stata V.17.

Assessment of certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence will be assessed by two independent reviewers using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach. GRADE is the most widely accepted framework for rating the quality of evidence in systematic reviews and clinical practice guidelines. It classifies evidence into four levels: ‘high’, ‘moderate’, ‘low’ or ‘very low’ based on explicit criteria.27 Factors that may downgrade the certainty of evidence include the risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias. Conversely, factors, such as a large effect size, evidence of a dose–response relationship or plausible confounding, that would reduce an observed effect may lead to upgrading the certainty rating.28 In the event of discrepancies in the certainty ratings among the reviewers, a third reviewer will be sought for consultation.

Ethical approval and dissemination

As this study is based solely on previously published data, ethical approval is not required. The findings will be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal.
