Progression of interstitial lung abnormalities and its impact on mortality in patients with lung cancer resection
Authors: Ruolin Mao, Haoyun Zhang, Qing Chang, Yilong Teng, Yanfen Ni, Jiani Chen, Mengnan Li, Ning Xu, Hai Zhang, Yuqing Chen, Jianqi Sun, Kian Fan Chung, Elisabetta A. Renzoni, Yi Lu, Huaping Dai, Feng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInterstitial lung abnormalities (ILAs) are incidental abnormal lung findings on computed tomography that can co-exist with lung cancer, but risk factors for their progression and impact on survival after lung cancer resection remain unclear. This study aimed to identify risk factors for ILA progression, develop a radiomics-based model to predict it, and evaluate the association between ILA progression and mortality.MethodsPatients with ILAs who underwent lung cancer resection in Shanghai Chest Hospital between January 2016 and May 2019 were retrospectively selected and divided into three subcategories at baseline: non-subpleural ILAs, subpleural non-fibrotic ILAs, and subpleural fibrotic ILAs; and three subcategories during follow-up: improved ILAs, unchanged ILAs, and progressive ILAs. Multivariate logistic regression was used to identify clinical and laboratory risk factors associated with ILA progression, and radiomics-based machine learning models were independently constructed to predict ILA progression using baseline CT imaging features. Survival data were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves and Cox proportional hazards regression.ResultsThere were 1363 ILA cases among 10,295 patients who underwent primary lung cancer resection, with a proportion of 13.24%. After 2- and 4-year follow-up, the progression rates of ILAs were 9.86% (65/659) and 10.19% (43/422), respectively. Subpleural fibrotic ILAs (2-year follow-up: OR = 6.078, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.633–14.028, P < 0.001; 4-year follow-up: OR = 3.339, 95% CI: 1.085–10.272, P = 0.035), and radiotherapy (OR = 13.595, 95% CI: 4.540–40.710, P < 0.001; OR = 11.496, 95% CI: 2.864–46.141, P = 0.001) were risk factors for ILA progression. Using radiomics features extracted from baseline CT, the AutoGluon machine learning model demonstrated high performance in predicting ILA progression among patients with confirmed ILAs (for all ILAs, mean area under curve value: 0.790, accuracy: 0.801 ± 0.049). The all-cause mortality rates at 2- and 4-year follow-up were 2.11% (27/1281) and 4.26% (53/1245), respectively. Both subpleural fibrotic (log-rank χ² = 23.910, P < 0.001) and progressive ILA groups (log-rank χ² = 26.098, P < 0.001) had higher mortality rates compared with non-subpleural, subpleural non-fibrotic ILA group and improved, unchanged ILA group; and progressive ILAs were a risk factor for death (HR = 2.824, 95% CI: 1.065–7.483, P = 0.037).ConclusionsILA progression occurs in about 10% of patients after lung cancer resection and is associated with higher mortality. Subpleural fibrotic ILAs and radiotherapy are key risk factors for progression. Radiomics features effectively predict ILA progression, enabling early identification of high-risk patients.

Introduction

Interstitial lung abnormalities (ILAs) are incidental findings on computed tomography (CT) that are characterized by non-dependent areas of increased lung density affecting over 5% of any lung zone.1 These abnormalities include ground-glass opacities, reticular patterns, diffuse centrilobular nodularity, non-emphysematous cysts, traction bronchiectasis, and honeycombing. According to the Fleischner Society position paper, ILAs can be categorized into three subclasses: non-subpleural ILAs, subpleural non-fibrotic ILAs, and subpleural fibrotic ILAs.1 Some ILAs are considered to represent an early stage of interstitial lung disease (ILD),2,3 and they have been associated with respiratory symptoms, disease progression, reduced lung function, and increased mortality.4

ILAs frequently coexist with lung cancer and adversely impact surgical outcomes. Recognized as an independent risk factor for lung cancer, ILAs are commonly detected in conjunction with pulmonary malignancies. Current evidence reports the presence of ILAs in 6% of patients undergoing lung nodule resection,2 with prevalence rates ranging from 3.9% to 21.7% across all tumor stages in patients with lung cancer.5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Compared with patients without ILAs, those with ILAs experience significantly higher rates of postoperative complications following curative surgery10,11 and demonstrate elevated mortality rates.12

The radiographic progression of ILAs exhibits considerable variability, being influenced by differences in study populations and follow-up durations. Reported progression rates range from 20% over 2 years to 80% over a median follow-up of 8 years.13 A recent meta-analysis revealed pooled progression rates of 64.2% and 31.0% for longer (≥4.5 years) and shorter (<4.5 years) follow-up periods, respectively.14 Notably, a study focusing on lung cancer patients with ILAs demonstrated progression in 50% of equivocal ILA cases and 75.8% (25/33) of definite ILA cases over a median follow-up of 3.6 years.15

Despite these findings, the progression patterns, mortality outcomes, and risk factors for ILA progression in large cohorts of patients undergoing lung cancer resection remain insufficiently characterized. To address these knowledge gaps, our study investigated the risk factors influencing the progression of ILAs and mortality in patients who have undergone lung cancer resection. Our analysis comprised two parts: (1) changes in ILAs and risk factors for ILA progression; and (2) survival conditions in patients with different ILA subcategories and ILA change status, and the risk factors for death. Furthermore, we developed a radiomics-based prediction model using baseline CT-derived radiomic features to predict subsequent ILA progression, potentially enabling early intervention and optimized management strategies for this group of patients.

Methods

Study design and subjects

The institutional review board of Shanghai Chest Hospital approved this retrospective study (No. IS23054) and granted a waiver for the requirement for informed consent. We enrolled patients who underwent curative lung cancer resection at our institution between January 2016 and May 2019. The inclusion criteria were: high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT)-confirmed ILAs, treatment-naïve status (no prior anti-tumor therapy or radiotherapy), and availability of preoperative HRCT performed at our facility.

Patients with any of the following were excluded: pre-existing diagnosis of ILD, history of thoracic surgery, concurrent or previous malignancy in other organ systems, non-primary lung cancer diagnosis, benign pulmonary nodules, incomplete medical records, stage IV lung cancer, and extensive small cell lung cancer.

We assessed ILA progression and patient mortality at 2 and 4 years after lung cancer resection. Chest CT, ILA diagnosis, and ILA progression

HRCT scan images were obtained using a Philips Ingenuity CT 128 or a Philips iCT 256 (Philips Medical Systems, Amsterdam, the Netherlands). The scanning parameters used were: 120 kVp, 250 mA, pitch 0.984, field of view 400 mm × 400 mm, reconstruction layer thickness of 5 mm, reconstruction interval of 5 mm, filter function C, image matrix 512 × 512 pixels or 1024 × 1024 pixels, and thin-layer reconstruction (0.625–1.250 mm) using standard reconstruction algorithms. The lung window level was −520 Hounsfield units (HU), with a window width of 1450 HU. At baseline, three subcategories of ILAs (non-subpleural ILAs, subpleural non-fibrotic ILAs, and subpleural fibrotic ILAs) were defined according to the position paper by the Fleischner Society.1 Additionally, three subcategories of ILA change during follow-up were defined as follows: progressive ILAs defined patients with either an increase in lung area affected by previously noted abnormalities or the new appearance of at least one such abnormality; improved ILAs indicated patients with a reduction in affected lung area; and unchanged ILAs defined patients with no significant radiologic change in either the distribution or nature of abnormalities compared with baseline. The degree of ILA involvement was scored based on the number of lung zones with abnormalities, on a scale of 1 (one lung zone involved) to 6 (all lung zones involved).1,16

All chest HRCT scans were independently evaluated by two senior radiologists and one senior respiratory physician, each with ≥10 years of experience in ILD. The reviewers were blinded to all patient clinical information and prior radiological reports. Any discrepant interpretations were resolved through consensus review.

Clinical data collection and assessment

Comprehensive multidimensional clinical data were collected for all participants, including demographic characteristics (age, gender), smoking history, preoperative laboratory values, pulmonary function tests, postoperative histopathological findings, tumor staging,17 and adjuvant treatment regimens. Survival outcomes were ascertained through telephone follow-up interviews, with final mortality data collected up to December 2023.

Radiomics studies of ILA progression

Preoperative imaging data were collected for all patients in the progressive ILAs group defined according to the results of the last chest HRCT follow-up. Additionally, 100 patients who did not have ILA progression were randomly selected through simple random sampling (random number generator method) for the collection of preoperative imaging data. To obtain the most accurate analysis data possible, image sets with a resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels and slice thickness of ≤1.0 mm were finally selected. The patients selected for the radiomics analysis were representative of the overall cohort, with no significant differences in key clinical findings between the radiomics subset and the full study population. Region-of-interest (ROI) placement involved two steps. First, lung segmentation was performed using an existing pre-trained neural network, U-net (R231). This U-net (R231) model was trained on a large and diverse dataset encompassing a wide range of visual variability, and it performed individual slice segmentation, extracting right and left lung separately. This first lung segmentation step was followed by manual segmentation of image data with different subcategories by the specialized physicians, with the segmentation method differing between the different subcategories. Considering that non-subpleural ILA subcategory lesions are mostly diffuse in both lungs, the procedure was fine-tuned with the pre-segmentation results from U-net (R231), including manual deletion of lung cancer lesions and significant non-ILA parts. For localized subpleural non-fibrotic ILA and subpleural fibrotic ILA subcategories, the coatings were reacquired based on manual segmentation according to the imaging findings. Furthermore, the two segments separating the left and right lungs were combined into one ILA segmentation, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

A total of 1218 radiomic features were extracted from the CT images using the PyRadiomics open-source library (version 3.0.1, https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io/). To balance deep representation capabilities with model generalizability, this study specifically targeted the radiological characteristics of ILA by focusing on Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) and Wavelet scale spaces, while excluding noise-sensitive non-linear transformations (e.g., Square and Logarithm) to mitigate overfitting. The feature set was categorized as follows: (1) Original features (n = 100): Extracted from unfiltered images, including 14 shape-based features, 18 first-order statistics, and 68 texture features. The texture descriptors comprised Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) (n = 22), Gray Level Run Length Matrix (n = 16), Gray Level Size Zone Matrix (n = 14), and Gray Level Dependence Matrix (n = 16). (2) LoG features (n = 430): Derived from 5 sigma scales (1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0, and 5.0 mm), providing 86 first-order and texture features per scale. (3) Wavelet-transformed features (n = 688): Extracted from 8 decomposition sub-bands (Low-Low-Low, Low-Low-High, Low-High-Low, Low-High-High, High-Low-Low, High-Low-High, High-High-Low, and High-High-High), yielding 86 features per sub-band. Shape-based features were only computed for the original images to avoid geometric redundancy. The final dataset integrated these 1218 radiomic features with 10 diagnostic metadata columns (providing technical context such as image mean and mask volume), the clinical outcome variable “Progress”, and the anatomical identifier "Whole Lung", totaling 1230 data columns for subsequent machine learning analysis.

Machine learning models (AutoGluon and Random Forest) were then used to construct a binary classification model to predict progression versus non-progression. The input features for the models were extracted from baseline CT imaging data, with the final progression/non-progression status at the time of follow-up serving as the predictive outcome. In addition to the uniform overall modeling of all ILA samples, we modeled two subclasses separately based on the characteristics of the disease imaging (non-subpleural ILAs spreading over almost the whole lung and localized subpleural ILAs).

Statistical analyses

Categorical data are presented as n (%), and continuous data are presented as mean with standard deviation or median with interquartile range (median [Q1, Q3]), depending on the distribution assessed by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. To compare sociodemographic and clinical characteristics among patients who underwent lung cancer resection, grouped by different ILA subcategories and progression status during follow-up, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used if both normal distribution and homogeneity of variance were satisfied; otherwise, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square tests. Two-tailed Fisher’s exact test were used when the expected frequencies in any cell were less than 5. For significant findings, post hoc analyses with Bonferroni correction were performed using an adjusted significance level of α/n (α= 0.05, where n is the number of comparisons). P <0.017 (0.05/3) was considered statistically significant in our study.

Multivariate logistic regression models were employed to identify variables associated with ILA progression. The model included variables that were significant at the P < 0.1 level in the univariate analysis conducted at both 2-year and 4-year follow-ups, as well as variables with clinical significance. Survival analyses utilized Kaplan-Meier curves with between-group comparisons assessed by log-rank test. A Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to analyze the risk factors for death. Model diagnostics, including assessment of multicollinearity (variance inflation factor <5), were performed to ensure validity and adherence to model assumptions.

In the radiomics studies, a Random Forest algorithm was used for preliminary analysis and the AutoGluon model (an ensemble learning system for multi-models, including LightGBM, CatBoost, and Neural Network) was used for optimization tuning.

To ensure robust model performance and eliminate bias due to random data splits during the model construction, 100 repeated experiments were conducted. In each experiment, 70% of the data were used for training and the remaining 30% for testing. Multiple rounds of testing were performed to minimize the potential impact of randomness in the data partitioning. Area under the curve (AUC) and accuracy were used to evaluate the predictive performance of the radiomics-based machine learning models in classifying ILA progression versus non-progression.

Data analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA), SPSS 23.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA), and R 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The radiomics studies were performed using Python 3.9.13 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, Delaware, USA). All analyses were two-tailed with statistical significance set at P < 0.05, except when adjusted for multiple comparisons.

Results

Proportion of ILAs

Between January 2016 and May 2019, a total of 11,681 patients underwent pulmonary nodule resection, among which 10,318 patients were excluded based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, including 8932 non-ILA patients. 1363 met the diagnostic criteria for ILAs, resulting in a proportion of 13.24% (1363/10,295) for ILAs in patients undergoing primary lung cancer resection.

Characteristics among the three subcategories of ILAs

Among the 1363 patients with ILAs, 781 (57.30%) were classified as non-subpleural ILAs, 452 (33.16%) as subpleural non-fibrotic ILAs, and 130 (9.54%) as subpleural fibrotic ILAs. Significant differences were observed across the three groups for multiple parameters (Table 1). Compared with the other two groups, patients with subpleural fibrotic ILAs were significantly older (both Bonferroni-corrected P < 0.001) and had higher proportions of males, current smokers, and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC) (all Bonferroni-corrected P < 0.001), but a lower proportion of stage I lung cancer (both Bonferroni-corrected P < 0.001). Progression rate of ILAs and risk factors

A total of 659 eligible subjects were followed up for >2 years. The duration of CT imaging surveillance among the patients with ILAs ranged from 24 to 87 months, with a median follow-up period of 54 months. ILAs improved in 417 patients (63.28%), remained unchanged in 177 patients (26.86%), and progressed in 65 patients (9.86%). The overall 2-year progression rate was 9.86%, with 29.73% (22/74) of the subpleural fibrotic ILA group exhibiting progression. At the 4-year follow-up, 422 subjects were eligible for evaluation. ILAs improved in 266 patients (63.03%), remained unchanged in 113 patients (26.78%), and progressed in 43 patients (10.19%). The overall 4-year progression rate was 10.19%, with 24.39% (10/41) of the subpleural fibrotic ILAs group showing progression (representative images are shown in Fig. 2). Within 2 years after surgery, 704 patients missed chest HRCT, of whom 27 died, so the loss to follow-up rate for HRCT was 50.67% (677/1336) after 2 years. All-cause mortality rate in patients with ILAs after lung cancer resection

As of December 2023, among the 1363 patients with ILAs, 76 had died and 154 did not complete telephone follow-up. After excluding deceased patients, the loss to follow-up rate for survival was 11.97% (154/1287). The overall all-cause mortality was 5.58% (76/1363), with a median follow-up of 70 months. Within 2 years after surgery, 82 patients were lost to survival follow-up and 27 patients had died; the 2-year all-cause mortality rate among patients with available follow-up was 2.11% (27/1281). Within 4 years after surgery, 118 patients were lost to follow-up and 53 patients had died; the 4-year all-cause mortality rate among patients with available follow-up was 4.26% (53/1245). The 2-year and 4-year group mortality rates were 1.76% (13/739) and 3.49% (25/717), respectively, in the non-subpleural ILAs group, 1.66% (7/421) and 3.39% (14/413) in the subpleural non-fibrotic ILAs group, and 5.79% (7/121) and 12.17% (14/115) in the subpleural fibrotic ILAs group. The log-rank test showed a significant between-group difference in survival rate (χ² = 23.910, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3A), with the non-subpleural ILAs group and the subpleural non-fibrotic ILAs group having a similar survival rate, but the subpleural fibrotic ILAs group having a worse survival rate. Radiomics model can classify ILAs progression

Based on the HRCT acquisition protocol and ILA classification criteria described above, a subset of patients was selected for radiomics analysis. Only patients with baseline HRCT images meeting radiomics quality requirements (thin-section reconstruction ≤1.0 mm and image matrix of 1024 × 1024 pixels) were eligible. A total of 139 patients, including 77 with non-progressive (42 non-subpleural and 35 subpleural ILAs) and 62 with progressive ILAs (35 non-subpleural and 27 subpleural ILAs), were selected for the radiomics study.

Using baseline CT-derived radiomic features, radiomics-based machine learning models were constructed to predict subsequent ILA progression versus non-progression. Across 100 repeated random training–testing splits, including analyses of all ILAs as well as the non-subpleural and subpleural ILAs subgroups, the model showed consistently high performance when applied to the testing sets, with mean AUC values of 0.790, 0.779, and 0.870, respectively (Fig. 4A–C). Consistently, the confusion matrix drawn from the model with the AUC closest to the average AUC of the 100 trials showed that the model predictions agreed well with the actual classification (Fig. 4D–E). For the testing cohorts, the AutoGluon model’s prediction accuracies for all ILAs, the non-subpleural ILAs subgroup and the subpleural ILAs subgroup were 0.801 ± 0.049, 0.779 ± 0.066, and 0.882 ± 0.066, respectively (mean ± standard deviation). However, the prediction results of the Random Forest algorithm were relatively poor, with AUCs of 0.736, 0.699, and 0.822, and accuracies of 0.716 ± 0.059, 0.629 ± 0.087, and 0.775 ± 0.098, for all ILAs, the non-subpleural ILAs subgroup and the subpleural ILAs subgroup, respectively. Discussion

The prevalence, progression patterns, and mortality outcomes of ILAs in patients who have undergone resection for lung cancer have previously been reported in several studies,5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 but our investigation represents one of the largest cohort studies with extensive follow-up duration. We identified an ILA proportion of 13.24% among patients undergoing lung cancer resection, with progression rates of 9.86% at 2 years and 10.19% at 4 years of follow-up. The corresponding mortality rates were 2.11% and 4.26%, respectively. Our analysis revealed significant associations between ILA progression and several key factors including subpleural fibrotic patterns and treatment modalities. Although overall mortality rates remained relatively low, progressive ILAs demonstrated substantially higher mortality than the other ILA subtypes. Additionally, the machine learning model AutoGluon demonstrated high discriminative performance in predicting ILA progression.

Current evidence suggests that ILAs represent an early radiographic manifestation of ILD, sharing common risk factors including advanced age, tobacco exposure, and occupational hazards.13,18,19 Our study found that advanced age and male were associated with a higher likelihood of having ILAs, particularly subpleural fibrotic ILAs, a finding consistent with previous studies.13,20 Although ILAs are more common in patients with a history of smoking, an effect of smoking on ILA progression was not observed in our study, possibly because of the subjects’ baseline characteristics. In our study, the majority of patients had a non-smoking history.

ILAs are recognized as an independent risk factor for lung cancer development and a poorer prognosis across disease stages,5, 6, 7, 8 with shared pathogenic mechanisms including genetic and epigenetic alterations, cellular senescence, signaling pathway activation, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition.21,22 Previous reports revealed that ILAs were linked to a higher incidence of lung cancer,23 with honeycombing patterns demonstrating a particularly strong association.24 While the incidence of lung cancer between ILA and non-ILA patients was not compared, our results indicated that subpleural fibrotic ILAs were associated with more advanced-stage disease at diagnosis. Furthermore, we found that the degree of ILA involvement was lowest for subpleural non-fibrotic ILAs among the three subtypes, which is consistent with the previous findings in Chinese population.16 However, no significant difference in the degree of ILA involvement was observed among groups stratified by ILA change status.

The radiographic pattern of ILAs significantly determines their progression risk. In our study, patients with subpleural fibrotic ILAs demonstrated significantly higher rates of progression than those with the other ILA subtypes over both 2- and 4-year follow-up periods. This finding is consistent with previous studies25,26 reporting that fibrotic ILAs have the highest progression risk. The underlying pathophysiology may be explained by surgical lung biopsy evidence showing that fibrotic ILAs frequently exhibit histologic features of definite or probable usual interstitial pneumonia.2,7,27 Similar to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, the progression of fibrotic ILAs appears to be driven by a combination of epigenetic and genetic factors, aberrant signaling responses, and activation of specific molecular pathways.

Tumor-related factors also influence ILA progression. Postoperative radiotherapy was associated with long-term ILA progression, and immunotherapy was also observed to significantly promote ILA progression during the 2-year follow-up period. These factors may all contribute to ILA progression and thereby increase mortality risk.28,29 Briefly, the pathogenesis of classic radiation pneumonitis involves cellular injury leading to cytokine release, recruitment of inflammatory infiltrates causing acute pneumonitis, and subsequent repair attempts resulting in pulmonary fibrosis.30 Acute-phase injury is radiation dose-dependent, as higher doses are associated with more severe pneumonitis.30 Five potential mechanisms for immune checkpoint inhibitor‑induced pneumonitis have been proposed31: (1) imbalance of T cell subsets; (2) activation and expansion of pre‑existing autoantibodies; (3) production and release of cytokines and chemokines; (4) cross‑antigen reactivity, where tumor antigens share epitopes with normal lung tissue; and (5) microbial modulation of host immunity. Therefore, radiotherapy and immunotherapy could promote the progression of ILA. Subpleural fibrotic ILAs appear to be particularly strongly associated with increased susceptibility to treatment-related pulmonary complications, including radiation pneumonitis and immune checkpoint inhibitor-related pneumonitis.29,32 These findings highlight the importance of implementing regular HRCT surveillance to monitor progression of ILAs in lung cancer patients receiving postoperative therapies, especially those with pre-existing fibrotic ILAs.

While CA125 is established as both a tumor marker and an indicator of lung epithelial injury,33 and has been associated with ILD progression in autoimmune diseases,34, 35, 36 its association with ILAs remains unexplored. Notably, patients with subpleural fibrotic ILAs showed higher CA125 levels than those with other ILA subtypes. While the association between CA125 levels and ILA progression was only borderline significant at 2 years (P = 0.051), it became strongly significant at the 4-year follow-up. To our knowledge, this study provides novel evidence for CA125 as a potential biomarker for fibrotic ILAs and their progression.

Existing evidence reliably demonstrates the adverse effects of ILAs on pulmonary function. The COPDGene Study reported that individuals with ILAs exhibit reduced total lung capacity accompanied by restrictive ventilatory defects.37 Similarly, the Framingham Heart Study documented significant reductions in both total lung capacity (9%) and DLCO (12%) in ILA-positive participants compared with controls.38 In patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), the presence of ILAs correlates with lower FVC and FEV1 values.39 Longitudinal data further indicate that ILA progression accelerates FVC decline relative to both non-ILA individuals and those with stable ILAs.4,39 Our findings corroborate these observations, showing that patients with subpleural fibrotic ILAs manifest the most severe pulmonary function impairment, characterized by the lowest FVC and DLCO measurements.

Multiple cohort studies have established that ILAs are associated with increased all-cause mortality in both the general population and patients with COPD.40 The Danish Lung Cancer Screening Trial further demonstrated that both fibrotic and non-fibrotic ILAs were linked to higher mortality among smokers.41 In treatment-naïve advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), ILAs correlated with worse overall survival (OS), independent of first-line therapy and smoking status,42 and stage IV NSCLC patients with ILAs at diagnosis had shorter OS after adjusting for age, gender, and smoking.5 Additionally, fibrotic ILAs were specifically associated with elevated cause-specific mortality in early-stage NSCLC.9 Consistent with these findings, our study found that the subpleural fibrotic ILAs group had a higher mortality rate than the non-subpleural and subpleural non-fibrotic ILAs groups. Furthermore, patients with progressive ILAs exhibited worse survival outcomes than those with improved or unchanged ILAs.

We developed and evaluated machine learning models using radiomic features extracted from CT images to predict ILA progression. Among the models tested, AutoGluon,43 a state-of-the-art gradient boosting algorithm, was assessed for optimization and hyperparameter tuning. In a previous radiomics study of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, texture features such as First-Order Median and GLCM Entropy were shown to reflect imaging heterogeneity and were associated with survival outcomes.44 In our study, we similarly observed that radiomic features were informative for distinguishing progression versus non-progression within each of the subpleural and non-subpleural ILA patterns. We assessed differences in radiomic features between corresponding ROIs in CT slices, thus capturing textural differences within the layers of the lung. However, poorer classification performance was observed for non-subpleural ILAs than for subpleural ILAs. The probable reason for this is that ROIs extracted from subpleural ILAs are largely localized to the subpleural region, whereas non-subpleural ILAs more typically involve almost the entire lung. Consequently, the broader range of ROIs in non-subpleural ILAs along with the potential introduction of greater noise impacts subsequent analysis.45 In subsequent studies, whole-lung segmentation could be replaced by the acquisition of localized ROIs with a fixed number of pixels.45 Prediction models based on radiomic features extracted from CT images offer a valuable approach for identifying patients at risk of ILA progression. In particular, certain radiomic features demonstrated significant weighting within the model, indicating their association with progression.

This study had several limitations. First, as a single-center investigation, our findings may lack generalizability to broader populations. Second, the retrospective design and substantial proportion of patients without complete HRCT (50.67%) or survival (11.97%) follow-up may have introduced selection bias. Third, we were unable to assess established ILA biomarkers46 including Krebs von den Lungen-6, interleukin-6, surfactant proteins A and D, and matrix metalloproteinase-7, nor did we routinely screen for rheumatic diseases, because these tests were not part of our standard clinical protocol. Fourth, the surgical specimens did not provide pathological information about ILAs. Fifth, cause-specific mortality could not be determined for some patients because telephone interviews with family members, who often lacked medical expertise or supporting documents, were the primary source of information. Thus, only all-cause mortality is provided.

Conclusions

In lung cancer patients who had undergone resection, the proportion of ILAs was 13.24%, the 2- and 4-year progression rates for ILAs were 9.86% and 10.19%, respectively, and the corresponding all-cause mortality rates were 2.11% and 4.26%, respectively. Subpleural fibrotic ILAs and radiotherapy were associated with ILA progression. Progressive ILAs negatively affected the mortality rate. Radiomic features were found to be useful for predicting ILA progression. A multidisciplinary approach integrating radiomic and biochemical data may provide a better understanding of ILA progression and help guide treatment strategies.
