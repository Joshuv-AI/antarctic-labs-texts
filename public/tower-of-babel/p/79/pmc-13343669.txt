Genetic divergence in Aedes aegypti mosquitoes potentially associated with enhanced capacity for arbovirus transmission
Authors: Xiaodi Wang, Jovana Bozic, Brian B. Tarimo, Tanise M. S. Stenn, Miles T. McCollum, Juliana Carrillo, Eva A. Buckner, Vincent O. Nyasembe, Billy Ngasala, Rhoel R. Dinglasan, Travis C. Collier, Eric P. Caragata, Yoosook Lee, Derrick K. Mathias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAedes aegypti is the most important mosquito vector of arboviruses worldwide, originating on islands of the southwest Indian Ocean before spreading to Africa and, more recently, the Americas and Asia. Migration out of Africa was enabled by the evolution of behaviors that increased human-vector contact, and as Ae. aegypti became more domesticated, arboviral competence may have also increased. Here we investigate variation in vector competence between present-day populations of Ae. aegypti from Florida (USA) and Tanzania (East Africa) and use a genomics approach to examine genetic differentiation potentially underlying traits important for arbovirus transmission.ResultsUsing vector-competence experiments with dengue virus serotype 2, we demonstrate greater susceptibility to infection in American mosquitoes, along with higher viral loads and a shorter time to virus dissemination. We then sought to identify highly divergent genes potentially underlying these differences and discovered more than 1.6 million single nucleotide polymorphisms via exome sequencing. Of these, 1058 were highly differentiated between populations (FST > 0.9) with more than half mapping to 17 outlier genes influencing chromatin remodeling, lipid metabolism, glycosylation, circadian rhythms, olfaction, and saliva bioactivity. Eight of these outlier genes formed a highly divergent genomic island on chromosome 2. Allele-frequency patterns of outlier genes across Ae. aegypti’s range highlight genetic differentiation within sub-Saharan Africa, as well as evolutionary changes accompanying the shift towards domestication in the Americas and Asia.ConclusionsThis work offers insights into phenotypic variation and evolutionary divergence within Ae. aegypti, including naturally occurring genetic variants that may influence transmission-related characters.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-026-02592-w.

Background

Aedes aegypti is the most important vector of arboviruses that cause human illness worldwide, including dengue, chikungunya, Zika, and yellow fever [1]. The spread of these diseases is driven by Ae. aegypti populations that vary geographically in characteristics that influence transmission. Global population-genetic analyses support two major clades corresponding to Ae. aegypti formosus (Aaf), an ecotype confined to sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), and Ae. aegypti aegypti (Aaa), an invasive ecotype now widespread outside of SSA [2, 3], with the two often considered distinct subspecies or even species [4]. Despite clear genetic distinction, these ecotypes can produce viable offspring [5], and admixture has been documented from multiple locations [2, 6, 7].

Throughout its range, Aaa generally has a high capacity for transmitting arboviruses while being highly synanthropic or “domesticated,” specializing on human hosts and utilizing artificial containers for oviposition and juvenile development [3, 8]. In contrast, Aaf tends to have a lower capacity for transmission and is not synanthropic, often feeding on a wider range of hosts and preferring natural containers (e.g., tree holes) for oviposition [3, 8, 9]. However, in some populations such as those in West Africa and Argentina, mosquitoes with dark coloration typical of Aaf have also been observed to bite humans or breed indoors [10–17]. The broad biological differences between Aaa and Aaf, along with local deviations from general phenotypic patterns relative to ecotype expectations, indicate a complex evolutionary history for Ae. aegypti.

Aedes aegypti originated on islands of the southwestern Indian Ocean roughly 7 million years ago and colonized the African continent within the last 85,000 years [6]. The two ecotypes likely diverged around 5000 years ago, with limited gene flow between the two occurring within the last 600 years [3, 18]. Synanthropy and specialization on human hosts likely arose once in an Aaf population in the West African Sahel before Aaa progenitors with these characteristics invaded the Americas in the 16th or seventeenth century [3, 7, 19, 20]. Evidence suggests that long dry seasons lasting several months provided selection pressure that pushed Ae. aegypti towards domestication and that trans-Atlantic colonization stopped gene flow with ancestral populations, accelerating Aaa divergence [20].

Intriguingly, as Aaa became more synanthropic, additional phenotypic changes evolved. Some changes (e.g., lighter color, increased pale scaling on first abdominal tergite [21, 22]) have no bearing on virus transmission, while others like vector competence and duration of the extrinsic incubation period (EIP, the time required for a mosquito’s bite to become infectious after virus ingestion), greatly impact arbovirus disease ecology and can vary substantially among populations [8, 9, 23]. This variability within and between ecotypes has raised questions about the nature of the evolutionary processes separating the two—questions for which genomic studies have been the primary approach [2, 6, 7, 19, 20].

To better understand phenotypic and genetic divergence between Ae. aegypti ecotypes, we examined populations from coastal Tanzania, sampled here for the first time, and Florida, USA. First, we compared vector-competence phenotypes and demonstrated that these two populations vary significantly in susceptibility to infection and permissiveness to virus production with dengue virus serotype 2 (DENV-2) (Asian II genotype). Taking a population genomics approach, we then used high-throughput sequencing (HTS) of exon-enriched libraries to investigate ecotype divergence at protein-coding loci and generate hypotheses for genes potentially contributing to variation in vector competence. With our HTS data, we investigated between-population variability in density of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), nucleotide diversity (π), differentiation (FST), and departures from neutrality (Tajima’s D) across chromosomes and functionally characterized the most divergent genes. We then combined our HTS data with previously published datasets to examine genetic relatedness of our study population to others from SSA, North America, and Asia, and characterize allele-frequency patterns of the most divergent genes across much of Ae. aegypti’s range. Our analyses further illuminate evolutionary divergence within Ae. aegypti, provide insight on naturally occurring variants that are ecotype-specific or region-specific, and reveal clusters of highly differentiated genes across the genome. Importantly, some of these genes already have empirical support as viral host factors (i.e., influence virus replication), while others have well-characterized roles in mosquito physiology or behavior. Our data highlight that many of the highly divergent genes we identified are worthy of follow-up study for naturally occurring variation that may impact arbovirus transmission.

Results

Floridian Ae. aegypti show greater vector competence for DENV-2 (Asian II genotype)

To examine variation in DENV-2 competence between Ae. aegypti from East Africa and the Americas, we exposed recently established colonies (Palmetto, Florida, USA (FL), and Bagamoyo, Tanzania (TZ)) to DENV-2 (Asian II genotype) in blood meals and quantified infection metrics in fed females over a 17-day period (Fig. 1A). Mosquito bodies with thoracic appendages removed were examined by plaque assay to determine infection rate (IR). The FL population showed higher IRs (Fig. 1B) than TZ except at 4 days post-exposure (dpe). At 7 dpe, the IR of FL was more than twice that of TZ (OR = 4.11, p = 0.007) and remained significantly higher at 10 dpe when its IR peaked (OR = 2.83, p = 0.033). The peak for TZ lagged that of FL by four days, and differences in IRs at 14 dpe and 17 dpe were not significant. Viral load in infected mosquito bodies also varied significantly between populations during the experimental EIP (Fig. 1C), and titers were consistently higher for FL (Table S1). Median viral loads ranged from 3.34–4.90 log10 PFU/mL in FL compared to 3.22–3.78 log10 PFU/mL in TZ and were significantly greater in FL at 7 dpe (IRR = 5.20, p = 0.004), 14 dpe (IRR = 6.88, p < 0.001), and 17 dpe (IRR = 6.14, p = 0.002). To examine potential barriers to DENV-2 midgut escape, we determined the virus dissemination rate (DR) to peripheral tissues. Disseminated infections were not detected in legs and wings of either population until 10 dpe, but DRs increased for both at each timepoint thereafter, peaking at 17 dpe. Similar to body infections, the FL population showed trends toward higher DRs except at 17 dpe (Fig. 1D). Viral loads in legs and wings at 10 dpe were similar in both populations, but DENV-2 reached significantly higher titers in FL at both 14 dpe (IRR = 7.75, p < 0.001) and 17 dpe (IRR = 9.36, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1E). As a proxy for the EIP, we estimated the disseminated infection period (DIP), as the median time required for virus particles to overcome the midgut escape barrier. For the FL population, we estimated DIP to be 12.2 days (95% confidence interval (CI95) = 11.0–13.4), approximately 2.4 days shorter than for the TZ population (DIP = 14.6, CI95 = 12.8–16.5).

Transmission potential of DENV-2 was also evaluated by measuring the virus transmission rate (TR) in saliva, with the earliest detection of positive samples at 10 dpi for both TZ and FL populations (Fig. 1F). However, the low number of mosquitoes with infectious saliva (n ≤ 1 per population per timepoint) was insufficient to support comparison of viral titers (Fig. 1G) and estimation of the EIP (Table S1). In combination, our results indicate that Ae. aegypti from FL have greater competence for DENV-2 compared to the TZ population, manifested through higher IRs approximately 1 week post-blood meal, higher viral loads at multiple timepoints, and a shorter time to virus dissemination.

Floridian and Tanzanian Ae. aegypti reflect global population genetic differentiation

Given the observed difference between FL and TZ populations for DENV-2 competence, we used genomic tools to explore population genetic divergence and identify polymorphisms potentially underlying variation in these transmission-related traits. We utilized probe-based capture and high-throughput sequencing of exons/untranslated regions (UTRs), identifying 1,687,282 biallelic SNPs that met our filtering criteria. The final dataset included 22.2 Mb of targeted sequence across 12,597 genes, leading on average to one SNP per 13.15 bp with a median depth of 38.1 × (Table S2). To examine how FL and TZ individuals are positioned within the geographic and genetic patterns observed among populations worldwide, we augmented this dataset with SNP calls at the same loci for 113 additional individuals using Ae. aegypti genome sequences originating from Asia, North America, and SSA [20, 24, 25] (Fig. 2A; Table S3). We constructed a phylogenetic tree, based on Hudson’s FST metric of population differentiation [26], which reveals three phylogenetically distinct groups (Fig. 2B; Table S4). Populations from North America and Asia formed a distinct clade while African populations formed two separate clades (Fig. 2B), corroborating previous studies of Ae. aegypti genetics and ecotype divergence [2, 19, 20, 27]. Within SSA, populations from east of the Rift Valley and South Africa formed a single clade, while inland East African populations from Kenya and Uganda clustered with those from Central and West Africa forming the second clade. These findings highlight a divergence within the African Ae. aegypti lineage and suggest that physical geographical features (e.g., the Rift Valley, the Kalahari Desert, the Congo Basin) might have played a major role in limiting gene flow and promoting regional differentiation among extant populations (Fig. 2A).

The principal components analysis (PCA) showed a clear genetic separation of mosquitoes from Asia/North America and SSA along principal component 1 (PC1), while PC2 further separated individuals within SSA (Fig. 2C). Consistent with the topology of the phylogenetic tree, African mosquitoes grouped into two major clusters—again corresponding to populations east and west of the Rift Valley. Two individuals from Rabai, Kenya, a location where both ecotypes co-occur [28, 29], clustered closer to individuals from North America than to those from coastal Kenya, consistent with a localized reintroduction of mosquitoes to Rabai, Kenya, from Asia as previously reported [2].

Our model-based ancestry analysis [30] (Figs. 2D and S1) corroborated the results of the phylogenetic and PCA findings. The first major subdivision (K = 2) separated mosquitoes between Asia/North America and SSA (Fig. 2D). At K = 3, individuals from SSA clearly segregated into two groups based on location of origin, west of the Rift Valley versus east of the Rift Valley and South Africa. Increasing K from 4 to 7 revealed regional population structure in SSA on both sides of the Rift Valley, as well as fine-scale genetic differences within single populations (Figs. 2D and S1). For American Ae. aegypti, individuals from FL were genetically more similar to other mosquitoes from Florida compared to those from Puerto Rico or those recently introduced into California. Together, multiple approaches to examining the genetic relatedness of our study populations to others demonstrated that FL and TZ mosquitoes belong to different ecotypes and are genetically consistent with expectations based on geographic origin.

Demographic histories have shaped divergence between Floridian and Tanzanian Ae. aegypti

To explore divergence between FL and TZ, we performed sliding-window analyses for SNP density, FST [26], nucleotide diversity (π) [31], and Tajima’s D [32] (Fig. 3). Overall, SNP densities varied across the genome in both populations with lower SNP densities near centromeres (Fig. 3A). FST between FL and TZ was 0.177 (s.d. = 0.081) and varied significantly among chromosomes (H = 221, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3B). FST was highest for chromosome 1 (mean = 0.214, s.d. = 0.082), followed by chromosomes 3 (mean = 0.172, s.d. = 0.070), and 2 (mean = 0.157, s.d. = 0.081). Mean π for FL (πFL = 2.72 × 10−4) was lower than for TZ (πTZ = 3.90 × 10−4, U = 3.61 × 106, p < 0.001), a trend that persisted in sliding-window analyses along all three chromosomes (Fig. 3C and D). These patterns may reflect demographic history of FL, such as population contractions associated with founder events and vector control activities. Additionally, we found significant differences in π by chromosome for FL (H = 19.3, p < 0.001) but not for TZ (H = 2.02, p = 0.364). In FL, π was significantly lower for chromosome 1 (πchr1 = 2.53 × 10−4) compared to chromosomes 2 (πchr2 = 2.85 × 10−4; adjusted p < 0.001) or 3 (πchr3 = 2.72 × 10−4; adjusted p = 0.026). Exons had significantly greater π than UTRs in both FL (H = 354, p < 0.001) and TZ (H = 1.10 × 103, p < 0.001), suggesting stronger background selection in UTRs than in exons (Table S5).

Last, we calculated Tajima’s D to measure departures from neutral expectations for nucleotide diversity and number of segregating sites per site [33]. Average D for FL was 0.782 (s.d. = 0.602), while TZ had an average D of − 0.754 (s.d. = 0.435). Genome-wide, D was generally positive for FL but negative for TZ (Fig. 3E), suggesting that FL experienced one or more population contractions in the recent past and/or balancing selection. In contrast, consistently negative D for TZ may signal a recent population expansion or result from background selection. In both populations, D varied by genic feature (FL: H = 189.3, p < 0.001; TZ: H = 2.53 × 103, p < 0.001) and was significantly higher in exons than either UTR (adjusted p < 0.001 for all four comparisons), suggesting that purifying selection has acted more strongly on mutations in UTRs than in exons (Table S5). Together, these analyses identified genomic locations with high FST and/or extreme differences in π or Tajima’s D, suggesting that genes within such regions were the focus of evolutionary processes leading to divergence.

Highly differentiated SNPs are clustered in the genome and enriched in a small set of genes with diverse functions

To characterize genes and pathways potentially contributing to phenotypic variation between populations, we narrowed our analysis to genes with SNPs at fixed or nearly fixed genotype frequencies between populations (FST, ≥ 0.9) and refer to these as informative SNPs (iSNPs). We discovered a total of 1058 iSNPs in exons and UTRs of 232 genes, many of which occur in highly differentiated clusters in the genome (Fig. 4A; Table S6). Linkage disequilibrium (LD) within these genes was generally low (average of median r2 = 0.0107), while a small proportion of SNP pairs (mean = 2.18% per gene) showed high LD (r2 ≥ 0.8) (Table S7). To explore functional implications, we annotated all genes containing iSNPs into 17 biological-process categories (Fig. 4B; Table S7). When ranked by iSNP count, gene expression (15.7%), other metabolic process (11.4%), and other biological process (11.4%) were the most represented categories, while average iSNP number per gene ranged from 2.0 for reproduction to 16.25 for behavior (Fig. 4B). To identify the most highly differentiated genes, we examined rank distributions of iSNP number per gene and iSNP number per kb (accounting for variation in gene size) and found 17 outliers beyond the 95th percentile (14.5 iSNPs/gene and 7.5 iSNPs/kbp, respectively) for at least one of these metrics (Fig. 4C and D). More than half of all iSNPs (585/1058 = 55.3%) occurred within this gene subset (17/232 = 7.3%). To better understand the biological relevance of this group of highly divergent genes, we further complemented our gene ontology-based categorization with a protein–protein interaction analysis (Fig. 5; Table S8). Among 17 outliers, the most represented biological-process category was other metabolic process, which included four paralogs on chromosome 2 that are fatty acid synthases (FAS) involved in lipid metabolism: AaFAS3 (AAEL022506), AaFAS4 (AAEL002237), AaFAS5 (AAEL002228), and AaFAS6 (AAEL029069) (Figs. 4D and 5). The gene expression category contained two outlier genes in adjacent iSNP clusters on chromosome 2, both involved in chromatin organization (Figs. 5 and S2). The first, AaCHD7 (AAEL019793), is 450 kbp downstream of AaFAS3–6, encodes a chromodomain helicase DNA-binding protein and is the likely ortholog of kismet in Drosophila and CHD7 in humans. The other, AaTou (AAEL001992), is the putative ortholog of toutatis in Drosophila and BAZ2A/Tip5 in humans. The protein metabolism category included two outliers, both of which play key roles in post- or co-translational modification. The first gene, AaOSTC (AAEL002198), encodes the subunit OSTC of the oligosaccharyltransferase (OST) complex. It is relatively small (transcript length = 699 bp) and is located in a major iSNP cluster on chromosome 2 between AaFAS3–6 and AaCHD7 (Figs. 5 and S2). The second gene, AaCk1ε (AAEL019753), is the ortholog of Ck1ε (casein kinase 1 epsilon), synonymous with discs overgrown/doubletime (dco/dbt) in Drosophila. Notably, its product was the most highly connected node in our protein-interaction network with connections to Wnt signaling, ribosome biogenesis, and circadian rhythms (Fig. 5).

Additionally, the behavior category had two outliers, one tied to circadian rhythmicity and the other to olfaction. The first gene is AaClk (AAEL022593). It is the ortholog of the circadian gene clock (clk) in Drosophila [34], which is functionally connected to AaCk1ε through interactions with the product of the gene period [35–37]. The second, the odorant receptor Or103 (AAEL017505), had the highest iSNP density. Odorant receptors play a major role in mosquito olfaction, and Or103 has been implicated in host preference assays [38]. The two outlier genes in the response to stimulus category were both cytochrome P450 monooxygenases (CYPs), genes with known or putative roles in detoxification of xenobiotic compounds [39]. In the immune system category, the snake-like serine protease AaSlsp (AAEL020470) was the sole outlier. It encodes a serine protease homologous to snake (snk) in Drosophila, acting within the toll pathway [40].

Lastly, two outlier genes were assigned to the other biological process category. The first, AaPSK (AAEL000229), encodes the precursor of a small salivary peptide prosialokinin that causes vasodilation and vascular permeability in human hosts [41–43]. The second gene AAEL002234 is located in a major iSNP cluster on chromosome 2 along with AaFAS3–6, AaOSTC, and AaCHD7 (Fig. S2). Its product has an ankyrin-repeat domain, typically involved in protein–protein interactions [44]. Outlier genes AAEL024267 and AAEL009844 had limited annotations for gene ontology, orthology, and protein domain conservation, so their putative functions remain unknown.

In summary, functional annotation and protein–protein interaction analysis revealed that the most differentiated genes in our dataset play roles in lipid metabolism, chromatin organization, circadian rhythmicity, N-linked protein glycosylation, olfaction, xenobiotic detoxification, saliva bioactivity, and potentially innate immunity. Some of these genes have been previously associated with processes relevant to arboviral transmission, such as host-vector contact and virus propagation. Importantly, these data point to genes and pathways that may have played prominent roles in phenotypic divergence during Ae. aegypti evolution, reflecting differentiation across metabolic, sensory, and regulatory systems that may have facilitated adaptation to distinct ecological environments.

Highly differentiated genes show distinct patterns in allele frequency distribution across Africa and into the Americas

Given these findings, we hypothesized that allele-frequency patterns among outlier genes across SSA and into the Americas/Asia could reveal adaptive divergence across populations and provide insight into Ae. aegypti evolution, possibly including aspects of increased host-vector contact and vectorial capacity for arbovirus transmission [3, 19, 20]. To this end, we examined average frequencies of FL-dominant iSNP alleles for all 17 outlier genes among the mosquitoes used in our population-genomics analyses. The most extreme pattern was also the most common, characterized by extreme allele frequency differences between populations from coastal East Africa/South Africa and populations from elsewhere. This pattern was observed in seven genes including the odorant receptor Or103 (AAEL017505), the four fatty acid synthases AaFAS3–6 (AAEL022506, AAEL002237, AAEL002228, AAEL029069), and the circadian genes AaClk (AAEL022593) and AaCk1ε (AAEL019753). These genes had high frequencies of a single allele at each iSNP to the extent that homozygotes of the alternative allele were absent or at low frequency. Mosquitoes from South Africa and coastal East Africa (excluding Rabai indoor mosquitoes) were clearly distinct, with mean frequencies of the FL-dominant allele mostly in the 0–0.3 range. Mosquitoes from elsewhere in SSA (except Gabon), North America, and Asia showed the opposite pattern, with mean frequencies of the same allele generally > 0.7.

The second pattern, observed in five genes, reflected regional variation in iSNP frequencies. Mosquitoes from coastal East Africa and South Africa again had low frequencies of FL-dominant alleles, while African populations west of the Rift Valley had intermediate to high frequencies (0.4–0.8) and those from North America and Asia mostly had frequencies > 0.8. Genes with this pattern included the chromatin-remodeling protein AaCHD7 (AAEL019793), the oligosaccharyl transferase subunit AaOSTC (AAEL002198), the snake-like serine protease AaSlsp (AAEL020470), an ankyrin-repeat protein (AAEL002234), and a protein of unknown function (AAEL009844).

The third pattern appeared population-specific and may reflect responses to chemical-based vector control, as it only involved CYP6P12 (AAEL014891), CYP12F6 (AAEL002005), and a gene of unknown function (AAEL024267) linked to CYP6P12. For these genes, FL-dominant alleles were completely fixed in the FL population, which experiences high pyrethroid exposure [45], while their frequencies ranged from low to high in other North American/Asian populations and from low to intermediate in SSA.

The final pattern occurred in just two genes, AaPSK (AAEL000229) and the chromatin-remodeling protein subunit AaTou (AAEL001992) but was arguably the most intriguing. For these genes, differentiation reflected ecotype status (Aaa vs. Aaf) in our population-genetic analysis and suggested that different alleles were favored between continents, as most African populations had low frequencies of FL-dominant alleles and all North American/Asian populations had high frequencies. These data may point to important roles for salivary proteins and chromatin remodeling in Ae. aegypti ecotype divergence.

Discussion

In this study we utilized newly established, field-derived colonies of Ae. aegypti from Tanzania and Florida to demonstrate that mosquitoes from divergent genetic backgrounds varied significantly in their susceptibility and permissiveness to a specific isolate of DENV-2 (Asian II genotype). How well these results reflect the transmission biology of other strains and serotypes is yet to be determined, but the implication for New World Aaa is that greater competence and a shorter incubation period (for at least this DENV serotype and strain), coupled with previously described preferences for human hosts [20, 38], make Aaa more efficient vectors compared to Aaf from coastal East Africa. Combining our exome-seq dataset from field-derived individuals with publicly available genome sequences, we confirmed that mosquitoes from our site in Tanzania belonged to ecotype Aaf and those from Florida to the globally invasive ecotype Aaa [20, 24, 25]. Our sliding-window, population-genetic analyses revealed heterogeneity in divergence across the exome and that iSNPs were located in clusters with 55.3% occurring in 17 outlier genes (0.135% of the total gene number). When examined by function, many of these outliers or their pathways were found to influence biological processes that could impact arbovirus transmission, including vector-host, host–pathogen, or vector-pathogen interactions (Fig. 7). Such population-level differences are consistent with ecotype variation of Ae. aegypti in vector competence for multiple arboviruses, especially flaviviruses, including yellow fever virus (YFV) [47, 48], Zika virus (ZIKV) [49], and all four serotypes of DENV [13, 48, 50–58], showing a general trend of higher competence in Aaa than Aaf. Moreover, the Palmetto, Florida, population in our study slightly differed from previous reports of DENV competence in Florida. For example, our population showed higher IR at 7 (56.5% vs ~ 28%) and 14 dpi (51.3% vs ~ 28%) compared to a Key West, Florida, population challenged with a DENV-2 Puerto Rican strain at a similar blood meal titer [59]. Relative to other studies using comparable or higher titers, our population showed lower IRs (51.3% vs 75–89% or 93%) and DRs (54.2% vs 100% or 80%) [60, 61], but higher TRs at 14 dpi (10.0% vs 0%) [60]. Since DENV re-emerged in Florida in 2009, locally acquired cases have mainly occurred in southern Florida. In Manatee County, no human cases have been reported; however, local transmission cannot be ruled out, as 84% of mosquito-transmitted DENV have clinically inapparent symptoms [62], and DENV-4 was detected in field-derived Ae. aegypti in 2016–2017 [63]. In the Tanzania populations, data from East Africa on DENV competence is extremely limited despite the occurrence of multiple epidemics along coastal Tanzania and Kenya during the last decade [64–70]. Only a single study has been found examining DENV competence in this region (the inland city of Nairobi and the coastal city of Kilifi, Keyna) [54], and none for Tanzania. Moreover, the Kenyan study lacked an Ae. aegypti population from outside of SSA, which would have been worthwhile to determine if the geographic differences we observed are consistent for other East African populations of Ae. aegypti. Thus, given the lack of data on DENV competence from East Africa, our study represents an early step in advancing research on this topic in the region.

In addition to vector competence, there is much less data for comparisons of EIP, but higher competence is often correlated with shorter EIPs [71, 72], and therefore, genetic variation underlying both phenotypes likely overlaps to a certain degree. For these traits, data strongly suggest that both vector genetic background and virus genotype (i.e., G × G interactions) play important roles [55, 73–75]. Growing evidence has revealed a more heterogeneous and regionally differentiated genetic landscape of Ae. aegypti than previously recognized, including the coexistence of both ecotypes or their genetic admixture reported in a few places in SSA [15, 18, 76]. To date, however, few genomic analyses have jointly considered vector competence and genetic divergence using the same field-derived populations. Our experimental results therefore provide valuable complementary insight into population genomics and divergence and point to potential genetic pathways that may have contributed to local adaptation.

Among the 17 highly divergent outlier genes identified between our Florida and Tanzania populations, a subset has known or putative roles related to viral replication and therefore may influence differences in vector competence between populations (Fig. 7; Note S1). For example, four of the outlier genes, AaFAS3–6, encode fatty acid synthases (FASs), which are involved in lipid biosynthesis and are all located in the 429-Mb region of chromosome 2. During DENV infection, viral non-structural proteins (NS) remodel host endoplasmic reticulum membranes to build replication complexes, a process that depends on lipid metabolism [77]. DENV-2 is known to hijack host’s lipid metabolic machinery [78, 79], and inhibition of FAS activity in mosquito-derived cells can greatly reduce viral replication [79]. In Ae. aegypti, only AaFAS1 has been experimentally confirmed as a DENV-2 host factor [80], while other FAS paralogs show infection-responsive expression patterns in transcriptomic studies that may also contribute, warranting further investigation [81–85]. Thus, DENV-2 may exploit multiple AaFAS paralogs, making divergence in these genes a plausible contributor to differences in viral replication between mosquito populations. Another potential host-factor gene, AaCHD7 (AAEL019793), produces a chromodomain helicase DNA-binding protein (CHD) that when silenced in Ae. aegypti cells significantly decreased DENV-2 replication [86]. CHD proteins can facilitate transcriptional elongation by repositioning nucleosomes and regulating enhancer activity, functions observed across insects and vertebrates [87–92]. Although human CHD7 has not been implicated in the flavivirus life cycle, other chromatin-associated proteins have been, including PAF1C, a known binding partner of DENV-2 NS5 [93, 94]. Like CHD7, PAF1C is a key player in transcriptional elongation, suggesting that DENV may manipulate this process to its advantage in both mosquito and human hosts.

A notable feature of the 17 highly divergent outlier genes is that eight were concentrated in a 920-kbp genomic island of differentiation on chromosome 2 (Fig. S2), which contained 18 polymorphic genes in total and 375 iSNPs (35.4%), a concentration of nearly-fixed variants that suggests biological significance. LD in these genes, consistent with patterns observed across all genes containing iSNPs, indicates that most SNP pairs show low correlation (average of median r2 across genes = 0.0183), with some forming localized clusters of strongly linked variants (r2 ≥ 0.8; mean proportion = 3.59% per gene) (Table S7). Functional annotations and the published literature indicated that this island contains genes that likely influence flavivirus replication [78–80, 86, 93, 95, 96] and potentially vector competence and/or the length of the EIP (Fig. 7; Note S1), including the examples noted above (AaFAS3–6 and AaCHD7). The location of this island does not overlap previously reported locations of quantitative trait loci contributing to ecotype variation in competence for DENV-2 (American genotype) or ZIKV [49, 97–99]. However, those studies used Aaf populations from West or Central Africa, and our allele frequency comparisons across SSA (Fig. 6) show that coastal East African populations are genetically distinct from populations west of the Rift Valley. Besides, a recent global genomic analysis of Ae. aegypti from 73 locations examined signatures of natural selection and found the strongest signal of differentiation shared among several North American populations on chromosome 2 (centered at 401.75 Mb, ~ 2 Mb window wide) [100]. Although this region lies ~ 28 Mb downstream of the genomic island identified in our study, its occurrence on the same q arm of chromosome 2 may reflect a hotspot of selection involved in the emergence of invasive Aaa ecotypes. Another global population genomic analysis, which included roughly half as many populations as the previous study [100], revealed adaptive genomic signatures in Aaa ecotypes [7]. Notably, eight of the outlier genes identified in our study were also detected in one of their selection analyses as globally or locally adaptive loci under selective sweeps or positive selection, all located near the 429 Mb region of chromosome 2. Six of these genes fall within the genomic island we identified. This overlap reinforces that this genomic region may have undergone selection reflecting Ae. aegypti adaptations to new environments, including changes in pathogen pressure.

Collectively, the 17 highly divergent outlier genes provide a foundation for generating hypotheses not only on how their functional diversity may relate to traits influencing vectorial capacity (a composite metric of transmission capability) and, more broadly, to some of the genetic changes contributing to Ae. aegypti adaptation and expansion within and out of SSA (Note S1). For example, one of the outlier genes, odorant receptor Or103 (AAEL017505), had the highest iSNP density (20.6 iSNP/kbp) and 29 total, with nearly half causing an amino acid change. This gene partly drives vector-host interactions and was linked to host preference in an earlier transcriptomic study. In comparisons of Ae. aegypti from Kenya, Uganda, and Thailand, Or103 was one of two odorant receptors upregulated in antennae of human-preferring mosquitoes but downregulated in mosquitoes preferring a non-human host [38]. The allele-frequency patterns of Or103 revealed distinct differentiation in coastal East Africa and South Africa, contrasting with the alleles shared by other African populations and American populations. This divergence may reflect region-specific selection on Aaf populations that influenced host preference before global expansion as Aaa, suggesting potential involvement in shaping variability in host-vector contact between the two ecotypes.

Another example of a gene for which variation may impact transmission is the salivary peptide precursor AaPSK (AAEL000229). It was the smallest gene among all outliers with a transcript length of 389 bp, while 3 of its 4 iSNPs caused amino acid substitutions. The active peptide, sialokinin, is a tachykinin-like peptide secreted in Ae. aegypti saliva that causes vasodilation [41] and increases blood perfusion in mammals leading to shorter probing times when Aedes mosquitoes feed [41, 42]. Sialokinin also reduces endothelial-barrier integrity, resulting in the influx of arbovirus-permissive monocytes at the bite site and an increased likelihood of infection by flaviviruses and alphaviruses [43, 101]. Thus, this small peptide influences both vector-host and host–pathogen interactions. An exciting finding from our analyses was that genotype frequencies of the four iSNPs (three nonsynonymous) identified in AaPSK strongly reflected ecotype status (Fig. 6). Ecotype Aaa is a frequent human biter, often taking multiple blood meals during a single gonotrophic cycle [102, 103]. Although it is unknown how the three amino acid substitutions uncovered affect peptide function, our data and the link between blood perfusion and shorter probing times [42] point to the importance of salivary proteins in the evolution of the Aaa ecotype towards human specialization.

Compared with broad global genomic analyses, our iSNPs were identified in a focused comparison between two field-derived populations that differed in transmission-related traits. This analysis complements global studies by allowing us to highlight more specific candidate genes that may underlie evolutionary processes within the broader regions identified. Recent modeling of ZIKV transmission in Africa has shown that mosquito genetics can influence arbovirus transmission more strongly than climate, through effects on mosquito-host contact and vector competence [104]. Therefore, additional studies integrating field-derived vector competence data with population-genomic analyses will greatly enhance our understanding of how local adaptation shapes Ae. aegypti as an efficient disease vector. More broadly, work that incorporates additional arboviruses or serotypes, especially locally circulating isolates, together with synanthropy assays will provide deeper insight into the role of Ae. aegypti population variation in arboviral transmission. Our study identified a small but biologically meaningful set of candidate genes that may contribute to divergence and adaptation in the species. Further functional investigation of these loci will be invaluable for illuminating the ecological and evolutionary processes that enabled the global spread of the Aaa ecotype.

Conclusions

Mosquitoes vary in their ability to transmit viruses, impacting the global burden and transmission patterns of vector-borne diseases. Aedes aegypti has evolved from a forest-dwelling form that feeds on animals in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) to a globally invasive form that prefers humans, placing nearly half of the world’s population at risk of infection. This study shows that genetically divergent populations of Ae. aegypti from the USA and East Africa also differ in viral load in mosquito tissues post-infection and in duration of the incubation period inside the mosquito vector. Our genetic analyses identified over 1.6 million SNPs across 232 genes, but 17 of these accounted for more than half of all highly differentiated variants. When patterns of genetic differentiation for these genes were examined across Ae. aegypti populations worldwide, allele frequency distributions highlighted genes and pathways that may underlie this species’ transformation into a highly efficient urban vector, including loci involved in circadian rhythms, olfaction, saliva bioactivity, lipid metabolism, and chromatin remodeling. Notably, eight of these outlier genes mapped to a 920-kb island on chromosome 2, which we hypothesize represents a cluster of flavivirus host factors underlying the phenotypic differences observed in DENV-2 competence. Our analyses contribute to the growing literature on Ae. aegypti ecotype evolution as it expanded through SSA and out of Africa by highlighting putative associations between ecotype status and iSNP frequency patterns in highly divergent genes. Collectively, these highly divergent genes represent candidates for experimental approaches to illuminate functional roles in basic vector biology, as well as potential targets for genetic modification to abrogate arbovirus transmission.

Methods

Experimental model and subject details

Mosquito field collection

Aedes aegypti eggs for both populations were collected using ovitraps, consisting of 350-mL black, plastic cups lined with seed germination paper and partially filled with water. The Florida population originated from a suburban neighborhood in northern Palmetto of Manatee County, Florida (FL; 27.54845° N, − 82.55948° W), in July and August of 2018 as described [105]. Briefly, eggs were collected weekly from fifteen ovitraps spaced across an approximately 1-square-kilometer area. Egg papers were dried at the Manatee County Mosquito Control District facility before being returned to the Florida Medical Entomology Laboratory (FMEL) in mid-August. The Tanzanian population was sampled in June 2019 from five ovitraps placed at random across an approximately 0.25-square-kilometer area in Bagamoyo, Tanzania (TZ; − 6.43648° S, 38.90681° E). Eggs were dried and shipped to the University of Florida (Public Health Service Permit No. 20190621-3322A). Eggs from both locations were pooled separately by ovitrap and collection date and reared to adulthood to establish colonies. Following oviposition, ovaries and spermathecae were dissected from a subset of randomly selected females from each location, and carcasses were stored in molecular biology grade 99.5% ethanol at − 80 °C.

Maintenance and generation of laboratory mosquito colonies

Field-derived colonies of Ae. aegypti were maintained in a climate-controlled insectary at 27 °C and 70% ± 5% relative humidity with a 12:12 (light:dark) photoperiod. Eggs were hatched in a dilute nutrient broth consisting of 0.7 L of autoclaved deionized water, 0.25 g CM0001 Nutrient Broth (Oxoid, Hampshire, England), and 0.05 g active dried yeast [106]. Larvae were maintained on finely ground fish food (Koi and Goldfish Staple Diet, Drs. Foster and Smith, Rhinelander, WI) until pupation. Adult mosquitoes were provided 10% (w/v) sucrose ad libitum. For egg production, blood feeding on chickens was performed one to two times per week over a three-week period each generation. All experiments were performed on mosquitoes colonized fewer than six generations from the field.

Vero cell lines

Vero cells were obtained from ATCC (CCL-81) and cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (HI-FBS) and 1 × Antibiotic–Antimycotic reagent (100 units/mL penicillin, 100 units/mL streptomycin, and 0.25 μg/mL amphotericin B) (Gibco®, Life Technologies Corporation, CA, USA).

Dengue virus

The DENV-2 New Guinea C strain (NCBI Taxonomy ID: 11,065) was passaged in Vero cells and used in this study. Vero cells were grown to confluency in 25 cm3 flasks and were inoculated at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.01 virus plaque-forming units (PFU) per cell. Following a 1-h adsorption period at 37 °C in DMEM supplemented with 2% HI-FBS, the cultures were maintained with 5 mL of DMEM with 10% HI-FBS and 1 × Antibiotic–Antimycotic reagent. Prior to oral challenges, confluent monolayers were inoculated in triplicate and incubated for 6 days. Supernatant was harvested by serological pipette followed by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 5 min.

Method details

Mosquito infections

Four- to 6-day-old Ae. aegypti from FL and TZ populations were transferred to 0.5-L cup cages made of food-grade polyethylene-coated paper, limited to approximately 50 mosquitoes per cup cage. Mosquitoes were starved of sucrose solution for 16 h prior to oral challenges with infectious blood. Feeding was performed using an artificial feeding system under BSL-2/ACL-2 plus containment. Infectious blood meals consisted of virus supernatant, red blood cells (bovine), and HI-FBS in a 2:2:1 ratio with ATP (1 mM, final concentration) as a phagostimulant. To determine virus concentration in supernatants used for blood meals, samples of supernatant were supplemented with trehalose (20% final concentration) as a cryoprotectant and stored at − 80 °C until quantification by plaque assay. DENV-2 blood meal concentrations were found to range from 6.4 to 7.3 log10 PFU/mL, and experimental mosquitoes were fed through sausage casings stretched over water-jacketed glass feeders (Chemglass Life Science, NJ, USA) warmed to 37 °C by a circulating water bath. Mosquitoes were allowed to feed for 45 min to 1 h and were then cold-anesthetized on ice. Unfed and partially fed mosquitoes were removed, and only fully engorged mosquitoes were maintained through a 17-day incubation period at 27 °C and 80% relative humidity in an incubator with a 12:12 (light:dark) photoperiod. A sugar solution consisting of 10% (w/v) sucrose was provided ad libitum. Each infection was performed at least twice with different (independent) virus preparations for each replicate.

Vector competence assessment

At days 4, 7, 10, 14, and 17 post-feeding, subsets of experimental mosquitoes were transferred to a glove box and cold-anesthetized on ice. Legs and wings were removed with forceps. Saliva was then collected through capillary assays, where their bodies were affixed to sticky tape with the proboscis inserted into capillary tubes filled with immersion oil pre-warmed to 37 °C [107]. Salivation was allowed for 45 min, after which the contents in capillary tubes were expelled into tubes containing 200 µL mosquito diluent (DMEM supplemented with 2% HI-FBS and 1 × Antibiotic–Antimycotic). Legs and wings, saliva, and bodies were each stored separately at − 80 °C in 200 µL mosquito diluent. Collection tubes for mosquito tissues also included approximately 50 µL of autoclaved stainless-steel beads (0.9–1.4 mm diameter, Next Advance Inc., NY, USA) that had been measured and distributed among tubes in a biological safety cabinet prior to dissections. Two independent experiments were performed under the same conditions for both populations simultaneously.

Virus quantification

Mosquito tissues were thawed on ice and homogenized using a Bullet Blender® Storm 24 Homogenizer (Next Advance Lnc., NY, USA) at speed 12 for 3 min. Samples were then centrifuged at 13,300 × g for 2 min, and clarified supernatants were tested in duplicate by plaque assay.

Vero cells were seeded for plaque assays in 24-well plates at a density of 50,000 cells/well in 0.5 mL complete media (DMEM supplemented with 10% HI-FBS and 1 × Antibiotic–Antimycotic). After 48 h, media was removed and cells were inoculated with 100 µL of sample serially diluted (tenfold) in DMEM (2% HI-FBS) with an adsorption period of 1 h shaking every 15 min. Infected monolayers in each well were then covered with 500 mL methyl cellulose (8 g/L in mosquito diluent) and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After an incubation period of 5 days, plates were fixed with a methanol/acetone solution (1:1) and stained with 0.8% crystal violet (w/v) in methanol/acetone (1:1). Plaques were visualized and counted using a magnifying glass (5 ×) for each sample. Using serially diluted infectious virus stocks, the Ct reads were converted into PFU equivalents (PFUe) per sample using a standard curve based on the relationship between Ct values and log10 PFU/mL from linear regression (Fig. S3).

DNA library preparation and exome sequencing

For exome capture and library preparation, customized biotinylated oligonucleotide probes targeting all protein-coding loci and UTRs in the Ae. aegypti genome (AaegL5.2 gene assembly) were designed and synthesized by Roche Sequencing Solutions (IN, USA) based on genome coordinates specified by us, yielding probes for 86,640 targets covering 43,056,903 bp of sequence. The genome targets included exons and UTRs (when applicable) for 19,763 genes, of which 14,677 were protein-coding genes and 5086 were non-coding genes including pseudogenes and non-coding RNAs. Prior to exome capture and library preparation, genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from the carcasses of 24 female mosquitoes per population collected from the field in the egg stage as described above. Extractions were performed using the E.Z.N.A.® Tissue DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Georgia, USA), and 200 ng of gDNA per mosquito was used for exome capture and library preparation following the SeqCap EZ HyperCap protocol (Roche Sequencing Solutions, IN, USA). All quality control steps were performed by the UF Interdisciplinary Center for Biotechnology Research (UF-ICBR) and sequencing was performed with an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer on an S1 flow cell with paired-end 100-bp reads.

Read mapping and variant calling

Raw sequences were quality trimmed using fastp v0.20.1 [109], and mapped to the AaegL5.2 reference genome with BWA-MEM (Burrows-Wheeler-Alignment) v0.715 [110]. The genomic regions were excluded from analysis if (i) the max missing proportion was larger than 20%; (ii) the coverage was less than 8 × [111]; (iii) the length of consecutive bases with > 8 × coverage was smaller than 50 bp; and (iv) the coverage was greater than 3 times the mean sequenced region coverage (= 38.5 ×). Variants were identified using Freebayes v0.9.2 using the –standard-filters (mapping quality > 30, base quality > 20), –theta 0.01, and –no-population-priors options [112]. Variants which only appeared at the ends of reads and variants not supported by both forward and reverse reads were removed. The SNPs in “soft-masked” repeat regions in the AaegL5.2 reference genome were excluded from analysis. Only biallelic SNPs were used for further analysis. Any genotype calls supported by less than 8 × coverage were recoded to missing and variants where more than 20% of the individuals had missing genotype calls were dropped. The LD filter was not applied unless otherwise indicated, because SNPs in the Ae. aegypti genome exhibit spatial clustering, and this clustering, including relative positions with respect to the centromere, was considered an informative genomic feature. The variant effect predictor software SNPeff LGPLv2 [113] was used to annotate variants and classify their predicted effects, including synonymous and nonsynonymous substitutions, using the reference genome AaegL5.3 gene annotations (VectorBase release 50).

In addition, we downloaded genome data of 113 individuals across the range of Ae. aegypti from the NCBI Sequence Reads Archive [20, 24, 25], emphasizing sub-Saharan Africa. These included West Africa (n = 36), Central Africa (n = 9), East Africa (n = 33), South Africa (n = 3), North America (n = 29, 20 from California, 3 from Florida, 6 from Puerto Rico), and Thailand (n = 3). After alignment, we performed SNP calling on all populations using the same pipeline as described in the previous paragraph. Results were utilized for the downstream phylogenetic and population structure analyses throughout regions.

Functional annotations of genes

Functional roles of all genes containing iSNPs were investigated using VectorBase (https://vectorbase.org), focusing on information provided for gene ontology (GO) (http://geneontology.org/), orthology (https://orthomcl.org/orthomcl/app/), and protein domains from InterPro scans (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/). For genes lacking a formal name in genome version AaegL5.3 (i.e., genes annotated as unspecified product), we followed the literature where possible, using names from previous publications. For unspecified products without associated literature, we deferred first to names used for orthologous genes in Anopheles gambiae or Drosophila melanogaster, and then if uncharacterized in those organisms, we proposed names based on putative function inferred from GO and InterPro. To classify genes into biological process categories, we utilized the 15 biological process categories provided on the FlyBase (http://flybase.org), GO summary ribbon with one modification (substituted other metabolic process for small molecule metabolism) and added two additional categories, non-coding RNA or pseudogene and unknown, bringing our total number to 17. We relied heavily on FlyBase categorizations for genes with patent orthology to Drosophila melanogaster (i.e., reciprocal best scores from blastp searches). However, it is worth noting that many of the orthologs on FlyBase fit into multiple categories, so in such cases we chose a single category weighted by functional information provided by FlyBase, as well as from the sources specified above.

Brief description of biological process categories for genes with known or predicted function(s)

Behavior (4): olfaction, circadian rhythms.

Cell cycle/proliferation (8): mitosis, centrosome duplication, cell division.

Cell organization/biogenesis (17): cytoskeleton, cell–cell adhesion, morphogenesis.

Development (11): segmental development, sex determination, factors linked to development, homeobox genes and other developmental regulators.

DNA metabolism (4): DNA unwinding, replication, synthesis, repair.

Gene expression (29): chromatin remodeling, transcription factors, mRNA splicing.

Immune system (5): genes associated with an innate immunity pathway, serine proteases linked to immunity, genes in the RNAi pathway.

Nervous system process (5): long-term potentiation, synaptic vesicle, including the transport of neurotransmitters, GPCRs associated with nervous system activity.

Non-coding RNAs/pseudogenes (3): include any annotated non-coding RNA.

Other biological processes (16): include miscellaneous biological processes that do not fit in other categories.

Other metabolic processes (20): chitin metabolism, lipid metabolism, transporters linked with metabolic function.

Reproduction (4): anything related to gametes.

Signaling (13): includes all GPCRs regardless of their ultimate function except those linked with the nervous system, all ion channels.

Unknown (29): includes genes annotated as unspecified products for which function could not be predicted from VectorBase, FlyBase, Interpro, or Gene Ontology databases.

Putative protein–protein interaction network

A putative protein–protein interaction network was constructed for genes with iSNPs above the 95th percentile for at least one of two metrics: (i) the informative SNP count per gene (95th percentile = 14.5 SNPs) and (ii) the informative SNP count per kilobase pair (95th percentile = 7.5 SNPs per 1000 bp). The network was created using the STRING database v11.5 [114], which uses an earlier version of the Ae. aegypti genome. Therefore, protein sequences of the 17 outlier genes meeting our criteria were downloaded from VectorBase (genome version AaegL5.3) and used to generate our STRING query list. Sixteen of these proteins were found in the database. Basic settings in STRING were as follows: Network type: full STRING network; meaning of network edges: evidence; active interaction sources: boxes checked for textmining, experiments, databases, co-expression, neighborhood, gene fusion, and co-occurrence; minimum required interaction score: high confidence (0.700), and max number of interactors to show: 1 st shell: no more than 50 interactors, 2nd shell: none. Interactions above the high-confidence level (score = 0.7) were shown. To generate Fig. S2, the STRING output was revised with Cytoscape (version 3.9.1) to annotate each protein node according to the most current VectorBase accession numbers (genome version AaegL5.3) and to color-code outliers according to the 17 biological process categories described above and used in Fig. 4B–D.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Vector competence analysis

To compare vector competence for DENV-2 (New Guinea C) between populations, we used plaque assay data from each time point to determine population-specific rates of infection and dissemination, viral loads by tissue type, and incubation periods required for virus dissemination from the midgut. Infection rates (IRs) were calculated as the proportion of blood-fed mosquito bodies that were positive for DENV, while dissemination rates (DRs) were calculated as the proportion of infected mosquito bodies with infected legs and wings. Viral loads for mosquito bodies and legs/wings were reported as PFU/sample as described above. To estimate the incubation period required for disseminated infections in 10%, 50% and 90% of mosquitoes (dissemination incubation period: DIP10, DIP50, DIP90), we used the following formula:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{DIP}p = \frac{\left(log\frac{p}{1-p}\right)-\mathrm{Constant}}{\mathrm{Coefficient}}$$\end{document}DIPp=logp1-p-ConstantCoefficientwhere the constant and coefficient in Eq. (2) are from population-specific logistic regression models that estimate the probability of a disseminated infection as a function of time post-exposure. In each model, infection status for legs/wings was the response variable and day post-exposure was the predictor variable.

For statistical analyses of IRs and DRs, we used logistic regression to model infection status as a binary outcome by tissue type and day post-exposure with mosquito population as the predictor variable. Statistical outcomes are reported as odds ratios (OR) at each time point, interpreted as the odds of an infection occurring in FL mosquitoes relative to the odds of an infection in mosquitoes from TZ. For viral load, we used regression models appropriate for over-dispersed count data, including negative binomial regression for data that excluded uninfected mosquitoes (bodies) and zero-inflated negative binomial regression for data from legs/wings, which included samples that did not produce virus plaques (i.e., bodies were infected but virus was absent or below limit of detection in legs/wings). For both types of models, virus titer from plaque assays was treated as a discrete, dependent variable modeled by tissue type and day post-exposure with mosquito population as the predictor variable. To facilitate interpretation of our results, we reported statistical outcomes as incidence rate ratios (IRR) rather than regression coefficients, which provides an estimate of the factor by which viral load changed in FL mosquitoes relative to TZ mosquitoes. All data analyses were performed in Stata v18.0 (StataCorp LLC, Texas, USA). Figures were produced using Python seaborn v0.11.2 and matplotlib v3.4.3.

Phylogenetic and population structure analysis

An unrooted neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree was constructed with PHYLIP v3.698 [115] based on exome-wide pairwise FST averages using sequencing data from our TZ and FL study populations and from the 113 Ae. aegypti individuals described above [20, 24, 25]. We used the Neighbor program in PHYLIP v3.698 [115] for calculating distances between Ae. aegypti populations and produced tree illustrations using MEGA 11 [116].

We inferred population structure by Principal Components Analysis (PCA), which clustered individuals based on genotype similarity from a panel of biallelic SNPs. SNPs were filtered for linkage disequilibrium (LD) to reduce marker independence (window size = 50 SNPs, step size = 5 SNPs, r2 threshold = 0.1). All calculations and analyses were performed in Python v3.6.6 scikit-allel module [117].

Populations were also reconstructed to infer the genetic history of Ae. aegypti by estimating the degree of admixture of ancestral alleles in individual genomes using ADMIXTURE v1.3.0 [30]. The same LD-filtered dataset was used for analysis. A total of three independent replicates with ten iterations of each replicate were performed to infer the optimum number of ancestral populations (K) from 2 to 10. The best-fitting K was determined by the low cross-validation error compared to other K values.

Genetic analysis and population statistics

Sliding window analyses of Hudson fixation index (FST) [26], nucleotide diversity (π) and Tajima’s D [32] were performed for Ae. aegypti sequences from FL and TZ. Comparisons across chromosomes used a 1-Mbp sliding window with a step length of 500 kbp. We first examined genetic differentiation (or distance) between populations using the Hudson FST, wherein a value close to 0 indicates that the genetic distance between populations is negligible and 1 implies complete differentiation (i.e., fixed genotypes). Nucleotide diversity was then assessed, which quantified the degree of polymorphism in a DNA sequence as the average number of nucleotide differences per site in all pairwise combinations of individuals sampled. Lastly, Tajima’s D was used to examine the molecular evolution of each gene region by population. This statistic quantifies the difference between two estimates of θ = 4Neµ so that D = θπ—θK, where Ne is the effective population size, µ is the mutation rate, and θπ and θK are based, respectively, on the number of pairwise differences and the number of segregating sites in a sample of nucleotide sequences from our study populations [118]. Under the idealized condition (i.e., drift-mutation equilibrium), θπ and θK are approximately equal (D = 0) and the DNA sequence follows the neutral model of molecular evolution [32]. Deviation from neutrality indicates that one or more evolutionary processes have influenced the nucleotide diversity segregating within the population. Positive Tajima’s D values indicate an excess of high-frequency mutations, which may result from balancing selection or population contraction, while negative values indicate the opposite, an excess of low-frequency mutations attributable to purifying selection, a population expansion, a recent selective sweep, or population admixture [33]. All calculations and analyses were performed in Python v3.6.6 scikit-allel module version 1.2.0 [117].

Following the sliding window analyses, FST, π, and Tajima’s D were further calculated for genomic regions using gene element annotations and region locations from VectorBase (https://vectorbase.org), based on the VectorBase-58_AaegytiLVP_AGWG.gff file. Analyses were performed in Python v3.6.6 scikit-allel module [117]. Comparisons of population genetic statistics across genomic features (coding vs. non-coding), feature types (exons vs. UTRs), and chromosomes (Chr 1–3), both within and between populations, were performed using Kruskal–Wallis tests with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons in Python v3.6.6 SciPy module [119].

Linkage disequilibrium was calculated for genes containing iSNPs. Gene regions were defined based on GFF annotations, without inclusion of flanking sequences, and only genes containing at least two SNPs were retained. Pairwise LD (r2) among SNPs within each gene was estimated using the Rogers and Huff method implemented in the Python v3.6.6 scikit-allel module version 1.2.0 [117, 120].

Genotype frequency estimation

To examine geographic divergence patterns for the most highly differentiated loci, we determined iSNP genotypes in each outlier gene for 24 mosquitoes from FL, 24 mosquitoes from TZ, and 113 additional individuals from across Ae. aegypti’s range [20, 24, 25]. For each sampling location, we estimated gene-specific average frequencies of the FL-dominant allele. To do this, we converted sequence data to numerical genotype values at each iSNP so that homozygotes of the FL-dominant allele = 1, homozygotes of the TZ-dominant allele = 0, and heterozygotes = 0.5. Genotype values at each iSNP were then averaged by gene for each individual and then gene-specific values were averaged for all individuals per location.
