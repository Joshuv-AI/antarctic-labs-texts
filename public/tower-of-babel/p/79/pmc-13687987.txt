Transcatheter suture embolization for ventricular premature beats originating from the distal great cardiac vein: A case report
Authors: Shen Huang, Xiuyu Wang, Chunwei Zhang, Qiyan Li
Published: 2026

Key Teaching Points •Nonabsorbable suture embolization is a novel salvage therapy for radiofrequency catheter ablation–refractory ventricular premature beats (VPBs) originating from the distal great cardiac vein. By occluding the small arterial feeders of the arrhythmogenic substrate, this technique achieves sustained ischemia-mediated elimination of ectopic foci.•Targeted embolization of 1–2 mm arterial branches can eliminate the arrhythmogenic substrate without major acute complications. The procedure relies on conventional coronary interventional skills and requires multidisciplinary collaboration between electrophysiology and coronary intervention teams.•Postoperative VPB resolution is delayed and may take up to 3 months; patient counseling is essential. This delayed effect is attributed to progressive ischemia-induced myocardial apoptosis, which differs from the immediate success end point of conventional radiofrequency ablation.•This technique is low-cost and feasible in resource-constrained settings. No specialized ablation equipment or proprietary consumables are required, making it an attractive alternative for centers with limited access to advanced electrophysiology tools.

Introduction

Ventricular premature beats (VPBs) originating from the distal great cardiac vein (DGCV) or deep myocardial sites pose a therapeutic challenge owing to their inaccessibility to conventional radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA).1 Salvage therapies such as ethanol ablation (transvenous anatomical approach), bipolar ablation (endocardial/epicardial anatomical approach), and epicardial ablation via pericardiocentesis (epicardial anatomical approach) are limited by high complication rates or specialized equipment requirements.2 Herein, we report the first case of transcatheter nonabsorbable suture embolization for RFCA-refractory DGCV-originated VPBs, providing a novel therapeutic approach.

Case report

A 65-year-old woman was admitted with a 13-year history of palpitations, which worsened over 4 months. She had no history of coronary artery disease, hypertension, or diabetes mellitus. Physical examination revealed a blood pressure of 112/78 mm Hg, irregular cardiac rhythm, and no pathological murmurs.

Surface electrocardiography (ECG) showed VPB bigeminy (Figure 1A), and 24-hour Holter monitoring recorded 33,869 VPBs (25.9% burden). Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a left ventricular end-diastolic diameter of 48 mm and a left ventricular ejection fraction of 50%. After written informed consent, RFCA was performed using the CARTO 3 system (Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA). Via right femoral vein puncture, a multipolar mapping catheter (Rithm Rx, Synaptic Medical,Beijing, China) was placed in the DGCV, identifying coronary sinus 3–4 as the earliest activation site (12 ms pre-QRS onset) (Figure 1B and 1C). A 3.5-mm ablation catheter (ThermoCool SmartTouch Surround Flow, Biosense Webster,Irvine, CA) was used for mapping at the right coronary cusp, left ventricular endocardium, and DGCV. The earliest activation times pre-QRS onset at these sites were 13, 17, and 21 ms, respectively (Figure 1D). No myocardial capture was achieved during pacing at the DGCV. Ablation (25 W, 43°C, 30 mL/min irrigated saline) reduced VPB frequency initially in the DGCV but was aborted because of abrupt impedance and temperature elevation, with immediate VPB recurrence. Subsequent ablation at the right coronary cusp and left ventricular endocardium (40–45 W, 43°C, 50–60 seconds) was ineffective.

Diluted contrast injection via the ablation catheter visualized the anterior interventricular vein (Figure 2A). Left coronary angiography (Figure 2B) showed that the VPB origin was supplied by the first diagonal branch (D1) of the left anterior descending artery and 2 left anterior ventricular branches (LAVB1 and LAVB2) from the circumflex artery. The embolization procedure was performed the next day with the assistance of the coronary intervention team. Right radial artery puncture was performed to place a 6-F Braidin II sheath (APT Medical, Hunan, China). A Blackeel guidewire (0.035 in, 150 cm, APT Medical, Hunan, China) and a March guiding catheter (6 F, 100 cm, ABU3.5, APT Medical, Hunan, China) were advanced into the left coronary artery for angiography and target vessel localization (Figure 2C). Precut nonabsorbable 4-0 suture segments (8–10 mm in length), percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty guidewire (Fielder XT, 0.014 in/0.009 in × 190 cm, Asahi Intecc, Seto, Aichi, Japan), and an Instantpass microcatheter (2.1 F × 130 cm, APT Medical, Hunan, China) were prepared and set up for the embolization procedure (Figure 2D). Intraoperatively, unfractionated heparin was administered for anticoagulation, with the activated clotting time maintained above 300 seconds. Under fluoroscopic guidance, the guidewire (ASAHI SION blue, 0.014 in × 180 cm, Asahi Intecc, Seto, Aichi, Japan) navigated the microcatheter into each target feeding artery sequentially, and the percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty guidewire gently advanced the suture segments through the microcatheter into the vessels (Figure 2E). Successful embolization of the 3 feeding arteries, including the first and second left anterior ventricular branches (LAVB1 and LAVB2) and the first diagonal branch (D1), was achieved (Figure 2F, 2H, and 2J). Repeat angiography confirmed no distal opacification of the target vessels, indicating complete occlusion (Figure 2G, 2I, and 2K). Postoperatively, the patient was prescribed oral aspirin (100 mg daily) for 1 month.

VPBs persisted immediately postprocedure. Serum myocardial necrosis markers were elevated: myoglobin (82.6 μg/mL vs preoperative 33.7 μg/mL), creatine kinase-MB (34.4 ng/mL vs 0.3 ng/mL), and high-sensitivity troponin (4687 pg/mL vs 1.5 pg/mL). Transthoracic echocardiography showed preserved cardiac function, and no ST-T changes were observed on postoperative ECG (Figure 3A and 3B). The patient’s VPBs were slightly decreased on continuous ECG monitoring on the first postoperative day, accompanied by relieved palpitations and absence of chest pain. The patient was discharged on the second day after the procedure. At 2-week outpatient follow-up, the patient reported further improvement in symptoms, and physical examination revealed irregular heart rhythm, while no VPBs were observed on routine 12-lead ECG (Figure 3C). 3 months after the procedure, the patient’s symptoms were completely resolved, and 24-hour Holter monitoring demonstrated total elimination of ventricular ectopy.Figure 3ECG findings after suture embolization. Discussion

VPBs originating from deep myocardial sites or the DGCV are often refractory to conventional RFCA because of the deep target location and vascular barrier effects.2 Current clinical salvage therapies include ethanol ablation, bipolar ablation, epicardial ablation via pericardiocentesis, and ablation targeting adjacent anatomical sites, all of which have significant limitations. Absorbable silk suture embolization has previously been used only for sealing distal coronary artery perforations and preoperative embolization of cerebral arteriovenous malformations.3,4 To our knowledge, this is the first case report globally describing the application of nonabsorbable suture embolization for the treatment of VPBs originating from the distal coronary vein. By occluding the feeding arteries of the arrhythmogenic substrate, this technique provides a novel therapeutic approach for refractory VPBs.

The core innovation of this technique lies in adapting the “physical occlusion + thrombus enhancement” dual mechanism for arrhythmia treatment. Segments of nonabsorbable 4-0 suture, with dimensions precisely matching the small feeding arteries (1–2 mm in diameter) of the target site, not only mechanically occlude the vessel lumen but also act as foreign bodies to activate platelet aggregation and the intrinsic coagulation cascade, forming stable thrombi that completely block blood supply to the target myocardium. Ultimately, we hypothesize that the arrhythmogenic substrate may be eliminated via ischemia-induced myocardial apoptosis (presumed to occur over 2–14 days), leading to sustained VPB suppression. This mechanism avoids the risks of chemical diffusion and reflux associated with ethanol ablation.2 In contrast, ethanol ablation relies on the chemical toxicity of anhydrous ethanol, resulting in uncontrollable lesion size and a high risk of nontarget tissue injury.2 Bipolar ablation requires specialized equipment and advanced operator skills, while epicardial ablation via pericardiocentesis carries a high complication rate (5%–30%).5

Vascular recanalization after transcatheter embolization is a key determinant of long-term procedural efficacy, which is primarily governed by the properties of embolic materials. Previous translational and clinical studies have confirmed that absorbable sutures and biodegradable embolic agents are prone to complete arterial recanalization within months after embolization, which may lead to the recurrence of arrhythmias.6 In contrast, nonabsorbable sutures can achieve stable mechanical occlusion of small arterial branches with markedly lower recanalization risk, providing durable vessel obliteration.7 In the present case, 4-0 nonabsorbable sutures were used to occlude the 1- to 2-mm small arterial branches adjacent to the DGCV, which contributed to the sustained suppression of VPBs during 3-month follow-up. Nonetheless, long-term clinical and angiographic follow-up is still required to further validate the persistent occlusive effect of this novel technique.

A prominent advantage of nonabsorbable suture embolization is its cost-effectiveness and safety. No specialized consumables or equipment are required, resulting in significantly lower costs compared with existing techniques. The procedure relies on conventional coronary interventional skills, with a short learning curve, making it easily adaptable in primary hospitals. Intraoperative anticoagulation with unfractionated heparin (ACT > 300 seconds) and postoperative oral aspirin for 1 month effectively reduce thrombotic complications, which is consistent with standard coronary intervention anticoagulation protocols. In this case, the patient developed only transient elevation of myocardial necrosis markers without serious complications (eg, bleeding, thrombosis, or cardiac dysfunction), maintaining stable cardiac function postoperatively with sustained suppression of VPBs. This safety profile is consistent with the proven safety of nonabsorbable suture embolization for coronary perforation sealing.3,6 Potential risks include nontarget vessel embolization, myocardial ischemia/infarction, and foreign body reactions. Therefore, this technique should be performed by experienced operators with multidisciplinary collaboration between electrophysiology and coronary intervention teams.

Limitations

This study has several limitations: (1) It is a single-case report, requiring validation in large-scale studies. (2) Long-term efficacy and vascular recanalization risk after suture placement need further follow-up (nonabsorbable sutures avoid absorption-related recanalization but require long-term monitoring for potential foreign body reactions). (3) Its application is restricted to VPBs with identifiable small arterial feeders, requiring collaboration between electrophysiology and coronary intervention teams. (4) Postoperative VPB resolution is delayed, requiring patient counseling to avoid anxiety.

Conclusion

Transcatheter nonabsorbable suture embolization is a safe, low-cost, and effective novel therapy for achieving sustained suppression of RFCA-refractory DGCV-originated VPBs. It overcomes the limitations of existing treatments (eg, ethanol ablation and bipolar ablation) with distinct approaches and is particularly suitable for resource-constrained settings. Future studies should validate its long-term effectiveness and expand its application to other refractory VPB subsets (eg, arrhythmias originating from the left ventricular summit).
