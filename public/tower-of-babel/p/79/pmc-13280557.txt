When Science Outpaces Infrastructure: People, Process, and Technology Challenges for Precision Dosing in Children
Authors: Tamorah Lewis, Karim Jessa, Vijay Ivaturi, Andreas Noack, Dilum P. Aluthge, David Müller‐Widmann, Kathryn Capello, Matthew Fong, Ashleigh Miatello, Melanie Barwick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe implementation of novel precision medicine tools in pediatric medicine requires buy‐in from multiple interest groups and a shift from traditional medical paradigms. In an effort to implement a precision drug‐dosing project for an anti‐coagulant drug in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) at a large children's hospital, a multi‐specialty team made up of clinicians, scientists, private industry, and technology specialists formed and worked collaboratively over multiple years. Despite best efforts, the project's success was hindered by multiple foreseen and unforeseen barriers. Through the telling of a specific case example, these barriers and the key considerations for mitigating them are discussed to give future teams better knowledge and preparation abilities. The themes discussed are encompassed by “people,” “process,” and “technology.” The integration of precision dosing tools into hospital systems (such as electronic health records [EHR]) demands careful coordination across clinicians, pharmacists, nurses, and hospital information technology (IT) teams. Informaticians play a key role in safely embedding innovations into clinical practice. Ethics boards face challenges evaluating novel technologies like AI/ML. Comprehensive representation on ethics boards is essential for effective oversight of new digital tools. Technical integration of third‐party applications into EHRs is complex, especially across international borders. Differences in measurement units, coding systems, and test environments can cause deployment issues. Privacy assessments, secure data access protocols, and data residency and compliance are critical. Finally, sustaining precision medicine innovations requires institutional investment beyond initial research funding. Success hinges on attention to people, process, and technology.

The Case for a Precision Dosing Feasibility and Implementation Trial (As Told by Dr. Tamorah Lewis, Lead of the Model‐Informed Precision Dosing Project)

As a neonatologist, I often witness how mg/kg‐based population‐based dosing fails our patients. For drugs with large inter‐individual variability in clearance and volume, population‐based dosing can lead to unpredictable exposures and clinical risk. For example, many infants fail to reach vancomycin exposure goals with current dosing guidelines [1, 2, 3], and there are some notorious drugs used in the NICU for which “titration” is the norm. Examples of drugs that undergo extensive titration and might benefit from improved a priori dosing include opiates such as fentanyl and morphine, and diuretics such as furosemide.

With increased pharmacological knowledge, there are ways to approach drug dosing in an individualized manner, thereby reducing variability and increasing predictability. By using pharmacokinetic models to estimate an individual patient's clearance and volume of distribution in real time, tools can suggest a precise dose tailored to a patient's physiology. Patient covariates, such as age, weight, laboratory values, and genetics, can be incorporated into the model for maximum precision. Such tools have led to improved precision in drug dosing [4, 5].

One example of a drug with wide variability in dose requirements is enoxaparin, a first‐line anticoagulant used in pediatric patients. We know that multiple‐dose titrations are routinely required to achieve therapeutic goals in neonates. Having been frustrated by this poor dosing as a clinician, I strongly believe that we can and must do better. Pharmacokinetic model‐informed precision dosing (MIPD) is a powerful tool for individualizing patient doses and improving attainment of therapeutic goals [6, 7, 8]. This is the promise and future of precision/personalized medicine.

In 2021, I was approached by a pharmacology scientist who had recently developed a novel dosing tool. The new prescriber‐friendly tool could be embedded in the electronic health record (I), providing real‐time recommendations for precision doses for medications. This collaborator was very interested in testing the feasibility of this tool in special populations, including neonates admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). I created a team comprising neonatologists, pharmacometrics experts, implementation scientists, and pharmacists. We wrote a grant together, which was funded, and we launched a project to test the feasibility of precision dosing in the NICU.

It has been three years since the grant was funded, and we have yet to dose a patient with the precision dosing tool. In this manuscript, we share our experience with barriers that we have overcome and those that we have failed to overcome in the pursuit of MIPD in neonates. We hope that this case study serves as a template for future clinical and research teams, ethics boards, and technical and hospital leadership teams when they consider undertaking precision medicine projects. This is a tale of people, process, and technology (Figure 1).

The Core Aspects of a Precision Drug Dosing Implementation Program.

People: Clinician Peer and Prescriber Buy‐In for a Different Way to Dose Drugs

Those of us who are embedded in pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic science understand that there is often very little evidence informing current dosing paradigms in pediatric medicine. Many prescribers either assume that the doses listed in formularies are evidence‐based or have grudgingly accepted the status quo—that we are using drugs off‐label with inadequate evidence.

When a new paradigm is introduced, such as using pharmacokinetic models to calculate precision doses based on an individual patient's predicted clearance and volume of distribution, it can raise eyebrows. When the idea of MIPD was first socialized among NICU colleagues, many were open‐minded and curious. After both casual conversations and detailed presentations, the clinician interest holders (NICU pharmacists and neonatal nurse practitioners) were open to proceeding with the idea under the auspices of research.

Lesson: Convene a local clinician champion group. This enables co‐design of study details, as well as ongoing real‐time updates to the clinical team closest to patient care.

Process: The Complex Interplay of EHR‐Prescriber‐Pharmacist‐Bedside Nurse in Modern Medicine

For clinicians to enter drug orders within an electronic health record, multiple behind‐the‐scenes activities must occur smoothly and safely before a patient is dosed with a drug. For example, both the drug and the dosing parameters must be built separately in the EHR; in this case, Epic. In addition, this order must be accessible to the correct type of user, have built‐in dosing bounds and safety checks, and contain sufficient clinical decision support for both the clinician and pharmacist to understand and approve the order. After the pharmacist has reviewed, checked, approved, and released the drug, the bedside nurse must have sufficient information to feel comfortable checking the dose and administering it to the patient.

For innovations in drug dosing, all these processes must be carefully considered and designed to meet the standards and workflows of traditional drug prescribing and dosing. For example, because the NICU primarily orders drugs through specialized NICU order panels, any new precision dosing tool must be built on or integrated with unit‐specific dosing processes. In large hospital systems, hundreds of pharmacists may encounter innovative drug orders, so innovation‐specific information must be included in the order to enable a pharmacist not on the research study team to understand and approve a non‐traditional dose. The involvement of an informatician can help safely introduce complex changes in regular clinical workflows.

Lesson: Some drug order and end‐user complexities are universal to all EHRs, and some are unique to certain EHR products. Regardless, it is important to engage stakeholders early in the design process of the study or clinical innovation program to identify unexpected complexities within the system of inpatient drug delivery.

Process: Hospital Ethics Boards and the Rapidly Changing Landscape of Novel Technology Tool Use

When patient‐facing, technology‐dependent innovations are introduced into research projects, research ethics boards face the challenge of ensuring that they understand the tools and that the planned protocols adequately protect patient safety. New technologies and clinical tools sometimes challenge the mental models of existing research ethics boards, and artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) are prime examples. In the rapidly evolving landscape of precision medicine, new tools are being developed, tested, and implemented. The ethics board is often one of the first “safety stops” before these tools reach patients.

Some hospitals have established new ethics board panels focused on AI and ML, enabling a critical mass of expertise to adequately vet the safety of novel technologies. But the creation of these special ethics boards can introduce unexpected complexities and challenges.

For example, if an AI/ML‐specialized board lacks specific domain expertise, there can be blind spots in research safety and process management. In this case study, the absence of a pharmacist on the AI/ML ethics panel led to many standard drug and pharmacy‐specific issues not being queried or addressed until after the research ethics board had issued approval. This sequential troubleshooting, as opposed to parallel troubleshooting, can significantly delay the overall project initiation and completion.

Lesson : Special ethics review boards for novel technologies (such as AI and ML) have both advantages and challenges. These specialized boards should still include traditional domain and subject‐matter experts in addition to members with expertise in the new technologies.

Process: Hospital Electronic Privacy Needs and the Interface With Precision Medicine Tools

Integrating a third‐party application into a hospital's EHR to achieve precision medicine goals is challenging. This challenge is compounded when the application is developed in an international market and when the hospital's EHR lacks the appropriate testing tools. The precision dosing tool, developed in the United States, was built to support the units of measurement (such as mg/dL for serum creatinine) and standardized clinical drug vocabularies (such as RxNorm) specified in the official documentation provided to the vendor by Epic. This documentation did not sufficiently represent the significant heterogeneity across Epic instances within different hospital systems (even within the same country) or the variations that exist across different countries. For example, SickKids uses molar units for serum creatinine and relies on ATC codes and First Databank to uniquely identify drugs. These discrepancies led to initial issues when processing SickKids' medication IDs and the units used for some model covariates.

Further complications arose from differences between Epic's official sandbox environment—an instance of the EHR available to third‐party developers—and SickKids' instance of Epic. Although the sandbox is intended to replicate a client's environment, it does not simulate hospital‐system‐specific configurations or data structures of real client environments. This is particularly impactful given that most Epic instances are highly customized for each specific hospital system. Consequently, behaviors implemented in the tool based on the official sandbox environment led to issues with hospital‐system‐specific patient identifiers and Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) coding systems when the application was deployed at SickKids. Additionally, inconsistencies across SickKids test and production environments—such as inactive endpoints, mismatched FHIR versions, and misconfigured URLs—caused further delays and test failures. Due to security and privacy concerns, SickKids did not permit the vendor to have access to any SickKids test environments. The lack of real‐time joint troubleshooting capabilities added time delays and personnel costs to every step of testing.

As with any external tool, integration also required a comprehensive Privacy Impact Assessment (PIA) and Threat Risk Assessment (TRA), given the tool's need to access patient data via Application Programming Interfaces (APIs) to generate dosing recommendations. This access triggered the need for strict governance protocols and data‐sharing agreements. At one point, temporary access to production‐level data was granted for debugging purposes, underscoring the importance of pre‐authorized, time‐limited access controls and clear privacy frameworks.

Lesson: It is crucial to factor in time to assess not only data and privacy considerations but also the technical limitations of vendor‐facing development environments, such as Epic's test instance, during project planning. Tools that appear functional in generic test environments may not be compatible with real‐world EHR configurations, particularly in cross‐border contexts. Early alignment between tool vendors and other stakeholders on data standards, measurement units, and environment‐specific testing is essential for successful deployment.

Process: International Borders, Data Storage, and Data Sharing When Protected Health Information Is Involved

In this study, the vendor's application was deployed on Amazon Web Services (AWS), which operates data centers across various countries. To align with data residency preferences, it was important that the AWS resources supporting the application be located in the same country as the hospital system—Canada. Fortunately, AWS has multiple data centers within Canada, allowing the application to be hosted in‐country. This setup ensures that data remains within Canadian borders, which is beneficial from a privacy and compliance standpoint.

However, this approach also presents some challenges. One notable drawback is cost. AWS resources in Canadian data centers tend to be more expensive than those in the U.S. Additionally, while Canada has AWS infrastructure, many countries do not. The absence of local AWS data centers in those regions could pose a significant barrier to innovation.

Because this study involved protected health information (PHI), the vendor was required to meet strict compliance standards. To support this, the vendor engaged a subcontractor with expertise in PHI‐related compliance. The financial cost of these services was substantial. Moreover, even with the subcontractor's support, the vendor had to dedicate considerable internal resources: Person‐hours for implementation, testing, deployment, and ongoing maintenance of the compliance program. These efforts not only increased the overall cost but also delayed the project timeline (Figure 2).

Lesson : During the project design phase, conduct detailed, accurate pre‐planning for data storage resources and costs, as well as for any international considerations. Create a budgetary estimate for costs (both initial and ongoing). Clarify assignments and responsibility for different line‐item costs.

Process: The Perception of Innovation in Special Patient Populations by Drug Regulatory Bodies

Each country has unique regulations around drug research in children. Often, research oversight regulations unintentionally impose additional burdens on certain patient populations and can deter innovative studies. For example, in neonates, many drugs that are very commonly used are prescribed off‐label [9]. If off‐label use of a drug is a trigger for regulatory oversight when this drug is used for research purposes, this means that many standard‐of‐care drugs are challenging to study.

In the case of precision dosing for enoxaparin in neonates, a regulatory agency became involved because the drug is used off‐label in neonates. This off‐label use has been the standard of care for over a decade in NICUs worldwide. During discussions with regulatory bodies regarding the precision dosing study, the team had to justify the initial clinical use and provide efficacy and safety information, despite the drug being prescribed daily for clinical purposes. Hence, discussions of actual innovations in dosing approaches are often convoluted and delayed by discussions related to off‐label clinical use.

Concerning innovative tools, regulatory bodies have separate “drug” sections and “device” sections. This siloing of review and expertise is especially challenging when the device under study relates to the administration of a drug. Whether a tool carries specific designations, such as “clinical decision support,” can significantly affect the level of regulatory scrutiny and the need for oversight. This does not account for the level of risk associated with the tool or innovation in its intended use context.

Lesson : Clinicians and researchers should recognize discrepancies between policy and practice. Often, policy and regulatory bodies’ approaches are incongruent with current practice and opportunities for innovation.

Technology: Unforeseen Cost and Need for Institutional Investment to Kick‐Start and Maintain Innovations in Precision Medicine

The implementation of the MIPD dosing intervention was two‐fold, requiring technical integration in Epic and organizational and clinical implementation. SickKids’ Information Services managed the technical aspects of the integration. Organizational change management and clinical implementation required an informatics‐based approach. A super‐user implementation team was formed, comprising interested and affected parties who could inform how best to incorporate the tool into workflows. The team provided staff education and change management supported by the research team, super‐users, and technical support at SickKids, who also interfaced with the intervention purveyor. The NIH grant awarded for this project supported many start‐up activities for the planned feasibility trial, but many resources and personnel required at SickKids and the vendor throughout the implementation process were not covered by the grant budget. This required both parties to invest financial support to keep the project moving forward. Because the grant did not cover vendor expenses, all costs associated with the tool installation and maintenance were borne by the vendor.

We became aware early on that the vendor's third‐party costs of tailored precision medicine interventions are not sustainable with research funds. This highlights a need for healthcare organizations to consider the additional costs of sustaining precision medicine interventions over time. In the era of generative AI, many organizations are seeking to innovate in the precision medicine space and are incorporating these goals into their strategic plans. This aligns with the common weighting of discovery science at the expense of implementation science, creating an issue in which we continue to create innovations that accumulate research and translational bottlenecks without considering how they may be implemented and sustained.

Lesson : Total cost of ownership over the lifetime of the tool (beyond the initial research and implementation phases) must be clearly defined and accounted for to ensure sustainability.

Conclusion

As pioneers in MIPD for special populations and other precision medicine innovations, we must give ourselves grace. It is tempting for individuals to shoulder failures as personal ones. “Did I not fight hard enough for this?” is a question that frequently bubbles into Dr. Lewis' mind when she thinks of the story shared here. However, the determination of individuals and teams is often inadequate in the face of increasingly complex systems, legal requirements, and a rapidly changing landscape. A standard approach to how teams and hospitals evaluate and “vet” these projects can help anticipate many of the issues when discussed a priori and, at a minimum, can initiate projects with “eyes wide open” to help achieve success and sustainability. Attention to people, process, and technology will determine the success or otherwise of your project.
