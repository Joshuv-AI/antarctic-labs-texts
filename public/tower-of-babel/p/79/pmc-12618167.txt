Biomechanical implications of lumbar intervertebral disc fenestration in rabbits: Comparison of ex vivo and in vivo conditions as an experimental model for chrondrodystrophic dogs with type 1 intervertebral disc disease
Authors: James D Crowley, Rema A Oliver, Tian Wang, Matthew H Pelletier, William R Walsh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare the effect of intervertebral disc (IVD) fenestration on L3/4 range of motion (ROM) under ex vivo and in vivo conditions in a rabbit model.Study designRandomized experimental study.AnimalsNew Zealand White rabbits (ex vivo: n = 18; in vivo: n = 12).MethodsL3/4 motion segments were tested in lateral bending (LB), flexion‐extension (FE), and axial rotation (AR) across five groups (n = 6 per group): control (C), ex vivo fenestration (EVF), in vivo fenestration with a 6 (IVF6) and 12 week (IVF12) timepoint, and intact/annulotomy/fenestration on the same specimen (IAF). Data are reported as ROM and neutral zone (NZ) and were compared between groups using ANOVA and t‐tests.ResultsIn IAF, fenestration increased ROM in LB, FE, and AR (p = .030) and NZ in FE (p = .010) compared to intact. EVF had greater ROM and NZ than C in all planes (LB, FE: p = .0010; AR: p = .030). IVF6 and IVF12 showed reduced ROM and NZ in LB and FE compared to C (p = .020–.0010) and EVF (p = .0010), with no difference in AR (p = .35). IVF6 and IVF12 did not differ significantly in any plane. Right lateral bending was significantly greater than left in EVF (p = .020), but not in IVF6 (p = .15) or IVF12 (p = .31).ConclusionFenestration increased spinal motion ex vivo, while in vivo specimens showed reduced ROM over time, consistent with a stabilizing biological response.Clinical significanceThese findings provide a foundation for investigating the biological and biomechanical effects of IVD fenestration in dogs.

INTRODUCTION

Hansen type 1 intervertebral disc disease (IVDD), common in chondrodystrophic dog breeds, is characterized by chondroid metaplasia of the nucleus pulposus (NP) and subsequent extrusion into the vertebral canal with associated neurological dysfunction. 1 Fenestration is defined as the surgical removal of the nucleus pulposus (NP) (nuclectomy) via annulotomy or annulectomy, and is commonly performed at adjacent disc spaces at the time of decompressive surgery for clinically affected dogs with type 1 intervertebral disc extrusion (IVDE) to prevent reoccurrence of extrusion. 2 The clinical practice of prophylactic fenestration in dogs, particularly during surgical management of thoracolumbar intervertebral disc extrusion (IVDE), remains variable and somewhat controversial, as highlighted by the recent ACVIM consensus statement. 3 There is a need for greater understanding of the biological and biomechanical consequences of this procedure.

Multiple studies have evaluated the effect of fenestration on reoccurrence of IVDE in dogs, 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 with most in support of the prophylactic effect of fenestration for those with previous episodes of symptomatic IVDE. 5 , 7 , 9 Published information suggests reoccurrence rates of 0%–24.4% with prophylactic disc fenestration 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 and 2.67% to 41.7% without prophylactic disc fenestration. 12 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 The randomized prospective clinical trial by Brisson et al. reported that dogs with single‐site disc fenestration (at the site of extrusion) at the time of decompressive surgery were more likely to develop subsequent thoracolumbar IVDE (17.89% [17/95]) compared with dogs that underwent multiple site disc fenestration from T11 through L4 (7.45% [7/94]). 5 From this difference of approximately 10%, as posed by Jeffery and Freeman, 1 , 2 a more difficult, philosophic, question centers on whether it is appropriate to treat a certain number of dogs to prevent a much smaller number from developing reoccurrence. To better answer this question, a deeper understanding of the biological and biomechanical effects of IVD fenestration in dogs is required.

The seminal work by Panjabi et al. documented the effect of various IVD injuries on spine biomechanics in human cadaveric specimens. 21 , 22 , 23 There are a number of ex vivo and in vivo studies that document the effect of disc injuries in research animal species such as rabbit, goat and sheep. 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 In dogs, there are a small number of biomechanical studies in cadaveric cervical and thoracolumbar specimens that investigated the effect of various disc injuries and destabilizing procedures. Briefly, Macy et al. and Fauber et al. demonstrated significantly increased range of motion (ROM) in flexion and extension following cervical IVD fenestration in cadaveric C5–6 vertebral motion units. 28 , 29 Other studies have evaluated the stability of the thoracolumbar vertebral motion unit after surgical approaches such as lateral corpectomy, mini‐hemilaminectomy and hemilaminectomy. 30 , 31 While these studies document important biomechanical implications for IVD injuries, there is a lack of standardization in the treatments performed (annulotomy vs. annulectomy and/or nuclectomy) and therefore, there is a need to determine the effect of fenestration (annulectomy and nuclectomy) on canine spine biomechanics, particularly in thoracolumbar and lumbar IVDs where prophylactic fenestration is more commonly performed. In addition, these studies do not account for the biological, in vivo response to fenestration and its effect on spine biomechanics. Robust, controlled experimental studies are required to investigate the biological and biomechanical effects of fenestration in ex vivo and in vivo conditions. The use of a laboratory species such as the rabbit in an ethically responsible surgical model, presents a unique opportunity to investigate these questions and provide foundational information that may foster future research in dogs.

The authors of this study recently characterized an in vivo rabbit model of human lumbar IVDD by lateral fenestration using multiple experimental endpoints. 27 In the current study, ex vivo rabbit IVD fenestration biomechanical ROM data was compared to in vivo biomechanical data to further address the paucity of information in the veterinary literature on the biological and biomechanical effects of IVD fenestration in dogs. An experimental model using purpose‐bred NZW rabbits was chosen to minimize variation in subjects and potential confounding factors on outcome variables of interest. While rabbits are not an established model for canine spinal biomechanics, this study serves to generate baseline data that may inform future studies in dogs. Specifically, the objective of this study was to compare the effect of lumbar IVD fenestration on biomechanical ROM in ex vivo and in vivo conditions in an experimental rabbit model. The null hypothesis was that the ROM for intact control, ex vivo and in vivo fenestrated lumbar motion segments would not be significantly different.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study groups

The number of rabbits and their allocation for ex vivo and in vivo groups is shown in Figure 1. All cadavers (n = 18) used in this study for ex vivo groups were collected from rabbits enrolled in ethically approved research projects at the Surgical and Orthopedic Research Laboratories (SORL), UNSW, Sydney. No rabbits were additionally sacrificed for enrolment in these groups in accordance with the “reduction” principle of animal ethics in research.

Group allocation and numbers of New Zealand White (NZW) rabbits enrolled in the study.

The ex vivo groups were intact, annulotomy and fenestration (IAF), control (C) and fenestration (EVF), with n = 6 specimens per group. For Group IAF, intact, annulotomy and fenestration treatment conditions were performed on the same L3/4 motion segment to determine the effect of these treatment conditions on ROM. Group C included intact control L3/4 motion segments. For Group EVF, L3/4 lateral fenestration was performed on entire NZW rabbit cadavers via a left lateral, muscle‐splitting surgical approach to provide a comparable situation to the in vivo fenestration condition. The in vivo groups were L3/4 fenestration with 6‐ (IVF6) and 12‐week (IVF12) timepoints, with data acquired from the previously published study. 27 The EVF group was used to validate the surgical technique and mechanical impact in cadaveric specimens prior to in vivo surgery. In addition, it may approximate an in vivo time zero or sham condition that was not possible due to ethical considerations.

The vertebral motion segment included two adjacent vertebrae (L3 and L4), IVD between them (L3/4) and the spanning ligamentous structures. Lumbar discs spaces were chosen due to the authors' familiarity with the surgical anatomy, in accordance with experimental in vivo rabbit models of IVDD 32 and the perceived complexity of in vivo fenestration and mechanical testing in the rabbit due to rib articulations of the thoracolumbar spine. All rabbits were skeletally mature, 6‐month‐old entire female New Zealand White (NZW) rabbits weighing 3.5–4.5 kg, acquired from a closed, breeding colony. Skeletal maturity was verified by fluoroscopic and radiographic documentation of proximal tibial physeal closure prior to study enrolment. 33

Phase 1 ‐ lateral L3/4 fenestration under experimental conditions

In phase 1, the effect of different treatment conditions on ROM of a L3/4 motion segment was investigated using ex vivo specimens (group IAF). Intact, annulotomy and fenestration treatment conditions were performed on the same L3/4 motion segment after being mounted on a robotic mechanical testing machine (Figure 2). After testing the intact control condition, a full thickness left lateral annulotomy was made with a no. 11 scalpel blade (Figure 2A). Following annulotomy condition testing, fenestration was performed by making an a 1.5–2 mm annular window followed by nucleus pulposus removal using a spinal curette (Figure 2B).

Phase 2 ‐ lateral L3/4 fenestration under surgical conditions

Ex vivo

Lateral lumbar fenestration of the L3/4 IVD was performed on NZW rabbit cadavers under surgical conditions (n = 6). This was done to characterize the surgical model for future in vivo use and for a more standardized comparison for mechanical testing to other groups. Identification of the L3/4 IVD was confirmed using a hand‐held fluoroscopy unit (Shanghai Bojin Electric Instrument & Device Co, Shanghai, China) and percutaneous 23 gauge needle placement. Briefly, a left lateral approach to each lumbar disc was performed by a muscle‐splitting approach through the transverse abdominis, external abdominal oblique and internal abdominal oblique muscles (Figure 3). Next, the psoas muscle was retracted ventrally from the vertebral transverse processes to expose the IVD (Figure 4A). Fenestration was performed by making a 1.5–2 mm window in the lateral annulus fibrosus (AF) with a no. 11 scalpel blade to remove the NP with a spinal curette (Figure 4B). The aim was to remove as much NP tissue as possible as per the intention of the technique in canine literature to prevent future IVDE. 3 The soft tissues were closed routinely.

In both images, cranial is to the left and dorsal is to the top.

In vivo

Data from previously published work was used for the in vivo group. 27 The in vivo study was approved by the Animal Care and Ethics Committee (#20/90A) at the University of New South Wales, Sydney, Australia. The surgical technique described above was performed in vivo as detailed in the previously published study. 27 Rabbits were randomly allocated to 6‐ and 12‐week timepoints (n = 6 per group per timepoint). All rabbits received peri‐ and intraoperative opioid analgesia (buprenorphine 0.03 mg/kg IM at induction then as required), and postoperative nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory medication (meloxicam 0.85 mg/kg every 12–24 h for 3–5 days). The rabbits were physically examined and weighed daily for the first week postoperatively then weekly until their timepoint.

Biomechanical testing

L3/4 motion segments were harvested from all sample groups (ex vivo cadaveric and in vivo operated specimens) and processed for mechanical testing. All muscles were dissected from the specimens with preservation of osseous and ligamentous structures. All specimens were stored at −18°C and thawed at room temperature for 24 h before testing. Specimens were set in custom molds with resin, anchored proximally at the L2 vertebral body and L2/3 disc space and distally at L4 vertebral body and L4/5 disc space, with the L3/4 IVD oriented in the horizontal position. Grub/wood screws were placed into the border vertebrae for additional fixation and to isolate the L3/4 disc space. Once set, the specimens were mounted on a Denso robot (simVITRO; Cleveland Clinic BioRobotics Lab, Cleveland, Ohio) and a three‐dimensional coordinate system was established by anatomical landmarks on L3 and L4 for motion tracking during testing using a custom script commonly used at the institution and as previously published in preclinical rabbit spinal fusion studies by the authors. 33 , 34 Specimens were tested with pure moments to avoid off axis moments and spurious loading as previously described. 27 Specimens were evaluated in lateral bending (LB), flexion‐extension (FE), and axial rotation (AR) (Figure 5). Each specimen was sequentially tested in flexion, extension, right lateral bending, left lateral bending, right axial rotation, and left axial rotation. Moments of 270 Nmm were applied at a rate of 33.3 Nmm per second to a maximum of 300 Nmm, held for 15 s. 35 Each specimen underwent 4.5 load‐unload cycles. The ROM of the final three cycles were averaged at a mean torque of 270 Nmm for ROM and NZ calculation using a custom MatLab script. NZ was defined by the “zero load method” in accordance with Panjabi et al. and Grauer et al., that is, the region in the joint motion on either side of the neutral position where there is little or no resistance to motion. 22 , 23 , 36 For group IAF, intact, annulotomy and fenestration conditions were tested on the same specimen, with the surgical procedure performed on harvested spines after being loaded on the robotic testing machine, as stated previously. Intact control specimens in group C were tested over three rounds to confirm the non‐destructive nature of the testing protocol by excluding ROM increases with repeated testing for group IAF. For the purposes of statistical comparison to other treatment groups, the mean value of the three rounds of testing for group C specimens was used. Specimens from groups EVF, IVF6 and IVF12 were tested as described above. All specimens were kept moist with saline spray throughout mechanical testing.

L3/4 motion segments were set in custom molds with resin, a three‐dimensional coordinate system established, and the specimens evaluated in lateral bending (LB), flexion‐extension (FE) and axial rotation (AR) on a Denso 6 degrees of freedom robot (simVITRO; Cleveland Clinic BioRobotics Laboratory).

Statistical analysis

Sample size of n = 6 per group and per timepoint for ex vivo and in vivo groups, respectively, was determined from biomechanical ROM pilot testing and a priori power assessment, with power set at 0.8 and an alpha of 0.05. Descriptive data are presented as mean ± SE. Outcomes of interest were ROM and NZ (measured in degrees).

Following statistical review of the manuscript, multiple levels of repeated measures within the data were pooled to avoid overly complex modeling to maintain clinical relevance. For the ex vivo condition, intact, annulotomy and fenestration conditions on the same specimen were averaged for all samples (n = 6) and conditions compared using a one‐way ANOVA with a Tukey HSD post hoc test. Ex vivo and in vivo conditions for fenestration and control specimens were compared with a two‐way ANOVA with post hoc Tukey HSD test. Right and left lateral bending were compared within groups for the in vivo fenestration cohorts at 6 and 12 weeks using paired t‐tests. Comparisons between time points (6 vs. 12 weeks) for right and left lateral bending were performed using unpaired t‐tests. In the EVF group, right and left lateral bending were compared within specimens using paired t‐tests. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 30 for Windows (SPSS, Chicago, Illinois). Statistical significance was set at p < .05.

RESULTS

Lateral L3/4 fenestration

The surgical technique of lateral L3/4 fenestration was developed ex vivo and successfully extrapolated to the in vivo setting. The procedure was well‐tolerated by all rabbits, with all surviving the procedure and reaching their respective timepoints. 27 No lameness or neurologic deficits were detected throughout the duration of the study. In ex vivo and in vivo surgical procedures, the lateral approach provided adequate exposure for fenestration of the disc space.

DISCUSSION

This study compared the biomechanical effects of lumbar IVD fenestration in ex vivo and in vivo conditions in an experimental rabbit model. In phase 1, the biomechanical effects of annulotomy and fenestration were compared to the intact state under ex vivo conditions on the same specimen. In phase 2, the effect of fenestration on L3/4 motion segment ROM in ex vivo and in vivo conditions was compared. The results of this study highlight important biomechanical differences between ex vivo and in vivo lumbar IVD fenestration in an experimental rabbit model. Notably, in vivo fenestration led to reduced ROM over time, suggesting that biological healing response, such as fibrous tissue proliferation and lateral ankylosis, can counteract the acute destabilization observed in ex vivo conditions. This contrast underscores the significance of including biological processes when assessing the mechanical impact of spinal procedures. The findings raise the possibility that similar biological responses in canine patients may influence postoperative stability following fenestration. While this model does not replicate all aspects of clinical canine surgery, the observed temporal reduction in ROM supports the rationale for postoperative rest and controlled activity and provides a foundation for further studies exploring the biomechanical consequences of fenestration in dogs.

The IVD is a key stabilizer of the vertebral column, and disruption of the annulus fibrosus, particularly in the sagittal plane, has been shown to significantly increase FE ROM. 28 In other studies, it has been shown that the IVD is the primary stabilizer of the thoracolumbar vertebral column in LB. 37 , 38 In phase 1, compared to intact, fenestrated L3/4 motion segments had greater ROM in all planes of motion and NZ in FE. Annulotomy ROM compared to intact approached significance in all planes of motion, while fenestration was not different from annulotomy. Our biomechanical testing methodology included an investigation into the effect of repeated ROM testing of intact, control rabbit lumbar spinal motion segments and confirmed that ROM and NZ were consistent in all planes of motion throughout all repeated tests. Therefore, we can be confident that the higher ROM for the fenestrated compared to the intact condition, is due to the disc injury rather than tissue creep with repeated testing cycles. Additionally, ex vivo fenestrated L3/4 specimens under surgical conditions (group EVF) had greater ROM in LB, FE and AR compared to group C. This result suggests that the fenestration procedure was appropriately performed on cadaveric specimens under surgical conditions. When considered with the biomechanical data of group IAF, these findings demonstrate the acute, destabilizing effect that fenestration has on the L3/4 motion segment under ex vivo conditions.

The ROM and NZ of in vivo fenestrated L3/4 motion segments in groups IVF6 and IVF12 were lower compared to control, non‐operated (group C) and ex vivo fenestrated (group EVF) in LB and FE. The lower motion for in vivo specimens is due to fibrous tissue formation and ankylosis on the left lateral aspect of the disc space. The 12‐week timepoints showed a greater difference compared to control specimens than did the 6‐week timepoint, which likely reflects increased deposition and maturation of fibrosis. Comparing the ex vivo and in vivo biomechanics of fenestrated lumbar IVDs in this rabbit model highlights the difference when biology is involved, with an almost 50% lower ROM compared to intact controls. The attenuation of motion likely mitigates the acute destabilizing effects seen in the ex vivo group, demonstrating the functional impact of the biological response. These results may have clinical relevance in dogs, where post‐fenestration fibrosis could contribute to reduced spinal mobility. Furthermore, the significant reduction in ROM at both time points supports the practice of enforced rest for at least 6 weeks postoperatively and may inform future studies examining post‐surgical rehabilitation and adjacent segment biomechanics.

Analysis of ROM:NZ ratios for group IAF and EVF revealed that although both ROM and NZ increased across all planes, the ratio remained relatively stable in LB but increased in FE and AR, suggesting a compensatory, plane‐specific response to discal injury. In vivo, ROM:NZ ratios in FE and AR trended toward normalization by 12 weeks, likely due to biological stabilization, while LB remained proportionally stiffer, potentially reflecting asymmetric fibrosis at the surgical site. These differences demonstrate the value of ROM:NZ ratios in characterizing both mechanical and biological responses to fenestration in this model and provide baseline understanding for future work in a canine model.

For the ex vivo condition, there was a statistically significant increase in right LB compared to left LB. This outcome is consistent with the immediate mechanical consequences of unilateral annular disruption, where surgical transection of the left lateral annulus reduces its structural integrity and ability to resist right‐sided bending forces. In contrast, the in vivo groups did not show a statistically significant difference between right and left LB at either 6 or 12 weeks postoperatively. At 6 weeks, lateral motion remained relatively symmetric, and this pattern persisted at 12 weeks, suggesting that the mechanical disruption by fenestration was mitigated by the biological response of ankylosis at the disc space. When comparing right LB between time points, a trend toward reduced motion at 12 weeks relative to 6 weeks was observed. Although not statistically significant, this trend likely reflects the influence of progressive biological healing, specifically the development and maturation of fibrosis at the site of the left annular injury. This tissue remodeling may have helped to restore mechanical balance across the disc, gradually limiting the excess motion initially permitted by the annular defect. Together, these findings underscore the important role of biological repair in modulating the biomechanical effects of annular injury over time. This is valuable foundational information from an experimental model. Further investigation is indicated to determine if similar compensatory healing occurs in the canine spine and what the clinical effect is of this.

The lower ROM for in vivo fenestrated samples in this study may be relevant to the concept of “adjacent segment disease” in dogs. For single versus multiple‐site thoracolumbar fenestration, Brisson et al. reported that 21/24 (87.5%) reoccurrences of IVDE did so at a disc space immediately adjacent to the previous lesion, at the last site of disc fenestration or one disc space away. 5 For the multiple‐site fenestrated dogs with the most caudal fenestration being L3/4, four of the seven dogs that developed a recurrence of IVDE did so at L4–5 or L5–6. This is of importance as IVDE at this level may be particularly problematic as this is the location of the lumbar intumescence where injury can result in lower motor neuron myelopathy and potentially a poorer prognosis. Additionally, the reported incidence of naturally occurring IVDE at L4–5 is between 3.7% and 7%, which may suggest that extensive, multiple‐site fenestration predisposes non‐fenestrated, adjacent discs to degeneration and extrusion. 5 In these dogs, ankylosis of the extensively fenestrated disc spaces may have lowered the ROM of the thoracolumbar spine, and thereby induced greater mobility and subsequent degeneration of the adjacent disc spaces predisposing them to extrusion. This is speculative, but suggestive from the findings of this experimental rabbit model and is an area of further investigation in dogs.

The interpretation of biomechanical findings in this study must consider interspecies differences in spinal mobility, particularly regarding the NZ and overall ROM. It has been established that rabbit lumbar spines exhibit greater laxity and a proportionally larger NZ compared to human spines, 36 a feature that may reflect species‐specific differences in IVD morphology and ligamentous support. While comparable data in dogs is limited, it is reasonable to assume that canine spines, especially those affected by type 1 IVDD, display different mechanical behaviors due to degenerative changes and altered disc composition. 39 These differences in spine biomechanics between rabbits, dogs, and humans highlight the importance of caution when extrapolating data from experimental models. The distinct biomechanical response observed in this study, including the directional variation in ROM:NZ ratios, may not fully mirror the canine clinical scenario. Accordingly, there is a clear need for further research to define the physiological ROM and NZ characteristics of the canine lumbar spine, particularly in chondrodystrophic breeds predisposed to IVDD. Despite the acknowledged limitations of the rabbit model used in this study, the findings provide novel, foundational insights into spine biomechanics and the effects of IVD fenestration within a controlled experimental framework.

Very few studies have explored the biological insult to the disc space following injury in dogs. Shores et al. investigated the biological and degenerative changes following lateral fenestrations of IVDs T12–13 through L2–3 in 16, 1‐year‐old chondrodystrophoid dogs using spinal needles of three different gauges. 40 A total of 42 of 48 discs had radiographic evidence of narrowing following surgery and all dogs demonstrated vascular and fibrous tissue invasion of the lateral annulus fibrosus on histology. The needle stab incision resulted in fibrocartilage formation on the outer annulus fibrosus and in surrounding fibrous and muscular tissues, similar to the histologic findings reports from in vivo fenestration in this rabbit model. 27 The findings from the Shores et al.40 study highlight the biological changes from the needle stab procedure, a comparatively minor procedure compared to annulotomy or annulectomy with NP removal as is performed during the fenestration procedure. Following these findings from Shores et al. and the results of the current study, the in vivo response to fenestration in dogs and the effect on spine biomechanics and adjacent segment disease, is worthy of further study.

The surgical approach for lateral L3/4 IVD fenestration in this study was practiced and refined in the laboratory setting, prior to performing the procedure on cadavers for the EVF group and prior to performing the procedure in vivo. Therefore, we can be confident that we mitigated as best possible the effect of the surgical approach itself to the biomechanical effects on spine ROM, though a sham group (surgical approach to lumbar disc without fenestration) could have been used. The inclusion of only the EVF group was guided by ethical considerations, with a focus on minimizing the number of animals used and avoiding unnecessary sacrifice of additional rabbits.

In the authors' opinion, there are still many questions that remain unanswered with respect to fenestration and the biological and biomechanical implications of this procedure. Despite a relatively low level of evidence, published data does seem to support prophylactic fenestration as a safe way to reduce future disc extrusion at fenestrated sites. Both ex vivo and in vivo data is lacking on the consequences of this procedure. Ex vivo biomechanical ROM data on cadaveric canine spines would help to define the implications of this procedure on spinal ROM. This study design does not account for biology and therefore, results would have to be interpreted with this in mind. Similarly, experimental in vivo animal models of fenestration similar to that described here, would allow for evaluation of the biological effects of fenestration. Surgical approaches and techniques for fenestration and their effectiveness in nucleus pulposus removal could also be evaluated by these animal models from which the results could be extrapolated to the clinical setting. Finally, prospective randomized clinical studies similar to that of Brisson et al. 5 are required to provide robust, reliable data on the reoccurrence of IVDE following fenestration of single and multiple disc spaces, as well as other variables such as the amount of nucleus pulposus removed, and possible biological and biomechanical effects of fenestration. The results of such studies would provide reliable data for veterinary surgeons to consider the biological and biomechanical implications of fenestration in their patients and balance this against the intended benefits of fenestration. Until such data is available in dogs, the true benefit of fenestration remains in question.

The limitations of the study should be considered. Although the rabbits used were of a similar size and weight to small breed dogs, the normal IVDs of rabbits do not replicate the clinical scenario of chrondrodystrophic canine IVDs. However, rabbits were more accessible and the use of age and sex‐matched rabbits of known health status from a closed breeding colony allowed for standardization. The study design, test set up and resultant low variance in mechanical data, allowed for a more robust assessment of the effect of treatment conditions on spine ROM. The use of thoracolumbar spinal segments may have greater clinical relevance to dogs, 3 though lumbar IVDE is commonly reported, particularly for French Bulldogs and Cocker Spaniel breeds. 6 , 41 Another limitation is the absence of an in vivo group sacrificed immediately post‐fenestration, which would allow direct comparison between acute and post‐healing biomechanics. This could be addressed in future studies. Other limitations include absence of a hemilaminectomy which is typically performed in conjunction with fenestration in canine cases, use of a lateral rather than dorsolateral approach, and removal of paraspinal musculature which may otherwise contribute to stability. Finally, further characterization of the physiological ROM of canine lumbar spines and those affected by type 1 IVDD would be a valuable area further research and comparison to the results of this study.

CONCLUSION

Ex vivo and in vivo lumbar IVD fenestration conditions demonstrated significantly different biomechanical implications in this experimental rabbit model, with in vivo fenestration resulting in lower ROM compared to ex vivo control and fenestration conditions. While the biological response to fenestration appears to reduce spinal motion in this model, further investigation is required before clinical recommendations can be confidently extended to canine patients.
