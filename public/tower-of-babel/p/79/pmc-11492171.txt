Synthesis of Multivalent Glycoside-Immobilized Carboxymethyl Cellulose Nanohydrogel Particles with Superadsorption Ability for Lectins
Authors: Makoto Ogata, Remi Anazawa, Lisa Connolly, Tomomi Ogata, Yuto Wada, Yuki Tanaka, Ami Koizumi, Mitsuki Asano, Hiroyuki Kono
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) is a water-soluble cellulose derivative that is nontoxic, biocompatible, biodegradable, and nonallergenic. As developing an adsorbent material for carbohydrate-binding proteins is challenging, we aimed to synthesize CMC nanohydrogel particles (CMCGPs) with an extremely high lectin adsorption tendency in this study. CMCGPs were used as the backbone of an adsorption carrier that was synthesized by cross-linking CMC with ethylene glycol diglycidyl ether. A series of glycoside-immobilized CMCGPs were synthesized by binding two types of glycans (LacNAc and lactose) to the polyvalent carboxymethyl groups that are present on the CMCGP surface and act as reaction sites. These immobilized glycosides function as molecular recognition sites. Glycan moieties were incorporated into the CMCGP backbone at degrees of immobilization (DI) ranging from 8.7 to 21.0% by altering the reaction composition. LacNAc-CMCGP (3b) showed a 19.9% DI of LacNAc glycoside to the CMCGP carboxymethyl group; on average, its particle size swelled to 418 nm in phosphate-buffered saline, which is approximately 1.4 times its dry-state size. Analyzing the adsorbent properties of glyco-CMCGPs using a lectin-binding assay showed the high structural specificity of glyco-CMCGPs to lectins. The equilibrium isotherm data was explained by the Langmuir adsorption model. Notably, compound 3b adsorbed 1.95 ± 0.05 μg of wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) lectin per 1.0 μg-dry of 3b particles at an adsorption equilibrium time of a few minutes. Furthermore, solid-state 13C nuclear magnetic resonance analysis showed that WGA lectin retained its natural structure without denaturation after binding to LacNAc-CMCGP. These results were also supported by affinity purification experiments of WGA from raw wheat germ extract using LacNAc-CMCGP, demonstrating that glyco-CMCGP is capable of adsorbing and desorbing lectin while maintaining its biological activity. Thus, multivalent glycoside-immobilized CMCGPs that use woody biomass derivatives as the backbone are expected to be applied as biorefinery materials, which specifically and abundantly adsorb not just plant lectins but also pathogenic viruses and toxin proteins.Keywords: adsorbent, biorefinery, carbohydrate, carboxymethyl cellulose, lectin, multivalent, nanohydrogel

Introduction

The interaction between glycans and lectins is observed in several important biological phenomena such as the transport and management of glycoconjugates, mediation and regulation of cell adhesion and information transmission, and adhesion of bacteria and pathogenic viruses for infection of host cells.1 Generally, one-to-one affinity between glycans and lectins is weak; however, this interaction is greatly enhanced in vivo by the glycocluster effect induced by local multivalency of the glycans.2−5 The glycocluster effect is an important phenomenon for the in vivo expression of biological functions. It is also widely used as a molecular design factor for the development of functional lectin-targeting glycomaterials.6−15 An example of the practical application of the glycocluster effect in glycomaterial design is the development of a multivalent glycoside-immobilized adsorbent for the separation and purification of lectins or enzymes and high-sensitivity pathogenic virus- and toxin protein-detection technology.16−18 We have designed, synthesized, and reported various adsorbents for plant lectins and influenza viruses.19−21 However, developing an adsorbent material for carbohydrate-binding proteins that satisfies all characteristics such as high binding affinity, high adsorption amount, and low nonspecific adsorption is challenging. In this regard, we studied polysaccharide-based nanogel particles as a support for adsorbents. Nanoscale polysaccharide-based gel particles are functional particles that possess the characteristics of an abundant surface area, hydrogel rheology, and nanoparticles. They are attracting attention in the field of biomaterials as drug delivery system carriers.22 Various preparation methods and particle size control techniques have been reported for polysaccharide-based particles and their chemical properties. These characteristics are heavily influenced by the polysaccharide that forms the backbone.22 We have previously reported the synthesis of amorphous hydrogels and millimeter-sized spherical hydrogel particles by cross-linking carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) polysaccharides with water-soluble cross-linkers.23,24 CMC is used in various fields as a derivative of cellulose, which is a woody biomass that is highly valuable as biorefinery material.25,26 In this study, we aimed to apply this technique to synthesize spherical CMC nanohydrogel particles (CMCGPs) as adsorbent carriers. Furthermore, we aimed to multivalently bind functional glycosides to CMCGPs to achieve selective high adsorption of lectins, depending on the number of glycans. CMC adsorbent carriers can render the particle surface layer completely hydrophilic owing to the presence of a huge number of hydroxy and carboxylic groups, which would suppress nonspecific adsorption through hydrophobic interactions. Additionally, the multivalent carboxylic groups on the CMC-derived nanohydrogel particle (CMCGP) surface would function as reaction sites for immobilizing glycosides, contributing to the effective expression of the glycocluster effect. The glyco-CMCGPs developed in this study are expected to be new high-performance adsorbents that are also environmentally friendly.

Experimental Section

Materials and Ligands

CMC (average MW, 270,000; degree of substitution, 0.83; as specified by the manufacturer) was supplied by Sansho Co., Ltd. (Osaka, Japan) (Figure S1). Ethylene glycol diglycidyl ether (EGDE) was purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Co., Ltd. (Osaka, Japan). LacNAc-β-NHCO-(CH2)3-NH2 (1, Table S1 and Figure S2) and Lac-β-NHCO-(CH2)3-NH2 (2, Table S1 and Figure S3) were synthesized by previously reported methods (see the Supporting Information).27 Plant lectins [wheat germ agglutinin (WGA), Erythrina cristagalli agglutinin (ECA), and Sambucus sieboldiana agglutinin (SSA)] and 4-aminobenzoic acid ethyl ester benzocaine (ABEE) labeling kit were purchased from MGC Woodchem Co. (Tokyo, Japan). Probumin bovine serum albumin (BSA) was purchased from EMD Millipore Co. (Kankakee, IL, USA). Raw wheat germ was purchased from Alishan Co., Ltd. (Saitama, Japan).

Structural Characterization

The morphologies and structures of the CMCGP and glyco-CMCGPs (3a–e and 4) were characterized using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), dynamic light scattering (DLS), solid-state 13C NMR (SSNMR) spectroscopy, and ABEE labeling high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (see the Supporting Information for detailed procedures).

Preparation of CMCGPs

CMC (3.0 g) was completely dissolved in 0.5 M aqueous NaOH (30 mL) to which EGDE (1.2 g) was added. The mixture was stirred at 300 rpm for 10 min using a Teflon impellor at 4 °C. To this aqueous mixture, silicone oil (90 mL; KF-96L-0.65cs, Shin-Etsu Chemicals Co., Ltd. Japan) and polyether-modified silicone surfactant (1.8 mL; KF-6048, Shin-Etsu Chemicals Co., Ltd.) were added, and the mixture was homogenized at 4 °C and 2000 rpm for 3 min using a T25 Digital ULTRA-TURRAX homogenizer (IKA Werke GmbH & Co. KG, Germany) to form water-in-oil micelles containing CMC and EGDE in silicone oil. The homogenized mixture was stirred at 200 rpm with Teflon blades at 25 °C for 24 h to allow the cross-linking of CMC with EGDE. Once the reaction was completed, the CMCGPs formed were recovered through centrifugation at 6000g for 10 min and washed three times with hexane until the silicone oil and surfactant were completely removed. Next, the CMCGPs were washed thrice with methanol to remove unreacted EGDE and NaOH. The purified CMCGPs were stored at 4 °C as a methanol dispersion. An aliquot of CMCGP suspension was dried under vacuum for structural characterization.

Synthesis of LacNAc-CMCGP

The terminal amino function of the aglycon moiety of 1 was coupled with the carboxymethyl groups of CMCGPs using common benzotriazol-1-yloxytris(dimethylamino)phosphonium hexafluorophosphate (BOP)/1-hydroxybenzotriazole hydrate (HOBt) chemistry. The compositions of the condensation reaction and the reagents used are summarized in Table 1. As a result, six types of LacNAc-CMCGPs (3a–3e) with different quantities of immobilized compound 1 and reaction scales were obtained. The quantity and rate of 1 immobilized on the CMCGP were determined by labeling the galactose residues that were liberated by acid hydrolysis with an ABEE reagent and performing HPLC analysis. For example, compound 3b′ was synthesized as follows. The CMCGP methanol dispersion (14.7 mg of dry/mL in methanol) was centrifuged at 8000g for 3 min. The supernatant was removed, and the precipitate was thoroughly washed thrice with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to obtain a DMSO dispersion. BOP (780 mg, 1.77 mmol) and HOBt (65.5 mg, 0.49 mmol) dissolved in DMSO (14 mL) were added to the prepared CMCGP–DMSO dispersion (140 mg-dry in 10 mL DMSO, carboxymethyl groups 0.37 mmol). The mixture was stirred at 25 °C for 15 min. Compound 1 (206 mg, 0.44 mmol) in DMSO (7 mL) was added to the activated CMCGP, and the reaction was performed under the same conditions with continuous stirring for 48 h. Once the reaction was completed, the nanogel particles were washed three times with DMSO (25 mL) and six times with distilled water (30 mL) to obtain LacNAc-CMCGP (3b′) (135 mg, DI = 19.6%). The synthesized glyco-CMCGPs were suspended in distilled water and stored at 4 °C. The other LacNAc-CMCGPs, namely, 3a (DI = 21.0%), 3b (19.9%), 3c (16.4%), 3d (11.5%), and 3e (8.5%), were synthesized similarly (see Table 1).

Synthesis of Lac-CMCGP

Lac-CMCGP (4) was synthesized using CMCGPs (5.0 mg), BOP (27.9 mg), HOBt (2.3 mg), and compound 2 (6.7 mg) in a similar manner as that of 3b’ (Table 1). The DI of the Lac-glycosides in 4 was adjusted to 20.4%.

Evaluation of Lectin Adsorption Ability on Glyco-CMCGP

Lectin-Binding Assay

First, the synthesized LacNAc-CMCGPs were uniformly dispersed in an appropriate amount of distilled water, and a portion of the dispersion was lyophilized to calculate the particle weight (Table 1) and concentration. The remaining dispersion was used to prepare 10 mM phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (20 μL, pH 7.4) containing LacNAc-CMCGPs (3a–3e; 1.25 μg-dry/μL), which was then added into a low protein-binding microcentrifuge tube containing 50 μM WGA solution (20 μL, in the same buffer). Binding Specificity Analysis

10 mM PBS (40 μL, pH 7.4) containing lectins (WGA and ECA: 25 μM; SSA: 6.3 μM) or BSA (25 μM) was poured into a low protein-binding microcentrifuge tube, to which compound 3b or 4 (final concentration: 0.63 μg-dry/μL) was added. Qt was calculated as mentioned for the lectin-binding assay.

Adsorption Isotherms

10 mM PBS (40 μL, pH 7.4) containing WGA with concentrations ranging from 3 to 50 μM was poured into a low protein-binding microcentrifuge tube, to which compound 3b (final concentration: 0.16 μg-dry/μL) was added. The interaction between particles and lectin post-treatment and Qt calculations were performed as mentioned for the lectin-binding assay.

Structural Analysis of Lectin Bound to Particles

A 10 mM PBS (16.2 mL; pH 7.4) dispersion containing 3b’ (15 mg-dry) and the same buffer (16.2 mL) containing WGA (35 mg) were mixed with occasional inversion at 4 °C for 30 min. After centrifuging the 3b′-WGA suspension at 7200g for 5 min, the supernatant was removed. The precipitate was washed thrice with H2O (32.4 mL). Finally, the precipitate was lyophilized. The final yield of 3b′-WGA was 41 mg. The LacNAc-CMCGP 3b′-WGA structure was analyzed using SSNMR spectroscopy (see the Supporting Information).

Affinity Purification of Lectin from a Natural Product Using Glyco-CMCGP

Raw wheat germ powder (100 mg) was added to 10 mM PBS (1.0 mL, pH 7.4) and extracted at 4 °C for 30 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 8000g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.20 μm filter to obtain wheat germ extract. Next, the same buffer dispersion (25 μL) containing 3b′ (0.1 mg-dry) and wheat germ extract (25 μL) were interacted with occasional inversion at 4 °C for 30 min. The 3b′ was then thoroughly washed (3×) with cold 10 mM PBS (50 μL, pH 7.4) using the same centrifugation conditions. Finally, the proteins specifically adsorbed on 3b′ were eluted with 250 mM LacNAc aqueous solution (50 μL). 3b′ and the LacNAc eluate were easily separated by centrifugation at 8000g for 3 min at 4 °C. The obtained eluate was desalted, lyophilized, and analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) using 15% polyacrylamide gel (e-PAGEL, ATTO Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The gel was visualized with Coomassie brilliant blue (EzStain Aqua, ATTO Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) staining. The molecular weight of the proteins was estimated by including standards (8.4–250 kDa, DynaMarker Protein MultiColor Stable II, BioDynamics Laboratory Inc., Tokyo, Japan) on the gel.

Results and Discussion

CMCGP Preparation

CMC is a water-soluble cellulose derivative with some hydroxyl groups on the cellulose molecular chain substituted with carboxymethyl groups. It is widely used as a thickener and food additive in a variety of applications such as food, pharmaceuticals, and cosmetics owing to its excellent nontoxic, biocompatible, biodegradable, and nonallergenic characteristics.28,29 The grafting of functional molecules on CMC through its carboxymethyl groups as a reaction point imparts various functions.30,31 Additionally, CMC is converted into biodegradable hydrogels through intermolecular cross-linking of some of the unsubstituted hydroxyl groups with ester- or ether-type chemical cross-linking agents.23,24,32,33 For example, cross-linking of CMC with double-ended epoxy compounds such as EGDE and polyethylene glycol diglycidyl ether produces hydrogels similar to acrylic acid-based superabsorbent resins.23,24 In this study, nanosized CMC hydrogel particles were prepared with the capacity to immobilize large numbers of oligosaccharide chains per unit surface area (Figure 1).

Glyco-CMCGP Synthesis

Several glycoconjugates dramatically increase their originally weak binding affinity for receptors by creating a multivalent state in the glycan moiety through the formation of branched structures or lipid raft structures.2,34 As mentioned in the Introduction, this enhancement of intermolecular interactions is called the glycocluster effect. We used this concept for the material development in this study. We attempted molecular designing to construct gel-like multivalent glyconanoparticles (glyco-CMCGPs) with molecular recognition sites (glycosides) bound to the CMCGP, which has several carboxymethyl groups that act as reaction sites. In this study, LacNAc-CMCGPs (3a–3e) and Lac-CMCGP (4) were obtained through the condensation reaction between the CMCGP-derived carboxymethyl groups and the LacNAc- or Lac-glycoside-associated aglycone terminal amino groups (1 or 2) in the presence of a peptide synthesis reagent (BOP and HOBt) in 99.9% pure DMSO (Figure 3 and Table 1). The reaction was performed under mild conditions. However, by altering the CMCGP carboxymethyl group/glycoside molar ratio, we were able to control the DI within the range of 21.0–8.5% (Table 1). The AI (DI) and structure of the synthesized glyco-CMCGPs were confirmed using the ABEE labeling HPLC method and SSNMR analysis. Structural evaluation has been explained using compound 3b [3b′ (large-scale synthesis)] as an example, in which the CMCGP carboxymethyl group and glycoside molar ratio is 1:1.2 (Table 1). ABEE analysis confirmed that 531 ± 12 nmol of compound 1 was immobilized per 1.0 mg-dry of 3b particles. This AI corresponds to a DI of 19.9% (Table 1). The AI and DI of 3b′ was 522 ± 9 nmol/mg-dry and 19.6%, and almost no difference was observed in the AI because of the change in the reaction scale (Table 1). Figure 4 shows the assigned SSNMR spectrum of 3b′. Furthermore, the DI of 3b′ was calculated based on the relative intensities of the signals in the carboxyl carbon region (180–160 ppm) and the O-glycosidic anomeric carbon region (110–90 ppm) of the SSNMR spectrum. The DI of 3b′ calculated from SSNMR analysis was 19.4%, and the result is similar to that obtained based on the ABEE method. In this study, the DI remained at approximately 20% even during synthesis with excess glycosides such as 3a. This may be attributed to the fact that the CMCGP, which was used in the reaction solution, is a poor solvent. That is, the glycans added to the glyco-CMCGPs synthesized in this study were primarily immobilized near the particle surface.

Properties of Glyco-CMCGP as a Bioadsorption Material

Effects of the Particle Amount and Adsorption Time on the Lectin-Binding Assay

We used LacNAc-CMCGP 3b and WGA lectin to optimize the amount of particles; lectin solution was used at a constant concentration, and the interaction time for reaching adsorption equilibrium was analyzed. WGA lectin is a two-subunit N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc)-binding protein derived from wheat germ with a binding affinity for LacNAc containing the GlcNAc moiety.35,36 Regarding the effect of the amounts of particles, 3b was added in the range of 6.25–100 μg-dry, and the Qt was measured. Other interaction parameters were set as described in Figure 5. The results showed that the Qt value was high when 6.25–25 μg-dry of 3b was added to 40 μL of WGA solution that was adjusted to a final concentration of 25 μM (Figure 5a). The amounts of these particles were 0.16–0.63 μg-dry/μL when converted to concentration. The adsorption time was evaluated by measuring the Qt at each time point during the interaction between 3b and WGA. As shown in Figure 5b, 3b and WGA bonded immediately after mixing, and a plateau was reached 10 min after the start of the interaction. Based on these results, the particle concentration was set as 0.63 μg-dry/μL, and the adsorption time was set as 30 min for the lectin-binding standard assay.

Binding Specificity of Glyco-CMCGPs

The binding specificities of LacNAc-CMCGP 3b and Lac-CMCGP 4 were evaluated by using three types of lectins (WGA, ECA, and SSA) and BSA. ECA and SSA lectins exhibit binding affinity for nonreducing terminal Gal or N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) and Neu5Acα2,6Gal/GalNAc, respectively.37−40 BSA was used as the protein because it did not exhibit carbohydrate binding affinity. Figure 6a shows the Qt values pertaining to all three lectins and BSA for 3b. Thus, 3b with immobilized LacNAc (Galβ1,4GlcNAc) glycosides bound to both WGA, which recognizes GlcNAc residues, and ECA, which recognizes nonreducing terminal Gal. In contrast, compound 4 containing lactose (Galβ1,4Glc) glycosides bound to ECA (Figure 6b). Additionally, 3b and 4 hardly bound to other lectins and BSA. These results imply that the synthesized glyco-CMCGPs are selective bioadsorption materials with almost no nonspecific adsorption. As mentioned in the Introduction, one of the properties of an excellent adsorbent is low nonspecific adsorption caused by intermolecular interactions such as hydrophobic interactions. Previously, we have developed several adsorbents with immobilized glycans; however, most of them exhibited strong nonspecific adsorption or required treatment with blocking agents such as BSA.19−21,41,42 In this study, we used CMC with a large number of hydroxy groups as the carrier backbone of the adsorbent and a highly water-soluble ethylene glycol structure as the cross-linking agent to form CMC into nanoparticles. As a result, we succeeded in minimizing nonspecific adsorption without the need for complicated treatments such as blocking against glyco-nanoparticles.

Effects of the LacNAc-Glycoside Immobilization Amount of LacNAc-CMCGPs (3a–3e) on Adsorption Capacity

We investigated the WGA adsorption ability of the five types of LacNAc-CMCGPs (3a–3e) with different amounts of immobilized glycosides. As shown in Figure 7, the amounts of glycoside immobilized on CMCGPs exerted a strong effect on the lectin adsorption. Specifically, compared with that of 3e (DI = 8.5%), which showed the least AI, 3b (DI = 19.9%) showed a 5.3-fold increase in Qt (Figure 7a). Furthermore, the rate of increase in Qt was the highest for 3d and 3c (2.3-fold), whereas 3a, 3b, and 3c showed almost no change (Figure 7a). Notably, the Qt values of 3a and 3b reached 1.95 μg/μg-dry in the lectin-binding assay (Figure 7a), implying approximately twice as much WGA in terms of mass ratio bound to 3a/3b. Generally, in adsorbents that use silica, metal, plastic, or other carriers as the backbone, the ratio of the adsorbate to the adsorbent is often overwhelmingly low.21,41,43−46 In contrast, gel-based adsorbents exhibit water molecule absorption and swelling, implying that a small amount of the adsorbent can bind to a large amount of adsorbate. Previously, a gel-based adsorbent that used cross-linked agarose as the backbone was reported; however, almost no adsorbents have been reported where the amount of adsorbate exceeds the weight of the binding adsorbent.47,48 In this study, we succeeded in synthesizing an adsorbent with superadsorption ability by converting CMC into nanoparticles using a water-soluble cross-linking agent to create a gel backbone with a large surface area, followed by functionalizing the surface with glycosides. Figure 7b shows the adsorption ability (Qt) per LacNAc glycoside in the glyco-CMCGPs. We found that among the particles on which LacNAc glycoside was immobilized, compound 3c (Qt = 4.01 ± 0.05 μg/nmol) showed the highest efficiency in binding to WGA. These findings indicate that the immobilized amount of glycosides bound on the CMCGP surface plays an important role in lectin adsorption. Taking all the results together, we consider that the mechanism behind LacNAc-CMCGP’s high-affinity adsorption of WGA is due to the synergistic effects of three factors: the gel-like structure of the adsorbent, the large surface area provided by the nanostructure, and the optimized glycocluster state on the particle surface.

Binding Mode of WGA to LacNAc-CMCGP and Structural Analysis of Adsorbed WGA

The adsorption isotherm was created to calculate the interaction between the adsorbent and adsorbate and the maximum adsorption ability of the adsorbate to the adsorbent (Figure 8a). The Langmuir and Freundlich BET models, which are often used to analyze the interaction between the adsorbent and adsorbate, were applied to WGA adsorption onto 3b (Figure 8b–d).49−51 Among the three models, the R2 coefficient of the fitting function showed the best value (0.9920) in the Langmuir model (Figure 8b). The Langmuir isotherm is a model that assumes that no interaction occurs between adsorbates and that a single layer of adsorbate is formed on the homogeneous surface of the adsorbent.50 The linear form of the Langmuir isotherm model is

where Qe indicates the amount of adsorbate involved in adsorption at equilibrium (g/g), Q0 indicates the maximum single-phase adsorption ability (g/g), KL indicates the Langmuir isothermal constant (L/g), and Ce indicates the equilibrium concentration of the adsorbate (g/L). As mentioned, WGA adsorption to 3b followed the Langmuir model, and the Q0 value calculated from the fitting curve was 2.3 g/g-dry, which was in good agreement with the Qe(exp) and Qt values (approximately 2.0 g/g-dry, Figures 5–8). Furthermore, we performed a nonlinear fitting using nonlinear-type isothermal equations (Figure S5). As a result, we confirmed that the adsorption behavior of WGA lectin is fitted by the Langmuir adsorption isotherm equation as well as the linear isotherm equation. The maximum adsorption amount Q0 determined from the nonlinear-type adsorption isotherm equation52 completely agreed with that determined from the linear-type adsorption isotherm equation (Figure S5a). In contrast, the BET model51 that explains multilayer adsorption showed a lower R2 than that of the Langmuir model (Figure 8d). These results indicate the absence of multilayer adsorption of WGA onto 3b with no interaction between the WGA lectins. Thus, based on the curve fitting analysis, we have concluded that among three isotherm models, lectin adsorption onto glyco-CMCGP follows the Langmuir model with only a monolayer forming at maximum adsorption.

As a result, the blue curve almost coincides with the SSNMR spectrum of WGA lectin (Figures 9b and S6). This result proves that WGA binds to 3b′ using a nondestructive analytical method and shows that the adsorbed WGA maintains its original three-dimensional structure.

Affinity Purification of WGA Using LacNAc-CMCGP

Finally, we evaluated the effect of contaminating proteins present in natural product samples on glyco-CMCGP and the desorption of adsorbed proteins. LacNAc-CMCGP 3b′ was used to affinity-purify WGA from raw wheat germ extract. After interaction between raw wheat germ extract and 3b′, proteins that specifically bound to 3b′ were eluted with hapten (LacNAc) and evaluated by SDS-PAGE (see the Experimental Section). As a result, various proteins were stained from the raw wheat germ extract, a natural product sample, as shown in lane 1 of Figure 10. On the contrary, in the affinity-purified fraction of 3b′, a single band (lane 2 of Figure 10) was detected at approximately 17 kDa, similar to the WGA standard (lane 3 of Figure 10). The position of this band is almost identical with the size (18 kDa) of one WGA subunit. These results indicate that glyco-CMCGP is an adsorbent allowing minimal nonspecific adsorption and that the adsorbate can be desorbed while maintaining its biological activity by simple and mild elution conditions using a hapten.

SDS-PAGE profile of WGA purified from raw wheat germ using LacNAc-CMCGP 3b′. Gel was stained with Coomassie brilliant blue.

In general, desorption of bioadsorbents to adsorbents without denaturing them is considered one of the important characteristics of adsorbents. Bioadsorbates (e.g., lectins, antibodies, microorganisms, viruses, etc.) that are adsorbed and recovered by adsorbents with such properties are expected to be used not just as materials for basic research but also for a variety of other purposes such as viral culture and vaccine production. In addition, considering practical applications, it is necessary to selectively adsorb the target lectin from various lectins with similar carbohydrate-binding specificity present in the sample such as bacterial surface lectins, viral surface lectins, and animal/plant lectins. In such cases, more advanced lectin separation can be achieved by performing a simple pretreatment step (sterile filtration, ultracentrifugation, centrifugation, etc.) according to the molecular size of the target adsorbate before lectin purification using glyco-CMCGP.

Conclusions

We have established a method using emulsification technology for producing a CMCGP that almost mimics the physical properties of living tissue. Furthermore, we have developed novel glyco-CMCGPs that have an extremely high lectin adsorption tendency by immobilizing multivalent glycosides on the CMCGP surface carboxy groups. These glyco-CMCGPs can be applied to adsorbents that specifically and highly adsorb pathogenic viruses and toxic proteins by customizing the glycoside moieties to function as molecular recognition elements. Additionally, functional groups other than carboxy groups can be selected by altering the biomass polysaccharides to act as the hydrogel backbone, which may potentially improve the functionality depending on the application.
