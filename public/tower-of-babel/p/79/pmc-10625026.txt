Overcoming the barriers to tackling childhood obesity
Authors: The Lancet Regional Health – Western Pacific
Published: 2023

Obesity in young people is a health crisis globally, with increasing prevalence in low-income and middle-income countries. Pacific Island countries dominate the ten nations with the highest prevalence of obesity in 10–19-year-olds worldwide. Children with obesity might have metabolic dysfunction, respiratory disorders, and postural malalignments, amongst a long list of physical health challenges that have long-lasting consequences. The psychosocial impact of obesity and the resulting experience also often affects their social and emotional wellbeing, with increasing anxiety, depression, or poor self-esteem. Additionally, children with overweight or obesity are at higher risk of having obesity and health issues associated with obesity in their adulthood. In adults, obesity is a substantial risk factor for many health conditions, such as cardiovascular diseases, cancers, and osteoarthritis, which then can dictate a life of poorer health, productivity, and quality of life that can culminate in premature mortality.

The myriad factors that impact obesity in childhood include biological predisposition, along with socioeconomic and environmental influences around a child. Several modifiable factors (eg, infant feeding practices) and non-modifiable factors (eg, genetic susceptibility) exist at the individual level. However, the socioecological factors span many levels from the individual, as Hiba Jebile and colleagues described in The Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology. These levels include family, surrounding community, general societal attitudes, and public policy. An example of a familial factor is the behaviour modelling of physical activity, nutrition, and sleep. The surrounding community might encompass the school curriculum, including nutrition of meals the child is exposed to or barriers and enablers to healthy lifestyles in public infrastructure. Historically, cultural norms have been a strong influencing factor. In today’s digital age, social media adds to this effect: trends can encourage positive messaging, such as the clean eating movement, but also glorify unhealthy behaviours, such as Mukbang, which portrays individuals eating excessive food. At the public policy level, transport, agriculture, and food marketing policies can strongly influence lifestyle behaviours. Although this framework highlights the many opportunities for prevention and intervention, it is a double-edged sword with many avenues for negative influences that undermine efforts.

A recent development in treating childhood obesity was the addition of anti-obesity medication to the American Academy of Paediatrics guidelines. This move was alongside the approval of four drugs by The US Food and Drug Administration for children aged 12 years and older. Despite the progress made in obesity treatment, the more common approach is to manage obesity by restoring the energy intake and energy expenditure balance: nutrition and physical lifestyle. In the Western Pacific region, some progress is being made at various levels, such as epidemiology research, guidelines, and policy changes. In the Marshall Islands, Tanner Smith and colleagues reported the prevalence and distribution of obesity-related risk factors among children. Recently, the Asia-Pacific consensus statement on integrated 24 h activity guidelines for children under the age of 5 years was published, which included representatives from 19 countries. Several countries have implemented sugar-sweetened beverage taxes, with Oliver Huse and colleagues critically appraising the cost–utility balance between proposed and enacted policies in the Philippines. Beyond nutrition and physical activity, other unhealthy behaviours such as high screen time and poor sleep patterns are associated with obesity and have been targeted to improve the situation.

Accessibility of prevention and treatment strategies is another substantial hurdle in reducing obesity rates. First and foremost, not all cultures see obesity as a problem, with some associating it with a sign of wealth—people do not seek solutions for problems they are unaware of. In cultures where obesity is a problem, societal stigma is a crucial barrier to health care seeking behaviours. Access to health-care professionals is another challenge, with many facing a scarcity of local health-care services, overburdened health-care systems, or financial difficulties. Although these factors affect all people with obesity, having treatment delivered to children and youth faces separate challenges. Breastfeeding in infancy is a known protective factor. However, many parents lack the support during a highly tiring time during the newborn period to initiate and maintain the practice while being exposed to industry-driven concepts of formula being the best and the first-line option. Habit development in children is also often based on modelled behaviours by adults in their environment. However, lack of knowledge, time, and support for adults to model healthy lifestyles are additional barriers. This challenge is more pronounced in the Western Pacific region, which experiences large volumes of labour migration, resulting in many children being separated from their parents and often overlooked. Another difficulty for childhood obesity is the lack of contact between this age group and the health-care system. After vaccinations in early life, there is often limited contact with primary health services for early intervention opportunities until adulthood, when many have developed chronic health conditions.

Addressing the barriers to preventing and treating childhood obesity is difficult and must come from a wholesale change in policy and infrastructure across the different socioecological levels. Obesity is a serious health problem, and individuals and families need greater access to support and to be proactively engaged for prevention. Government policies to reduce exposure to contributing factors while providing community infrastructure to encourage healthy behaviours are much needed. Tackling childhood obesity can maximise quality of life and reduce the risk of poor health, with ongoing effects to improve lifelong health and reduce the burden on health-care systems.
