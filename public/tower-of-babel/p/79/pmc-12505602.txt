Ten mouse organs proteome and metabolome atlas from adult to aging
Authors: Qingwen Wang, Zhixiao Xu, Xinwen Ding, Aiting Wang, Sunfengda Song, Shuang Zhang, Youming Chen, Yi Ding, Lai Jiang, Xianting Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAging is a complex biological process characterized by progressive molecular alterations across multiple organ systems, significantly influencing disease susceptibility and mortality. Unraveling molecular interactions driving aging is crucial for interventions promoting healthy aging and mitigating senescence. However, the systemic mechanisms governing both inter-organ interactions and organ-specific aging trajectories remain incompletely characterized.MethodsTo investigate the molecular dynamics of aging, we conducted a systematic multi-omics analysis of 400 tissue samples collected from 10 organs (brain, heart, intestine, kidney, liver, lung, muscle, skin, spleen, and stomach) in mice at four distinct life stages: 4, 8, 12, and 20 months (from youth to elderly). Proteomic profiling was performed using data-independent acquisition (DIA) technology, while metabolomic analysis was performed in both positive and negative ion modes. Differential expression analysis of proteins and metabolites was employed to construct a comprehensive multi-organ aging dataset.ResultsProteomic profiling across ten organs at four age stages identified a total of 14,763 protein groups (PGs). Of these, 18 proteins, including Ighm, C4b, and Hpx, exhibited consistent age-related differential expression patterns across all ten organs. Functional enrichment analysis highlighted the humoral immune response as a primary driver of age-related expression changes. Additionally, this study mapped a set of age-unique proteins, such as Hp, Egf, and Arg, with distinct expression patterns in aging organs. Metabolic analysis identified 3779 metabolites, with key aging-related metabolites such as NAD+, inosine, xanthine, and hypoxanthine showing significant expression changes across multiple organs. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed consistent alterations in purine metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism, riboflavin metabolism, and nicotinate/nicotinamide metabolism during multi-organ aging.ConclusionsThis study provides a multi-omics atlas of multi-organ aging, revealing both intra- and inter-organ similarities and heterogeneities. These findings offer valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying geriatric health decline and serve as a foundational resource for organism-systematic early warning and targeted interventions against aging-associated pathologies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13073-025-01535-4.

Background

As an intrinsic inevitability of life, aging encompasses a complex interplay of biological, physiological, and environmental factors [1, 2]. Aging represents a multiscale biological process involving progressive deterioration across molecular, cellular, and organ system levels, leading to time-dependent decline in physiological resilience [1, 3]. Individuals progress along the chronological continuum, where a myriad of changes occurs at the molecular levels. These molecular dynamics partially increase susceptibility to age-related conditions, reduce organismal resistance, and ultimately contribute to mortality [4–6]. Recent aging research focuses on unraveling the underlying molecular mechanisms driving this multifaceted phenomenon [7, 8]. Acquiring a temporal molecular atlas of multi-organ aging is pivotal not only for extending healthy lifespan but also for mitigating age-related diseases, such as cancer, cardiovascular disorders, and neurodegenerative conditions.

One of the complexities of aging lies in the lack of synchronization in the aging process of various organs and tissues, yet their interactions are intricate and interdependent [4, 9]. To elucidate the specific mechanisms underlying biological aging and its associated diseases, this study aims to quantify the molecular changes that accumulate across various organs and tissues during aging. Comprehensive studies of transcriptome changes during aging in multiple mouse organs have uncovered the aging trajectories of RNA molecules across various organs [9–13]. These datasets have facilitated an invaluable systematic investigation of the transcriptomic effects of aging in entire organisms. However, aging involves intricate and sophisticated biological mechanisms, the complexity of which far exceeds the capacity of a single molecular perspective to capture. Proteins, as the main functional biomolecules in biological functions, are involved in nearly all biochemical reactions from basic energy metabolism to complex signal transduction pathways. Growing evidence suggests that protein and RNA levels are often not consistently correlated [14–17]. Metabolomics studies can provide endpoint information on life activities, reflecting the metabolic state and health of an organism. However, our knowledge about the proteomics and metabolomics of aging across multiple organs remains limited.

In this study, we conducted an analysis of proteomics and metabolomics during aging process across ten mouse organs using liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS). All mass spectrometry data are accessible via ProteomeXchange under the identifier PXD047296 [18] and EMBL-EBI MetaboLights database with the identifier MTBLS10642 [19]. Recent advances in deep learning have improved the parsing of complex mass spectrometry profiles and enhanced the depth and accuracy of proteomics identification [20, 21]. Our primary objective was to elucidate the qualitative and quantitative alterations in protein expression throughout the aging process. We identified multi-organ aging-related dynamic differentially expressed proteins (aging-dynamic DEPs) that exhibit changes throughout the entire lifespan, age-unique DEPs that are expressed only at a specific age and aging-process DEPs with expression differences compared to younger individuals. Additionally, by leveraging the findings of DEPs enriched in metabolic process-related signaling pathways, we present metabolomic data derived from the aging process of these ten organs. We constructed an interactome network illustrating associations between proteins and metabolites that jointly reshape during aging. Collectively, our study bridges the gap in multi-omics data on organismal multiorgan aging, providing robust resource for the multi-organ evolution of natural aging. In addition, we identified a number of previously unreported biomarkers and signaling pathways potentially linked to the systemic aging of the organism.

Methods

Animals

All animal experiments were performed with the ethical protocol approved by the Institutional Biomedical Research Ethics Committee at the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Shanghai, China. C57BL/6N mice were housed under controlled conditions (20–26 °C, 50–70% humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. This study included four distinct age cohorts of mice: 4 months, 8 months, 12 months, and 20 months old. For each age cohort, we employed a balanced mixture of male and female mice, with five individuals of each sex included. Mice were deeply anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection, Sigma-Aldrich), perfused with pre-cooled 1 × PBS via the myocardium, and dissected. Brain, heart, lung, liver, kidney, spleen, stomach, intestine, muscle, and skin were collected. Calf gastrocnemius muscle was extracted as a muscle sample and 1 cm2 of skin tissue was removed from the neck as skin sample. The remaining organs were cut off intact and preserved. Samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Proteomics sample preparation, mass spectrometry and data analysis

Tissue homogenization

Tissues (~50 mg) were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and immediately homogenized. All samples were homogenized again after adding RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) with the protein inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, P1008). Protein lysates were sonicated on ice and centrifuged to collect supernatants. After centrifugation, supernatant lysates were collected and stored at −80 °C.

Protein digestion

One hundred micrograms of proteins per sample was acetone-precipitated at −20 °C overnight. The precipitate was re-dissolved in 7 M guanidine hydrochloride, followed by reduction with dithiothreitol (DTT) at 55 °C for 1 h and alkylation with iodoacetamide (IAA) in the dark. Samples were then washed with ammonium bicarbonate using 10 kDa ultrafiltration concentrators (Sartorius) before trypsin (Promega) was added at a 1:50 (w:w) ratio for overnight digestion at 37 °C. Peptides were desalted using a C18 column (Monospin) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, vacuum-dried, and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Mass spectrometry

The peptides were analyzed using a nanoLC coupled with QE HF-X system (EASY-nLC 1200, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Each sample was separated on 2 cm × 75 μm homemade reversed-phase C18 column (particle size, 3 μm; pore size, 100 Å. Thermo Fisher Scientific) and separated by 25 cm × 75 μm analytical column (particle size, 2 μm; pore size, 100 Å. Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a flow rate of 300 nl/min. The mobile phases performed in the binary gradient were of water (buffer A) and acetonitrile (buffer B), both containing 0.1% formic acid. Gradient: 0–1 min with buffer B ranging from 1 to 8%; 1–81 min with buffer B ranging from 8 to 22%; 81–98 min with buffer B ranging from 22 to 28%; 98–112 min with buffer B ranging from 28 to 36%; 112–116 min with buffer B ranging from 36 to 100%; 116–120 min with buffer B 100%.

The DIA acquisition strategy involved scanning an m/z range of 320 to 1220 at a resolution of 60,000, with an AGC target set to 3E6. During each cycle, all precursor ions were transferred to MS/MS scans using a 20 m/z isolation width and a series of 45 overlapping windows (each with a 1 m/z overlap), spanning the mass range of 330–1210 m/z. Precursor ions with charge states of +1 or +6 and higher were excluded from sequencing.

Indexed retention time (iRT) peptides were spiked into each sample, and their responses were monitored during every MS run. The iRT peptides served as internal standards to normalize retention times and monitor instrument performance across different batches. Prior to sample analysis and following daily instrument calibration, quality control samples derived from HeLa cells were analyzed to verify instrument stability. Additionally, a pooled sample mixture was included as a control to assess sensitivity and reproducibility throughout the experimental process.

Data analysis

All DIA raw MS data were analyzed with DIA-NN-1.8 (https://github.com/vdemichev/DiaNN) [20] using a library-free search according to the instruction manual. FASTA database of Mus musculus (download on 6 March 2022) from Uniprot was used to generate a predicted spectral library. The samples in this study were used to generate an experimental spectral library, and ultimately, the results of this study were retrieved based on this spectral library. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine, N-terminal acetylation, and oxidation of methionine were set as modifications. An FDR threshold of 1% was applied for peptide and protein identifications. The other parameter conditions are default parameters.

Proteins with valid values identified in at least three samples out of five biologicals were retained. Following log2 transformation, normalization and missing value imputation were conducted using Perseus (v1.6.15.0) [22]. Sample correlations were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis, and all heatmaps presented in the study were generated in Perseus. Analyses of paired comparisons of age between the two groups were performed using two-sided Student’s t test to identify DEPs. DEPs comparisons across multiple age groups were evaluated using one-way ANOVA.

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using STRING-version 11.0 (https://version-11-0b.string-db.org/) with the organism set to Mus musculus and the minimum required interaction score set to 0.7. The remaining parameters were left at default values. PPI networks were visualized and analyzed using Cytoscape (version 3.8.2) software. To identify key nodes within the network, Degree and Betweenness values were calculated. The Degree value refers to the number of connections a node has with other nodes in the network; nodes with high Degree values are considered hubs and may play critical roles in maintaining network structure and function. The Betweenness value refers to the frequency at which a node lies on the shortest path between other nodes in the network; nodes with high Betweenness values are considered bottlenecks and are often crucial for information flow or communication within the network. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional enrichment analyses of proteins were performed using DAVID (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) and Metascape (http://metascape.org/). Only the species was selected as Mus musculus, with the remaining parameters kept at default values.

For dimensionality reduction and visualization of proteomics data, t-SNE (t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding) analysis was performed using the Rtsne R package (version 0.17). Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted with the tinyarray R package (version 2.4.3). Hierarchical clustering was implemented using the stats R package (version 4.3.3). Protein clustering analysis was performed using the Mfuzz package in R (version 2.62.0), where the fuzzification parameter was set to 1.35 and any other parameters were kept at their default values. For data visualization, ranked abundance plots of quantified protein groups and multiple volcano plots were generated using the ggplot2 R package (version 3.5.1). All analyses were performed in R (version 4.2.0) and RStudio (version 2023.09.1 + 494). Additionally, Venn diagrams, UpSet plots, and boxplots were created using the online tool Hiplot (https://hiplot.com.cn/). Default parameters were used except for customized color schemes to enhance data interpretability.

Metabolic sample preparation and mass spectrometry

Tissue homogenization

Approximately 50 mg of tissue was homogenized in water, followed by extraction with a methanol–acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) containing L-chlorophenylalanine (Sigma-Aldrich). The mixture was vortexed and incubated at −20 °C for 2 h. After centrifugation, the supernatant was concentrated to dryness and stored at −80 °C.

Mass spectrometry

Before the mass spectrometry assay, methanol–water buffer (3:7, v/v) was used for re-dissolution. All samples were loaded into the UPLC-ESI-Q-TOF-MSE system (Acquity UPLC I-class & Vion IMS Qtof MS, Waters) fitted with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS C18 T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm, Waters) with the column temperature set to 45 °C. The mobile phases comprised 0.1% formic acid in water (buffer A) and acetonitrile (buffer B). The flow rate was set to 0.4 ml/min. Gradient: 0–3 min with buffer B ranging from 1 to 20%; 3–6 min with buffer B ranging from 20 to 100%; 6–9 min with buffer B maintained at 100%; 9–10 min with buffer B ranging from 100 to 1%; 10–13.5 min with buffer B maintained at 1%.

Both positive and negative electrospray ion modes were acquired for mass acquisition strategy. The capillary voltage was 2.0 kV for ESI positive and negative modes. Cone voltage: 40 V; desolation temp: 450 °C; desolation gas: 900 L/h and cone gas: 50 L/h; source temp: 115 °C. Acquisition ranges from 50 to 1000 m/z with the scan time at 0.2 s. The collision energy was set at 6 eV for low and at 20–45 eV for high. Leucine-enkephalin was introduced as the lockmass generating a reference for positive ion mode and negative ion mode. A pooled mixture of all samples was used to assess instrument stability throughout the acquisition, and one QC sample was inserted every 10 samples during testing to monitor in-process stability.

Data analysis

All raw MS data processing utilized the Progenesis QI-v2.3 (Waters) software for peak detection, alignment, and standardization. Peak annotation was performed by matching experimental m/z and fragmentation spectra information to HMDB databases (http://www.hmdb.ca/). The raw metabolomic abundance matrices were uploaded to MetabAnalyst 5.0 (http://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) for further analysis. After removing features with less than 50% missing values, KNN was applied to estimate missing values. Next, data was normalized by the median and transformed based on log10 and auto-scaling. Criteria for identifying potential different metabolites included ensuring achieving statistical significance (p < 0.05). Partial least-squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was performed according to the organ systems and ages.

Metabolite feature annotation was performed using the Enrichment Analysis module in MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/). Pathway enrichment analysis was carried out using the “Over Representation Analysis” approach, with metabolite IDs in HMDB format and the KEGG pathway database as reference. Default parameters were applied for all analyses.

Protein-metabolite interactions were analyzed using OmicsNet 2.0 (https://omicsnet.ca/) [23]. The analysis was configured for Mus musculus, with UniProt IDs for proteins and KEGG IDs for metabolites. The KEGG (organism-specific) database was selected for interaction analysis. Networks were visualized in 2D format with the “Auto Layout” setting, while any other parameters were kept at default values.

Statistics and reproducibility

ANOVA was used for comparisons involving more than two groups, followed by the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction to control for the false discovery rate. Two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test was used for pairwise comparisons between two groups. All experiments were performed with 9–10 biological per age group replicates. Details of statistical analyzes are indicated in figure legends.

Results

Untargeted proteomic analysis of ten mouse organs during natural aging

We employed data-independent acquisition (DIA) LC–MS to investigate alterations in protein profiles across ten organs (brain, heart, lung, liver, kidney, spleen, stomach, intestine, muscle, and skin) isolated from C57BL/6JN male and female mice at 4, 8, 12, and 20 months of age (Fig. 1a). Each of the four age groups comprised five biological replicate mice of each sex, and 10 organs were collected from each mouse. This setup yielded a total of 400 samples for analysis. All samples were analyzed using quadrupole orbitrap mass spectrometry in 120-min DIA mode (Q Exactive HF-X, see Methods). The raw DIA mass spectrometry data were processed using DIA-NN software (version 1.8) [20]. A total of 14,822 protein groups (PGs) were detected, with a false discovery rate (FDR) of less than 1% at both the peptide and protein levels. We filtered protein groups present in at least 70% of the biological replicate samples with valid values, obtaining a total of 14,763 PGs for subsequent data analysis (Additional file 2: Table. During the aging process of the ten organs, the number of detected PGs ranged from 9149 (spleen at 20 months) to 4814 (muscle at 4 months) (Fig. 1b). Comparative analysis was conducted between our proteomic data and multi-organ protein datasets from previous studies [24, 25]. The high overlap between our proteomic data and previous studies supports the robustness of our findings. For instance, the results showed a 67.7 and 58.5% overlap with the proteins identified in the brain and skin, respectively, as reported by Takasugi et al. (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Similarly, a 64.5% overlap was observed for proteins in the spleen compared to the findings of Keele, Gregory R et al. The proportion of proteins co-identified across all three studies was 51.5% in the kidney, 49.1% in the liver, 44.1% in the heart, and 40.0% in the lung. Moreover, our study identified a substantial number of proteins not reported in the two comparative studies. In the skin, we detected 3334 additional proteins compared to the existing database. In muscle, we identified an extra 2749 proteins, increasing the total protein count by 50% across all three muscle aging databases. This study expands the current understanding of multi-organ aging.

We evaluated and confirmed the data quality from several perspectives. First, quantitative analysis of each organ showed that the median coefficient of variation (CV) was consistently below 0.025 (Additional file 1: Fig. S2a). Housekeeping proteins, such as Pdk1, Arfgef2, and Osbp, showed stable expression throughout the aging process, consistent with findings reported in existing literature [26] (Additional file 1: Fig. S2b). Furthermore, based on studies assessing the stability of data from large cohorts [27, 28], analysis of the log-transformed quantitative data revealed no significant batch effect between groups (Additional file 1: Fig. S2c). Correlation analysis showed that all biological replicates had correlation coefficients above 0.9 (Additional file 1: Fig. S2d, e). To further evaluate the data quality of the samples, we constructed a dendrogram of all 400 samples using hierarchical clustering and one outlier sample was excluded (Additional file 1: Fig. S2f). The remaining samples were primarily clustered by organ type. In summary, the above results demonstrate the stability and reliability of our generated proteomic data. Logarithmically transformed proteomic data were organized based on expression levels, revealing a dynamic range spanning ten orders of magnitude (Fig. 1c, Additional file 2: Table. S2). The protein abundance across different age stages in each organ is shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S3. This observation highlights the extensive dynamic range of proteomic data, encompassing both highly prevalent and scarcely abundant proteins. Non-linear t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) analysis effectively organized samples by organ type, distinguishing aging stages and genders (Fig. 1d, Additional file 1: Fig. S4 a). As depicted in Additional file 1: Fig. S4 a, the aggregation patterns of samples within each organ varied across ages. In the spleen and stomach samples, the 20-month-old (20 M) sample deviated clearly from the other age groups. Conversely, the 4-month-old (4 M) intestinal samples exhibited a marked divergence from the remaining age groups. Principal component analysis (PCA) of protein composition data for individual organs revealed age-dependent separation patterns. Clear clustering of age groups was observed across different organs (Fig. 1e, Additional file 1: Fig. S4b).

Sex influenced the clustering outcomes of organ samples, particularly in the skin, liver, and kidney (Additional file 1: Fig. S5). To evaluate sex-specific effects during organ aging, we conducted differential expression analysis, which identified distinct sets of differentially expressed proteins in each organ across various age groups (Additional file 1: Fig. S6, Additional file 2: Table. S3). Notably, Serpina3k and Serpina1e were consistently identified as sex-related DEPs across multiple organs and age groups. The Serpina family, a group of serine protease inhibitors, is widely involved in regulating biological processes such as inflammation, immune response, apoptosis, and oxidative stress [29]. However, given the complexity of sex-related effects on aging, further studies are needed to validate these findings. Our study provides comprehensive molecular insights into protein changes during aging across different organs.

Investigating multiple organs simultaneously offers a comprehensive understanding of the intricate interactions between organs during aging. Spearman correlation analysis across the dataset revealed a robust correlation coefficient of 0.853 between the protein profiles of muscle and heart tissues (Fig. 1f, Additional file 2: Table. S4). This notable correlation can be attributed to the shared expression of 64.0% of identified proteins in both organs. Furthermore, kidneys, spleen, skin, lungs, intestines, and stomach also displayed a similarly high correlation coefficient of 0.747, suggesting a distinct interconnectedness in the aging processes across various organs.

Characterization of temporal dynamic differentially expressed proteins with aging in all ten organs

To investigate the dynamics of aging-associated proteins across multiple organs, we analyzed proteins that exhibited significant dynamic changes throughout the aging process (Fig. 2a). First, a one-way ANOVA was performed to evaluate age-related protein expression in each organ, identifying differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) with statistically significant changes across the lifespan (Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-value < 0.05) (Additional file 2: Table. S5). Subsequently, DEPs from each organ were subjected to enrichment analysis, revealing their involvement in regulating signaling pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation, endocytosis, and reactive oxygen species in their respective organs (Fig. 2b) (Additional file 2: Table. S6). Additionally, pathways such as the insulin signaling pathway, TCA cycle, and autophagy signaling pathway were consistently observed in more than half of the organs, all of which have been implicated in aging [1, 6, 30, 31]. In contrast, pathways such as NF-kappa B and basal transcription factors were specifically enriched in the spleen. A comprehensive proteomic analysis of aging in ten organs identified 3066 PGs consistently expressed in all organs (Fig. 2c). The top non-redundant enrichment results [32] indicate that these proteins are majorly involved in fundamental biological processes, including RNA metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and intercellular transport (Additional file 1: Fig. S7). Of these 3066 proteins expressed across organs and throughout the life cycle, 18 proteins with statistically significant differences were identified by age-related ANOVA analysis (adjusted p < 0.05). Within these 18 proteins, three showed a significant decline in expression with aging, while the remaining fifteen exhibited increased expression (Fig. 2e).

Of the proteins exhibiting increased abundance, Hpx was reported as a pro-aging gene in intestinal aging, and older colorectal cancer patients with high Hpx levels were reported with poor prognosis [33]. Our results reveal a substantial increase in Hpx abundance not only in the intestine but also across the other nine organs during aging (Additional file 1: Fig. S8 a). The constant region of the immunoglobulin heavy chains, Ighm, showed significantly increased expression across all ten organs during aging. Ighm encodes the constant region of secretory immunoglobulin M (sIgM), which is crucial for an effective humoral immune response against infections [34, 35]. Besides, C4b (Complement C4-B), a major histocompatibility complex class III protein, exhibits an age-induced increase that delays muscle progenitor cell expansion and impairs functional recovery [36, 37]. We observed that C4b increased in abundance during muscle aging and also displayed a marked increase in abundance across the other nine organs during the aging process. To validate our findings, we compared the 18 DEPs with published data from Takasugi et al. This analysis confirmed consistent expression trends of key proteins, including Ighm, Hpx, and Clu (Additional file 1: Fig. S8b). Notably, the 18 DEPs have not been previously reported in the context of multi-organ aging, highlighting the novel contributions of our study. These findings not only validate the robustness of our dataset but also provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying aging across multiple organ systems.

GO enrichment analysis revealed a strong association of these 18 aging-dynamic DEPs with humoral immune response, immunoglobulin-mediated immune response, and regulation of B cell-mediated immune responses (Fig. 2f, Additional file 1: Fig. S9 a, b). PPI results showed these 18 DEPs form a tightly connected network, supporting their biological plausibility (Additional file 1: Fig. S9 c). This underscores the profound involvement of immune-related processes in cross-organ aging. Collectively, these findings on 18 aging-dynamic DEPs not only substantiate the pivotal role of immune regulation in the aging process at the organismal level but also suggest potential biomarkers for profiling overall organismal aging.

Aging-unique differentially expressed proteins associated with multi-organs are mostly implicated in immune regulation

As previously described, the 18 proteins were expressed throughout the lifespan during aging, with their levels fluctuating across different life stages. However, some proteins appear only at specific ages, which we termed age-unique DEPs (Fig. 3a). We performed a comparative analysis of protein profiles across various organs at each age point to identify these age-unique proteins (Additional file 1: Fig. S10 a, Additional file 2: Table. S7). Protein profiles for each age group were derived from at least seven out of ten biological replicate samples (five females and five males) with reliable quantitative data. We observed that very few age-unique proteins (only 11 proteins) were shared between organs at 4, 8, and 12 M (Additional file 1: Fig. S10 b). In contrast, the unique proteins identified at 20 M showed more prominent inter-organ sharing compared to other younger groups (Fig. 3c). Saa1 (serum amyloid A1) has been identified as a novel pro-inflammatory oncoprotein and a secretory factor in the IL1β pathway [38, 39]. Previous research has shown that Saa1 is enriched in fibroblast-rich organs, particularly the colon and lung, in elderly individuals [40]. In this study, we observed the pan-expression of Saa1 in aging organs with enrichment of fibroblasts, including the heart, kidney, skin, lung, and spleen. This finding suggests a potential intricate involvement of Saa1 in the aging process of organs with substantial fibroblast populations, including the heart, kidney, skin, lung, and spleen.

Next, we analyzed the enrichment of age-unique proteins identified at different ages individually (Additional file 1: Fig. S10 c). We focused on the functional roles of proteins appearing during organ aging (20 M). These 20 M-unique proteins are involved in regulating various immune-related pathways, including regulation of chemokine production, innate immune response, and inflammatory response (Fig. 3d). Protein digestion and absorption pathways related to protein homeostasis were also identified in the functional analysis of 20 M-unique proteins from six organs, namely intestine, kidney, muscle, skin, spleen, and stomach. Some signaling pathways were specific to a single organ; for example, IL-17A signaling was detected only in the liver, and the renin-angiotensin system (RAS) was specific to the skin. RAS holds a pivotal position in regulating immune responses and inflammation via its primary effector molecules. Prior research has demonstrated the crucial regulatory role of RAS in the aging of blood vessels, brain, kidney, muscle, and skin [41, 42]. These findings highlight the critical importance of 20 M-unique proteins in orchestrating immune regulatory pathways during the aging process.

Additionally, a protein–protein interaction network was constructed for the 20 M unique proteins (Fig. 3e). Key proteins such as App, Cyp2e1, and Kras exhibited high degree and high betweenness centrality, identifying them as “hub nodes” within the network. These hub proteins are critical regulators of multiple signaling pathways, indicating their central roles in cellular functions and their potential significance in the aging process. Furthermore, comparative analysis with human longevity gene datasets revealed 50 known longevity genes in the 20 M unique proteins (Additional file 2: Table. S7), highlighting the relevance of aging-unique proteins to lifespan regulation [43]. Egf stimulates the growth of various epidermal and epithelial tissues in vivo and in vitro and the blocking of EGF signaling would prevent the resolution of damage induced by mitogenic stress during senescence [1]. The number of Arg1 + macrophages is tightly regulated during the regenerative repair of young tissues [44]. Proteins in the middle circle are part of the top 15 proteins with the highest betweenness centrality scores, including well-known aging marker proteins such as App and Kras [45]. Amyloid-β, formed through sequential proteolytic processing of App, has been closely associated with the pathogenesis of Alzheimer’s disease [46]. In Drosophila, studies have shown that the deficiency of the Appl (ortholog of App) gene during aging leads to aging-dependent protein homeostasis defects, which is a characteristic hallmark of aging [47].

To validate the biological relevance of the 20 M unique proteins, we performed comparative analyses with human datasets. Comparison with the Human Protein Atlas identified 275 proteins associated with human diseases, underscoring their potential clinical significance [48]. Moreover, cross-referencing with cell senescence signatures from the Human Aging Genomic Resources (HAGR) database identified 50 proteins linked to cellular aging [43]. The genes highlighted with black borders in Fig. 3e are longevity-associated genes reported in HAGR, further supporting the relevance of our findings to human aging. In conclusion, these findings encompass established aging markers as well as numerous proteins with significant but unexplored ties to aging. These 20 M-unique proteins represent potential characteristic biomarkers for aging-related research and may also serve as candidate longevity genes.

To characterize these proteins, we performed pairwise comparisons between 4-month-old (4 M) samples and those at other ages. A total of 1664 DEPs were identified across 10 organs in the pairwise comparison between 8 and 4 M. This number increased to 3948 DEPs in the comparison between 12 and 4 M and further rose to 6290 DEPs in the comparison between 20 and 4 M. As shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S11 a, Venn diagrams compare the pairwise aging-process DEPs in each organ. Notably, aging-process DEPs often reside on the periphery of Venn diagram intersections, indicating their exclusive occurrence in specific aging processes. This also underscores the representative protein changes characteristic of the different aging process.

We then investigated changes in the number of DEPs across the aging process in individual organs. These findings revealed a marked increase in the number of proteins showing more abundance or less abundance expression in age-pairwise comparisons across all organs (Fig. 4d). Specifically, the number of DEPs gradually increased from 8 M vs. 4 M pairwise comparison to 20 M vs. 4 M pairwise comparison (Fig. 4e). The rate of DEP abundance increase varied across organs and temporal intervals. The most rapid rise in aging-process DEPs occurred in muscle during the transition from 8 M vs. 4 M to 12 M vs. 4 M comparisons, while the intestine exhibited the fastest growth in DEPs from 12 M vs. 4 M to 20 M vs. 4 M comparisons. These variations in DEP counts may reflect differences in organ-specific aging rates, with muscle and intestinal aging processes showing more pronounced proteomic changes compared to other organs.

We observed more aging-process DEPs in old age compared to young age, along with increasing overlap of these DEPs between organs. As shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S11 b, as the age of comparison groups increased, the number of DEPs in various organs relative to youth also increased. This was accompanied by greater sharing and complexity of differential protein expression patterns across organs. These findings require further experimental validation to confirm their biological significance.

To validate the functions of different aging-process DEPs, we conducted enrichment analysis for three representative DEPs (Fig. 4f, Additional file 1: Fig. S11 c). While all three DEPs identified across different aging stages were associated with metabolic pathways, notable differences were observed. For example, DEPs from the 8 M vs. 4 M comparison were specifically enriched in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and spliceosome pathways. In contrast, DEPs from the 12 M vs. 4 M and 20 M vs. 4 M comparisons consistently implicated the HIF-1, AMPK, and apelin signaling pathways, consistent with their previously reported roles in aging regulation [49, 50]. Furthermore, pairwise comparisons across ten organs revealed synchronous patterns of metabolic pathway involvement during aging.

We next examined how known markers of aging fared in pairwise comparisons. In lung, a pairwise comparison between 20 and 4 M indicated a significant decline in the expression levels of longevity regulatory pathways proteins, including Atg5, Rptor, and Foxo3 (Fig. 4g). Furthermore, consistent with existing reports, key aging hallmarks such as the autophagy-related protein Becn1 and the nuclear enzyme Sirt1 exhibited reduced expression levels as the organism aged [51, 52]. Conversely, mTOR expression, a central driver of aging associated with increased disease susceptibility and mortality, was significantly elevated in older individuals [1, 53]. These results confirm the expression patterns of established aging markers across different age groups. Furthermore, our analysis also identified a series of previously unreported DEPs implicated in the aging process of various organs. These findings provide new insights into the proteins specifically involved or uniquely present during aging in distinct organs.

We first performed pairwise comparisons between 4 M and other older age groups to identify differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs). Similar to aging-process DEPs shown in Fig. 4e, the pairwise comparison between 20 and 4 M identified the highest number of DEMs (Additional file 1: Fig. S12b). Stomach exhibited the greatest number of DEMs in pairwise comparison between 20 and 4 M, followed by the intestine and liver. Analysis of DEMs from pairwise comparisons across organs showed that most DEMs were located in the periphery of Venn diagrams (Additional file 1: Fig. S12c), indicating their specificity to distinct aging stages. The presence of the largest number of DEMs in the 20 M and 4 M pairwise comparisons for all 10 organs suggests that significant accumulation of DEM species occurs only after a sufficiently long aging period. Moreover, interactions between DEMs across organs showed an age-dependent increase, as illustrated in Additional file 1: Fig. S12d, e, f.

To elucidate the potential relationships between age-related DEMs and functional metabolic changes in ten organs during the aging process, we conducted enrichment analysis on the DEMs (Additional file 1: Fig. S12g). Enrichment results revealed that DEMs from more than half of the organ pairwise comparisons were enriched in signaling pathways, including purine metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism, riboflavin metabolism, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, as well as amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism (Fig. 5b). The findings revealed that changes in purine and pyrimidine metabolism are not limited to the liver and heart, as previously reported, but also extend to other vital organs [55]. Notably, riboflavin metabolism, significantly enriched in more than half of the pairwise comparisons, is linked to the activation of key longevity factors such as AMPK, FOXO, and the mitochondrial unfolded protein response, underscoring its potential relevance in aging [56].

Next, we specifically examined changes in metabolite expression levels were specifically examined in pathways including purine metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism, riboflavin metabolism, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, and amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism (Fig. 5c). The nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor zidovudine has been shown to prolong the healthy lifespan of C. elegans through alterations in the expression of pyrimidine metabolism metabolites, including thymidine, cytidine, and uridine [57]. Similarly, human plasma metabolome studies have reported a significant age-related increase in purine metabolism degradation products, including inosine, xanthine, and hypoxanthine [58]. These findings are consistent with our study. Importantly, this work also reveals temporal shifts in previously unreported metabolites within the purine metabolic pathway across ten vital organs during aging.

Alterations in nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) homeostasis are increasingly recognized as central to nearly all aging-related diseases, including neurodegeneration, diabetes, and cancer [59]. For instance, NAD+ has been identified as a key metabolite in muscle aging, with its abundance directly correlating with health and aging status [60]. In this study, we found that NAD+ expression declined with aging in the liver, intestine, kidney, lung, spleen, muscle, and stomach, while a significant increase was observed in the heart. Given the therapeutic and preventive potential of NAD+ boosting, demonstrated in numerous preclinical studies and disease models, further research across multiple organs and systems may accelerate progress in clinical trials targeting aging-related conditions [61].

Discussion

This study employed proteomics and metabolomics assays across four age groups to investigate molecular changes associated with aging in ten critical mouse organs: brain, heart, intestine, kidney, liver, lung, muscle, skin, spleen, and stomach (Fig. 7). Systematic analyses were conducted to explore similarities and differences in aging patterns, focusing on four key aspects: (1) aging-dynamic differentially expressed proteins, (2) age-unique differentially expressed proteins, (3) aging-process differentially expressed proteins, and (4) aging-process differentially expressed metabolites. The findings revealed that proteins altered during multi-organ aging are primarily associated with humoral immune response, innate immune response, and metabolic pathways, while metabolites are significantly involved in nucleic acid metabolism and nicotinate-nicotinamide metabolism pathways. Protein alterations affect immune responses and metabolic alterations impact nucleotide metabolism during multi-organ aging. Proteins involved in immune responses and metabolites associated with nucleic acid metabolism undergo significant paradigm shift during multi-organ aging. This dataset includes previously established aging biomarkers such as Bcl2, Kat7, Sirt3, Atg5, and NAD +, and also identifies previously unreported multi-organ general aging biomarkers, including both proteins (e.g., Ighm, Hpx, C4b, Fgb) and metabolites (e.g., L-Glutamine, xanthine, cytidine)

This study identified 18 immune-related proteins as common aging markers across ten critical organs, with Ighm (a B-cell activity marker) and Hpx (involved in heme metabolism and oxidative stress response) being particularly notable. Cross-referencing with existing databases confirmed that many of these proteins exhibit increased abundance during aging in multiple organs, validating their reliability as key molecular markers and underscoring their potential for understanding systemic aging mechanisms. Additionally, a series of age-unique proteins were identified. Age-unique proteins could be attributed to several factors, including dramatic changes with age or technical limitations in detection sensitivity. To demonstrate the biological functions of age-specific proteins, we analyzed the dataset of 20 M-unique proteins in comparison to the Human Protein Atlas and found that proteins specific to this aging period in mice are associated with human diseases, including cancer and neurodegenerative disorders. Furthermore, 50 of these proteins were identified as potential human longevity genes, linking murine aging mechanisms to human health and longevity [43, 48].

In line with previous findings, this research reveals asynchronous and unequal aging processes across different organs [3]. The number of unique proteins associated with muscle aging is the highest (Fig. 3b), and the quantity of differentially expressed proteins during aging increases substantially compared to younger stages (Fig. 4e). These observations partially explain why muscle aging may precede other organs and why changes in muscle proteins are more pronounced during aging. However, limitations inherent to mass spectrometry, such as incomplete proteome coverage and detection thresholds, may result in the omission of certain proteins. Consequently, further research employing complementary methodologies is necessary to obtain more definitive evidence and a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms driving aging across organs.

In addition to protein changes, multi-organ metabolomics revealed that the DEMs during aging predominantly clustered in key metabolic pathways, including purine metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism, riboflavin metabolism, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, and amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism. These findings underscore the profound reorganization of metabolic networks over time, with critical aging-related metabolites such as NAD+, inosine, xanthine, and hypoxanthine playing central roles. Notably, thymidine, cytidine, and NAD+ have already been actively utilized in aging studies. Thymidine, cytidine, and NAD+ have been already actively utilized in aging studies.

Aging is intricately intertwined with a myriad of complex biological processes, and a singular focus on studying individual organs typically reveals only a fraction of the associated complexities [65, 66]. The field of aging research has transitioned into the era of multi-organ studies, but current efforts remain largely confined to the transcriptomic level [11, 67]. However, growing evidence indicates a lack of conservation between transcriptomics and proteomics [17, 68, 69]. Thus, a multi-omics approach could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the natural aging process. In recent years, studies have gradually begun to explore proteomic changes associated with aging across multiple organs [24, 70, 71]. Here, 400 tissue samples from 10 organs—brain, heart, intestine, kidney, liver, lung, muscle, skin, spleen, and stomach—across four life stages (4, 8, 12, and 20 months) were systematically collected. DIA technology was employed to achieve comprehensive and unbiased analysis, enhancing protein identification coverage and reproducibility. This study presents a multi-omics, multi-organ atlas of aging, addressing critical gaps in aging research and serving as a valuable resource for elucidating systemic aging mechanisms.

Given that sex is a critical determinant of aging, this study included both male and female subjects [33, 72, 73]. Clear boundaries between sexes were observed in heart, kidney, skin, stomach, and liver samples (As shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S5 and S6). However, this study did not include sex-specific organs such as the ovaries, testes, or prostate. Future research should incorporate these organs to investigate the influence of sex hormones on organ-specific protein expression, providing insights into sex differences in aging. Advanced computational models, such as network analysis and machine learning, can identify sex-specific molecular signatures and their roles in age-related diseases.

Temporal dynamics are pivotal in understanding aging, as aging is inherently linked to time-dependent molecular changes. While this study examined four age points (4, 8, 12, and 20 months), a denser temporal analysis, particularly during critical transitions, could uncover key windows of vulnerability or resilience. The asynchronous aging rates across organs suggest that temporal dynamics significantly shape organ-specific aging trajectories [9]. Future studies should incorporate denser temporal sampling and expand the range of organs to better capture these dynamics.

Due to limitations of available analytical algorithms, we could not integrate proteomics and metabolomics data in this study with previously reported transcriptomic data. Nevertheless, such integration could potentially illuminate the comprehensive impact of aging on physiological systems. Future advancements in detection technologies, improvements in data analysis and rigorous experimental validation will enhance our comprehension of the molecular mechanisms of aging and potential intervention strategies. Collectively, this study provides valuable resources and methodologies for advancing research into aging processes across organisms.

Conclusions

This study delivers high-value proteomic and metabolomic datasets obtained from identical samples, thereby facilitating systematic investigations into the aging process across multiple organs. The comprehensive molecular landscape of aging-related alterations in organs lays crucial groundwork for deciphering the mechanisms underlying aging-associated diseases and deepens our comprehension of the biological, molecular, and clinical implications of aging. While our execution of preliminary proteomic-metabolomic integrative analyses, there remains a compelling need to explore more robust and broadly applicable algorithms for multi-omics data fusion. Our dataset provides a well-established and dependable foundation to underpin the development of such algorithms. Future studies encompassing a broader range of ages, organs, and multi-omics, combined with algorithmic analyses and functional studies of candidate molecules, are essential for clarifying the mechanisms underlying systemic multi-organ aging and advancing anti-aging interventions.
