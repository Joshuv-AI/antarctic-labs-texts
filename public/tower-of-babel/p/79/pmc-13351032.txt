Combined application of hot water and salicylic acid treatment preserves postharvest quality of mangoes
Authors: Udit Singh, Devi Darshan, Sampurna Nand Singh, Ayush Bhushan, Saba Siddiqui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mango fruits undergo various biochemical changes and ripen rapidly after harvest. This study evaluated the effects of salicylic acid (SA) and hot water treatment (HWT) on the maintenance of postharvest quality of mango cv. Dashehari under ambient storage conditions. Physiologically mature fruits were subjected to different treatments, including SA 1 mM, SA 2 mM, HWT, HWT + SA 1 mM, HWT + SA 2 mM, and distilled water (control). Compared with untreated fruits, the combined treatments HWT + SA 1 mM and HWT + SA 2 mM significantly reduced weight loss, decay incidence, electrolyte leakage, reactive oxygen species accumulation, and activities of fruit softening enzymes, while better maintaining fruit firmness, soluble solids content, titratable acidity, and ascorbic acid during storage. The responses of these two combined treatments were statistically comparable for most quality attributes; however, fruits treated with HWT + SA 1 mM exhibited significantly lower malondialdehyde content after storage. These findings suggest that the integration of SA with HWT is an effective strategy for delaying ripening and preserving the postharvest biochemical quality of mango fruits under ambient conditions.

Introduction

Mango (Mangifera indica L.), belonging to the Anacardiaceae family, is one of the most widely cultivated and economically significant tropical fruits due to its high nutritional value, rich sensory attributes, and extensive varietal diversity1. India occupies a leading position in global mango production, with cultivation spread across approximately 2,698 thousand hectares and an annual production exceeding 26,236 thousand metric tons, thereby contributing substantially to the world mango supply2. Among the commercially important mango cultivars, Dashehari was selected for the present investigation due to its high economic importance in northern India and its widespread cultivation in domestic supply chains3. In addition to its commercial relevance, this cultivar is characterized by a high respiration rate, rapid ethylene production, and pronounced susceptibility to postharvest softening, oxidative stress, and decay under ambient conditions3–6. The high perishability and physiological sensitivity of Dashehari make it an ideal model for evaluating postharvest interventions targeting oxidative stress and ripening, enabling clear detection of treatment effects with broader relevance to other mango cultivars under ambient conditions.

Mango is a climacteric fruit that ripens rapidly after harvest due to a rapid increase in ethylene production and respiratory activity at room temperature. Under ambient conditions, elevated temperature further accelerates respiration, ethylene production, and oxidative stress, leading to faster deterioration compared with cold storage systems. These physiological changes accelerate senescence and substantially impact postharvest shelf-life6,7. The ripening process involves extensive physicochemical modifications, including enzymatic degradation of structural polysaccharides and metabolic changes, which collectively increase susceptibility to microbial infection and postharvest decay7,8. Fruit softening critically affects mango marketability and shelf life, resulting from starch hydrolysis into sugars and the breakdown of cell wall components (cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin), leading to reduced firmness and quality9. Reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as hydrogen peroxide and superoxide anions regulate fruit ripening and senescence. However, excessive ROS disrupt redox balance, causing oxidative damage and accelerating tissue deterioration10. Therefore, modulating oxidative stress is essential for maintaining postharvest quality and extending shelf life.

Salicylic acid (SA) is an endogenous phenolic compound widely recognized for its role in regulating plant growth, development, and stress tolerance11–13. Postharvest application of salicylic acid (SA) delays ripening and senescence in mango by enhancing antioxidant defenses, reducing oxidative damage, and maintaining firmness and ascorbic acid content14. Moreover, SA effectively reduces ethylene production and inhibits the activity of cell-wall degrading enzymes, thereby slowing fruit softening and membrane degradation15,16. Similar beneficial effects of SA have been observed in pomegranate, where it significantly preserves the vitamin C content while reducing chilling injury and electrolyte leakage throughout storage17. HWT is an established, eco-friendly postharvest technique used to control decay and maintain quality in fresh produce. The method involves exposing fruits to carefully regulated temperatures ranging from 40 to 55 °C for specific durations18. HWT is considered a safe and nonchemical approach that effectively reduces postharvest losses while maintaining fruit quality in several horticultural commodities19. HWT suppresses fungal pathogens and insect pests, reduces respiration rate, weight loss and delays quality deterioration during storage20. Recent findings have shown that the combined application of hexanal-based enhanced freshness formulation (EFF) with HWT significantly reduces lipid peroxidation and softening enzyme activity in mango fruits21.

Although the individual effects of SA and HWT have been widely reported, previous studies have primarily focused on their independent or additive roles. In contrast, this study provides a mechanistic and integrative evaluation of their combined application by linking oxidative stress, membrane stability, and cell wall-degrading enzymes within a unified framework. A key novel contribution is the use of correlation analysis to establish functional relationships among reactive oxygen species accumulation, lipid peroxidation, membrane integrity, and enzymatic softening during ripening.

Importantly, the study was conducted under ambient storage conditions (20–25 °C), reflecting real supply chain environments in tropical regions where cold storage is limited. The use of the highly perishable Dashehari cultivar and comparison of SA concentrations (1 vs. 2 mM) further provide cultivar-specific and dose-dependent insights. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of SA and HWT in mitigating oxidative stress, membrane deterioration, and enzymatic softening under ambient conditions, with the goal of developing a practical and physiologically informed postharvest strategy for non-refrigerated systems.

Materials and methods

Fruit harvesting

Mango fruits cultivar Dashehari were harvested along with their stalks at the physiological maturity stage, which had a soluble solids content of (12.76%) and a fruit firmness of (102.30 N). Harvesting was conducted early in the morning from a mango orchard located in Bakshi Ka Talab, Lucknow, Uttar Pradesh, India (latitude 26.982279°; longitude 81.002902°). The harvested mango fruits were subsequently transported to the postharvest laboratory of IIAST for experimental analysis.

Fruit treatments and storage

The harvested mango fruits were washed with a disinfectant solution of sodium hypochlorite (2.5 mL/L) and allowed to dry22. Subsequently, the mango fruits were subjected to HWT by immersion in water maintained at 55 °C for 5 min14. Salicylic acid (SA) solutions were prepared according to23. The experiment consisted of six treatments (i) control (untreated); (ii) salicylic acid 1 mM; (iii) salicylic acid 2 mM; (iv) hot water treatment (HWT); (v) HWT + SA 1 mM; (vi) HWT + SA 2 mM and three replications. Mango fruits were dipped in SA solutions (1 mM and 2 mM) for 5 min. at room temperature. For the combined application, the fruits were first subjected to HWT followed by SA 1 mM and SA 2 mM, respectively, whereas untreated mango fruits dipped in distilled water were used as a control. All treated and untreated fruits were air-dried to remove moisture from the fruit surface. The treated and untreated mango fruits were packed in ventilated corrugated fiberboard boxes (approximately 40 × 30 × 12 cm in size), each containing about 5 kg of fruits depending on fruit size, and stored under ambient conditions (20–25 °C) with 65–85% relative humidity24. The physiochemical and enzymatic properties of the fruits were assessed on days 0, 3, 6 and 9 of storage.

Observation recorded

Protein content

Protein content was determined according to25 using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard. All enzyme activities were expressed on a protein basis (U/mg protein), where one unit (U) of enzyme activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to catalyse the formation of 1 µmol of product per min. under the assay conditions.

Measurement of enzymatic activities

PME activity was determined following the method described by26. The reaction mixture consisted of 2.0 mL of 0.5% pectin solution, 0.01% bromothymol blue, 0.75 mL of deionized water, and 0.1 mL of crude enzyme extract. The change in absorbance at 620 nm was recorded, and enzyme activity was calculated from the rate of absorbance change per min. One unit (U) of PME activity was defined as the amount of enzyme causing a change of 0.01 in absorbance per min. under the assay conditions. The activity was expressed as U/mg protein.

Electrolyte leakage (EL)

Electrolyte leakage was determined using approximately 5 g of mango peel tissue cut into small uniform discs and rinsed with distilled water to remove surface-adhered electrolytes. The peel samples were then immersed in 35 mL of deionized water and incubated for 30 min. at 25 °C to measure initial electrical conductivity29. The initial electrical conductivity (EC1) was assessed via a conductivity meter (Eutech Instruments, CON 700). Thereafter, the samples were boiled at 99 °C for 20 min, cooled to room temperature, and the final electrical conductivity (EC2) was measured to calculate electrolyte leakage, expressed as a percentage.

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) content

Hydrogen peroxide content was determined following the method of30. Mango mesocarp tissue (0.5 g) was homogenized in cold trichloroacetic acid (5 mL) and centrifuged at 12,000 × g. The resulting supernatant was collected and mixed thoroughly with 10 mmol L− 1 phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and potassium iodide. The absorbance was measured at 390 nm, and the values are expressed as µmol kg− 1 FW.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content

The malondialdehyde content was analyzed according to the protocols of31. The mango mesocarp (2.0 g) samples were homogenized in ethanol (80%) and centrifuged at 12,000 x g. An equal volume of supernatant and trichloroacetic acid (0.65%) were added, boiled and promptly cooled on ice. The optical density was read at 600 nm, 532 nm and 450 nm. Superoxide anion (O2¯) content

One gram of mesocarp tissue was homogenized in 50 mmol L− 1 phosphate buffer (pH 7.8) and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min. Superoxide anion production was quantified on the basis of the formation of NO2 from hydroxylammonium chloride, and the results are expressed as nmol min− 1 kg− 1 FW30.

Measurement of ascorbic acid content, SSC and titratable acidity (TA)

The ascorbic acid content was determined via a titrimetric method in which the filtrate of the fruit extract was titrated with 2, −6-dichlorophenolindophenol. The results are expressed in mg/100 g, following the procedure described by21.

SSC in the fruit juice were measured via a handheld refractometer (Atago, Tokyo, Japan; range 0–53%), calibrated at 20 °C, and expressed as percent soluble solids. TA, expressed as citric acid, was determined by titrating a known volume of juice with 0.1 N NaOH using phenolphthalein as the indicator3.

Measurement of fruit firmness

A small slice of skin was peeled off along each of the three opposing sides at the midsection of the fruit. Firmness was then assessed using a penetrometer fitted with an 8-mm probe. Firmness was recorded after two measurements were averaged and expressed in Newtons30.

Statistical analysis

The experiment was conducted in a completely randomized design (CRD) with a two-factor factorial arrangement. Data were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in SPSS (version 22.0), and assumptions of normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test) were verified prior to analysis. When significant differences were detected (P ≤ 0.05), mean comparisons were performed using the least significant difference (LSD) test. The LSD test was selected because the experiment involved a limited number of treatments with predefined and biologically relevant comparisons, particularly among control, individual treatments (SA and HWT), and their combinations. Under such conditions, and following a significant ANOVA, the LSD test provides greater statistical power for detecting meaningful treatment differences while maintaining an acceptable Type I error rate. Therefore, its use was considered appropriate for this factorial experimental design. In addition to significance testing, percentage changes were calculated to represent the magnitude of treatment effects. Furthermore, correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationships among the studied parameters using OriginPro 2024 software.

Results

Fruit firmness (N)

Fruit firmness exhibited a continuous reduction with increasing storage duration across all treatments (Fig. 1A). However, the rate of softening was markedly slower in fruits treated with SA and HWT compared with untreated fruits. Fruits treated with HWT + SA 1 mM and HWT + SA 2 mM were most effective in maintaining higher firmness throughout the storage period and were statistically comparable. At the end of storage, fruit firmness increased from 14.86 N in control to 26.71 N in fruits treated with HWT + SA 1 mM, representing an approximately 79.7% higher firmness. In contrast, control fruits exhibited the greatest reduction in firmness over the storage period.

Enzymatic activities

The activities of cell wall-degrading enzymes, including pectin methylesterase (PME), polygalacturonase (PG) and cellulase, gradually increased throughout the study period across all treatments (Fig. 1B, C, D). However, fruits treated with SA and HWT exhibited significantly lower enzymatic activities compared with untreated fruits. At the end of storage, PME activity decreased from 118.73 to 67.09 U/mg protein in fruits treated with HWT + SA 1 mM, representing an approximately 43.5% reduction compared with control. Similarly, PG activity decreased from 48.33 to 32.56 U/mg protein (32.6% reduction), whereas cellulase activity declined from 72.11 to 38.23 U/mg protein (47.0% reduction) under the same treatment. Control fruits consistently exhibited the highest enzyme activities during storage.

Hydrogen peroxide and superoxide anion contents

H2O2 and O2¯ contents showed a consistent upward trend during storage (Fig. 2A, B). However, fruits treated with SA and HWT exhibited significantly lower accumulation of H2O2 and O2¯ compared with untreated fruits. Postharvest application of HWT + SA 1 mM and HWT + SA 2 mM was most effective in suppressing H2O2 and O2¯ accumulation and remained statistically comparable throughout the storage period. At the end of storage, H2O2 content decreased from 20.37 to 11.86 µmol kg− 1 FW in fruits treated with HWT + SA 1 mM, representing an approximately 41.8% reduction compared with control. Similarly, O2¯ content decreased from 26.33 to 16.80 nmol min− 1 kg− 1 FW, corresponding to an approximate 36.2% reduction. Untreated fruits displayed the greatest oxidative stress during storage.

Electrolyte leakage and malondialdehyde content

EL and MDA content increased significantly over the storage period (Fig. 2C, D). However, fruits treated with SA and HWT exhibited significantly lower EL and MDA accumulation compared with untreated fruits. Fruits subjected to HWT + SA 1 mM and HWT + SA 2 mM showed statistically comparable reductions in EL throughout the storage period. At the end of storage, EL was reduced by approximately 39.6% in fruits treated with HWT + SA 1 mM compared with control. Similarly, MDA content was reduced by 37.2% under HWT + SA 1 mM treatment. Furthermore, fruits treated with HWT + SA 1 mM exhibited significantly lower MDA levels than those treated with HWT + SA 2 mM at the end of storage.

Ascorbic acid, SSC and titratable acidity (TA)

Ascorbic acid content declined gradually over the storage period in all treatments (Fig. 3A). However, the decline was more pronounced in untreated fruits, while SA and HWT treatments were most effective in preserving ascorbic acid content. At the end of storage, ascorbic acid content increased from 19.43 to 38.77 mg/100 g in fruits treated with HWT + SA 1 mM, representing an approximately 99.5% higher retention compared with control. In contrast, control fruits recorded the lowest ascorbic acid content.

SSC showed an initial increase up to day 3, followed by a decline in most treatments (Fig. 3B). However, this decline was more pronounced in untreated fruits, whereas SA and HWT treatments helped maintain higher SSC levels during storage. Notably, the combined treatments HWT + SA 1 mM and HWT + SA 2 mM exhibited a continuous increase throughout the storage period. At the end of storage, fruits treated with HWT + SA 1 mM recorded the highest SSC (21.09%), representing approximately 32.9% higher values compared with the control (15.87%). Similarly, HWT + SA 2 mM showed about 29.7% higher SSC than the control.

TA decreased progressively over the storage period across all treatments (Fig. 3C). The decline was more pronounced in untreated fruits, whereas SA and HWT treatments effectively slowed the reduction in TA. At the end of storage, fruits treated with HWT + SA 1 mM retained the highest TA (0.23%), representing approximately 64.3% higher values compared with the control 0.14%. Similarly, HWT + SA 2 mM maintained about 50.0% higher TA than the control.

Decay incidence

Decay incidence increased progressively during storage across all treatments (Fig. 3D). However, decay development was markedly higher in untreated fruits, whereas SA and HWT treatments effectively delayed both the onset and severity of decay. The combined treatments HWT + SA 1 mM and HWT + SA 2 mM recorded the lowest decay incidence and remained statistically comparable throughout the storage period. At the end of the storage period, decay incidence was reduced by over 80% in fruits treated with HWT + SA 1 mM compared with the control. In contrast, control fruits exhibited the highest decay incidence.

Weight loss

Weight loss gradually increased during storage (Fig. 3E). However, fruits treated with SA and HWT exhibited significantly lower weight loss compared with untreated fruits. The combined application of HWT + SA 1 mM and HWT + SA 2 mM were most effective in minimizing weight loss and remained statistically comparable throughout the storage period. At the end of storage, fruits treated with HWT + SA 1 mM showed a 52.8% reduction in weight loss compared with control.

Correlation analysis

The correlation matrix clearly demonstrated strong and biologically meaningful associations among the enzymatic, physiological, biochemical, and quality attributes of mango fruits stored under ambient conditions (Fig. 4). The cell wall degrading enzymes PME, PG and cellulase were highly and positively correlated (r = 0.969–0.991; p ≤ 0.001), indicating their coordinated involvement in cell wall disassembly during ripening and senescence. These enzymes also exhibited strong positive correlations with EL, H2O2, MDA, O2¯, decay incidence, and weight loss (r = 0.893–0.996; p ≤ 0.001), collectively reflecting enhanced membrane deterioration and oxidative stress as ripening progressed. In contrast, fruit firmness was strongly negatively correlated with PME, PG, and cellulase activities (r = −0.919 to −0.974; p ≤ 0.001), confirming that accelerated enzymatic degradation of cell wall polymers is a primary driver of textural softening. Similarly, ascorbic acid content was strongly and negatively correlated with oxidative stress markers (H2O2, MDA, O2¯), EL, and enzymatic activities (r = −0.948 to −0.991; p ≤ 0.001), highlighting the depletion of antioxidant reserves under increasing oxidative load. The SSC was moderately positively correlated with the enzymatic activity and oxidative stress indices, whereas the TA was strongly and negatively associated with these parameters but positively correlated with fruit firmness and ascorbic acid content. These relationships indicate a shift from organic acid-rich, firm fruits toward softer, sweeter fruits as ripening progresses.

Discussions

The present study was conducted under ambient storage conditions (20–25 °C) to simulate real postharvest supply chain environments, where high temperatures accelerate oxidative stress, membrane deterioration, and enzymatic softening. The results clearly demonstrate that oxidative stress regulation functions as a central regulatory hub governing postharvest deterioration under ambient conditions. Specifically, the accumulation of H₂O₂ and O2¯ acts as an upstream signal that triggers lipid peroxidation, membrane destabilization, and activation of cell wall-degrading enzymes, thereby accelerating fruit senescence. These findings demonstrate a mechanistic interaction between SA and HWT in regulating oxidative metabolism and fruit softening under ambient storage conditions.

The combined treatment significantly decreased weight loss, decay incidence, and EL, while effectively preserving fruit firmness, SSC, TA, and ascorbic acid levels compared with untreated fruits. Fruit firmness is one of the most important physical parameters of mango fruits and is markedly influenced by the ripening process33. The present investigation revealed that the postharvest application of SA alone or in combination with HWT significantly preserved fruit firmness. SA treatment maintains firmness by reducing the activities of cell wall-degrading enzymes and decreasing ethylene production16. The preservation of firmness may also be attributed to reduced respiration rates and inhibition of cell wall- and starch-degrading enzyme activities34. HWT further contributes to firmness retention by influencing enzymatic activity and inducing heat stress responses that stabilize the cell wall structure. HWT appears to suppress cell wall-degrading enzyme activities, possibly through the induction of heat shock proteins and modulation of stress-related signaling pathways14,19. The combined application of HWT and SA enhances membrane functionality and reduces the activity of cell wall hydrolases in several fruit crops35,36.

The disassembly of cell wall pectin is a decisive biochemical event underlying textural softening in ripening fruits. Pectin-degrading enzymes, notably PME and PG, mediate the hydrolysis and de-esterification of α-(1→4)-linked galacturonic acid polymers, resulting in progressive pectin depolymerization and disruption of cell wall integrity37. These enzymatic transformations substantially impair cell adhesion and contribute to the decline in mechanical firmness during ripening38. In parallel, enhanced cellulase activity facilitates cell wall polysaccharide solubilization and accelerates tissue disintegration26. In the present study, mango fruits treated with HWT + SA 1 mM and HWT + SA 2 mM showed significant suppression of these enzymes and exhibited statistically comparable responses during the storage period. This enzymatic downregulation effectively delayed textural deterioration. Suppression of cell wall-degrading enzymes is closely associated with reduced oxidative stress. As reactive oxygen species act as signals inducing genes for cell wall hydrolases during ripening, lower H2O2 and O2¯ levels may downregulate PME, PG, and cellulase activities, thereby delaying pectin solubilization and cell wall disassembly. This suggests a coordinated mechanism where oxidative stress modulation preserves structural integrity and fruit texture. The inhibitory effect of SA on softening enzymes can be attributed to its ability to modulate ethylene biosynthesis and respiratory metabolism16, whereas HWT may restrict pectin solubilization and depolymerization through stabilization of the middle lamella39. Comparable findings have been reported by15,21.

EL and MDA accumulation serve as critical biomarkers of membrane deterioration and lipid peroxidation under stress conditions30. These findings indicate that oxidative stress, membrane integrity, and enzymatic activity are tightly interconnected in regulating fruit senescence. Membrane perturbations arise from an imbalance between H2O2 and O2¯ production and antioxidant defence mechanisms28. Excessive accumulation of H2O2 and O2¯ induces oxidative damage, compromises membrane integrity, and accelerates senescence. Elevated MDA levels and EL reflect increased membrane permeability and loss of cellular compartmentalization. In the present study, fruits subjected to combined treatments showed significantly reduced EL, which may be associated with enhanced antioxidant defence systems40,41. The superior efficacy of HWT + SA 1 mM may be attributed to the anti-senescence properties of SA and its regulatory effect on lipoxygenase activity21. Comparable reductions in EL and MDA have been reported in sweet pepper and plum fruits subjected to SA and HWT42–44. Reduced EL and MDA accumulation indicate preservation of membrane lipid integrity through suppressed lipid peroxidation. Lower MDA levels reflect reduced lipoxygenase-mediated peroxidation under oxidative stress. SA helps regulate membrane-associated enzymes and stabilize phospholipids, while heat treatment enhances membrane fluidity and protective protein conformations. Together, these effects maintain membrane semi-permeability and cellular compartmentalization, thereby delaying senescence.

Reactive oxygen species such as H2O2 and O2¯ are key mediators of oxidative stress during storage. Excessive accumulation of H2O2 and O2¯ accelerates senescence and reduces the shelf life of perishable commodities28. The reduced accumulation of H2O2 and O2¯ indicates that the combined treatment modulates cellular redox homeostasis by enhancing both enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems. SA upregulates key antioxidant enzymes superoxide dismutase, catalase, and peroxidase which collectively detoxify H2O2 and O2¯, thereby preventing oxidative damage7,45. In parallel, HWT may induce heat shock proteins and stress-responsive signaling pathways that stabilize cellular metabolism and improve ROS-scavenging efficiency46,47. The synergistic interaction between SA and HWT therefore appears to operate through coordinated activation of antioxidant defense pathways, resulting in reduced oxidative burden and delayed senescence. Similar findings have been reported in mango and other fruit systems40,48. Collectively, these responses indicate that the combined SA and HWT treatment effectively coordinates antioxidant defence, membrane stabilization, and enzymatic regulation to delay fruit senescence.

Ascorbic acid plays a vital role in maintaining nutritional quality due to its antioxidant properties49. SSC and TA are important indicators of fruit quality and flavor balance6. Their deterioration throughout storage is primarily associated with respiration and metabolic activity7. SA mitigates these changes by reducing respiration rate and enhancing antioxidant systems, thereby preserving ascorbic acid, SSC, and TA45,12,40,41. HWT further stabilizes cellular metabolism by inducing stress-response mechanisms14,18,19. Decay incidence is a major factor limiting shelf-life and is primarily associated with microbial infection. In the present study, combined treatments significantly reduced decay incidence. SA enhances disease resistance by inducing systemic acquired resistance and activating defense-related proteins12,45. HWT reduces microbial load and suppresses pathogen development through heat-induced responses14,18. Weight loss is a critical indicator of postharvest deterioration caused by transpiration and respiration32. The reduced weight loss observed in treated fruits may be attributed to SA-mediated regulation of physiological processes and HWT-induced enhancement of surface barrier properties, including cuticular wax deposition14,19.

The superior performance of HWT + SA 1 mM compared with HWT + SA 2 mM suggests a concentration-dependent physiological response. The superior performance of SA 1 mM compared with SA 2 mM suggests a concentration-dependent physiological response, which can be explained through a hormetic mechanism. Hormesis refers to a biphasic dose response relationship in which low concentrations of a bioactive compound stimulate beneficial physiological processes, whereas higher concentrations may exert inhibitory or even detrimental effects. At lower concentrations SA 1 mM likely acts as a signaling molecule that enhances antioxidant defense systems, including the activation of enzymes such as superoxide dismutase, catalase, and peroxidase, thereby effectively scavenging reactive oxygen species and maintaining cellular redox homeostasis.

In contrast, higher concentrations SA 2 mM may disrupt cellular homeostasis by inducing hormonal imbalance and oxidative stress, potentially leading to partial phytotoxic effects. Excess SA has been reported to interfere with endogenous hormone signaling pathways, particularly ethylene and auxin, which may alter ripening regulation and stress responses. Furthermore, supra-optimal SA concentrations can lead to excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species, exceeding the detoxification capacity of antioxidant systems and thereby compromising membrane stability, as reflected in comparatively higher MDA levels. The relatively better membrane integrity and lower lipid peroxidation observed in fruits treated with HWT + SA 1 mM further support the hypothesis that moderate SA levels optimize redox balance without inducing stress-related damage. These findings indicate that the efficacy of SA is strongly dose-dependent, and that optimal concentrations are critical for achieving maximum physiological benefits under ambient storage conditions.

Conclusion

The study demonstrates that combined application of SA and HWT provides a synergistic and mechanistically coordinated strategy for preserving postharvest quality of mango (Mangifera indica L.) cv. Dashehari under ambient conditions. The treatment effectively modulated oxidative metabolism by reducing reactive oxygen species, limiting lipid peroxidation, and maintaining membrane integrity, thereby delaying senescence. A key finding is the strong linkage between oxidative stress regulation and cell wall metabolism, where suppression of PME, PG, and cellulase activities improved fruit firmness. Correlation analysis further confirmed that oxidative damage, membrane deterioration, and enzymatic softening are tightly interconnected processes governing ripening, highlighting that integrated treatments act on multiple physiological pathways simultaneously.

Among treatments, HWT + SA 1 mM was most effective, emphasizing the importance of optimal SA concentration in balancing antioxidant defense and metabolic regulation without inducing stress. From an applied perspective, this combined approach is simple, cost-effective, and environmentally safe, making it suitable for supply chains lacking cold storage, especially in tropical and subtropical regions. Thus, the findings provide both mechanistic insight and a practical alternative for improving shelf life and fruit quality under ambient storage conditions.
