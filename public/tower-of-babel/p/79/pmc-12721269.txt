Female department chairs and diversity among general surgery applicants and entering residents☆
Authors: Alexander L Ren, Jeff Choi, Sherry M Wren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAcademic surgical department chairs play a critical role in guiding the missions of their departments. One understudied aspect of chairs' influence is their effect on trainee recruitment and diversity. This study explores whether the appointment of female surgical chairs is associated with an increase in the proportion of female and underrepresented-in-medicine applicants and entrants to academic general surgery residency programs.MethodAcademic programs in which a female chair was appointed to replace a male chair were identified. Male-led programs were identified and matched 2:1 to each female-led program based on similar geographic location and time span. Data on applicant and entrant demographics for all included programs was obtained from the Association of American Medical Colleges. A difference-in-difference analysis of applicants and entrants was performed comparing female and underrepresented-in-medicine proportions between the pre-chair appointment and post-appointment years.ResultsNo significant changes were found in the proportions of female or underrepresented-in-medicine applicants/entrants following the appointment of a female chair.ConclusionsOur study found that the appointment of new female chairs is not associated with the gender or racial/ethnic composition of residency applicants/entrants in the immediate post-appointment period. This indicates that increasing diversity among surgical trainees likely relies on the combined efforts of multiple department leaders and faculty members, as well as broader advocacy and outreach efforts in the surgical community.Keywords: Diversity, Residency, Gender, Representation, Leadership

Introduction

In a specialty with predominantly male surgeon leaders [1], female surgeon role models are critical for recruiting and retaining female general surgery residents [2]. It is not fully understood how female representation interacts with racial/ethnic diversity in the recruitment of surgical trainees. Furthermore, the role of female department chairs in promoting diversity among general surgery trainees has yet to be determined.

We aimed to evaluate the association between the appointment of female chairs and diversity among academic general surgery program applicants and entering residents using a difference-in-difference analysis, hypothesizing that compared with departments led by male chairs, those with newly appointed female chairs would see an increased proportion of female and underrepresented-in-medicine (URiM) applicants and entrants in the immediate years post-appointment.

Methods

We selected US medical school-affiliated general surgery residency programs with female department chairs appointed between 3/18/2005 (Match Day 2005) and 6/7/2018 (2018–19 Electronic Residency Application Service [ERAS] cycle opened) with 1) a preceding male department chair, 2) at least four entering resident classes following the female chair appointment, and 3) program match data available from the Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC). Female-led programs were identified in Q2 of 2023 using the Association of Women Surgeons (AWS) website (https://www.womensurgeons.org/female-chairs-of-surgery). The official appointment dates of incoming chairs were approximated as best as possible from online searches of press releases and news articles. Each of the 14 identified programs were matched with two male chair-led general surgery programs across the same 8-year time span based on geographical proximity (Supplemental Table 1). Data regarding the number of categorical general surgery residency programs was obtained from the Advance Data Tables for the 2023 Main Residency Match data published by the National Resident Matching Program (NRMP).

Demographic data of general surgery residency program applicants and entrants from the four Match cycles preceding (pre-appointment) and succeeding (post-appointment) each female chair appointment date was acquired from AAMC. We defined URiM as those who self-identified as “American Indian or Alaska Native”, “Black or African American”, “Hispanic, Latino, or of Spanish Origin”, or “Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander.”

Difference-in-differences analysis evaluated changes in female and URiM representation among applicants and entering residents between female chair-led and male chair-led programs in the pre- and post-appointment periods (i.e. 4 years preceding vs. 4 years succeeding female chair appointment). Graphical analysis evaluated the parallel trends assumption. The Bonferroni-adjusted threshold for statistical significance was p < 0.03. Statistical analysis was conducted using R (version 4.2.2) and graphical analysis was conducted with GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.3).

Results

Among 355 US categorical general surgery residency programs accepting applications for the Match, fifteen programs (4.2 %) were identified as having female chairs in 2023. Our study cohort examined 14 female-led programs (one program did not submit data to the AAMC) and 28 matching male-led programs, encompassing 400,428 applications (36.9 % female, 11.0 % URiM) and 4315 entering residents (38.6 % female, 6.0 % URiM), between 2008 and 2022. Across all programs, the average percentage of female and URiM applications (entrants) ranged between 28.3–42.3 % (24.2–61.0 %), and 8.0–15.1 % (0–15.4 %) per year, respectively.

Difference-in-differences analysis did not find a statistically significant association between a newly-appointed female chair and the proportion of female applicants or entering residents between the pre- and post-appointment years (applicants: p = 0.29; entrants: p = 0.28; Fig. 1). We also did not find a statistically significant association between a newly-appointed female chair and the proportion of URiM applicants or entering residents between pre- and post-appointment years (applicants: p = 0.62; entrants: p = 0.46; Fig. 1).

Discussion

Our study did not find an association between the appointment of a new female department of surgery chair and the proportion of female or URiM general surgery applicants and entering residents. Our findings mirror those of a cross-sectional study of plastic surgery departments, which did not find a statistically significant correlation between female chairs and female representation among residents [3]. Prior studies reported the importance of faculty female representation [4] and ethnic diversity [5] for female and URiM applicants, respectively, but the impact of gender among those ranking highest among department leadership remains unclear. Additionally, there is little literature examining the intersection of chair gender and ethnic diversity among surgical trainees.

While chair gender may not have an association with trainee diversity, we cannot discount that diversity may be an important priority among female and male chairs. Our study suggests that new chairs are unlikely to directly increase the diversity of their residency programs in the short-term. However, increasing trainee diversity will likely culminate from years-long multimodal efforts including mentorship and sponsorship programs, increasing representation in surgical leadership, and broader cultural shifts in the field [1].

Study limitations

The residency match is a culmination of multifactorial consideration by both applicants and programs; finding associations between a single factor and the composition of residents may be challenging. We may not have found an association between a newly-appointed female chair and applicant diversity because this factor on its own does not play a considerable role in female or URiM applicants' interest in specific programs, or because there are too few female chair-led programs and our study lacked adequate power. It is important to recognize that the final matches that the residency algorithm outputs may not hew exactly to a program's submitted rank list; therefore, it is difficult to ascribe a program's trainee diversity to their selection priorities. Analysis was limited to the 4 years immediately after chair appointment, which would fail to capture long-term effects of chair gender on recruitment. Matching criteria were restricted to geography and time span. Included chairs were appointed at various times over the last 20 years (Supplemental Table 1), during which a steady rise in female representation across all levels of medical training was observed. Our study is also limited by not accounting for diversity of program directors and other faculty as this information is challenging to ascertain for a difference-in-difference analysis.

Conclusion

Few US academic surgery departments are led by female chairs. While we did not find an association between newly-appointed female chairs and sex or ethnic diversity of applicants and entering residents, the importance of female leaders to support and inspire the growing proportion of female surgeons persists, and this impact warrants further study.
