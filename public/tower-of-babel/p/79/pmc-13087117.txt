Prognostic value of the fibrosis-4 index for predicting in-hospital mortality in sepsis patients: evidence from MIMIC-IV and eICU databases
Authors: Xiangli Kong, Bin Jiang, Cuiping Xu, Xiaohong Zhou, Feifei He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSepsis-induced hepatic dysfunction contributes significantly to poor clinical outcomes. Although the Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index is established for assessing chronic liver fibrosis, its utility as a prognostic marker for acute sepsis mortality—particularly in patients without pre-existing liver disease—remains unclear. We aimed to evaluate the predictive value of FIB-4 in sepsis using two large, multi-center databases.We conducted a multicenter retrospective study using two large critical care databases: MIMIC-IV (n = 13,983) and eICU (n = 9,976). Sepsis was defined based on established clinical criteria. Missing values were handled using multiple imputation to preserve data integrity. The optimal FIB-4 cutoff points were determined via outcome-based stratification (1.25). Patients were categorized into high and low FIB-4 groups accordingly. We applied restricted cubic spline (RCS) modeling to evaluate nonlinear trends, followed by weighted Cox regression to determine independent associations with in-hospital mortality. Kaplan–Meier survival curves assessed time-to-event differences, while subgroup analyses, ROC curves, and sensitivity analyses explored effect consistency and underlying biological pathways. Across 23,959 sepsis patients from the MIMIC-IV and eICU databases, elevated FIB-4 levels were significantly associated with in-hospital mortality (P < 0.001). An FIB-4 index > 1.25 served as an independent risk factor for mortality (adjusted HRs: 1.38–1.55) and outperformed traditional SOFA and APACHE scores in prognostic discrimination. Kaplan–Meier curves showed significantly reduced survival in the high FIB-4 group. Importantly, sensitivity analyses excluding patients with known liver disease, suspected MASLD, or cardiac admissions confirmed the robustness of these findings. The FIB-4 index serves as a robust, independent prognostic marker for in-hospital mortality in sepsis patients, outperforming traditional scores such as SOFA and APACHE II in predictive accuracy. Crucially, its prognostic value persists even after excluding patients with pre-existing liver disease or acute cardiogenic hepatic congestion, suggesting it reflects broader sepsis-induced physiological derangements rather than solely baseline hepatic fibrosis. Introduction

Sepsis is defined as a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. It remains one of the predominant causes of critical illness in intensive care units (ICUs) globally, imposing a substantial burden on public health1. Despite advances in medical care, sepsis affects millions of patients annually, with mortality rates ranging from 20 to 40%2. In 2020, the sepsis-related mortality rate in China was 76.6 per 100,000 people3. Early identification of high-risk patients and timely intervention are crucial for improving the prognosis of patients with sepsis. However, commonly used clinical scoring systems, such as the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE), although effective in assessing the severity of sepsis, involve complex calculations and require real-time monitoring of multiple physiological indicators, which limits their practical clinical application4. Therefore, finding a simple and reliable biomarker to assist in the early identification of high-risk patients with sepsis is of great significance for optimizing clinical management.

The Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index is a non-invasive indicator based on age, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and platelet count. It was initially developed to assess the degree of liver fibrosis in patients with chronic liver disease5. As its calculation requires only standard blood tests and does not necessitate specialized equipment or complex methodologies, this approach is highly accessible and extensively adopted in clinical settings. In recent years, the prognostic value of FIB-4 in various acute and chronic diseases has gradually attracted attention. For example, in patients with chronic kidney disease and cardiovascular disease, FIB-4 has been shown to be associated with disease progression and adverse outcomes6–8. These findings suggest that FIB-4 may have broader potential applications beyond the field of liver disease.

Although FIB-4 was originally designed to evaluate liver fibrosis, its potential connection with inflammatory responses and organ dysfunction has also drawn attention in sepsis research9,10. Patients with sepsis often have a systemic inflammatory response, leading to multi-organ dysfunction, with liver and coagulation system abnormalities being particularly common. Some studies have shown that FIB-4 may be related to the intensity of inflammation and the degree of organ dysfunction in patients with sepsis11. Unlike the SOFA score, which primarily relies on bilirubin to detect hepatic dysfunction, the FIB-4 index integrates markers of hepatocellular injury (AST/ALT) and coagulopathy (platelet count). This distinction is vital because sepsis-associated liver injury often manifests as hypoxic hepatitis (“shock liver”) or microcirculatory dysfunction before the onset of overt jaundice; thus, FIB-4 may provide a more sensitive and holistic reflection of the ‘liver-coagulation axis’ in the early stages of sepsis. In this acute context, the components of FIB-4 likely serve as acute-phase reactants reflecting systemic inflammation-driven hepatic stress and consumptive coagulopathy rather than chronic structural damage. Thus, FIB-4 may offer a pragmatic, cost-effective, and standardized tool for early risk stratification.

Consequently, this study aims to evaluate the prognostic value of the FIB-4 index for in-hospital mortality in sepsis patients and compare its performance with established scores like SOFA and APACHE. We hypothesized that an elevated admission FIB-4 index is independently associated with increased in-hospital mortality in septic patients and provides incremental prognostic value over established scores like SOFA and APACHE. To test this hypothesis, we used the large-scale MIMIC-IV database for primary analysis and validated the findings in the multicenter eICU Collaborative Research Database. Furthermore, we conducted a head-to-head comparison of the predictive performance of FIB-4 against established critical care scoring systems and performed rigorous sensitivity analyses. Ultimately, we aim to provide a simple, evidence-based tool that enhances early risk stratification for sepsis patients.

Methods

Data source

This was a retrospective observational study utilizing data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) version 3.1 database, hosted on the PhysioNet platform. MIMIC-Ⅳ is a publicly available database that contains de-identified electronic health records of patients admitted to Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 201912. The database includes comprehensive demographic information, physiological monitoring parameters, laboratory test results, and disease diagnoses based on the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Edition (ICD-9) and Tenth Edition (ICD-10). All personal identification information has been removed to protect patient privacy, therefore, this study does not require patient consent or ethical review board approval. The study was conducted in strict accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines13.

The eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD) contains more than 200,000 ICU admissions from hospitals across the United States between 2014 and 2015. The database integrates comprehensive clinical data, including demographics, hourly vital signs, laboratory results, medication records, and nursing notes. All data were de-identified in accordance with the HIPAA Safe Harbor provision.

Study population

This study included patients diagnosed with sepsis based on the Sepsis 3.1 criteria, which define sepsis as a suspected or confirmed infection accompanied by a SOFA score of ≥ 214. To capture the full clinical spectrum of the disease, we included both sepsis and septic shock patients, the latter of which was characterized by persistent hypotension and serum lactate levels > 2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation. Patient selection from the MIMIC-Ⅳ and eICU databases was conducted in accordance with methods established in previous studies15. Exclusion criteria included: (1) patients with multiple ICU admissions due to sepsis (only the first admission was included); (2) patients diagnosed with acute pancreatitis; and (3) patients with missing essential data, such as FIB-4 values. In total, 13,983 patients were included in the study, with 3,280 in-hospital deaths and 10,703 survivors, all data were derived from the MIMIC-Ⅳ dataset, spanning the years 2008 to 2019 (Fig. 1-A). The eICU database contains patients from 2014 to 2015, including a total of 9,976 cases, of which 1,701 died in the hospital and 8,275 survived (Fig. 1-B). The data for all laboratory tests were first obtained within 24 h of admission to the ICU.

FIB-4

FIB-4 is a simple, rapid, and widely accessible non-invasive marker for liver fibrosis staging. It is one of the most reliable and useful tools for non-invasive liver fibrosis assessment16. Originally derived from the APRICOT database, FIB-4 not only evaluates liver function but also reflects the systemic effects of liver disease and the extent of liver fibrosis17.

Definition of study outcomes and follow-up

The primary outcome was defined as survival status and corresponding length of stay hospital. The survival time was defined as the period from admission to the earliest of death or discharge, with discharged patients recorded as survivors. Follow-up began from the date of admission and continued until discharge or death.

Covariate evaluation

In this study, potential confounding factors were extracted and analyzed from the MIMIC-Ⅳ database and eICU using PostgreSQL (version 13.7.2) and Navicat Premium (version 16) by executing structured query language (SQL), including Race, Albumin, Potassium, Lactate, RBC, Total Bilirubin, WBC, Creatinine, Urea Nitrogen, Prothrombin Time, pH, Hypertension, Heart Failure, COPD. Additional data included basic patient information, laboratory test results, and clinical outcomes. All laboratory parameters were derived from the first available measurements after admission, extracted from the MIMIC-IV (v3.1) database and eICU.

Statistical analysis

There is currently no universally accepted standard for exclusion criteria based on missing data in variables. In the study by Zhang et al.18, variables with more than 80% missing data were excluded. In contrast, this study applied a threshold of 40% for missing values. Prior to model fitting, we assumed that the data were “Missing at Random” (MAR) and employed multiple imputation using the “mice” package in R Studio to address missing data. The “maxstat” package was used to calculate the maximum statistics and determine the optimal cutoff value of FIB-4, which divided the study population into low FIB-4 and high FIB-4 groups. To explore the potential non-linear relationship between FIB-4 and in-hospital mortality in sepsis, restricted cubic splines (RCS) analysis was employed. After testing 3–7 knots, the 5-knot model with the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was selected, and the inflection point was determined based on the shape of the RCS curve. The independent association of FIB-4 with in-hospital mortality risk in sepsis was evaluated using a weighted Cox proportional hazards model, and the results were presented in three models: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for Race), and Model 3 (further adjusted for Albumin, Potassium, Lactate, RBC, Total Bilirubin, WBC, Creatinine, Urea Nitrogen, Prothrombin Time, pH, Hypertension, Heart Failure, COPD). Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated to compare the survival probabilities of sepsis patients with different FIB-4 levels, and group differences were assessed using the log-rank test. Stratified and interaction analyses were performed by age group, gender, race, and ICU type. The discriminatory ability of the FIB-4 index was evaluated by generating Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves. The Area Under the Curves (AUCs) were compared with those of established scoring systems, including the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE), using the DeLong test. Furthermore, sensitivity analyses were performed to verify the robustness and independence of FIB-4 as an acute prognostic marker for sepsis. These analyses were conducted by excluding patients with pre-existing liver diseases, potential metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), and those admitted to the Cardiac Intensive Care Unit (CICU). All analyses were performed using R software, and a two-sided P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of sepsis patients in the MIMIC-IV and eICU cohorts

In this study, a total of 13,983 and 9,976 sepsis patients were identified from the MIMIC-IV test cohort and the eICU validation cohort, respectively, based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria (Fig. 1A-B). Missing data were addressed using multiple imputation, ensuring that the distribution of variables remained consistent before and after imputation (Tables S1 and S2).

In the MIMIC cohort, age differed significantly between groups (P < 0.001), with the non-survivor group being older than the survivor group (68.60 ± 15.43 vs. 64.53 ± 16.67 years). No significant difference was observed in sex distribution (P = 0.44). Racial composition, however, showed a highly significant difference (P > 0.99): the proportion of White patients was lower in the non-survivor group (57.77%) than in the survivor group (63.43%), whereas other racial groups were more prevalent among non-survivors. Regarding anthropometric measures, body weight, height, and BMI were all significantly lower in the non-survivor group than in the survivor group (all P < 0.001); In addition, FIB-4 scores were significantly higher in non-survivors compared with survivors (6.21 ± 5.98 vs. 4.65 ± 5.05, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1C).

In the eICU cohort, age also differed significantly between groups (P < 0.001), with higher mean values observed in non-survivors (69.16 ± 14.17 years) than in survivors (64.86 ± 15.89 years). Sex distribution was comparable between groups (P = 0.83), with nearly identical proportions of females (48.03% vs. 48.34%) and males (51.97% vs. 51.66%). Unlike the MIMIC cohort, racial composition did not differ significantly in the eICU cohort (P = 0.33). Finally, FIB-4 levels were significantly higher in the non-survivor group than in the survivor group (5.81 ± 5.98 vs. 3.82 ± 4.42) (Fig. 1D).

To evaluate the prognostic accuracy of FIB-4, we calculated the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) for predicting in-hospital mortality, comparing it against widely used critical illness scores (APACHE and SOFA). In the MIMIC cohort (Fig. 2A), the FIB-4 index yielded an AUROC of 0.678 (95% CI: 0.667–0.689), which was significantly superior to both the APACHE score (AUROC: 0.633, 95% CI: 0.622–0.644) and the SOFA score (AUROC: 0.613, 95% CI: 0.601–0.625). DeLong tests confirmed that these differences were statistically significant (all P < 0.001). These findings were validated in the eICU cohort (Fig. 2B), where FIB-4 similarly demonstrated superior predictive efficacy compared to the other scores (all P < 0.001). Finally, the optimal cutoff value for FIB-4, determined via the Youden index in the MIMIC cohort, was applied to the eICU cohort. Nonlinear association between FIB-4 index and in-hospital mortality in sepsis patients

To investigate the potential nonlinear relationship between the FIB4 index and in-hospital mortality risk among sepsis patients, a restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was performed. Based on the combined analyses of the MIMIC and eICU databases (Fig. 3), elevated FIB-4 levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of mortality in both cohorts (overall P < 0.001). In both populations, FIB-4 values were primarily distributed between 0 and 10. However, the patterns of association differed between the two databases. In the MIMIC database, a significant nonlinear relationship was observed (P < 0.001); the mortality risk increased rapidly to approximately 1.8 when FIB-4 was below 10 and remained at a high level thereafter. In contrast, the nonlinear relationship was not significant in the eICU database (P = 0.406), with no clear inflection point. Instead, the mortality risk gradually rose to over 2.0 as FIB-4 levels increased. Despite these different patterns, both databases consistently showed that higher FIB-4 levels are associated with an increased risk of mortality (Tables 3 and 4).

FIB-4 independently predicts in-hospital mortality in patients with sepsis using Weighted Cox Regression

To clarify the relationship between the FIB-4 index and in-hospital mortality among patients with sepsis, weighted Cox regression analysis was conducted in both the MIMIC-IV test cohort and the eICU validation cohort, revealing a significant positive association between FIB-4 and mortality risk. In the MIMIC-IV cohort, each one-unit increase in FIB-4, treated as a continuous variable, was associated with a significantly elevated risk of in-hospital mortality in the unadjusted model (HR = 1.032, 95% CI: 1.027–1.037, P < 0.0001), and this association remained robust after multivariable adjustment (Model 2: HR = 1.032; Model 3: HR = 1.017; both P < 0.0001). When stratified using a cutoff value of 1.25, patients in the high FIB-4 group had significantly higher mortality risks compared to the low FIB-4 group (Model 1: HR = 1.563; Model 2: HR = 1.56; Model 3: HR = 1.382; all P < 0.0001, see Table 5). Further stratified Cox regression indicated that while the low FIB-4 group showed an elevated hazard ratio (HR = 1.127), it was not statistically significant (P = 0.1343), whereas the high FIB-4 group showed a significant association (HR = 1.252, P < 0.0001), with a log-likelihood ratio test result of < 0.0001 (Table S7).

Kaplan–Meier analysis of the impact of FIB-4 on survival probability in sepsis patients

To further investigate the impact of the FIB-4 index on the survival outcomes of sepsis patients, Kaplan–Meier survival curve analysis and between-group comparisons were performed in both the MIMIC-IV test cohort and the eICU validation cohort. The Kaplan–Meier curves revealed that patients in the high FIB-4 group exhibited a lower survival probability early in the follow-up period, with a markedly faster decline in survival over time compared to those in the low FIB-4 group. This suggests that individuals with elevated FIB-4 levels face a higher risk of mortality within a given time frame. In contrast, the low FIB-4 group demonstrated consistently higher survival probabilities and a slower rate of decline, indicating a more favorable survival prognosis. The survival differences between the two groups were statistically significant in both datasets (MIMIC-IV: P < 0.0001, eICU: P < 0.0001, Fig. 3A-B).

In addition, to assess the distribution of FIB-4 across different survival outcomes, the study conducted a comparative analysis of FIB-4 levels between survivors and non-survivors in both the MIMIC-IV and eICU cohorts (Fig. 3C-D). The results showed that FIB-4 values were significantly higher in the non-survivor group compared to the survivor group in both datasets (P < 0.001). Boxplots further illustrated that the median FIB-4 level was substantially elevated in the non-survivor group. These findings reinforce the potential of FIB-4 as a prognostic risk marker for poor outcomes in sepsis and demonstrate its consistency and robustness across different clinical datasets (Fig. 4).

Subgroup analysis of the association between FIB-4 and in-hospital mortality across clinical and demographic variables

Subgroup analysis based on the MIMIC database demonstrated that the association between the FIB-4 index and in-hospital all-cause mortality in sepsis patients was robust across subgroups defined by different demographic characteristics and comorbidity statuses. The interaction P-values for all stratified variables were > 0.05 exclude age, indicating no significant differences in the effect size among subgroups. In the age stratification, elevated FIB-4 levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of mortality in the < 35 years, 35–65 years, and > 65 years subgroups (HR = 1.85, 1.95, and 1.45, respectively; all P < 0.05), with a more pronounced risk elevation observed in younger patients. For BMI stratification, significant associations were consistently found across the < 18.5, 18.5–30.0, and > 30.0 subgroups, with the greatest risk increase noted in obese patients (HR = 2.21, P < 0.001). Results from comorbidity stratification showed that elevated FIB-4 significantly increased mortality risk regardless of the presence of hypertension (both P < 0.001) or diabetes (both P < 0.001). However, the association did not reach statistical significance in the hepatitis (HR = 1.88, P = 0.051) and cirrhosis (HR = 1.6, P = 0.162) subgroups (Table 7).

Subgroup analysis based on the eICU database revealed that the association between the FIB-4 index and in-hospital all-cause mortality in sepsis patients varied across subgroups with different characteristics, and the interaction P-values of some stratified variables were statistically significant. Specifically, in the age stratification, the interaction P value was 0.203; elevated FIB-4 levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of mortality in the 35–65 years (HR = 2.11, 95% CI: 1.68–2.64, P < 0.001) and > 65 years (HR = 1.75, 95% CI: 1.4–2.17, P < 0.001) subgroups, while the association in the < 35 years subgroup was not significant (HR = 1.2, 95% CI: 0.61–2.38, P = 0.598). For BMI stratification, the interaction P-value was 0.88; elevated FIB-4 significantly increased mortality risk across the < 18.5, 18.5–30.0, and > 30.0 subgroups (HR = 1.98, 2.1, and 2.21, respectively; all P < 0.05) with similar effect sizes. In the comorbidity stratification, the interaction P-value for hypertension was 0.744, and elevated FIB-4 significantly increased mortality risk in both patients with and without hypertension (P < 0.01). For diabetes, the interaction P-value was 0.113; significant associations were observed in both patients with and without diabetes (P < 0.05 for the latter), with a more pronounced risk elevation in diabetic patients. The interaction P-value for hepatitis was 0.885, and elevated FIB-4 was only significantly associated with mortality risk in patients without hepatitis (P < 0.001). A significant interaction was noted in the cirrhosis stratification (P < 0.001), where elevated FIB-4 significantly increased mortality risk in patients without cirrhosis (Table 8).

Sensitivity analysis

To clarify whether the prognostic value of FIB-4 is independent of established liver disease, we conducted sensitivity analyses by excluding all patients with liver diseases from both cohorts. In the MIMIC cohort, which included 10,701 patients after this exclusion (liver disease defined by the ICD-9/ICD-10 codes listed in Table S9), results demonstrated that a higher FIB-4 index (Model 3: HR: 1.584, 95% CI: 1.394–1.800, P < 0.001) remained a significant independent predictor of in-hospital mortality in sepsis patients (Table S11). Similarly, in the eICU cohort, which included 8,443 patients after excluding those with liver disease (liver disease defined by the internal clinical diagnosis strings, codes listed in Table S10), results showed a consistent association (Model 3: HR: 1.892, 95% CI: 1.608–2.227, P < 0.001) (Table S12). These findings indicate that the predictive ability of FIB-4 is robust and suggest that its association with mortality may reflect broader pathophysiological disturbances beyond diagnosed liver conditions.

Building upon the exclusion of patients with known liver disease, we further performed a sensitivity analysis by excluding those with a high probability of undiagnosed Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD), defined as having a body mass index (BMI) greater than 30 kg/m2 in the absence of hypertension and diabetes. In the MIMIC cohort, which comprised 2,980 patients after these exclusions, a higher FIB-4 index (Model 3: HR: 1.275, 95% CI: 1.024–1.588, P = 0.0297) remained a significant independent predictor of mortality (Table S13). In the eICU cohort, which comprised 8,443 patients, results also demonstrated a consistent association (Model 3: HR: 1.829, 95% CI: 1.477–2.265, P < 0.0001) (Table S14).

Furthermore, to minimize the potential confounding influence of cardiogenic factors on FIB-4 interpretation, we excluded patients admitted to cardiac-specialized intensive care units. In the MIMIC cohort, after excluding patients from the Coronary Care Unit (CCU), 12,485 patients remained. Among them, a higher FIB-4 index (Model 3: HR: 1.527, 95% CI: 1.353–1.722, P < 0.0001) persisted as a significant independent predictor (Table S15). In the eICU cohort, after excluding patients from cardiac-focused units (CCU-CTICU, Cardiac ICU, CTICU, and CSICU), 8,330 patients remained. Results similarly showed a consistent association (Model 3: HR: 1.869, 95% CI: 1.577–2.214, P < 0.0001) (Table S16).

Discussion

Sepsis, a systemic inflammatory response syndrome, has an extremely high mortality rate3. Early identification of the death risk in patients with sepsis is crucial for taking timely and effective therapeutic measures. In recent years, more and more studies have focused on the application of biomarkers in sepsis19. In this large-scale, multi-center retrospective study involving over 23,000 sepsis patients from the MIMIC-IV and eICU databases, we demonstrated that the FIB-4 index is a powerful, independent, and robust predictor of in-hospital mortality. A key finding of our revised analysis is that FIB-4 exhibits superior discriminative ability compared to established critical care scoring systems, including SOFA and APACHE II, while offering significantly greater clinical simplicity. To verify the reliability of FIB-4 across different demographics, we performed stratified analyses across three age groups (< 35, 35–65, and > 65 years), which confirmed consistent prognostic value across the adult lifespan. Crucially, to rule out the confounding influence of baseline hepatic pathology, we conducted rigorous sensitivity analyses by systematically excluding patients with acute or chronic liver diseases, cirrhosis, alcohol-related liver disorders, and other hepatic conditions. Furthermore, additional analyses excluding patients with a high probability of undiagnosed metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD/MASH) further corroborated our results. Collectively, these comprehensive analyses underscore the stability and generalizability of the FIB-4 index, suggesting that its association with sepsis mortality reflects systemic physiological derangements rather than pre-existing liver disease.

Several studies have explored the relationship between FIB-4 and the prognosis of various diseases, especially in liver diseases and metabolic syndrome, where it has been widely used5,20,21. While research on sepsis is more limited, preliminary results suggest that FIB-4 may play an important role in the prognosis evaluation of patients with sepsis11,22. The cutoff values for FIB-4 vary across different studies, which may be related to the study design, sample characteristics, and disease types. For example, in certain liver disease studies, FIB-4 cutoff value is often higher (e.g., > 3.25) to distinguish significant liver fibrosis or cirrhosis, with a sensitivity of 51.7% and specificity of 88.3%23. In contrast, this study found that a lower cutoff value (such as 1.25) might be more applicable for identifying high-risk sepsis patients with high mortality. This difference may arise from the following reasons: (1) Differences in disease pathophysiology: Liver disease mainly involves chronic liver damage and fibrosis, while sepsis is a systemic inflammatory response syndrome with a more complex pathophysiology involving multi-organ dysfunction. In sepsis, liver injury may be part of the systemic inflammation rather than the primary cause24. (2) Heterogeneity in study populations: Different studies include patients with varying characteristics. For instance, liver disease studies typically include patients with chronic liver disease who have relatively severe liver damage, whereas patients with sepsis may have relatively milder liver damage but show multi-organ dysfunction that impacts prognosis25. Therefore, lower FIB-4 values may be sufficient to reflect the impact of liver dysfunction on overall prognosis. Furthermore, this study found a nonlinear relationship between FIB-4 and sepsis mortality, suggesting that the impact of FIB-4 on sepsis prognosis is not a simple linear increase. Instead, a threshold effect may exist, and individuals around the cutoff value require personalized assessment of their sepsis death risk. Importantly, this study extended the validation of FIB-4’s predictive value by including the eICU database, which contains a broader, multicenter ICU patient population. In the eICU cohort, an optimal cutoff of 1.25 was identified, and elevated FIB-4 levels remained significantly associated with higher in-hospital mortality, consistent with the findings in the MIMIC-IV cohort. Moreover, the nonlinear association between FIB-4 and in-hospital mortality was also observed in the eICU cohort, further supporting the robustness and generalizability of FIB-4 as a prognostic indicator in sepsis. Similarly, our results support the utility of FIB-4 in sepsis prognosis. However, our study extends these findings through a rigorous multi-center design that overcomes the limitations of smaller, single-center cohorts. By utilizing massive datasets (MIMIC-IV and eICU) and applying strict exclusion criteria, we effectively minimized selection bias and potential confounding from anesthetic agents or pre-existing hepatic conditions. This ensures that the predictive value of FIB-4 is driven by sepsis-related pathophysiology rather than external factors, reinforcing its reliability as a generalizable clinical tool.

From a biological perspective, there may be complex associations between FIB-4, liver fibrosis, inflammation, and immune function23. Sepsis is a systemic inflammatory response, and the liver is an important immune organ. Sepsis may exacerbate systemic inflammation and organ dysfunction by affecting the liver’s metabolic and detoxification functions, thus increasing the death risk from sepsis26. Moreover, components of FIB-4, such as transaminases and platelet count, are closely related to inflammation and coagulation function21. Mediation analysis results showed that indicators such as lactate and coagulation markers (e.g., PT, PTT) mediate the relationship between FIB-4 and mortality, reflecting the interaction between liver dysfunction, systemic inflammation, and coagulation dysfunction in patients with sepsis27. Lactate levels, an important biomarker in sepsis, reflect the patient’s hypoxic state and tissue perfusion28–30, which may interact with elevated FIB-4 levels, further increasing the death risk11. These indicators play an important role in the pathophysiology of sepsis, emphasizing the potential mechanisms of FIB-4 in assessing the prognosis of patients with sepsis.

There were several limitations in this study. Firstly, the data for this study were sourced from the MIMIC-Ⅳ database, which primarily contains data from ICU patients12, and may not fully represent all sepsis patient populations, thus limiting the generalizability of the results. Secondly, although this study minimized confounding bias through multivariable adjustment, unmeasured confounders cannot be entirely ruled out due to its retrospective design. Thirdly, the cross-sectional design of our study limited our ability to establish causality between FIB-4 levels and in-hospital mortality in sepsis. Conducting long-term randomized controlled trials would provide more definitive evidence for establishing a causal relationship between FIB-4 levels and in-hospital mortality in sepsis. Finally, while we excluded patients with known liver diseases, we did not specifically exclude those with gut ischemia. Although our multivariable models adjusted for serum lactate—a pivotal metabolic marker of ischemia—to mitigate potential confounding, the distinct pathophysiology of gut ischemia may still exert a residual influence on the FIB-4 index. Future prospective studies focusing on specific sepsis etiologies are warranted to further validate and refine these findings. Nonetheless, the inclusion of a large, multicenter eICU cohort strengthens the external validity of our findings and supports future prospective studies in varied clinical populations.

Conclusion

In summary, this study confirmed a significant positive correlation between FIB-4 and in-hospital mortality in patients with sepsis through large-scale retrospective data analysis. Lactate and AST partially mediated the relationship between FIB-4 and in-hospital mortality. Therefore, FIB-4 can be an effective non-invasive biomarker to predict in-hospital mortality risk in patients, with sepsis providing a potential tool for clinical risk assessment.
