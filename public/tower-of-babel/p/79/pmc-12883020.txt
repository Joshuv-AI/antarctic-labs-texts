Mixed-phenotype acute leukemia with trilineage differentiation presenting as a non-leukemic mediastinal tumor
Authors: Satoshi Ichikawa, Maya Yamato, Shusuke Hatta, Koichi Onodera, Noriko Fukuhara, Ryo Ichinohasama, Hideo Harigae
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mixed-phenotype acute leukemia (MPAL) is an uncommon hematological malignancy, and cases exhibiting trilineage differentiation are extraordinarily rare. Here, we report the case of a 63-year-old male patient who presented with severe dyspnea secondary to a massive anterior mediastinal mass and pleural effusion, in the absence of detectable abnormal cells in the peripheral blood. Immunophenotypic analysis of pleural fluid and tumor biopsy revealed an unprecedented trilineage phenotype with concurrent expression of T-lymphoid (cytoplasmic CD3+ and CD7+), B-lymphoid (CD19+ and CD22+), myeloid (cytoplasmic MPO+ and CD33+) markers, along with stem cell (CD34+ and HLA-DR+), and lymphoblastic (TdT+) markers. B-lineage commitment was further confirmed by immunoglobulin heavy chain gene rearrangement. These findings established a diagnosis of T/B/myeloid triphenotypic MPAL presenting as an aleukemic disease with minimal bone marrow infiltration (<1%). Initial treatment with acute myeloid leukemia-directed induction therapy (daunorubicin plus cytarabine) proved refractory. However, the hyperCVAD regimen, which is an acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL)-directed approach, achieved a complete metabolic response. Subsequent consolidation with cord blood transplantation resulted in durable remission lasting 2 years before a late relapse, which occurred in the mediastinal region. This is the first reported case of non-leukemic, triphenotypic MPAL presenting as a primary mediastinal tumor, highlighting the diagnostic complexity and therapeutic challenges associated with this rare malignancy. Based on our experience, intensive ALL-directed chemotherapy followed by allogeneic stem cell transplantation may offer an effective therapeutic strategy for this exceptionally rare clinical entity.

INTRODUCTION

Mixed-phenotype acute leukemia (MPAL) is a rare and diagnostically complex subtype of acute leukemia, characterized by the co-expression of lineage-defining markers from two or more hematopoietic lineages.1 It accounts for approximately 1–5% of all acute leukemia cases1–3 and is noted for its clinical aggressiveness and generally poor prognosis compared with standard acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL).4 MPAL is immunophenotypically heterogeneous, encompassing cases with a single blast population expressing markers of multiple lineages (biphenotypic) and those with two or more distinct blast populations, each with a single lineage (bilineal).4

Although MPAL usually presents in the leukemic phase, which is characterized by bone marrow and peripheral blood involvement, it rarely manifests as an isolated extramedullary disease—typically known as a myeloid sarcoma—without a definite leukemic component.5,6 This presentation poses a considerable diagnostic challenge, particularly when the mediastinum is involved. T-cell lymphoblastic lymphoma (LBL) is an archetypal hematopoietic malignancy presenting as an anterior mediastinal mass, particularly in adolescents and young adults.7 This diagnostic challenge is compounded by the fact that T-cell LBL, particularly the early T-cell precursor (ETP) subtype, can aberrantly express myeloid-associated antigens,8 thereby producing an immunophenotypic profile indistinguishable from that of T/myeloid MPAL.

MPAL cases that involve all three lineages (triphenotypic: T, B, and myeloid) are exceptionally rare, accounting for approximately 2% of all MPAL diagnoses.9 Here, we describe, to the best of our knowledge, the first reported case of a non-leukemic, triphenotypic (T/B/myeloid) MPAL presenting as a mediastinal mass in an older adult male. We outline the comprehensive diagnostic approach required to establish a definitive diagnosis and discuss the therapeutic implications of this highly unusual clinical presentation.

CASE REPORT

A 63-year-old male patient with no particular medical history presented to another hospital with a 2-week history of dyspnea. Chest X-ray and computed tomography (CT) revealed a massive right pleural fluid and an anterior mediastinal mass expanding into the pericardium (Figure 1a, b; maximal tumor diameter: 116 mm). Positron emission tomography (PET) revealed a positive uptake of fluorodeoxyglucose within the mediastinal mass (Figure 1c; maximum standardized uptake value: 8.0). Hematological malignancy was suspected based on pleural fluid cytology, and the patient was subsequently referred to our department. Upon admission, the patient could not lie down due to severe dyspnea and cough, without fever, night sweats, or weight loss. Although his vital signs were stable, tachypnea and hypoxemia were observed. A complete blood count was within the normal range, and no abnormal cells were detected. Serum biochemistry revealed slightly elevated levels of lactic dehydrogenase (270 IU/L), C-reactive protein (1.38 mg/dL), soluble IL-2 receptor (1,339 U/mL), and WT1 mRNA (5,600 copies/µgRNA). Examination of the pleural fluid revealed a markedly elevated cell count (5,650/µL), approximately 40% of which consisted of large abnormal lymphoid cells, suggestive of hematological malignancy. Flow cytometry (FCM) analysis revealed that they were CD45dim+, CD2–, sCD3–, cyCD3+, CD4–, CD5dim+, CD7+, CD8–, CD10–, CD19dim+, CD20–, CD22dim+, cyCD79adim+, sIgκ–, sIgλ–, CD13dim+, CD33+, cyMPO+, CD34+, HLA-DR+, and TdT+ (Figure 2a). Pathological examination of a needle biopsy sample from the mediastinal tumor indicated a monomorphic proliferation of medium- to large-sized abnormal lymphoid cells, characterized by nuclear swelling and atypia (Figure 2b–h). In the pleural fluid analysis, Southern blotting revealed immunoglobulin heavy chain (IgH) JH gene rearrangement (Figure 2i), while T-cell receptor Cβ1 gene rearrangement was inconclusive (Figure 2j). G-band analysis revealed a complex polyploid karyotype in a single cell (Figure 2k), and 19 other polyploid cells exhibiting similar chromosomal abnormalities were observed. However, they could not be analyzed further because good banding patterns were not obtained. FCM of bone marrow cells indicated minimal infiltration (approximately 1% of all nucleated cells) of abnormal cells with the same phenotype. Based on these findings, we postulated a diagnosis of MPAL with a T/B/myeloid lineage, manifesting as mediastinal LBL.

Figure 3 shows the clinical course of treatment. The patient initially received induction therapy for AML (daunorubicin + cytarabine: daunorubicin 50 mg/m2 on days 1–5 and cytarabine 100 mg/m2 on days 1–7) based on the strong expression of cytoplasmic MPO on FCM. Although this treatment resulted in a partial relief of symptoms, such as dyspnea and cough, follow-up CT scan after 1 month revealed no reduction in tumor size. The patient was subsequently treated with a hyperCVAD regimen for ALL (comprising cyclophosphamide 300 mg/m2 every 12 hr on days 1–3, vincristine 2 mg/body on days 4 and 11, doxorubicin 50 mg/m2 on day 4, and dexamethasone 40 mg/body on days 1–4 and 11–14), considering the lymphoblastic phenotypes. This treatment resulted in a marked improvement in symptoms, with a reduction in pleural effusion and mediastinal mass. The patient subsequently received a high-dose cytarabine regimen (1 g/m2 every 12 hr on days 1, 3, and 5), which resulted in a complete metabolic response as revealed by PET/CT imaging (Figure 4a). FCM revealed an absence of abnormal cell populations in the bone marrow.

Given the typically poor prognosis of MPAL, we planned to proceed with allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) as a curative treatment strategy. Cord blood was selected as the donor source since human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-matched sibling or non-sibling donors were unavailable. An infusion of 1.2 × 105/kg CD34+ cells was performed following a conditioning regimen that comprised fludarabine (30 mg/m2 for 5 days), melphalan (40 mg/m2 for 2 days), and total body irradiation (4 Gy in 2 fractions). The HLA antigens and alleles of the transplanted cord blood were mismatched at three of the eight loci. Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis consisted of tacrolimus and short-term methotrexate (10 mg/m2 on day 1 and 6 mg/m2 on days 3 and 6). Regimen-related toxicities, including febrile neutropenia, were neither severe nor uncontrollable. Neutrophil engraftment was achieved on day 16, and bone marrow aspiration on day 28 confirmed complete donor chimerism. No apparent signs of acute GVHD were observed, and the patient was discharged in good health on day 50. Moreover, no apparent signs of chronic GVHD were noted, and tacrolimus was discontinued on day 266. A complete metabolic response was sustained without additional therapy (Figure 4b). However, asymptomatic relapsed lesions in the pericardium, myocardium, and mediastinum were confirmed approximately 2 years post-transplantation with PET and CT imaging. A bone marrow examination revealed no leukemic cell infiltration. Although the relapse was unresponsive to the hyperCVAD regimen, radiotherapy resulted in a partial response.

DISCUSSION

MPAL is a rare type of acute leukemia in which blast cells express markers from more than one hematopoietic lineage. Its diagnosis requires identifying specific markers of myeloid, T-lymphoid, and B-lymphoid lineages, the updated criteria of which have recently been documented in the 5th edition of the World Health Organization Classification of Haematolymphoid Tumours (WHO-HAEM5).10 In the present case, FCM revealed the strong cytoplasmic expressions of CD3 and MPO, confirming the T-lymphoid and myeloid lineage assignments based on the WHO-HAEM5 criteria. A positive IgH gene rearrangement does not definitively indicate commitment to the B-cell lineage in acute leukemias, including MPAL.11–13 However, it is considered a strong indication of commitment in this case when accompanied by the expression of B-lymphoid lineage markers, such as CD19, CD22, and cytoplasmic CD79a. The expressions of CD34 and HLA-DR further indicated the immature nature of these neoplastic cells, which aligns with acute leukemia originating from a multipotent progenitor. Based on these findings, the diagnosis of MPAL with trilineage differentiation was confirmed, a presentation that is considerably rarer than bilineage MPAL.14

MPAL also typically presents with marked bone marrow infiltration and peripheral blood leukocytosis. A non-leukemic presentation of MPAL, as observed in this case, is exceedingly rare, accounting for only a small fraction of MPAL cases.5,15,16 Consequently, the combination of a non-leukemic presentation with a mediastinal mass and trilineage phenotype singles out this case as being particularly unusual. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of MPAL presenting as a primary mediastinal tumor with a clear trilineage (T/B/myeloid) immunophenotype.

T-cell LBL is considered one of the most plausible differential diagnoses in this case, as 80–90% of its cases present with a mediastinal mass.17 However, notable lineage ambiguities within T-cell LBL cases typically blur the boundaries with other categories of malignancies. For example, although 30–40% of T-cell LBL cases exhibit aberrant myeloid antigen expression (CD13, CD33, and CD117), they do not fulfill the MPAL criteria.18 These cases are referred to as ETP-ALL/LBL, in which myeloid and stem cell markers (e.g., CD13, CD33, CD34, and CD117) are co-expressed, along with an absence or weak expression of CD1a, CD8, and CD5.8 Although CD19 and CD79a are considered lineage-specific markers for B cells, their aberrant expression has been observed in some T-cell ALL/LBL cases.19–21 IgH rearrangement has been observed in some T-cell ALL/LBL cases.11 These findings indicate the multilineage differentiation potential of T-cell ALL/LBL. Although this case can be considered a T-cell LBL with multilineage differentiation, no similar instances have been previously reported in the literature.

MPAL has a poorer prognosis than standard-risk AML or ALL,22 and remains one of the most challenging acute leukemias to treat. Its rarity, genetic heterogeneity, and inherent lineage plasticity complicate therapeutic strategies. Although the optimal induction regimen for MPAL remains controversial, ALL-directed induction therapy appears to be the preferred approach based on the findings from several retrospective studies. The findings of a large meta-analysis of 1,300 patients revealed that ALL-directed regimens contributed to significantly higher complete remission rates and lower mortality than AML-directed regimens.23 Similarly, pediatric data from the iBFM-AMBI2012 study revealed a 5-year event-free survival of 80% ± 4% with ALL-directed therapy, compared with 36% ± 7.2% achieved with AML-directed or combined treatments.24 Among adult patients, those receiving ALL-directed therapy achieved a 15-mon survival of 56%, compared with 32% for those receiving AML-directed therapy.25 The initial lack of a response to AML-directed therapy in the patient described herein, and the subsequent complete remission in response to an ALL-directed regimen, align with these findings.

Allo-HSCT is an effective treatment strategy for MPAL that provides a promising option for cure, particularly in cases where remission is achieved. It is considered the most reliable therapeutic strategy for refractory pediatric acute leukemia. The findings of a study from the Center for International Blood and Marrow Transplant Research involving 95 patients revealed an encouraging 3-year overall survival (OS) and leukemia-free survival (LFS) rate of 67% and 56%, respectively, when using this approach.26 A further study of 519 adult patients with MPAL in initial complete remission reported 3-year OS and LFS rates of 56.3% and 46.5%, respectively.27 Another study reported a 5-year OS rate of 54% in 50 adult patients who underwent transplantation.28 The disease status at the time of transplantation is a key factor that influences the prognosis of patients with MPAL, with significantly better outcomes observed when they are in complete remission. A Japanese study revealed a 5-year OS rate of 71.8% for patients in remission, compared with 0% for those who are not in remission.29 Negativity in measurable residual disease (MRD) at the time of transplantation has been associated with more favorable OS and disease-free survival rates. A study reported a 5-year post-allo-HSCT OS of 75.8% in MRD-negative patients compared with 45.2% in MRD-positive patients.28 MPAL treatment outcomes are speculated to be associated with a beneficial graft-versus-leukemia effect, since chronic GVHD has been identified as a favorable prognostic factor for progression-free survival and OS.27 For the patient described herein, cord blood transplantation resulted in durable disease remission, indicating that this therapeutic approach is a viable alternative when a matched related or unrelated donor is unavailable. A subsequent late relapse occurred despite the promising initial response in this case. This late relapse is likely attributable to the absence of chronic GVHD, potentially reflecting the negative aspect of an insufficient graft-versus-leukemia effect. Most of the relevant studies have been based on retrospective analyses with limited patient cohorts, given the rarity of this disease. Consequently, further research is necessary to standardize treatment protocols and optimize transplantation strategies.

In conclusion, we report an exceptionally rare case of MPAL with trilineage antigen expression that presented as a non-leukemic mediastinal tumor. This case highlights the diverse and perplexing clinical presentation of MPAL. The favorable outcomes achieved with intensive ALL-directed chemotherapy followed by consolidative cord blood transplantation suggest that this approach can be effective even for high-risk and unusual presentations of MPAL. Further collaborative studies will be essential to gain a better understanding of the biology of this rare disease and establish standardized, risk-adapted therapeutic strategies.
