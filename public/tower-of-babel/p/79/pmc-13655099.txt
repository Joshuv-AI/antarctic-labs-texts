ImmuGT-ConRes: a consistency learning residual network for predicting pan-cancer immunotherapy response from gene expression data
Authors: Keru Ma, Hao Wang, Genshen Mo, Hao Li, Xinyue Zhang, Meihong Yan, Jialin Wang, Junchao Shao, Haonan Xie, Yuze Huang, Huiying Li, Dalin Li, Peng Han, Shenghan Lou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmunotherapy is leading the transformation in cancer treatment, but its response rate remains low, and there is a scarcity of immune targets that can produce lasting effects. Multi-omics data is widely used in immunotherapy. However, traditional analysis methods often struggle to uncover hidden correlations within the data, limiting the exploration of potential immunotherapy targets. Here, we pooled immunotherapy data from over 40 cohorts and developed ImmuGT-ConRes (Genomic Image Transformation with Consistency Learning and Residual Networks). ImmuGT-ConRes uses a contrastive learning approach, incorporating a dual-branch data augmentation strategy and a multi-scale stride convolution structure. By integrating residual networks and attention mechanisms, ImmuGT-ConRes adapts to multi-scale gene feature capture, reduces information loss between shallow details and deep semantics, and enhances the model’s robustness to input perturbations. We demonstrated that ImmuGT-ConRes exhibited superior predictive performance and provided interpretability by ranking gene weights based on the attention mechanism, providing an effective approach for immunotherapy target mining. These results suggest that ImmuGT-ConRes offers a promising framework for immunotherapy target discovery and merits further investigation.Subject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Immunology

Introduction

Immunotherapy has promoted survival in patients with various cancers, marking a transformative change in cancer treatment1,2. However, response rates to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting existing antigens remain as low as 6–30%, with durable responses limited to a minority of patients3,4. The clinical efficacy of ICI therapy depends heavily on specific antigen expression, and the scarcity and heterogeneity of targetable antigens hinders effective targeting in most patients, substantially limiting the utility of ICIs5. Consequently, developing novel, broad-spectrum targets through innovative techniques has emerged as a key strategy to overcome these efficacy limitations6.

Deep mining of cancer genomic data may facilitate identifying and validating such targets. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) has revolutionized cancer research by providing an unprecedented foundation for systematic target exploration7. However, NGS data are typically presented or analyzed as tabular one-dimensional feature vectors8. While these vectors are common in traditional statistical analysis, they rely on prior knowledge and preset mathematical assumptions, often yielding suboptimal results for complex data. Furthermore, in conventional genomic analyses, characteristic dimensions frequently exceed sample sizes, which increases the risk of accidental association. Even with multiplicity correction, few significant results are retained, potentially overlooking several weak and real associations.

Deep learning (DL) offers a powerful paradigm for analyzing high-dimensional biological data. For instance, residual networks preserve key information during deep feature learning, enabling better capture of global gene expression patterns while minimizing noise9. Recent studies have converted non-image multi-omics data into image-like representations, allowing convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to detect complex feature interactions that elude conventional methods10–15. Nonetheless, existing image-based omics approaches have limitations, including heavy reliance on layout design, poor scalability, trade-offs between network depth and performance, and suboptimal optimization. These limitations can distort biological representations, undermine predictive robustness, and waste computational resources10–15. Notably, such methods remain underexplored in cancer immunotherapy. In pan-cancer studies, high-throughput transcriptomic data are especially prone to systematic variations from differences in sampling protocols, sequencing platforms, and cohort compositions16. Although batch-effect correction during quality control mitigates technical noise, overcorrection risks obscuring true biological heterogeneity. Therefore, effective modeling strategies need to standardize raw gene expression matrices while preserving global gene spatial organization and establishing stable mappings between fixed gene indices and image pixels for consistent cross-dataset representations.

Reportedly, tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) and immune cell abundance identifier (ImmuCellAI) are widely used computational tools for predicting immunotherapy response, primarily in melanoma and lung cancer17,18. Although extended to other cancer types, their predictive performance in pan-cancer settings lacks systematic validation using large-scale clinical immunotherapy cohorts. Notably, identifying emerging immunotherapy targets often depends on prior statistical information and cancer-specific models19, followed by experimental validation to boost translational relevance20,21. However, simulated response estimates from these approaches may fail to capture the full complexity of real-world immunotherapy outcomes, limiting their utility for robust target discovery. This study aimed to curate a large pan-cancer immunotherapy cohort collection and develop a DL framework, termed genomic image transformation with consistency learning and residual networks (ImmuGT-ConRes), to address the aforementioned challenges and predict pan-cancer clinical immunotherapy responses. This model employed convolutional architectures and discriminative signal extraction from high-dimensional transcriptomic data to generate compact feature representations and improve prediction accuracy.

In ImmuGT-ConRes, a dual-branch data augmentation strategy was implemented based on contrastive learning, combining weak and strong augmentations with targeted operations to accelerate convergence and enhance training stability. Using the Mean Teacher framework, we enforced consistency between student and teacher model outputs via a dedicated consistency loss, enhancing robustness to input perturbations. Its core is a semi-supervised learning algorithm based on consistency regularization. It generates pseudo-labels by constructing a stable teacher model through exponential moving average (EMA) of the student model weights, thereby guiding the student model to maintain prediction consistency under perturbation. Unlike the method reported by Sokač et al., which fixes gene spatial positions, GENIUS freezes its autoencoder, and non-dynamic optimization of the input image in downstream tasks potentially leads to the loss of key information14. In contrast, ImmuGT-ConRes employs multi-level feature enhancement with consistency constraints and a temporal dynamic encoder to improve robustness and generalization. The results of this study show that ImmuGT-ConRes effectively predicts pan-cancer immunotherapy responses and identifies targets significantly associated with response efficacy. The proposed method outperforms TIDE and ImmuCellAI in prediction performance. Building on existing multi-omics research, where machine learning often uncovers viable targets, we further evaluated the predictive performance of machine learning models on existing data22,23. ImmuGT-ConRes outperformed the predictive ability of common machine learning models, highlighting its application value and providing a reference for immunotherapy target exploration.

Results

ImmuGT-ConRes can accurately predict the efficacy of immunotherapy

We applied image transformation to RNA-seq data and generated attention heatmaps (Fig. 1B). Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection dimensionality reduction confirmed strong discrimination between response and non-response groups (Fig. 1F, G). In the training and test sets, ImmuGT-ConRes achieved excellent performance, with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.932 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.916–0.949) and 0.914 (95% CI: 0.886–0.943), respectively (Fig. 1H, I). Across the full cohort, it delivered superior performance, with an AUC of 0.926 (95% CI: 0.916–0.949), precision 0.880, recall 0.876, and F1 score 0.918 (Fig. 2K). The nomogram based on ImmuGT-ConRes showed stable predictive performance based on the calibration curve (Fig. 2F, G). Regarding the clinical value of the model, compared with TIDE and ImmuCellAI17,18, ImmuGT-ConRes excelled in the full cohort, showing superior AUC than that of the control group (AUC: 0.926 [95% CI: 0.912–0.941] versus 0.507 [95% CI: 0.480–0.537] and 0.504 [95% CI: 0.477–0.531]) (Fig. 2C) and melanoma tissue cohort (AUC: 0.926 [95% CI: 0.905–0.947] versus 0.522 [95% CI: 0.476–0.568] and 0.558 [95% CI: 0.514–0.603]) (Fig. 2D). However, it did not predict ImmuCellAI-generated responses (AUC: 0.526 [95% CI: 0.500–0.553) (Fig. 2E). These results show that ImmuGT-ConRes accurately identifies the response status of patients undergoing immunotherapy. Notably, the AUCs for random forest (RF), multilayer perceptron (MLP), and logistic regression were 0.703 (95% CI: 0.658–0.749), 0.730 (95% CI: 0.655–0.805), and 0.688 (95% CI: 0.641–0.734) (Supplementary Fig. 2), which were also lower than that of ImmuGT-ConRes.

Attention mechanism weights for response effectiveness

We ranked all genes based on the weight results and used a leave-one-out ablation strategy to assess key gene importance, progressively removing top-weighted genes and monitoring ImmuGT-ConRes performance. Excluding the top nine genes (MOBAT7, MROH1, SBK2, TMEM55B, MYCBP2, PCBP2, TKTL1, TARM1, and OR2H1; Supplementary Table 2), the reduced accuracy of ImmuGT-ConRes dropped from 0.851 to 0.678 and then stabilized (Fig. 2A). Notably, the AUC of ImmuGT-ConRes for predicting response decreased from 0.926 (95% CI: 0.912–0.941) to 0.595 (95% CI: 0.568–0.623). In melanoma and peripheral blood sets, prediction efficiency decreased similarly: melanoma, from 0.926 (95% CI: 0.905–0.947) to 0.560 (95% CI: 0.516–0.605); blood, from 0.624 (95% CI: 0.562–0.687) to 0.505 (95% CI: 0.445–0.565) (Fig. 2K). RCS further revealed the significant nonlinear correlation of ImmuGT-ConRes with the probability of immune response (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2I), which lost significance after removing the top nine genes (P = 0.117; Fig. 2J). These results confirm the pivotal role of these nine genes in model predictions.

Response or non-response weighting mechanism

We identified the top 500 weighted genes from response and non-response groups, revealing 187 overlapping genes (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Table 4). To assess their coordinated expression at the sample level, ssGSEA scores were computed, which measure relative enrichment in individual transcriptomic profiles. In pan-cancer RNA-seq data, higher ssGSEA scores for this 187-gene set strongly correlated with immune checkpoint expression, as follows: PD-1 (r² = 0.253, P < 0.001), PD-L1 (r² = 0.221, P < 0.001), and CTLA-4 (r² = 0.222, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3B). These associations suggest preferential enrichment in immune checkpoint-active transcriptional context. Moreover, the STRING database analysis confirmed multiple interactions, such as high-confidence binding scores of human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-DQA1, HLA-DQB1, killer immunoglobulin-like receptor, two domains, long cytoplasmic tail (KIR2DL)3, and KIR2DL4 (Fig. 3C).

Biological explanation of weight genes

The aforementioned top nine weighted genes variably influenced immune infiltration per ImmuCellAI. For instance, MROH1 positively correlated with B and T cells but negatively with macrophages and monocytes (Fig. 4A), whereas OR2H1 showed no significant correlation with B or T cells. All nine genes associated with PD-1/PD-L1/CTLA-4 to varying degrees (Fig. 4B). Hallmark enrichment analysis highlighted functional heterogeneity of these genes (Fig. 4C), while GO/KEGG enrichment highlighted ATP synthesis, mRNA processing, and fatty acid metabolism (Supplementary Table 3). These results indicate that the top nine genes play an important role in immune response (Fig. 4D–G).To further interpret the biological relevance of the overlapping genes, the relationship between ssGSEA enrichment scores and immune cell infiltration signatures was examined. The ssGSEA scores positively correlated with adaptive immune cell infiltration, indicating an immune-inflamed state (Fig. 3D). Conversely, negative correlations emerged with natural killer (NK) cells (r² = −0.32, P = 0.007) and neutrophils (r² = −0.41, P = 0.002), suggesting specialized immune contexture patterns and tumor microenvironment dynamics. Consistently, Hallmark enrichment showed overrepresentation of ssGSEA signal-associated genes in multiple immune/inflammatory pathways (e.g., inflammatory response, interleukin [IL]-6/Janus kinase [JAK]/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 [STAT3], tumor necrosis factor α via nuclear factor-κB), along with stress/proliferation-associated pathways such as p53 and MYC targets (FDR P < 0.05; Fig. 3E). GO/KEGG analyses further implicated these genes in immune effector functions, including NK cell- cytotoxicity, lymphocyte-mediated immunity, and immune receptor activity (Fig. 3F–I; Supplementary Table 5). Together, these results indicate that these genes reflect a coordinated immune-related transcriptional program associated with immune checkpoint activity and tumor immune microenvironment remodeling. The present results are consistent with the previously reported theoretical basis. For example, Martin et al. reported OR2H1 as an epithelial tumor CAR-T target24 and TKTL1 as a renal cancer immunotherapy marker25. MBOAT7, TARM1, and MYCBP2 have also been implicated in immune regulation26–28, and the MYC and IL-6/JAK/STAT3 pathways are widely associated with immunotherapy29,30.

Model component ablation experiment

We performed ablation experiments on ImmuGT-ConRes components. The full-component model exhibited superior predictive performance in the test set (Supplementary Table 6). Retaining only the CNN component reduced the model’s accuracy to 0.843, with corresponding declines in accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score.

Discussion

In this study, we aggregated transcriptome data from over 40 immunotherapy datasets to predict response. Applying the Mean Teacher consistency-learning framework to these genomic profiles, we developed ImmuGT-ConRes, integrating consistency learning, strided convolutions, and residual learning frameworks. The model achieved robust predictive performance on training (AUC: 0.932, 95% CI: 0.916–0.949) and test sets (AUC: 0.914, 95% CI: 0.886–0.943). Attention mechanisms identified key weighted genes, enabling biological interpretability and traceability to genomic features, with detailed biological explanations significantly enhancing the credibility of clinical translation. Ablation of attention mechanisms reduced AUC from 0.926 to 0.595, and this trend was validated in melanoma tissue and peripheral blood sets. Overall, these results indicate that ImmuGT-ConRes excels at capturing multi-scale feature interactions from tabular genomic data and provide a reference for new artificial intelligence-driven immunotherapy prediction tools.

Traditional statistics have identified key cancer immunotherapy targets31,32, but they rely on prior knowledge and assumptions to reduce model complexity, struggling with nonlinear, high-dimensional data. Emerging targets often stem from single-cancer statistical models19, requiring validation across cancer types20,21 to enhance their clinical value. Comparatively, high-throughput data are affected by systematic variations in the sampling process16, which are often addressed by handling batch effects in quality control to reduce systematic errors and obscure confounding biological differences. However, overcorrection can mask true biological signals. ImmuGT-ConRes standardizes original expression matrices while preserving gene spatial structure via fixed index-to-pixel mapping, ensuring stable training convergence during training, and thereby avoiding potential biological differences between different samples. Unlike the method used by Sokazei et al. to fix the spatial location of genes, GENIUS froze its autoencoder, which risks losing dynamic features in downstream tasks, potentially leading to the loss of key information14. In contrast, ImmuGT-ConRes employs a multi-level enhancement strategy, feature consistency constraints, and a temporal dynamic encoder for superior robustness and generalization. The comparison with traditional ML models further showed that ImmuGT-ConRes exhibited superior prediction. This further enhances the application value of ImmuGT-ConRes.

Among the existing strategies for converting tabular data to images, including DeepInsight, DeepFeature, and Genome-Wide Association Study-selected single nucleotide variant11,13,15, CNN is mainly used to extract spatial features while using varied layouts to retain gene correlations or the topological structure of functional modules. However, challenges such as complex dimensionality reduction algorithms, poor layout stability, and static coding destabilize model training, making it hard to converge when downstream CNN designs fail to accommodate these issues. Furthermore, shallow CNNs struggle to capture deep, complex spatial structure features, further limiting the model’s performance. Bavykina et al. proposed a hybrid network architecture with CNN and RF for gene visual transformation12, but the complexity of this architecture makes convergence difficult during training, increases reliance on input layout, lengthens the entire process, and restricts end-to-end optimization. In contrast, ImmuGT-ConRes offers various advantages: (1) standardized structure design avoiding layout instability and supporting batch training; (2) a hierarchical data augmentation strategy to boost robustness against perturbations and missing data, minimizing static layout reliance; (3) integration of strided convolutions and residual blocks for scalable deep architectures that capture multi-scale features, expand receptive fields, enable global modeling, and stabilize training on complex genomic patterns; (4) fully end-to-end pipeline, eliminating information loss and training bottlenecks in traditional multi-stage processes.

Herein, we incorporated channel attention (e.g., Squeeze-and-Excitation module) to weigh semantic features dynamically, as not all channels contribute equally to treatment response prediction. Global pooling compresses spatial information, followed by a lightweight network that assigns adaptive weights, thereby amplifying relevant features and suppressing irrelevant noise. This attention operates on intermediate-layer hidden channels, rather than on the original input pixels. Channel weights indirectly gauge gene module importance, as the input image comprises ordered genes and convolution kernels learn spatial/functional adjacencies between genes during training. High-weight channels often align with response-linked gene subnetworks or pathways. Furthermore, channel–gene association analysis was performed to validate this connection, and the correlation between the characteristic responses of high-attention channels and the original gene expression was significantly enriched in treatment responses. These findings indicate that, although the attention mechanism is applied to the hidden layer, the channel importance it learns essentially reflects the discriminative nature of the underlying gene expression patterns.

Residual blocks promote stable information flow via skip connections, mitigating vanishing gradients in deep networks and enabling the network to learn useful features more effectively. While attention mechanisms and residual blocks enhance key feature capture, ImmuGT-ConRes remains a black-box model with limited internal decision-making transparency. Nevertheless, the proposed model still identifies significant genes contributing to immunotherapy, based on their weights. Prior evidence links immunotherapy to some genes (such as OR2H1 and TKTL1)24,25, with these attention-weighted genes being implicated in immune microenvironment regulation and enriched pathways such as the MYC and IL-6/JAK/STAT3 pathways; these pathways have also been associated with response efficacy of immunotherapy29,30. These results of ImmuGT-ConRes provide a biological theoretical basis for the discovery of immunotherapy targets.

This study has some strengths. First, the integrated immunotherapy-related data were comprehensively analyzed, with findings generalizable across multiple cancer types. Second, in contrast to studies on different gene image transformations11,12,14, independent external tests were performed in melanoma, achieving robust predictive performance and demonstrating the strong generalization ability of ImmuGT-ConRes. Nonetheless, some limitations persist. First, further validation and exploration via more extensive clinical trials and basic research are required for the present findings. Second, ImmuGT-ConRes is primarily applied to transcriptomics, and its utility in proteomics, metabolomics, and other omics fields needs to be further explored.

In conclusion, ImmuGT-ConRes is a predictive framework integrating genomic visualization, DL, and attention mechanisms, showing potential for pan-cancer immunotherapy response prediction. Altogether, these results provide novel insights into target discovery and personalized cancer treatment strategies.

Methods

Research process

Figure 1 illustrates the complete workflow. Ethical approval was not required for the current study as all data were derived from publicly published studies. To predict ICI responses and identify beneficial targets, we developed a consistency learning-based approach with dual-branch data augmentation. First, high-dimensional gene expression profiles were structured into two-dimensional image representations, enabling CNN processing of transcriptome data. CNNs then extracted multi-level local and global patterns via layered convolutions, yielding high-dimensional feature maps with multiple channels. These channels represent learned abstract semantic features rather than raw genes or pixels, accelerating convergence and enhancing training stability (Fig. 1B–E). In the data augmentation module, we applied an asymmetric dual-branch augmentation strategy with weak and strong transformations. The feature extraction module integrated ResNet and attention mechanisms with the Mean Teacher framework for semi-supervised learning. Residual blocks mitigated vanishing gradients in deep networks through skip connections, preserving shallow features. Finally, the downstream classification module fed dual-encoder features through a fully connected layer for final predictions.

Data source

Supplementary Table 1 presents detailed information on all ICI datasets, sourced from public repositories and encompassing 2801 patients with cancer treated with immunotherapy. Responses were assessed per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (version 1.1)33 and categorized as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD). We binarized outcomes as response (CR/PR) or non-response (SD/PD) for consistent pan-cancer analysis, with annotations harmonized across datasets and validated by original institutions; all grouping information was derived from the immunotherapy results of each study (Supplementary Table 1). RNA-seq data from anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)/cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) therapies were collected from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA; https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov), Gene Expression Omnibus (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), and prior studies. The cohort spanned tumors of the head/neck, respiratory, skin, digestive, urinary, endocrine, blood, and nervous systems, including glioblastoma, oral, head/neck, lung, breast, esophageal, gastric, colorectal, renal, bladder, ovarian cancers, lymphoma, and others. For external validation, we used melanoma tissue RNA-seq and peripheral blood RNA-seq from GSE94872 to enhance the model’s generalization ability. All analyses employed a fixed random seed of 42.

Data processing

We randomly split the original dataset into training (80%, n = 1135) and test (20%, n = 284) sets using a stratified sampling strategy to ensure consistency in the distribution ratios of each category remains between sets. This approach ensures consistency of sample proportions of each category (“response” and “non-response”) in both sets with the original data set, thereby effectively avoiding random division-induced category distribution deviations and improving the model’s evaluation stability and generalization ability. Additionally, we used melanoma tumor tissue data (n = 662) and peripheral blood data (n = 720) for external testing.

In the preprocessing of the original dataset, sequence alignment was performed, with those with inconsistent lengths being dimension-unified using zero-padding. Subsequently, the obtained numerical features were normalized to eliminate differences and improve data comparability and the stability of model training. The original matrix was first subjected to total-count normalization, where the expression value of each gene was divided by its total number and multiplied by a fixed constant of 10,000. Then, the normalized data were subjected to a natural logarithmic transformation (adding 1 to avoid taking the logarithm of zero), as follows:

This log-normalization strategy was employed to effectively compress the dynamic range of the data, reduce the dominance of highly expressed genes, and normalize expression distribution, providing a stable foundation for subsequent dimensionality reduction, clustering, and visualization analysis.

The Genome Reference Consortium Human Build 38 patch (GRCh38) was downloaded from UCSC (https://genome.ucsc.edu/), and the order of all genes follows the default gene sorting in the source file.

Module overview

The overall model structure is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Transcriptome data were characterized by high dimensionality and sparsity, with a single sample typically containing expression information for numerous genes. Furthermore, annotating clinical immunotherapy response samples is extremely costly, which can easily lead to overfitting in supervised learning models.

Contrastive learning, as an efficient self-supervised paradigm, avoids the need for manual labeling by mining data’s intrinsic structure through “positive and negative sample pairs”34. The model uses a two-branch data augmentation strategy with weak and strong augmentations, as follows: positive pairs are generated by applying weak and strong augmentations to the same sample, while augmented views from different samples serve as negative pairs. This approach compels the model to learn general feature representations of gene expression data, reducing dependence on labeled samples and enhancing feature extraction for high-dimensional, sparse data35. Data augmentation plays a key role in gene–image conversion, thereby boosting model generalization, but it can introduce distribution shifts36. Contrastive learning mitigates this by enabling the model to ignore augmentation-induced perturbations and focus on core biological features, thereby improving the stability of “gene–image” transformations, feature representation consistency, and reliability for downstream immunotherapy response prediction.

In contrast, strong augmentation results in more substantial image transformations, such as noise, occlusion, and geometric distortions, further diversifying the training data and boosting the robustness and generalizability of the model. This approach enhances the model’s ability to handle various perturbations in real-world data. The feature extraction module is the model’s core and combines a residual network with an attention mechanism37,38. The residual network addresses the gradient vanishing problem common to deep networks, allowing for the retention of shallow features through skip connections. Furthermore, the attention mechanism refines feature learning by focusing on the most relevant parts of the data. Both components work together with the Mean Teacher framework39, thereby enabling semi-supervised learning and enhancing the model’s ability to effectively leverage both labeled and unlabeled data. The overall architecture includes convolutional layers, attention modules, residual blocks, and global pooling, which allow the progressive extraction and dimensionality reduction of multi-scale features40. In the final classification module, a fully connected structure processes the features obtained through the dual-path encoder to generate the final output for classification.

Model training method for consistency learning

The Mean Teacher framework is introduced to address the overfitting problem of models in high-dimensional sparse genomic data. In cancer immunotherapy prediction, high-quality labeled data is often limited. Mean Teacher constructs a “teacher” model by maintaining an EMA of the model parameters. This framework forces the “student” model to produce features consistent with the “teacher” model when faced with different data augmentations (such as noise and occlusion). This mechanism significantly reduces the model’s sensitivity to input perturbations and enhances the model’s robustness in generalizing across different cancer types.

For the same input, surface-level changes from perturbations should not alter its intrinsic features, which remain consistent41. To capture these intrinsic features, the model applies weak and strong augmentation perturbations to the input.

Weak augmentation

Weak enhancements were performed to diversify the data while retaining the core image features via a slight, low-perturbation transformation on the input image at the weakly enhanced position, avoiding major changes to the semantic information of the image42. The main operations are as follows:

Each image is in the PIL image format. Let the input image be x∈RC×H×W. By horizontal flipping, vertical flipping, and translation, the original image is transformed into a new image be x(1)∈RC×H×W.

The image was flipped horizontally (random horizontal flip) and then vertically (Random Vertical Flip), with a 30% probability to flip the image left/right and upside/down, maintaining the spatial symmetry of gene expression. Next, the image was translated (Random Affine) with a horizontal and vertical translation range of ±5% of the image size to simulate the slight positional shift of the gene expression region in the heat map and enhance the overall model’s robustness to local features.

Strong augmentation

The model applied stronger transformations to the image at the strong enhancement position, introducing noise, occlusion, and larger geometric transformations to force the model to learn more robust feature representations, thereby greatly improving the robustness and global perception of feature representations43. The main operations are as follows:

First, the original input image x∈RC×H×W was flipped and translated with a higher probability.

Herein, the flip probability was increased to 50%, and the translation amplitude was expanded to ±10% (maximum translation of ±51 pixels for a 512 × 512 image) to enhance data diversity and perturbation intensity. However, unlike weak enhancement, strong enhancement enabled the model to learn more essential and robust feature representations by introducing more challenging transformations, such as Gaussian noise and random erasure.

Standard normal distribution noise of the same shape was generated as the input tensor by adding Gaussian noise (torch.randn_like(x)), multiplying it by 0.03, and adding it to the original data. The image with added noise was randomly erased, which enabled the simulation of the local data missing or occlusion that may occur in the experiment, forcing the model to learn non-local features. The strong enhancement image x(3)∈RC×H×W contains multiple perturbations such as flipping, translation, noise, and erasure.

Residual backbone network based on strided convolution

Backbone network

To reduce the spatial size of the feature map, the model constructed strided convolution and increased the step size s, while also causing the receptive field of subsequent layers to grow faster44.

RFl−1 denotes the receptive field of the previous layer, kl is the convolution kernel size of the current layer, and si is the step size of the i-th layer. This design brings significant advantages to the receptive field, as rapidly expanding the receptive field allows for the capture of broader contextual information from the input data within fewer network layers. The accelerated growth of the receptive field further facilitates model learning of more global feature representations.

To solve the gradient vanishing problem in deep network training, residual networks and jump connections were introduced. The model uses convolution kernels of different sizes45, as follows: large (Conv7×7), responsible for global feature capture and directly obtains large-scale context information; medium (Conv5×5), balances local details and context information; and small (Conv3×3), performs fine feature extraction.

The input is the image tensor. xin∈RC×H×W processed by the weak enhancement and strong enhancement modules. First, the first layer of convolution is performed with a convolution kernel size of (7 × 7).

The output is then passed through the attention module (Attention) + BN + ReLU, mapping x1∈RC′×H′×W′ to the hidden dimension h(0)∈Rxmodel:

Channel weighting enhanced key gene expression features across 32 channels, followed by batch normalization (BN) and ReLU for faster convergence. A residual block was added after ReLU to preserve output size while mitigating gradient issues. To enable the model to learn more fundamental feature representations, max pooling was performed with a kernel size of 2 × 2, halving the output size, which was followed by a second convolutional layer (5 × 5 kernel). The resulting tensor enters the attention module, where 64 channels are weighted to capture more complex dependencies. Another max pooling was performed, reducing the output size by half to 32 × 32. Finally, a third convolution (3 × 3 kernel) was performed. BN + ReLU and residual block were introduced, maintaining the output size. Residual structure

Traditional neural networks stack multiple layers of nonlinear transformations to learn the input-to-output mapping; however, with increasing network depth, they are restricted by challenges such as vanishing/exploding gradients and degradation. By introducing skip connections, residual networks only need to learn the residual mapping F(x) = H(x) - x, which is typically easier to optimize and allows the learning of the residual relationship between input and output, thereby mitigating the aforementioned issues. For the input image tensor x∈RC×H×W, the residual block extracts its features through two Conv3×3 convolutions. After the first Conv3×3 convolution, BN+ReLU+Dropout are added to obtain a new image tensor x′∈RC×H×W

Only BN is added after the second convolution Conv3×3 and applied to the two convolutions to accelerate training and enhance stability. After the second convolution, instead of a ReLU layer, BN is added to the original input and then passed through a ReLU to ensure the retention of linear properties. Subsequently, dropout regularization is applied to randomly drop channels, reducing overfitting risks (p = 0.3 indicates a 30% probability of dropping). Adding dropout between two convolutions to randomly discard the entire channel is more suitable for the convolution feature map than traditional dropout, as it enhances the generalization ability and prevents underfitting due to over-regularization by performing Dropout only once. The final output is the tensor out∈RC×H×W.

GAP was used instead of global max pooling because the average value can better preserve the overall statistical information of the channels, avoiding the local noise interference. GAP extracts channel-level global features by compressing the spatial dimensions of each channel to 1×1, followed by a dimensionality-reducing convolution layer Conv1×1, and ReLU-mediated activation of the convolved image to enhance the model’s expressive capability. Loss function

The total loss function in this study consists of two parts: a classification loss (using Focal Loss) for predicting the response to immunotherapy and a consistency loss (using MSE) for feature alignment under the Mean Teacher framework.

Focal loss46 was used to primarily address the excessively large positive-to-negative ratio (more positive samples). The cross-entropy loss allows the model to predict more samples as positive, resulting in corresponding overfitting while losing the ability to predict negative samples. In the Mean Teacher framework, consistency loss enhances the model’s robustness and generalization ability by constraining the output consistency of both student and teacher models for different augmented views of the same sample. Even with the addition of different levels of perturbations, the essential characteristics of images remain unchanged. These designs mainly update model parameters through backpropagation, forcing the model to eliminate noise and superficial differences from the original data, allowing it to focus on essential features that are discriminative, robust, and generalizable, thereby enhancing model performance.

Enrichment analysis

To evaluate the biological relevance of ImmuGT-ConRes, we integrated TCGA pan-cancer RNA-seq data, the immune cell abundance signature dataset47, and Hallmark datasets. Further, we calculated gene set enrichment scores using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA). Spearman rank correlation analysis was performed to assess associations among feature genes, immune infiltration, and biological pathways, with statistical significance defined as a false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected p < 0.05, with multiple hypothesis testing correction performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg method.

Using feature genes identified by the attention mechanism weights for response effectiveness, the top 100 relevant genes from pan-cancer RNA-seq data were extracted, and Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed. GO analyses encompassed Molecular Function, Biological Process, and Cellular Component categories. We also used expression profiles of these feature genes to compute correlations with immune infiltration (via ImmuCellAI) and Hallmark gene set scoring systems, revealing their links to immune processes and biological functions.

For the response or non-response weighting mechanism, the top 500 feature genes with the highest attention weights were selected from both response and non-response groups, and then their intersection was performed to form a core gene set linked to immunotherapy response. We conducted GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on this set. Additionally, using pan-cancer RNA-seq data, we quantified its enrichment via ssGSEA and evaluated correlations with ImmuCellAI immune cell infiltration scores and Hallmark pathway activity using Spearman correlation analysis. Furthermore, the STRING database (v12.0) was used to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network for the core gene set with a high-confidence interaction threshold (0.700).

Statistical analyses

We assessed intergroup differences with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and evaluated associations between continuous variables using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. Receiver operating characteristic curves measured model predictive performance, where a higher AUC indicated superior predictive ability48,49. We calculated TIDE and ImmuCellAI scores and compared their immunotherapy response prediction against ImmuGT-ConRes17,18, along with comparing scaled machine learning models, including RF, MLP, and logistic regression, for predictive value. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) visualized the relationship between ImmuGT-ConRes and response status. A nomogram was generated using the rms package, and the calibration curve was used to assess its predictive performance. Decision curve analysis evaluated the net benefit of ImmuGT-ConRes. All analyses were performed using R 4.3.0 and Python. A p-value of <0.05 denotes statistical significance. This computing platform is equipped with an Intel Xeon Gold 6430 processor and an NVIDIA A100 80GB GPU, running the Ubuntu 24.04 LTS operating system.
