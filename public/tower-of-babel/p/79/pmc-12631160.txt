Uncovering Core Qualities of Digital Interventions for Health Promotion: Perspectives From People With Intellectual Disabilities, Relatives, and Professionals
Authors: Ellinor Nilsson, Stefan Johansson, Kristin Alfredsson Ågren, Ulrika Müssener
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPeople with intellectual disabilities experience significant health inequities. Digital interventions for health promotion are rarely designed from their specific needs. This study aimed to uncover core qualities of digital interventions for health promotion by exploring factors influencing health behaviours amongst people with intellectual disabilities.MethodsThis study was conducted in Sweden. Co‐design was used across 10 workshops with 18 people with intellectual disabilities, five relatives, and 12 professionals. Verbal and visual data were analyzed using qualitative content analysis.ResultsThree categories were generated as factors influencing health behaviours: Integrating a Sense of Belonging, Building Confidence Through Involvement, and Ensuring Structural and Contextual Coherence. For each category, two core qualities for digital interventions were uncovered.ConclusionsDigital interventions for health promotion targeting people with intellectual disabilities should integrate accessible design with social, emotional, and structural support, and be embedded within everyday contexts to foster empowerment, meaningful participation, and social connectedness.Keywords: co‐design, eHealth, health promotion, intellectual disability

Introduction

People with intellectual disabilities experience significant health inequities (World Health Organization 2022) as they have a higher risk for poor physical health (Hirvikoski et al. 2021; O'Leary et al. 2018; Flygare Wallén et al. 2018), mental health challenges (Frazier et al. 2025), and limited social networks (Harrison et al. 2021), than the general population. Intellectual disability is a neurodevelopmental disorder that emerges during the developmental period and is characterised by limitations in intellectual and adaptive functioning, across conceptual, social, and practical domains (American Psychiatric Association 2022). This involves difficulties in literacy, tasks including abstract thinking, learning from previous experiences and problem‐solving. The overall prevalence of intellectual disabilities is about 1%–2%, with higher prevalence in low‐income countries (Maulik et al. 2011).

People with intellectual disabilities face specific challenges in engaging in health behaviours. Although more research is needed, current findings indicate low levels of physical activity (Dairo et al. 2016), sedentary lifestyles (Melville et al. 2017), and unhealthy diets (Hoey et al. 2017). Multiple factors contribute to these outcomes, including cognitive and communicative limitations, environmental barriers, and inadequate support from caregivers and services (Marks et al. 2019). Addressing these behaviours with evidence‐based, effective, and accessible health promotion interventions is crucial for preventing lifestyle‐related diseases (O'Leary et al. 2018). Preferably, these interventions should target individuals during adolescence or young adulthood, being formative life stages during which health‐related behaviours often are established (Griffin et al. 2013). Previous research suggests that health promotion efforts for people with intellectual disabilities are more likely to be effective when they address relational and environmental determinants of health and include families and support workers (Geukes et al. 2019; Kuijken et al. 2019). However, health promotion efforts for this population show mixed effectiveness in improving physical activity and nutrition (Maine et al. 2020; Rana et al. 2024), although interventions targeting multiple health behaviours appear more likely to generate positive effects (Rana et al. 2024). One reason for these limited effects is weak study methodology and inappropriate outcome measures. Additionally, interventions may also fail to meet the needs and desires of people with intellectual disabilities.

To ensure that interventions meet the actual needs and preferences of the intended target group, researchers increasingly advocate for inclusive research approaches where stakeholders are actively involved in the design process and in the development of interventions (Rana et al. 2024). Such inclusive research involving people with intellectual disabilities has seen growing interest in recent years, whereas earlier research, to a large extent, has been limited to studies conducted on them or from a proxy perspective rather than with them (Tanabe et al. 2018; Di Lorito et al. 2018; Hatzikiriakidis et al. 2023; van Calis et al. 2023).

Using a co‐design methodology that engages the relevant stakeholders and end‐users (Sanders and Stappers 2008) is particularly important in the development, evaluation, and implementation of digital interventions. Whilst digital interventions e.g., mobile apps, websites, and short messaging services, have been effectively used to promote health behaviours for the general population (Cotie et al. 2018; Champion et al. 2019; Müssener et al. 2016), corresponding research targeting people with intellectual disabilities remains limited (Van Biesen et al. 2024). These highly scalable technologies could also be beneficial for people with intellectual disabilities if designed for their specific needs such as using clear icons, symbols, and accessible written and audiovisual language (Van Biesen et al. 2024; Wahl and Kiuppis 2023). However, this is rarely the case, as research highlights a persistent digital divide despite the fact that many people with intellectual disabilities use the internet and engage with digital technologies (Ågren et al. 2020; Caton et al. 2024). People with intellectual disabilities remain underrepresented in digital usage and often face difficulties when technology becomes complex, as in the case of digital health systems (Pettersson et al. 2023; Johansson et al. 2021; Normand et al. 2024). The UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (United Nations 2007) urges governments to ensure digital accessibility, underscoring the need for inclusive solutions that promote equitable health outcomes.

Together, this highlights a clear need to centre the voices of people with intellectual disabilities and those who support them, to better understand what makes health‐promoting interventions effective and relevant. In particular, there is a need to identify the core qualities of digital interventions, referring to factors and features that enhance motivation, ensure accessibility and enable practical use, thereby increasing the likelihood that such interventions are effective and feasible in practise.

Therefore, as an initial step in developing a co‐designed digital intervention for health promotion, this study aimed to uncover core qualities by exploring factors influencing health behaviours amongst adolescents and adults with intellectual disabilities.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study employed a participatory research methodology (Robertson and Simonsen 2012; Vaughn and Jacquez 2020) integrating co‐design (Sanders and Stappers 2008) within an explorative research approach, operationalised through a series of collaborative workshops (Ørngreen and Levinsen 2017). Inspired by Sanders and Stappers (Sanders and Stappers 2008), co‐design was applied as a form of collaborative creativity amongst participants with diverse backgrounds, experiences, and areas of expertise, with end users, regarded as experts, playing a central role in knowledge development, idea generation, and concept development.

This study represents the first step in the co‐design process of developing a digital intervention for health promotion (Figure 1). It is part of a larger research project–Intellectual Disability Integrating Digital Intervention Technology for health (I DID IT)– promoting the health and well‐being of people with intellectual disabilities. The methodologies used to develop a digital intervention have been previously published (Müssener et al. 2023). The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist (Tong et al. 2007) guided the reporting of this study (Supporting Information).

Study Context

This study was conducted in the southeast and central parts of Sweden. In Sweden, people with certain lifelong disabilities, including intellectual disabilities, are entitled to support under the Act Concerning Support and Service for Persons with Certain Functional Impairments (LSS) (SFS 1993). This act expresses rights in line with the principles of the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (United Nations 2007), and reflects a political and legal commitment to equality, autonomy, and inclusion. The overarching aim of the LSS is to promote equal living conditions and full participation in society for individuals covered by the Act. It states that individuals should be given the opportunity to live like others, and that services must be of high quality, coordinated across sectors, and tailored to individual needs. The Act also emphasises respect for personal integrity and self‐determination, in its vision to ensure that individuals have influence over the support they receive. For many individuals, the support provided through LSS is essential not only for meeting basic needs, but also for enabling a more independent and socially engaged life. The system carries a structural responsibility to strengthen individuals' capacity to make informed choices, participate in society, and maintain good health. Municipal Social Services are responsible for implementing LSS provisions and play a central role in supporting people with intellectual disabilities. This includes facilitating access to health care and health‐promoting activities, reducing social isolation, and enabling participation in community life.

Participants and Recruitment

Participants, who had no prior relationship with the authors, were recruited through purposive sampling via an established network of social services, municipalities, healthcare providers, and disability organisations. This approach ensured diversity in age, gender, experience, and perspectives from people with intellectual disabilities, relatives, and professionals.

People With Intellectual Disabilities

Contact persons within the above‐mentioned services identified people with mild to moderate intellectual disabilities as potential participants. To facilitate recruitment, the researchers asked these contact persons to present an informational video outlining the researchers behind the project, the project's objectives, ethical considerations, and the purpose of the workshops to potential participants. People with intellectual disabilities were eligible if they were at least 16 years old, verbally communicative, and regularly used digital devices and/or had experience with health‐promoting activities.

Professionals and Relatives

To recruit relatives and professionals, invitations and information were sent to contact persons in interest organisations and to managers within Municipal Social Care Services, Habilitation units in the healthcare system, leisure activities for people with intellectual disabilities, and upper secondary schools for students with intellectual disabilities. Potential participants had to be over 16 years of age and have experience working with or supporting people with intellectual disabilities.

In total, 35 participants—18 people with intellectual disabilities and 17 professionals and relatives—were included in the study. Most participants with intellectual disabilities lived in supported housing and were engaged in daily activity services and/or education. The remaining 17 participants were relatives, either a mother (n = 4) or a sister (n = 1), or a professional in social care services, the education sector, or healthcare (n = 12). Procedure and Data Collection

Ten workshops were conducted between May 2023 and October 2024 with seven different groups. Five workshops included people with intellectual disabilities divided into two groups. Group one participated in three consecutive workshops at their after‐school club and group two participated in two consecutive workshops, one remote and one at their folk high school (i.e., a publicly funded, non‐formal adult education promoting democracy, and personal development). Four workshops included professionals and one relatives, with three conducted at the university and one at a high school. The rationale behind the differing number of workshops was based on the specific needs and contexts of each group. One group of participants with intellectual disabilities attended only two workshops, as they already knew each other well and did not require the same trust‐building process as the group that participated in three workshops. Professionals and relatives participated in one workshop each, as these groups were easier to engage directly and could begin working on the tasks without extended preparation. The workshops, which were audio recorded, lasted between 90 and 230 min (M = 108).

The workshops were led by different combinations of the authors taking on different tasks. EN is a female physiotherapist and PhD student with extensive experience working with people with intellectual disabilities. KAÅ is a female researcher and occupational therapist with extensive experience in digital inclusion for people with intellectual disabilities. SJ is a male researcher with extensive experience in co‐designing digital interventions with people with intellectual disabilities and leading workshops. UM is a female researcher with extensive experience in qualitative research and digital interventions for health promotion.

Workshop content and structure were co‐developed by the research team and a research partner who is a close relative of a person with intellectual disability. Five themes guided the discussions in the workshops (Figure 2).

During the preparation phase, materials were prepared, including cognitive adaptations, such as the use of visual representations, structured task sequences and time management aids. Participant information was finalised, and team roles (e.g., moderator and notetaker) were assigned. In the introduction phase (i.e., at the start of each workshop), a welcoming and inclusive atmosphere was fostered through a shared meal to ease social barriers. The digital workshop, however, did not include this element and therefore transitioned more quickly into the main act. The agenda and principles for respectful interaction were presented, followed by participant introductions. Moderators provided an overview of the structure, including duration, schedule, and breaks. In the main act of each workshop, moderators introduced discussion themes structured into five modules (Figure 2). Participants expressed their views using both visual and verbal methods, individually and in groups, supported by materials such as sticky notes, whiteboards, images, and stickers to ensure inclusive participation. During the evaluation phase, participants shared their experiences participating in the workshop. During and after workshops fieldnotes were taken by the moderator and notetaker. Although the workshops followed a structured format, the co‐design process was iterative and responsive. Adjustments were made based on participant feedback and contextual needs, and recurring sessions allowed for increased familiarity and confidence. These adaptations were essential for ensuring meaningful participation and reducing hierarchical dynamics.

Data Analysis

The dataset from the workshops consisted of two types of data: artefacts (e.g., written words, drawings, and sketches) and audio‐recorded data.

Artefacts

In line with the co‐design process, an iterative analysis of the artefacts began during the workshops and involved the participants (Sanders and Stappers 2008). This collaborative process involved interpreting images and ideas with the aid of guiding questions from the moderator. Participants voted on various proposals using sticky notes, and decisions were made collectively. For example, participants were asked to write or draw what contributes to feeling good, and then placed stickers on the suggestions they resonated with. These proposals, generated by participants across all workshops, informed which topics were explored in greater depth.

Visual artefacts were used as prompts to support verbal reflection during the workshops. This iterative process ensured that the analysis was both dynamic and inclusive. After the workshops, all the authors inductively analysed the artefacts, focusing on latent content. Verbal interpretations from the workshops and the visual content were combined and treated as meaning units, allowing creative contributions to be systematically integrated into the content analysis. Symbolic elements and contextual factors generated from artefacts were interpreted in relation to the broader implications and potential applications of the proposed ideas.

Transcript

After the completion of all the workshops, transcribed data from the audio recordings was analysed by EN and UM following content analysis (Elo and Kyngäs 2008) employing an inductive approach to the manifest content in the transcribed data. To ensure a comprehensive understanding, the systematic analysis process followed several steps (Elo and Kyngäs 2008). Initially, EN reviewed the transcribed data multiple times. Key segments corresponding with the research questions were identified and documented as units of analysis, using Microsoft Excel. Artefacts were documented in the same file following the same method. Subsequently, EN coded, grouped, and aggregated these units of analysis into categories and sub‐categories (Figure 3). This process was closely followed by UM who reviewed the codes, groups, sub‐categories, and categories ensuring coherence, consistency, and alignment with the study aim. Finally, all authors discussed and collectively defined the categories.

Example from the abstraction process of the analysis.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Regional Ethics Board (Dnr 2022‐06682‐01). Informed consent was collected from all participants, either written or orally. The study information for participants with intellectual disabilities was adapted using easy‐to‐read text and supplemented with an informational video recorded by researchers KAÅ, SJ, and UM. For participants who provided oral consent, this was obtained during the digital workshop and recorded with their permission. Support workers familiar with the participants were present to assist with communication and understanding when needed. Prior to the workshops, participants received the easy‐to‐read materials, which were also shared with their support persons to review together. These measures were taken to ensure that participants fully understood the study and were able to provide informed consent.

Results

The analysis resulted in three categories that reflect factors influencing health behaviours amongst people with intellectual disabilities. For each category, the analysis uncovered two sub‐categories, hereafter referred to as core qualities that a digital intervention should embed to address these perceived influencing factors: (1) Integrating a Sense of Belonging, with core qualities Mitigating loneliness and Fostering a feeling of validation, (2) Building Confidence Through Involvement, with core qualities Having tailored support for partaking and Providing manageable challenges, and (3) Ensuring Structural and Contextual Coherence, with core qualities Facilitating coordination of health promotion and Supporting the crucial role of support workers (Figure 4). Integrating a Sense of Belonging

A sense of belonging was seen as a major factor impacting health behaviours, influencing motivation and emotional engagement. Participants emphasised the importance of being acknowledged, involved and socially supported, both in everyday life and within digital environments.

Mitigating Loneliness

The ideas for a digital intervention to support healthy behaviours, generated by participants with intellectual disabilities, were all closely linked to social involvement, ultimately aiming to mitigate loneliness by creating opportunities for social interaction as a core quality of such an intervention. Meeting others and feeling involved were highlighted as key factors for enhancing health and well‐being. Participants with intellectual disabilities described ideas for a digital intervention where social interactions, such as meeting friends, facilitated healthy activities like exercising together (Figure 5). Similarly, professionals and relatives noted that a strong sense of belonging motivates individuals to engage in group activities regardless of the specific nature of the activity. One relative noted, “He wouldn't say himself that it's to get exercise, but it's to play with others and have a sense of social belonging” (Relative, 3). Furthermore, both relatives and professionals emphasised the prevalence of loneliness amongst people with intellectual disabilities and the need for creating safe social spaces and opportunities for social activities. They indicated that having a reliable, supportive network increases the willingness to embrace new challenges, including the adoption of health behaviours.

Idea for an online activity allowing friends to connect online and dance together remotely. The importance of friendship or even love is illustrated with hearts (Person with intellectual disability, 15).

Fostering a Feeling of Validation

Promoting emotional validation through recognition of identity was described as a core quality of a digital intervention. All the participants consistently highlighted emotional encouragement from family, friends, and support workers as crucial for engaging in healthy activities and maintaining health behaviours. Participants with intellectual disabilities described it as challenging when people were not listening to them, when discussing barriers to health behaviours, but found that positive feedback and rewards encouraged health behaviours: “It's that one time when one feels truly noticed and feels like someone, that one is respected” (Person with intellectual disability, 17).

Both participants with intellectual disabilities and relatives believed digital interventions could reinforce a sense of validation by encouraging the pursuit and sharing of personal interests through engaging activities, interactive games, and self‐tracking: “It is very important for those I know to be interested in things, to develop and to engage, and also to be able to share what they find exciting with others” (Relative, 2). Similarly, underscoring the health benefits of sharing positive experiences, the participants with intellectual disabilities described an idea for a digital intervention where you could recommend activities or places to visit that evoke feelings of freedom and relief (Figure 6).

Idea for an online activity capturing photos of places that make you feel good and sharing them with friends and family. The idea connects people digitally and promotes health behaviours by encouraging outdoor exploration (Person with intellectual disability, 16).

Moreover, strategies for achieving internal reinforcement, e.g., listening to music, dancing, time in nature, or taking screen‐free breaks, were perceived as important for overall well‐being: “When you're out in nature, you feel free. You can really relax” (Person with intellectual disability, 18). Reminders of memorable moments and personalised positive feedback, whether from a coach, from photos, or via self‐tracking tools like a pedometer, were also highlighted as ways to reinforce health behaviours.

Building Confidence Through Involvement

Confidence and active engagement were seen as a factor influencing health behaviours. Participants described how tailored support, clear expectations and opportunities for involvement, both online and offline, enabled individuals to initiate and maintain health behaviours. These aspects were consistently emphasised across stakeholder groups.

Having Tailored Support for Partaking

Facilitating meaningful partaking by providing tailored support was identified as a core quality of a digital intervention. Relatives and professionals emphasised that tailored and consistent support, including digital support, for people with intellectual disabilities is crucial for increasing participation in healthy activities, as it builds confidence and enhances health behaviours. In all workshops, the participants with intellectual disabilities expressed a desire to be actively involved in planning and undertaking activities, rather than being passive observers. Most participants living in group homes reported having little influence over meal planning and preparation and expressed a desire to be more involved. Clear structure and predictable routines in daily life were deemed important, stressing that support workers need to be competent in creating such conditions. However, some support workers lack knowledge about the importance of using, for example, picture‐based support, reducing opportunities for active partaking: “I think that pictures, they would probably help. We have a lot of instructions [in the daily activity service], for example, on how to perform the morning routines, but it's all just text” (Professional, 13). Additionally, when individuals understand what is expected of them, they feel more confident and motivated to engage in activities.

In addition to tailored support to enhance health behaviours, the participants with intellectual disabilities wanted digital interventions to have easy‐to‐read instructions, picture‐based checklists, sounds, text‐to‐speech, reminders, and clear content. They emphasised the need for an intuitive design with easy startup procedures and minimal registration, noting that other digital solutions are often too difficult to initiate.

Providing Manageable Challenges

Relatives and professionals emphasised supporting personal growth by offering achievable and meaningful challenges as a core quality of a digital intervention. They highlighted the importance of creating digital opportunities where people with intellectual disabilities can take on and overcome challenges, thereby fostering confidence and engagement in healthy behaviours. In addition to tailored support, they highlighted the importance of gradual task difficulty and a supportive atmosphere that fosters confidence and independence: “Challenges that are just a little bit too difficult, but still achievable. Succeeding in things you didn't think you could do.” (Relative, 2). Professionals noted that some people with intellectual disabilities were overly sheltered and insufficiently challenged. In addition, past failures were said to prevent individuals from taking on challenges: “Being able to succeed and feel a sense of accomplishment helps build the confidence to try again” (Professional, 10). Participants described how a digital intervention could support this process by offering tasks of increasing difficulty, tailored to individual ability levels. They envisioned features incorporating motivational messages and progress tracking.

Ensuring Structural and Contextual Coherence

Structural and contextual coherence was considered a factor influencing health behaviours. Participants highlighted how organisational, societal and individual factors shape both opportunities and barriers for engaging in health behaviours and using digital tools, underscoring the need for interventions to fit within existing support systems.

Facilitating Coordination of Health Promotion

Supporting coordinated health promotion through the facilitation of structural coherence was highlighted as a core quality of a digital intervention. Relatives and professionals emphasised the critical need for enhanced collaboration between organizations to promote health behaviours, highlighting the challenges in coordinating health activities and the possibilities for digital interventions to facilitate this coordination. They noted a lack of clearly defined responsibilities for health promotion amongst the various organizations supporting people with intellectual disabilities. This includes, for example, a lack of guidelines for food‐related decisions in group homes, which was said to create uncertainty regarding accountability.

Relatives underscored the municipality's responsibility to offer diverse daily activity services to motivate participation and contribute to reducing inactivity. Participants with intellectual disabilities and professionals noted that incorporating physical activities at daily activity services makes engagement easier. In addition, relatives expressed disappointment over limited community opportunities such as adapted physical activities, which often rely on a few dedicated individuals. This scarcity of opportunities was linked to uncertainty about how to be active: “When people say ‘health’, I think, for example, that you are out walking. It's more about moving around than just sitting, like I mostly do […]. It's mostly because I don't know what to do” (Person with intellectual disability, 16). However, both professionals and relatives highlighted poor communication between services: “Even though activities are created, and many of us are involved in creating them, they just get stuck in someone's inbox […]. We don't have the channels to reach the supported housings” (Relative, 5). Participants believed that a digital intervention could address these challenges by displaying available activities, including modules for support workers.

In all workshops, participants described how a lack of support workers limited opportunities for engagement in everyday activities, such as cooking, attending events, or walking instead of relying on transportation services: “But they get picked up door‐to‐door by taxi but could perhaps walk. But there's no one who can walk with them” (Professional, 10). Consequently, relatives felt compelled to organise health activities: “I see it with my son, that it is the parental responsibility, you can never let go of it because too little happens at the residences” (Relative, 4). This aspect was highlighted as crucial when introducing a digital intervention. Despite the value of a digital intervention, people with intellectual disabilities will need the assistance of support workers. That is, professionals and relatives believed that digital interventions could never replace in‐person support, also to facilitate digital use.

Finally, structural issues such as the lack of Wi‐Fi or digital devices available at group homes were also highlighted as potential barriers to healthy activities. Participants highlighted the need for solutions that did not depend on internet connectivity.

Supporting the Crucial Role of Support Workers

The crucial role of support workers as enablers in health promotion, and supporting this role, was identified as a core quality of a digital intervention. In all workshops, participants emphasised the role of support workers as role models, motivators, and facilitators, both for health behaviours and digital use. Professionals and relatives stated that successful implementation of digital health interventions would require both involvement and education of support workers, as well as consideration of their attitudes towards digital solutions.

Participants highlighted how support workers can serve as role models by demonstrating alternative approaches through their own behaviour: “By eating together, one can still be a role model during meals and provide support on how to properly arrange food on the plate” (Professional, 35). Additionally, the importance of engaged and well‐matched support workers who are attuned to and curious about the individuals they support as well as positive and cheerful was emphasised in all workshops as a factor influencing health behaviours.

According to both relatives and professionals, finding motivation for healthy activities is challenging, underscoring the importance of support workers also having skills in motivational techniques. In this context, the balance between respecting autonomy and encouraging health behaviours was problematized, emphasising the individual's right to their own decisions and choices. However, it was noted that sometimes respecting autonomy was misinterpreted as avoiding motivation for behaviour change altogether. Rather, using motivational techniques to encourage health behaviours was seen as a responsibility for support workers, provided it is done respectfully and empathetically: “A lot of this is that support workers are claiming ‘autonomy’ and ‘no, we can't say anything because it is their choice’. Well yes, but you can motivate, and you can encourage” (Professional, 8).

Relatives and professionals stated that many people with intellectual disabilities rely on support workers for day‐to‐day decision‐making about food, exercise, and routines. They noted that support workers need basic knowledge about health to facilitate healthy behaviours: “They [the support workers] need to have knowledge about health and what we generally consider to be healthy. […] But [my relative] would never be able to advocate for themself” (Relative, 3). One participant with an intellectual disability stressed the need for this support: “Everything about food is hard, what to choose in the store, how much to eat” (Person with intellectual disability, 25).

Discussion

From the perspectives of people with intellectual disabilities, relatives, and professionals, this study uncovered core qualities of a health‐promoting digital intervention through exploring factors influencing health behaviours, providing a foundation for the development of a digital intervention addressing the unique needs and preferences of this population.

The results revealed integrating a sense of belonging as a central motivational factor when promoting health behaviours and mitigating loneliness as a core quality of digital interventions for health promotion. For example, participants suggested that social connection could be a key motivator for engaging in exercise, with physical activity serving as a means to achieve social involvement rather than as a valued goal itself and an end result. The participants' suggestions, that reducing loneliness may contribute to health behaviours, align with existing research suggesting that social connections influence various health outcomes, including mental and physical health, health behaviours, and mortality risk (Umberson and Karas Montez 2010). This is also consistent with previous findings identifying loneliness as an independent risk factor for physical inactivity in adults without intellectual disabilities (Hawkley et al. 2009). Amongst people with intellectual disabilities, research has shown that physical activity can enhance peer social connectedness (Maenhout and Melville 2024). The findings of our study suggest that this relationship may also operate in the opposite direction, with social connectedness perceived as a primary motivator for engaging in health behaviours. These insights underscore the importance of designing digital interventions that foreground social engagement as a pathway to fostering health behaviour. Whilst this study did not explicitly distinguish between loneliness as an emotional state and social isolation as a structural condition, our findings suggest that these phenomena are closely intertwined for people with intellectual disabilities. Participants described experiences of limited opportunities for meaningful interaction, which may reflect structural barriers to social engagement. In this context, loneliness can be understood as a consequence of social isolation, where societal structures, including support services under the Swedish Act LSS (SFS 1993), have a responsibility to facilitate social engagement. A digital intervention was perceived as a potential tool to reduce social isolation by enabling interaction based on individual interests and capacities, which may in turn contribute to reducing feelings of loneliness.

Further, the results correspond to a digital intervention that gradually increases challenges tailored to participants, including personalised support through adaptive feedback and guidance. This ensures that tasks remain both achievable and stimulating, thereby fostering a sense of autonomy specifically in relation to health behaviours. Additionally, an intervention should foster a supportive atmosphere using motivational messages and progress tracking. These approaches contribute to the experience of a sense of accomplishment, enhancing motivation for healthy behaviours. Previous studies found that interventions that encourage autonomy can effectively promote health and well‐being in this population (Carey et al. 2023). From an organisational perspective, participants described how the Swedish Act LSS (SFS 1993) emphasises autonomy and the individual's right to influence the support they receive, ensuring that services are shaped in collaboration with the person concerned. However, health promotion and informed decision‐making are rarely articulated as explicit responsibilities for support workers. Although the LSS aims to strengthen individual agency and enable participation in society on equal terms with others, support workers sometimes interpret this as a rationale for non‐intervention, even in situations involving unhealthy behaviours. Simultaneously, some support routines, such as staff assuming full responsibility for meal preparation, represent a more controlling form of care that unintentionally reinforces dependency. These conflicting interpretations of autonomy and participation risk limiting the opportunities for individuals to actively engage in activities, learn, and develop (Luckasson et al. 2022). Similarly, the participants discussed the importance of support workers facilitating the understanding of healthy choices, as well as supporting digital use.

This double dependency, where both the user and the support workers face challenges, undermines even the best‐designed digital interventions. A related challenge is digital dependency. As noted in earlier studies (Alfredsson Ågren et al. 2020), people with intellectual disabilities often need to rely on the support of others to access and use digital tools, yet these support workers may lack digital competence themselves and organisations may lack the technology, structures and culture needed, as also highlighted in earlier research (Eriksson and Ineland 2024). Although some participants noted limited internet or device access as a barrier, we frame this as an equity issue. Digital exclusion is more than a technical problem; it reflects broader social neglect. Rather than designing low connectivity solutions, we advocate for policy that guarantees internet access and digital tools for people with intellectual disabilities, a view shared in the 2006 United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (United Nations 2007).

Our findings indicate that people with intellectual disabilities seldom initiate or sustain health behaviours independently; rather, they rely significantly on their social and environmental contexts, as is often the case with many other everyday activities. Consequently, digital interventions should address structural conditions, including levels of support, inter‐organisational collaboration, and role clarity. These results align with previous work that emphasises the relational and environmental determinants of health amongst people with intellectual disabilities (Geukes et al. 2019; Kuijken et al. 2019) and the inclusion of families and communities–i.e., using a socio‐ecological model (Hamdani et al. 2019). Importantly, participants did not express a need for health information. Instead, they emphasised the importance of receiving positive feedback, encouragement, and content that is social or entertaining. This underscores the notion that knowledge alone is rarely sufficient to drive behavioural change (Hendel et al. 2024). For people with intellectual disabilities, the issue may not be what to do, but how to do it and with whom. However, it is important to acknowledge that this lack of expressed need may also reflect communication barriers, previous experiences of being overwhelmed by health messaging, or the structure of the workshops themselves.

Methodological Considerations

The co‐design process provided deeper insights into both the everyday lived experiences and the broader systemic conditions affecting health behaviours amongst people with intellectual disabilities. Unlike traditional qualitative focus group interviews, the co‐design approach is participatory and extends beyond verbal communication. Using various tasks, discussions, and practical activities, people with intellectual disabilities were facilitated to express their ideas and feelings, engaging in ways that were more accessible and meaningful to them, capturing a more nuanced understanding of their perspectives. However, obtaining true thoughts and opinions as well as illustrative quotations from people with intellectual disabilities can be challenging. This difficulty is partly due to the nature of the disability and the limited opportunities these individuals have to discuss abstract concepts. Often, their thoughts are embedded in seemingly superficial sketches or proposals, but these expressions can reveal deeper meanings. That is, the inability to articulate thoughts does not diminish one's contributions. Researchers should serve as facilitators and advocates, empowering people with intellectual disabilities to engage as equal partners in the research process (Di Lorito et al. 2018). To support this, several measures were taken to mitigate researcher influence and manage power dynamics. Workshops were held in familiar settings such as schools or leisure activity centres, and participants were offered multiple modes of expression, including speech, writing, drawing, and visual materials. Researchers emphasised collaboration and framed participants as co‐researchers, actively working to reduce hierarchical distance, for example, through informal interactions and mindful clothing choices. Recurring workshops allowed participants to become familiar with the format and build confidence in the group process. After each session, participants were invited to reflect on the structure and suggest changes for future workshops. Moreover, our research team's extensive research and clinical experience enhanced our ability to adapt approaches during the workshops and the analysis. These adaptations to the workshops were intended to facilitate genuine participation by people with intellectual disabilities, as highlighted in previous research (Raman and French 2022).

The trustworthiness (Kyngäs et al. 2020) of the results was considered in several ways. Prolonged engagement with the participants with intellectual disabilities allowed for a deeper understanding of their experiences, ensuring that the findings accurately reflect their experiences. Credibility was further supported by a diverse sample, multiple data sources (transcripts and artefacts), and multiple researchers. Regularly checking in with participants during workshops ensured accurate and authentic data. Focusing on data authenticity and accuracy ensured the truth value of the study. This involved rigorous data collection and analysis procedures as well as validation techniques such as continuously checking in with participants, an iterative process that ensured findings were grounded in participant experience. By integrating both verbal and non‐verbal data, the dataset provided a comprehensive account of participants' contributions, allowing for analysis of both what was articulated and what was expressed visually and textually. In determining the adequacy of the dataset, we applied the concept of information power (Malterud et al. 2016), in relation to the focus and scope of the study, richness of the material, and diversity of perspectives rather than relying on the notion of data saturation, which is referred to in the COREQ checklist. Recruitment was facilitated through our established network, with contact persons identifying potential participants. Out of consideration for their workload, they were not required to report back on this process. Consequently, it is not possible to account for non‐participation amongst people with intellectual disabilities, which represents a limitation of the study. However, the multi‐faceted recruitment strategy increased the transferability of our findings. The transferability may however be affected by the fact that all participants with intellectual disabilities were active users of digital devices, such as smartphones, potentially excluding perspectives from individuals with lower levels of digital literacy or limited access. However, in this phase of the project, the aim was to engage participants who had relevant experience of digital tools, in order to generate insights into features that support usability and motivation in a digital intervention. Furthermore, all participating relatives and professionals were female. Whilst this may be representative of the context, reflecting the gender distribution amongst support workers, it also limits the gender diversity of perspectives, particularly amongst relatives, and may affect the transferability of the findings. The small number of relatives may also have limited the diversity of perspectives from this stakeholder group. Dependability was achieved by following the methodology outlined by Elo and Kyngäs (Elo and Kyngäs 2008). The preunderstandings of the researchers enhanced the depth and relevance of the analysis. Prior experience and knowledge facilitated a more nuanced understanding of the participants' experiences. However, preunderstanding may also present challenges. This was managed through regular team debriefings, where interpretations were discussed and challenged collaboratively. The involvement of multiple researchers in the analysis process helped ensure that different perspectives were considered. Additionally, discussing with participants about preliminary interpretations during workshops supported transparency and reduced the risk of researcher‐imposed meaning.

Future Research

Future research should continue to apply co‐design methodologies involving people with intellectual disabilities and relevant stakeholders throughout all phases of development processes. These should also include individuals with lower levels of digital experience, in order to better understand barriers to engagement and inform the design of more inclusive and accessible digital interventions. This approach can help ensure that digital health promotion interventions are not only accessible, but also perceived as relevant, engaging, and feasible by end users themselves. The findings highlight the importance of addressing loneliness and strengthening confidence as central components in promoting health behaviours amongst people with intellectual disabilities. Digital interventions that foster social connection and create opportunities for positive experiences, such as succeeding at manageable tasks, may enhance motivation and engagement. A particular focus of future research should be placed on transforming the identified core qualities into tangible design features through workshops with people with intellectual disabilities, user experience (UX) designers, and developers, laying the foundation for a conceptually grounded and co‐designed digital intervention. Finally, successful implementation depends on contextual factors such as the availability of support staff, organizational responsibility, and access to digital infrastructure. Future studies should explore how these structural and environmental conditions shape the long‐term sustainability and everyday integration of digital interventions.

Conclusions

The findings suggest that a digital health promotion intervention for people with intellectual disabilities should be multifaceted, addressing both health‐promoting content and accessible, tailored support. Participants perceived that digital interventions are more likely to be effective if they foster social connectedness and reduce loneliness, whilst simultaneously creating opportunities to experience success and build confidence. These elements can support a sense of empowerment and motivation for engaging in health behaviours. Importantly, interventions must be embedded in everyday life and supported by support workers with the necessary health knowledge and digital competence. Without adequate infrastructure, organisational responsibility, and a reliable support network, implementation is unlikely to succeed. The context in which digital interventions are introduced plays a crucial role in shaping engagement, relevance, and sustainability.
