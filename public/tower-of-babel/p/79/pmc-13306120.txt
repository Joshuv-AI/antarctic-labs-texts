Increased expression of aromatase after focal cerebral ischemia: Relevance to neuroprotection and functional recovery
Authors: Lindsay Gallagher, Anna F Dominiczak, Luis M Garcia‐Segura, Nobuhiro Harada, J B Hutchison, James M Mullin, Delyth Graham, Peter O'Shaughnessy, Hilary Victoria O Carswell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimAromatase is the key enzyme in the biosynthesis of 17β‐estradiol, the most potent estrogen, which has pleiotropic neuroprotective properties. Aromatase levels increase in the brain after stroke, and its gene variants increase susceptibility to stroke. This study aims to determine whether aromatase overexpression improves stroke outcome and whether aromatase inhibition exacerbates outcome after permanent focal cerebral ischemia.Methods/DesignAll animals (3–4 months old) underwent permanent middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) by diathermy. Time course of aromatase expression following MCAO in female ovariectomized spontaneously hypertensive stroke prone (SHRSP) rats was assessed by semi‐quantitative immunohistochemistry and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). The effect of aromatase expression on stroke outcome was assessed using a male mouse model over‐expressing aromatase (Dax‐1 KO mice) and by letrozole treatment. Volume of ischemic damage was assessed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and functional recovery was assessed by the corner test and foot‐fault test. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0.ResultsThe key findings are that aromatase expression was significantly increased in SHRSP in both the dorsal and ventral peri‐infarct zones and hippocampus at 24 h post‐MCAO as measured by areas of immunostaining but not qPCR. There was no improvement in stroke outcome by Dax‐1 KO, despite significantly higher plasma 17β‐estradiol levels and increased brain aromatase immunoreactivity after stroke. There was no exacerbation on stroke outcome by letrozole, despite decreased plasma 17β‐estradiol levels.ConclusionThe time course and location of increased aromatase indicate a potential role in neuroprotection and repair; however, manipulation of aromatase expression does not influence outcome after permanent MCAO in male mice.Keywords: cerebral ischemia, estrogen, middle cerebral artery occlusion

INTRODUCTION

Stroke is a devastating disease responsible for 5 million deaths globally every year, with a further 5 million people being permanently disabled. 1 This emphasizes the urgent need to find effective new stroke therapies. Given its pleiotropic neuroprotective properties, the female sex hormone 17β‐estradiol has shown promise as a neuroprotectant in models of cerebral ischemia. 2 However detrimental effects of exogenous 17β‐estradiol treatment in stroke need to be overcome. Exogenous 17β‐estradiol can worsen stroke outcome in post‐menopausal women 3 , 4 , 5 and in ovariectomized or aged female rats. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Rodent and post‐menopausal women studies suggest that these detrimental effects of exogenous 17β‐estradiol are possibly related to a pre‐existing inflammatory condition, 13 prolonged estrogen deficiency, 14 , 15 supraphysiological circulating 17β‐estradiol levels, 16 , 17 mode of administration, 18 or transferrin upregulation. 6 On the other hand, evidence exists that increased endogenous 17β‐estradiol has beneficial effects in stroke. Spontaneously hypertensive rats sustained smaller infarcts after experimental stroke during proestrus versus metestrus (highest vs. lowest levels of circulating 17β‐estradiol) 19 and blocking endogenous 17β‐estradiol production exacerbated stroke severity. 20 , 21 Further, brain overexpression of 17β‐estradiol, through stem cell delivery, induced neuroprotection after stroke. 22

Aromatase, also known as cytochrome P45019A1 (CYP19A1), is a rate‐limiting enzyme for the biosynthesis of endogenous 17β‐estradiol from testosterone. Since aromatase is present in brain regions commonly compromised in human stroke, such as the cerebral cortex in both males and females, 23 brain aromatase may help improve stroke outcome by the local synthesis of endogenous 17β‐estradiol. Such an endogenous neuroprotective effect could circumvent exogenous 17β‐estradiol detrimental effects and act as an alternative to the selective delivery of 17β‐estradiol to the brain via stem cells, 22 nanoparticles, 24 or prodrugs such as 10b,17b‐dihydroxyestra‐1,4‐dien‐3‐one. 25 The potential of aromatase to increase endogenous 17β‐estradiol as a therapeutic target in stroke and cardiovascular disease has been of recent interest. 26

Increased aromatase expression after brain injury has been reported in rodents and humans. In rodents, brain aromatase expression was significantly increased after kainate injection, 27 global ischemia, 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 and focal ischemia. 32 , 33 In postmenopausal women, serum aromatase levels were shown to be increased 24 h after stroke. 34 Therefore, the increased expression of aromatase may be an endogenous neuroprotective mechanism in response to brain injury. Indeed, some genetic variants of CYP19A1 have been linked to increased susceptibility and severity of human stroke 35 , 36 and traumatic brain injury. 37 Therefore, aromatase may be neuroprotective through its endogenous production of 17β‐estradiol, which is an appealing approach given the complexities that surround exogenous 17β‐estradiol actions in stroke. 38

We hypothesized that aromatase overexpression would improve stroke outcome and that the reverse strategy of aromatase inhibition would exacerbate stroke outcome. We quantified brain aromatase levels in hypertensive animals and found increased protein but not mRNA levels within the therapeutic time window of neuroprotection and repair after stroke. Further, we report no effect of aromatase overexpression or inhibition on stroke infarct size or outcome.

METHODS

All experiments were performed under a Project License from the UK Home Office, with the approval by the University of Glasgow Ethical Review Panel (Project License number; 60/3054), and subject to the Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act, 1986, and this manuscript was written in accordance with the Animal Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines. 39 All animals were assessed for welfare daily until 7 days post‐operative and every 2–3 days thereafter. Any excluded animals are stated in the relevant sections below. All animals were randomized by computer, and investigators were blinded by an independent investigator to the conditions (sham/MCAO, genotype, treatment) for the duration of the experiments and analyses. All animals were allowed ad libitum access to food and water and group housed in open cages in a controlled environment (12:12 h light cycle beginning at 06:00 and temperature maintained at 22°C).

Assessment of the time course of aromatase expression following MCAO in the stroke‐prone spontaneously hypertensive rat (SHRSP)

Animals and surgery

Since stroke is commonly associated with co‐morbidities such as hypertension, we quantified the expression of aromatase in the stroke‐prone spontaneously hypertensive rat (SHRSP). Female SHRSP (3–4 months of age) were anesthetized for bilateral ovariectomy with Hypnorm (fentanyl citrate 0.315 mg/mL and fluanisone 10 mg/mL, Janssen Animal Health, Leeds, UK) and Hypnovel (10 mg/2 mL midazolam, Roche, Welwyn Garden, UK) (1:1, 0.3–0.5 mL), and 0.02 mL of carprofen (Rimadyl, 50 mg/mL) was provided for post‐operative analgesia. One week later, animals underwent MCAO using a modified version of the Tamura experimental stroke model 40 in which a 2 mm distal segment of the left MCA was occluded by electrocoagulation, resulting in a predominantly cortical lesion. 19 Anesthesia was induced using a bolus (intraperitoneal injection, 0.88 mL/kg) of propofol (Rapinovet 10 mg/mL, Schering‐Plough Animal Health, UK) and maintained by infusion (6 mL/kg/h) through the tail vein. Animals were artificially ventilated with air to prevent propofol‐induced apnea. Rats were perfusion fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Thermo Fisher Scientific,Hemel Hempstead, UK) in 50 mM phosphate buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK) at four time points after MCAO to observe the time course of aromatase protein expression using immunohistochemistry: 2 h (n = 3), 24 h (n = 4), 8 days (n = 3), and 30 days (n = 3). These time points were chosen as previous studies have shown increased aromatase immunoreactivity up to 14 days after brain injury 27 , 30 , 33 and therefore we extended to 30 days to further inform on the temporal profile of brain aromatase expression after ischemic stroke. Brains were processed and paraffin‐embedded, and sectioned coronally (6 µm) at 3.20, 1.20, −0.26, −0.92, −1.80, −3.80, −5.30, and −6.30 mm from bregma. 41

Immunohistochemical analysis of aromatase

Immunostaining was performed on sections corresponding to −5.20 mm from bregma, at the coronal level of the posterior hippocampus, based on the presence of cortex and hippocampus, known to have positive staining, with a polyclonal anti‐aromatase antibody raised in rabbit (source N. Harada, 1:100 dilution). Briefly, sections were cleared of wax, endogenous peroxidase was quenched using hydrogen peroxide (3% in methanol for 30 min); sections were rinsed in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) and blocked for 1 h (10% normal goat serum, 0.5% bovine serum albumin in PBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK). The sections were incubated overnight with the primary antibody at 4°C, rinsed with PBS, incubated at room temperature for 1 h with secondary antibody for aromatase (anti‐rabbit IgG made in goat 1:500). Subsequently, solutions from the Vectastain ABC kit (Vector, UK) were applied for 1 h before and staining was visualized using a DAB peroxidase substrate kit (Vector, UK). Areas of increased immunostaining were quantified by delineating onto line diagrams and measuring by image analysis (MCID, Imaging Research, St. Catherines, Canada). In addition, the density of the increased immunostaining was assessed using a semi‐quantitative scoring system: 0 = absence, + = low density, ++ = medium density, and +++ = high density immunostaining. Immunostaining was verified as described previously, 32 utilizing a separate polyclonal anti‐aromatase antibody raised in rabbit (gifted by: J. Hutchison, L.M. Garcia‐Segura dilution: 1:250) on random sections, which were also re‐assessed by the same observer and a second observer to confirm good observer reproducibility. The primary antibody was omitted, as a negative control, revealing no staining, and human placental tissue, as a positive control, revealed positive staining.

Histology infarct size determination

Infarct sizes were calculated to allow comparisons of ischemic damage between groups. Coronal histology sections from the pre‐determined coronal level were stained with hematoxylin‐eosin, and the areas of neuronal perikaryal damage were delineated onto line diagrams, to correct for brain swelling, and total area measured using image analysis. 42

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis of aromatase

From a parallel set of ovariectomized SHRSP, tissue from sham (n = 5) and MCAO (n = 5) animals were prepared for quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis of aromatase mRNA at 24 h post‐MCAO after anesthetic overdose. This time point was chosen given that the first time point of increased aromatase protein was observed at 24 h and was sustained at 8 days. Dorsal and ventral peri‐infarct areas, as well as the ipsilateral hippocampus, were dissected and snap frozen in liquid nitrogen. Technical errors resulted in n = 4 for ventral and hippocampal MCAO tissue. RNA was extracted and reverse transcribed before transcript measurement by qPCR as described previously. 43 To act as an external standard 5 ng of luciferase mRNA (Promega UK, Southampton, UK) was added to each tissue at the start of the RNA extraction. 44 RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Life Technologies, Paisley, UK), and the RNA was reverse transcribed using random hexamers and Moloney murine leukemia virus reverse transcriptase (Superscript II; Life Technologies, UK). 45 The real‐time PCR approach used the SYBR green method in a 96‐well plate format using a MX3000 cycler (Stratagene, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). Reactions contained 5 μL twice (2×) the final working concentration SYBR master mix (Stratagene, The Netherlands), primer (100 nm), and template in a total volume of 10 μL. At the end of the amplification phase, a melting curve analysis was carried out on the products formed. Primers were designed using Primer Express 2.0 (Applied Biosystems, UK) and primer efficiency was greater than 90%, determined as described by Czechowski et al. 46 The aromatase primers used were: 5'‐GCCGTCAATCACGTCATCCT‐3' for forward and 5'‐CGAGAGCCTGCGGTATCAG‐3' for reverse (MWG Biotech AG).

Assessment of the effect of increased aromatase expression on stroke outcome

Animals and surgical preparation

Dax‐1 (dosage‐sensitive sex reversal adrenal hypoplasia congenita region on X‐chromosome gene‐1) is a negative regulator of steroidogenesis, repressing the trans‐activation of the aromatase gene, 47 which encodes the enzyme that converts testosterone to 17β‐estradiol. Therefore, the knockdown of Dax‐1 induces endogenous overexpression of aromatase and increased local 17β‐estradiol production. 22 The Dax‐1 KO and wild type (WT) 129svJ colony was gifted by Dr Jameson (Chicago, USA) and males were used in these studies as only male Dax‐1 KO exhibit the phenotype.

Dax‐1 KO and WT male mice (3–4 months old) were anesthetized and maintained with halothane in a 70:30 nitrous oxide: oxygen mixture and subjected to electrocoagulation of a 2 mm distal segment of the left MCA, as described above and modified for mice. 48 Mice were sacrificed over time after MCAO by anesthetic overdose to observe the time course of aromatase protein expression in Dax‐1 KO mice using immunohistochemistry (2 h, 24 h, 8 days, 14 days, and 30 days, n = 3 at each time point for both Dax‐1 KO and WT, except n = 4 at 24 h) and underwent semi‐quantitative immunohistochemical analysis of aromatase at the level of the septal nuclei corresponding to 0.14 mm from bregma as described above. Tissue from one animal was lost from 14‐day WT group leaving n = 2 in that group.

Infarct size and sensorimotor function measurements

In a separate series, animals underwent infarct size and sensorimotor function measurements for which a priori power calculations using previous data 22 with an effect size of 30% and standard deviation (SD) of three indicated at least n = 5 needed for each group to allow 80% power. Dax‐1 KO (n = 8) and WT (n = 8) mice underwent distal diathermy MCAO as above and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed at 48 h post‐MCAO, to ensure the infarct had fully evolved. MRI was performed on a 7 T horizontal bore magnet (Bruker BioSpin, Coventry, UK) with a surface coil set up for radio frequency transmission (Bruker) to allow longitudinal functional recovery analysis on the same animals. A rapid acquisition relaxation enhancement (RARE) T2 weighted sequence (TR = 5086, TE = 73 ms) with 600 µm slice thickness and total scan time of 11 min was used to determine infarct volume. Infarct, ipsilateral, and contralateral hemisphere areas were manually delineated, using the region of interest tool in the Paravision software program for each slice, multiplied by interslice distance and edema correction performed. 49

At 8 weeks post‐MCAO, mice underwent two separate tests of long‐term sensorimotor function, namely the corner test and a modified foot‐fault test on a 1 cm2 grid. Spontaneous motor recovery plateaus by 4–6 weeks post‐MCAO, 50 hence we chose 8 weeks post‐MCAO to avoid any potential confounding effects of spontaneous functional recovery and thereby any improved function at that time point would be evidence of sustained effects driven by aromatase. These sensorimotor tests were chosen specifically since they are sensitive enough to pick up and evaluate long‐term (e.g., 8 weeks) functional outcome post‐MCAO in mice. 51 Two Dax‐1 KO mice and one WT mouse were lost during the MRI scanning procedure leaving n = 6 for Dax‐1 KO mice and n = 7 for WT mice.

For the corner test, two cardboard pieces (30 cm × 20 cm × 1 cm) were placed at a 30° angle with a small opening at the joint. The mouse was allowed to approach the joint until both vibrissae touched the sides of the boards. Behavioral testing scoring criteria in the corner test were validated by Zhang et al., 51 where a turn was counted when the mouse fully reared then turned away from the corner. If the mouse did not fully rear, the turn was not counted. Two sets of ten turns were performed for each mouse, and the number of turns to the impaired side was calculated as a percentage of total turns. Performance on the foot‐fault test was filmed from a ventral perspective with a high‐speed Canon MV750i digital camcorder (30 frames/s; shutter speed of 1/500) (Jessops, UK) and the tapes were analyzed on a KV‐14LT1U Trinitron monitor (Sony, UK). Scoring criteria of forelimb stepping was based on a validated foot fault scoring system 52 which scored accuracy of limb placement on the rungs or protrusion between rungs on the following criteria: 0 = total miss, deep slip, or slight slip; 1 = replacement where the limb was placed and quickly moved; 2 = correction, where the limb was aimed but then placed elsewhere, or the stepping limb was repositioned; 3 = partial placement where only wrist or digits were placed; 4 = correct placement where palm placement on the rung occurred with full support. Three sets of ten steps were analyzed on two separate trials. Intra‐observer reproducibility was tested by the same observer re‐assessing and rescoring behavior and validated by a second independent observer. At the conclusion of the experiments, mice were deeply anesthetized, and trunk blood was collected for analysis of blood plasma 17β‐estradiol levels to confirm aromatase overexpression using a competitive radioactive binding kit (Diagnostic Products Corporation, Los Angeles, USA).

Assessment of the effect of aromatase inhibition on stroke outcome

Animals and surgical preparation

Male WT (129svJ) mice (3–4 months old) were treated daily for 15 days by subcutaneous injections with 0.1 mL vehicle (0.3% hydroxypropylmethylcellulose) (named vehicle 10, n = 7) or 10 μg (n = 5) of aromatase inhibitor letrozole (gifted by Novartis Pharma AG, Basel, Switzerland). Additional animals were treated with vehicle (named vehicle 100, n = 7) or a higher dose (100 µg, n = 8) of letrozole. Males were used in this study, using the reverse strategy of aromatase inhibition, to consolidate or refute the Dax‐1 KO findings.

Infarct size and sensorimotor function measurements

After 7 days of letrozole/vehicle treatment, mice were subjected to distal diathermy MCAO, then T2 MRI scanning for infarct measurement at 48 h post‐MCAO, to ensure the infarct had fully evolved as described above. For behavioral testing, animals were habituated to and trained in the ladder rung walking test 53 chosen as a more sensitive sensorimotor function test than the corner or foot fault test, as rungs can be arranged in an irregular pattern. Animals were filmed as described above at pre‐MCAO and at 2 days and 8 days post‐MCAO to compare sensorimotor deficits post‐MCAO between vehicle‐ and letrozole‐treated animals. Animals were trained pre‐MCAO on a regular pattern of rungs and at 2 days and 8 days post‐MCAO were tested using an irregular pattern. One mouse from each group except the letrozole 100 µg group was lost after scanning due to MCAO mortality or morbidity. In addition, one mouse from the letrozole 10 µg group and two mice from the vehicle 10 group did not get recorded due to a technical error for pre‐ and 2‐h time points. The resulting final group numbers for the behavioral study were vehicle 10: n = 4–6, letrozole 10 µg: n = 3–4, vehicle 100: n = 6, letrozole 100 µg: n = 8. At the conclusion of the experiments, mice were deeply anesthetized, and trunk blood was collected for analysis of blood plasma 17β‐estradiol levels to confirm aromatase inhibition using a competitive radioactive binding kit (Diagnostic Products Corporation, USA).

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality was assessed using Shapiro–Wilk test. Homogeneity of variances was verified with Levene's test. Student's unpaired two‐tailed t‐test was employed for statistical analysis of volumes of neuronal damage, aromatase mRNA levels, corner and foot‐fault tests. Two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a post hoc Bonferroni's test to correct for multiple comparisons was used for areas of infarct and immunostaining over time. Repeated measures ANOVA was employed for the ladder rung test as stated. Simple linear regression analysis was used for the correlation of infarct with plasma 17β‐estradiol levels. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0.

RESULTS

Aromatase expression is increased after stroke

Increased aromatase immunostaining was apparent in female ovariectomized SHRSP rats in both the dorsal and ventral peri‐infarct and hippocampus at 24 h post‐MCAO as measured by areas of immunostaining (Figure 1A,B). There was no increased immunostaining observed in the peri‐infarct zones at 2 h, and by 8 days post‐MCAO, there was a non‐significant trend towards increased aromatase immunoreactivity in the peri‐infarct areas. By 30 days post‐MCAO, the increased immunostaining had diminished to virtually zero immunostaining (Figure 1A). These results were corroborated using a semi‐quantitative scoring system (Table 1). Infarct sizes in adjacent sections were comparable across these time‐points indicating that differences in severity of ischemic damage were not responsible for the elevation in aromatase immunoreactivity at 24 h post‐MCAO (Figure 1C). Analysis of qPCR results showed no observable increase in aromatase mRNA in the SHRSP rats at 24 h post‐MCAO in any of the regions analyzed, namely the peri‐infarct cortices and hippocampus; moreover, a trend towards decreased aromatase mRNA was evident (Figure 1D).

No effect of increased aromatase expression on stroke outcome

Plasma 17β‐estradiol levels were significantly higher in Dax‐1 KO mice (n = 6) compared to WT(n = 7) controls (14.76 ± 6.31 vs. 8.69 ± 4.76 pg/mL, respectively, p = 0.037; one‐tailed unpaired t‐test), providing evidence of increased 17β‐estradiol synthesis due to aromatase overexpression. Dax‐1 KO mice had significantly higher aromatase immunoreactivity than WT mice at 24 h post‐MCAO and showed a similar trend over time to SHRSP (Figure 2A). There was no significant difference in infarct volume between the Dax‐1 KO and WT mice (Figure 2B) as measured by T2‐weighted MRI (Figure 2B inset). The relationship between the volume of infarct and the plasma 17β‐estradiol levels was also investigated. The volume of infarct was significantly influenced by the plasma level of 17β‐estradiol in the Dax‐1 KO group with higher plasma levels being associated with smaller lesions and vice versa (r 2 = 0.76, p < 0.05) but not in the WT group (Figure 2C). Sensorimotor deficits were similar between the two groups for both behavioral tests (Figure 2D,E).

No effect of aromatase inhibition on stroke outcome

Plasma 17β‐estradiol levels in all letrozole‐treated male WT mice were in the non‐detectable range of the radioimmunoassay, whereas levels in most of the vehicle‐treated male WT mice were in the detectable range (5.1 ± 0.6 pg/mL), providing evidence of reduced 17β‐estradiol synthesis due to aromatase inhibition. There was no significant difference in infarct volume between WT mice treated with vehicle and low dose (10 µg daily) or higher dose (100 µg daily) of letrozole (Figure 3A,B). Results from the ladder rung test revealed no significant differences between vehicle and letrozole groups at either 2 days or 8 days post‐MCAO (Figure 3C,D).

DISCUSSION

The key findings of the present study are that: (1) aromatase expression is increased in SHRSP rats in both the dorsal and ventral peri‐infarct and hippocampus at 24 h post‐MCAO as measured by areas of immunostaining but not qPCR; (2) there was no improvement in stroke outcome by Dax‐1 KO, despite higher plasma 17β‐estradiol levels and increased brain aromatase immunoreactivity after stroke and; (3) there was no exacerbation on stroke outcome by letrozole, despite decreased plasma 17β‐estradiol levels.

Increased aromatase protein expression after stroke

Increased aromatase protein expression was observed in female SHRSP in the present study at 24 h post‐MCAO but this had lost its statistical significance by 8 days and was diminished by 30 days post‐MCAO. This finding was established using two approaches, quantitative analysis of the area of immunostaining and descriptive semi‐quantitative scoring of immunostaining. These results were reproduced in male Dax‐1 KO mice. There was no difference in the amount of neuronal perikaryal damage among these time points in SHRSP, indicating the increase in aromatase staining at 24 h post‐MCAO was not due to differences in the amount of ischemic damage over time. Increased aromatase immunoreactivity has previously been shown at 2 days after penetrating brain injury in zebra finch, 54 3 and 7 days after global ischemia, 30 and 8 and 10 days after systemic kainate administration in mice or penetrating brain lesions in rats, respectively. 27 Increased aromatase immunostaining levels have been shown at 14 days after focal ischemia 33 and by 30 days after focal ischemia; in the present study, the increased immunostaining in the peri‐infarct zones had diminished. Therefore, the temporal profile of brain aromatase expression is within the neuroprotective time‐frame after stroke according to the evolution of focal ischemic damage. 55 Whether local brain 17β‐estradiol levels changed as a result of increased brain aromatase would be unclear, given the inability to discriminate the source as either brain or systemic 17β‐estradiol. In addition, neuronal as well as astrocytic localization of aromatase have been reported, 27 , 29 , 30 , 32 and whilst its localization with estrogen receptors warrants further investigation, both appear to be critical for neuroprotection. Whereas the above‐mentioned previous studies used normotensive wild types, the present study adheres to STAIR guidelines, which recommend using animals with comorbidities found in human stroke, such as hypertension. 56 In addition, a link between estrogen signaling in the brain and hypertension has been shown. 57 Therefore, the present study improves clinical relevance by using the SHRSP strain, which has genetically determined hypertension, increased sensitivity to stroke 58 and similar pathology to strokes in humans. 59 In addition, the SHRSP have been shown to derive more benefit from endogenous estrogen than normotensive strains. 19

We found increased brain aromatase expression after MCAO in both female and male rodents. This finding is in line with rodent studies that have shown no significant sex difference in brain aromatase expression after brain injury or ischemia, 27 , 30 , 54 but contrasts with a human study where peripheral blood aromatase levels were raised within 24 h of stroke in women but not men. 34

Reasons for the lack of increased aromatase mRNA in the present study are unclear and are contrary to a previous study showing aromatase mRNA upregulation at 24 h post‐penetrating neural injury in the zebra finch, measured by in situ hybridization. 60 This discrepancy is unlikely due to the sensitivity of the assays, given that qPCR involves amplification and is therefore more sensitive than in situ hybridization, and is unlikely due to inaccuracy of sampling of peri‐infarct tissue precluding damaged tissue, given aromatase mRNA was also unaffected in the hippocampus which is not within the MCA territory. As far as we are aware, no studies have measured aromatase mRNA after experimental acute brain injury in rodents, and it is not necessarily surprising that mRNA and protein levels appear contradictory. For example, protein levels reflect rates of both synthesis and degradation and hence a decrease in degradation will increase protein levels. In addition, the efficiency of translation can change to oppose the impact of alterations in mRNA levels. 61 Therefore, whilst our study may point towards a post‐transcriptional regulation of aromatase after stroke, the mechanisms remain unclear and need further study. Therefore, our temporal profile of aromatase expression after stroke in SHRSP is consistent with other studies and occurs within a neuroprotective time‐frame after stroke. Taken together with the anatomical location of the aromatase staining, in the peri‐infarct zones, our studies indicate the potential of therapeutic targeting of aromatase in stroke that is not sex specific. We therefore used two strategies to determine if aromatase is a therapeutic target for stroke, namely aromatase overexpression and reverse strategy aromatase inhibition.

No improvement in stroke outcome with aromatase overexpression

Aromatase overexpression by Dax‐1 KO did not affect ischemic damage or motor function post‐MCAO compared to control WT mice, although there was a trend towards smaller infarcts in the Dax‐1 KO group. The lack of significant effects of Dax‐1 KO versus WT is unlikely to be due to type II error. Firstly, T2‐weighted MRI imaging is an established technique sensitive enough to quantify and detect changes in volumes of ischemic infarcts. 62 We have followed the STAIR guidance of using two outcome measures (infarct and functional test). 56 The functional tests were chosen for their ability to detect deficits at chronic timepoints. In the corner test, animals still exhibit increased turning to the ipsilateral side out to 8 weeks following stroke. 51 Furthermore, the methodology modified for the foot‐fault test was previously validated 52 for measuring chronic impairments in models with ischemic lesions confined to the cortex. Lastly, our studies are unlikely to be underpowered as a priori power calculations, as stated in the methods, showed n = 5 is needed for effect size 30% and SD of 3 to allow 80% power. Whilst SD for infarct size in the WT group was 3, there was increased variability in the Dax‐1 KO group with an SD of 4.4. The increased variability in the Dax‐1 KO group is unexplained but on speculation may be due to the variability of plasma 17β‐estradiol in Dax‐1 KO mice after stroke, given the significant negative correlation between the volume of infarct in the Dax‐1 KO mice and their plasma 17β‐estradiol levels, albeit any causal relationship needs further investigation. The lack of significant correlation between the volume of infarct and their plasma 17β‐estradiol levels in the WT mice is unexplained but likely due to significantly lower 17β‐estradiol levels in the WT mice, potentially below neuroprotective levels. Nevertheless, posteriori power analysis using SD of 4.4 showed n = 7–8 is needed for each group to allow 80% power, which is the n number used for our infarct analysis.

The lack of significant effects may be due to the choice of the model of aromatase overexpression. We are confident that the Dax‐1 KO mice model does indeed overexpress aromatase given their elevated plasma 17β‐estradiol levels, which is consistent with previous work, 47 which we recognize does not necessarily accurately represent brain 17β‐estradiol levels, as well as their elevated brain aromatase expression after stroke observed in the present study. In addition, Dax‐1 KO was previously used as a successful approach to induce neuroprotection in male mice through stem cell delivery in our laboratory. 22 Other mutant mouse models of aromatase overexpression include tissue‐specific doxycycline‐inducible human aromatase transgenic mouse 63 and promoter cytomegalovirus‐driven human aromatase cDNA expression. 64 Like the Dax‐1 KO model, these models exhibit global chronic aromatase overexpression. As far as we are aware, this is the first study to use a transgenic mouse model that overexpresses aromatase in ischemic stroke, albeit the cytomegalovirus‐driven aromatase model of aromatase overexpression 64 was used in a study on ischemic male hearts. Interestingly, aromatase overexpression in that model was shown to exacerbate, not reduce as hypothesized by the authors, cardiac ischemic stress responses. 65 A potential progression for future studies is to use mice with brain‐specific expression of aromatase, 66 , 67 which have not yet been used in brain injury or stroke. Lastly, the lack of effect in infarct in Dax‐1 KO versus WT mice may be because brain aromatase is not overexpressed sufficiently after MCAO in Dax‐1 KO mice to produce neuroprotective local levels of 17β‐estradiol in the brain due to potential sub‐threshold estradiol rises. Alternatively, MCAO may induce brain aromatase overexpression in WT mice that is optimal for 17β‐estradiol neuroprotection. Therefore, we tested whether aromatase inhibition exacerbates stroke outcome in WT mice.

No exacerbation in stroke outcome with aromatase inhibition

Using the reverse strategy of aromatase inhibition, our letrozole results consolidate the Dax‐1 KO findings of no effect of aromatase on infarct or motor function. In the letrozole study, we followed STAIR guidance of using functional outcome measures in the acute phase (1 to 7 days) and chronic phase (beyond 7 days). 56 We opted for the ladder rung test as this offers increased specificity for mice and the ability to distinguish subtle effects on sensorimotor function that may remain hidden using less sensitive tests. 53

The lack of effect of aromatase inhibition in the present study is not in agreement with previous focal ischemia rodent studies. 20 , 21 In those studies, aromatase inhibitors and aromatase knockout significantly exacerbated neuronal death after transient MCAO in females. 20 , 21 Our choices of the aromatase inhibitor or dosing regimen are unlikely to cause this discrepancy, given that we used similar letrozole doses previously used 21 and decreased plasma 17β‐estradiol levels are evidence of aromatase inhibition in our study. Age is unlikely to explain the discrepancy, given that all studies used adult animals. The dosing regimen is also unlikely to result in any discrepancy, as 7 days pretreatment was used previously 20 , 21 , 68 as were doses of 10–150 μg per day. 20 , 21 , 69 Likewise, the assessment time points are unlikely to be the reason for discrepancies. The other studies measured infarct or neuronal damage at 24 h 20 , 21 or 7 days 68 , 69 post‐insult, versus 48 h in our study. Our time points for behavioral analysis are similar to the other studies, which were 1 day 21 or 4–7 days 69 post‐insult compared to 2 and 8 days in our study. Whilst we used 129svJ mice, other studies used C57BL6 mice 20 , 68 or Sprague–Dawley rats. 21 , 69 129svJ mice appear to be more resistant to ischemia than C57BL6 mice. 70 In addition, unlike C57BL6 mice and Sprague–Dawley rats, after an inflammatory stimulus, 129svJ mice lack increased expression of the polyamines putrescine, 71 a neuroprotectant that can be boosted by estrogen. 72 Whether such inherent strain/species differences account for the lack of effect of aromatase in our studies would need further investigation. However, differences in experimental protocols of those studies and the present study probably explain the different results. Firstly, whereas we used the permanent diathermy MCAO model, previous studies used transient intraluminal thread (ILT) MCAO with reperfusion. 20 , 21 We have followed the initial STAIR guidance of using the permanent MCAO model over the transient model. 56 , 73 We chose the diathermy distal model of MCAO specifically because, although revascularization is standard in many stroke settings, pMCAO models closely mirror the pathophysiology 73 in the ischemic stroke patients where reperfusion either does not occur, is not achieved or is delayed or incomplete. 74 For example, permanent occlusion can induce more severe ischemia, with potentially faster infarct expansion, less salvageable penumbra, and more severe secondary damage; therefore, it is important to assess neuroprotection against injury independent of reperfusion, especially given aromatase neuroprotection has not yet been studied in permanent models. However, the mechanisms of aromatase in ischemic stroke are likely mediated through 17β‐estradiol, which improves cerebral blood flow after transient MCAO, 75 , 76 most likely via an influence on endothelial cells, 77 , 78 hence an intact endothelium and restoration of blood flow would be required, which would be present in the ILT model but not the diathermy model. In addition, 17β‐estradiol may act through reducing oxidative stress, which is increased during the reperfusion phase. 79 Therefore, the beneficial effects of estrogen may be more apparent when ischemia is transient, and our results highlight the need for future studies to elucidate the mechanisms of aromatase in ischemic stroke. Therefore, of note, is that our results apply only to permanent ischemia and cannot be generalized to reperfused strokes. Secondly, it may be argued that the lack of significant effects in the present study was due to the use of male mice not deriving as much benefit as females, which were used in the previous studies, 20 , 21 due to sex differences in aromatase function at the cellular level. Female mice exhibited higher aromatase endothelial cell expression and significantly greater vasodilatory response to acetylcholine than males, which was reversed with aromatase gene deletion. 80 In addition, female astrocytes exhibited higher aromatase expression, which sustained less cell death from oxygen‐glucose deprivation than male astrocytes, and again these effects were reversed by aromatase inhibition 81 or knockout. 82 Whether these factors impact stroke outcome is unclear but may imply that male mice might not derive as much benefit from aromatase as females. Nevertheless, endogenous 17β‐estradiol successfully induced neuroprotection in male mice in our previous study 22 and exogenous 17β‐estradiol neuroprotection in males is well‐documented. 83 , 84 , 85 , 86 Indeed, aromatase inhibition or knockout exacerbated damage in males in kainate‐induced brain injury 68 and global ischemia, 30 , 31 , 69 indicating a neuroprotective effect of endogenous 17β‐estradiol in males. These models induce neuronal damage in selectively vulnerable regions such as hilar neurons and CA1 region of the hippocampus. Focal ischemia, on the other hand, has a quite distinct pathophysiology with a spectrum of hypoperfusion leading to infarction in the compromised regions of the MCA territory, namely the cortex and caudate nucleus. It is increasingly apparent that the actions of 17β‐estradiol in cerebral ischemia are complex and may be dependent on the type (permanent/transient or focal/global) of ischemia.

Clearly, one of the limitations of this study includes the use of permanent ischemia, and our results cannot be generalized to reperfused strokes. Therefore, the translational implications of our negative findings have relevance for stratified medicine in stroke, where an aromatase therapeutic strategy would not be beneficial without vessel recanalization status. The results of our permanent occlusion model, therefore, provide insights into the management of stroke patients who do not undergo reperfusion therapy. In addition, translational implications include breast cancer clinicians and patients, providing the knowledge that letrozole would not worsen stroke, should they be at risk of stroke. Whilst, breast cancer patients prescribed aromatase inhibitors anastrozole, letrozole or exemestane did not have an increased stroke risk compared to non‐users, 87 , 88 , 89 as far as we are aware, no human studies have yet investigated whether aromatase inhibitors exacerbate stroke severity, which would be more akin to experimental studies.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, the results from the present study indicate that higher plasma 17β‐estradiol levels can influence infarct size in Dax‐1 KO mice; however, despite increased brain aromatase immunoreactivity after stroke, this does not translate to a significant effect of Dax‐1 KO on infarct or functional recovery following stroke. The lack of effect of aromatase on stroke outcome in the present study is corroborated using letrozole. Possible reasons why increased aromatase did not improve outcome, as discussed above, include potential sub‐threshold estradiol rise, inherent strain/species differences, diathermy versus ILT MCAO, and male mice might not derive as much benefit as females. Therefore, although aromatase overexpression appears in the appropriate location (cortical) and time window (24 h) for neuroprotection, our results provide no evidence for a role of increased aromatase in improving stroke outcome after permanent MCAO in male mice.
