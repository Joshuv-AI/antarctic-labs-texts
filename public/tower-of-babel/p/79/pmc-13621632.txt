Dual-decoder multi-task network with graph attention mechanism for OCT retinal layer and fluid segmentation
Authors: Xuan Dong, Idowu Paul Okuwobi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic macular edema (DME) is a leading cause of visual impairment and blindness among the diabetic population, and leads to abnormal retinal morphology, distorted layer boundaries and blurred structures in optical coherence tomography (OCT) images. Accurate segmentation of retinal layers and pathological fluid regions is critical for clinical diagnosis, but remains challenging due to irregular fluid distribution and low boundary contrast. This study aims to develop an effective segmentation method to jointly extract retinal layers and fluid regions for assisting clinical screening.MethodsA novel dual-decoder multi-task network with graph attention mechanism was proposed for joint segmentation. A primary decoder completed region segmentation, while an auxiliary decoder focused on boundary detection. A cross-decoder spatial attention module was designed for bidirectional feature interaction, and a global reasoning module was embedded to capture long-range anatomical dependencies. Experiments were conducted on the public Duke DME dataset with five-fold subject-independent cross-validation, and paired t-tests were adopted for statistical significance analysis.ResultsThe proposed method outperformed comparative mainstream segmentation models in overall and category-wise evaluation. It achieved stable accuracy in normal retinal layer segmentation and obtained competitive performance in identifying fluid regions, effectively reducing the interference of pathological changes and improving boundary consistency of segmentation results.ConclusionsThe proposed method enables accurate joint segmentation of retinal layers and fluid regions. It provides a reliable automated analysis tool for diabetic macular edema, and can serve as an effective auxiliary reference for routine clinical screening and quantitative evaluation.

Introduction

Diabetic macular edema (DME) is one of the leading causes of visual impairment and blindness among patients with diabetic retinopathy and a severe ocular complication of diabetes [1]. The reported age range of 20–74 years corresponds to the general adult diabetic population, rather than being uniquely limited to DME patients [2]. Notably, diabetic macular edema presents no obvious symptoms in its early stages. However, as the condition progresses, patients may experience impaired vision or even complete vision loss. Therefore, early detection and diagnosis of DME are crucial for preventing blindness. Diabetic macular edema develops when prolonged hyperglycemia damages the retinal microvasculature in the macular region. This damage causes plasma to leak from blood vessels and accumulate in the macula, forming macular edema. Macular edema disrupts the layered structure of the retina, causing phenomena such as boundary breaks. Furthermore, it exerts pathological effects on critical retinal layers including the nerve fiber layer, inner nuclear layer, and outer nuclear layer. By measuring changes in the thickness of these retinal layers and assessing fluid accumulation, physicians can accurately diagnose the severity of DME. Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) is a high-resolution, non-invasive fundus imaging technique capable of clearly displaying the intricate layered structures of the retina [3, 4]. Through OCT imaging, clinicians can identify and diagnose macular edema at an early stage and monitor disease progression. However, in clinical practice, manually segmenting retinal layers and fluid areas relies heavily on expert experience and is highly time-consuming. Therefore, developing an efficient and accurate automated method for segmenting retinal layers and fluid has important clinical application value.

In recent years, deep learning-based automatic segmentation of OCT images has become a research hotspot. He [5] proposed a lightweight retinal layer segmentation network, Lightered, for automatic retinal layer segmentation in OCT images. This network adopts an encoder-decoder structure, with the encoder combining multi-scale feature extraction and Transformer blocks to enhance global reasoning capability. The decoder designs a multi-scale asymmetric attention (MAA) module to retain semantic information. Hu [6], addressing the issues of imaging device differences, irregular fluid region shapes, and blurred boundaries in retinal OCT fluid segmentation, proposed an enhanced U-Net architecture, DAA-UNet. This model integrates dense connections, atrous spatial pyramid pooling (ASPP), and attention gates (AG) on the U-Net basis to improve feature extraction and multi-scale information capturing abilities. However, these methods ignore explicit boundary supervision, which is critical for DME segmentation: blurred or disrupted boundaries caused by fluid often lead to layer breaks and segmentation artifacts in single-task models. Due to the impact of fluid, boundaries are difficult to utilize. Therefore, how to integrate boundary information into the model for accurate segmentation of OCT retinal layers and fluid remains a valuable research topic.

Inspired by multi-task networks [7, 8], we propose GADNet: a dual-decoder framework that jointly learns region segmentation and boundary detection via collaborative training. During each training iteration, the network checks for “inconsistencies” between the two decoders’ outputs. If detected, a penalty is applied via the loss function, compelling the model to learn intrinsically consistent features. This approach enables us to refine and enhance the retinal layers and fluid segments segmented by the primary decoder using the extracted boundaries. To further leverage the extracted boundaries and higher-level semantic information, we introduce a Cross-Decoder Spatial Attention Module (CDSAM) at each stage of both decoders. This module establishes a spatial attention-based information exchange channel between corresponding layers of the two decoders. It enables bidirectional complementarity and enhancement between the global contextual information of the region segmentation task and the boundary information of the boundary segmentation task, thereby overcoming the bottlenecks of single-task approaches and ultimately improving the performance of both tasks simultaneously. Furthermore, to enhance the model’s robustness against fluid interference, we propose a Global Reasoning Module (GRM) based on graph attention mechanisms for learning and inferring global image information. The GRM module strengthens the model’s ability to establish long-range dependencies and infer global contextual information, enabling precise localization of fluid positions and reducing their impact on segmentation results. We designed a joint loss function to train this multi-task network end-to-end. To validate the model’s effectiveness, we conducted experiments on the publicly available Duke DME dataset. Compared to other state-of-the-art network models, our model demonstrates superior performance across all metrics.

Related work

Retinal segmentation methods

The development of OCT retinal segmentation technology can be broadly categorized into two mainstream paradigms: traditional image processing-based methods and modern deep learning-based approaches. Early traditional methods relied on handcrafted features (e.g., A-scan intensity peaks) for boundary detection, which are efficient but fail under speckle noise and pathological changes [9]. In DME cases, massive fluid regions generate numerous false gradient extrema, inevitably causing boundary breaks and layer discontinuity.

To alleviate boundary discontinuity, graph-based shortest path algorithms are introduced for OCT segmentation [10]. This paradigm constructs a weighted directed graph over the entire image, where pixels are treated as nodes and spatial adjacencies as edges. Edge weights are defined by intensity and gradient cues, and the optimal global path is solved to obtain continuous layer boundaries. These methods formulate segmentation as a global optimization problem with inherent boundary smoothness, and later extensions added shape constraints and multi-scale analysis for multi-layer segmentation [11]. Nevertheless, these graph-based methods still suffer from obvious drawbacks: they heavily depend on manually designed weight functions and lack adaptive modeling for pathological structures. When severe fluid completely obscures layer boundary signals, shortest path algorithms tend to traverse fluid regions indiscriminately, resulting in irreversible segmentation errors.

Active contour models and level set methods introduced shape priors to address graph search limitations, adapting to complex retinal topology via contour evolution [12, 13]. However, they are highly sensitive to initial contour initialization and easily fall into local optimal solutions, making them unreliable for large-variation DME OCT images with complex fluid distributions.

With the prosperity of deep learning in medical image analysis, encoder–decoder architectures represented by U-Net have become the dominant framework for automated OCT segmentation [14]. Benefiting from skip connections and hierarchical feature fusion, U-Net and its variants excel at capturing both shallow detail and high-level semantic information, and have been widely adopted as the backbone of retinal segmentation models. ReLayNet pioneered end-to-end deep learning for this task [15], while subsequent works adopted Transformer self-attention for global context modeling [16]. Despite the progress, existing single-task CNN and Transformer-based methods still have inherent limitations in DME segmentation: most of them only perform isolated region segmentation without explicit boundary supervision, and lack effective interactive fusion between semantic segmentation and edge detail features. When facing blurred boundaries and structural disruption caused by fluid, these methods inevitably produce broken edges and semantic misclassification.

Graph Convolutional Networks

Standard CNNs’ fixed local receptive fields fail to model long-range anatomical dependencies, a critical bottleneck for segmenting fluid-disrupted retinal structures. To compensate for this deficiency, Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) have been widely introduced to capture global contextual information by propagating features on non-Euclidean graph structures.

Existing GCN-based segmentation methods can be divided into two categories. The first is a two-stage pipeline: CNN generates coarse segmentation results first, then the image is transformed into a graph with superpixels as graph nodes, and GCN is applied to refine segmentation masks and suppress noise [17]. The major limitation of such two-stage schemes is the lack of end-to-end trainability, which restricts joint optimization of CNN feature extraction and graph reasoning. The second category embeds graph convolution modules into CNN backbones in an end-to-end manner. Meng et al. [18] integrated graph convolution into a U-Net architecture and explicitly constructed anatomical topology graphs to model adjacency and sequential constraints between retinal layers. By projecting CNN features onto graph nodes and updating node features via graph convolution, the model enforces inherent retinal anatomical priors and mitigates boundary disruption caused by fluid. Another typical graph reasoning framework treats each spatial position of CNN feature maps as an independent node to form a fully connected graph, and aggregates global context through graph convolution to enhance pixel-level feature representation [19].

Although GCN-based methods improve global reasoning ability, they still have an obvious shortcoming: the connection weights between nodes are fixed by predefined graph topology and cannot be adaptively adjusted based on feature similarity and pathological variation. All neighboring nodes contribute equally to feature aggregation, which limits the model’s flexibility in modeling complex pathological patterns in DME.

Graph Attention Networks (GAT) introduced adaptive node-wise attention [20], but vanilla GAT uses globally shared computation, limiting its ability to model heterogeneous retinal anatomy [21]. To address this limitation, GATv2 revises the order of linear transformation and non-linear activation, realizing fully dynamic node-wise attention with stronger expressive power.

Targeting these gaps, this paper proposes GADNet, a boundary-aware multi-task network tailored for DME OCT segmentation. First, we construct a dual-decoder multi-task framework, where boundary labels are derived from segmentation masks via morphological dilation and erosion to provide explicit edge supervision. Second, we design the CDSAM module to enable adaptive spatial attention interaction between two decoder branches, realizing complementary fusion of global semantic and local boundary features. Third, we develop a GATv2-based GRM module to perform fully dynamic global graph reasoning, which effectively models long-range retinal topology and suppresses the negative interference of fluid. The proposed GADNet deliberately addresses the core limitations of previous methods and achieves more accurate and robust joint segmentation of retinal layers and fluid in DME OCT images.

Segmentation network

We adopt a U-Net variant as the backbone, which inherits the classic encoder-decoder structure with skip connections to fuse shallow spatial details and deep semantic information. As shown in Fig. 2, the network is based on a shared encoder and two parallel decoders (the region segmentation decoder and the boundary decoder). The shared encoder has four stages: each stage comprises a DoubleConv block (two 3 × 3 convolutions) followed by 2 × 2 max pooling, progressively down sampling feature maps and expanding channel dimensions. The feature maps extracted by the encoder at each stage are passed through skip connections to the corresponding layers of both decoders, allowing the fusion of deep semantic information and shallow spatial details. The two parallel decoder paths perform different segmentation tasks, and different attention modules are introduced at the deepest layer (the bottleneck layer) to enhance the performance of each task. The layer segmentation path consists of the GRM module (described in section “Global Reasoning Module (GRM)”) and four upsampling modules, which progressively restore the resolution of the feature maps through the Upsample\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:2\times\:2$$\end{document} operation and the DoubleCov module, while fusing the skip connection features from the encoder. At the bottleneck layer, the path introduces the graph-attention-based GRM module to capture long-range dependencies between features, enhancing the model’s reasoning of global context, which is especially helpful for dealing with fluid regions. The boundary path consists of four upsampling modules, which also progressively restore the resolution of the feature maps through the Upsample\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:2\times\:2$$\end{document} operation and the DoubleCov module, while fusing the skip connection features from the encoder.

In addition, to leverage the boundary features extracted by the network along with the original high-level semantic information, we introduce the CDSAM module. This module is placed at the corresponding layers of both decoders and establishes a bridge for information interaction, allowing the two parallel segmentation tasks to enhance each other in real-time and bidirectionally. Specifically, the layer segmentation path (which has stronger global context information) can pass its features to the boundary path to help with better boundary localization. The boundary path (which has more precise local detail information) can pass its features to the layer segmentation path to help optimize the segmentation of boundaries in the region.

Global Reasoning Module (GRM)

Global reasoning capability is crucial for segmenting OCT retinal layers and fluid. Inspired by global reasoning networks [19, 22], we propose a GRM module based on graph attention mechanisms to localize fluid positions, thereby achieving more accurate segmentation of retinal layers and fluid. We apply the GRM module to the bottleneck section of the layer path. As shown in Fig. 3, the core idea of GRM module is to project the original feature map into graph space to transform it into node features, then utilize the GATv2 to learn and aggregate the global information of these node features. The module consists of three components: Feature Projection, Graph Attention-based Global Reasoning, and Feature Inverse Projection and Residual Fusion. Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{L}}_{Dice}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{L}}_{CE}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{L}}_{FFL}$$\end{document} represent the Dice Loss [23], Cross-Entropy Loss [24], and Focal Frequency Loss 25], respectively. The terms λ1, λ2, λ3 are the corresponding weights for these loss functions. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\:p}_{i}$$\end{document} denotes the predicted probability of a pixel x belonging to class i, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{g}_{i}$$\end{document} represents the ground truth. Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{B}\mathrm{W}\mathrm{L}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{L}}_{Tversky}$$\end{document} represent the BCEWithLogitsLoss [26] and TverskyLoss [27], respectively. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\:p}_{i}$$\end{document} denotes the predicted probability of a pixel belonging to class i, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{g}_{i}$$\end{document} is the ground truth. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document} represents the sigmoid function. The terms TP, FP and FN represent True Positives, False Positives, and False Negatives, respectively. The parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:$$\end{document}and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document} are used to balance the penalties for False Positives (FP) and False Negatives (FN). A higher value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:\:$$\end{document}increases the penalty on FPs, which encourages the model to be more “conservative,” reducing the instances of misclassifying the background as the target. Conversely, a higher value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document} increases the penalty on FNs, prompting the model to be more “aggressive” in identifying all potential target regions to reduce missed detections. Since the area of the retinal layers to be segmented is significantly smaller than the background, increasing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document} can substantially improve model performance. Experiment

Dataset

In this work, we utilized the publicly available Duke DME dataset [28], which contains 110 OCT B-scan images (496 × 768 pixels) acquired via spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) from 10 subjects diagnosed with diabetic macular edema (DME). The dataset provides expert manual annotations for seven retinal anatomical layers for all images, as well as interlaminar fluid region annotations for relevant slices. To ensure experimental rigor, eliminate potential data leakage, and obtain statistically robust evaluation results, we adopt a 5-fold subject-independent cross-validation strategy in this study. All data splits are performed strictly at the subject level, rather than random image-level division. Specifically, the 10 enrolled subjects are evenly divided into 5 folds with 2 subjects per fold. In each cross-validation iteration, 8 subjects are assigned to the training and validation sets, while the remaining 2 held-out subjects form the independent test set. All B-scan images from a single subject are exclusively assigned to one subset within each fold, with no subject overlap between the training/validation and test sets, which effectively prevents overestimation of the model’s generalization performance caused by data leakage.

Notably, for interlaminar fluid annotations, only some of the enrolled subjects have fluid regions, and among these subjects, only a subset of OCT B-scan images contain valid interlaminar fluid regions and corresponding ground truth annotations. Therefore, when calculating the segmentation performance of interlaminar fluid, we only include B-scan images with complete fluid ground truth. This evaluation strategy strictly follows the inherent annotation distribution of the dataset, avoids invalid metric calculation on images without fluid regions, and ensures the quantitative results accurately reflect the model’s fluid segmentation capability without introducing additional bias.

Experimental settings

The proposed GADNet was implemented using the PyTorch framework and trained on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU. The network was optimized using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.00012, which was decayed by a factor of 0.6 every 30 epochs during training. For the Duke DME dataset, data augmentation strategies including horizontal flipping, random translation, and elastic deformation were applied to enhance the diversity of training samples and improve the model’s generalization ability. All OCT images were resized to a uniform resolution of 496 × 496 pixels to balance computational efficiency and spatial detail preservation.

Evaluation metrics

In this work, the following evaluation metrics were used to compare the performance of different methods. Dice similarity coefficient (DSC)、Intersection over Union (IoU)、Class-wise Pixel Accuracy(PA) and F1-Score were employed to assess the similarity between ground truth and segmentation results. The formulas are as follows:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:DSC=\frac{2|A\cap\:B|}{\left|A\right|+\left|B\right|}$$\end{document}13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:PA=\frac{|A\cap\:B|}{\left|B\right|}$$\end{document}14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:IoU=\frac{|A\cap\:B|}{|A\cup\:B|}$$\end{document}15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:F1=\frac{2|A\cap\:B|}{\left|A\right|+\left|B\right|}$$\end{document}

A represents the predicted region, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{B}$$\end{document} corresponds to the ground truth. Specifically, DSC and IoU are core metrics to quantify the spatial overlap between the predicted region and the ground truth, which are highly sensitive to the segmentation accuracy of foreground regions. Class-wise PA calculates the proportion of correctly classified pixels in all ground truth pixels of the target category, reflecting the model’s detection capability for the region of interest. F1-Score, as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, balances the model’s precision and recall performance, and is mathematically equivalent to DSC in the single-category binary segmentation scenario. For this multi-class retinal and fluid segmentation task, we compute the above metrics for each category independently, and further calculate the mean value across all categories to comprehensively evaluate the model’s overall and category-specific performance.

Results

As shown in Tables 1 and 2, through 5-fold subject-independent cross-validation, the model obtains an average Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) of 81.2 ± 1.7%, an average Intersection over Union (IoU) of 69.4 ± 2.8%, an average Pixel Accuracy (PA) of 83.0 ± 1.4%, and an average F1-Score of 81.2 ± 1.7%. The model achieves stable and high-precision segmentation for normal retinal layers including ILM, NFL-IPL, and INL, and for the fluid region, the core biomarker for DME diagnosis, it achieves a DSC of 58.2 ± 4.4% and an IoU of 37.9 ± 7.3%, realizing effective identification and segmentation of pathologically abnormal regions. Fluid segmentation shows a larger standard deviation due to high inter-subject heterogeneity in fluid morphology and the small sample size of pathological regions, which is common in subject-independent validation on clinical dataset.

The segmentation accuracy of the fluid region in this study is significantly lower than that of normal retinal layers, which is limited by the inherent technical challenges of DME fluid segmentation and the experimental settings. As a pathologically abnormal region, fluid has no fixed anatomical location or morphological pattern, and is often accompanied by distortion of retinal structure and blurred boundaries, with an extremely low grayscale contrast with surrounding normal tissues, which inherently brings extremely high segmentation difficulty. Meanwhile, the limited number of fluid samples in the Duke DME dataset makes it difficult for the model to fully learn the general features of fluid with different phenotypes. In addition, in the task setting of joint segmentation of retinal layers and fluid, model optimization will be partially biased towards normal retinal layers with higher pixel proportion and more stable features, which also has a certain impact on the segmentation accuracy of fluid. Although the current segmentation accuracy of fluid has not yet reached the ideal level for clinical diagnosis, it can already meet the needs of preliminary DME screening and automated quantitative evaluation, and can provide effective auxiliary reference for clinical work. In follow-up research, we will further improve the segmentation accuracy of fluid by introducing large-sample multi-center clinical datasets, designing a small target feature enhancement module, and incorporating prior knowledge of retinal anatomy, to promote the development of the model towards clinical practicality.

Comparison results

To comprehensively verify the segmentation performance and algorithmic advancement of the proposed GADNet, two categories of representative segmentation models are selected as comparison baselines. All comparative experiments are conducted under identical datasets, training configurations, and a 5-fold subject-independent cross-validation strategy, ensuring the fairness and reliability of the comparison results. To guarantee reproducibility and impartiality of the benchmark, all comparative models are implemented based on the PyTorch framework with unified key hyperparameter settings. The Adam optimizer is adopted with an initial learning rate of 0.00012, and the learning rate decays by a factor of 0.6 every 30 epochs. All models maintain consistent training epochs, data augmentation strategies, and hardware environments, and are trained on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU. The first category is the classic general baseline U-Net [14] in the field of medical image segmentation. As a foundational framework for medical image segmentation, U-Net adopts a symmetric encoder–decoder structure with skip connections, serving as a widely accepted golden standard for various medical segmentation tasks. The introduction of U-Net can intuitively validate the performance improvement of GADNet over the classic general segmentation framework, and further highlight the performance gains brought by the dual-decoder structure, CDSAM module and GRM module in the joint segmentation task of retinal layers and fluid regions.

The second category consists of state-of-the-art OCT-specific segmentation methods for retinal images, including RelayNet [15], MGUNet [22], LightReSeg [5] and DAAUNet [6]. These methods are specially designed for anatomical structure segmentation or fluid detection of retinal OCT images, and have been widely adopted as comparison baselines in peer studies. Selecting these dedicated methods can validate the task adaptability and performance superiority of GADNet under professional retinal segmentation frameworks, especially its capability to jointly process normal layered anatomical structures and pathological abnormal regions. As shown in Tables 1 and 2, experimental results demonstrate that the proposed GADNet outperforms all comparison baselines in terms of overall segmentation performance and segmentation accuracy of each target region. Benefiting from the designed dual-decoder architecture, GADNet achieves an average DSC of 81.2 ± 1.7% and an average IoU of 69.4 ± 2.8%. Compared with the general baseline U-Net, the respective improvements are 2.3% points and 3.1% points. Compared with RelayNet, the best-performing dedicated baseline, the improvements reach 3.7% points and 4.7% points, which fully verifies the overall segmentation superiority of the proposed method.

For the segmentation of normal retinal layers such as ILM, NFL-IPL and INL, GADNet achieves the optimal accuracy in most layered structures. This is mainly attributed to the Global Reasoning Module (GRM) embedded in the layer path, which models long-range feature dependencies, effectively captures spatial correlations and continuity constraints between different retinal layers, and guarantees the overall connectivity of segmentation results. It also outperforms single-decoder models such as U-Net, which only transmit low-level details through skip connections, and compensates for the deficiencies of some dedicated baselines in modeling inter-layer consistency.

As the core biomarker for DME diagnosis, the fluid segmentation result of GADNet reaches a DSC of 58.2 ± 4.4%, which is 5.5% points higher than U-Net and at least 2.6% points higher than all other dedicated baselines. This prominent advantage benefits from the synergy of GRM and Cross-Decoder Spatial Attention Module (CDSAM). GRM enhances the global feature extraction capability of the network to accurately locate fluid regions and capture the overall distribution of fluid. CDSAM constructs a spatial attention-based information interaction channel between corresponding stages of dual decoders, enabling bidirectional complementation and enhancement between global context from region segmentation and local detail from boundary segmentation. This strategy effectively addresses the segmentation challenges of fluid with irregular morphology and blurred boundaries, which is a unique advantage unavailable in other comparison models. U-Net adopts a single-decoder structure and cannot realize information complementation between multiple tasks. In contrast, RelayNet, MGUNet and other dedicated methods are either optimized only for normal retinal layered structures or lack specialized boundary enhancement and global localization mechanisms.

Vanilla U-Net outperforms some dedicated OCT models because its concise structure avoids overfitting on the small Duke DME dataset, whereas more complex dedicated models suffer from poor generalization. In contrast, GADNet’s dual-decoder design maintains parameter efficiency while introducing task-specific enhancements.

Ablation study

The ablation study follows the framework of 5-fold subject-independent cross-validation to avoid data leakage, and a one-tailed paired Student’s t-test is performed to verify the statistical significance of performance improvements (where * denotes p < 0.05, indicating the proposed method significantly outperforms the control method). The core purpose is to validate the independent effect, complementary value, and synergistic enhancement capability of three core components: the Graph Reasoning Module (GRM), dual decoder structure, and Cross-Dimensional Self-Attention Module (CDSAM), with the experimental results shown in Tables 4 and 5.

As shown in Tables 4 and 5, the final proposed method fully integrating the three core components achieves the optimal comprehensive performance on all 8 segmentation categories, including 7 retinal anatomical layers and fluid, with a mean Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) of 81.2 ± 1.7% and a mean Pixel Accuracy (PA) of 83.0 ± 1.4%, corresponding to an absolute improvement of 2.1 and 2.5% points over the baseline model, respectively. The improvements of the overall average metrics all pass the statistical significance test of p < 0.05, which fully verifies the rationality and effectiveness of the multi-component fusion design. In the single-component effectiveness validation, as shown in Tables 4 and 5, Proposed(a), which only adds the GRM module to the baseline, increases the mean DSC from 79.1% to 80.4% and the mean PA from 80.5% to 82.5%, and achieves statistically significant improvements in the DSC, Intersection over Union (IoU), PA, and F1-Score of the ILM layer, the IoU of the INL layer, as well as the PA and F1-Score of fluid. This module breaks through the inherent local receptive field limitation of traditional convolution operations through the graph attention-based global reasoning mechanism, and can model the global topological correlation between retinal layers in the whole OCT image, as well as the spatial position dependence between fluid and surrounding normal retinal tissues. It achieves more accurate feature capture especially for shallow retinal structures with clear boundaries and strong inter-layer positional correlation such as the ILM, as well as tiny fluid with small volume and scattered distribution, thus delivering significant performance gains in the corresponding categories.

As shown in Tables 4 and 5, Proposed(b), which introduces the dual decoder structure based on the baseline, achieves a mean DSC of 79.5% and a mean PA of 80.7%, with significant improvements in the PA and F1-Score of the ISE layer, the DSC and IoU of the OS-RPE layer, as well as the DSC and IoU of fluid. The core design idea of the dual decoder is to completely decouple the segmentation tasks of normal retinal layers and fluid, aiming at the huge differences in foreground proportion and morphological features between the two types of targets. It avoids the mutual interference and gradient conflict between the two types of targets with extremely imbalanced foreground proportion during feature learning, allowing the two decoders to focus on the continuous boundary segmentation of normal layers and the irregular region recognition of fluid respectively, thus bringing more prominent performance optimization for deep retinal layers near the RPE and fluid with extremely high segmentation difficulty.

As shown in Tables 4 and 5, Proposed(c), which adds the CDSAM module to the dual decoder framework, further lifts the mean DSC to 80.6% and the mean PA to 82.4%, and achieves significant improvements in all evaluation metrics of the ILM layer, the DSC of the INL layer, as well as the DSC, IoU, PA, and F1-Score of fluid. The CDSAM module combines the advantages of channel attention and spatial attention, can adaptively enhance the feature channels and spatial regions that play a key role in the segmentation task, suppress the interference of irrelevant background and noise features, further amplify the feature learning advantage brought by the decoupling design of the dual decoder, and especially strengthen the feature representation ability of retinal layer boundaries and tiny fluid, thus further consolidating and expanding the performance gains on the ILM layer and fluid. The final full method integrating all three core components achieves statistically significant improvements in most core metrics of the three key retinal layers (ILM, NFL-IPL, INL) and fluid, which fully demonstrates that the three modules form a complete optimization link from global modeling, task decoupling to feature enhancement, with excellent complementarity and synergistic effect.

As shown in Tables 4 and 5, the non-significant results (p > 0.05) of some metrics are not caused by the invalidation of the proposed modules. The core reasons are as follows: the large annotation variance and high metric fluctuation for deep retinal layers and fluid weaken the statistical significance; the modules bring limited marginal gains for layer structures with simple morphology and baseline performance close to the ceiling; the small sample size of 5-fold cross-validation reduces the statistical test power, which is prone to false negative results. In summary, this ablation study fully validates the effectiveness of our proposed components, which achieve more remarkable improvements on fluid and low-contrast retinal layers with high segmentation difficulty and great clinical value, accurately matching the clinical requirements of DME segmentation.

Discussion

Visualization of segmentation results

To analyze the challenges in DME segmentation tasks, we have identified three typical segmentation difficulties, as shown in Fig. 6. The first, second, and third rows display images illustrating three segmentation challenges. In each row: Fig. a is the Original Image, Fig. b is the Ground truth, Fig. c is the Proposed result, and Figs. d, e, and f are local enlargements of the yellow-boxed regions respectively. First, low-contrast boundaries represent a prevalent issue, as demonstrated in the first row of Fig. 6. Due to the histological similarity between adjacent retinal layers (e.g., the inner nuclear layer INL and outer plexiform layer OPL), their interface often appears as a blurred, weakly signaled dark line in OCT images. Models focused solely on local information are highly prone to misclassification in such regions, leading to erroneous fusion of retinal layers. This necessitates robust contextual reasoning capabilities to infer the true location of blurred boundaries by integrating broader structural information. Second, extensive pathological changes cause severe structural deformation. As shown in rows 2 of Fig. 6, massive fluid accumulation can compress, displace, and completely disrupt the original anatomical topology of the retina. This is critical limitation for models relying on fixed spatial priors or consistent shape features, as conventional structural assumptions become entirely invalid, leading to catastrophic segmentation failure. Therefore, models must possess robust capabilities to adaptively comprehend the global structure distorted by pathology in a data-driven manner. Finally, the precise detection of minute fluid poses significant challenges, as illustrated in rows 3 of Fig. 6. These extremely small, isolated interlaminar fluid collections closely resemble the inherent speckle noise of OCT imaging in both size and grayscale. This creates a dilemma for the model: increasing sensitivity to capture these faint signals risks misclassifying substantial noise as fluid (false positives), while excessive noise suppression may filter out genuine micro-fluid as noise, as demonstrated by the missed detections labeled “Proposed” in rows 3 of Fig. 6. This necessitates achieving a delicate balance between sensitivity and specificity.

To visually assess the segmentation performance of our proposed model and gain deeper insight into its architectural advantages, we present qualitative comparison results for three representative samples in Fig. 7. The first, third, and fifth rows represent Sample 1, Sample 2, and Sample 3, respectively. The second, fourth, and sixth rows show enlarged images of their yellow-boxed sections. These selected samples exemplify the inherent challenges discussed earlier, posing rigorous tests for the model’s pathological recognition and structural preservation capabilities. Sample 1, located in an area with no primary fluid but complex interlaminar texture, challenges the model’s precision and specificity, demonstrating the low-contrast boundary issue mentioned previously. Our approach successfully generates smooth, continuous, and well-defined boundaries for all layers (e.g., NFL-IPL, INL, OPL) with smooth, continuous, and well-defined boundaries that closely match the ground truth (Fig. 7b). This high precision is achieved through our dual-decoder architecture. The boundary decoder, focused on pixel-level details, refines the segmentation results from the main decoder, effectively avoiding “leakage” and inter-layer adhesion issues common in other models. In contrast, DAAUnet (Fig. 7h) and RelayNet (Fig. 7e) produce erroneous, noise-like segmentation artifacts, misclassifying portions of healthy layers. This exposes a fundamental flaw in DAAUnet’s “feature stacking” design: its single-path architecture suffers from feature conflicts when handling both layer segmentation and fluid segmentation tasks, resulting in poor specificity and misclassification of normal tissue. U-Net (Fig. 7d) also performed poorly in boundary smoothness and continuity, struggling with complex textures. Sample 2 demonstrates moderate structural distortion caused by interlamellar fluid (dark blue). Models must accurately detect and localize fluid regions while preserving the structural integrity and continuity of adjacent retinal layers (e.g., INL) despite fluid interference. Our model (c) excellently accomplishes both tasks simultaneously. It not only successfully detects the fluid but also maintains a smooth and continuous INL layer segmentation above the fluid, consistent with the ground truth (b). This is achieved through our dual decoder synergy mechanism. The primary decoder identifies fluid-filled areas, while the boundary decoder ensures layer contours remain intact. The CDSAM module enables information exchange between these tasks, allowing the model to locate fluid while preserving layer boundaries. Other models fail to balance both objectives. LightReSeg (Fig. 7g) nearly completely missed fluid-filled regions, as its global attention mechanism clearly cannot localize pathological features of this scale. Although U-Net (Fig. 7d) vaguely detected partial fluid accumulation, its INL layer completely ruptured at the fluid site, clearly demonstrating that its single network path cannot segment fluid while preserving surrounding anatomical structures. Sample 3 presents an upgraded version of Sample 2, where the massive fluid accumulation (Fig. 7a) has compressed, distorted, and completely disrupted the normal retinal anatomy. Even under such extreme pathological stress, our model’s segmentation results maintained exceptional continuity and smooth boundaries, accurately reflecting the true, deformed anatomy. This resilience is directly attributable to our Global Reasoning Module (GRM). By learning long-range pixel dependencies, the GRM adaptively infers global structure from the data itself, rather than relying on fragile local cues or fixed assumptions. This sample triggers catastrophic failures in other models, exposing a fatal weakness in MGUNet (Fig. 7f). MGUNet’s core design relies on a rigid anatomical prior based on a visual disc GCN. This fixed template completely fails when confronted with severe deformation in the macular region, resulting in poor robustness. U-Net (Fig. 7d) similarly fails, with its segmentation results collapsing into a series of breaks, adhesions, and jagged edges, demonstrating its complete inability to maintain structural consistency. In summary, our model resolves the “feature conflict” issue through its core dual-decoder architecture (separating regional and boundary tasks). The CDSAM module and loss function compel these decoders to collaborate, leveraging boundary details to refine regional segmentation. This proves critical in Sample 2, enabling the model to detect effusions while preserving layer integrity. Furthermore, as demonstrated in Sample 3, the GRM module provides the global structural reasoning necessary to overcome severe deformations. These architectural strengths directly address core challenges in OCT segmentation, proving our model’s exceptional accuracy and robustness when processing complex clinical images.

The qualitative visualization results in Fig. 8 are fully consistent with the quantitative ablation findings, providing intuitive verification of the effectiveness of each core module in the proposed GADNet.

The baseline U-Net model (column c) exhibits prominent segmentation errors across all scenarios. In the mild DME sample, blurring between retinal layer boundaries and misclassification of fluid regions are observed. In the moderate-to-severe DME sample with retinal deformation, the model fails to maintain anatomical topology, showing severe layer discontinuity and large-scale misclassification of fluid as the OPL (yellow). The magnified views reveal extensive pixel-level noise and incorrect labeling. Even in the normal retinal sample, thin retinal layers are broken and boundaries are mislocalized, confirming the baseline’s limitations in modeling global topology and fine boundary information. The addition of the GRM module (column d) significantly improves performance. Layer continuity is restored in the deformed moderate-to-severe DME sample, with anatomical topology closely matching the ground truth. Layer breaks in the other two samples are also largely repaired, with only minor misclassification remaining at fluid edges. This directly demonstrates that the global reasoning capability of GRM explicitly models retinal anatomical priors, alleviating segmentation errors caused by local deformation and speckle noise in OCT images.

The dual-decoder baseline model (column e) further enhances inter-layer boundary discrimination, with clearer separation between fluid and surrounding tissues. The scope of fluid misclassification in the moderate-to-severe DME sample is greatly reduced, verifying that collaborative learning of layer segmentation and boundary detection provides complementary information for the segmentation task. Building on this, adding the CDSAM module (column f) improves fine structure segmentation accuracy. Magnified views show fluid edges align more closely with the ground truth, thin retinal layer boundaries are smoother, and pixel-level misclassifications are significantly reduced. This confirms that the cross-decoder spatial attention mechanism enables efficient bidirectional interaction of dual-branch features, enhancing semantic consistency between layer segmentation and boundary localization. The complete proposed GADNet (column g) achieves the most accurate segmentation results closest to the manual ground truth. In both normal and DME-fluid regions, the model maintains full continuity of retinal layers, precisely locates fine inter-layer boundaries, and accurately segments irregularly shaped fluid regions with blurred edges. Magnified views show that GADNet’s predictions have the highest pixel-level consistency with the ground truth, with no obvious breaks or misclassifications. These results fully demonstrate the effectiveness and clinical application potential of the proposed method for DME OCT image segmentation.

Although our proposed model demonstrates outstanding overall performance in addressing various segmentation challenges (as shown in Fig. 6), it still has limitations. The first row of Fig. 9 illustrates two key limitations our model currently faces when dealing with extreme cases caused by fluid, with the second and third rows showing local magnified images of the yellow-boxed and red-boxed regions respectively. From the second row of Fig. 9, we can observe the model’s insufficient sensitivity in detecting minute fluid. The enlarged view reveals a small, weakly signal cystic effusion present in the ground truth label (2b), which our model (2c) completely missed. This failure exposes an inherent challenge in balancing sensitivity and specificity within the model. To suppress speckle noise prevalent in OCT images and avoid misclassifying noise as fluid (false positives), the model may have learned to ignore weak features with signal intensities similar to noise. Although our dual-decoder and CDSAM modules aim to enhance sensitivity to fine details, their detection capabilities remain suboptimal for such extreme low-signal-to-noise-ratio scenarios, leading to the omission of early or minute pathological signals. The third row of Fig. 9 reveals a more severe failure mode: semantic misclassification occurs when fluid cause severe structural deformation. In this region, a large fluid collection (visible in the main image) has significantly compressed and distorted the normal retinal anatomy. The ground truth label (3b) indicates this area as the purple ONL-ISM layer. However, our model (3c) incorrectly segments it as the yellow OPL layer. This case demonstrates that while the Global Reasoning Module (GRM) can handle a certain degree of structural deformation, it fails when deformation becomes extreme enough to severely disrupt the morphological and textural features of a layer (ONL-ISM), rendering them atypical. It no longer relies on the disrupted structural context but erroneously depends on ambiguous local textures, misidentifying them as another layer (OPL) with similar appearance. This indicates the model’s global structural prior knowledge is insufficient to handle such cases where anatomical topology is completely disrupted. In summary, this complex failure case in Fig. 9 highlights two core limitations of our model when addressing fluid: (1) Insufficient sensitivity, manifested as missed detection of minute fluid in the yellow box magnification (second row). (2) Insufficient robustness, manifested as semantic misclassification in the red box magnification (third row), where the model erroneously identifies one layer as another under severe structural deformation induced by the fluid. Future work will address these issues through the following research directions: (1) Introducing more advanced loss functions or data augmentation strategies to enhance the model’s sensitivity to small targets and difficult-to-distinguish samples; (2) Exploring more robust global modeling approaches, such as graph neural networks (GNNs) incorporating anatomical topology constraints, to prevent structural semantic confusion under extreme deformations.

Conclusion

This work addresses the segmentation challenges in diabetic macular edema (DME) OCT images, particularly the blurred and discontinuous retinal layer boundaries caused by pathological changes, by proposing a novel multi-task deep learning network named GADNet. The core innovation of the network lies in its dual-decoder architecture, which jointly performs retinal layer and fluid segmentation and layer boundary detection. By leveraging explicit, fine-grained boundary priors from the auxiliary boundary detection task, the model effectively guides and corrects boundary breaks and errors in the primary segmentation task caused by fluid interference. Specifically, we design a dual-decoder architecture to concurrently segment regions and boundaries, with a consistency loss function to constrain the alignment between the two tasks and refine segmentation results using clear boundary information. To enable efficient information fusion between the two tasks, we further propose the Cross-Decoder Spatial Attention Module (CDSAM), which dynamically interacts high-level semantic information from the region segmentation branch with low-level detail features from the boundary detection branch at each decoder layer. This bidirectional information enhancement mechanism overcomes the limitations of single-task learning. Additionally, to address the disruption of local features caused by severe fluid accumulation, we introduce the Global Reasoning Module (GRM) based on graph attention mechanisms. The module learns long-range dependencies between pixels to infer global structure, thereby significantly improving the model’s reasoning ability for complex pathological morphologies and enhancing the completeness of segmentation results.

Extensive experiments conducted on the publicly available Duke DME dataset demonstrate that the proposed GADNet outperforms other state-of-the-art segmentation models across all performance metrics. Nevertheless, in-depth analysis of failed cases reveals two key limitations of the current model: first, the model may fail to detect fluid regions when the signal-to-noise ratio of fluid is extremely low due to insufficient sensitivity to weak signals; second, the model’s global reasoning capability may break down when fluid-induced structural distortions become overly extreme, leading to reduced robustness and semantic misclassifications. In summary, the proposed GADNet provides a powerful and reliable solution for precise automated segmentation of DME by effectively integrating boundary features with global contextual information. The identified failure cases under extreme pathological conditions also point to clear directions for future research, including further enhancing the model’s sensitivity to weak signals while maintaining its robustness against severe structural distortions.
