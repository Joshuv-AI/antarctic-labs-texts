Intercropping ajowan (Trachyspermum ammi L.) and Securigera (Securigera securidaca L.) enhances soil quality, biodiversity, and land use efficiency under integrated phosphorus management
Authors: Raziyeh Karami, Alireza Yadavi, Mohsen Movahhedi Dehnavi, Amin Salehi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and AimsIntercropping is a sustainable agricultural system that provides numerous ecological benefits, including enhanced biodiversity, improved soil quality, and increased productivity. This study assessed the ecological advantages of intercropping Ajowan (Trachyspermum ammi L.) and Securigera ( Securigera securidaca L.) under various phosphorus fertilizer treatments and planting ratios.MethodsA two-year field experiment (2021–2022) was conducted in Mamasani County, Iran, using a randomized complete block design with three replications. The treatments included five types of fertilizers: 100 kg ha− 1 of triple superphosphate (TSP), 50 kg ha− 1 of TSP with phosphate solubilizing bacteria (PSB), 50 kg ha− 1 of TSP with arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF), 50 kg ha− 1 of TSP with PSB + AMF, and a control. The intercropping ratios tested were 1:1 (A₁S₁), 1:2 (A₁S₂), 2:1 (A₂S₁), as well as sole cropping for each species. Measurements included root colonization, microbial respiration, phosphatase activity, seed yield, land equivalent ratio (LER) and phosphorus use efficiency (PUE).ResultsThe results indicated that the combination of AMF + PSB with the 1:2 intercropping ratio resulted in the highest root colonization (70.7%), basal respiration (95.6 mg CO₂ kg− 1 day− 1), and phosphatase activity (562.8 µg PNP g− 1 h− 1). Although sole cropping with chemical fertilizer produced the highest seed yield for both species, the land equivalent ratio (LER) for the 1:1 and 1:2 intercropping systems was greater than 1 (1.13 and 1.17, respectively), indicating higher land use efficiency.ConclusionsThese findings underscore the effectiveness of integrated AMF + PSB applications in enhancing soil health, land productivity, and biodiversity in intercropping systems, presenting a sustainable strategy for the cultivation of medicinal plants. Keywords: Medicinal plants, Microbial respiration, Phosphatase, Intercropping, Land equivalent ratio

Introduction

Crop diversity in agricultural ecosystems can be substantially enhanced through intercropping systems involving two or more plant species. This practice has been widely reported to increase crop productivity [1, 2], improve land-use efficiency [3], enhance soil quality [4], suppress pests and diseases [5], and reduce dependence on chemical fertilizers relative to monoculture systems, while maintaining yield stability [6]. In recent years, intercropping has gained particular attention as a key agroecological strategy for medicinal plant production, where sustainability, product quality, and ecosystem services are of primary concern.

Ajowan (Trachyspermum ammi L.) is an aromatic medicinal plant from the Apiaceae family, extensively used in pharmaceutical, food, and health-related industries due to its high seed essential oil content [1]. The integration of legumes into medicinal plant intercropping systems offers additional ecological benefits by improving nitrogen availability and resource-use complementarity. Securigera (Securigera securidaca L.), a leguminous medicinal plant belonging to the Fabaceae family [8], is a neglected species native to West Asia, Europe, and North Africa, with notable distribution across several regions of Iran (East Azerbaijan, Khuzestan and Fars provinces). Its seeds have long been employed in traditional medicine for the treatment of diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, parasitic infections, and neurological disorders [9].

Phosphorus (P) availability is a major limiting factor in agroecosystems, as more than 80% of applied phosphorus is rapidly immobilized through fixation or adsorption processes [10] or converted into organic forms unavailable to plants [11]. Consequently, excessive reliance on chemical phosphorus fertilizers not only reduces nutrient-use efficiency but also poses environmental risks. In response, environmentally friendly fertilization strategies, particularly biofertilizers, have been proposed to enhance nutrient availability while minimizing chemical inputs [12]. Among these, arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) and phosphate-solubilizing bacteria (PSB) play a pivotal role in mobilizing soil phosphorus through enzymatic activity, organic acid secretion, and improved root–soil interactions [13, 17]. The ecological benefits of AMF include enhanced phosphorus acquisition, improved uptake of low-mobility mineral nutrients, alterations in root system architecture, and modulation of phytohormone production [14–16]. Phosphate-solubilizing bacteria (PSB) similarly promote phosphorus bioavailability through diverse biochemical pathways, including the secretion of phosphatases, the production of organic acids (e.g., 2-ketogluconic, aspartic, citric, gluconic, lactic, malic, malonic, oxalic, succinic, and tartaric acids), and cation chelation mediated by organic metabolites and siderophores [12, 17]. Although numerous studies have independently documented the positive effects of AMF or PSB on crop performance, their combined influence within medicinal plant intercropping systems—particularly under different planting ratios—remains insufficiently explored.

While intercropping systems are well recognized for their superior land-use efficiency, as reflected by land equivalent ratio (LER) values exceeding unity [21, 22], most existing research has primarily focused on aboveground productivity metrics. In contrast, the interactive effects of intercropping patterns and integrated phosphorus biofertilization on belowground processes—such as soil microbial activity, enzyme dynamics, and phosphorus-use efficiency—are still poorly understood. Soil enzymes, especially phosphatases, serve as sensitive indicators of soil biological functioning and play a central role in phosphorus cycling [18]. Changes in root exudation patterns induced by intercropping and microbial inoculation can significantly alter soil microbial respiration and enzyme activity [19, 20].

One of the most prominent ecological advantages of intercropping is its enhanced land-use efficiency [21]. A meta-analysis of 100 independent studies reported an average land equivalent ratio (LER) of 1.17, indicating that one hectare of intercropping can produce yields equivalent to those obtained from 1.17 hectares of sole cropping [7]. Asgharpour et al. [23] reported that in an additive intercropping system of ajowan and fenugreek, the highest grain yield, biological yield, essential oil yield, and land-use efficiency were achieved under the 100% ajowan : 25% fenugreek treatment. Similarly, a study on lentil–psyllium intercropping demonstrated that the highest land equivalent ratio (1.67) was obtained from the 4:2 psyllium : lentil treatment inoculated with mycorrhizal fungi [1]. Despite the growing interest in sustainable medicinal plant production, no comprehensive study to date has simultaneously evaluated intercropping configuration, combined AMF and PSB inoculation, and soil biological responses in ajowan–securigera systems under varying phosphorus fertilizer regimes. This knowledge gap limits the development of integrated nutrient management strategies that optimize both productivity and ecosystem services.

Therefore, the present study was conducted to investigate, for the first time, the combined effects of intercropping ratios and integrated AMF + PSB application under different phosphorus fertilizer treatments on grain yield, land-use efficiency, soil microbial activity, phosphatase enzyme dynamics, and phosphorus-use efficiency in an ajowan–securigera intercropping system in southwestern Iran. The findings are expected to provide novel insights into sustainable nutrient management and ecological service optimization in medicinal plant-based agroecosystems.

Materials and methods

Experimental site and experimental design

The experiment was conducted during the 2021 and 2022 growing seasons on a farm in Mamasani County, Fars Province (51°31’ E, 30°11’ N, 920 m above sea level). It was arranged as a factorial experiment in a randomized complete block design with three replications. The first factor was fertilizer application at five levels: 100% chemical fertilizer (50 kg ha⁻¹ of pure phosphorus from triple superphosphate, equivalent to approximately 100 kg ha⁻¹ of 46% P triple superphosphate fertilizer or 100% TSP), 50 kg ha⁻¹ chemical fertilizer + phosphate-solubilizing bacteria (50% TSP + PSB), 50 kg ha⁻¹ chemical fertilizer + mycorrhizal fungi (50% TSP + AMF), 50 kg ha⁻¹ chemical fertilizer + phosphate-solubilizing bacteria + mycorrhizal fungi (50% TSP + PSB + AMF), and control (no phosphorus fertilizer, bacteria, or fungi application = P0). The second factor was the intercropping system at five levels: 1 : 1 Ajowan : securigera (A₁S₁), 1 : 2 Ajowan : securigera (A₁S₂), 2 : 1 Ajowan : securigera (A₂S₁), sole cropping of Ajowan (A₀), and sole cropping of securigera (S₀) (Fig. 1). The mycorrhizal fungus species used was Funneliformis mosseae, and the phosphate-solubilizing bacteria were a combination of Pseudomonas fluorescens and P. putida. Before planting, a composite soil sample from 0 to 30 cm depth was taken; its physicochemical properties are reported in Table 1. Given the importance of meteorological data, the average temperature and rainfall during the growing months over two consecutive years are presented in Fig. 2.

Each experimental plot measured 4 m in length and 2 m in width, consisting of five planting rows spaced 40 cm apart. Within rows, plant spacing was set at 7 cm for ajowan and 25 cm for securigera. The sowing depths were 2 cm for ajowan and 4 cm for securigera. Inter-plot spacing was maintained at 50 cm, and the distance between adjacent blocks was 2 m. Ajowan seeds were supplied by Pakan Bazr Isfahan Company, while securigera seeds were collected from a local landrace in Khafr County, Fars Province. The AMF inoculum (Funneliformis mosseae) was obtained from the Organic Company of Hamedan, and the phosphate-solubilizing bacteria (PSB) were prepared in the Agronomy Laboratory, Faculty of Agriculture, Yasouj University. The bacterial suspension contained approximately 10⁹ viable cells g⁻¹ of biofertilizer and included standard strains of Pseudomonas fluorescens and P. putida, procured from the Iranian Fungus and Bacteria Collection Center.

In the chemical fertilizer treatments, triple superphosphate (TSP) was applied alongside planting ridges, approximately 5 cm below the seed level. For bacterial treatments, seeds of both species were inoculated 20 min before sowing, following the standard inoculation protocol. To facilitate biological nitrogen fixation in securigera, its seeds were pre-inoculated with Rhizobium leguminosarum (supplied by the Soil and Water Research Institute of Iran) before planting. For mycorrhizal inoculation, a soil-based inoculum containing hyphae, root fragments, and approximately 120 spores g⁻¹ of soil was applied within planting rows directly beneath the seeds at a rate of 80 kg ha⁻¹ before sowing. This procedure was carried out consistently during both experimental years to ensure uniform colonization.

Agricultural operations

Planting was carried out manually in late November in both years. Irrigation was carried out using the furrow-ridge method, and weeds were controlled manually. Urea was applied at 50 kg ha⁻¹ in two separate stages: at the five-leaf stage (for both ajowan and securigera) and at the beginning of stem elongation (for ajowan only). No fertilizer was applied at planting based on the soil potassium status. Harvesting was carried out in late May.

Measurements

Determination of root colonization percentage

The percentage of root colonization by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) was determined following the method of Vierheilig et al. (1998), with minor modifications. Approximately 50 segments (1 cm each) of fine roots were randomly collected from each treatment. The root samples were placed in test tubes containing 10% KOH solution and incubated in a water bath at 95 °C for 5–30 min, depending on root pigmentation and phenolic content. The duration was adjusted until the roots became translucent and glassy in appearance. After clearing, the roots were rinsed thoroughly with diluted acetic acid (vinegar) to neutralize residual alkali. Subsequently, they were stained in a mixture of 3% blue ink and 5% acetic acid (prepared by diluting 5 mL of ink to a final volume of 100 mL with commercial vinegar) and boiled for 3 min. Stained roots were then washed with acidified water or undiluted vinegar to remove excess dye. From each sample, 20 stained root segments were mounted on slides and examined under a compound light microscope. The presence of stained internal or external hyphae, vesicles, or arbuscules was recorded as evidence of fungal colonization, following the procedure of Biermann and Linderman [24]. The percentage of root colonization was calculated as the ratio of the number of infected root segments to the total number of observed segments.

Determination of the number of root nodules in Securigera

The number of root nodules was determined at the flowering stage of Securigera plants. For each treatment, two representative plants were carefully excavated from the central rows to minimize border effects. Root samples were collected from 0 to 30 cm soil depth, ensuring that both main and lateral roots were included. Soil particles were gently removed under running water, and visible nodules formed by Rhizobium leguminosarum were counted manually.

Measurement of soil microbial respiration (BR)

Soil microbial respiration (basal respiration) was measured using sealed containers and the back-titration method of residual NaOH (Eq. 1). In this test, CO₂ released from the soil was absorbed by NaOH. First, 25 g of each soil sample was weighed in special glass containers, and then the moisture content was adjusted to 60% of field capacity. The samples were kept in an incubator at 25 °C in completely sealed containers in the presence of 10 mL of 0.25 N NaOH. After the incubation period, the residual NaOH was titrated with 0.5 N hydrochloric acid, and the CO₂ resulting from microbial respiration was calculated based on the amount of neutralized NaOH. This was calculated from the difference in acid volume consumed for titration between the sample and the control. A control was also prepared using the same procedure without soil [25].

Eq. (1): BR = (B - S) × N × 22 × 100 / (SW × %dm).

Where B and S are the volume of HCl consumed in the control and soil sample (ml), respectively; SW is the initial soil sample weight (g); dm is the dry soil conversion factor; BR is the basal respiration (mg CO₂ g⁻¹ h⁻¹); N is the normality of the acid; 22 is the equivalent weight of carbon dioxide (mg).

Measurement of phosphatase activity

Phosphatase (alkaline) activity was determined using the method of Tabatabai [26]. First, one gram of each soil sample was weighed into test tubes, and then 4 mL of modified universal buffer (MUB) and 1 mL of para-nitrophenyl phosphate (pNPP) substrate were added. The buffer used for alkaline phosphatase had a pH of 11. After mixing, the tubes were placed in an incubator at 37 °C for one hour to reach the optimal temperature for enzyme activity. Then, the reaction was stopped by adding 1 mL of 0.5 M calcium chloride and 4 mL of 0.5 M sodium hydroxide. Before reading the absorbance, the samples were filtered through filter paper. This substrate was hydrolyzed by phosphatase activity, releasing para-nitrophenol (PNP) and phosphate. To measure the amount of PNP released, its absorbance was read at 410 nm using a spectrophotometer. To express enzyme activity as µg PNP g⁻¹ soil h⁻¹, a standard curve was prepared using PNP standards of 0, 10, 25, 50, and 100 µg. For the control sample, the stopping solution was added to the sample and substrate before filtration. To account for soil moisture, enzyme activity was corrected and expressed on a dry soil basis.

Data analysis

After data collection, tests for data normality and homogeneity of variances were performed for both experimental years. Based on these tests, data analysis from the two-year experiment was conducted using SAS software, and mean comparison was performed using the LSD test at the 5% probability level.

Results

Micobaal Basal Respiration in soil (BR)

The main effects of year, cropping system, phosphorus fertilizer treatments, and their interaction (cropping system × phosphorus fertilizer) were significant (p < 0.01) for basal respiration (Table 2). The mean comparison of the interaction showed that the highest basal respiration value (95.64 mg CO₂ kg⁻¹ day⁻¹) was recorded in the A₁S₂ intercropping system under the 50% TSP + PSB + AMF treatment. In contrast, the lowest basal respiration (38.19 mg CO₂ kg⁻¹ day⁻¹) was obtained from the sole cropping of ajowan (A₀) under the control treatment (P₀= no fertilizer application) (Fig. 3).

Root colonization of Securigera

The main effects of year, cropping system, phosphorus fertilizers, the interaction of cropping system × phosphorus fertilizers (p < 0.01) and year × phosphorus fertilizers (p < 0.05) on the root colonization percentage of securigera were significant (Table 2). Mean comparison of the cropping system × phosphorus fertilizers interaction showed that the highest root colonization percentage of securigera with mycorrhizal fungi (70.66%) was obtained from the A₁S₂ system under the 50% TSP + PSB+ AMF treatment. The lowest colonization (21%) was related to the A₁S₁ intercropping system under the 100% TSP fertilizer treatment, which was not significantly different from the A₂S₁ system (21.83%) at the same fertilizer level (Fig. 5). The highest colonization percentage showed a 70% increase compared to the control.

Interaction of intercropping system and phosphorus fertilizer on root colonization of Securigera

Number of root nodules in Securigera

The effects of year, cropping system, phosphorus fertilizer treatments, and the year × phosphorus fertilizer interaction on the number of root nodules in securigera were statistically significant (p < 0.01) (Table 2). In the first year, the highest nodule number (10.75) was obtained from the 100% TSP treatment, which was not significantly different from the 50% TSP + PSB + AMF treatment. The lowest nodule number (3.25) was recorded under the control (P₀) treatment (Table 3). A similar trend was observed in the second year, where the maximum nodule number (18.33) occurred under the 100% TSP treatment, showing no significant difference from the combined 50% TSP + PSB + AMF application. The minimum number of nodules (7.25) was again recorded in the control treatment (Table 3). Across cropping systems, the highest nodule count (12.70) was observed in securigera grown under the A₁S₂ intercropping system, which did not differ significantly from the pure securigera (S₀) cropping system. The lowest nodule number (7.40) was recorded under the A₂S₁ system (Table 4).

The effects of year, cropping system, phosphorus fertilizers (p < 0.01) and the interactions of year × phosphorus fertilizers and year × rcropping system (p < 0.05) were significant on ajowan seed yield (Table 5). Mean comparison of the year × phosphorus fertilizers interaction showed that the highest seed yield in 2021 and 2022 was obtained from the 100% TSP treatment (545.8 kg ha− 1 and 517.0 kg ha− 1, respectively), which was not significantly different from the 50% TPS + PSB + AMF. The lowest seed yield in the first and second years was obtained from the P₀ treatment (no fertilizer application), with averages of 344.0 kg ha− 1 and 395.2 kg ha− 1 respectively (Table 3). Mean comparison of the year × cropping system interaction showed that the highest seed yield in the first (621.2 kg h− 1) and second (704.9 kg h− 1) years was obtained from the A₀ system (. The lowest seed yield in the first and second years was obtained from the A₁S₂ system (419.9 kg ha− 1 and 322.1 kg ha− 1, respectively). Among the intercropping systems, in a similar trend in both years, the highest yield was obtained from the A₁S₁ system (Table 4).

Securigera seed yield

The effects of year, cropping system, phosphorus fertilizers, the interactions of cropping system × phosphorus fertilizers, year × cropping system (p < 0.01) and year × phosphorus fertilizers (p < 0.05) were significant on securigera seed yield (Table 5). The results of the mean comparison for the cropping system × phosphorus fertilizer interaction revealed that highest grain yield (123. 8 kg ha− 1) was obtained from the sole cropping system of securigera (S0) with the fertilizer treatment of 100% TSP. The lowest value (49.4 kg ha− 1) was recorded from the A2S1 intercropping system under the control (no fertilizer) treatment. This lowest yield was statistically in the same group as the A1S1 intercropping system under the same control treatment and the A2S1 intercropping system treated with 50% TSP + PSB (Fig. 6). The superior treatment combination showed an 87% increase compared to the lowest yield.

Phosphorus Use Efficiency (PUE) in ajowan

Analysis of variance showed that the main effects of cropping system, phosphorus fertilizer treatments, and their interactions—including cropping system × phosphorus fertilizer, year × cropping system, and year × phosphorus fertilizer were highly significant (p < 0.01) for the phosphorus use efficiency (PUE) index in ajowan (Table 5). Mean comparison of the cropping system × phosphorus fertilizer interaction revealed that the highest PUE (45.14 kg kg− 1) was obtained under the sole Ajowan (A₀) cropping system treated with 50% TSP + PSB + AMF, which was statistically similar to the A₀ cropping system under the 50% TSP + AMF treatment. In contrast, the lowest PUE value (3.78 kg kg− 1) was recorded under the A₁S₂ intercropping system with the 100% TSP treatment (Fig. 7).

Interaction of intercropping system and phosphorus fertilizer on phosphorus use efficiency in ajowan

Phosphorus Use Efficiency (PUE) in Securigera

The main effects of year, cropping system, phosphorus fertilizer treatments, and their interactions (cropping system × phosphorus fertilizer, year × cropping system, and year × phosphorus fertilizer) were also highly significant (p < 0.01) for the PUE index in securigera (Table 5). The cropping system × phosphorus fertilizer interaction indicated that the highest PUE (6.90 kg kg− 1) was achieved under the sole securigera (S₀) cropping system treated with 50% TSP + PSB + AMF, whereas the lowest PUE (1.01 kg kg− 1) was obtained under the A₂S₁ intercropping system treated with 100% TSP phosphorus fertilizer (Fig. 8).

Land Equivalent Ratio (LER)

Among the experimental factors and their interactions, only the main effect of the intercropping system on the land equivalent ratio of ajowan (LERa), securigera (LERs), and total LER (LERt) was significant (p < 0.01) (Table 6). The highest partial LER for ajowan (0.69) was obtained from the A₁S₁ system, and the lowest (0.47) from the A₂S₁ system. The highest partial LER for securigera (0.69) was obtained from the A₁S₂ system and the lowest (0.33) from the A₂S₁ system. In addition, the total land equivalent ratio for the two systems (A₁S₁ and A₁S₂) was greater than one, and for the A₂S₁ system, it was close to one (0.99) (Table 6).

Discussion

The results showed that the highest basal microbial respiration in soil was observed in the intercropping system (one row of ajowan - two rows of securigera) under the integrated (50% TSP + PSB + AMF) fertilizer treatment. The enhanced microbial activity in these treatments can be attributed to the increased microbial population resulting from the simultaneous action of phosphate-solubilizing bacteria (PSB) and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF). Soil microbial respiration is one of the most fundamental and widely accepted indicators of soil biological activity, as it quantifies CO₂ released during microbial respiration; a parameter closely associated with microbial biomass and activity [29, 30]. According to Vahedi et al. [31], plants colonized by mycorrhizal fungi or rhizospheric bacteria typically allocate a greater portion of photosynthetic carbon to the rhizosphere through root exudation. These exudates, comprising sugars, organic acids, and amino acids, serve as readily available carbon sources for soil microorganisms. Consequently, they enhance soluble carbon pools, microbial biomass, and overall soil respiration. The relatively higher basal respiration in the 50% TSP + PSB treatment compared with 50% TSP + AMF may result from differences in carbon use efficiency among microorganisms. As Islam and Weil [32] structural biomass, thereby respiring less CO₂ per unit of substrate.

Results also showed that intercropping treatments had higher microbial activity compared to sole cropping. The increase in microbial respiration in intercropping systems can be linked to the coexistence of two plant species with distinct functional traits and rhizosphere interactions. Such diversity facilitates broader substrate accessibility for microorganisms, ultimately leading to enhanced microbial respiration compared to sole cropping. Zhalnina et al. [33] reported that greater plant diversity through intercropping supports a richer microbial community and, consequently, enhances microbial-driven ecosystem functions such as nutrient cycling and soil fertility. In agreement with the present findings, Nourbakhsh et al. [34] observed that the highest and lowest basal respiration rates occurred in a corn + soybean + marshmallow intercropping system and in sole marshmallow cropping, respectively. Almethyeb et al. [35] also demonstrated that the combined application of AMF and Enterobacter radicincitans in wheat–faba bean intercropping significantly enhanced soil basal respiration compared with sole crops. Collectively, these results emphasize that integrating biofertilizers (AMF + PSB) with intercropping contributes to greater soil microbial activity by reinforcing root–microbe feedbacks, improving substrate availability, and fostering a more dynamic soil food web mechanisms crucial for sustainable soil fertility and nutrient turnover in low-input agroecosystems.

The results also showed that the highest alkaline phosphatase activity was obtained in the intercropping system (A1S2) and integrated fertilizer treatments (50% TSP + PSB + AMF), respectively. The significant increase in alkaline phosphatase activity in treatments containing arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi and phosphate-solubilizing bacteria can be attributed to enhanced rhizosphere biological activity and an increased demand by plants and microorganisms for bioavailable phosphorus. Mycorrhizal symbiosis, through extending the effective root surface area and releasing carbonaceous compounds, stimulates microbial population size and activity. Concurrently, phosphate-solubilizing bacteria accelerate the mineralization of organic phosphorus via the secretion of organic acids and phosphatase enzymes. Consequently, the synthesis and release of alkaline phosphatase in the rhizosphere is intensified to mobilize phosphorus from soil organic pools, reflecting improved phosphorus cycling and plant nutritional efficiency under these treatments. Phosphatase enzyme is a key enzyme in the availability of phosphorus from organic fertilizer residues. Although plant roots play a role in producing phosphatases, microbial phosphatases (fungi and bacteria) are more effective in hydrolyzing soil organic compounds [36]. Given that alkaline phosphatase is mainly produced by microorganisms [37], its higher secretion in combined treatments (including fungi and bacteria) is not unexpected. They in their research reported the secretion of phosphatase enzyme by mycorrhizal fungi and plant growth-promoting bacteria.

Root colonization by mycorrhizal fungi and rhizospheric bacteria is a key indicator of biological soil functioning and nutrient cycling efficiency. The results of this experiment demonstrated that the highest colonization percentages for both ajowan and securigera occurred under the combined treatment (50% TSP + PSB + AMF), particularly in the A₁S₂ intercropping system, whereas the lowest colonization rates were recorded under the control (P₀) and high‑input chemical fertilizer (100% TSP) treatments. The results showed that the application of phosphate-solubilizing bacteria, mycorrhizal fungi, and the combined fungus and bacteria treatment increased the colonization percentage in plants, while higher application of chemical fertilizers reduced the share of symbiosis percentage. It can be inferred that the simultaneous use of some amounts of biofertilizers (bacteria and mycorrhiza) causes a synergistic effect on the colonization process. Phosphate-solubilizing bacteria release insoluble phosphorus through the production of organic acids and chelating agents. Mycorrhizal fungi rapidly absorb the released phosphorus and transfer it to the plant, which promotes the growth of lateral roots and increases root length and surface area, thereby expanding the contact surface for fungal colonization. In light of the increased microbial respiration and phosphatase enzyme activity observed in the combined treatment (50% TSP + PSB + AMF), indicating enhanced microbial activity and a synergistic effect between the fungus and bacteria, the rise in colonization percentage in this treatment is not unexpected.

Frey-Klett et al. [38] in their research stated that direct inoculation of mycorrhiza increases the density of reproductive organs (spores/hyphae) and microbial adaptation in the rhizosphere. Simultaneously, mycorrhiza helper bacteria (MHB) species (often from the genera Bacillus and Pseudomonas, as well as rhizobia) facilitate spore germination and mycorrhizal hyphal growth through volatile secretions, vitamins, and growth-stimulating compounds, thus increasing the likelihood of successful hyphal encounter with the root and initiation of symbiosis. They also stated that mycorrhiza helper bacteria (MHB) increase root hair density and root contact surface by producing auxin and cytokinin. These results also showed that the symbiosis percentage in the legume plant (securigera) was higher than in the non-legume plant (ajowan).

The data revealed that leguminous securigera exhibited higher symbiosis percentages than non‑leguminous ajowan, supporting the concept that legumes are inherently more microtrophic and naturally predisposed to symbiotic associations. Klabi et al. [39] explained that the presence of legumes enhances AMF diversity and soil bacterial abundance by elevating organic matter and nitrogen levels factors that positively influence root colonization efficiency in mixed systems. Fornara et al. [40] reported that when the concentration of available phosphorus in the soil reached 50 mg kg⁻¹ or more, the colonization rate decreased significantly. They believed that if the net cost of symbiosis with mycorrhiza is greater than its benefit, the mycorrhizal fungus may be a parasite for its host. In the present study, this phenomenon was evident in the A₂S₁ intercropping system, where securigera experienced greater shading by ajowan and higher phosphorus levels. The combined stress of reduced light and surplus P appears to have lowered the net benefit of symbiosis, resulting in the lowest colonization percentage recorded for securigera in that system. The evident reduction in symbiosis at high fertilizer levels highlights an important ecological implication: excess chemical phosphorus input hampers the establishment and function of beneficial microbial associations, which are essential for sustainable nutrient acquisition. Conversely, moderate phosphorus supplementation integrated with AMF and PSB fosters mutualistic efficiency, balancing nutrient supply with biological soil processes. This synergistic equilibrium contributes to enhanced root architecture modification, hormone regulation, siderophore production, and micronutrient uptake (Zn, Cu, Ca, Mg, Mn and Fe) through active mycorrhizal networks.

The application of phosphorus fertilizers, whether chemical (100% TSP) or integrated with biofertilizers (50% TSP + PSB + AMF), significantly increased the number of nodules in securigera plants. This enhancement is primarily attributed to the critical role of phosphorus in legume nodulation. The simultaneous application of AMF and PSB further improved nodulation by ensuring adequate phosphorus availability for the plant roots. Investigations into intercropping systems revealed that sole cropping and systems with a higher proportion of securigera rows consistently exhibited greater nodule counts. This is likely due to increased root exudates from securigera identified as sugars and flavonoids which play a vital role in attracting rhizobia and promoting nodule formation [41]. Conversely, the A₂S₁ intercropping system, characterized by increased shading from ajowan, showed a decrease in nodule number in securigera roots. This reduction was exacerbated under shading conditions, as simultaneous symbiosis with both rhizobia and mycorrhizal fungi imposed additional metabolic costs on securigera.

The results of this study showed that there was no significant difference in ajowan grain yield between the 100% chemical fertilizer treatment and the integrated treatment combining chemical fertilizer with phosphate-solubilizing bacteria and mycorrhizal fungi (50% TSP + PSB + AMF). Increased application of phosphorus fertilizers, due to enhanced vegetative and reproductive growth, increases seed yield. Since phosphorus plays an important role in the physiological and biochemical activities of plants, biofertilizers increase seed yield through the production of organic acids and siderophores, secretion of plant growth regulators (such as IAA, cytokinin, GA, and ethylene), and enhancement of nutrient uptake [42]. Increased colonization percentage, and subsequently microbial respiration rate and phosphatase enzyme secretion, in the 50% TSP + PSB + AMF treatment played an important role in providing phosphorus and other nutrients. The increase in microbial respiration, phosphatase enzyme activity, and colonization percentage in the combined treatment due to the simultaneous activity of mycorrhizal fungi and phosphate-solubilizing bacteria plays a key role in enhancing the availability of phosphorus and other essential nutrients for the plant, which subsequently leads to increased yield. Etesami et al. [37] reported that phosphate-solubilizing bacteria can grow along AMF hyphae and use them to access additional soil volume containing insoluble phosphorus. Furthermore, PSB can promote synergistic microbial interactions and increase plant growth parameters through a stimulatory effect on the establishment of native AMF [13].

Furthermore, the results of this study indicated that the highest grain yield was obtained from sole cropping, while the lowest yield was recorded in the intercropping system with the A₁S₂ ratio. The increased yield in sole-cropped ajowan is attributed to the higher number of planting rows. A more detailed analysis of the results revealed that the reduction in ajowan yield in intercropping systems was due to lower plant density (fewer planting rows); however, the grain yield did not decrease proportionally with the reduction in density, and this trend was evident in all three intercropping systems and in both years. It appeared that the presence of securigera alongside ajowan enabled ajowan to benefit from the biological nitrogen fixation potential of securigera based on the facilitation principle, with mycorrhizal fungi acting as a connecting bridge in this process [19]. Fotohi Chiyaneh et al. [3] investigated different intercropping systems of ajowan, fenugreek, and pea and reported that the highest seed yield of all three plants was obtained from sole cropping combined with the integrated application of biological and chemical fertilizers (50% chemical fertilizer + 100% humic acid). They reported that in sole cropping, due to the absence of interspecific competition, plants did not compete for the same ecological niches, and all available resources were accessible [1]. This process was also observed in sole cropping of securigera.

Enhanced phosphorus use efficiency (PUE) in crop plants reflects their ability to convert phosphorus into economic yield. However, a decline in PUE with increased phosphorus application occurs across various crops [29; 39]. This response can be explained by the law of diminishing returns; one reason for reduced PUE at high application rates is the accelerated loss of phosphorus (e.g., through leaching, fixation, or runoff). This decline indicates that the relationship between phosphorus fertilizer application and crop yield is not linear. The results further indicated that the highest phosphorus use efficiency was achieved in the sole cropping systems for both plants. This can be attributed to the higher plant density and number of rows in sole cropping, which led to a greater seed yield. Based on the PUE formula (seed yield divided by fertilizer input) and the direct relationship between seed yield and resource use efficiency, PUE consequently increased in these systems. However, among the intercropping systems, the A₁S₂ and A₂S₁ ratios yielded the highest PUE for Ajowan. In contrast, for securigera, the highest PUE was obtained only in the A₁S₂ intercrop ratio.

The LER index can show the advantages and disadvantages of intercropping patterns compared with sole cropping. Researchers concluded that an increase in partial LER to more than 0.5 depended on the degree of complementarity of the intercropping components [42]. Therefore, these conditions appeared for ajowan in the A₁S₁ and A₁S₂ systems and for securigera in the A₁S₂ cropping pattern. The low land equivalent ratio of securigera in intercropping treatments is due to increased competition with ajowan, resulting from shading and the larger canopy of ajowan. Generally, in intercropping, the companion species with greater height and foliage acts as the dominant plant, and a major share of the available resources is allocated to it [1]. Fotohi Chiyaneh et al. [3] showed that LER in intercropping systems of Ajowan, fenugreek, and pea was greater than one. Higher LER in intercropping compared with sole cropping can be attributed to improved resource use efficiency of the constituent plants. The different root systems of intercropping components lead to better spatial, temporal, and chemical complementarity. Additionally, atmospheric nitrogen fixation by legume plants (fenugreek and pea) and its direct and indirect transfer to the non-legume plant (ajowan) reduce competition for mineral nitrogen [1].

Conclusion

The results of this research clearly showed that the integrated application of phosphate-solubilizing bacteria (PSB) and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) significantly increases root colonization percentage, soil microbial respiration, and phosphatase enzyme activity. This, ultimately, improves phosphorus availability and plant growth and reduces dependence on chemical phosphorus fertilizerss. The synergistic effect of these biofertilizers, by enhancing soil biological activities, helps maintain and improve soil ecosystem health and sustainability. Regarding intercropping systems, although sole cropping showed higher yield in some cases due to the absence of interspecific competition, specific intercropping patterns (such as A₁S₁ and A₁S₂) achieved a land equivalent ratio (LER) greater than one, indicating more efficient use of environmental resources. However, the dominance of competitive species like ajowan in the A₂S₁ system can negatively affect the yield of the companion species (such as securigera). Therefore, selecting compatible species and optimizing planting arrangement design to minimize competition is essential. In summary, the findings of this research attest to the high potential of integrated nutrient management (chemical and biological) and the use of optimized intercropping systems as an effective strategy for achieving sustainable production goals, improving soil fertility, and increasing its biological activity in low-input agricultural systems. Future studies are suggested to focus on long-term investigations and identifying the molecular mechanisms governing plant-microbe interactions in these systems.
