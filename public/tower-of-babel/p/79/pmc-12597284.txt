Oxytocin enhances neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity to attenuate age-related cognitive decline in aged mice
Authors: Mi Hee Shin, Zhan Zhi Yin, Kyeong-No Yoon, Qing-Ling Quan, Dong Hun Lee, Jin Ho Chung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrain aging is characterized by progressive structural and functional deterioration, leading to cognitive decline and impaired social functioning. A key factor in this process is the age-related decline in adult neurogenesis, particularly in the hippocampal dentate gyrus, which is linked to deficits in learning, memory, and increased social anxiety. Oxytocin, a neuropeptide synthesized in the hypothalamus, regulates social behavior, cognition, and emotion by acting on brain regions including the hippocampus. Importantly, oxytocin levels decrease with age, potentially contributing to cognitive impairment. Here, we examined whether chronic intraperitoneal oxytocin administration could attenuate cognitive decline in aged mice. Twelve-month-old mice received oxytocin injections (0.5 mg/kg) five times weekly for 13 weeks. Behavioral testing at 12 weeks of treatment using the object-place recognition task showed enhanced spatial learning and recognition memory in oxytocin-treated mice compared with saline controls. Immunohistochemistry revealed significantly increased doublecortin (DCX)-positive cells in the hippocampus, indicating enhanced neurogenesis. Furthermore, oxytocin treatment upregulated the expression of glutamate receptor 1 (GluR1) and N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor subunit 2B (NMDAR2B), which are markers of synaptic plasticity. These findings suggest that chronic oxytocin treatment is associated with enhanced neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity, which may contribute to improved cognition in aged mice. Our results support oxytocin as a potential therapeutic agent for age-related cognitive decline.Keywords: Oxytocin, Cognitive function, Neurogenesis, Synaptic plasticity

Introduction

Oxytocin, an essential neuropeptide, is primarily synthesized in the paraventricular (PVN) and supraoptic (SON) nuclei of the hypothalamus and plays a crucial role in physiological and behavioral processes (Lin et al., 2017). While oxytocin is well known for its role in inducing uterine contractions during labor and facilitating milk ejection during lactation, emerging evidence suggests that it also has profound effects on brain function, particularly in modulating social behaviors, cognitive processes, and emotional regulation (Jones et al., 2017, Leuner et al., 2012, Walter et al., 2021). Beyond these classical reproductive and social functions, oxytocin has been increasingly implicated in neurogenesis, synaptic plasticity, and memory formation (Lin et al., 2017, Lin and Hsu, 2018, Neumann and Landgraf, 2012, Stevenson et al., 2019), providing a strong rationale for investigate its potential in the context of age-related cognitive decline, where hippocampal plasticity is known to be compromised.

Neurogenesis plays a critical role in cognitive function, particularly in memory consolidation and spatial navigation (Culig et al., 2022). Cognitive decline in aging populations has become a significant concern, with aging associated with marked declines in hippocampal neurogenesis and synaptic integrity, contributing to memory deficits and increased susceptibility to neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease (Culig et al., 2022, Selkoe, 2002, Yoon et al., 2024a). Although the molecular mechanisms underlying age-related cognitive decline remain incompletely understood, synaptic plasticity and neurotransmitter systems dysregulation are key contributing factors. Given its role in neural circuit modulation, oxytocin has been proposed as a potential therapeutic agent to attenuate age-related cognitive impairment (Jones et al., 2017, Leuner et al., 2012, Lin et al., 2017). However, the precise mechanisms by which oxytocin influences hippocampal neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity in aged populations are not yet fully characterized. Moreover, the therapeutic potential of oxytocin is challenged by its short half-life in circulation approximately (3–5 min) (Uvnäs-Moberg, 2024), limited blood-brain barrier penetration (Smith et al., 2019), and context-dependent effects (Bartz et al., 2011). The present study was designed to investigate whether chronic peripheral administration of oxytocin can attenuate age-related cognitive decline in mice by enhancing hippocampal neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity. Specifically, we examined the effects of 13-week intraperitoneal oxytocin treatment on spatial memory performance, doublecortin (DCX)-positive neuronal populations, and expression of key synaptic plasticity markers (GluR1 and NMDAR2B) in 12-month-old C57BL/6 J mice.

Notably, this study is among the first to demonstrate that chronic peripheral oxytocin treatment for 13 weeks concurrently modulates hippocampal neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity markers in 12-month-old mice, an age at which age-related cognitive decline begins to manifest (Belblidia et al., 2018). Given that long-term treatment studies of oxytocin in aging contexts are scarce, our findings provide novel evidence for the sustained effects of oxytocin on brain plasticity during aging and extend previous research in this field.

Materials and methods

Animals and oxytocin administration

Twelve-month-old male C57BL/6 J mice were purchased from Orient Bio (Seongnam-si, Korea). All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Seoul National University Hospital (case number: 22–0148-S1A0), and were conducted in an AAALAC International-accredited facility in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Mice were randomly assigned to two groups: vehicle-treated (0.9 % saline, 100 μL, intraperitoneal (IP)) and oxytocin-treated (0.5 mg/kg oxytocin in 100 μL saline, IP). The dosage of 0.5 mg/kg oxytocin was selected based on prior rodent studies that reported central and behavioral effects following peripheral administration of oxytocin (Ferris et al., 2015, MacFadyen et al., 2016). Although allometric scaling from rats would suggest a higher mouse-equivalent dose of approximately 1.0 mg/kg, we deliberately chose a more conservative dose within the range commonly used in mice (0.5 mg/kg) to minimize potential adverse effects while ensuring alignment with the short half-life of oxytocin. Injections were administered five times per week for 13 weeks. The administration frequency of five times per week was chosen to establish a chronic treatment model that accounts for the short half-life of oxytocin while minimizing animal stress from excessive handling. Given the short plasma half-life of oxytocin, relatively frequent dosing is required to maintain biological effects, while the 5-day-per-week schedule provides a practical balance between therapeutic efficacy and animal welfare considerations. All mice were sacrificed 6 hours after the final injection. This timing was chosen to avoid the immediate pharmacokinetic peak while remaining within the pharmacological window of oxytocin action (Smith et al., 2019, Yeomans et al., 2021). Only male mice were used in this study to minimize variability associated with the estrous cycle in females. Each group initially consisted of eight animals. However, owing to age-related mortality, three mice in the control group and one mouse in the oxytocin group were lost during the study, resulting in final group sizes of five (control) and seven (oxytocin). Mice were housed in groups of 3–4 per cage under controlled environmental conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, 22 ± 1 °C, and 50–60 % humidity) with ad libitum access to food and water. Environmental enrichment was provided, and all animals were monitored daily for general health status and any potential adverse effects throughout the treatment period. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a random number generator. Behavioral scoring, immunohistochemistry, and Western blot densitometry analyses were conducted by investigators blinded to the treatment conditions.

Object-place recognition (OPR) test

Spatial recognition memory was evaluated using the OPR test after both 8 and 12 weeks of treatment. At 8 weeks, no significant differences were observed between groups; therefore, the data presented represent the 12-week assessment, which was conducted one week prior to tissue collection. The test was performed in a 25 × 25 × 25 cm opaque plastic chamber over three phases: habituation, training, and testing. On day 1 (habituation), mice freely explored the empty chamber for 5 min. On day 2 (training), they were exposed to two identical objects for 5 min. After 24 h, one object was moved to a new location (testing phase), and exploration behavior was recorded. Exploration was operationally defined as the time spent with the mouse’s nose ≤ 1 cm from the object and directly oriented toward it; climbing, looking over, digging, biting, and other non-exploratory behaviors were excluded (Vogel-Ciernia and Wood, 2014). Video recordings were analyzed by investigators blinded to group assignments. To ensure reliable baseline behavior and minimize the influence of pre-existing object preferences, mice displaying over 20 % exploration bias toward either object during the training phase were excluded from the analysis (Vogel-Ciernia and Wood, 2014). One mouse from each group was removed based on this criterion, resulting in five control mice and six oxytocin-treated mice being included in the final behavioral analysis. This test assessed recognition of spatial novelty, a key aspect of hippocampal-dependent memory.

Tissue preparation

Mice were anesthetized using an intramuscular injection of Zoletil (Virbac, Fort Worth, TX, USA) and xylazine solution at a 3:1 ratio. Transcardial perfusion was performed with 0.9 % normal saline. Brains were dissected, and hemispheres separated. The left hippocampus was snap-frozen for biochemical assays for Western blot analysis. The right hemisphere was fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight and cryoprotected in 30 % sucrose solution until the tissue sank (typically 24–48 h). Coronal sections (40μm) were prepared using a cryostat and stored in 24-well plates.

DCX immunohistochemistry

Immunostaining for doublecortin (DCX) was performed using the free-floating technique. Three coronal sections per animal (−1.34 to −2.92 mm from the bregma, right hemisphere) were analyzed. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies against DCX (sc-8066, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA, 1:200) for 48 h at 4 °C in a diluent buffer containing 1 % bovine serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 1 % Triton X-100 in 0.1 M phosphate buffer. The sections were then incubated with a biotinylated rabbit anti-goat IgG secondary antibody (BA-5000–1.5, Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA, 1:200) for 24 h at 4°C. Signal amplification was performed using a Vector ABC kit (Vector Laboratories). DCX-positive cells were visualized with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (Vector Laboratories) and imaged using a Leica DM5500B microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). The subgranular zone was defined as a 50 μm band adjacent to the granule cell layer. The total number of DCX-positive cells were manually counted using ImageJ software (version 1.53, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) by an investigator blinded to group allocation and normalized to section area (cells/mm²). Antibody specificity and expected band sizes were confirmed based on manufacturer datasheets and previous studies (see Supplementary Table 1)

Western blotting

The hippocampus was homogenized in RIPA buffer (Merck Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) containing a protease inhibitor mixture (Roche Applied Science, Penzberg, Germany) and a phosphatase inhibitor mixture (Sigma-Aldrich). Protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid assay (Sigma-Aldrich). Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10 % SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Amersham, Buckinghamshire, UK) at 100 V for 90 min. After blocking with 5 % non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1 % Tween 20, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against GluR1 (sc-55509, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA, 1:200), NMDAR2B (AB1557P, Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA, 1:1000), and β-actin (MA5–11869, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, 1:1000). Membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-mouse or anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system (Biomax Co. Ltd., Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea). Band density was quantified using ImageJ software, with standardized background subtraction and normalized to β-actin as a loading control, and results are expressed as fold change relative to control values.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Oxytocin concentrations in mouse serum and brain tissue were measured using a commercially available ELISA kit (MyBioSource, San Diego, CA, USA, MBS704379), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, blood samples were collected by cardiac puncture and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain serum. For brain samples, hippocampal tissues were rapidly dissected, homogenized in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected for analysis. Standards and samples were added to ELISA plates in duplicate, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Oxytocin concentrations were calculated from a standard curve and normalized to sample volume.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10; Graphpad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Normality was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk test. Since some datasets showed deviations from normality, non-parametric Mann-Whitney U tests were used for all group comparisons, with significance set at p < 0.05. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). To assess the adequacy of the sample size and the reliability of the detected effects, a post hoc power analysis was performed using the observed means, standard deviations, and group sizes for each key endpoint (object-place recognition index, hippocampal DCX, GluR1, and NMDAR2B protein expression). The analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism's power and sample size module, based on the Mann–Whitney U test framework with α = 0.05. The resulting statistical power exceeded 0.8 for these primary outcomes, indicating that the detected group differences were sufficiently robust despite the modest sample sizes (n = 5–7). These results are consistent with the large effect sizes observed in behavioral and molecular measures.

Results

Intraperitoneal oxytocin injection improved age-related memory impairment in aged mice

To investigate the effects of peripheral oxytocin administration on hippocampal function and spatial memory in aged mice, we intraperitoneally injected 12-month-old C57BL/6J mice with either saline or oxytocin (0.5 mg/kg, 100 μL) five times per week for 13 weeks (Fig. 1a). After 12 weeks of treatment, cognitive function was assessed using the object-place recognition (OPR) test, a well-established behavioral paradigm that depends on intact hippocampal function (Lee and Silva, 2009). Behavioral testing was also performed at 8 weeks of treatment but showed no significant differences between groups, indicating that the therapeutic effects of oxytocin require prolonged administration. Consistent with enhanced spatial memory, the Discrimination Index (DI) was significantly higher in the oxytocin-injected mice compared with the saline-injected controls (n = 5 control, n = 6 oxytocin) (Fig. 1b). These results indicate that chronic oxytocin administration significantly enhances spatial cognitive performance and memory retention in aged mice.

Intraperitoneal oxytocin injection improves age-related memory impairment in aged mice. (a) Experimental schedule: Twelve-month-old mice were intraperitoneally injected with either saline or oxytocin (0.5 mg/kg, 100 μL) five times per week for 13 weeks. (b) OPR test results: The discrimination index (DI) was calculated as (Time spent in novel place - Time spent in old place) / (Time spent in novel place + Time spent in old place). Oxytocin treatment significantly increased DI compared with saline controls (n = 5 control, n = 6 oxytocin; Mann-Whitney U test, **p < 0.01). (c) DCX immunostaining: Representative immunohistochemical staining of DCX-positive cells in the subgranular zone of the dentate gyrus. Both images shown at same magnification (scale bar: 100 μm). Arrows indicate representative examples of DCX+ cells; quantification was based on all DCX+ cells within the defined region of interest. The graph shows DCX+ cell density (cells/mm²), quantified from three coronal sections per animal (−1.34 to −2.92 mm from bregma, right hemisphere) by a blinded investigator, and presented as the mean±SEM (control group: n = 5, oxytocin group: n = 6). Oxytocin-treated mice exhibited a significant increase in DCX+ cell density compared with controls (Mann-Whitney U test, *p < 0.05). (d) Western blotting: Protein expression levels of GluR1, NMDAR2B, and β-actin in the hippocampus were analyzed by Western blotting. Representative blots showing bands at expected molecular weights: GluR1 (∼106 kDa), NMDAR2B (∼180 kDa), β-actin (∼42 kDa). Relative protein expression levels of GluR1 and NMDAR2B were quantified using ImageJ software with background subtraction and normalized to β-actin as loading control. Results, presented as mean±SEM (control group: n = 5, oxytocin group: n = 7), showed significant increase in both protein expression levels in oxytocin-treated mice compared with controls (Mann-Whitney U test, *p < 0.05).

Intraperitoneal oxytocin injection increased the number of DCX+ cells in aged mice hippocampus

Next, we examined whether oxytocin administration influenced hippocampal neurogenesis by quantifying DCX+ cells in the subgranular zone of the dentate gyrus using immunohistochemistry. DCX is a well-recognized marker for newly generated neurons (Dhaliwal et al., 2015). As shown in Fig. 1c, the number of DCX+ cells was significantly increased in the oxytocin-injected group compared with the control group (n = 5 control, n = 6 oxytocin). These findings suggest that chronic oxytocin administration promotes adult neurogenesis in the aged hippocampus.

Intraperitoneal oxytocin injection increased the expression GluR1 and NMDAR2B of in the hippocampus of aged mice

To investigate the molecular correlates of the improved memory performance, we examined the expression of key synaptic plasticity-related proteins, including GluR1 and NMDAR2B. These proteins play an essential role in excitatory synaptic transmission and are involved in the formation and stabilization of synaptic connections (Hunt and Castillo, 2012, Lee et al., 2016). Given the critical role of GluRs in synaptic plasticity, learning, and memory, we next investigated the protein expression levels of GluR1 and NMDAR2B in the hippocampus using Western blot analysis. Quantitative analysis revealed that GluR1 expression was significantly upregulated in oxytocin-injected mice compared with control mice, with a 133.0 % increase (Fig. 1d). Similarly, NMDAR2B expression was markedly elevated in the oxytocin-injected mice compared with control mice, with a 101.7 % increase (n = 5 control, n = 7 oxytocin) (Fig. 1d). Since these receptors play crucial roles in long-term potentiation, a fundamental mechanism underlying learning and memory, the observed upregulation suggests that oxytocin enhances synaptic plasticity in the aged hippocampus. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that chronic peripheral oxytocin administration is associated with improved hippocampal-dependent spatial memory, enhanced adult neurogenesis, and upregulated synaptic plasticity markers in aged mice, supporting its potential as a therapeutic intervention for age-related cognitive decline.

Discussion

In the central nervous system, aging leads to neurodegeneration, which is often caused by reduced neurogenesis and decreased synaptic plasticity (Culig et al., 2022). Neurodegeneration leads to a series of serious clinical problems such as Alzheimer's disease (Hung et al., 2010, Selkoe, 2002). Previous studies have shown that cognitive ability, spatial memory, and hippocampal neurogenesis decline with age (Culig et al., 2022, Hung et al., 2010). Oxytocin, primarily known for its role in social bonding and reproductive functions, has recently been investigated for its broader effects on cognition and neuroprotection. Research suggests that oxytocin modulates the release of neurotransmitters such as dopamine and serotonin, both of which play critical roles in cognitive processes (Fernández et al., 2018). Furthermore, the role of oxytocin in synaptic plasticity further supports its potential in enhancing cognitive function, particularly in aging populations where synaptic integrity is compromised (Neumann and Landgraf, 2012, Stevenson et al., 2019). Additionally, oxytocin's ability to enhance social cognition and emotional regulation, often impaired in neurodegenerative conditions, further strengthens its therapeutic potential. Individuals with Alzheimer’s disease and other forms of dementia exhibit behavioral and psychological symptoms such as social isolation, anxiety, and depression. As oxytocin is known to modulate these social and emotional behaviors, it may offer additional benefits beyond cognitive enhancement (Takahashi et al., 2023). In our study, we administered intraperitoneal oxytocin injections for 13 weeks to assess its effects on cognitive performance, neurogenesis, and synaptic plasticity in aged mice. Behavioral assessments revealed a significant enhancement in spatial memory among oxytocin-injected mice. To validate these findings, we performed DCX immunostaining, which confirmed a marked increase in hippocampal neurogenesis. We also examined GluR1, a key regulator of these processes in the central nervous system. Western blot analysis revealed a significant upregulation of GluR1 in oxytocin-injected mice. The upregulation of these receptors may contribute to enhanced hippocampal synaptic plasticity by facilitating long-term potentiation (LTP) (Qu et al., 2021, Tomizawa et al., 2003). These molecular changes provide plausible mechanistic candidates for the observed spatial memory improvements. However, we acknowledge that direct correlations between protein expression levels and behavioral performance were not assessed, and future studies incorporating such analyses, along with electrophysiological recordings, will help establish more definitive mechanistic links.

Regarding the mechanism of action, we measured oxytocin concentrations in plasma and hippocampus at 6 h post-final injection (Supplementary Fig. 1). Given oxytocin's very short half-life, no significant differences were detected between groups at this time point. This suggests that the observed neurogenic and plasticity-enhancing effects are not mediated by sustained central oxytocin elevation, but rather through transient receptor activation during brief pharmacokinetic peaks or indirect mechanisms (Iwasaki et al., 2015, Smith et al., 2019). Future studies employing earlier sampling time points (5–30 min post-injection) or direct measurement of receptor activation may clarify the pharmacodynamic profile.

While the ability of oxytocin to cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) is limited, the observed hippocampal effects in our study may be mediated through several potential mechanisms. First, even small amounts of peripherally administered oxytocin that successfully cross the BBB could exert functionally significant effects on hippocampal oxytocin receptors (Yamamoto and Higashida, 2020), with repeated activation over 13 weeks potentially inducing long-lasting neuroplastic changes. Second, peripheral oxytocin may act through indirect pathways, such as activating vagal afferent neurons, which subsequently modulate central nervous system activity and neurotransmitter release in brain regions (Iwasaki et al., 2015). Although direct evidence for oxytocin receptor expression on vagal afferents remains inconclusive, studies suggest peripheral oxytocin may activate vagal afferents (Iwasaki et al., 2015) and engage the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS), which projects to the hippocampus via a noradrenergic pathway involving the paragigantocellularis and locus coeruleus. Vagal-cholinergic mechanisms may also modulate hippocampal plasticity and memory circuits (Maurer and Williams, 2017, Mello-Carpes and Izquierdo, 2013). These pathways could plausibly contribute to the observed effects, though this remains hypothetical.

Taken together, our findings support a model in which chronic oxytocin treatment promotes both structural and functional hippocampal plasticity. The increased DCX+ cells likely reflect enhanced neurogenesis, which can improve pattern separation - the ability to distinguish between similar spatial contexts - thereby facilitating encoding of new spatial memories and explaining the improved OPR performance (Kim et al., 2025, Sahay et al., 2011, Yoon et al., 2024b). Concurrently, the upregulation of GluR1 and NMDAR2B indicates strengthened synaptic plasticity, enhancing synaptic transmission and contributing to long-term potentiation (Huganir and Nicoll, 2013). This dual mechanism - combining structural plasticity through neurogenesis with functional plasticity via receptor upregulation - provides a biologically plausible framework for understanding the spatial memory improvements in aged mice.

While GluR1 and NMDAR2B upregulation and increased neurogenesis provide plausible mechanistic candidates, we acknowledge that other unmeasured pathways may contribute. For instance, oxytocin may influence neuroinflammatory processes (Mehdi et al., 2022), modulate other neurotransmitter systems (e.g., cholinergic, serotonergic) (Yoshida et al., 2009), or alter network-level brain connectivity (Bethlehem et al., 2013). Comprehensive molecular profiling and functional assessments in future studies will be necessary to establish more definitive causal relationships.

It should be noted that the 12-month-old C57BL/6 J mice used in this study represent early-stage cognitive decline (∼15 months at endpoint) (Belblidia et al., 2018). Future studies with older cohorts (18–24 months) would extend these findings to more advanced aging stages.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The relatively small sample sizes (n = 5–7), constrained by limited availability of aged mice, may restrict generalizability despite achieving adequate statistical power (>0.8 in post hoc analysis). Additionally, attrition due to age-related mortality resulted in unequal group sizes, potentially introducing bias, and the exclusive use of male mice limits applicability to both sexes. Regarding mechanistic interpretations, we did not measure central oxytocin at early post-injection time points that might capture transient increases, leaving uncertainty about BBB penetration dynamics. We also did not assess correlations between molecular changes and behavioral performance or examine whether effects persist beyond treatment cessation, which limits our ability to establish definitive causal relationships. Methodological constraints include reliance on a single behavioral paradigm (OPR test), use of non-stereological quantification for DCX+ cells, and evaluation of only one dosing regimen and administration route (0.5 mg/kg, IP). Additional behavioral assessments, stereological approaches, and systematic comparisons of different doses or routes (e.g., intranasal) would provide more comprehensive and rigorous findings.

Our findings highlight oxytocin's potential as a neuroprotective agent against age-related cognitive decline. By improving spatial memory, enhancing hippocampal neurogenesis, and promoting synaptic plasticity, oxytocin may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for neurodegenerative diseases including Alzheimer's disease. Given the growing aging population, further studies exploring the clinical applications of oxytocin in aging and neurodegeneration are warranted.
