Mesenchymal stromal cells alleviate neuropathic pain in association with M2 macrophage polarization in dorsal root ganglia and peripheral nerve repair
Authors: Hui Chen, Jie Sun, Lihong Yu, Xiaoru Lin, Qiqiao Wu, Chao Liang, Weiyu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeuropathic pain (NP) is a debilitating condition with limited effective treatments. Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) have emerged as a potential therapeutic strategy due to their immunomodulatory properties. This study investigated whether intravenous administration of human umbilical cord-derived MSCs could alleviate NP in a rat chronic constriction injury (CCI) model and explored the associated changes in macrophage polarization and nerve structure.MethodsMale Sprague Dawley rats were subjected to CCI of the sciatic nerve and randomly divided into Control, CCI, and CCI+MSCs groups (n = 10/group). The CCI+MSCs group received 1 × 10⁶ MSCs via tail vein injection on postoperative day 7. Mechanical withdrawal threshold (MWT) and thermal withdrawal latency (TWL) were assessed at baseline and on postoperative days 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, and 15. On day 15, dorsal root ganglia (DRG) were collected for immunofluorescence staining of CD68 (pan-macrophage marker) and CD206 (M2 marker), and sciatic nerves were examined by hematoxylin-eosin staining and transmission electron microscopy (TEM).ResultsCCI induced significant mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity from postoperative day 7 through day 15 (p < 0.05 vs. Control). MSC administration partially restored MWT and TWL values compared with the CCI group (p < 0.05). Immunofluorescence revealed increased CD68⁺ macrophage infiltration in the DRG of CCI rats, which was attenuated by MSC treatment. Concurrently, CD206 expression was enhanced in the CCI+MSCs group, suggesting a shift toward an M2-like macrophage phenotype. Histological and ultrastructural analyses showed that MSC treatment was associated with reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, increased Schwann cell counts, lower G-ratio, and improved myelin sheath integrity compared with the untreated CCI group (p < 0.05).ConclusionsIntravenous MSC administration was associated with attenuation of nociceptive hypersensitivity, enhanced M2-like macrophage polarization in the DRG, and partial structural repair of the injured sciatic nerve in a rat CCI model. These findings suggest that MSCs may represent a promising cell-based approach for NP treatment, although further studies are needed to establish causal mechanisms and evaluate long-term safety and efficacy.Graphical abstract

Background

Neuropathic pain (NP) is a form of chronic pain arising from neurological damage, affecting approximately 7–10% of the global population. This condition not only severely diminishes patients’ quality of life but also imposes a significant socio-economic burden [1]. According to the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP), NP is defined as “pain caused by a lesion or disease of the somatosensory system” [2], characterized by nociceptive hypersensitivity, abnormal pain perception, and other debilitating symptoms. Conventional pharmacological treatments, including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and opioids, have demonstrated limited efficacy and are associated with substantial adverse effects when used over extended periods [3, 4]. Consequently, there is an urgent need for the development of innovative therapeutic strategies.

Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) are adult multipotent cells with self-renewal and multi-lineage differentiation capabilities that have shown considerable promise in the treatment of various conditions, such as Alzheimer’s disease and osteoarthritis [5, 6]. In recent years, the application of MSCs for NP treatment has garnered increasing attention. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that MSCs can attenuate pain behaviors through modulating neuroinflammation, promoting axonal and myelin regeneration, enhancing angiogenesis, and secreting neurotrophic factors [7, 8]. Furthermore, MSCs exert therapeutic effects through the release of extracellular vesicles (EVs), which facilitate cell proliferation, regulate inflammatory responses, reduce cell death, and promote axonal regeneration and angiogenesis, thereby mitigating pain progression [9–11].

The polarization state of macrophages has emerged as a key determinant in the pathogenesis of various diseases. Classically activated M1-type macrophages secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines and have been implicated in tumor progression and chronic inflammation [12, 13]. In contrast, alternatively activated M2-type macrophages exhibit anti-inflammatory and tissue repair properties, contributing to the resolution of inflammation in conditions such as pulmonary fibrosis and renal injury [14, 15]. In the context of nerve injury, macrophages become activated and migrate to the injury site, where they participate in both the inflammatory response and subsequent tissue repair [16]. The polarization balance between M1 and M2 phenotypes significantly influences the progression of neuroinflammation and neuropathic pain. M1-type macrophages predominantly promote neuroinflammation by releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), whereas M2-type macrophages contribute to anti-inflammatory responses and tissue repair [17, 18]. Thus, modulating macrophage polarization represents a potential therapeutic strategy for NP. Additionally, interactions between macrophages and neurons have gained attention, as macrophages can regulate neuronal excitability and nociceptive transmission by releasing specific signaling molecules, such as chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 3 (CCL3) [19].

In this study, a chronic constriction injury (CCI) rat model was employed to simulate NP, and the effects of intravenous MSC administration were investigated. Specifically, the study examined changes in macrophage polarization within the dorsal root ganglion (DRG) and assessed peripheral nerve structural integrity. The mechanical withdrawal threshold (MWT) and thermal withdrawal latency (TWL) were systematically evaluated as behavioral endpoints. Dynamic changes in DRG macrophage markers were analyzed using immunofluorescence staining. In addition, histological analyses, including hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining and transmission electron microscopy (TEM), were conducted to assess inflammatory cell infiltration, Schwann cell proliferation, and myelin ultrastructure in the sciatic nerve. Quantitative parameters including the G-ratio, myelin sheath thickness, and axon diameter were measured. This study aimed to provide experimental evidence regarding the association between MSC-mediated modulation of macrophage phenotype and functional recovery in a rat model of NP, contributing to the preclinical foundation for MSC-based therapies.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male Sprague Dawley rats, aged 5–6 weeks and weighing 150–200 g, were obtained from Shanghai Slaccas Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. The animals were housed in the Laboratory Animal Center of Fujian Medical University under controlled conditions, including a temperature of 23 ± 0.5 °C, humidity of 60 ± 5%, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Food and water were provided ad libitum. All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University (Approval No. FJMU IACUC 2021-0034) and were carried out in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. At the end of the experiments, animals were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of 2% pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg). This method of euthanasia is consistent with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020 Edition). For immunofluorescence assays, rats were deeply anesthetized with 4% sevoflurane before transcardial perfusion, which served as the terminal procedure.

CCI model

The chronic constriction injury (CCI) model was established based on the method described by Bennett and Xie [20]. Rats were anesthetized with 4% sevoflurane and placed in the prone position. The intermuscular space between the biceps femoris muscles was carefully separated to expose the right sciatic nerve. A 4 − 0 chromic gut suture was loosely tied around the middle of the sciatic nerve trunk. Four ligations were performed, each spaced approximately 1 mm apart. Care was taken to ensure that the blood supply was not completely disrupted, as indicated by a slight tremor in the right hind limb. All surgical procedures were conducted by the same individual under aseptic conditions to ensure consistency in the degree of nerve ligation and the surgical incision.

MSC culture

Human umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs, passage 6) were purchased from Health-Biotech Stem Cell Research Institute Co. (Jiangxi, China; batch certificate No. 202107JF22), meeting the ISCT minimal criteria for MSC identification. The cells were cultured in DMEM-F12 basal medium (Gibco, Montana, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), 1% L-glutamine (Gibco), 1% non-essential amino acids (NEAA; Gibco), 4 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF; PeproTech, New Jersey, USA), and 4 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF; PeproTech).

Experimental design and grouping

Rats were randomly divided into three groups (n = 10 per group): the Control group (sham surgery, sciatic nerve exposed without ligation), the CCI group, and the CCI+MSCs group. In both the CCI and CCI+MSCs groups, the sciatic nerve was ligated as described above. On postoperative day 7, the CCI group received an intravenous injection of 200 µL sterile saline, while the CCI+MSCs group was administered 1 × 10⁶ MSCs suspended in 200 µL sterile saline via the tail vein. The dose of 1 × 10⁶ MSCs was selected based on a recent systematic review of preclinical MSC studies in neuropathic pain models, which reported that intravenous doses in this range demonstrate therapeutic efficacy without significant adverse effects [21]. Sample sizes were determined in accordance with the 3R principles to minimize animal use.

Behavioral testing

Mechanical Withdrawal Threshold (MWT)

The mechanical withdrawal threshold (MWT) was assessed using Von Frey filaments. Rats were acclimated for 30 min in a specially designed Plexiglas cage with a grid bottom. Following acclimation, the animals were stimulated using a series of standardized Von Frey filaments (0.4, 0.6, 1.4, 2.0, 4.0, 6.0, 8.0, and 15.0 g), beginning with the 2.0 g filament applied to the mid-plantar region of the hind paw, following the up-down method described by Chaplan et al. [22]. A positive response was defined as paw withdrawal, licking, or shaking of the stimulated paw. If no response was observed to the initial filament, a filament with the next higher force was applied. Conversely, if a response was elicited, a filament with the next lower force was used. This sequence continued until five responses were recorded. Each filament was applied for 1–2 s, with a 10-minute interval between stimuli to prevent sensitization or fatigue.

Thermal Withdrawal Latency (TWL)

The sensitivity of rats to thermal stimuli was assessed following the method described by Hargreaves et al. [23]. A plantar thermal analgesia apparatus (390G, IITC Life Science Inc., USA) was used for the measurements. The thermostatic glass plate of the apparatus was preheated for 30 min and maintained at approximately 30 °C. During this time, rats were acclimated to the testing environment for at least 30 min. A thermal radiation beam, set at 40% of the maximum light intensity, was directed at the middle and posterior third of the right plantar surface of the rat’s hind paw. The latency to paw withdrawal, licking, or shaking was recorded as the thermal withdrawal latency (TWL). Each evaluation was repeated three times, with a 5-minute interval between measurements, and the average value was calculated. To prevent tissue damage, the duration of thermal radiation exposure was limited to a maximum of 20 s. All behavioral measurements were conducted by investigators blinded to the experimental groups to ensure objectivity and reliability.

Hematoxylin-Eosin (HE) staining

Of the 10 animals per group in the behavioral cohort, 9 were used for histological analysis; tissue from the remaining one animal per group was reserved for parallel exploratory assays not included in the present report. The sciatic nerve at the injury site was collected 15 days after CCI induction and processed into 4 μm transverse sections. The sections were dewaxed and stained using hematoxylin solution for 15 min, followed by rinsing under running water for 3–5 s. The slides were then treated with 1% hydrochloric acid in ethanol for 30 s and briefly rinsed in double-distilled water (ddH₂O). Subsequently, the sections were stained with eosin solution for 3 min, dehydrated twice in 95% ethanol for 30 s each, and further dehydrated twice in 100% ethanol for 3 min each. Transparency was achieved using xylene three times, for 3 min each. Finally, the sections were sealed with neutral gum. Photomicrographs were captured using an Olympus biomicroscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Immunofluorescence staining

Fifteen days after CCI induction, rats were anesthetized with 4% sevoflurane, and the left ventricle was perfused with 100 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde. The L5 dorsal root ganglion (DRG) was extracted and post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C. After washing in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), dehydration through a graded ethanol series, and embedding in paraffin, 4 μm sections were prepared. The sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through descending concentrations of ethanol. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in citrate buffer (pH 8.0) in a microwave oven at 95 °C for 15 min. After cooling to room temperature, sections were blocked with 10% goat serum in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Consecutive serial sections were used for single-antibody staining to allow the use of primary antibodies raised in the same host species. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber with either anti-rabbit CD206 (1:200, catalog #GB13438, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) or anti-rabbit CD68 (1:4000, catalog #GB13430, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Following primary antibody incubation, sections were washed three times in PBS and incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:300, catalog #A-11008, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 50 min at room temperature in the dark. Sections were then counterstained with DAPI (G1012, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 10 min to visualize cell nuclei. Finally, the stained sections were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium. Images of CD68- and CD206-stained serial sections were captured separately using a confocal microscope (DM6, Leica, Germany) and aligned for comparison. For visualization purposes, CD68 and CD206 signals were pseudo-colored green and red, respectively. Quantification of fluorescence intensity was performed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). For each section, three random fields were selected for analysis.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

On day 15 post-CCI, the sciatic nerve from the ligation site was harvested, trimmed to 1 mm³, and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4) overnight at 4 °C. After washing in buffer, specimens were post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h, dehydrated through a graded acetone series, and embedded in epoxy resin. Following polymerization at 60 °C for 48 h, ultrathin Sects. (90–100 nm) were prepared using an ultramicrotome (UC-7, Leica, Germany) and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The sections were examined and imaged using a transmission electron microscope (TECNAI, FEI Company, USA). For quantitative analysis, 10–15 randomly selected axons per section were measured using ImageJ software (NIH, USA) by an observer blinded to the experimental groups. The G-ratio was calculated as the ratio of the inner axonal diameter to the outer myelinated fiber diameter. Myelin sheath thickness was measured as the distance from the inner to the outer boundary of the myelin sheath at four points per axon, and the average was recorded.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). All datasets were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test before statistical testing. For behavioral data (MWT and TWL), which involved repeated measurements over time, a two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Greenhouse-Geisser correction was performed, with group and time as the two factors, followed by Bonferroni-Šídák post hoc test for multiple comparisons. For immunofluorescence quantification data, differences among groups were evaluated using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. For TEM quantification data, the Brown-Forsythe test was used to assess homogeneity of variances; when variances were equal (p > 0.05), ordinary one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test was applied; when the homogeneity assumption was violated, Brown-Forsythe ANOVA with Dunnett T3 post hoc test was used instead. All data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Exact p-values and F statistics are reported in the text and figure legends where applicable. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

MSC administration attenuated nociceptive hypersensitivity in CCI rats

Following CCI surgery, body weight increased over time without significant differences among groups (p > 0.05; Fig. 1A), indicating that neither CCI surgery nor MSC administration adversely affected general health or feeding behavior. Behavioral testing revealed significant effects of group, time, and their interaction on both MWT and TWL. For MWT, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of group (F(2, 27) = 81.09, p < 0.001), a significant main effect of time (F(3.23, 87.32) = 43.31, p < 0.001, Greenhouse-Geisser corrected), and a significant group × time interaction (F(8, 108) = 14.74, p < 0.001). Post hoc comparisons (Bonferroni-Šídák) showed that MWT in the CCI group was significantly lower than the Control group from postoperative day 7 through day 15 (day 7: p < 0.001; day 15: p < 0.001). MSC treatment significantly increased MWT compared with the CCI group at day 15 (p = 0.004; Fig. 1C). For TWL, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of group (F(2, 27) = 8.36, p = 0.0015), a significant main effect of time (F(3.01, 81.27) = 9.68, p < 0.001, Greenhouse-Geisser corrected), and a significant group × time interaction (F(8, 108) = 7.32, p < 0.001). Post hoc comparisons showed that TWL in the CCI group was significantly lower than the Control group from day 7 onward (day 7: p < 0.001; day 15: p < 0.001; Fig. 1D). MSC treatment significantly increased TWL compared with the CCI group at day 11 (adjusted p = 0.045) and day 15 (adjusted p = 0.010). Behavioral changes, such as paw pronation and reduced weight-bearing on the affected side, were also observed (Fig. 1B). These findings demonstrate that intravenous MSC administration significantly attenuated mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity in CCI rats.

MSC treatment is associated with enhanced M2-like macrophage polarization in the DRG

Previous studies have demonstrated that neuropathic pain induced by the CCI model is associated with significant inflammatory cell infiltration in the dorsal root ganglion (DRG) [24]. To characterize the changes in macrophage phenotype in the DRG of CCI rats following MSC administration, immunofluorescence staining was performed on consecutive serial sections to detect CD68 (a pan-macrophage marker) and CD206 (a marker associated with M2-type polarization) in the L5 DRG of each experimental group (Fig. 2A). In comparison with the Control group, a substantial increase in CD68⁺ macrophage infiltration was observed in the DRG of CCI rats, with only a small proportion of these cells showing positive signals for CD206 in matched serial sections. In the MSC-treated group, the excessive infiltration of CD68⁺ macrophages in the DRG was significantly reduced compared with the CCI group, accompanied by a marked enhancement of CD206 signals in corresponding serial sections.

Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity revealed that CD68 expression was significantly lower in the MSC-treated group compared with the CCI group (one-way ANOVA: F(2, 6) = 101.26, p < 0.001; Tukey’s post hoc: Control vs. CCI, p < 0.001; CCI vs. CCI+MSCs, p < 0.001; Fig. 2B). These findings indicate that MSC administration was associated with reduced accumulation of macrophages in the DRG and an enhanced M2-like polarization phenotype.

MSC treatment attenuates sciatic nerve inflammation and is associated with structural repair in CCI rats

Previous studies have indicated that CCI models are associated with structural disorganization of the local sciatic nerve and inflammatory infiltration, both of which are thought to play critical roles in the initiation and maintenance of neuropathic pain [25]. To evaluate the effects of intravenous MSC administration on CCI-induced nerve injury, tissue structural changes in the sciatic nerve on the operated side were assessed by hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining on day 15 post-CCI (Fig. 3A). Compared with the Control group, the CCI group exhibited pronounced tissue disorganization and extensive inflammatory cell infiltration within the nerve bundles. In contrast, the CCI+MSCs group displayed a relatively well-organized tissue structure with markedly fewer inflammatory cells. Quantitative analysis revealed that the number of Schwann cells in the CCI group was significantly reduced compared with the Control group (p < 0.001), while inflammatory cell infiltration was significantly increased (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3B, C). Notably, in the CCI+MSCs group, Schwann cell counts were significantly higher (p < 0.001 vs. CCI) and inflammatory cell numbers were significantly lower (p < 0.001 vs. CCI), suggesting that MSC treatment was associated with attenuated tissue damage and inflammatory response after CCI.

Ultrastructural examination of the myelin sheath and axonal morphology of sciatic nerve fibers was conducted using transmission electron microscopy (Fig. 3D). In the CCI group, severe myelin degeneration and loose, disorganized myelin sheaths were observed. In contrast, the CCI+MSCs group exhibited relatively intact and tightly arranged myelin sheaths. Quantitative analysis demonstrated that the G-ratio was significantly higher in the CCI group compared with the Control group (Brown-Forsythe ANOVA: F(2, 26.68) = 12.73, p < 0.001; Dunnett T3 post hoc: Control vs. CCI, p = 0.0013), and was significantly lower in the CCI+MSCs group compared with the CCI group (CCI vs. CCI+MSCs, p = 0.0013; Fig. 3E). Myelin sheath thickness was significantly reduced in the CCI group compared with the Control group (Brown-Forsythe ANOVA: F(2, 19.70) = 13.74, p < 0.001; Dunnett T3 post hoc: Control vs. CCI, p < 0.001), and was partially restored in the CCI+MSCs group (CCI vs. CCI+MSCs, p = 0.019; Fig. 3F). Axon diameter was also significantly reduced in the CCI group (one-way ANOVA: F(2, 27) = 7.23, p = 0.003; Tukey’s post hoc: Control vs. CCI, p = 0.003), with improvement in the CCI+MSCs group (CCI vs. CCI+MSCs, p = 0.024; Fig. 3G). Collectively, MSC intervention was associated with amelioration of the structural disruption and inflammatory response induced by CCI, and with increased Schwann cell numbers and improved axonal morphology. These observations suggest a potential relationship between MSC administration and a tissue environment more permissive for peripheral nerve repair.

Discussion

Neuropathic pain (NP) presents a significant challenge in clinical management due to its complex and incompletely understood underlying mechanisms [1, 26]. In this study, a rat CCI model of the sciatic nerve was employed, a well-established model of peripheral NP that replicates clinical manifestations such as nociceptive hypersensitivity and hyperalgesia. Using this model, the effects of umbilical cord-derived MSCs on nociceptive hypersensitivity were evaluated. The results demonstrated that intravenous MSC administration was associated with significant attenuation of mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity induced by CCI. Furthermore, MSC treatment was associated with enhanced M2-like macrophage polarization in the DRG and improved structural integrity of the injured sciatic nerve. These findings suggest that intravenously administered MSCs may exert analgesic effects on NP, potentially through modulation of the local immune microenvironment and preservation of peripheral nerve structure. However, as discussed below, the causal relationships among these observations require further investigation.

MSCs are multipotent cells with well-documented immunomodulatory capabilities, and have shown substantial therapeutic potential in various diseases [27–30]. Their beneficial effects are largely attributed to their paracrine activity, including the secretion of growth factors, cytokines, and extracellular vesicles (EVs), thereby suppressing inflammation and promoting tissue repair and regeneration [31–33]. The results of this study are consistent with these properties, demonstrating that intravenous MSC injection was associated with reduced pain behaviors in CCI rats. Both mechanical and thermal pain thresholds were significantly elevated following MSC treatment compared with the CCI group. These findings align with the anti-inflammatory and tissue-protective effects of MSCs reported in other inflammatory and injury models [34–36].

Neuroinflammation, particularly the activation and polarization state of macrophages, is increasingly recognized as an important contributor to the pathogenesis of NP [37, 38]. Pro-inflammatory M1-type macrophages release large amounts of pro-inflammatory cytokines that can exacerbate nerve injury and pain, whereas M2-type macrophages (anti-inflammatory/pro-repair) suppress inflammation and promote tissue repair [39, 40]. Recent studies have specifically highlighted the role of DRG macrophage polarization in neuropathic pain, with M2-skewing associated with pain resolution [41, 42]. In the present study, immunofluorescence staining revealed a significant increase in CD68⁺ macrophages in the DRG of CCI rats, the majority of which lacked CD206 expression, consistent with a predominantly pro-inflammatory phenotype. Following MSC treatment, the total number of CD68⁺ macrophages in the DRG was reduced, and CD206 expression was significantly enhanced. These findings suggest an association between MSC administration and enhanced M2-like polarization of DRG macrophages, which may contribute to a shift in the local immune microenvironment from a pro-inflammatory toward a more anti-inflammatory and pro-reparative state. This observation is consistent with previous studies demonstrating the capacity of MSCs to modulate macrophage polarization in various inflammatory disease models [39, 43, 44]. However, several important caveats must be noted. First, only CD68 (pan-macrophage) and CD206 (M2-associated) markers were examined; M1-associated markers (e.g., iNOS, TNF-α, IL-1β) and M2-associated cytokines (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β) were not measured. Without direct evidence of M1 suppression and functional M2 polarization, the conclusions that can be drawn regarding M1-to-M2 phenotypic switching are limited. The observed changes should therefore be interpreted as an association between MSC treatment and enhanced M2-like polarization, rather than definitive evidence of a causal mechanism. Future studies incorporating comprehensive M1/M2 marker profiling, flow cytometry, and functional cytokine assays are needed to strengthen this mechanistic link.

In addition to the changes observed in the DRG immune microenvironment, this study also found that MSC treatment was associated with structural preservation of the injured peripheral nerve. The CCI model resulted in significant structural disorganization, inflammatory cell infiltration, and demyelination of the sciatic nerve. HE staining revealed that MSC treatment was associated with reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and increased Schwann cell numbers at the injury site. Transmission electron microscopy further demonstrated that MSC-treated animals exhibited improved myelin sheath integrity, as evidenced by a lower G-ratio and greater myelin thickness and axon diameter compared with the untreated CCI group. Since myelin integrity and axonal preservation are critical for maintaining normal nerve conduction and preventing NP [45, 46], these structural observations may partly explain the functional improvement observed in behavioral tests. MSCs may contribute to these effects through the secretion of neurotrophic factors or through indirect modulation of Schwann cell activity [47–49], although the specific mediators were not identified in this study.

Taken together, the findings of this study suggest that intravenously administered MSCs are associated with attenuation of NP-related behaviors in the CCI rat model, accompanied by two potentially interrelated observations: (1) enhanced M2-like macrophage polarization in the DRG, and (2) reduced local inflammation and improved structural integrity of the injured sciatic nerve. These changes may collectively create a microenvironment more conducive to nerve repair and functional recovery. However, it remains to be determined whether these observations reflect direct causal effects of MSCs, or whether they represent parallel or secondary phenomena.

Several important limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as noted above, the mechanistic evidence is correlational rather than causal. Only two macrophage markers (CD68 and CD206) were assessed by immunofluorescence on serial sections, without quantitative profiling of M1-associated markers (e.g., iNOS, TNF-α, IL-1β) or M2-associated cytokines (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β). The use of consecutive serial sections for single-antibody staining also precluded direct quantification of CD68⁺CD206⁺ double-positive cells. The absence of functional assays, such as flow cytometry or cytokine measurements, further limits mechanistic interpretation. Future studies should employ comprehensive immune profiling to validate and extend these findings. Second, human umbilical cord-derived MSCs were administered to immunocompetent rats, representing a xenogeneic transplantation setting. The survival, engraftment, biodistribution, and potential immune rejection of human MSCs in the rat host were not evaluated. It remains unclear whether the observed therapeutic effects are mediated by the administered MSCs directly, by host responses to the xenogeneic cells, or by paracrine factors released before cell clearance. Additionally, it is unknown whether MSCs or their derivatives reached the DRG or the injured sciatic nerve. In vivo cell tracking studies, or the use of syngeneic rat-derived MSCs, would be valuable in future investigations to address these questions. Thirdly, the Control group (sham surgery) did not receive a saline vehicle injection, whereas the CCI group received saline and the CCI+MSCs group received MSCs suspended in saline. The absence of a sham + saline control precludes assessment of potential injection-related effects on the measured outcomes. Additionally, isotype control antibodies were not used in immunofluorescence experiments, which limits the ability to fully exclude non-specific antibody binding. A single dose of 1 × 10⁶ MSCs was used without dose-response evaluation. The optimal dose, dosing frequency, and timing of MSC administration for neuropathic pain remain to be systematically determined. The MSC characterization in this study relied on the manufacturer’s specifications rather than independent verification. While the cells were reported to meet ISCT minimal criteria, confirmatory flow cytometry data were not generated by the investigators. Independent phenotypic validation and functional characterization (e.g., tri-lineage differentiation assays) would strengthen the reproducibility of the findings. The exclusive use of umbilical cord-derived MSCs is another limitation. MSCs from different tissue sources (e.g., bone marrow, adipose tissue) may exhibit distinct functional properties, and the generalizability of these findings to other MSC sources warrants further investigation. Finally, the observation period was limited to 15 days post-CCI. The long-term efficacy, durability of pain relief, and potential adverse effects of MSC treatment, including tumorigenicity and immunogenicity, were not assessed. Extended observation periods and comprehensive safety evaluations are needed before clinical translation can be considered. Future studies could also employ techniques such as single-cell sequencing, proteomics, and specific pathway inhibition to identify the key molecular mediators of the observed effects.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides preclinical evidence that intravenous MSC administration is associated with attenuation of neuropathic pain behaviors and enhanced M2-like macrophage polarization in a rat CCI model. While definitive causal mechanisms remain to be established, these findings warrant further investigation into the immunomodulatory mechanisms of MSCs in peripheral nerve injury and their potential clinical translation as a cell-based therapy for neuropathic pain.
