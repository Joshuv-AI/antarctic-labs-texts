Combination of essential oils from Ocimum americanum L., Ocimum gratissimum L., and Lippia multiflora Mold.: a potential solution for controlling the cattle tick Rhipicephalus microplus (Acari: Ixodidae)
Authors: Anass Coulibaly, B Jethro Delma, Abel S Biguezoton, Firmin F Dah, Adama Kaboré, Martin Kiendrebeogo, Roger C H Nébié
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe cattle tick Rhipicephalus microplus is one of the most significant ectoparasites affecting livestock in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide. This tick causes substantial economic losses in cattle production by reducing weight gain and milk yield and by transmitting pathogens responsible for diseases such as babesiosis and anaplasmosis. Conventional control strategies rely largely on synthetic acaricides; however, their use poses risks to human health and the environment and has contributed to the rapid emergence of resistant tick populations. Under these circumstances, the search for natural acaricidal alternatives has become essential, with essential oils emerging as a promising solution. The present study aimed to evaluate the acaricidal activity of a combination of Ocimum americanum (1:6), Ocimum gratissimum (2:3), and Lippia multiflora (1:6) essential oils against adult R. microplus using the adult immersion test (AIT). Combination indices and dose reduction indices were calculated to assess the interaction effects between the essential oils. The combination completely inhibited oviposition and larval hatching of R. microplus at concentrations of 100 and 80 mg/mL, respectively. Synergistic effects were observed at all tested concentrations, with achieving 93% efficiency starting at 80 mg/mL. These findings highlight the potential of this essential oil combination for developing natural acaricidal formulations to effectively control R. microplus.Keywords: Rhipicephalus microplus, Acaricidal activity, Essential oils combination, Adult Immersion Test, Synergetic interaction, Hatching rates, Oviposition inhibition rates

Introduction

The cattle tick Rhipicephalus microplus is the most widespread tick species in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide and represents a major challenge for cattle breeders (Hurtado & Giraldo-Ríos 2019). This species causes substantial economic losses through severe blood loss and reduced weight gain in infested cattle (Heylen et al. 2023). In addition, it acts as a vector for several pathogens, including Babesia spp. and Anaplasma marginale (Kumar et al., 2019). Synthetic acaricides have played a central role in controlling R. microplus populations; however, their intensive and indiscriminate use has led to the emergence of resistant tick strains (Abbas et al. 2014). Moreover, these products adversely affect non-target organisms and pose risks to human health due to toxic residues in milk and meat (Ajith Kumar et al. 2016). In light of these challenges, there is a pressing need to identify alternative control strategies that are both effective and environmentally sustainable. In this context, plant-derived products, particularly essential oils (EOs), represent a promising source of novel acaricides (Adenubi et al. 2016; Benelli et al. 2016; Selles et al. 2021).

EOs are complex natural mixtures comprising approximately 20–60 bioactive constituents present at varying concentrations (Bakkali et al. 2008). Their use may contribute to delaying the development of resistance due to the presence of multiple active compounds with different modes of action targeting several molecular sites simultaneously, thereby reducing selective pressure (Wang et al. 2007). Furthermore, several studies have reported synergistic effects when essential oils are used in combination (Balboné et al. 2022; Jyoti et al. 2019; Novato et al. 2018). Such synergistic formulations are often more effective than individual oils and allow for reduced concentrations of each component in the final mixture, which may contribute to lowering overall toxicity.

Ocimum americanum, Ocimum gratissimum, and Lippia multiflora are aromatic plant species commonly found in Burkina Faso, whose EOs exhibit diverse biological activities (Balboné et al. 2022; Bayala et al. 2020; Kiendrebeogo et al. 2011). In a previous study, the acaricidal activity of several combinations of EOs derived from these plants was evaluated against R. microplus larvae (Coulibaly et al. 2023). Among these combinations, the one containing EOs of O. americanum, O. gratissimum, and L. multiflora in proportions of 1/6, 2/3, and 1/6, respectively, exhibited the highest acaricidal activity.

The present study therefore aimed to assess the acaricidal activity of this combination against adult R. microplus ticks. The results obtained could lead to the development of an effective bioacaricide to control the R. microplus tick.

Materials and methods

Plant material

The plant material consisted of aerial parts (stems, leaves, and flowers) of three plants: Ocimum americanum L., Ocimum gratissimum L., and Lippia multiflora Mold. These species were collected in Burkina Faso in November 2023. O. americanum and O. gratissimum were harvested from the Central region (12.41315, − 1.49649), while L. multiflora was collected from the Western region (11.405315, − 4.415665). A specimen of each species was authenticated by Dr Issouf ZERBO and deposited in the herbarium of Joseph KI-ZERBO University under numbers 6949 (O. americanum), 6951 (O. gratissimum), and 6931 (L. multiflora).

Origin of ticks and extraction of essential oils

Adult female Rhipicephalus microplus ticks were obtained through artificial infestation of cattle at CIRDES. This procedure was approved by the National Ethics Committee on Animal Experiments of Burkina Faso under reference CE-UJKZ/2023–07. EOs were obtained by steam distillation of the fresh aerial parts of each plant for 3 h using a Clevenger-type apparatus. The collected oils were dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate and stored in amber glass bottles at 4 °C until use.

Preparation of the combination and analysis of essential oils

The essential oil combination evaluated in this study was prepared by blending the EOs of O. americanum, O. gratissimum, and L. multiflora in proportions of 1:6, 2:3, and 1:6, respectively (For example, in a 3 mL mixture, the essential oil of O. gratissimum accounted for 2 mL, while those of O. americanum and L. multiflora each contributed 0.5 mL). Chemical analyses were performed using an Agilent gas chromatograph (Agilent 7890 A, Palo Alto, CA, USA) equipped with a DB-5 capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm film thickness) and a flame ionization detector (FID), coupled to an Agilent 5975 mass spectrometer (MS) (Coulibaly et al. 2023).

Determination of acaricidal activity

The acaricidal activity of the EO combination was evaluated using the adult immersion test, following the protocol described by Drummond et al. (1973) with minor modifications. Eight concentrations, obtained by geometric serial dilution, were tested: 1, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, and 100 mg/mL. Tween 20 (3%) was used as the negative control, while deltamethrin (2% or 20 mg/mL) served as the positive control (Adehan et al., 2016). Groups of ten adult female ticks, each weighing at least 0.2 g, were immersed in 10 mL of the test or control solution for 1 min. After immersion, ticks were removed, dried on Whatman filter paper, and placed individually in Petri dishes. The same procedure was applied for each concentration in ascending order. For each concentration of the combination, three replicates were performed by treating three groups of 10 ticks. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.1. Mean values for each treatment were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test at a significance level of 5%. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The drc package was used to perform non-linear regression analysis of the dose–mortality data. Lethal concentrations (LC₅₀ and LC₉₀) were estimated by selecting the models that best fitted the data, based on the lowest residual variance, using the mselect function implemented in the drc R package. Four parameters: b (slope), c (lower asymptote), d (upper asymptote), and e (ED₅₀) were estimated using a selected generalized log-logistic function (Ritz et al. 2015).

Results and discussion

The effects of the individual EOs and their combination on R. microplus adult female resulted in direct mortality, and the corresponding results are presented in Table 1.

The results showed that the combination of the three essential oils induced more than 91% mortality in adult females at a concentration of 80 mg/mL. The LC₅₀ and LC₉₀ values were 8.24 and 77.61 mg/mL, respectively, whereas none of the individual essential oils in the combination achieved 50% mortality when tested alone. In contrast, no mortality of adult females was observed with deltamethrin. To our knowledge, this study constitutes the first report on the effects of a combination of EOs from O. americanum, O. gratissimum, and L. multiflora on the mortality of adult female R. microplus. However, Vinturelle et al. (2017) reported that a combination of Citrus limonum and Piper nigrum essential oils caused 100% mortality in adult female R. microplus, with LC₅₀ and LC₉₀ values of 2.2 and 5.1 mg/mL, respectively. Similarly, a combination of Copaifera officinalis and Laurus nobilis essential oils was shown to induce 98.9% mortality at 5 mg/mL (Vinturelle et al. 2021).

These results demonstrate that the combination of the three EOs exhibited synergistic activity (CI < 1) at all tested concentrations, indicating a synergistic interaction among O. americanum, O. gratissimum, and L. multiflora EOs against adult R. microplus. Moreover, the combination showed a favorable dose-reduction effect (DRI > 1) across all concentrations, indicating that the effective concentrations in the mixture were lower than those required when the essential oils were applied individually. The DRI values of O. americanum and L. multiflora EOs were higher than those of O. gratissimum. However, the DRI of O. americanum was close to that of L. multiflora. This means that the combination of the three EOs is more active when the proportion of O. gratissimum EO is higher and those of O. americanum and L. multiflora are lower. These findings indicate that the relative proportions of individual essential oils within a mixture can significantly influence the overall efficacy of the combination.

These results indicate that the combination was highly effective in inhibiting oviposition in adult females, achieving 100% inhibition at 100 mg/mL. Among the individual EOs, the most active (O. gratissimum EO) induced 81.44% inhibition at the same concentration. Regarding larval hatching, the combination completely reduced this reproductive parameter at 80 mg/mL, whereas the most active individual essential oil in the mixture (O. gratissimum EO) reduced hatching by only 67.67% at this concentration. In all samples tested, the combination consistently exhibited greater inhibitory effects on oviposition and larval hatching than the individual EOs and deltamethrin.

The inhibition of oviposition and larval hatching in adult R. microplus by combinations of EOs from O. americanum, O. gratissimum, and L. multiflora has not been previously reported. However, the results align with those of Díaz Lazcano et al. (2019), who demonstrated that a combination of EOs from Cinnamomum zeylanicum, Cuminum cyminum, and Pimenta dioica inhibited oviposition in adult R. microplus at rates ranging from 34% to over 74%. When these EOs were mixed in proportions of 2/3, 1/6, and 1/6 for C. zeylanicum, C. cyminum, and P. dioica, respectively, larval hatching of R. microplus was completely inhibited.

The acaricidal activity of the combination was compared with that of deltamethrin, a product commonly used by breeders in Burkina Faso for the control of R. microplus (Ouedraogo et al. 2021). Despite being applied at a relatively high concentration (2%), deltamethrin exhibited practically no effect on any of the reproductive parameters evaluated, including mortality rate, oviposition inhibition, and larval hatching inhibition. These findings could confirm the resistance of R. microplus populations to this synthetic compound, as previously reported (Ouedraogo et al. 2021; Kandé, 2014; Yessinou et al. 2016).

Regarding overall efficiency, the combination achieved values exceeding 90% from 80 mg/mL. In contrast, all individual EOs included in the combination showed global efficiencies below 65%, while that of deltamethrin was less than 1%. Comparable global efficiencies for combinations ranging from 65.5% to 100% and from 40.6% to 100% were reported by Díaz Lazcano et al. (2019) and Vinturelle et al. (2021), respectively.

Previous studies have demonstrated that the biological activity of an EO is closely related to its chemical composition (De Sousa et al. 2023). The three EOs evaluated in this study were predominantly composed of monoterpenes (Coulibaly et al. 2023). The main constituents of O. americanum EO were 1,8-cineole (38.6%) and camphor (21.8%). O. gratissimum EO was rich in p-cymene (10.9%), γ-terpinene (17.3%), and thymol (34.5%), while L. multiflora EO contained p-cymene (25.4%), γ-terpinene (8.5%), and β-caryophyllene (12.6%) as major constituents. Except β-caryophyllene, the acaricidal activities of these compounds have been widely documented. Several studies have reported the acaricidal activity of 1,8-cineole against R. microplus (Castro et al. 2018; Jaiswal et al. 2025). Yang et al. (2021) demonstrated the strong acaricidal effect of camphor on this tick species. The acaricidal properties of thymol, p-cymene, and γ-terpinene against R. microplus have also been described in numerous investigations (Cardoso et al. 2020; Novato et al. 2022; Silva et al. 2017). The acaricidal activity observed in the present study with the EO combination could therefore be related to interactions between these various chemical constituents (Novato et al. 2018).

This study evaluated the effect of a combination of essential oils from O. americanum, O. gratissimum, and L. multiflora in proportions of 1:6, 2:3, and 1:6, respectively, on adult R. microplus. The combination exhibited synergistic effects at all tested concentrations and proved to be active against adult ticks, particularly through the inhibition of oviposition and larval hatching. This acaricidal activity is probably attributable to interactions of the chemical compounds present in the mixture. The findings of this study may contribute to the development of an effective bioacaricide based on this combination for the control of cattle ticks. However, the lack of data on the toxicity of these essential oils, particularly with respect to host animals and the environment, constitutes a major limitation to their practical application. Furthermore, the mechanisms of action underlying the observed synergistic effects were not investigated, thereby highlighting the need for further studies to elucidate better the interactions among the chemical constituents of the combinations and to optimize their use in bio-acaricidal formulations.
