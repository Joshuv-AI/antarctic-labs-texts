In-situ efficiency and parameter estimation for induction motors using heuristic optimization
Authors: Murat Göztaş, Mehmet Akif Sahman, Mehmet Çunkaş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectric motors, especially three-phase induction motors, consume a significant portion of industrial electricity, making their efficiency critical for energy conservation. Traditional efficiency assessments require intrusive, labor-intensive laboratory tests, limiting in-situ evaluations. We propose a non-intrusive, heuristic optimization-based method compliant with IEEE Std-112 A/F1 to simultaneously estimate equivalent circuit parameters and in-situ efficiency using only field-measurable electrical quantities. Eight meta-heuristic algorithms were applied across six motors of varying power ratings and load conditions, with results validated against standard test data. The method achieved mean absolute errors below 0.7% at full load, demonstrating high accuracy and stability. This approach enables reliable, low-cost, and continuous efficiency monitoring under real operating conditions, offering practical benefits for industrial energy management and motor performance assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-34932-1.Keywords: Induction motor efficiency, Equivalent circuit parameter estimation, Heuristic optimization algorithms, Non-intrusive in-situ measurement, IEEE std-112 complianceSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In recent years, particularly since the energy crisis of the 1970s and the rapid industrialization that followed, concerns over energy consumption and efficient energy use have become increasingly prominent1. Electric motors consume a significant proportion of the electricity produced in industry, accounting for two-thirds of the total. This increases both energy costs and greenhouse gas emissions. Consequently, it boosts energy demand while exacerbating environmental problems2. Similar patterns are observed in developing countries such as South Africa, where motorized systems account for 60% of total electricity consumption. In contrast, developed countries like the US and Canada have responded to rising energy costs and sustainability concerns by implementing demand management programs aimed at improving energy efficiency3.

Given this global emphasis on energy conservation, the role of electric motors becomes even more critical. Due to their simple design, durability, and low maintenance costs, induction motors (IMs) are preferred and used in industry. Nearly all motors used in industry, 99.7% to be exact, are below 500 horsepower. The fact that these motors are operated at 60% of their rated load causes significant efficiency losses4. Consequently, these efficiency losses reduce energy efficiency and lead to economic losses. In response to rising energy expenses associated with inefficient motor operation, manufacturers have been motivated to develop more efficient electric motors5.

However, improving motor efficiency requires not only better motor design but also accurate assessment of the efficiency of motors already in operation. In industrial applications, low-efficiency machines draw significantly more power to deliver the same output, making energy efficiency a critical concern. Although manufacturers provide nameplate efficiency data, these values may not always be reliable1. According to international standards (IEEE 112, IEC 60,034–2-1), accurate efficiency determination requires laboratory-based testing under controlled unloaded conditions. However, such procedures are labor-intensive, time-consuming, and impractical for many businesses6. As a result, there is a strong need for reliable, non-intrusive efficiency estimation methods that can be applied directly in the field while the motors remain in operation7.

Accurate determination of equivalent-circuit parameters is essential for the modeling, simulation, and control of induction motors. Although manufacturers typically provide these parameters, they are often unavailable or incomplete. Direct measurement of unknown parameters using specialized equipment is generally impractical for operational motors8. Parameter estimation methods are therefore widely used; however, the limited availability of measurement data and the fact that the parameters cannot be expressed in a closed-form mathematical solution make the estimation process challenging9. Because equivalent-circuit parameters directly affect the accuracy of both motor modeling and efficiency estimation, uncertainties in these parameters can significantly reduce the reliability of efficiency calculations. Conventional standards such as IEEE Std. 112-A/B (direct input–output tests) and IEEE Std. 112-F1 (segregation of losses) are considered the benchmarks for efficiency determination10,11. Nonetheless, these methods require costly laboratory facilities, dynamometers, and torque transducers, making them unsuitable for in-situ applications6,10. To address these limitations, researchers have developed non-intrusive efficiency estimation techniques based on identifying equivalent-circuit parameters from field measurements (voltage, current, power, and speed)11–14. Heuristic and meta-heuristic optimization algorithms, including PSO, GA, WOA, GWO, and various hybrid approaches, have shown promise in reducing modeling errors between measured and simulated performance15–18. Besides these efforts, GWO, WOA, and GA have been widely applied in control-oriented applications, including direct torque control and maximum power point tracking19,20.

However, several important research gaps remain:

Most studies employ only one or two optimization algorithms, limiting the ability to evaluate robustness across a wider algorithmic set11,14,21.

Existing research predominantly focuses on low-power motors (< 10 kW), while medium- and high-power machines—which account for the majority of industrial electricity consumption—are insufficiently investigated.

Parameter constraints are often chosen arbitrarily, which can result in slow convergence, premature stagnation, or unstable solutions.15.

In this study, a comprehensive approach is proposed for both equivalent-circuit parameter estimation and non-intrusive efficiency determination of three-phase induction motors. The approach employs eight state-of-the-art heuristic optimization algorithms (PSO, GA, GWO, HHO, TSA, DSA, WOA, and FOX) to estimate the per-phase equivalent-circuit parameters using only field-measurable quantities such as voltage, current, input power, and speed. Two estimation schemes (Method I and Method II) were developed to address uncertainty in nameplate output power and to evaluate the robustness of the algorithms under different constraint formulations. The proposed method was applied to six induction motors with different power ratings, enabling systematic comparison of algorithm performance across four load levels and validating the results against IEEE Std-112 A/F1 efficiency measurements.

The results show that the proposed methodology provides high accuracy—particularly at full load—without requiring intrusive laboratory tests, dynamometers, or torque sensors. By integrating physics-based parameter-range constraints with heuristic optimization, the approach improves convergence stability, reduces modeling errors, and enhances repeatability across different motor sizes. Literature review

Today, induction motors are a fundamental component of industry and are widely used in applications ranging from compressors and conveyors to rail systems and electric vehicles. Their popularity stems from their low cost, simple design, robustness, and minimal maintenance requirements22,23. The frequent use of induction motors and current energy efficiency concerns have made in-situ efficiency estimation a critical issue. In real industrial environments, stopping motors from performing traditional laboratory tests is impractical and can disrupt operations. Therefore, there is a growing need for methods that can estimate motor efficiency directly in the field, without interrupting operation or requiring intrusive procedures24. This has attracted the attention of researchers.

In the equivalent-circuit approach, motor efficiency can be evaluated not only under the measured operating conditions but also across different load levels. Once the equivalent-circuit parameters are accurately identified, it becomes possible to compute performance at various loading stages without performing additional tests25. Optimization algorithms, especially heuristic methods, are widely used for parameter estimation due to their flexibility and ability to effectively search for large solution spaces. Relevant studies in the literature are summarized below.

Several studies in the literature have focused on estimating induction motor efficiency and equivalent-circuit parameters using heuristic and meta-heuristic optimization techniques.

Pillay et al.1 employed genetic algorithms for efficiency estimation and showed that using measurements from two loading points and incorporating additional constraints improves accuracy. They also demonstrated that incorporating rated power in the objective function reduces errors when output power remains close to its nominal value. Charette et al.26 further advanced GA-based non-intrusive efficiency estimation by accounting for temperature effects at multiple load points, achieving errors below 1%. Similarly, Sakthivel et al.12 proposed an immune-algorithm-based approach using a modified equivalent circuit that includes rotor leakage load loss, validating its accuracy against torque-sensor measurement methods.

Other studies have explored multi-algorithm comparisons and the limitations of traditional parameter estimation. Lu et al.27 evaluated Newton–Raphson, Simulated Annealing, and PSO using input-impedance phasor data, but the method suffered from sensitivity to operating-point selection and simplified treatment of stray-load loss, yielding errors around 2–3%. Sakthivel et al.13,28 also applied a bacterial foraging algorithm with a modified equivalent circuit that models PSLL through an added rotor-series resistance. While multi-load (25–100%) measurements improved accuracy, errors above 3% remained—particularly under low-load conditions—due to sensitivity to operating points and modeling assumptions. Thangaraj et al.14 compared GA, PSO, and Differential Evolution (DE) for in-situ efficiency estimation on a 5 hp motor, while Chandrakanth et al.29 used PSO and validated results through physical measurements, achieving low error across multiple objective function formulations.

Several researchers have aimed to improve the robustness of the estimation process by modifying objective functions or broadening the measurement set. Al-Badri et al.11 used a three-layer GA structure incorporating input power, current, phase angle, and output power. Although accuracy improved, reliance on output power is a disadvantage in practical conditions where this information is rarely available. Siraki and Pillay30 proposed an evolutionary-based algorithm requiring only current and active power, which performs well under balanced and unbalanced conditions and avoids intrusive no-load tests.

Studies focusing on parameter-range formulation and search-space improvement have also emerged. Bijan et al.15 optimized parameter consraints using GA and validated the method on three motors. Diarra et al.31 introduced a hybrid QPSO–TRA technique that improves parameter determination, reduces computational burden, and enables real-time monitoring. Selek and Terzioğlu16 applied DEA and PSO for parameter identification, illustrating that nonlinear and time-varying parameters can be estimated with low error. Chayakulkheeree et al.17 proposed a PSO-based equivalent-circuit estimation method (PSOBECE), demonstrating significantly higher accuracy than slip and current-based techniques.

Additional contributions include Pillay et al.32 who showed that GA offers greater robustness and accuracy than Newton–Raphson for transient torque prediction using limited manufacturer data. Ramos and Lopez33 assessed three evolutionary algorithms statistically for parameter estimation, while Sun et al.34 estimated motor load and efficiency using PSO with hysteresis breakdown behavior. Guo et al.18 used GWO to identify optimal magnetic flux, showing efficiency improvements at the optimum point. Silva et al.35 investigated temperature, magnetic saturation, and skin-effect influences on efficiency. Srited and Chayakulkheeree36 obtained equivalent-circuit parameters by measuring voltage, current, real power, and power factor at multiple operating points, demonstrating high estimation accuracy. Bhowmick and Chowdhury37 propose a PSO-based method to estimate the equivalent circuit parameters, losses, and rotor inertia of three-phase induction motors. Relying on transient current and voltage data, the proposed approach provides a high-accuracy and low-cost monitoring solution suitable for motor control and fault diagnosis applications. Geravandi and Moradi38 present an air-gap torque (AGT)-based non-intrusive technique for estimating the efficiency of low-voltage induction motors using only nameplate information and line measurements. The method demonstrates significantly higher accuracy than conventional AGT approaches, even under unbalanced supply or harmonic distortion conditions. Manoharan et al.39 propose an enhanced algorithm termed Adaptive Weighted Grey Wolf Optimization (AWGWO) for identifying the complex internal parameters of commercial induction motors. Experimental validation on eight different motor models confirms that the proposed algorithm achieves faster convergence and higher solution accuracy compared to existing methods. Paramo-Balsa et al.40 introduce a novel non-intrusive approach for load and efficiency estimation without interrupting motor operation. By optimizing models based on input power and speed measurements, the method outperforms conventional techniques, achieving estimation errors below 3.5%.

Overall, the literature demonstrates that heuristic and meta-heuristic approaches offer a practical foundation for non-intrusive efficiency estimation. However, existing methods are often limited by a narrow range of algorithms, restricted validation scope, and insufficiently defined parameter constraints. Most prior studies concentrate on small motors or simplified models, resulting in limited understanding of medium- and high-power industrial machines. To overcome these gaps, this study proposes an integrated approach that applies eight meta-heuristic algorithms together with nameplate-based parameter coinsraints to estimate both the equivalent-circuit parameters and efficiency. The methods were tested on six motors at different load levels and validated using IEEE Std-112 A/F1 procedures. These results provide a broader and experimentally supported foundation for in-situ efficiency assessment and offer a practical tool for industrial monitoring and energy-efficiency applications.

Methodology

Proposed method

For efficiency estimation, heuristic algorithms can be employed to identify the equivalent-circuit parameters of induction motors, after which the efficiency is calculated in full compliance with IEEE Std-112 Form F2/Method F1 procedures41. In the literature, such approaches are generally referred to as optimization-based efficiency estimation methods. However, the effectiveness of these algorithms strongly depends on the accurate specification of the permissible search ranges for each parameter. Improperly defined bounds may lead to slow convergence or physically unrealistic solutions. To address this issue, the present study introduces a systematic technique that determines realistic parameter ranges using nameplate information and manufacturer data provided by AEMOT Electric Motors & Generators. This range-determination approach was then integrated into various optimization algorithms and incorporated into the overall efficiency estimation method.

The proposed approach was evaluated under two different scenarios to assess its performance. In the first scenario, the objective function was constructed using only the stator current, input power, and the phase angle between the stator voltage and current. In this case, the motor’s nominal power was intentionally excluded, and efficiency estimation relied solely on the parameter bounds determined by the proposed range-determination technique. In the second scenario, the nominal power value was additionally incorporated into the objective function, enabling the optimization algorithm to utilize both the range constraints and the motor’s rated operating characteristics. The results obtained from these two scenarios were compared with each other and further validated against experimentally measured data to evaluate their accuracy and robustness.

Figure 1 illustrates flowchart of heuristic-based efficiency estimation method. The process begins with in-situ acquisition of electrical and mechanical measurements, including stator voltage, current, input power, and shaft speed. Based on these measurements, parameter search ranges are defined using a combination of nameplate specifications and manufacturer data. An initial population of candidate solutions is then generated for the optimization algorithm. During each optimization iteration, the objective function is evaluated, and candidate solutions are updated according to the specific heuristic technique employed (PSO, GA, GWO, etc.), while the best-performing candidates are retained. This iterative process continues until the convergence criterion is satisfied. Once convergence is reached, the set of equivalent-circuit parameters corresponding to the best candidate is selected. Using these parameters, motor efficiency is computed under different load conditions. Finally, the estimated efficiency values are compared with the reference IEEE Std-112 A/F1 results to validate the accuracy of the proposed method.

Each parameter has a distinct physical meaning that influences the torque–speed characteristic, efficiency, and power factor of the motor. Stator resistance is closely related to copper losses and varies with temperature, typically accounting for 25–40% of total losses43. Rotor resistance affects the shape of the torque–speed curve and determines the slip at which maximum torque occurs44. The magnetizing reactance and core loss resistance represent the magnetization branch, modeling the energy conversion and magnetic losses in the stator core9.

The stator resistance affects both the shape of the torque–speed characteristic and the slip at which the breakdown torque occurs44. Optimization algorithms

Inspired by intelligent behaviors observed in nature, these algorithms iteratively search for optimal or near-optimal solutions within predefined bounds and constraints. Although they do not guarantee the global optimum, they typically achieve solutions very close to it within reasonable computational time, making them highly effective for complex and nonlinear engineering problems. In this section, the meta-heuristic algorithms employed in the study are introduced.

Certain differences were observed between the efficiency results obtained from the two methods. These discrepancies mainly arise from uncertainties in the calculation of equivalent circuit parameters. In particular, the reliance on experimental data for stray load losses, the approximate estimation of motor temperature based on load conditions, and the way core losses are determined are significant sources of error. Although core losses increase with motor load in practice, they are represented by a constant resistance in the equivalent circuit model and assumed to be fixed across all load levels. This assumption may lead to deviations in efficiency estimation, especially at higher loads. As the load increases, the voltage drops across and increase, whereas the voltage across decreases, resulting in reduced core losses. Therefore, the primary reason why the efficiency values obtained from the equivalent circuit method appear lower than those measured directly using Method A at low loads is the assumption of constant core losses. For this reason, the efficiency values directly measured using Method A were taken as the reference in the subsequent analyses, and the efficiency results estimated by the optimization algorithms were compared against these reference values.

The relatively higher estimation error observed at 25% load can be primarily attributed to the assumptions adopted for core-loss resistance and stray-load losses in non-intrusive efficiency estimation methods. At light-load operation, the magnetizing current constitutes a larger portion of the stator current, increasing the relative contribution of iron losses. Since core losses are assumed to be load-independent in IEEE Std. 112-based formulations, this simplification leads to higher uncertainty under partial-load conditions41,58. Moreover, stray-load losses are commonly modeled using fixed ratios recommended in IEC 60,034–2-1, although previous studies indicate that these losses vary nonlinearly with load11,48. Consequently, the increased error at 25% load reflects inherent modeling assumptions rather than limitations of the proposed optimization algorithms.

Discussion

In Appendix A (see supplementary file), the convergence graphs of the parameters (, , and ) obtained from the first two runs of the algorithms (GA, PSO, DS, TSA, GWO, WOA, FOX, and HHO) for the AE_280M4A_90 kW motor are presented. The analyses indicate that all algorithms exhibit very high estimation accuracy for the parameter. For nearly all motors, the algorithms successfully estimated the value with close agreement to the IEEE 112-F reference value. Among these, the DS, PSO, and TSA algorithms demonstrated the highest consistency.

For the parameter, the estimated values generally exhibited low deviation levels. In most cases, the estimates approached the IEEE 112-F reference values within ± 0.03 Ω. The GA algorithm was observed to produce more inaccurate results for in some motors, whereas the PSO, HHO, DS, and FOX algorithms provided more accurate estimations for this parameter.

Regarding the parameter, the performance of the algorithms was generally consistent. The estimated values typically remained within ± 1.0–2.0 Ω of the reference value, indicating that the algorithms achieved a high level of accuracy for . In particular, the TSA algorithm produced estimates that matched the reference value exactly for certain motors and demonstrated stability, especially alongside the DS and PSO algorithms.

The parameter emerged as the parameter with the highest deviation across all motors. The estimated values often exceeded the IEEE 112-F reference values by 20% to 100%. This outcome is attributed to the limited set of input measurements available for induction motor parameter estimation. However, is the parameter that has the least influence on the efficiency characteristics of an induction motor. Therefore, the deviations observed in have minimal impact on the overall motor efficiency analysis.

“In-situ efficiency estimation for induction motor” section revealed important findings into the in-situ efficiency estimation performance. For all six motors, the full-load estimation errors remained below 0.7% across all algorithms, demonstrating that the proposed heuristic-based approach can reliably reproduce IEEE 112-A reference efficiencies under real operating conditions. At low load levels, particularly at 25%, all algorithms exhibited higher errors, reflecting the reduced observability of motor characteristics in lightly loaded operation. PSO, WOA, and DS consistently delivered the lowest error values across multiple load levels, while GA and FOX tended to show higher deviations, especially at low loads. Furthermore, Method II provided slightly lower average errors for several algorithms due to its use of rated power, whereas Method I gives a good result in situations where nameplate data may be uncertain.

Additionally, Figs. 9 and 10 present the convergence, efficiency, and torque curves obtained using the parameters estimated by the WOA and DS algorithms for the AE_280M4A_90 kW motor under Method I. The torque and efficiency curves generated using these estimated parameters exhibit a high degree of similarity and strong agreement with those obtained from the IEEE Std 112-F method.

Compared to conventional in-situ efficiency estimation methods reported in the literature—which typically rely on a single optimization algorithm, limited motor power ranges, or intrusive laboratory measurements—the proposed approach provides a broader and more robust framework. Classical IEEE Std-112 Method A requires direct torque measurement and laboratory infrastructure, while Method F1 depends on no-load and locked-rotor tests. In contrast, the proposed method achieves comparable accuracy using only field-measurable quantities, without interrupting motor operation. Furthermore, while most previous studies report efficiency errors in the range of 1–3% and focus on low-power motors, the proposed approach maintains full-load errors below 0.7% across six motors ranging from 1.1 kW to 132 kW. The inclusion of eight meta-heuristic algorithms and statistical robustness analysis further distinguishes this study from earlier works.

The proposed method is particularly suitable for industrial environments where induction motors operate continuously and cannot be removed from service for laboratory-based testing. Since the approach relies solely on field-measurable electrical quantities—such as voltage, current, input power, and speed—it can be applied during normal operation without production downtime. This makes the method practical for routine energy audits, condition-based maintenance, and efficiency monitoring in industrial facilities.

However, several limitations should be noted. Estimation accuracy decreases under light-load conditions (25%), which is attributed to reduced parameter observability and inherent assumptions of the equivalent-circuit model. In addition, Method II relies on accurate nameplate data; therefore, Method I is preferable for motors with uncertain or altered nameplate information (e.g., rewound motors). Finally, the use of meta-heuristic algorithms introduces higher computational effort compared to analytical methods, limiting real-time embedded implementation.

Conclusions

This study introduces a practical and non-intrusive approach for estimating the efficiency and equivalent-circuit parameters of three-phase induction motors under real operating conditions. By combining field-measurable electrical quantities with heuristic optimization, the method provides a flexible and low-cost alternative to traditional laboratory-based testing. Eight meta-heuristic algorithms—PSO, GA, GWO, HHO, TSA, DSA, WOA, and FOX—were evaluated across six motors with different power ratings and load levels using two estimation schemes (Method I and Method II) to assess their accuracy and robustness. The results demonstrate that both methods achieve high estimation accuracy, with the mean absolute error between measured and estimated efficiency values remaining below 0.7% at full load for all algorithms. Method II yielded lower overall error levels and more stable convergence, particularly under medium and high load conditions. Among the algorithms, PSO, WOA, and DSA produced the most accurate and repeatable efficiency estimates, whereas GA and FOX exhibited relatively higher deviations, especially at low-load operating points.

Parameter estimation results confirm that the proposed method can closely reproduce IEEE 112-F reference values for parameters such as , , and . The strong agreement observed between the simulated and experimentally obtained torque–efficiency curves reinforce the validity of the method and demonstrates that the estimated parameters remain physically meaningful across different motors and operating points.

Overall, the proposed method offers a practical solution for industrial energy-efficiency assessments and motor performance monitoring applications. By enabling continuous and reliable tracking of motor efficiency under real operating conditions, it contributes to energy management tasks.
