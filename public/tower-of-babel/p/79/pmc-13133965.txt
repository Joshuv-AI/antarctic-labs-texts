Phylogeny of Wolbachia strains in populations of Aedes aegypti and Culex pipiens in Hormozgan Province, South of Iran
Authors: Alireza Sanei-Dehkordi, Amir Hossein Roozitalab, Saeed Shahabi, Mehrnaz Rahimi, Zahra Rousta, Raziyeh Shahheidari, Elahe Golestani, Azim Paksa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAedes aegypti, and Culex pipiens are the primary vector of human arboviral diseases, which represent an important public health problem. Controlling mosquitoes with Wolbachia as an endosymbiont bacterium is a new approach in this field. This study was conducted to detect Wolbachia infection in Aedes aegypti and Culex pipiens in Hormozgan Province, South of Iran.Material and methodSpecimens of Ae. aegypti and Cx. pipiens were collected from 10 localities in Bandar Abbas City, Hormozgan province of southern Iran. All samples were identified using valid identification keys. After DNA identification by Collin's method, genomic DNA of the mosquitoes were used for detection of Wolbachia infection by Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) amplification of Wolbachia surface protein (wsp gene).ResultsIn this study, a total, 460 Ae. aegypti and 716 Cx. pipiens were collected. From a total of 358 adult Cx. pipiens screened using the wsp primer, 212 (59.22%) Cx. pipiens mosquitoes infected Wolbachia. The Cx. pipiens wsp sample sequences were similar to Wolbachia strains belonging to supergroups B. Out of a total of 230 Ae. aegypti investigations of Wolbachia infection, the results showed that there is no Wolbachia infection in this species.ConclusionsBy utilizing wsp molecular maker, our study demonstrated the presence of supergroup B strain of Wolbachia in individual Cx. pipiens samples. Although no Wolbachia infection in Ae. aegypti was detected in present study but it is possible to introduce Wolbachia populations with Wolbachia-free populations by infecting this species and it can be used as a promising tool for controlling vectors and reducing the transmission of arboviral diseases by cytoplasmic incompatibility.Keywords: Wolbachia, Mosquito, Wsp, Supergroups, Arbovirus, Intracellular symbionts

Introduction

Mosquitoes such as in Aedes aegypti and Culex pipiens are the most important vectors of arbovirus diseases and transmit diseases such as dengue, yellow fever, Zika, chikungunya and West Nile virus to humans (Escobar et al., 2022; Azizi et al., 2023; Paksa et al., 2023). Widespread infections of mosquitoes in different continents have led to several outbreaks of arboviruses during the last five decades and threaten the emergence of new potential arboviruses (Espinal et al., 2019; Zambrano et al., 2019; Dorzaban et al., 2022).

Problems such as increasing insect resistance to insecticides, drug resistance in parasites and the lack of vaccines for many vector-borne diseases have forced researchers to find a new and advanced way to control vectors (Roberts and Andre, 1994; Belluco et al., 2023). Today, one of these methods is to use the advanced advantages of Wolbachia bacteria to control vectors (Iturbe-Ormaetxe et al., 2011; Minwuyelet et al., 2023).

Wolbachia is a natural intracellular symbiotic bacterium that is inherited from mother to the next generation and can manipulate host reproduction through mechanisms such as cytoplasmic incompatibility, thereby influencing host fitness (Hochstrasser, 2023; Sanaei et al., 2021). Between 22% - 76% of all insect species are infected with Wolbachia (Minwuyelet et al., 2023; Jeyaprakash and Hoy, 2000). Wolbachia can have wide-ranging effects on insect performance and pathogenicity. Wolbachia depend on host cell resources for reproduction and live in the cytoplasm of host cells. The primary mode of Wolbachia transmission is vertical inheritance from mother to offspring, while horizontal transmission across species occurs rarely (Ahmed et al., 2015; O'Neill et al., 1992; Jiggins and Hurst, 2011). Considering that Wolbachia can interfere with virus transmission among mosquito populations, it has led to interest in using Wolbachia to control mosquito transmission of arboviruses (Bourtzis et al., 2014; Hoffmann et al., 2011).

There are 17 major supergroups (A-Q) of Wolbachia that infect arthropods such as insects. Wolbachia alter insect reproduction in a number of ways including: cytoplasmic incompatibility, sperm-egg incompatibility, feminization of genetic males, thelytoky or parthenogenesis induction, and son killing. Therefore, utilizing these effects in medically important mosquito vectors has led to significant progress over the past two decades (Wang et al., 2016; Ma et al., 2014).

In cytoplasmic incompatibility (CI) by Wolbachia, the reproductive system is manipulated in insects, which increases the proportion of Wolbachia-infected individuals in the population (Ma et al., 2014). Under these conditions, Wolbachia-infected females can successfully mate with an uninfected male or a male infected with the same type of Wolbachia. CI occurs when a Wolbachia-infected male mates with a female infected with an incompatible type of Wolbachia or with a non-Wolbachia-infected female (Stouthamer et al., 1999). Currently, in Australia, Brazil, Colombia, and Southeast Asia, the method of releasing mosquitoes infected with Wolbachia is used to control Dengue and chikungunya in the nature (Enayati et al., 2005). Research studies have shown that using a specific strain of Wolbachia suppresses these viruses in the body of Ae. aegypti mosquitoes (Aliota et al., 2016; Bian et al., 2010; Xue et al., 2018). This strategy has been introduced to apply this technology in endemic areas of Dengue fever in Southeast Asia. Additionally, Wolbachia is utilized for population suppression through the release of incompatible males (Incompatible Insect Technique), a strategy currently implemented in several countries including Singapore, USA, Thailand, and Australia (Wiwatanaratanabutr and Zhang, 2016; Ryan et al., 2020; Kulkarni et al., 2019).

Previous studies have reported Wolbachia infection in Iranian mosquitoes, including Cx. pipiens in northwest Iran (Bozorg-Omid et al., 2020; Karami et al., 2016). However, to our knowledge, no study has specifically investigated Wolbachia infection in Ae. aegypti and Cx. pipiens populations from Hormozgan Province, southern Iran. Therefore, this study provides novel baseline data on Wolbachia prevalence and strain diversity in this understudied geographical region. The purpose of this study is to investigate and determine the Wolbachia infection in Ae. aegypti and Cx. pipiens, in Bandar Abbas City. The results of this study provide basic information for better control of arbovirus vectors and the possibility of using new environmentally friendly biological methods to control the transmission of dangerous arbovirus diseases such as dengue fever, chikungunya, and West Nile in the country.

Material and method

Study area

Bandar Abbas city, the capital of Hormozgan province, is located on the southern coast of Iran, facing the Persian Gulf, geographical coordinates of 27.187°N; 56.281°E. In terms of weather conditions, this city has a scorching and humid climate. In terms of geographic coordinates, it lies between the 56.15° and 56.42° E and 27.13° and 27.27° N (latitude).

Mosquito collection

Sample collection of Ae. aegypti

Eggs of Ae. aegypti were collected by ovitrap from May to July 2024 in 10 urban localities in Bandar Abbas City (Fig. 1). Ovitraps were placed indoors and outdoors in shaded areas to avoid direct sunlight and rain. Ten ovitraps were placed at each study site for each sampling. They were replaced after four days with new ovitraps. The collected ovitraps were transferred to the mosquito insectary at the Hormozgan Medical Sciences, Iran. After emerging larvae, used dry fish food for feeding in 26 ± 2 °C temperature, 12:12 light and dark period, and 65 ± 5% relative humidity.

Map of the study location in Bandar Abbas created by Arc GIS Pro.

Sample collection of Cx. pipiens

Larvae of Cx. pipiens samples were caught by the dipping method in selected places of larval habitats, then the larvae were preserved and transported to the laboratory to determine their abundance and identity. Adult Cx. pipiens samples were collected using UV traps in the outdoor, aspiration inside human dwellings, landing catches on human baits and animal baited trap from May to July 2024 (Silver, 2007). In the last stage, the collected samples were identified based on valid keys and registered in standard forms (Azari-Hamidian and Harbach, 2009; Means, 1979). Finally, they were kept in a freezer at −20 °C for DNA extraction.

DNA extraction, PCR amplification and sequencing

Genomic DNA from individual mosquitoes was extracted using the Collins method (Collins et al., 1987). Out of f 1176 samples, 588 were subjected to genetic analysis. For detection of Wolbachia infection by PCR amplification used of wsp gene. For detection of Wolbachia infection 632-bp region of the wsp gene was PCR-amplified in a thermal cycler using forward primers (TGG TCC AAT AAG TGA TGA AGA AAC) and reverse primers (AAA AAT TAA ACG CTA CTC CA) (Zhou et al., 1998; Carvajal et al., 2019). PCR was conducted with a total volume of 25 μl consisting of 50 ng of total genomic DNA, 2.5 μl of 10× PCR buffer, 4 mM of MgCl2, 1 μM of each primer, 200 mM of each dNTP, 1 U Taq DNA polymerase and deionized water. PCR amplifications were done under the following thermal profile: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of amplification (denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, annealing at 59 °C for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min) and a final extension at 72 °C for 3 min (Wiwatanaratanabutr, 2013).

All PCR amplification tests included negative and positive controls. The negative control was water and positive controls was Wolbachia-infected Drosophila melanogaster samples. The product size of wsp primer was checked through 1% agarose gel electrophoresis set at 70 V for 1 h and observed under ultraviolet light. The size of the amplified wsp gene is approximately 632 bp.

Identity of Wolbachia strains and their positions in phylogeny

All obtained sequences were edited with BioEdit software and then compared by BLAST online software with Wolbachia sequences registered in GenBank (Table 1). In this study, we used MEGA6 and PhyML v.3.0 softwares with 1000 bootstrap replicates to investigate phylogenetic relationships and determine the type of Wolbachia supergroups (Guindon et al., 2010). Finally, all newly sequences were recorded in GenBank.

Results

In the present study, Ae. aegypti and Cx. pipiens were collected from Bandar Abbas County, Hormozgan Province, South of Iran. A total, 460 Ae. aegypti and 716 Cx. pipiens were collected and then identification based on morphological characteristics.

We screened 230 Ae. aegypti and 358 Cx. pipiens species for Wolbachia infection by PCR assays with Wolbachia-specific wsp gene primers.

From a total of 358 adult Cx. pipiens screened using the wsp primer, 212 (59.22%) Cx. pipiens mosquitoes infected Wolbachia. The molecular results showed the level of Wolbachia infection in the female population of Cx. pipiens is 68.2% and in the male population is 50.3%. From a total of 230 Ae. aegypti investigations of Wolbachia infection in Ae. aegypti showed that there is no Wolbachia infection in this species. (Table 2 and Fig. 2).

Discussion

During the present study, 115 male mosquitoes and 115 female mosquitoes of Ae. aegypti And 179 male mosquitoes and 179 female mosquitoes of Cx. pipiens were investigated by PCR to determine Wolbachia infection. The results of this study showed that Ae. aegypti mosquitoes are not infected with Wolbachia bacteria, While mosquitoes of Cx. pipiens are highly infected with the Wolbachia bacterium (Table 2, and Fig. 2, Fig. 3). In general, the level of infection with Wolbachia bacteria in Cx. pipiens, was different in various places, which seems to be related to environmental conditions and important environmental factors such as temperature. Previous studies have demonstrated that elevated temperatures can significantly reduce Wolbachia density in mosquito hosts, particularly in Aedes aegypti (Yixin et al., 2016; Ross et al., 2017). High temperature exposure during larval development has been shown to decrease Wolbachia titers in adult mosquitoes, with strain-specific sensitivity observed (e.g., wMel being more thermally sensitive than wAlbB) (Ross et al., 2017; Echaubard et al., 2010). Comparing our results with previous studies in Iran, Bozorg-Omid et al. (2020) reported Wolbachia infection in West Nile Virus vectors in northwest Iran (Bozorg-Omid et al., 2020). Our study confirms the presence of Wolbachia in Cx. pipiens in the south of Iran (Hormozgan Province) with a prevalence of 59.22%, which varies from other regions, potentially due to environmental differences. Consistent with previous global and regional studies, no natural infection was found in Ae. Aegypti (Karami et al., 2016). Results of our phylogenetic analysis based on wsp gene showed that, the Wolbachia strains found in our sampled Cx. pipiens belong to supergroups B. The same observation has been reported in previous studies (Kulkarni et al., 2019; Coon et al., 2016; Thongsripong et al., 2018). Most different Wolbachia strains are found in medically important mosquitoes such as Ae. albopictus, An. gambiae and Drosophila species (Zhou et al., 1998; Kittayapong et al., 2002; Baldini et al., 2014). Usually, dipterans, especially mosquitoes, are infected by Wolbachia strains from supergroups A and B. Therefore, the present study is consistent with previous studies. Different Wolbachia strains cause phenotypic effects such as cytoplasmic incompatibility, male killing and feminization in the insect host (Ellegaard et al., 2013). Our findings align with a consistent global pattern reported across multiple geographical regions: Wolbachia infection is typically absent in natural populations of Ae. aegypti but prevalent in the Cx. pipiens complex. Studies from Italy (Ricci et al., 2002), Portugal (de Pinho et al., 2016), Egypt, and Turkey have repeatedly confirmed this species-specific distribution (Ricci et al., 2002; de Pinho et al., 2016). Notably, apparent Wolbachia detection in some Ae. aegypti samples from filaria-endemic areas likely reflects co-infection with Wuchereria bancrofti rather than true insect infection (Dyab et al., 2016). Overall, the Cx. pipiens complex remains the most frequently reported natural host of Wolbachia among Culicidae mosquitoes worldwide (Ricci et al., 2002; Morçiçek et al., 2018; Atyame et al., 2011; Calado et al., 2016), supporting the species-dependent nature of Wolbachia infection observed in our study. In our study, infection with Wolbachia bacteria in Ae. aegypti was not reported, while the infection with Wolbachia bacteria in Cx. pipiens was reported with a high rate, therefore, the result of the present study confirms the results of the previous study and is consistent and similar to them. According to the results of the present study and comparison with previous studies, it can be said that Wolbachia infection is different among different species of Culicidae, in other words, Wolbachia infection depends on the species and even the amount of this infection is different among different populations of the same species.

Considering that in all studies conducted in the world Ae. aegypti, was not infected with Wolbachia bacteria, and no bacterial infection was observed in this species in the present study. This mosquito is artificially infected with Wolbachia bacteria in laboratory conditions. Noting that Aedes have the ability to transmit Wolbachia bacteria to the next generation so in the case of Ae. aegypti Wolbachia can be used to control the population. Therefore, in emergency situations, this species can be artificially infected with Wolbachia bacteria, and by releasing it among non-infected populations, the benefits of these bacteria such as cytoplasmic incompatibility, reducing the lifespan of the mosquito, and preventing the multiplication of the disease agent in the vector's body for used to control diseases transmitted by this species.

Conclusion

The results of this study demonstrated the detection of Wolbachia from field-collected Cx. pipiens and lack of infection in Ae. aegypti in Bandar Abbas city, Iran. Totals of 358 (59.22%) individual mosquito samples were determined to be infected with Wolbachia using the wsp markers. According to the results of the present study and proof of Wolbachia infection in Cx. pipiens belongs to supergroup B and lack of contamination in Ae. aegypti, one of the main vectors of arboviruses in the region, Wolbachia bacteria can be used to control the vectors of arbovirus diseases through cytoplasmic incompatibility.
