Association of circulating ketone bodies with cognitive performance and dementia in the Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA)
Authors: Parag Anilkumar Chevli, Christopher L Schaich, Alexis C Wood, Luqman A TK, Anurag Mehta, Vardhmaan Jain, Margery Connelly, Suzanne Craft, Elad Shemesh, José A Luchsinger, Kathleen M Hayden, Bonnie Colleen Sachs, Timothy M Hughes, Michael D Shapiro
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGrowing interest centers on the association between circulating ketone bodies (KB) and cognitive function, notably in aging and neurodegenerative diseases.MethodsAssociations of plasma KB with incident dementia and cognitive performances were examined among Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) participants. KB were measured using plasma samples collected following an overnight fasting at Exam 1 (2000–02) and detailed cognitive testing at Exam 5 (2010–2012, N = 4392), Exam 6 (2016–2018, N = 1838), and in MESA‐MIND (2019–2021, N = 2060).ResultsOver 16.7 years, a doubling of total KB was associated with a greater risk of incident dementia (hazard ratio [HR]: 1.14 [1.04–1.29]). Higher total KB was associated with worse cognitive performance in the Digit Span test at exam 5 [β: −0.30 (−0.47, −0.14)]. We also found that a higher KB was associated with greater functional impairment and a higher Quick Dementia Rating Scale (QDRS) score.DiscussionIn a diverse, cardiovascular disease‐free population, elevated KB levels were associated with incident dementia and impaired cognitive performance in specific domains.Highlights A study of ketone bodies (KB) and cognitive performance and incident dementia.Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy was used to measure plasma KB at baseline.Doubling of baseline total KB was associated with higher incident dementia.Higher KB was also associated with worse performance on a test of working memory. BACKGROUND

The brain is a highly metabolic organ that requires constant energy to maintain its function. Under normal physiological conditions, glucose is the main fuel for the brain. Glucose is transported across the blood‐brain barrier by specific glucose transporters and is metabolized by glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation in the mitochondria to produce ATP, the universal energy currency of the cell. 1 However, glucose is not the only substrate used by the brain. In situations where glucose availability is limited, the liver produces ketone bodies (KB) from fatty acids to provide an alternative source of energy for the brain and other tissues. KB are molecules produced by the liver from fatty acids during periods of low glucose availability, such as fasting, starvation, extreme exercise, excess alcohol intake, or ketogenic diets. KB include acetoacetate (AcAc), β‐hydroxybutyrate (β‐OHB), and acetone. Among them, β‐OHB is the most abundant and readily transported across the blood‐brain barrier by monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs). 1 , 2

There is growing interest in the potential role of KB in modulating cognitive function, especially in the context of aging and neurodegenerative diseases. It has been postulated that KB may have neuroprotective effects by enhancing brain metabolism, increasing cerebral blood flow, reducing oxidative stress and inflammation, and modulating neurotransmission. 3 , 4 Moreover, some clinical trials have shown that increasing plasma ketone levels through dietary interventions or supplements may improve cognitive performance in older adults with memory impairment or mild cognitive impairment (MCI), as well as in patients with Alzheimer's disease (AD). 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 A recent systematic review found that ketogenic interventions effectively improved cognitive function in patients with mild‐to‐moderate AD who were apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4‐negative, as well as in patients with MCI. 9

Even though exogenous KB may have positive effects on brain function in certain contexts, the elevation of endogenous KB might reflect an underlying metabolic dysfunction. An animal study including aged rats demonstrated that elevated KB coincided with myelin degeneration, suggesting mitochondrial dysfunction early in aging. 10 Additionally, the relationship between fuel supply systems, transport mechanism, and mitochondrial metabolic enzyme expression and activity was examined in female mice throughout aging (3–15 months). The results indicated a trend toward reduced expression of monocarboxylate transporters (MCT)‐1 in the blood‐brain barrier, implying a decreased ability to transport ketones from the periphery to the brain with aging. 11 In this study, we aimed to examine the association of circulating KB with cognitive performance and dementia among a racially and ethnically diverse population free from cardiovascular disease at baseline. We hypothesized that a higher KB is associated with incident dementia and worse cognitive performance.

METHODS

Study participants

The Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) is an ongoing longitudinal study that involves a total of 6814 participants from four distinct racial/ethnic groups (Non‐Hispanic White, African American, Hispanic, and Chinese American) residing in the United States. 12 The recruitment process took place throughout the years 2000–2002 (Exam 1) in six field centers: New York, New York; Baltimore, Maryland; Chicago, Illinois; Los Angeles, California; St. Paul, Minnesota; and Forsyth County, North Carolina. Subsequent examinations were administered during the periods of 2002–2004 (Exam 2), 2004–2005 (Exam 3), 2005–2007 (Exam 4), 2010–2012 (Exam 5), 2016–2018 (Exam 6), and 2019–2021 (MIND A). The approval of MESA was obtained from the institutional committees at each field center, and all participants submitted signed informed consent upon their enrollment. 12

KB measurement

Plasma KB was measured in 6783 (> 99%) participants at baseline. Plasma samples containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) were collected following an overnight fasting period during Exam 1 (2000–2002). Samples were afterward frozen and stored at a temperature of less than −70°C. Plasma β‐OHB, AcAc, and acetone were quantified utilizing a nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopic assay in 2003; samples were not subjected to any freeze‐thaw cycles. 13 The quantification of the three KBs was based on the resolved NMR signals they generated. The correlation between the results obtained from NMR and mass spectrometry is significant, with R2 values of 0.996, 0.994, and 0.994 for β‐OHB, AcAc, and acetone, respectively. 13 In epidemiological investigations, it is customary to aggregate the plasma concentrations of the three KB into a single measure known as plasma total KB. Previous studies have documented the stability of KB at various temperatures and coefficients of variation. 13

Assessment of cognitive testing

The evaluation of cognitive performance was conducted on the MESA participants beginning with a cognitive screening battery at the fifth examination (Exam 5, 2010–2012) and was repeated at all subsequent examinations. This evaluation employed three standardized tests, namely the Cognitive Abilities Screening Instrument (CASI, version 2), the Digit Symbol Coding test (DSC), and the Digit Span test (DS). 14 , 15

The CASI is a standardized test used to measure an individual's overall cognitive abilities. It consists of 25 items that assess nine different cognitive domains, including attention, abstraction/judgment, concentration, language, orientation, verbal fluency, visual construction, short‐term memory, and long‐term memory. Each item was scored on a scale ranging from 0 to 100. The assessment of processing speed was conducted using the DSC test, which yielded scores ranging from 0 to 133. In this test, the participants were initially provided with a key consisting of nine pairs of digit‐symbol combinations. Subsequently, they were assigned a list of digits arranged in a random sequence. The participants were given the challenge of generating as many accurate equivalent symbols as they could beneath the given numerals, within a time limit of 120 s. The assessment of working memory involved the administration of the DS test. Participants were instructed to verbally repeat a series of random numbers that were increased in length. The numbers were presented at a rate of one per second. The test consisted of a maximum of 16 trials for the forward order and 14 trials for the backward order. A single point was allocated for each accurately remembered span, and the scores for both forward and backward recall were combined to yield a comprehensive score ranging from 0 to 30. Lower scores indicate poorer performance on each individual test.

At Exam 5, 4591 (97%) MESA participants completed the CASI assessment. Of these, 199 (4%) CASI tests were excluded from the analysis. This exclusion comprised 125 tests that were declared invalid either by the test administrator or because of technically invalid scores below 20. Additionally, 74 participants with documented dementia, as confirmed through a review of the International Classification of Diseases codes with external validation, were excluded from the study. Additionally, 14 participants had missing KB values. This resulted in a dataset consisting of complete and valid CASI data from 4378 participants (93%). Furthermore, 3989 (91%) participants completed the DSC test, and 4363 (> 99%) completed the DS test. A subset of participants without clinically recognized dementia were re‐administered these tests at Exam 6 in 2016–2018. Exclusion of incomplete or invalid tests yielded 1829 participants with valid Exam 6 CASI, 1612 participants with valid Exam 6 DSC, and 1716 participants with valid Exam 6 DS data (Figure S1).

MESA expanded the cognitive assessment with the Uniform Data Set Neuropsychological Battery version 3 (UDS‐NBv3) at all sites with MIND A (2019–2021, n = 2060) by incorporating translations of the UDS‐NBv3 for Spanish, Mandarin, and Cantonese. The UDS‐NBv3 battery comprises the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), 16 Story Recall, 17 , 18 Benson Complex Figure Test, 19 Number Span, Category Fluency (Animals, Vegetables), Trail Making Test Parts A and B, 20 , 21 and Phonemic Fluency. The cognitive assessment also includes questionnaires regarding family history of AD, medications, and health history; clinician‐assessed physical and neurological examination; Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS), 22 and an informant interview to collect the Quick Dementia Rating Scale (QDRS), 23 Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire (NPI‐Q), 24 , 25 and Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ). 26 MESA MIND cognitive data were administered by trained staff in English, Spanish, Mandarin, and Cantonese by in‐person clinic visits and telephone administration (eliminating visual items) when participants were unable to attend clinic visits. Cognitive test scores are adjusted normative data in English. 27 , 28

Ascertainment of dementia

In the MESA study, telephone interviews were conducted at regular intervals of 9 to 12 months to gather information regarding interim hospital admissions and occurrences of mortality. At each facility, a compilation of death certificates together with their corresponding International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th version (ICD10) codes, face sheets, and ICD9 codes from hospital records and some outpatient diagnoses were gathered. Dementia diagnosis was based on death certificates and hospital discharge ICD codes. 29 The use of ICD codes in defining dementia has been shown to be valid with positive predictive values ranging from 73.2% to 92.6%. 30

Other measures and covariates

Using standardized questionnaires, data on age, sex, race/ethnicity, smoking status, highest level of education, medical history, and medication use were collected. We classified smoking status into never, former, and current categories. Current smoking status was defined as smoking within the previous 30 days. A fasting glucose level ≥ 126 mg/dL or the use of hypoglycemic medication use was used to define diabetes mellitus. Using a Dinamap Pro 100 automated oscillometric sphygmomanometer (Critikon, Tampa, Florida), seated blood pressure was measured three times, and the average of the second and third readings was recorded. Hypertension was defined as a blood pressure of 140/90 mm Hg, or a history of hypertension accompanied by the use of blood pressure medication. Height and weight were measured by trained study staff at the clinic examination, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight by height squared (in meters) in kilograms. The MESA Typical Week Physical Activity Survey (TWPAS) is a questionnaire administered during the baseline examination to assess the duration and frequency of different physical activities engaged during a typical week in the preceding month. The total duration of each distinct activity type was calculated in minutes and then multiplied by the corresponding metabolic equivalent (MET) level. After a 12‐h fast, the levels of total cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), and triglycerides were measured in plasma samples. Plasma triglycerides (mg/dL) were measured using the glycerol‐blanked enzymatic method (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, Indiana). Plasma HDL‐C (mg/dL) was measured by the cholesterol oxidase method (Roche Diagnostics) after precipitation of non‐HDL magnesium/dextran. The low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) level was calculated using the Friedewald equation. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the 2009 CKD‐EPI equation. 31 Single nucleotide polymorphisms rs429358 and rs7412 were used to estimate isoforms of the APOE ε4 allele from plasma, using the Affy 6.0 chip, a genetic risk factor for dementia. 32

Statistical analyses

Participant's baseline characteristics were reported using count (percentage) for categorical variables and mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range) for continuous variables, depending on the distribution of the data. Using analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed data, the Kruskal–Wallis test for skewed distributed data, and the chi‐squared test for categorical variables, these characteristics were compared across total KB quartiles on Exam 1. Total KB, β‐OHB, AcAc, and acetone were all log2‐transformed to yield an approximately normal distribution and to facilitate the interpretation of data in terms of a doubling of total and individual KB. For survival analysis, the time to incident dementia or the last follow‐up completed before the end of 2017 among the total KB quartiles was used. Log‐rank p‐values were reported to assess statistical significance. We calculated hazard ratios (HRs) for incident dementia from the date of baseline until the date of dementia classification or administrative censoring on the date of last contact using Cox proportional hazards regression. The two Cox models included (a) model 1 adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, and education level; and (b) model 2 additionally adjusted for BMI, smoking status, systolic blood pressure, blood pressure medications, total cholesterol, HDL‐C, triglyceride, lipid‐lowering therapy, diabetes, eGFR, physical activity, alcohol use, and APOE‐ ε4 allele carriership. We also assessed effect modification of the association between total KB and incident dementia according to age, sex, race/ethnicity, diabetes status, and APOE‐ε4 allele carriership using interaction terms as an additional modification of model 2.

We used multivariable linear regression to assess the association of KB with cognitive test scores at Exam 5 and MESA MIND A using the same covariates with the exception that model 2 was additionally adjusted for language of cognitive tests administration and mode of administration at MESA MIND A (in‐person vs. remote [telephone, or video]). Finally, we examined the association between KB and changes in cognitive test scores between Exam 5 and 6 using multivariable linear models adjusted for Exam 5 cognitive test scores using the same sequential specification of covariates. Stabilized inverse probability weights for providing repeat cognitive testing data were created using logistic regression conditioned on Model 2 covariates. To determine whether individual KB is differentially associated with dementia and cognitive measures, we examined the association between individual KB (β‐OHB, AcAc, and acetone) and incident dementia, as well as cognitive performance and decline.

We used SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA) and R 4.1.2 (R Foundation, 2022) for analyses. Significance testing was two‐sided with a type 1 error rate of 0.05.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the study participants are presented as quartiles of total KB in Table 1. Mean (SD) age at the baseline examination was 62.1 (± 10.2) years; 3554 participants were women (52.8%); and 2606 (38.7%) were non‐Hispanic White, 1867 (27.7%) were African American, 1465 (21.8%) reported Hispanic ethnicity, and 793 reported Chinese ancestry (11.8%). In general, older, and female participants had higher KB levels. In addition, participants in the higher quartiles of total KB levels were more likely to have a higher BMI, a higher prevalence of diabetes (predominantly type 2 diabetes, T2D), and hypertension, but had lower LDL‐C, higher HDL‐C, lower triglycerides, and lower total cholesterol levels. Moreover, alcohol consumption tended to be higher among those in the highest quartile of total KB. Similar associations with quartiles of total KB were observed among the 4378 participants with cognitive test data at Exam 5 (Table S1).

Association of circulating KB with incident dementia

The 6731 participants that were included in this analysis were followed for incident dementia for a median duration of 16.7 [11.8–17.5]) years. A total of 582 (8.7%) cases were observed. Participants with higher total KB at Exam 1 were at higher risk of incident dementia (log‐rank p‐value < 0.0001; Figure 1). Higher circulating KB at baseline was associated with a greater risk of incident dementia after adjusting for age, sex, race/ethnicity, and education (HR: 1.17 [95% confidence interval [CI], 1.05–1.32]) (Table 2, Model 1). This association remained significant after further adjusting for BMI, diabetes, smoking status, systolic blood pressure, blood pressure medications, total cholesterol, HDL‐C, triglyceride, lipid‐lowering therapy, eGFR, physical activity, alcohol use, and APOE‐ε4 allele carriership (HR: 1.14 [95% CI, 1.04–1.29], per doubling of total KB) (Table 2, Model 2). We did not observe evidence for effect measure modification by age, sex, race/ethnicity, or diabetes status in the associations between total KB and incident dementia (interaction p of 0.434 for age, 0.105 for sex, 0.334 for race/ethnicity, and 0.551 for diabetes). We also assessed for effect measure modification by APOE‐ε4 allele carriership. The interaction p‐values were 0.379 for incident dementia, 0.400 for CASI, 0.681 for DSC, 0.150 for DS, 0.352 for the NPIQ score, 0.272 for the FAQ score, and 0.826 for the QDRS score, suggesting that APOE‐ε4 allele carriership did not modify the effect of KB on cognitive outcomes.

Association of circulating KB with cognitive performance and decline

A two‐fold increment of total KB was associated with lower performance on the DSC and DS tests, but not on the CASI test at Exam 5, after adjustment for age and sociodemographic factors (Table 2, Model 1). The association of total KB with the DS test persisted after additional adjustment for cardiovascular risk factors and APOE‐ε4 status (β: −0.30 [95% CI, −0.47, −0.14]) (Table 2, Model 2), although the association of total KB with the DSC test was attenuated to non‐significance after these adjustments. Among participants with repeat cognitive test scores at Exam 6, we found no evidence of an association between total KB and a decline in cognitive performance (Table 2).

Association of circulating KB with cognitive performance at MESA‐MIND

For participants enrolled in the MESA‐MIND study (n = 2060), we found that a doubling of total KB was associated with 0.22 (95% CI, 0.01, 0.42) point increase in FAQ and 0.15 (95% CI, 0.02, 0.27) increase in QDRS score suggesting worse cognitive performance. We also examined the association of individual KB with these scores and found evidence of differential effects by types of KB. We discovered that only β‐OHB was associated with poor performance on the FAQ and QDRS scores (Table 3). KB were not significantly associated with neuropsychiatric symptoms.

Sensitivity and subgroup analysis

We also examined the association of individual KB with incident dementia and cognitive performance to determine whether these associations varied by type of KB (Table 4). We found that the doubling of β‐OHB and acetone was associated with incident dementia, whereas there was no association between AcAc and dementia. Acetone was also associated with worse CASI performance at Exam 5. Similar to total KB, all individual KB were associated with worse Exam 5 DS performance. Lastly, a doubling of AcAc was associated with a decline in the DSC score (β: −0.65 [95% CI, −1.25, −0.06]) (Table 4). β‐OHB and acetone were not associated with changes in cognitive test scores.

DISCUSSION

The present study investigated the association of KB with incident dementia, cognitive performance, and cognitive decline in a multi‐ethnic population free from cardiovascular disease at baseline. Our findings indicate that higher levels of baseline circulating KB were associated with time to ICD‐defined incident dementia and worse performance on a test of working memory. We also found that elevated KB levels were prospectively associated with greater functional impairment, and more pronounced cognitive decline evidenced by a higher QDRS score. The association between KB and incident dementia was not modified by age, sex, race/ethnicity, or diabetes status.

Few population‐based studies have examined the association between KB and dementia. A large‐scale metabolomics study including 118,021 UK Biobank participants demonstrated that higher levels of KB were associated with an increased risk of all‐cause dementia. 33 In contrast, many small interventional studies of the ketogenic diet have shown that elevated levels of KB may improve cognition. 5 , 6 , 7 The discrepancies in these findings underscore the multifaceted nature of KB metabolism and its intricate implications for brain health, necessitating further exploration into the underlying mechanisms. Our study findings revealed a significant association between higher levels of total KB and impaired working memory, as indicated by poorer scores on the DS test. This association was further supported by our analysis of individual KB, including β‐OHB, AcAc, and acetone, all of which demonstrated a consistent link with compromised working memory performance. The observed association between elevated KB levels and impaired working memory suggests potential disruptions in the neural circuits and processes underlying the encoding, maintenance, and manipulation of information in the short‐term memory system. These processes are vital for various cognitive tasks, including problem‐solving, decision‐making, and complex reasoning. 34 In addition to the association with dementia, our study found that higher levels of circulating KB were associated with increased mortality risk. The Kaplan–Meier curve (Figure S2) illustrates a dose‐response relationship, indicating that participants with higher KB levels had a higher risk of mortality. This suggests that diverse uncaptured medical factors related to KB levels might account for at least some of the increased dementia risk observed. These factors could include underlying metabolic disturbances, comorbid conditions, or other health issues not fully captured in our dataset. Future research should aim to identify and account for these potential confounders to better understand the relationship between KB, dementia, and mortality.

The other novel finding of our study is the observed association of higher levels of total KB with increased FAQ scores, indicating greater functional impairment in activities of daily living. Moreover, elevated KB levels were associated with higher QDRS scores, suggesting a more pronounced degree of cognitive decline and dementia‐related symptoms. These associations underscore the potential impact of KB on broader cognitive domains beyond specific cognitive tests, emphasizing their role in functional abilities and overall cognitive health.

The exact mechanism underlying the association between elevated KB and cognitive decline remains unclear. Increasing evidence suggests that dysregulated glucose metabolism in the brain may lead to energy deficiencies in neuronal mitochondria, which are responsible for converting nutrients into energy. 35 This, in turn, may contribute to the development of dementia. A bioenergetic shift in energy metabolism has been suggested as a contributing factor in the development of AD. 36 The bioenergetic shift involves the upregulation of KB and fatty acid metabolism, possibly compensating for the decreased efficiency of glucose metabolism. 37 Although this mechanism potentially serves as an adaptive response to the compromised energy metabolism in the brain, the excessive reliance on KB could signify an underlying metabolic insufficiency that may contribute to cognitive deterioration. 38 This compensatory mechanism may precede the development of dementia and explain the elevation in peripheral circulating KB.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of this study. The observational nature of this study precludes the establishment of causal relationships between KB levels and cognitive outcomes, emphasizing the need for further interventional studies to validate the observed associations. Additionally, reliance on a single measurement of KB at baseline may not fully capture the dynamic fluctuations in KB levels over time, warranting the need for longitudinal assessments of these molecules over study course. We used standardized and verifiable clinical diagnoses of dementia based on ICD codes from death certificates and hospital records. However, we recognize the limitation of not incorporating direct cognitive measures, such as the MoCA, into our primary analysis. The MESA did not obtain MoCA or other cognitive assessment data at baseline. However, using the ICD code information overcomes concerns related to the heterogeneity in language and education levels within our diverse cohort, which could affect the validity of cognitive test scores. Finally, cognitive scores were not available at baseline, so we lacked information on the participants' baseline status of MCI. Our study has several strengths, including the measurement of KB after an overnight fasting period. This fasting state is crucial as it reduces variability in KB levels, providing a more reliable and consistent measure of baseline ketone concentrations. Fasting minimizes the influence of recent dietary intake and metabolic fluctuations, thereby ensuring that the KB measurements reflect a more stable metabolic state. This enhances the validity of our findings by reducing potential confounding factors related to diet and recent food intake. Other strengths of our study include the use of a large and ethnically diverse cohort, robust cognitive assessments, and comprehensive adjustments for potential confounders.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that higher levels of baseline circulating KB were associated with an increased risk of incident dementia and poorer performance in specific cognitive domains, such as working memory. The study contributes to the growing body of evidence on the intricate relationship between KB and cognitive function, shedding light on the potential risks associated with elevated KB levels and their implications for cognitive health. Future research endeavors should focus on elucidating the precise mechanisms through which KB influences cognition and exploring therapeutic strategies that effectively modulate KB levels to mitigate the risk of cognitive impairment.
