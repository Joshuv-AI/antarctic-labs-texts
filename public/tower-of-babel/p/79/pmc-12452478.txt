Do farnesol and tyrosol production in Candidozyma auris biofilms reflect virulence potential?
Authors: Murilo Moreira dos Santos, Henrique Santos Moure, Marcelo José Pena Ferreira, Kelly Ishida
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCandidozyma auris is associated with nosocomial outbreaks presenting high resistance rates to antifungal. Since its emergence in 2009, research has been carried out on the underlying quorum sensing molecules (QSMs), such as farnesol (Far) and tyrosol (Tyr). Our main goal was to quantify Far and Tyr in C. auris biofilms and assess their role in virulence using a Galleria mellonella larvae model. Among the tested strains, the most virulent CBS 12766 produced the highest QSMs levels, whereas the least virulent strain CBS 10913 produced the lowest. Notably, C. auris exhibited higher QSM production per biofilm dry weight and the virulence profile was associated with greater QSM levels. In addition, the virulent reference strain Candida albicans SC5314 produced largest amounts of QSMs and higher biofilm total biomass. In conclusion, we reported for the first time the production of Far and Tyr in C. auris biofilms, suggesting a potential association with the virulence of this emerging pathogen.KEYWORDS: Candidozyma auris, quorum sensing, farnesol, tyrosol, biofilm, virulence

Introduction

Quorum sensing (QS) is a communication mechanism among microorganisms that involves the secretion of signaling molecules that accumulate as the population grows until it reaches a threshold to trigger specific behaviors. It was first described in prokaryotes in the 1970s [1–3]. In fungi, it was demonstrated in 2001 that endogenous farnesol mediates the filamentation of Candida albicans by treating yeast cultures with a supernatant containing this quorum sensing molecule (QSM) [4]. Since then, studies on the role of QSMs have expanded, and more molecules have been discovered, such as farnesoic acid, morphogenic autoregulatory substance (MARS), tyrosol, phenylethanol, and tryptophan, in C. albicans planktonic cultures [5]. In addition, quorum sensing mechanisms have also been shown in other fungal species, such as Histoplasma capsulatum, Ceratocystis ulmi and Neurospora crassa [6–8].

\Among all QSMs, farnesol (Far) and tyrosol (Tyr) have been the most described and studied. Previous studies have also described Far production only in planktonic cultures of Candida dubliniensis, Candida kefyr, Candida parapsilosis strictu sensu, Candida tropicalis, Meyerozyma guilliermondii (formerly Candida guilliermondii), Nakaseomyces glabratus (formerly Candida glabrata), Pichia kudriavzeveii (formerly Candida krusei) [9], and Tyr production in C. parapsilosis, C. tropicalis, N. glabratus, and Candidozyma auris (formerly Candida auris) [10,11].

Far biosynthesis pathway belongs to ergosterol pathway in fungi and has farnesyl pyrophosphate as a major precursor [12,13]. Tyr is a phenolic compound that belongs to the tyrosine biosynthesis pathway described as an antioxidant component [13]. Furthermore, the growth of several fungal species was affected by exogenous Far or Tyr using an antifungal approach [12,14–17]. In this regard, it is believed that both compounds play relevant roles in diverse cellular processes that are still under investigation [3,12]. It’s a consensus on the broad proposal role of Far and Tyr in virulence traits of C. albicans and related species. Indeed, both molecules play a key role in controlling morphogenesis and biofilm formation [12,13]. However, several questions remain unanswered, including how Far and Tyr are transported across the cell, the extent of their production under biological conditions, the mechanisms and dynamics of their synthesis and secretion [9].

Simulating conditions that evaluate the function of QSMs in a fungal community is challenging and requires understanding when, how much, and whether a given species produces that metabolite. With this idea, we believe that only the biological production of QSMs can elucidate the cellular mechanisms involved. Furthermore, we observed that there is no description of Far and Tyr produced by Candida or Candidozyma biofilms, despite several studies already including Far and Tyr as regulatory molecules in the biofilms [13,18,19]. In silico previous study also supported our hypothesis, indicating that C. auris possesses key enzymes such as farnesyl-diphosphate farnesyltransferase, farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase, and farnesyl transferase from the Far synthetic pathway [20], already described in C. albicans [21]. The presence of several enzymes in the tyrosine metabolic pathway, an intermediary in Tyr synthesis [22], has also been found in C. auris [20].

Thus, based on the previous description of QSMs in correlated species and the presence of key enzymes of the biosynthetic pathway, our main goal was to detect and quantify the QSMs Far and Tyr in C. auris biofilm cultures and for the first time suggest a role in virulence. This finding may impact further studies on the role of QSMs in the virulence and in the regulation of the biofilm development of C. auris, an emerging yeast on the rise in global epidemiology.

Results

First, we characterized the biofilms in terms of total biomass, metabolic activity, and colony former unit counts produced by four C. auris strains (CBS 12766 – clade I, CBS 10913 – clade II, B11221 – clade III, and B11244 – clade IV), and the virulence profile using the Galleria mellonella larvae model. In addition, we detected and quantified the QSMs Far and Tyr from C. auris biofilms using gas chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry (GC-MS) to compare all data with the reference strain C. albicans SC5314, our known control for QSM production.

All biofilms of C. auris and C. albicans had the same metabolic activity based on the XTT salt reduction assay (Figure 1(A)). On the other hand, C. albicans biofilm produced a larger total biomass (~2-fold) and had the lowest number of viable cells in dispersed and biofilm cell analysis compared to C. auris biofilms (1–2 Log) (Figure 1(B,C)). Among C. auris strains, CBS 10913 presented more biofilm biomass than the other three strains (Figure 1(B)), while the B11221 strain had more colony-forming units counted in biofilm cells, followed by B11244, CBS 12766, and CBS 10913 (Figure 1(C)).

All C. auris strains produced Tyr and Far during biofilm formation in both the supernatant and biofilm samples (Figure 2, Tables 1 and 2). In addition to enabling the detection of QSMs, GC-MS data – combined with calibration curves for the Far and Tyr standards – allowed for the quantification of their concentrations in the supernatant, biofilm cells, and the entire culture. Furthermore, it enabled the determination of the ratio between Far or Tyr levels and the dry weight of the biofilm. CBS 10913 strain produced the least absolute QSM values (in µg), whereas C. albicans was considered the largest Tyr and Far producer, followed by the CBS 12766 and B11244 strains (Tables 1 and 2). However, although C. albicans biofilm produced a higher content of QSMs by lower number of viable cells, it produced almost double the total biomass, as determined by dry weight and crystal violet stain (Tables 1 and 2, Figure 1(B)). In this regard, all C. auris strains produced more Far and Tyr (µg per mg of biofilm dry weight) than C. albicans, highlighting C. auris CBS 12766 and B11221 strains, both in the supernatant and biofilm cells, while CBS 10913 strain was the lowest QSMs producer (Figure 2(A,B)).

Regarding in vivo data, we observed that C. auris CBS 12766, the major producer of Far per milligram of dry biofilm, was the most virulent strain in G. mellonella larvae inducing higher morbidity (i.e. lower health index) and mortality (i.e. lower survival percentage) rates as well as C. albicans, followed by B11244 and B11221 strains. While the CBS 10913 strain, the lowest producer of total QSMs, had the lowest ability to induce larval death (Figure 3(A,B)). Therefore, the highest Far-producing strains induced a higher mortality rate. Otherwise, all C. auris strains showed significantly higher fungal burden in the larval tissue when compared to C. albicans (Figure 3(C)).

Discussion

Candidozyma auris was recently classified by WHO as a critical priority pathogen due to its higher resistance profile to antifungal agents and capacity to promote nosocomial outbreaks around the world [23]. The hypothesis for C. auris emergence involves several factors previously discussed, which may be linked to its adaptation from environment to survive in animal host. These factors include global warming, bird-mediated dispersion, intraspecies transmission through rural activities, and the acquisition of traits that facilitate survival in urban environments, i.e., all of which may contribute to its capacity to cause disease in humans [24]. In this regard, thermotolerance is a relevant characteristic for overcoming the body temperature of mammals. Moreover, other important virulence factor is the ability of fungus to adhere and form biofilms on surfaces to promote infection, persistence, and dissemination [25].

Currently, C. auris strains are distributed in genotypic clades (I, II, II, IV, V, and VI), being clades I to IV the longest known and the most studied and the last two recently described [26,27]. Here, we worked with the most representative C. auris clades in current epidemiology (I, II, III, and IV). Recent findings recognized clades I and III as isolates resistant to fluconazole, clade IV as the most multidrug resistant, while isolates of the clades II, V, and VI have the lowest resistance rate [26]. As evidenced in this present study, in terms of virulence, and recent data suggested that isolates from clade II are less virulent when compared to clades I, III, and IV and, in general, do not present host niche preference for colonization and infection [28].

Regarding biology traits, QSMs are poorly studied in C. auris, with no previous description for biofilm lifestyle and no comparison between different clades. In related species, like C. albicans and C. tropicalis, Far and Tyr have been proposed to be part of many metabolic processes and found to play active roles in regulating biofilms, specifically in C. albicans filamentation, an important virulence factor [3,13,18]. For detection and quantification, HPLC and UHPLC-MS methods have also been used to target these molecules [29,30], but GC-MS is the most sensitive and effective method for detecting volatile compounds [31]. Then, we proposed here the detection and quantification of QSMs Far and Tyr produced by biofilm cells from four representative strains of C. auris clades using GC-MS and the association to fungal virulence in G. mellonella larval model.

All C. auris strains formed less biofilm than C. albicans, in terms of total biomass. Although variations were observed among C. auris strains, our findings were consistent with previous studies [25,28]. It’s interesting to note that CBS 10913 exhibited a higher biofilm biomass production among C. auris strains tested, already described in the literature [32], even with the high antifungal susceptibility and low virulence profile of this strain and commonly observed for the clade II isolates [28]. Moreover, C. auris biofilms are more compact, whereas C. albicans biofilms are thicker and exhibit a complex architecture with hyphae/pseudohyphae and extracellular matrix polysaccharides, as shown by confocal laser scanning microscopy [25,33]. In this regard, phenotypic variation in a biofilm context can also interfere with virulence profiles. For C. albicans, filamentation capacity is positively correlated with biofilm structure and virulence, but for C. auris, non-aggregative biofilm-forming isolates are associated with enhanced virulence traits, as seen for CBS 12766 and B11244 strains [34,35].

The G. mellonella larval is widely recognized by the literature as a valuable model for studying microbial virulence and pathogenicity, aligning with the 3 R principles (refinement, reduction, and replacement), in addition to strong correlation with mice model [36]. In this regard, we evaluated the health state and survival of larvae infected by C. auris or C. albicans strains according to Loh and colleagues, and this analysis can show differences among pathogens in inducing disease and death [37]. Thereby, the survival curve and the health index data showed that highly Far-producing strains, C. albicans SC5314 and C. auris CBS 12766, lead the infection to a critical state faster than other strains. However, higher production of Far and Tyr was observed in C. auris B11221 than in B11244, and the reverse profile in virulence data can be explained by some works that described the aggregative phenotype (e.g., B11221 strain), a multicellular state that may include cellular separation failure, adhesion-mediated aggregation, and cell separation defects [28], can result in reduced virulence [38].

Interestingly, regardless of the virulence profile, CFU recovery was higher for infection caused by C. auris and significantly lower for C. albicans in G. mellonella larvae. Our findings were consistent with data obtained from a murine model of catheter implantation for biofilm formation and intraperitoneal infection, that CFU counting was significantly higher for C. auris in both models compared to C. albicans CFU counting, with no significant differences between C. auris strains [25].

Notably, C. albicans SC5314 is known to possess greater virulence traits that enhance host infection, generally making it more virulent than many other related species [39]. C. auris CBS 12766 strain exhibited a virulence profile comparable to C. albicans, as demonstrated by the in vivo data presented here, highlighting the complexity of fungal biology and the multiple factors that can influence fungal virulence. Additionally, C. albicans SC5314 and C. auris CBS 12766 profiles highlight the possible involvement of the physiological production of Far and/or Tyr in virulence.

Genetic investigation of the Far biosynthesis pathway showed that the deletion of the DPP3 gene (encoding a phosphatase that converts farnesyl pyrophosphate to Far) reduced Far production and virulence in vivo in C. albicans cells [3,40–42] as well as in Candida lusitaniae DPP3 knockout strain [43]. In addition, Far was also predicted to have an immunomodulatory effect during systemic infection, enhancing C. albicans virulence [9,42], promoting inflammation and mitigating Th1 response, fundamental factor for fungal clearance [44]. Exogenous Far triggered a rapid inflammatory response, leading to increased mortality in mice with intra-abdominal candidiasis. This was further supported by comparisons between infections caused by wild-type C. albicans (which produces Far) or DPP3 deletion mutant (which does not), resulting in a high and low mortality rate, respectively [42]. Furthermore, exposure to a sub-inhibitory concentration of fluconazole increased Far production and virulence in C. albicans in a mice model [40]. In summary, these reports support the role of Far as an important adaptation mechanism from a commensal to a pathogenic lifestyle of C. albicans and its immunomodulatory effect.

Previous works had demonstrated that C. albicans lacks enzymatic pathways to recycle Far into farnesyl pyrophosphate or to cleave farnesylated proteins, which limits the cell flexibility in controlling these processes [12]. This suggests that intracellular Far levels act as a central regulator, determining which proteins will be farnesylated and to what extent [12]. These findings have a direct impact on the understanding of pathogenesis and help explain how C. albicans adapts to different host niches and regulates its virulence. Moreover, Far shows potential both as a therapeutic target and as an antifungal molecule itself, possibly serving as an adjuvant. Furthermore, strategies that interfere with protein farnesylation and exploration of its restricted metabolic pathways as pharmacological targets emerge as a promising avenue for the development of new antifungal agents [12].

Although Far and Tyr production is involved in C. albicans morphogenesis [3], species such as N. glabratus, P. kudriavzeveii, and C. auris, which do not form true hyphae, have also been found to produce Far and Tyr [11,13]. In this context, Far and Tyr may be involved in many other cellular processes associated with fungal virulence. In planktonic cultures, C. auris metabolism prioritizes β-oxidation, enhanced oxidative stress, and efflux pump activity under Far exogenous exposure [45]. Recently, a study showed that exogenous Tyr on C. auris upregulated ribosome biogenesis and processes involving membrane-bound organelles, while downregulating fatty acid metabolism, vacuole function, peroxisome function, and reticular response to stress [46]. Moreover, Far and Tyr share upregulation of transmembrane transport and downregulation of sterol biosynthesis, cell wall, nucleosome, and chromosomal centromeric domains [45,46].

Exogenous treatment with Far and Tyr revealed diverse cellular C. auris responses to high concentrations [45,46]. We believe that C. auris cells produce biological QSMs and this phenomenon may help us better understand how these compounds influence the pathogenesis; however, studies on the role of QSMs in regulating biofilm formation and pathogenesis mechanisms with this physiological approach are limited. In our study, we quantified Far and Tyr production in C. auris pioneer biofilm model and suggest an association between QSMs production and enhanced virulence in G. mellonella larvae model. Despite our efforts, the primary limitation of our study is the limited number of C. auris representatives from each clade, which hinders to determine a correlation between QSM abundance and C. auris virulence of different genotypes.

Therefore, the regulation by QSMs Far and Tyr may play an important role in the virulence of C. auris and our findings open questions to better understand their impacts on C. auris metabolic processes that are involved in the development of biofilms and pathogenicity.

Material and methods

Fungal samples

Four C. auris strains were used in this study: CBS 12766 (South Asian Clade – I, human blood isolate and fluconazole-resistant), CBS 10913 (East Asian Clade – II, external ear canal isolate, fluconazole-susceptible), B11221 (or MYA-5002, South African Clade – III, human blood isolate, fluconazole-resistant), and B11244 (or MYA-5003, South American Clade – IV, human blood isolate, fluconazole-resistant), along with C. albicans SC5314 for comparison. The SC5314 and CBS strains were kindly provided by the Laboratory of Medical Mycology of the Institute of Tropical Medicine of São Paulo (IMT-USP, São Paulo/SP, Brazil). While B11221 and B11244 strains were purchased from Pensabio (São Paulo/SP, Brazil), an exclusive distributor of ATCC strains in Brazil.

All isolates were maintained in Brain Heart Infusion broth (BHI, Becton Dickinson and Company, Sparks, USA) with 20% glycerol at −80°C, recovered in Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA, Becton Dickinson and Company, Sparks, USA) and subcultured twice in the same medium at 35°C for 24 h before each assay.

Characterization of biofilms

Briefly, an aliquot of 100 μL of a yeast suspension at 106 CFU/mL in RPMI 1640 buffered with 0.16 M MOPS (RPMI-MOPS), pH 7.0, was incubated in flat-bottom 96-well polystyrene plates. After 1.5 h (adhesion phase) of incubation at 35°C, the medium was replaced with fresh medium and reincubated for 48 h at 35 ºC, without shaking. C. auris and C. albicans biofilms were characterized for metabolic activity (XTT reduction assay), total biomass (crystal violet assay), and cell viability (colony-forming unit counting assay), as previously described [47,48]. Three biological replicates were conducted in quadruplicate.

Extraction of farnesol and tyrosol from a biofilm model

A volume of 10 mL of a yeast suspension at 106 CFU/mL in RPMI 1640 buffered with 0.16 M MOPS, pH 7.0, was incubated in 60 × 15 mm Petri dishes for 1.5 h (adhesion phase). The same volume of medium was then changed and incubated for 48 h at 35 ºC, without shaking. The 10 mL-supernatant (S) was collected directly in a 15 mL-conical tube while sessile cells from the biofilm (BC) were collected in 10 mL of sterile ultrapure water with a cell scraper. Simultaneously, a replicate of BC samples was dried to determine the biofilm dry weight. Three biological replicates were conducted in triplicate.

The extraction, detection, and quantification of Far and Tyr were based on the methods described previously [31] with some modifications. The extraction phase is determinant to get the Far and Tyr in the solvent at a 10:1.5 ratio. Briefly,1.5 mL of ethyl acetate was added to 10 mL of samples (S or BC) followed by agitation for 2 min. Subsequently, 1 mL of 5 M NaCl was added and agitated for 10 s to facilitate separation of aqueous and organic phases. Then, the tubes were centrifuged at 7,200 × g for 3 min to transfer the organic phase into 1 mL amber autosampler sealed vials.

Detection and quantification of farnesol and tyrosol by gas chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry (GC-MS)

The samples were analyzed using an Agilent Technologies 6850 gas chromatography system (Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with an autosampler and an ion fragmentation detector. Chromatographic separation was performed on an HP-Innowax column with a film thickness of 0.25 μm, diameter of 0.25 mm, and 30 m length (Agilent 19,091 N-133I). An injection of 1 μL was used with helium as carrier gas at an initial temperature of 40°C, ramped at a rate of 10°C/min to a final temperature of 320°C, and held there for 7 min. The scan ion monitoring method (SIM) was applied to detect Far and Tyr, and the data were analyzed using OpenLab Services (Agilent Technologies). Quantification settings were calculated using the calibration curves of Far (0.1–1 mg/mL, CAS: 4602–84–0, Sigma-Aldrich) and Tyr (0.1–1 mg/mL, CAS: 501–94–0, Sigma-Aldrich). The detection limits (DL) and quantification limits (QL) were calculated using 3.3*(SD/slope) and 10*(SD/slope), respectively. These values for Far and Tyr were, respectively, 0.28 and 0.84 mg/mL, and 0.22 and 0.65 mg/mL.

Virulence in Galleria mellonella larvae model

The virulence of four C. auris strains and C. albicans SC5314 was evaluated using a Galleria mellonella larval model [37]. For infection, 20 µL of a yeast suspension at 2.5x107 CFU/mL (5x105 CFU/larvae), in PBS, was inoculated into the last left proleg of the larvae (150–200 mg of weight and 2.0–2.5 cm of length). The larvae were incubated at 35°C for seven days, with daily observations to determine and construct the morbidity and survival curves. Both graphics were obtained after the sum of points for larval health indicators such as activity, cocoon formation, melanization, and dead individuals as previously suggested [37]. Uninfected larvae were included in the assay as a control group for mechanical injury by PBS injection (PBS group). Survival percentage was calculated as the ratio of number of live larvae by the total number of larvae, considering censured larvae in the assay. Three independent experiments were performed (n = 20 per group).

Additionally, the fungal burden was evaluated using four larvae per group, weighed, macerated in PBS for posterior serial dilutions, and plated on SDA medium with 50 μg/mL chloramphenicol for colony-forming unit counting (24 h/35 °C). The result was shown as the number of colony-forming units per gram of larvae (CFU/g), and then the Log CFU/g was calculated, in a biological duplicate.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed by GraphPad Prism 8.0 using two-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons test, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test, and Kaplan-Meier survival curve with log-rank test. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
