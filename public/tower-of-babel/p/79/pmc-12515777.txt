Association between glycated haemoglobin A1c and total haemoglobin mass in adults with type 1 diabetes mellitus. A proof‐of‐concept study
Authors: Janis Schierbauer, Sabrina Sanfilippo, Nadine Wachsmuth, Othmar Moser
Published: 2025

BACKGROUND

Glycated haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) is the standard‐of‐care parameter for assessing long‐term glycaemic control in people with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1D). As a percentage‐based indicator, HbA1c reflects the proportion of haemoglobin mass (Hbmass) that has undergone glycation due to chronic hyperglycaemia over the preceding 2–3 months. 1 However, this metric is inherently dependent on Hbmass, which may vary due to physiological and pathological conditions, for example, exercise, hypoxia, anaemia, polycythaemia or blood donation. 2 , 3

METHODS

The local ethics committee of the University of Bayreuth approved the study protocol (O 1305/1‐GB, 28 July 2022) which was conducted as a proof‐of‐concept study and in conformity with the declaration of Helsinki and guidelines for good clinical practice. 4 , 5 Before any trial‐related activities, participants were informed about the study protocol and provided written informed consent.

Eligibility criteria were assessed by the same researcher during a screening visit. Inclusion criteria included a medical diagnosis of type 1 diabetes mellitus (ICD‐10‐Code: E10. 9 diabetes mellitus, type 1 without complications) for at least 12 months and an age between 18 and 75 years (both inclusive). Participants were excluded if they were exposed to altitude (>20 h per day for at least 14 days at >1800 m), or had a blood donation within the last 3 months prior to their first study visit. Participants with anaemia, renal complications or heart failure were also excluded from this study as assessed by their medical records. Additionally, females who were pregnant or breast‐feeding were not included in the study.

Upon inclusion in the study, body composition was analysed in duplicate using a bioelectrical impedance analysis (InBody 770, InBody Co., Seoul, South Korea). A venous blood sample (4 mL, ethylene diamine tetra acetic acid, EDTA) was collected in a vacutainer for the determination of HbA1c and a large blood count. Blood samples were tested in an external medical laboratory (Labor Staber, Medical Diagnostics, Bayreuth, Germany) where a turbidimetric immunological inhibition assay for hemolysed whole blood (Cobas C 513, Roche Holding AG, Basel, Switzerland) was used for the analysis of HbA1c.

CGM metrics including glucose management indicator (GMI), time below range (TBR), time in range (TIR) and time above range (TAR) were collected for the last 90 days, respectively. In the following, HbA1c and glucose management indicator (GMI) are shown as percentage value (%), concentration (mmol·mol−1) and as absolute value in grams (g), with the latter being calculated based on percentage values and Hbmass in g. Additionally, HbA1c and GMI are also expressed scaled to body mass (g·kg−1) and body surface area (BSA, g·m−2) according to Dubois & Dubois. 6

Participants performed a duplicate carbon monoxide (CO) rebreathing method for the determination of Hbmass, and intravascular volumes according to previously published methods. 7 Briefly, an individual dose of CO (0.8–1.0 mL·kg−1, CO 3.7, Linde AG, Unterschleißheim, Germany) was administered and rebreathed along with 3 L of pure medical oxygen (Med. O2 UN 1072, Rießner‐Gase GmbH, Lichtenfels, Germany) for 2 min. Capillary blood samples from a hyperemized earlobe (Finalgon Duo, Sanofi‐Aventis Deutschland GmbH, Frankfurt, Germany) were collected in heparinized plastic capillary tubes (safeCLINITUBES, Radiometer Medical ApS, Brønshøj, Denmark) before, and 6 and 8 min after the administration of the CO dose. Blood samples were then analysed for percentage carboxyhaemoglobin (%HbCO) using a blood gas analyser with optical spectrophotometry (ABL 90 Flex plus, Radiometer, Thalwil, Switzerland). The Hbmass was subsequently calculated based on the mean change in %HbCO (∆HbCO) before and after the CO‐rebreathing procedure according to Equation (1): (1) Hbmassg=K×MCOmL×100×∆HbCO%×1.39−1 A detailed explanation of the equation and the methods has been described elsewhere. 7 BV was calculated based on Hbmass, haemoglobin concentration and a given cell factor. 8 , 9 The typical error for the determination of Hbmass in our laboratory is 1.5%, which is in line with previous investigations. 10 , 11 Between the two procedures, a maximum of 7 days had to be adhered to. Both CO‐rebreathing procedures were performed by the same investigator to allow for the determination of intra‐rater reliability.

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Data were summarized in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Washington, U.S.A.) and analysed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, Inc., Boston, U.S.A.). Intra‐class correlations for continuous scores were calculated to determine intra‐rater reliability of the duplicate CO rebreathing measurements. 12 Normal Gaussian distribution was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk normality test. Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation, lower and upper 95% confidence interval of the mean, minimum and maximum, and coefficient of variation. Paired t tests were calculated to test for significant differences between absolute and scaled HbA1c and GMI values. Pearson's correlation coefficients and simple linear regressions were calculated to test for significant correlations between the dependent (HbA1c and GMI) and independent (Hbmass) variables. Statistical significance was accepted at p ≤ 0.05 (two‐tailed).

RESULTS

Twenty participants with T1D and a mean ± SD age of 37.2 ± 14.9 years and Body Mass Index of 25.6 ± 3.2 kg·m−2 participated in this study. Diabetes duration was 15.1 ± 9.6 years, with 11 participants using multiple daily injections (MDI) and 9 using hybrid closed‐loop (HCL) insulin delivery systems (mylife Ypsopump with camAPS FX: n = 7, t:slim X2 with Control IQ: n = 1, Accu‐Check Insight with DBLG1: n = 1) as current insulin therapy. All participants were using a real‐time continuous glucose monitoring device (Dexcom G6: n = 7, Dexcom G7: n = 3, FreeStyle Libre 2: n = 1, FreeStyle Libre 3: n = 9). CGM metrics can be found in the Supporting information Appendix.

Mean percentage HbA1c was 7.0 ± 0.9% (53.4 ± 9.8 mmol·mol−1) and ranged between 5.5 and 9.2% (36.1 and 76.9 mmol·mol−1). Mean absolute HbA1c calculated based on percentage HbA1c and absolute Hbmass was 51.0 ± 12.8 g (0.67 ± 0.12 g·kg−1) and ranged between 32.5 and 74.7 g (0.46 and 0.87 g·kg−1).

Mean percentage GMI was 7.0 ± 0.7% (52.5 ± 6.1 mmol·mol−1) and ranged between 6.2% and 8.3% (44.2 and 67.2 mmol·mol−1). Metrics for TBR, TIR and TAR can be found in the appendix (Tables S1 and S2). Mean absolute GMI calculated based on the percentage GMI and Hbmass was 50.2 ± 12.2 g (0.64 ± 0.12 g·kg−1) and ranged between 31.9 and 69.6 g (0.48 and 0.82 g·kg−1). Paired t tests showed no statistically significant differences between HbA1c and GMI for percentage (p = 0.49) and absolute values (p = 0.57) or when scaled to body mass (p = 0.62) or body surface area (p = 0.60).

Mean Hbmass was 722 ± 153 g (9.5 ± 1.3 g·kg−1) and ranged between 476 and 1024 g (7.7 and 11.8 g·kg−1). Calculated intra‐rater reliability for the duplicate Hbmass measurements based on intra‐class correlation was 0.964 (Table 1).

Pearson correlation analysis revealed no statistically significant association between percentage HbA1c and Hbmass in g (r = 0.05, p = 0.81, Figure 1A). In contrast, HbA1c and Hbmass, both in g, were significantly correlated (r = 0.88, p < 0.0001, Figure 1C). The correlations between HbA1c and Hbmass when scaled to body mass (r = 0.71, p < 0.0004, Figure 1E) or BSA (r = 0.78, p < 0.0001, Figure 1G) were also significant. The correlations between absolute and scaled HbA1c and percentage TBR (p = 0.36 and p = 0.15) and TIR (p = 0.08 and p = 0.06), respectively, were not significant. However, both absolute (p = 0.04) and scaled HbA1c in g·kg−1 (p = 0.02) were significantly correlated to percentage TAR. For GMI, significant results were only found for scaled values when correlated with TAR (p = 0.02, see Figure S1 in appendix).

CONCLUSIONS

This study investigated the association between glycated haemoglobin A1c and haemoglobin mass in adults with T1D. Our results indicated that the percentage HbA1c is independent of the absolute Hbmass in g. However, HbA1c expressed as an absolute value in g or scaled to body mass and body surface is directly proportional to both the absolute and scaled Hbmass.

This is the first study that quantified absolute amounts of HbA1c in g and investigated the association between HbA1c and Hbmass in adult individuals with T1D. Mean absolute HbA1c calculated based on the percentage HbA1c and Hbmass was 51.0 ± 12.8 g, and ranged between 32.5 and 74.7 g. This demonstrates that the absolute amount of HbA1c in g can differ by more than double between individuals with T1D. However, this appears to be physiological since we found no correlation between percentage HbA1c and Hbmass, indicating that the glycated fraction remains independent of Hbmass. However, absolute HbA1c is linked to absolute Hbmass (both in g) since it is mathematically derived from the product of percentage HbA1c and absolute Hbmass. This explains the wide range of absolute HbA1c found in this study and why we found no correlation between percentage HbA1c and absolute Hbmass. Since we also found significant correlations between HbA1c and Hbmass when scaled to body mass and BSA, it can be concluded that Hbmass has a direct influence on the absolute amount of HbA1c, regardless of body mass or BSA.

It was previously reported by Koponen et al. that there was no association between percentage HbA1c and Hbmass in male individuals with T1D with a mean HbA1c of 7.65%, 13 which is in line with our findings, although HbA1c for the males in our sample was lower (7.14%). By contrast, Hbmass was slightly higher in our male participants (772 vs. 824 g). This implies that Hbmass associations hold independently of fitness status since higher Hbmass is associated with higher aerobic capacity and vice versa. When compared with recently published reference values for adult, non‐athletic women and men, 14 mean sex‐specific BV in this study is approximately 8% lower for males and 3.8% lower for females. For Hbmass, 8.3% (males) and 5.3% (females) lower values were found.

It is critical to recognize that while HbA1c is conventionally expressed as a percentage value to reflect long‐term glycaemic control, it may be confounded by fluctuations in absolute Hbmass. Recent evidence underscores the relevance of absolute HbA1c in g in specific contexts. Blood donation, for instance, transiently alters absolute Hbmass, leading to a falsely lowered percentage HbA1c in both healthy individuals and those with T2D, risking misinterpretation of glycaemic control. 15 Sugimoto et al., who also concluded that percentage HbA1c is likely to be underestimated after autologous blood donation, indicated glycated albumin as a better indicator of these participants' glycaemic control. 16 Similar inaccuracies are observed in conditions affecting erythropoiesis, such as anaemia, chronic kidney disease, and pregnancy, where Hbmass is diminished or at least variable, which is also why these individuals were not included in our study. Additionally, elevated absolute Hbmass, i.e., in polycythemia, EPO‐treated renal patients, or endurance athletes, may also distort percentage HbA1c. 17 In a clinical scenario involving altered erythropoiesis, changes in red blood cell turnover, lifespan or production rate may affect both Hbmass and the kinetics of glycation and thus HbA1c. In such cases, our approach, namely, quantifying absolute HbA1c in relation to Hbmass, could serve as a complementary tool to help disentangle whether observed changes in percentage HbA1c are truly reflective of glycaemic control or are confounded by haematological dynamics. Using these examples of the offset regulation of changes in HbA1c and/or Hbmass, determining absolute HbA1c adjusted for Hbmass may yield a more accurate assessment across diverse physiological (and pathological) states. In research, especially in inter‐population comparisons or therapeutic evaluations, this metric could enhance data standardization and interpretation.

Another positive aspect is that determining Hbmass using the optimized CO rebreathing method is a minimally invasive, cost‐effective and safe procedure which only takes approximately 10 min per individual. 7 This would significantly facilitate the integration of this parameter into routine clinical practice, as has already been demonstrated recently in patients with coronary heart disease and in preoperational patients, where it was demonstrated to be safe. 18 In this regard, it has also been investigated whether the use of a portable and non‐invasive CO pulse oximeter allows for reliable and valid determination of Hbmass and blood volumes. 19 Although the authors concluded that it cannot be recommended for scientific purposes due to a high typical error (5.8% for Hbmass), it may nonetheless be useful in clinical settings, for example, as a screening tool, where higher margins of error may still be regarded as useful.

Since we found similar correlation coefficients for absolute and scaled values when correlating Hbmass with the GMI, it can be concluded that there exists a consistency between CGM‐derived and lab‐measured glycaemic indices. While we found no correlation between TBR or TIR and scaled HbA1c, TAR was significantly correlated to both absolute and scaled HbA1c. In contrast, GMI showed a significant correlation to TAR only when using scaled, not absolute HbA1c. Therefore, scaling HbA1c might enhance its clinical utility, especially when comparing it with CGM‐derived metrics like GMI or assessing glucose excursions above target. These findings support a nuanced view that HbA1c as a percentage is not universally sensitive to glucose variability, especially in individuals with divergent erythropoietic status or body composition, and further undermine the importance of CGM‐derived metrics in the glycaemic management of T1D. This is of special importance because percentage GMI, in contrast with percentage HbA1c, is solely calculated based on continuously measured interstitial glucose levels, and is thus not affected by the aforementioned confounding factors. 20 This also means that if HbA1c and GMI differ from each other within the same individual, it might be at least in part due to fluctuations in Hbmass and thus HbA1c, which would alter diagnostic or therapeutic decisions by considering absolute rather than percentage HbA1c. However, when evaluating CGM‐derived metrics, it should be noted that discordant glycaemic values may be obtained depending on the system used, as recently demonstrated by Freckmann et al. 21 This represents a limitation to the study, as our participants used different CGM systems. Given the small sample size, a sub‐analysis of the association between GMI and Hbmass based on individual CGM systems would not be conclusive.

Based on the available data, it must be noted that the interpretation of our findings may only apply to individuals with T1D, and no conclusions can be drawn regarding the relationship between HbA1c and Hbmass in other populations with impaired glucose tolerance, for example, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D). For the purposes of this study, we intentionally limited our recruitment to individuals with T1D to ensure a higher degree of clinical and therapeutic homogeneity across participants. Individuals with T1D universally require exogenous insulin therapy from the time of diagnosis, which provides a consistent therapeutic framework. In contrast, individuals with T2D are managed through a highly heterogeneous array of treatments, including lifestyle interventions, oral antidiabetic agents, GLP‐1 receptor agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors, and– in some cases – insulin. 22 This therapeutic variability in T2D could have introduced significant confounding factors, particularly in outcomes related to glycaemic variability.

Lastly, it must be stated, however, that the results of this study are based on a small sample size, and thus, likely underpowered. Therefore, the confidence in these associations (particularly regarding scaled data) should be cautiously interpreted. Future investigations should incorporate larger samples in a variety of clinical settings to make broader claims about inter‐individual variability in Hbmass and its impact on glycaemic interpretation.

In conclusion, percentage HbA1c normalizes Hbmass differences, so the glycated fraction is independent of absolute Hbmass. However, absolute HbA1c in g is directly linked to absolute Hbmass in g. From a clinical perspective, percentage HbA1c is safely measured to assess long‐term glycaemia; however, the effects of acute and chronic fluctuations of Hbmass on HbA1c warrant further investigation.
