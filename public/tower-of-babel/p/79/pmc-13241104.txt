Severe dysgeusia and weight loss associated with terbinafine use: A case report
Authors: Jacqueline J. Cavendish, Rebecca M. Garan, Mary M. Gibbs, Erika M. Harris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTerbinafine is a widely used antifungal agent, with dysgeusia recognized as an uncommon and typically self-limited adverse effect. Severe or prolonged taste disturbance is exceedingly rare. We report the case of a 72-year-old woman who developed profound dysgeusia shortly after initiation of oral terbinafine for onychomycosis, manifesting as a persistent metallic taste, marked aversion to sweet foods, anorexia, and progressive unintentional weight loss. Despite early discontinuation of terbinafine, symptoms progressed, leading to electrolyte abnormalities and significant functional decline and prompting an extensive diagnostic evaluation to exclude malignancy and other serious etiologies. Supportive management following drug cessation resulted in gradual but incomplete recovery over several months. This case demonstrates that terbinafine-associated dysgeusia may be severe, prolonged, and clinically consequential in older adults, emphasizing the need for early recognition and prompt discontinuation to mitigate morbidity.

Introduction

Terbinafine is a widely prescribed antifungal agent used to treat dermatophyte infections. Dysgeusia is a recognized but rare adverse effect, with a reported incidence of approximately 0.6–2.8%.[123] Dysgeusia is typically mild and resolves after discontinuation, whereas severe or prolonged cases are exceedingly rare, with only three incidences of permanent taste alteration reported [Table 1].[3456] In older adults, profound taste disturbance may precipitate severe weight loss, often prompting evaluation for serious disease. We describe a 72-year-old woman who developed profound terbinafine-associated dysgeusia resulting in marked anorexia, a 35-pound unintentional weight loss, electrolyte abnormalities, and functional decline, with partial recovery despite early drug cessation. This case underscores the importance of recognizing terbinafine-induced dysgeusia, as early identification and prompt drug discontinuation may reduce morbidity and improve recovery.[4]

Case History

A 72-year-old woman with a medical history notable only for osteopenia and who was otherwise in good baseline health was referred to podiatry for persistent pain and recurrent bleeding caused by medial deviation and impingement of the left hallux toenail. She was diagnosed with tinea unguium and initiated on 250 mg of oral terbinafine daily. Her chronic medications included alendronate 70 mg weekly, calcium carbonate 1000 mg daily, and cholecalciferol 1000 IU daily. She lived independently, was fully ambulatory without assistive devices, and denied tobacco, alcohol, or illicit drug use. There was no relevant family history of neurologic disease, malignancy, or inherited metabolic disorders, and she reported no psychiatric symptoms.

Approximately 1 week after starting terbinafine, the patient developed severe dysgeusia characterized by a persistent unpleasant metallic taste, accompanied by tongue paresthesia. She reported marked intolerance to sweet foods and fruits, which provoked abdominal discomfort, while salty and savory foods were only partially tolerable. Her oral intake declined significantly, resulting in progressive fatigue and unintentional weight loss of approximately 15 pounds over the subsequent month. Four weeks after treatment initiation, she sought medical attention due to persistent taste disturbance and weight loss, at which time terbinafine was discontinued due to concern for an adverse drug reaction.

Despite discontinuation after a total of 33 days of therapy, her symptoms persisted and worsened. Over the following 3 months, she experienced a cumulative weight loss of 35 pounds, with a nadir body mass index of 18.9 kg/m2. During this period, she developed profound generalized weakness and functional decline, necessitating the use of a motorized scooter for ambulation. On day 65 following terbinafine initiation, laboratory evaluation revealed hypokalemia (2.9 mmol/L) and hypomagnesemia (1.6 mmol/L), requiring oral electrolyte replacement in the emergency department.

An extensive diagnostic evaluation was undertaken to assess alternative etiologies of dysgeusia and weight loss. Upper endoscopy and colonoscopy were unremarkable, and computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis with intravenous contrast showed no acute or structural abnormalities. Otolaryngologic evaluation, including laryngoscopy, revealed no evidence of oral, laryngeal, or sinonasal pathology. Her medication list was reviewed and did not include other agents commonly associated with taste disturbance. There was no history of recent upper respiratory infection, smoking, radiation exposure, or psychiatric illness. Nutritional, metabolic, infectious, gastrointestinal, neurologic, and psychiatric causes of dysgeusia were considered but deemed unlikely given the absence of supportive findings.

Given the close temporal relationship between terbinafine initiation and symptom onset, symptom progression during therapy, the exclusion of alternative diagnoses, and gradual improvement following drug discontinuation, her presentation was determined to be most consistent with terbinafine-induced dysgeusia. Supportive management was pursued, including intranasal fluticasone and olfactory training as recommended by otolaryngology. Approximately 3 months after the cessation of terbinafine, the patient reported partial recovery of taste function, with improved tolerance of salty foods and protein sources, though aversion to sweet foods and fruits persisted. She regained approximately 12 pounds during this period, with gradual improvement in strength and functional status. At 5-month follow-up, she had not fully returned to baseline but demonstrated meaningful clinical recovery, consistent with a prolonged and clinically significant adverse drug reaction attributable to terbinafine [Figure 1].

Discussion

Although terbinafine-induced dysgeusia is uncommon, severe presentations in older adults can mimic serious underlying disease and pose a significant diagnostic challenge. In such cases, substantial weight loss and functional decline warrant careful exclusion of serious pathology, particularly malignancy, which was a key strength of our diagnostic approach. Extensive gastrointestinal, otolaryngologic, laboratory, and imaging studies allowed for confident exclusion of neoplastic, structural, infectious, and inflammatory etiologies, strengthening the attribution of symptoms to a drug-induced adverse effect. Longitudinal follow-up demonstrating symptom persistence and partial recovery after terbinafine discontinuation further supports a causal relationship. Limitations include reliance on patient-reported symptom severity without formal gustatory testing and the inability to fully exclude subclinical contributors such as micronutrient deficiencies or age-related sensory decline. Nonetheless, the temporal association, exclusion of alternative diagnoses, and clinical course provide strong evidence supporting terbinafine-induced dysgeusia as the primary etiology.[7]

The pathophysiology of terbinafine-associated dysgeusia remains incompletely understood but is likely multifactorial, involving both peripheral taste receptor dysfunction and disruption of afferent gustatory pathways.[8] Terbinafine inhibits squalene epoxidase, a key enzyme in fungal sterol synthesis that also participates in human cholesterol biosynthesis, raising the possibility that altered cholesterol homeostasis may impair taste receptor or gustatory neuron structure and signaling. The selective impairment of bitter and sour taste modalities supports a receptor- or pathway-level mechanism and may reflect vulnerability of more sparsely represented gustatory fibers.[9] Additional proposed mechanisms include interference with cytochrome P450-dependent enzymatic cascades involved in taste receptor signal transduction.[101112] Advanced age and low body mass index are recognized risk factors that may increase susceptibility to terbinafine-associated dysgeusia and prolong recovery. Management is therefore primarily supportive, with prompt discontinuation of the offending agent remaining the cornerstone of treatment.[1314]

This case underscores that terbinafine-induced dysgeusia, though uncommon, can be severe, prolonged, and clinically consequential, particularly in older adults. Profound taste disturbance may precipitate significant anorexia, weight loss, electrolyte abnormalities, and functional decline, closely mimicking serious underlying disease and prompting extensive diagnostic evaluation. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for medication-induced dysgeusia in patients presenting with unexplained taste disturbance and weight loss, as early recognition and prompt drug discontinuation may mitigate morbidity and facilitate recovery.

Key message
