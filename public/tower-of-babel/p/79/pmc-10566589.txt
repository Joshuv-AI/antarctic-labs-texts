Exploring current nutritional programming and resources available to people living with HIV in Canada: a scoping review
Authors: Jessica Mannette, Yingying Zhang, Melissa Rothfus, Chelsey Purdy, Winta Tesfatsion, Mary Lynch, Barbara Hamilton-Hinch, Patricia L Williams, Phillip Joy, Shannan Grant
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:The objective of this scoping review was to map the current literature and resources available on nutrition and food programming for people living with HIV in Canada. This review is phase 1 of a 4-phase project, called FoodNOW (Food to eNhance Our Wellness), a community-based nutritional needs assessment of people living with HIV in Nova Scotia, Canada.Introduction:People living with HIV may experience nutritional challenges, including nutritional deficiencies associated with the virus, food insecurity, and nutrition-drug interactions. Nutritional programming is often required for optimal care for people living with HIV. The literature, however, has not been sufficiently mapped to create a comprehensive picture of available programming. This review has informed the development of subsequent study phases, and will contribute towards shaping and planning food programs, as well as evaluating the need for subsequent systematic reviews.Inclusion criteria:This review considered literature focused on nutrition and food programming and resources in Canada for people living with HIV. People living with HIV of any age, sex, race, gender identity, or sexual orientation, as well as pregnant and lactating people, were included as the population of interest.Methods:The databases searched were MEDLINE (Ovid), CINAHL (EBSCO), Academic Search Premier (EBSCO), Social Services Abstracts (ProQuest), and Scopus. Sources of gray literature searched included government and organization websites, and Google searches. The database search was conducted in July 2021, and the gray literature searches were conducted in August and October 2021. Searches were limited to evidence published or translated in English. Two independent reviewers conducted title and abstract screening, and potentially relevant results were retrieved in full. Full-text screening and data extraction was conducted by 2 independent reviewers using a data extraction tool designed specifically for the scoping review objectives and research inclusion criteria, and any conflicts were resolved through discussion. Results are presented in both tabular and diagrammatic formats, with a narrative summary.Results:A total of 581 results were screened (published and gray literature). A total of 64 results were included in the review. The 6 reasons for exclusion at full-text review were i) not nutrition and food programming (n= 83), ii) not Canadian (n= 37), iii) duplicates (n= 22), iv) not focused on people living with HIV (n= 6), v) conference abstract (n= 1), and vi) not in English (n= 1). A total of 76 resources were located, as some of the 64 included sources offered more than 1 resource. The 76 resources were organized into 6 categories: i) charitable food provision (n = 21; 27.6%), ii) financial aid (n = 14; 18.4%), iii) nutrition care (n =12; 15.8%), iv) providing access to secondary sources (n= 10; 13.2%), v) food and nutrition expertise (n= 10; 13.2%), and vi) population health promotion (n= 9; 11.8%). Recommendations for future research and programming are discussed.Conclusions:This scoping review demonstrates that current programming relies heavily on charitable food provision services for people living with HIV and that there is an unequal distribution of resources across Canada. Program expansion to target diverse populations with more equal distribution across Canada may improve overall health outcomes for people living with HIV. Future research is needed to evaluate the effectiveness of available programming and the needs of end users (people living with HIV and their supports). FoodNOW will build on these findings to further explore and address the needs of people living with HIV.Review registration:Open Science Framework https://osf.io/97x3r Keywords: AIDS, HIV, food programming, food provision, nutrition programming

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) attacks the body’s immune system, deteriorating and weakening it, and increasing the risk of a person acquiring life-threatening infections and diseases (eg, cancer). If left untreated, HIV can progress into acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) and result in death.1 There were an estimated 62,050 Canadians living with HIV by the end of 2018.2,3 In 2019, the number of positive HIV cases identified in Canada was slightly lower than 2018; however, this decrease was not as great as in other countries.3 For instance, positive HIV cases identified among gay, bisexual, and men who have sex with men (gbMSM) have declined by 47% in the United Kingdom since 2014, whereas the cases among the same population in Canada have decreased by 26% over the past 10 years,3 providing rationale for a Canadian-focused scoping review.

A cornerstone of HIV treatment is medical nutrition therapy (MNT),4 which has been highlighted by Dietitians of Canada and the American Dietetic Association (now named the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics) in their reports.1 Through nutrition assessment and co-developed support, the nutrition care process and MNT aim to prevent and manage the signs, symptoms, and progression of HIV.4 MNT assessment includes nutrition-related problems, causes, and significance; medical, physical, social, economic, and dietary history; pharmacotherapy; and recognition/integration of social determinants of health.4 While a referral to a registered dietitian is part of the treatment stage of the cascade of care for individuals upon HIV diagnosis, there is no guarantee they will have access to subsequent nutrition care in their own communities.5

HIV has had “a significant impact on domestic and global health, social, political, and economic outcomes.”1(p.1425) There were 2122 new cases of HIV reported in Canada in 2019.2,3 Additionally, Dietitians of Canada and the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics reported that virus cases are highest among those who have and continue to experience barriers in accessing medical care: 30% (n = 626) were immigrants, and 56% (n = 1193) were gbMSM, while approximately 25% (n = 531) of new cases were among Indigenous populations.1 Important to note, Indigenous peoples made up 5% (n = 1,807,250) of the total population (approximately 37 million) in Canada in 2021.6

A widely accepted definition of food insecurity, from Anderson is “limited or uncertain availability of nutritionally adequate and safe foods or limited or uncertain ability to acquire acceptable foods in socially acceptable ways.”7(p.1598) In high-income, high-resource settings such as Canada and the United States, 49% to 71% (30,404 to 41,055 of 65,050) of people living with HIV (PLWH) experience food insecurity, largely due to socioeconomic status.8,9 Moreover, PLWH may experience a range of nutrient deficiencies, including anemias, antioxidant depletion, or protein-energy malnutrition, due to their pathology alone, directly impacting nutritional status and adequacy and, thus, their food security. For instance, sufficient caloric, micronutrient, and protein intake is key to managing symptoms of nutrition-related immune disfunctions in this population and is patient- or case-specific.1 Translated into practice, this can equate to higher caloric and nutrient needs, which may not be achievable with limited income or food access.

An individual’s overall health and well-being are impacted by economic and social intersections, otherwise known as social determinants of health.9 PLWH may experience multiple prejudices simultaneously, such as race, gender, sexual orientation, and socioeconomic status, which create complex systems of oppression and privilege. Using an intersectional10 lens when conducting HIV research has proved beneficial in understanding PLWH and their challenges in accessing care.11,12 As such, an intersectional approach to HIV prevention and management would greatly benefit food programming research and planning towards inclusivity while considering the multiple social identities to which PLWH are often connected.

In attempts to meet nutritional needs of PLWH in Canada, there are different types of nutrition and food programming. For example, food banks and meal programs provide emergency food supply for those in need,9,13–17 named in this review as charitable food provision. MNT and other dietitian services, such as community-based cooking classes, aim to treat nutrient deficiencies, maintain lean body mass, improve quality and length of life, manage symptoms, facilitate medication therapy and avoid food–drug interactions, and address complications.1,4 Community-based cooking classes have also been shown to improve health and life skills and increase the likelihood of participation in nutrition and food programming.9,13

Charitable food provision services were originally created to serve as an emergency food service for those in immediate need.18,19 Despite this, food banks and soup kitchens are commonly used as a long-standing response to address food insecurity, despite low intervention efficacy.19 Much research has been conducted to highlight why providing food in this way does not address the root cause of food insecurity.13,18–22 For example, although there are more than 50 free and low-cost food centers operating in the Downtown Eastside of Vancouver, British Columbia, there continue to be high rates of food insecurity reported among residents in this area.19 Research has shown that only 20% to 30% of people experiencing food insecurity utilize charitable food provisions, such as food banks and meal programs, due to their stigmatizing nature, inadequacy to meet people’s real needs, and many other limitations and negative consequences.22 Further, Miewald et al. and others highlight the importance of considering the intersection(s) of HIV, poverty, and gender, and how this may affect an individual’s experience in gaining proper food and nutrition by creating more challenges to navigate.13,18,19

The federal government in Canada distributes health care funding to provinces and territories with the aim of meeting the specific needs and priorities of each region. This model of federal funding and provincial or territorial service provision makes up the Canadian publicly funded health care system.23 For Indigenous populations, the Non-Insured Health Benefits Program offers coverage for medically necessary goods and services.24 As a result of each province having individualized funding and programming, Canada does not have a national plan for MNT or community-based nutrition for PLWH,25,26 despite there being evidence-based agreement on MNT for patients with chronic disease, including HIV.26,27 A coordinated and comprehensive approach would be beneficial towards ensuring efficient and effective sharing of knowledge and resources.26,27 To optimize results, organizations that work with targeted populations, including women and children, aging populations, and gbMSM, may attempt to offer specialized programming that best suits their needs. Resources often differ from region to region and, thus, tailored esponses that correspond with intersecting local geographical, social, and political contexts are common, and frequently necessary, to address local needs.28

In addition to the programming itself, health care workers and service providers meant to deliver programming for PLWH require specific supports to be able to provide the best care possible. Consequently, the Global Health Workforce Alliance (2010) conducted a review on the state of health care services for PLWH in which they offered recommendations, including improved support for health care workers and service workers in primary care.29 The supports identified included resources, flexibility, interprofessional education opportunities, and quality communication. Several peer-reviewed original works, within and beyond Canada, have identified similar findings.9,30

A preliminary search of MEDLINE, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, PROSPERO, and JBI Evidence Synthesis was conducted (May–June 2021), and no current or in-progress systematic reviews or scoping reviews on the research topic were identified. There was, however, a review report conducted in 2015 on the status of community-based HIV food program organizations in Canada. The researchers created a questionnaire and contacted by telephone the service providers of 80 identified (via internet search) community-based organizations across Canada.9 Their conclusions suggested that more funding was needed for improved community-based resources; employing the use of dietitians would maximize impact; and offering bundled services (ie, food, housing, income support, and health services) would be beneficial to overall goals of alleviating poverty. Preliminary results of the 2015 report9 indicated a need for this scoping review, consistent with both peer-reviewed works27 and input from stakeholders and rights and title holders.

“FoodNOW” (Food to eNhance Our Wellness) is a community-based nutritional needs assessment project conducted in collaboration with researchers, health care workers, service providers, students/trainees, and PLWH in Nova Scotia, Canada. This scoping review represents phase 1 of a 4-phase project led by FoodNOW and funded by Research Nova Scotia. Consistent with a participatory approach30,31 and integrative knowledge translation,32 this phase of the project engaged community partners in reaching consensus on the content of the review, with the goal of influencing policy and programming that will directly impact stakeholders, rights and title holders, and end users.

Through co-learning and capacity building throughout the review process, FoodNOW aims to balance research and action for beneficial outcomes for all partners.30 In initial meetings, project partners identified a lack of resource mapping of nutrition and food programming available for PLWH in Canada, confirmed by preliminary searching27 and community consultation. This lack of mapping may limit the information available to health care workers delivering food programming, which prevents PLWH from receiving optimal nutritional care and education. This gap has also resulted in limited knowledge exchange on the impacts of stigma, homophobia, racism, gender (ie, cisnormativity), and food security on nutrition and food programming for PLWH. The results of this scoping review are a starting point for future research in completing knowledge-to-action31 for nutrition and food programming that matches the wants and needs of PLWH in Canada, as per community feedback.

The overall objective of FoodNOW is to engage in a community-based nutritional needs assessment of PLWH in Nova Scotia, Canada. Subsequent phases of the project have built on the scoping review data to further explore and identify the needs of PLWH. This review aimed to map the current literature and resources available on nutrition and food programming for PLWH in Canada in order to highlight needs in currently available nutrition and food programming. Details on who offers the programs and who the programs target were included (eg, community-based programs, provincially and/or federally funded programs).

Review question

What nutrition and food programming is available to PLWH in Canada?

Inclusion criteria

Population

This review considered studies that included PLWH in Canada of any age, sex, race, gender identity, or sexual orientation, including those who are pregnant and/or lactating.

Concept

This review considered studies that explored nutrition and food programming. Any food programming available in Canada targeting PLWH was eligible for review, including, but not limited to, charitable food provision services, clinical recommendations, nutritional assessments, and nutrition education and counseling.

Context

This review considered studies that discussed or described nutrition and food programming available in any province or territory in Canada.

Types of sources

This scoping review considered both peer-reviewed and gray literature, including, but not limited to, primary research studies, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, guidelines, reports, health care websites, community-based service provider websites, provincial and territorial government websites, publicly available online news articles, and theses/dissertations. Both quantitative and qualitative research was considered for inclusion.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the JBI methodology for scoping reviews and the updated guidance on scoping reviews by Peters et al. 33,34 This review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR).35 This review was also conducted in accordance with a published a priori protocol.36

Search strategy

The search strategy, including all identified keywords and index terms, was adapted for each database and/or information source to capture both peer-reviewed and gray literature. To develop the search strategy, a preliminary search was conducted by JM to locate relevant papers, from which a JBI-certified research librarian (MR, in collaboration with JM) used the index terms listed in the relevant papers to develop a full search strategy with a set of keywords for CINAHL. After the final search strategy was developed (MR), it underwent a Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategy (PRESS) by a second research librarian (LG) before being adapted for each database and/or information source. The search strategy, including all identified keywords and index terms, was adapted for each information source and a second search was undertaken on July 14, 2021. The full search strategy is provided in Appendix I. Studies published in or translated into English were included, as both JM and YZ are fluent in English. No restriction on date of publication was set in order to capture resources that may be long-standing.

The databases that were searched included MEDLINE (Ovid), CINAHL (EBSCO), Academic Search Premier (EBSCO), Social Services Abstracts (ProQuest), and Scopus. Sources of gray literature included government and organization websites and Google searches. Types of gray literature documents that were eligible for review included reports from service providers, theses and dissertations, and publicly available online news articles written by, with, or about PLWH that discuss food and nutrition programming. Google was used to find documents and to identify organizations whose websites were searched specifically.

Study selection

Following the search, all identified peer-reviewed records were collated and uploaded into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) and duplicates were removed. Gray literature results were uploaded into Zotero (Corporation for Digital Scholarship and Roy Rozenweig Center for History and New Media, VA, USA) and duplicates were removed. Following a calibration exercise, titles and abstracts were screened by 2 independent reviewers (JM, YZ) for assessment against the inclusion criteria for the review. Potentially relevant papers were retrieved in full, and their citation details imported into Covidence. Full-text studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded, and reasons for their exclusion are provided in Appendix II. Any disagreements that arose between the reviewers were resolved through discussion. Results of the search and paper selection process are presented in a PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1).37

Data extraction

Data were extracted from papers included in the scoping review by 2 independent reviewers (JM, YZ) using a data extraction tool developed by the reviewers.36 For the peer-reviewed literature results, the extraction tool was transferred into Covidence. The data extracted included specific details about the population, concept, context, and key findings relevant to the review question. The extraction tool was tested by 2 independent reviewers (JM, YZ) during a calibration exercise with 10 papers. Any disagreements that arose between the reviewers at this stage were resolved through discussion. No modifications were made to the data extraction tool.

Consultation with stakeholders and rights and title holders

This review supplemented the most recent JBI scoping review framework proposed by Peters et al. 34 with consultation, as advocated by Arksey and O’Malley.38 Current evidence suggests that systematic reviews can be enhanced—and the results made more useful—if stakeholders, rights and title holders, and end users participate in the work.38,39 It is important to note that Indigenous peoples are not stakeholders; they are rights and title holders.40

Consultation was guided by the Delphi method (DM), a method for the systematic collection and aggregation of data provided by a group of experts on specific questions or issues. As an evidence-informed method, the DM is used to gather input, feedback, and opinion on questionnaires, reports, and educational tools.41 This method relies on individuals considered to have knowledge on the topic (ie, people with lived experience). The expert group aims to reach a consensus on a complex and multifaceted topic. In this case, the DM provided a framework to guide consultation and subsequent decision-making (eg, how the review will inform subsequent surveying). Contributors to the consultation had the opportunity to highlight additional references and provide valuable insights about problems and ideas that the scoping review alone had not identified. A DM flow chart is provided in Appendix III, adapted from Green.42

A list of 13 experts was generated from existing FoodNOW steering and advisory committees, as well as colleagues of the research team from relevant fields (ie, nutrition, health care providers), who were then invited to participate in the DM process. Eight experts agreed to participate as panelists, and the DM was employed by a trained facilitator to a group that included registered dietitians (n= 2), service providers (n= 4), a medical advisor (n= 1), and a dietitian candidate (n= 1). Panelists were invited to share feedback in the form of typed responses collected via email or pre-recorded video.42 The scoping review manuscript was provided 2 weeks in advance of the first feedback submission deadline (round 1). The panelists were asked to offer feedback on the practical application of the findings and to identify any reservations about materials included and/or excluded. Informed by Hemming et al. 43 and Williams and Webb,44 the facilitator collated the feedback into an anonymous summary report and circulated it back to the DM panelists for review 1 week in advance of the round 2 feedback submission deadline. The facilitator then integrated all final additional feedback into the review.

Data analysis and presentation

The results of this review are presented in tables and figures, and accompanied by a narrative summary that describes how the results relate to the review objective and question. The focus of this review was the broad concept of nutrition and food programming available to PLWH, and as such, we reported a broad range of programming types.

Results

Study inclusion

A search of the peer-reviewed literature conducted on July 14, 2021, yielded 696 results from 5 databases. Gray literature searches conducted on August 24, October 5, and October 15, 2021 (see Appendix I), yielded another 143 results (see Appendix IV). A total of 839 results were recorded. For purposes of this study, identified articles (peer-reviewed) and evidence (gray literature) are referred to as results, and individual programming and resources available to PLWH or AIDS are referred to as programming and resources.

After duplicates were removed from the published literature, 439 results remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 366 results did not match the research inclusion criteria, and 2 results were unable to be retrieved, leaving 213 results for full-text-review (71 published and 143 gray literature results). After full-text screening, 64 results were included in the review (17 published and 47 gray literature results). See Figure 1 37 for the complete search results and study selection and inclusion process. The 6 reasons for exclusion after full-text screening were i) not nutrition and food programming (n = 83), ii) not Canadian context (n = 37), iii) duplicates (n = 22), iv) not focused on PLWH (n = 6), v) conference abstract (n = 1), and vi) not in English (n = 1). See Appendix II for the list of excluded results. Duplicates identified among the published literature during the full-text review were due to multiple records published on the same resource/service organization. As the objective of the review was to identify the number of resources available in Canada, only 1 record per resource/service organization was eligible to be included in the scoping review.

Characteristics of included studies

Details of the 64 results included in the scoping review are provided in Appendix IV. Study characteristics were recorded as they relate to the research objectives and include full citations. The included results were published between 1993 and 2021. Four results had no date available. Per decade, the years of publication of the remaining 60 results were found to be distributed as such: 1990–1999 (n = 4; 6.7%), 2000–2009 (n = 5; 8.3%), 2010–2019 (n = 24; 40%), and 2020–2021 (n = 27; 45%). As outlined in the inclusion criteria, the item of data/evidence had to be based in Canada and describe/discuss nutrition and food programming or resources for PLWH. The following types of data/evidence were included: service provider websites (n = 30; 46.9%), provincial and territorial government websites (n = 13; 20.3%), qualitative research studies (including cohort and participatory research studies; n = 6; 9.4%), narrative reviews and summaries (n = 6; 9.4%), health services websites (including health clinics; n = 4; 6.3%), non-randomized experimental studies (n = 2; 3.1%), cross-sectional studies (n = 2; 3.1%), and a needs assessment paper (n = 1; 1.6%). We have organized the findings based on the objectives of this scoping review: i) population: PLWH, ii) context: Canada, and iii) concept: available nutrition and food programming.

Review findings

Population

Most results for available nutrition and food programming targeted the general population of PLWH in Canada. Of the 64 results included in the review, some offered specific programming for more than 1 population, for a total of 69 targeted programming resources. Of these 69 results, 44 (63.8%) were broadly focused on PLWH, rather than breaking programming down to target specific diverse populations. Some programming identified in the review did target specific populations that would have distinct needs compared to the general population of PLWH (see Figure 2 for resources offering targeted programming per Canadian city). The programming and resources identified from these results were focused on women and/or children (n = 6; 8.7%); PLWH who use drugs (n = 3; 4.3%); transgender, non-binary, and gender-diverse PLWH (n = 3; 4.3%); PLWH on antiretroviral therapy (n = 3; 4.3%); African, Caribbean, and Black PLWH (n = 2; 2.9%); Indigenous PLWH (n = 2; 2.9%); gbMSM (n = 2; 2.9%); low-income PLWH (n = 1; 1.4%); French-speaking PLWH (n = 1; 1.4%); sex workers (n = 1; 1.4%); and PLWH who have been marginalized (n = 1; 1.4%).

Context

All results included in the review were based in Canada, per the research inclusion criteria. Of the 64 results identified, 18 (28.1%) were published in British Columbia; 14 (21.9%) in Ontario; 10 (15.6%) were Canada-wide non-province/territory specific; 7 (10.9%) in Nova Scotia; 3 (4.7%) in Alberta; 3 (4.7%) in Saskatchewan; 2 (3.1%) in Manitoba; and 1 (1.6%) each in Quebec, New Brunswick, Prince Edward Island, Newfoundland and Labrador, Yukon, Northwest Territories, and Nunavut. For context, the 2019 provincial and territorial diagnosis rates of HIV per 100,000 people were as follows: Saskatchewan, n = 16.9; Manitoba, n = 8.8; Quebec, n = 7.4; Alberta, n = 5.8; Ontario, n = 4.7; British Columbia, n = 3.5; the Atlantic provinces, n = 3.0; and the Northwest Territories, n = 1.7.2

Concept

A total of 76 resources were located, as some of the 64 results included in the review offered more than 1 resource (see Figure 3 for distribution of resources per province and territory). We have classified these 76 resources as charitable food provision (eg, food banks, soup kitchens, grocery vouchers; n = 21; 27.6%); financial aid (ie, provincial government subsidies; n = 14; 18.4%); nutrition care (eg, dietitian services including the nutrition care process and MNT; n =12; 15.8%); providing access to secondary sources (ie, websites that do not offer nutrition and food programming but offer links to different websites that do; n= 10; 13.2%); food and nutrition expertise (eg, nutrient and diet guidelines from professionals, books, PDFs, and workshops offered by organizations; n= 10; 13.2%); and population health promotion (eg, cooking classes and general healthy eating tips focused on community inclusion and sociability; n= 9; 11.8%). Through searching the website links provided by these resources, we discovered an additional 69 secondary sources of food and nutrition programming, 10 of which had already been captured through initial searches (primary results). Thus, a total of 135 resources for nutrition and food programing for PLWH were located across Canada.

Gaps in known services and areas for future research

The results show a lack of peer-reviewed literature on the topic of available nutrition and food programming for PLWH in Canada, as most results were from gray literature (n = 47/64; 73.4%). This highlights the importance of engaging with scoping reviews, because systematic scoping reviews are considered by many policymakers to offer the strongest evidence.45 Moreover, policymakers, such as public health departments and federal governments, most often look to peer-reviewed literature and original research when gathering policy-relevant evidence.45 For any interventions to be made at a policy level, more peer-reviewed research on this topic is needed. The results from the gray literature indicate that while there is no standard approach to providing nutrition and food programming to PLWH by programs offered across Canada by an array of organizations, there are similarities among the provincial approaches and supports offered. Four areas were identified as areas for future research on the topic of known services: i) limited targeted programming, ii) geographical distribution of resources, iii) type of resources offered, and iv) resource/program effectiveness as it relates to individual experience.

First, a limited amount of identified programming was designed for diverse subpopulations. For example, many women and children; Indigenous people; transgender and gender-diverse people; and Black, African, and Caribbean people have different needs to be addressed. Cisgender and transgender women living with HIV, for instance, need trauma-informed and gender-safe spaces to access food services.17 Black, African, and Caribbean people, such as the clients of the Black Coalition for AIDS Prevention (Black CAP), require programming that considers social barriers such as racism, homophobia, and poverty.46 Despite this difference in needs, only 36% of results offered targeted programming. This leaves many people who have been marginalized without the specific resources they need. For example, 3% of resources identified in this review were targeted towards Indigenous people, despite this population accounting for almost 25% of new HIV cases in 2019.3 Black CAP in Toronto is an example of a targeted resource. Their programming is focused on providing basic food items that are culturally specific for low-income Black, African, and Caribbean households in Toronto.46 Also found in Toronto, the Ontario Aboriginal HIV/AIDS Strategy (OAHAS) provides assistance in accessing food and shelter for urban-based First Nations, Inuit, Metis, and non-status community groups/organizations/agencies, schools, and individuals in Ontario.47 OAHAS performs outreach in streets, neighborhoods, events, powwows, agencies, in-reach in correctional facilities, hospitals, medical centers, and schools. This also demonstrates that most of the targeted programming is offered in large cities.

Second, large cities, such as Vancouver (n = 11/18; 61% of the results from British Columbia) and Toronto (n = 7/14; 50% of the results from Ontario), were identified as home to most of their provinces’ resources (see Figures 2 and 3), leading to speculation that there are not as many resources offered in rural areas. Collectively, the programming identified in these regions covered a wide range of needs for PLWH, offering different resources for targeted population groups. The resources found were mostly provided by nonprofit organizations, private (for-profit) clinics, and provincial governments. For example, in British Columbia, we identified 18 results that include programming from all 6 categories, whereas in Manitoba we identified 2 results that covered 3 of the 6 categories, which were charitable food provision, nutrition care, and financial aid. The Manitoba HIV Program, operated by Nine Circles Community Health Centre (a nonprofit organization), 7th Street Access Centre (a provincially operated health clinic), and the Winnipeg Health Sciences Centre (a provincial hospital), offers both charitable food provision and nutrition care.48 While there were not many resources identified in Manitoba, organizations such as Nine Circles offer multiple types of programming (eg, emergency food supply, nutrition education with a dietitian) that may be able to target PLWH with different needs.48 There do remain, however, many regions that do not have any services available to them. Having fewer resources to reach large, highly impacted populations can leave programming stretched and potentially unable to meet demand.

Third, the most common type of nutrition and food programming identified was charitable food provision services. This included supports such as food banks, grocery vouchers, and soup kitchens, which accounted for 27.6% (21/76) of all nutrition and food services identified. These supports do not appear to be different for PLWH than for the general population; however, certain food provision services are reserved for PLWH. Many of the organizations identified offer charitable food provision as their only nutrition-related resource available for PLWH. For example, ACT (formerly the AIDS Committee of Toronto) offers hot lunches to PLWH twice per week49 and the HIV/AIDS Resources and Community Health offers monthly $10 grocery store gift cards to PLWH.50 For many PLWH, charitable food provision services may be the only nutritional support they are receiving to manage their symptoms and medication side effects.21 Future research would benefit from exploring long-term solutions; however, charitable food services are needed in the interim, not as a solution, but to meet immediate needs. The second-most common type of support identified was financial aid (14/76; 18.4%), as each province and territory has its own form of governmental financial aid for PLWH under employment support and income assistance if the individual qualifies (ie, referral from a dietitian). The amount provided to each individual can consist of allowances for basic food, diet supplements, and caloric intake/special diet. The third-most common type of support identified was nutrition care (12/76; 15.8%). Nutrition care included resources such as access to dietitian services for diet supplement referrals and MNT process, which has proven beneficial for PLWH.1 Secondary sources (ie, organization websites that do not offer nutrition and food resources but provide a list/website links of others that do) were tied for fourth-most common support identified (10/76; 13.2%). Secondary sources, however, may be less helpful to those in search of nutrition and food support. As a significant number of resources found were from service-provider websites offering links to secondary sources, this indicates a lack of available support, as some of the secondary sources provide further links to tertiary sources, making it challenging to find a tangible resource that provides nutrition or foods programming. The other fourth-most common type of resource (10/76; 13.2%) was food and nutrition expertise, which included items such as PDFs that offered nutritional guidelines and recommendations for PLWH (eg, energy expenditure, micronutrients, dietary supplements). The least-common resource identified was population health promotion (9/76; 11.8%), which paired the social aspect of sharing food together and learning food skills, such as offering cooking classes or community dinners.

Lastly, the top supports offered in Canada point to a single overarching research need: researchers and organizations for nutrition and food programming across Canada do not appear to be asking/interacting directly with PLWH on what they need as individuals to succeed. This finding is indicated by the needs of PLWH consistently being unmet through the currently available programming as demonstrated by the continued high rates of food insecurity, poverty, homelessness, and injection drug use. The implications of not aligning programs with the feedback of PLWH are that, firstly, PLWH are often food insecure, which is positively related to poverty (ie, income disparity); second, they may also be receiving suboptimal nutritional care; and, third, current resources may be wasted, as they are not allocated to the pivotal places that would maximize benefits. As such, current programming would benefit from considering the lived experiences of PLWH to be most effective.

Discussion

For this scoping review, we mapped the current literature and resources available on nutrition and food programming for PLWH in Canada. To this end, we recorded what types of nutrition and food resources were offered in locations across Canada. We then mapped these resources according to the 7 Partnership for Dietetic Education and Practice (PDEP) competencies that dietitians must demonstrate before becoming a registered dietitian and entering the practice.51 The 7 competencies are i) food and nutrition expertise, ii) professionalism and ethics, iii) communication and collaboration, iv) management and leadership, v) nutrition care, vi) population health promotion, and vii) food provision. We found that 3 of the 6 types of resources identified in this review reflected the competencies. These were i) food and nutrition expertise (eg, nutrient and diet guidelines from professionals; education through books, PDFs, and workshops offered by organizations), ii) nutrition care (eg, dietitian services including the nutrition care process and MNT), and iii) population health promotion (eg, cooking classes and general healthy eating tips focused on community inclusion and sociability). The 3 types of resources that did not fit within the PDEP competencies were i) charitable food provision (eg, food banks, soup kitchens, grocery vouchers), ii) financial aid (ie, provincial government subsidies), and iii) providing access to secondary sources (ie, websites that do not offer nutrition and food programming but offer links to different websites that do). As some resources fit within the PDEP competencies and some did not, this helps us to understand what falls beyond the scope of dietitians and highlights the need to train those delivering these services to PLWH.

Most resources identified in this review were charitable food provision services; therefore, it appears that long-term approaches/solutions to ensuring PLWH in Canada obtain adequate nutrition are needed. While we have noted that charitable food provision is a necessary service, it is not sustainable long-term. It may be challenging to maintain adequate nutrition for individuals who access charitable food provision services as their only source of nutrition to manage their HIV condition. Food banks and meal programs are shown to support daily needs as emergency food supply for those in need.4,9,13–16 They are not shown, however, to provide adequate long-term nutrition and food security support for individuals and families, especially for PLWH with complex nutrition needs.13,19 Tarasuk and Eakin20 report that charitable food provision services may operate with a dissociation between food assistance and client need, resulting in food assistance becoming a symbolic gesture rather than solely providing food aid. Additionally, charitable food provision may be damaging and counterproductive for those in need, as it can decrease income supports for individuals, leading them further into poverty.20 More recent work by Tarasuk et al. 21 echoes the same sentiment, stating that charitable food provision services are not able to address or assist the root causes of food insecurity, such as income disparity and homelessness.

This scoping review identified a need in available programming in that the majority of food programming offered (charitable food provision) is not meeting the complex nutritional needs of PLWH in Canada.4,13,19 Many charitable food provision programs do not have adequate supply to address these specific needs, such as fresh fruits and vegetables,17,19 or to address the food insecurity that 49% to 71% of PLWH face.8,9 Miewald et al., among others, have reported that for PLWH, “food insecurity has been linked to lower CD4 counts, reduced drug adherence (i.e., ART [antiretroviral therapy]) and effectiveness, poor viral suppression (viral suppression being the primary goal of HIV treatment), and increased risk of mortality.”19(p.511-2)

Additionally, it is evident from the review that distribution of the resources across Canada appears to be unequal in both quantity and type of resource offered, as most of the results identified were services offered in provinces with high populations or larger cities (see Figures 2 and 3). This could indicate that provinces and territories with smaller and more remote populations may be left with little to no resources. Further, PLWH who also belong to populations that have been marginalized, (eg, Black, Indigenous, or person of color; low-income; sexual minorities such as transgender, non-binary, or gender-diverse people) may have more difficulty finding programming that is specific to their needs and cultural healing practices, especially if they reside in less-populated areas of Canada (see Figure 3). For instance, Nova Scotia has the highest population of gender-diverse people (of those aged 15 to 34 years) among the Canadian provinces,52 but as outlined in Figure 2, we did not identify current nutrition and food programming in Nova Scotia that is specifically targeted for this population. PLWH may also have additional chronic conditions or disability considerations, especially as they get older.

In their study on food access for women living with HIV in Vancouver, Sernick et al. 17 highlighted the barriers that some populations face in finding appropriate nutrition services including immigrant, Indigenous, women, and transgender PLWH. Despite the majority of identified resources targeting the general PLWH population, there were some programs identified targeted towards a more specific population (eg, women and children, transgender, non-binary, gender-diverse people), which are better tailored to their needs. Black CAP46 and OAHAS47 mentioned previously are examples that offer programming for specific populations. These organizations demonstrate that specialized care for different population groups is warranted.

More specialized resources that offer an intersectional approach to nutrition and food programming could be beneficial for these population groups in providing adequate nutritional support and safe spaces.12,17 For example, Sernick et al. 17 found that peoples’ safety based on gender (ie, gender identity safety) was a main concern for those accessing food banks at the cis and trans women–focused centers, which was a main source of food for their study population. Sernick et al. 17 demonstrated why it is important for individual experience and accounts from PLWH to be a part of the dialogue in research towards nutrition and food programming in Canada. Without consulting end users, research on which programming should be developed may miss key learnings and opportunities for improving programming.

We found that the number of results identified increased throughout the years, with 40% of results published between 2010 and 2019, and 45% of results between 2020 and 2021 alone. As many of our results came from service provider websites, this large percentage of results from 2020–2021 could be explained by the number of service organization websites identified in the review that update their website information each year, resulting in a large number of sources identified for the year 2021. With 85% of review results published between 2010 and 2021, this signifies that the results reported are relatively up-to-date and, thus, an accurate depiction of the currently available programming.

Strengths of the review

There are several strengths to be noted in the creation of this scoping review. First, we have a dynamic research team with backgrounds including, but not limited to, dietetics, applied human nutrition, nursing, anthropology, 2SLGBTQIA+ nutrition and health, and Black, Indigenous, or person of color studies. This multitude of perspectives provided the review with a degree of depth that may not have been accessible had the research team been limited to 1 field of study. Community consultation through the DM technique also provided this review with an additional layer of feedback that came from community members and service providers who work with PLWH, allowing the review to report more thoroughly on available food and nutrition resources for PLWH in Canada. The time and care put into this review from the research team and community advisory members will prove beneficial for the overarching goals of the FoodNOW project, of which this review is a part.

Limitations of the review

This scoping review has a few limitations to note. Given the reviewers’ language abilities, only those resources in English were captured, potentially resulting in selection bias. Practical challenges, such as time and funding constraints, made the use of translation services not feasible for this project. Thus, for example, resources available for francophones and Indigenous peoples (eg, Mi’kmaq, Cree, Innu) are not mapped. We intend to lift the language restriction for subsequent global reviews on this topic. Also inherent when conducting any scoping review is the possibility of publication bias, such as only positive results being identified due to authors or journals only publishing studies with positive results. Further, large cities such as Vancouver and Toronto that are home to individuals of many different nationalities could offer services in languages other than English; these results were not captured in this review. This is a substantial limitation of the review, as the researchers note that language barriers further marginalize populations. Another limitation to note is that the extraction of gray literature was solely based on what was described on the websites and was not verified with the authors or organizations. It is possible that there are more resources available than we identified.

Conclusions

This scoping review identified and mapped 64 results for nutrition and food resources and programming available for PLWH in Canada, noting where they are located and which populations are targeted. The review also demonstrates that there is limited peer-reviewed work on this topic. The peer-reviewed literature identified was often based on grant-funded work, as are many nonprofit organizations identified in the review. Additionally, the review revealed that current programming relies heavily on charitable food provision services, intended as an acute or short-term solution, and that PLWH with specific needs may often not have access to specialized resources that are equitable, diverse, inclusive, and accessible. These findings point to the need for long-term consistent funding, so energy can be put into the population (PLWH) rather than maintaining short-term projects. The review does also suggest that targeting population groups with specific needs, as well as diversifying the types of resources offered, may improve overall health outcomes for PLWH in Canada.

Implications for future research

It would be beneficial for research to be conducted on the quality, efficiency, and fidelity of nutrition and food programming available to PLWH. As the resources identified in this review were from a range of study designs, future scoping reviews should consider mapping designs, study methodology, and continue to monitor geographic location, including urban vs rural availability of resources, in addition to assessing program outcomes. A systematic review on this topic would allow us to further investigate the identified programming (eg, if/how the available programming is meeting needs of PLWH) and, eventually, inform program development in Nova Scotia and beyond, guided by examples/evidence of well-designed programming across Canada as expressed by the PLWH patient population.

As we have outlined throughout this review, limited information is available from PLWH themselves. In working towards filling this research need, phases 2 through 4 of FoodNOW engage directly with PLWH in Nova Scotia (ie, food costing, interviews, and mixed-form questionnaires). Qualitative interviews and mixed-method surveying could provide personal accounts of food and nutrition program experiences, which could offer more detailed evidence towards program policy planning, implementation, and change. In addition to phases 2 through 4 of the FoodNOW study, our research team has engaged directly with PLWH in other activities, such as the World AIDS Day Event FoodNOW hosted at Mount Saint Vincent University on December 1, 2022, in which we provided program updates and facilitated an interactive workshop with community members.

Implications for practice

The data presented in this review will allow those engaging in program delivery to have a resource that summarizes the work that has been done and identifies programming needs. This body of work highlights the need for the voices of PLWH to be at the forefront of the conversation surrounding available nutrition and food resources in Canada. This extends beyond research and into practice. Counseling and communications that are inclusive and recognize the diversity of this patient population and their needs are key.

Dissemination of the review results have been and will continue to be formative (eg, community meetings, consultation) and summative. Results were used to build the subsequent 3 FoodNOW project phases, which have been disseminated via traditional academic routes, including conferences, manuscripts, and reports. Innovative approaches, such as infographics and short videos, depending on audience (eg, funders, academics, government, PLWH, service providers), may also be employed. For instance, we have presented preliminary findings in the context of integrated knowledge translation (iKT) through a poster presentation at the KT Canada Scientific Meeting in spring 2022.53 We have also presented a needs assessment summary of the findings in the form of a poster and oral presentation at the KT Scientific Meeting in spring 2023.54 Sharing the learnings from this project and plans on how the data will be used/implemented with PLWH and other community members remains one of the key priorities towards our iKT objectives.
