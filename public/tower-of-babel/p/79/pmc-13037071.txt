Postoperative pulmonary complications and response to recruitment maneuvers during hypoxemia in one lung ventilation
Authors: Bélaid Bouhemad, Eléana Rogard, Ophélie Dransart-Rayé, Pierre-Benoit Pages, Maxime Nguyen, Pierre-Grégoire Guinot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Pulmonary atelectasis frequently occurs during and after surgery, impairing gas exchange and respiratory mechanics, and may contribute to postoperative respiratory failure and pneumonia [1].

Thoracic surgery with one-lung ventilation (OLV) represents a specific clinical model of intraoperative hypoxemia, resulting from a combination of lung collapse, ventilation–perfusion mismatch, and impaired respiratory mechanics in the dependent lung [2]. Although hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV) partially limits intrapulmonary shunt, it is frequently insufficient, and worsening hypoxemia occurs in approximately 5–10% of patients despite high inspired oxygen fractions.

Importantly, the mechanisms underlying hypoxemia during OLV are heterogeneous, including recruitment-dependent atelectasis of the ventilated lung and impaired or inhibited HPV. These distinct mechanisms may respond differently to recruitment maneuvers and ventilatory optimization strategies [3].

We therefore conducted a retrospective study of patients undergoing one-lung ventilation for thoracic surgery to assess the association between intraoperative hypoxemia patterns, response to recruitment maneuvers, and the incidence of PPCs, particularly pneumonia.

Materials and methods

Patients

We conducted a retrospective, single-center study at Dijon University Hospital, including patients who required one-lung ventilation (OLV) for thoracic surgery between April 14, 2022, and March 3, 2023. Recruitment maneuvers is a part of a routine diagnostic strategy aimed at optimizing the respiratory status of the patients during thoracic surgery.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Hence, this retrospective study was reviewed by the Comité de Protection des Personnes Est I, which waived the requirement for ethics approval and for informed consent, as the study involved anonymized retrospective data and met the criteria for exemption according to national regulations. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. (Supplementary file)

Data Availability

This report was prepared in accordance with the STROBE Statement. The datasets used and analyzed during the current study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were patients aged ≥ 18 years who underwent elective thoracic surgery at Dijon University Hospital requiring OLV.

Exclusion criteria included patients under 18 years of age, adults unable to provide informed consent, patients who experienced intraoperative complications that could interfere with the assessment of hypoxemia under standard surgical conditions (e.g., hemorrhagic shock, bronchial injury, poorly tolerated arrhythmia), and patients undergoing esophagectomy due to the specific complications associated with that procedure.

Protocol

The same protocol was followed for all patients. Anesthesia induction involved intravenous administration of propofol (1–2.5 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.1–0.5 µg/kg), and cisatracurium (0.15 mg/kg). Ketamine (0,1 − 0,2 mg/kg), droperidol (1.25 mg), and dexamethasone (4–8 mg) were also administered. Following loss of consciousness, assisted ventilation was initiated, and a double-lumen endotracheal tube (DLT) was inserted under video guidance. Proper positioning of the DLT was confirmed using fiberoptic bronchoscopy immediately after intubation and again after placing the patient in the lateral decubitus position, prior to surgery.

Mechanical ventilation was standardized according to institutional practice. FiO₂ was adjusted to maintain an SpO₂ ≥ 96%, using a respiratory rate of 12–16 breaths per minute, a tidal volume of 6–8 ml/kg, and an inspiratory-to-expiratory ratio of 1:2. One-lung ventilation was initiated before surgery, with ventilatory settings adjusted as needed. Anesthesia was maintained with a continuous intravenous infusion of propofol, titrated according to clinical requirements.

Patient data were collected retrospectively from the local electronic medical record system (DX CARE®), the anesthesia record software (OPESIM®), and the patients’ non-digitized medical records. Collected data included patient characteristics (age, BMI, preoperative albumin levels, etc.), results of preoperative pulmonary function tests, details of the surgical procedure, intraoperative anesthesia protocols, and information related to intraoperative hypoxic episodes. We also calculated the PHYX-IT score [4].

All postoperative complications and vital status between Day 1 and Day 30 were recorded, including major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), acute kidney injury (KDIGO ≥ 1), and postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) among them pulmonary infections. Postoperative respiratory failure was defined by the need for respiratory support, including high-flow nasal cannula, noninvasive ventilation, CPAP, or invasive mechanical ventilation, initiated in response to postoperative hypoxemia. Duration of mechanical ventilation, ICU length of stay, and total hospital length of stay were also documented. For patients discharged before Day 30, follow-up data regarding their health status were collected from their general practitioner.

Radiologic evolution was evaluated using the RALE score (Radiographic Assessment of Lung Edema) [5], on Day 1 and Day 3. This score assesses the radiographic severity of pulmonary edema and is recognized as being associated with clinical outcomes in ARDS. Postoperative chest radiographs were reviewed and scored by the anesthesiology team (ER and BB), with discrepancies resolved by consensus.

Definitions

Intraoperative hypoxemia was defined as an episode of oxygen desaturation (SpO₂ <92% with FiO₂ = 1) lasting at least 5 consecutive minutes.

A recruitment maneuver consists of applying a peak airway pressure of 30–40 cm H₂O for a short duration (10 to 30 s). Recruitment maneuvers during OLV were performed only in cases of intraoperative hypoxemia according to the routine care in our unit. Patients with hypoxemia that does not respond to recruitment maneuvers—requiring the insertion of an oxygen catheter into the excluded lung or the resumption of two-lung ventilation—are considered non responders. Patients with hypoxemia were considered responders if recruitment maneuvers allow to decrease FiO2.

Postoperative desaturation was defined as a decrease in SpO₂ below 92%, measured by pulse oximetry, which led to the initiation of supplemental oxygen therapy.

Statistical analysis

We compared three groups of patients: those without intraoperative hypoxemia, and those with hypoxemia, further divided into responders and non-responders to recruitment maneuvers.

The normality of quantitative data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Based on the results, quantitative variables were presented as either median with interquartile range or mean with standard deviation. Comparisons between groups were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis non-parametric test. Qualitative variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and comparisons were made using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

We also conducted multivariable logistic regression analyses. The primary exposure of interest was the group, defined of patients with hypoxemia who responded to a recruitment maneuver, compared with non-hypoxemic patients and non-responders. Two separate multivariable models were constructed to explore the association between this group classification and postoperative outcomes. In the first model, the dependent variable was pneumonia, defined as a composite of early and late pneumonia. In the second model, the dependent variable was a composite outcome of early and late respiratory failure. In both models, the PHYX-IT score was used as a composite adjustment variable, as it summarizes several clinically relevant preoperative risk factors, including body mass index, preoperative oxygen saturation, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease status, and surgical laterality.

No a priori sample size calculation was performed due to the retrospective nature of the study. A two-sided significance level of 0.05 was used for all statistical analyses.

Statistical analysis was performed with R.

Regarding early postoperative complications (Day 0 to Day 4 inclusive), respiratory distress was significantly more frequent in the responders group [9 (21.4%) vs. 0 (0%) vs. 12 (8.7%); p = 0.04], as were cases of pneumonia [9 (21.4%) vs. 2 (13.3%) vs. 4 (2.9%); p < 0.001].

For late postoperative complications (Day 5 to Day 7 inclusive), pneumonia was the only complication that occurred significantly more often in the responders group compared to both the non-responders group and the non-hypoxemic group [12 (28.6%) vs. 9 (7%); p < 0.001]

The RALE score was also significantly higher in the non-responders group compared to the other groups on Day 1 [22.1 ± 11.3 vs. 22.8 ± 11.1 vs. 18.3 ± 8.6); p = 0.008]. However, this difference was no longer observed on Day 3 [20.3 ± 10.9 vs. 21.6 ± 5.9 vs. 16.5 ± 9.5; p = 0.07].

Discussion

During one-lung ventilation (OLV), patients who respond to recruitment maneuvers may be at increased risk of developing postoperative pulmonary complications.

Our results showed that patients who initially responded to recruitment maneuvers experienced more early and late postoperative pulmonary complications, including pneumonia and respiratory distress. In contrast, patients with more severe hypoxemia who did not respond to recruitment maneuvers presented with greater initial pulmonary edema (as indicated by a higher RALE score) but had fewer postoperative complications. Overall, these findings suggest distinct underlying pathophysiological mechanisms and support the differentiation between atelectasis and impaired hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV) as causes of intraoperative hypoxemia. Accordingly, two distinct hypoxemia profiles during one-lung ventilation (OLV) can be identified: atelectasis-related hypoxemia, which responds to recruitment maneuvers, and HPV impairment-related hypoxemia, which does not respond and may require increased FiO₂ or a return to two-lung ventilation.

Patients with presumptively intraoperative atelectasis have higher rates of postoperative pulmonary complications, including respiratory distress and pneumonia. This increased risk is due to a combination of factors [1, 2]. Collapsed lung regions are poorly ventilated, leading to impaired ciliary function and reduced airflow, which hinders mucus clearance. The resulting secretion retention creates an environment favorable to bacterial growth, increasing the likelihood of colonization and infection. Additionally, atelectasis is associated with local immune suppression and reduced macrophage activity, further impairing the lungs’ ability to eliminate pathogens. The mechanical consequences of atelectasis—such as reduced cough effectiveness and limited lung expansion—also contribute to the accumulation of secretions and the development of infection. Together, these mechanisms explain why atelectasis is a significant contributor to postoperative pulmonary infection [1, 2].

Interestingly, non-responder patients exhibited higher intraoperative ventilatory pressures and a higher RALE score on Day 1, suggesting a potentially deleterious effect of recruitment maneuvers in this group [6]. Fortunately this increase RALE score was not found at Day 3. The RALE score is a validated tool to quantify radiographic pulmonary edema severity, primarily in ARDS. Moreover, after lung resection (e.g., lobectomy), postoperative chest radiographs may reflect a mixture of atelectasis, edema, and postoperative consolidation; therefore, RALE findings in this setting should be interpreted cautiously and primarily as a nonspecific measure of postoperative radiographic lung opacification.

Clinical relevance

Compared to the PHYX-IT study by Zorrilla-Vaca et al., which reported a 9% incidence of hypoxemia (SpO₂ <90% for ≥ 5 min) in over 3,000 patients, our study found a higher rate (29%) using a more sensitive threshold (SpO₂ <92% with FiO₂ = 1), which systematically triggered clinical interventions. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in definitions and clinical thresholds for action. Nevertheless, they reported risk factors consistent with ours, particularly regarding BMI. Moreover, our cohort included patients with fewer comorbidities and lower BMI, resulting in a more physiologically homogeneous population, better suited to exploring the underlying mechanisms of hypoxemia. Importantly, hypoxemia in our study systematically triggered specific ventilatory interventions (recruitment maneuvers or ventilator adjustments), underscoring its direct clinical relevance.

Recent large randomized trials, such as the PROTHOR study [7], evaluated the effects of systematic intraoperative strategies combining high PEEP and recruitment maneuvers on postoperative outcomes during thoracic surgery. In contrast, our study did not aim to assess the efficacy of a predefined ventilatory strategy, but rather to explore individual oxygenation responses to recruitment maneuvers as a physiological marker. Identifying intraoperative hypoxemia profiles based on the response to recruitment maneuvers may help stratify the risk of postoperative pulmonary complications. This physiological classification could inform a more targeted and individualized perioperative approach, particularly with respect to postoperative respiratory managing, rather than a uniform strategy applied to all patients.

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, it is a retrospective observational study conducted at a single center. This model is subject to information bias due to the retrospective nature of data collection. Statistical adjustments and selection criteria partially accounted for confounding factors, these could not be entirely controlled. In particular, postoperative respiratory physiotherapy was routinely applied according to institutional practice, and its specific impact on postoperative pulmonary complications could not be separately assessed. Additionally, the monocentric nature of the study limits the generalizability of our findings to other centers. Finally, as an observational study, it does not allow for conclusions regarding causal relationships.

The small number of patients in the non-responder group (n = 15) limits statistical power for subgroup analyses, particularly for secondary outcomes. Accordingly, nonsignificant findings—especially those involving the non-responder group—should be interpreted with caution and should not be considered evidence of equivalence between groups.

Finally, our interpretation is based on physiologic inference rather than direct measurements, and future studies incorporating direct assessments of lung aeration and gas exchange—such as intraoperative lung ultrasound, computed tomography imaging, or shunt fraction measurements—would be required to confirm this mechanistic distinction.

To our knowledge, only one previous study has examined hypoxemia during one-lung ventilation, reporting risk factors consistent with ours, particularly regarding BMI [4]. However, our study extends these findings by also including the postoperative period and patient outcomes. Specifically, we explored the relationship between pulmonary complications and presumed intraoperative hypoxemia mechanism, according to the response to recruitment maneuvers.

Conclusion

Our study suggests that hypoxemic patients who respond to recruitment maneuvers during one-lung ventilation are patients particularly prone to atelectasis and postoperative complications. These patients, with an hypoxemia compatible with atelectasis, more so than non-responders or those without hypoxemia, may benefit from a tailored perioperative approach specifically aimed at reducing the occurrence of atelectasis during both the intraoperative and postoperative periods. Further studies are needed to confirm this hypothesis and to better define optimal management strategies for this high-risk group.
