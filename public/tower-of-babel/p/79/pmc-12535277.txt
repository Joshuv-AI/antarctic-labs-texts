A Phase 2a Trial of Myeloperoxidase Inhibitor Mitiperstat in Non‐Cirrhotic Metabolic Dysfunction‐Associated Steatohepatitis
Authors: Javier Armisen, Armando Flor, John Eriksson, Karin Nelander, Chandrali S Bhattacharya, Mikael Sunnåker, Debra Carter, Monika Trebski, Emma Gårdmark, Malin Aurell, Carl Whatling, Benjamin Challis, Umberto Vespasiani‐Gentilucci, the COSMOS Investigators

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsNeutrophil myeloperoxidase is involved in different inflammatory diseases, including metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH). Mitiperstat is a highly potent myeloperoxidase inhibitor. This phase 2a clinical trial evaluated the efficacy, safety, pharmacokinetics, and pharmacodynamics of mitiperstat in patients with non‐cirrhotic MASH.MethodsThe study enrolled 112 adult participants with histological MASH F1–3 fibrosis and increased alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels. Participants were randomised 1:1 to once‐daily mitiperstat 5 mg or placebo. Primary and secondary endpoints were absolute and placebo‐adjusted changes from baseline to week 12 in ALT and N‐terminal type‐III collagen propeptide (Pro‐C3) levels. Exploratory endpoints included other MASH‐related biomarkers.ResultsAt week 12, there were no significant changes versus placebo for ALT (+7.5%, p = 0.893) and Pro‐C3 (−0.9%, p = 0.396), or any other MASH‐related biomarkers. Mitiperstat was safe and well tolerated.ConclusionThis study does not provide proof of concept that mitiperstat 5 mg exerts an anti‐inflammatory/anti‐fibrotic effect in MASH.Trial Registration ClincalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05638737Keywords: metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH), metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), mitiperstat, myeloperoxidase inhibitor

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most common cause of chronic liver disease in Western industrialised countries [1]. Non‐alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), more recently redefined as metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH), is the progressive inflammatory form of the disease, with a prevalence of approximately 2%–3% of the general population. MASH can lead to cirrhosis and related complications, including hepatocellular carcinoma and end‐stage liver disease [2], and is expected to become the leading cause for liver transplantation over the next decade [3].

In the face of this rapidly growing epidemic, few therapeutic options are available for MASH. Indeed, only two drugs have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for non‐cirrhotic MASH to date [4, 5], with lifestyle changes still being the only available treatment in most countries. Therefore, there is a significant need for new therapies to manage MASH more effectively.

One of the key conditions that distinguishes MASH from simple steatosis is liver inflammation, to which lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction are key contributors [6]. Kupffer cells and neutrophils are the main players in this inflammatory process [7]. Neutrophils are elevated in patients with MASH and a high neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio is associated with advanced fibrosis [8]. Once recruited, neutrophils induce hepatocyte injury through the production of proinflammatory mediators and reactive oxygen species, as well as the release of granule proteins. Myeloperoxidase (MPO) is a highly abundant protein mainly present in azurophilic granules of neutrophils, constituting 5% of the dry weight of the cells, and is deposited and active in the livers of patients with MASH [9, 10, 11]. As such, MPO has been identified as a potential target for the treatment of MASH. Mitiperstat is a highly potent MPO inhibitor.

This phase 2a trial aimed to obtain proof of concept that mitiperstat can reduce the inflammation and fibrogenesis typical of MASH, while confirming a good safety profile in this patient population.

Methods

Study Oversight

This phase 2a clinical trial was a randomised, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, parallel‐group, multicenter study in adult participants with biopsy‐proven non‐cirrhotic MASH (non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease activity score [NAS] ≥ 4 and fibrosis stages F1–3). The study was conducted at 44 sites across 9 countries (Argentina, Denmark, Italy, Mexico, Norway, Portugal, Spain, Sweden, and the US) from 26 October 2022 to 4 April 2024.

Prior to study initiation, the protocol was approved by the independent ethics committees at each participating center, and all participants provided their informed consent.

Mitiperstat Dose

The selection of the 5 mg dose was informed by prior clinical studies, including a multiple ascending dose study in healthy volunteers [12] and the phase 2a SATELLITE study in patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction [13]. In these studies, the 5 mg dose consistently achieved 40%–60% inhibition of MPO activity in the range of 40%–60% and was considered the upper safety dose, as no rash was observed at doses below 15 mg.

Patients

Participants were between 18 and 75 years of age, had histologically confirmed MASH with NAS ≥ 4 and F1–3 fibrosis as diagnosed by liver biopsy within 12 months of screening, and two elevated serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) measurements (at screening and on at least one occasion in the 6 months prior to screening), defined as > 1.5 × upper limit of normal (ULN: 48 U/L for male and 43 U/L for female).

Key exclusion criteria were excessive alcohol consumption; concomitant liver disease of other aetiology; ongoing clinically significant cardiovascular, respiratory, gastrointestinal, or psychiatric disease; and recent use of weight loss drugs or therapies associated with the development of MASLD. For participants on glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists, sodium‐glucose co‐transporter 2 inhibitors, or pioglitazone, the medication dose had to be stable for at least 6 months prior to randomization. All participants were required to have stable weight for at least 3 months prior to randomization.

Following an 8‐week screening period, participants were centrally randomised 1:1 to receive once‐daily mitiperstat 5 mg tablet or matching placebo using a computerised procedure.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was change in ALT levels from baseline to Week 12 and over placebo. The secondary endpoint was change in N‐terminal type III collagen propeptide (Pro‐C3) levels from baseline to Week 12 and over placebo. Exploratory endpoints were effects on circulating markers of hepatic injury and apoptosis/necrosis (i.e., cytokeratin‐18 [CK‐18]), fibrosis/fibrogenesis (i.e., fibrosis‐4 [FIB4] index, enhanced liver fibrosis [ELF] score, and NASH Identification and Severity score [NIS4]), and lipid/metabolic parameters.

Adverse events (AEs) and serious AEs (SAEs) were assessed at each scheduled visit during the 12‐week treatment period and the 4‐week safety follow‐up period.

Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of mitiperstat were also assessed.

Statistical Analyses

Sample Size

Sample size calculations determined a target enrollment of 45 participants per arm to have 80% power to detect a 25% decrease in ALT levels from baseline to week 12 for mitiperstat versus placebo. The calculation was based on the full analysis set (FAS), assuming 10% missing data, and a one‐sided alpha of 0.05. The analysis employed a linear mixed effects model with participant as a random effect; treatment, visit, and treatment by visit interaction as fixed effects; and baseline as a covariate, including all participants in the FAS. An autoregressive variance–covariance structure was primarily used.

Endpoint Analysis

For the analysis of the primary and secondary endpoints, missing data were imputed before analysis. Non‐monotone missing data were handled via multiple imputations using a Markov Chain Monte Carlo method. Monotone missing data were to be addressed using retrieved dropouts with predictive mean matching or, when this was not possible, placebo‐washout imputations would be used. Sensitivity analyses were conducted, adjusting for factors like type 2 diabetes mellitus and fibrosis score, both with and without imputation.

Sparse pharmacokinetic sampling was employed in the present study. Pharmacokinetic analyses were conducted for all participants in the FAS who received at least one dose of mitiperstat and who had at least one post‐dose pharmacokinetic sample. Steady state predose plasma concentrations of mitiperstat were assessed at day 29 (week 4) and day 85 (week 12). In addition, day 29 included post‐dose sampling at 0.5–1.5 h and 1.5–3 h following administration.

Safety analyses were performed using the safety analysis set, which included all participants who received at least one dose of mitiperstat or placebo.

Results

Study Population

Of the 296 participants screened, 112 were randomised: 56 to the mitiperstat 5 mg group and 56 participants to the placebo group. Of the 56 participants who received mitiperstat, 49 participants (87.5%) completed the study, and 7 participants (12.5%) prematurely discontinued treatment, including four who met a protocol‐specified withdrawal criterion. Of the 56 participants who received placebo, 52 (92.9%) completed the study, and four (7.1%) prematurely discontinued treatment, including three who met a protocol‐specified withdrawal criterion (Figure 1).

Safety

Mitiperstat was generally well tolerated (Table 3). The mean total duration of treatment was 76.1 days in the mitiperstat 5 mg group, and 82.3 days in the placebo group. The proportion of participants reporting AEs was comparable between the mitiperstat group and the placebo group. There were no SAEs reported in the mitiperstat group, while three participants (5.4%) reported SAEs in the placebo group. No deaths occurred.

The proportion of participants with AEs leading to discontinuation of investigational product was 7.1% in the mitiperstat group and 5.4% in the placebo group, with maculopapular rash being the most frequent AE leading to discontinuation.

There were no imbalances in the occurrence of AEs of infection in the mitiperstat group versus the placebo group (21.4% and 25%, respectively), and there was a similar occurrence of maculopapular skin reaction/rash between the mitiperstat and placebo groups (8.9% and 7.1%, respectively).

No clinically significant changes in clinical laboratory results, vital signs, or electrocardiogram were observed.

Pharmacokinetics

The geometric mean pre‐dose plasma concentration of mitiperstat 5 mg appeared to reach steady state at week 4 (week 4 pre‐dose: 18.17 nmol/L, week 12 pre‐dose: 16.84 nmol/L), consistent with the long half‐life of mitiperstat of 52.3 h [14]. The geometric mean plasma concentrations observed in week 4 from 0.5 to 1.5 h post‐dose and 1.5 to 3 h post‐dose appeared to be similar at 35.41 nmol/L and 36.91 nmol/L, respectively, and are consistent with steady‐state maximum concentration exposures observed in a previous study (NCT03136991) [12].

Discussion

Inflammation plays a crucial role in both the pathogenesis and progression of MASH. This complex process involves intricate interactions between immune cells, signalling pathways, and molecular mediators. Upon hepatic injury, neutrophils infiltrate the liver, intensifying the inflammatory response by releasing MPO and reactive oxygen species. The release of MPO in the liver promotes apoptosis and fibrosis, primarily through the activation of inflammatory myeloid cells and hepatic stellate cells, which drive disease progression. Furthermore, MPO enhances the activation of additional immune cells, such as macrophages, further amplifying the hepatic inflammatory environment. This persistent immune activation significantly contributes to fibrosis and the progression toward cirrhosis.

Nonclinical studies have shown that MPO deficiency may reduce fibrosis induced by MASH [10, 15]. In a diet‐induced mouse model of MASH, mitiperstat was administered at a dose achieving a predicted plasma exposure corresponding to approximately 75% inhibition of extracellular MPO activity, as estimated through pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic modelling. This intervention resulted in significant improvements in the histological scoring of liver inflammation and fibrosis (unpublished data). These insights suggested that targeting MPO activity through therapeutic inhibition could be a promising strategy to mitigate liver inflammation and fibrosis associated with MASH.

In this phase 2a study, mitiperstat was well tolerated, with findings consistent with the known clinical safety profile established for mitiperstat in other patient populations [13]. Similarly, the pharmacokinetic properties of mitiperstat observed in the current study aligned with previous reports [16].

For the primary efficacy endpoint, no significant reduction in ALT levels at week 12 relative to baseline was observed for mitiperstat compared with placebo. Additionally, treatment with mitiperstat versus placebo did not result in any significant reductions from baseline to week 12 in Pro‐C3 levels or any of the other exploratory biomarkers related to hepatic inflammation and fibrosis.

The lack of efficacy observed with the MPO inhibitor mitiperstat in patients with MASH may be explained, at least in part, by two key factors. First, although neutrophil‐derived MPO contributes to oxidative stress and inflammation, its role may not be central or sufficient to drive the complex pathophysiology of MASH. Moreover, the limited efficacy may not be due to targeting MPO per se, but rather to the specific targeting of inflammation. Although inflammation represents a central mechanism driving the transition from hepatic steatosis to steatohepatitis, with the associated pro‐fibrogenic switch, most clinical studies have shown that selective targeting of inflammatory pathways does not translate into meaningful benefits in MASH. For example, selonsertib, cenicriviroc, and several other agents directed against key inflammatory mediators all failed to demonstrate significant histological or clinical benefits [17, 18, 19, 20, 21]. Collectively, these findings indicate that blocking individual inflammatory pathways alone has not been sufficient to improve outcomes in MASH, highlighting the complexity of the disease and the likely need for broader or combination therapeutic strategies.

Second, the lack of efficacy could be related to suboptimal drug exposure at the hepatic level. As outlined in the Methods section, the selected dose of mitiperstat was informed by prior studies that demonstrated a significant reduction in MPO activity while maintaining an optimal safety profile [12, 13]. In these studies, neutrophil activity was measured in peripheral blood, a practical and widely accepted surrogate for MPO target engagement. However, circulating neutrophils may not fully reflect the activation state of neutrophils within the hepatic microenvironment, where local inflammatory signals could modulate their behaviour. These considerations underscore the importance of both comprehensive pathway validation and pharmacokinetic optimization in the development of targeted therapies for MASH.

Finally, a potential limitation of our study is the use of ALT and Pro‐C3 as primary and secondary endpoints, as both are potentially affected by limited sensitivity. While ALT has been criticised as a relatively insensitive and non‐specific marker in MASH [22], several prior studies involving effective agents have consistently reported robust ALT reductions in parallel with histological improvement [23, 24, 25, 26]. Similarly, although fibrosis may not significantly change within 12 weeks, both Pro‐C3 and the ELF score primarily reflect fibrogenic activity rather than fixed fibrosis. Multiple studies have shown that these biomarkers can undergo early changes in response to effective therapies [26]. In addition, given the mechanism of action of mitiperstat, we did not anticipate significant changes in intrahepatic fat content. While magnetic resonance imaging proton density fat fraction (MRI‐PDFF) offers a reliable and noninvasive method for quantifying hepatic fat, its inclusion was not aligned with the expected pharmacodynamic profile of the compound.

In conclusion, the results from this phase 2a study do not support the hypothesis that the use of mitiperstat 5 mg is a clinically effective strategy for the treatment of MASH. Further research is required to better understand the complex inflammatory mechanisms underlying MASH pathogenesis and to identify more effective treatment strategies.
