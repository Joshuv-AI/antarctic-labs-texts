Early-life endurance sports lowers frailty and falls in former male athletes
Authors: Keziban Gamze Bas, Rabia Hürrem Ozdurak Singin, Tuba Denizci
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The long-term impact of professional endurance sports on frailty and fall risk in older age remains underexplored. This study examined frailty, physical activity (PA), and health outcomes in older men with and without a history of professional endurance sports. We assessed 200 men aged 60–85 years, 100 former professional endurance athletes (For-A) with ≥ 5 years of competitive experience and 100 age-matched non-athletes (Non-A). Frailty was evaluated using the Frailty Assessment Screening Tool (FAST), PA with the International PA Questionnaire-Short Form, quality of life (QoL) perception via self-report, and falls/hospitalizations via examination. Group differences were tested with Independent t-tests, Mann-Whitney U, and chi-square, while ANCOVA and logistic regression were employed to adjust for current PA. For-A showed lower frailty prevalence (24% vs. 68%, p = 0.001), higher PA (2385 vs. 299 MET-min/week, p = 0.001), better QoL (median 7 vs. 5, p = 0.001), fewer falls (OR = 0.023, 95% CI [0.004, 0.127], p < 0.001), and a non-significant reduction in hospitalizations (OR = 0.414, 95% CI [0.142, 1.202], p = 0.105) in the past year compared to Non-A. Frailty (p = 0.003) and falls (p < 0.001) remained significantly lower in For-A after adjustment for current PA (p = 0.003), but hospitalization differences attenuated. Early-life endurance training is associated with reduced frailty and potentially lower fall risk. While psychological health and hospitalization outcomes appear more dependent on current activity, the potential benefits for mobility, physical performance, continence, and memory suggest the importance of structured youth sports in supporting healthy and resilient aging. Introduction

Frailty, a multidimensional geriatric syndrome, arises from cumulative declines in physiological reserves across multiple systems, increasing vulnerability to stressors and reducing resilience to minor insults1. It is characterized by chronic inflammation, unintentional weight loss, muscle weakness, reduced physical activity, accelerated osteoporosis, cognitive impairment, impaired balance, and diminished gait speed and endurance, all of which impair physiological homeostasis2,3. Musculoskeletal aging, particularly osteoporosis and sarcopenia, serves as a primary driver of frailty, contributing to disability, altered body composition, and hormonal imbalances4. Bone and muscle tissues, functioning as both target and endocrine organs, interact through paracrine and endocrine signaling, including myokine and osteokine pathways, to modulate systemic health4,5. Neurological, endocrine, and immune dysregulation in frailty impairs muscle homeostasis, hindering myogenesis and hypertrophy while promoting protein catabolism driven by inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6 and TNF-α. These cytokines accelerate muscle breakdown to supply amino acids for energy during physical exertion, ultimately leading to reduced muscle mass, strength, and functional capacity6,7.

Frailty is underpinned by fundamental aging processes, including genomic instability, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cellular senescence, which collectively compromise physiological resilience8,9. Consequently, frailty manifests as deficits in mobility, physical performance, cognitive function, and overall health, significantly increasing the risk of falls, hospitalizations, long-term care dependency, and mortality. Its prevalence ranges from 11 to 25% in adults over 65 years, rising to 50% in those over 85 years10,11. This creates a vicious cycle wherein frailty limits mobility and PA, further exacerbating functional decline and elevating risks of adverse outcomes, posing a significant public health challenge in aging populations12–14. As a cornerstone of public health strategy, regular PA mitigates frailty and reduces the burden of chronic diseases, aligning with geroscience principles linking aging to disease prevention14,15. Validated tools, such as the Frailty Assessment Screening Tool (FAST), enable precise, multidimensional assessment of frailty, facilitating targeted clinical interventions and research into preventive strategies16.

Regular PA is pivotal in preventing frailty by attenuating age-related declines across musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, pulmonary, and endocrine systems17–21. However, only approximately 25% of adults over 65 years meet the World Health Organization’s recommendation of at least 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise per week, highlighting a critical gap in health maintenance among older adults19–21. PA counteracts frailty by enhancing mitochondrial function, improving insulin sensitivity, reducing systemic inflammation, and preserving muscle and bone integrity, thereby bolstering resilience and mitigating chronic disease burden22,23. The dynamic interplay between PA and frailty underscores that sustained activity improves mobility, balance, and cognitive function, whereas inactivity exacerbates frailty progression and associated risks, such as falls and hospitalizations10,24. The efficacy of PA varies by type, intensity, and timing across the lifespan25–27. Endurance sports, characterized by high-volume, high-intensity training, induce robust physiological adaptations, including mitochondrial biogenesis, enhanced antioxidant defenses, and preservation of muscle fiber integrity, all of which promote healthy aging27–29. Notably, early-life participation in endurance sports preserves physical capacity and attenuates age-related functional decline in later life29,30. For example, former elite endurance athletes exhibit superior cardiorespiratory fitness, greater muscle strength, and lower sarcopenia prevalence compared to sedentary peers, reflecting sustained physiological reserves established in youth30,31. These adaptations likely reduce frailty-related risks by enhancing neuromuscular coordination, cardiovascular health, and cognitive reserve, thereby decreasing the incidence of falls, hospitalizations, and cognitive decline28,30. Longitudinal studies of former Olympic athletes further demonstrate that early-life endurance training is associated with reduced mortality and extended health span, underscoring its protective role against age-related decline32. However, the specific impact of early-life endurance sports on PA levels, frailty, and fall risk in former professional male athletes remains underexplored.

This study investigates the effects of early-life professional endurance sports participation on frailty levels, fall risk, and health outcomes in men aged 60–85 years compared to age-matched non-athletes. We hypothesize that early-life endurance sports participation reduces frailty and fall risk in later life, promoting healthy aging through sustained physiological adaptations.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional quantitative study with a parallel design was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and CONSORT guidelines, with approval from the ethical committee (2022/GOAEK/13).

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1.9.6 (Franz Faul, Universität Kiel, Germany) to detect a statistically significant difference in frailty between groups with an effect size f = 0.60, α = 0.05, power 1-β = 0.95. A minimum of 88 participants per group was required, but to account for potential exclusions, a larger initial sample was recruited.

Sampling strategy

A total of 502 older male adults aged 60–85 years volunteered for the study and provided written informed consent prior to screening for inclusion and exclusion criteria, using a convenience sampling strategy. The Former athlete (For-A) group was recruited via criterion sampling, where 180 participants for For-A were invited by phone from a list of professional elite athletes obtained from the National Sport Federations. For-A were defined as individuals with at least 5 years of competitive history at an international, national, or professional level in endurance sports, specifically cycling, swimming, rowing, triathlon, wrestling and long-distance running including 5,000-meter, 10,000-meter, half-marathon and marathon events, who had ceased professional competition at least 25 years prior to the study to ensure the assessment of long-term effects of early-life training. The mean time since cessation of competitive participation was 35.6 ± 6.8 years (range: 31–42), as reported during the initial phone interview. While wrestling involves short-duration, high-intensity efforts such as 6-minute matches in Olympic freestyle or Greco-Roman formats, its inclusion as an endurance sport is justified by the requirement for repeated high-intensity bouts across multiple matches in a single day, for example 3–5 matches in Olympic tournaments and training programs that emphasize aerobic and anaerobic endurance through high-intensity interval training and wrestling-specific drills33,34. Sports with a high risk of concussion or head trauma such as football, boxing and combat sports were excluded to minimize confounding effects of traumatic injuries. The distribution of For-A participants across these sports is provided in Supplementary File 1. Conversely, the non-athlete (Non-A) group was recruited via simple random sampling, where 322 age-matched community-dwelling older males without a professional athletic background were invited through posters displayed in public spaces such as parks and shopping centers. Groups differed in recruitment but were age-similar (median 70 years, IQR: 8), while all inclusion and exclusion criteria was same for both groups except the sport background.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were screened via an initial phone interview assessing age between 60 and 85 years, body mass index (BMI < 30 kg/m²), and alcohol/tobacco consumption, with For-A additionally queried about serious sports injuries, whereas Non-A were assessed for prior sports history to confirm the absence of participation in amateur or professional sports requiring structured training, in accordance with the inclusion criteria. Eligible participants underwent a face-to-face health examination to exclude those with a history of sports injuries, head trauma, traffic accidents, severe emotional distress such as loss of spouse or child, psychiatric disorders, mental illness, physical disabilities, or medical conditions contraindicated for PA. Of the initial volunteers, 32 former athletes and 129 non-athletes were excluded, primarily due to smoking or BMI ≥ 30 kg/m². Participants with a BMI > 30 kg/m² were excluded from the study to minimize the confounding effects of obesity on frailty and physical performance outcomes, consistent with the study’s inclusion criteria. Following screening, 100 For-A were deemed eligible and allocated to the For-A group. From 132 eligible Non-A, an equal number (n = 100) were randomly selected for the Non-A group using a computerized simple random sampling method, ensuring no prior participation in amateur or professional sports. The detailed participant flow is presented in Fig. 1.

Data collection and assessment protocol

Both groups were assessed by the same trained specialist, who also collected QoL perception data. Participants were assigned anonymous codes on a single list and used pseudonyms during assessments to conceal their group identity. Frailty was evaluated using the Frailty Assessment Screening Tool by a blinded geriatrician, while PA levels were measured via the PA Questionnaire-Short Form by a third researcher, also blinded to group allocation. QoL was self-reported on a zero to ten (0–10) scale, while falls, hospitalizations, and age perceptions were collected via interview.

BMI was calculated to assess body composition and control for its potential confounding effects on frailty outcomes. Weight and height were measured for all participants by the same trained research staff. Weight was measured to the nearest 0.1 kg using a calibrated digital scale (Seca 813, Hamburg, Germany), with participants wearing light clothing and no shoes. Height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm using a stadiometer (Seca 217, Hamburg, Germany), with participants standing barefoot, heels together, and head in the Frankfurt plane. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m²), expressed as kg/m², following the standard formula35.

Frailty assessment screening tool (FAST)

Frailty was assessed using FAST, a validated and reliable instrument designed to identify frailty and pre-frailty in community-dwelling older adults16. FAST was chosen for its strong psychometric properties with a Cronbach’s alpha = 0.794, test-retest reliability = 0.747 and its ability to evaluate multiple frailty dimensions, including general health, functional status, mobility, mood, memory, and physical performance, in a concise and practical manner16,36. Unlike other frailty tools, FAST incorporates a broader range of domains classified under 10 sub-domains including mobility, physical performance, continence, medication use, memory, functional status, mood, general health, nutrition, and pain, with each domain scored on a Likert scale (0–4, where 0 indicates no impairment and 4 indicates severe impairment). It also includes a physical performance measure, the Timed Up and Go test, assessing mobility, balance, walking ability, and fall risk in older adults. The easy to use, short screening tool with strong psychometric properties makes it particularly suitable for detecting subtle frailty differences in relatively healthy populations, such as former athletes16. The tool’s adaptation to the Turkish population ensured cultural and linguistic appropriateness for this study36. Participants completed the self-reported components via structured interviews, while performance-based measures were assessed using standardized protocols36. Assessments were conducted by a blinded geriatrician to minimize bias, with participants categorized as non-frail (score ≤ 4), pre-frail (score 5–6), or frail (score 7–14).

PA questionnaire-short form

PA level was determined by using International PA Questionnaire-Short developed by Craig et al.37 and adapted to Turkish by Saglam et al.38. Participants’ PA levels were calculated in MET-minutes per week and converted to METs, using the standard definition of 1 MET as 3.5 ml O₂/kg/min, representing the basal metabolic rate. Weekly PA scores below 600 MET were classified as ‘inactive’, whereas scores between 600 and 3000 MET were classified as ‘low PA level’ and scores greater than 3000 MET were named as ‘active’38.

Statistical analysis

Normality of sample was tested via Kolmogorov-Smirnov test while variance was confirmed with Levene’s test. T-tests compared means, Mann-Whitney U tested medians, whereas categorical data was analyzed with and chi-square analysis. To compare frailty scores between For-A and Non-A while adjusting for current PA levels measured as MET-min/week, ANCOVA was used with group as the independent variable and PA as a covariate. For falls and hospitalizations, logistic regression models were employed to assess the association between group status (For-A vs. Non-A) and outcomes (falls: 0 = none, 1 = any; hospitalizations: 0 = none, 1 = any), with PA as a covariate. All analyses were conducted using SPSS 22.0, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

The median age of For-A (n = 100) and Non-A (n = 100) was 70 years (IQR: 8, p > 0.05), while BMI did not differ between For-A (25.3 ± 3.6) and Non-A (24.7 ± 3.1) (p > 0.05). For-A had lower frailty scores (4.93 ± 2.34 vs. 8.51 ± 3.34 p = 0.001), higher PA (2385.24 ± 893.88 vs. 298.75 ± 305.51 MET-min/week, t(198) = −22.08, p = 0.001), and better QoL (Md = 7, IQR: 6–9 vs. Md = 5, IQR: 4–6, p = 0.001) compared to Non-A (Table 1). For-A reported fewer falls (2% none vs. 50%, χ² =68,389; p < 0.001), hospitalizations (80% none vs. 30%, χ² =53,194; p < 0.001), and negative age perceptions (e.g., 38% “very old” vs. 15%, χ² =35,860; p < 0.001) than Non-A, with 37% feeling “young” vs. 7% (Table 1) (Fig. 2).

To account for the confounding effect of current PA on frailty, falls, and hospitalization, we adjusted for PA measured as MET-min/week in our analyses. Logistic regression analyses, adjusted for current PA levels, showed that the For-A group had significantly lower odds of falls (OR = 0.023, 95% CI [0.004, 0.127], p < 0.001) and a non-significant reduction in hospitalizations (OR = 0.414, 95% CI [0.142, 1.202], p = 0.105) compared to the Non-A group. These findings indicate that a former professional endurance sports career is associated with reduced falls, independent of current PA levels, while hospitalization differences may be influenced by other factors.

Frailty remained lower in For-A after adjustment for current PA levels (F(1,198) = 9.24, p = 0.003, partial η² = 0.045). To further explore group differences in frailty sub-domains while controlling for current PA, a General Linear Model (GLM) was conducted with group (For-A vs. Non-A) as the independent variable and PA (MET-min/week) as a covariate. The multivariate test revealed significant group differences across frailty sub-domains (Pillai’s Trace = 0.336, F(10,188) = 9.495, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.336), indicating a moderate-to-large effect of group status. Univariate tests showed significant group differences in several frailty sub-domains such as memory (F(1,198) = 10.119, p = 0.002, partial η² = 0.049), mobility (F(1,198) = 10.668, p = 0.001, partial η² = 0.051), physical performance (F(1,198) = 38.969, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.165), medication use (F(1,198) = 8.524, p = 0.004, partial η² = 0.041), and continence (F(1,198) = 20.509, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.094). No significant group differences were observed for nutrition (F(1,198) = 2.993, p = 0.085), functional status (F(1,198) = 3.399, p = 0.067), mood (F(1,198) = 0.314, p = 0.576), general health status (F(1,198) = 1.649, p = 0.201), or pain (F(1,198) = 0.444, p = 0.506) after adjustment (Table 2). These findings suggest that former professional endurance athletes exhibit lower frailty in specific domains, particularly mobility, physical performance, and continence, independent of current PA levels.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study reveals that early-life participation in professional endurance sports is associated with significantly lower frailty prevalence, reduced fall risk, higher PA levels, enhanced QoL, and more positive age perceptions in For-A compared to age-matched Non-A (Table 1; Figs. 2 and 3). After adjusting for current PA levels, the For-A group maintained significantly lower frailty scores (p = 0.003, partial η² = 0.045) and superior performance in key frailty sub-domains, including mobility (p = 0.001), physical performance (p < 0.001), continence (p < 0.001), and memory (p = 0.002), while differences in mood, general health, and hospitalization rates became non-significant (p > 0.05) (Table 2). These findings, persisting despite an average 35.6 ± 6.8 years since competitive training cessation, suggest that early-life endurance training may contribute to active aging, with sustained benefits in specific frailty sub-domains and psychological outcomes, partially independent of current PA levels.

PA is a critical determinant of frailty, directly influencing mobility, physical performance, and other frailty criteria, as established by Fried’s phenotype and supported by literature demonstrating an inverse relationship between PA and frailty risk15,39. In our study, the For-A group’s markedly higher PA (Fig. 3b) reflect an athletic identity fostered through early-life competitive endurance sports, promoting lifelong exercise habits29,40. This athletic identity, characterized by discipline, goal-setting, and psychological resilience, contributes to enhanced QoL and more positive age perceptions in the For-A group, aligning with studies of master and track athletes showing that continued sports participation enhances health span, life satisfaction, and counters negative aging stereotypes25,40,41. These findings suggest that the discipline and resilience fostered through competitive endurance sports promote an active aging paradigm, significantly reducing frailty and enhancing well-being in later life39, as evidenced by the higher physical activity levels and lower frailty prevalence observed in the For-A group.

The For-A group’s sustained benefits in frailty sub-domains, observed after adjusting for current PA, may reflect specific physiological adaptations established through early-life endurance training, with lasting effects independent of current PA levels42–46. A key adaptation to training is increased capillarization, driven by vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling in response to elevated oxygen demands during exercise, which enhances muscle perfusion, oxygen delivery, and metabolic efficiency47,48. This persistently contributes to the For-A group’s superior mobility (p = 0.001) and physical performance (p < 0.001) independent of current PA (Table 2). Similarly, endurance training stimulates mitochondrial biogenesis via peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ coactivator-1α (PGC-1α), activating nuclear respiratory factors (NRF-1/2) and mitochondrial transcription factor A (Tfam), thereby increasing mitochondrial density and oxidative capacity that persist long-term49,50. Muscle biopsies from trained athletes reveal enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis, increased capillarization, and elevated antioxidant enzyme activity, which durably preserve muscle fiber integrity and cellular homeostasis, attenuating sarcopenia and frailty even 35–40 years post-retirement29,51,52. Former endurance athletes exhibit sustained increases in type I fiber proportion, mitochondrial density, and cardiovascular efficiency, reflecting a long-lasting “muscle memory” that primes skeletal muscle for enhanced responsiveness to PA in later life29,53. These persistent adaptations underpin the For-A group’s lower frailty scores and reduced fall risk (OR = 0.023, p < 0.001), as evidenced by their sustained neuromuscular coordination and proprioception, critical for balance and gait control54. Likewise, improved continence may result from strengthened pelvic floor muscles and neuromuscular coordination developed through early-life high-intensity training, maintaining benefits despite decades since training cessation40.

Beyond physical adaptations, early-life endurance sports foster enduring cognitive benefits that sustain active aging, independent of current PA levels. The For-A group’s cognitive advantages, particularly in memory (p = 0.002 post-adjustment), reflect the lasting impact of aerobic exercise on cognitive reserve, which might be driven by sustained brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) expression, hippocampal neurogenesis, and synaptic connectivity established in early life55,56. These effects, amplified by but not dependent on the For-A group’s higher PA levels, might reduce neuroinflammation and support hippocampal function, as evidenced by longitudinal studies of master athletes showing cognitive performance comparable to younger adults57,58. The psychological resilience fostered through early-life competitive sports may enhance stress coping mechanisms, with physically active individuals demonstrating lower cortisol responses and better emotional regulation, contributing to long-term psychological well-being59. These cognitive and psychological benefits, rooted in early-life training, support an active aging paradigm, independent of current PA levels60,61.

Lower medication use in For-A reflects a reduced chronic disease burden, driven by long-term enhancements in cardiovascular and metabolic health from early-life vascularization and mitochondrial function due to intensive training62. The enduring protective effects of early-life endurance training are mediated by exercise-induced hormetic responses, which establish a long-term anti-inflammatory and antioxidant milieu that counteracts inflammaging, independent of current PA. Hormesis, characterized by low-dose reactive oxygen species (ROS) acting as signaling molecules, stimulates redox-sensitive pathways, upregulating antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD2) and glutathione peroxidase (GPX)63,64. This adaptive response sustains cellular resilience to oxidative stress, durably reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 that drive inflammaging65. In our study, the For-A group’s lower frailty prevalence and reduced fall risk, observed after PA adjustment, likely reflect these hormetic adaptations, which might persistently preserve muscle and cognitive function while mitigating frailty sub-domains such as muscle weakness and fatigue (Table 2). By establishing a robust anti-inflammatory milieu, early-life endurance training might protect against age-related degenerative processes, supporting the For-A group’s lower medication use and enhanced functional outcomes, independent of current PA levels60,61. This aligns with the inflammaging theory, suggesting that early-life endurance training might create a lasting anti-inflammatory legacy that mitigates chronic inflammation, thereby reducing frailty and promoting healthy aging.

In contrast, outcomes such as mood, general health, and hospitalization rates were primarily driven by current PA, as group differences became non-significant after adjustment (p > 0.05) (Table 2). Regular moderate-to-vigorous PA enhances psychological well-being through endorphin release and reduced systemic inflammation, benefits that are not unique to early-life sports but are accessible through ongoing activity regardless of prior athletic status66,67. The For-A group’s initially better mood and general health scores likely reflect their higher PA levels (Fig. 3b), but the non-significant hospitalization differences post-adjustment (OR = 0.414, p = 0.105) suggest that current PA, rather than training in youth, drives reduced healthcare utilization (Table 2). Factors such as chronic disease prevalence, healthcare access, or socioeconomic status may also influence hospitalization outcomes, as indicated by the wide confidence intervals in our findings66. Notably, frailty sub-domains like nutrition and pain showed no significant group differences, even before adjustment (p = 0.078 and 0.459), likely due to their dependence on lifestyle factors such as diet quality and pain perception, which are not directly influenced by athletic history. These findings emphasize the importance of sustained PA in older age to maintain mental health, reduce healthcare needs, and address frailty sub-domains not directly tied to early-life adaptations.

The clinical and practical implications of these findings are significant. Frailty and falls are major predictors of morbidity and healthcare costs in older adults, and the substantial reductions in frailty prevalence and fall risk in the For-A group might support the promotion of structured endurance sports in youth as a preventive strategy for healthy aging (Tables 1 and 2). Longitudinal studies, as Antero-Jacquemin et al.32, demonstrate that male Olympic endurance athletes exhibit the lowest mortality rates and longest survival, even 40 years post-career, highlighting the enduring protective effects of early-life training. Similarly, former elite endurance athletes maintain superior cardiorespiratory fitness into their eighth and ninth decades, with VO2 max levels comparable to sedentary adults decades younger60. Colletti et al.68 further showed that older adults with a history of youth sports retain greater muscle fiber preservation and maximal muscle force compared to non-athletes, despite subsequent inactivity. These findings suggest that early-life endurance sports establish physiological reserves that persist into later life, fostering resilience against age-related decline. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that increasing the training volume and intensity beyond the minimum guidelines may enhance positive physiological effects and mitigate the adverse impacts of aging19. Consistent with WHO guidelines, our study demonstrates that high-intensity endurance sports participation in youth, coupled with sustained PA in later life, synergistically promotes healthy aging. Early-life high-intensity training might establish durable physiological reserves, while ongoing PA amplifies these benefits, collectively fostering resilience against age-related decline.

The benefits of exercise extend to frail and pre-frail populations, supporting the applicability of our findings to broader aging cohorts. Randomized controlled trials demonstrate that tailored exercise programs, combining aerobic, strength, and balance training, can reverse frailty and improve physical function in community-dwelling older adults69–72. For example, a 24-week multimodal program enhanced physical functioning and mental health in frail adults, while a 6-week high-intensity interval training program improved aerobic capacity and strength in pre-frail and frail individuals71. A 12-month multicomponent intervention further reduced frailty scores and improved mobility and muscle strength in frail older adults72. These findings highlight the potential of exercise to mitigate frailty across all ages, reinforcing the applicability of our results to diverse populations. Clinically, healthcare providers should advocate for youth sports participation to build long-term resilience and promote community-based exercise programs for older adults, incorporating endurance, strength, and balance training to reduce frailty and healthcare costs. Practically, policymakers should prioritize accessible sports programs for youth and community fitness initiatives to promote healthy aging and alleviate the societal burden of frailty.

This study provides robust contributions to healthy aging research. A large cohort of former professional endurance athletes, with a mean 35.6 ± 6.8 years since competitive training cessation, offers strong statistical power to assess the long-term effects of early-life high-intensity training. Use of the validated FAST16, alongside blinded protocols and strict inclusion criteria contributed to a comprehensive frailty assessment with potentially reduced bias26,27. Findings of lower frailty prevalence and fall risk in former athletes, even after adjusting for current PA (p = 0.003), highlight enduring benefits in mobility, physical performance, continence, and memory, offering novel insights into frailty prevention. Despite these strengths, several limitations must be acknowledged. The exclusion of sports with high concussion risk, such as boxing and football, limits generalizability to other athletic populations, as these sports could potentially have distinct long-term effects on frailty due to neurological impairments73. The use of criterion-based sampling for For-A and random sampling for Non-A could potentially introduce selection bias, potentially influencing observed frailty differences. Reliance on self-reported data for PA and health outcomes increases the risk of recall bias, underscoring the need for objective measures like muscle mass or biomarkers69. The male-only, non-obese sample restricts generalizability to women and individuals with diverse BMI profiles, necessitating further research in these groups. Finally, the absence of physiological markers, such as muscle mass or inflammatory biomarkers, limits our ability to fully elucidate the mechanisms underlying the For-A group’s reduced frailty. Future longitudinal studies should incorporate these measures to disentangle the contributions of early-life training and sustained PA, informing evidence-based strategies for frailty prevention across diverse populations.

Conclusion

Early-life high-intensity endurance training may contribute to lasting physiological and cognitive adaptations that could reduce frailty and fall risk in later life, potentially independent of current PA levels. While psychological health and hospitalization outcomes appear more dependent on current activity, the potential enduring benefits for mobility, physical performance, continence, and memory, along with an athletic identity that may promote lifelong PA, suggest the importance of structured youth sports in supporting healthy and resilient aging. Future longitudinal research is warranted to disentangle the effects of early-life training from sustained physical activity and to guide evidence-based strategies for frailty prevention across diverse populations.
