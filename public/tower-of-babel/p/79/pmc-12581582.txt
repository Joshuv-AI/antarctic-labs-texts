Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly termed non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), is a chronic hepatic condition marked by excessive lipid accumulation in the liver. The disease encompasses a continuum ranging from simple steatosis (MASL) to metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH), potentially progressing to hepatic fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [1–3]. Affecting approximately 32.4% of the global population, MASLD has become a leading cause of liver failure and transplantation [4, 5]. It is commonly associated with obesity, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular disease [6–8]. As a metabolism-related liver disorder, MASLD involves complex pathogenic mechanisms, including dysregulated lipid metabolism, oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and hepatocyte apoptosis [9–11]. Clinically, MASLD presents in two distinct stages: the MASL phase is characterized by hepatic lipid accumulation, while the MASH phase involves hepatocellular injury and inflammation, often leading to fibrosis, cirrhosis, and HCC. Collectively, these epidemiological and clinical features underscore an urgent need to delineate the molecular basis of MASLD to guide prevention and therapy [12, 13]. Nevertheless, major mechanistic uncertainties remain, necessitating the identification of key regulatory molecules and signaling pathways.

Palmitoylation is a reversible post-translational modification whereby palmitate is covalently attached to cysteine residues of target proteins via a thioester bond [14]. This reaction is catalyzed by a family of 23 mammalian palmitoyl acyltransferases defined by the Asp–His–His–Cys (DHHC) motif, whereas depalmitoylation is mediated primarily by acyl-protein thioesterases (APT1/2) and members of the α/β hydrolase domain (ABHD) protein family [15]. Palmitoylation is crucial for controlling protein localization, stability, function, and signal transduction [16]. A growing body of evidence implicates dysregulated palmitoylation in cancer progression, immune evasion, and the pathogenesis of several disorders, including inflammatory bowel disease and Alzheimer’s disease [17–21]. Within hepatic biology, palmitoylation also governs lipid-handling machinery: for example, it regulates the membrane targeting and activity of CD36, and inhibition of CD36 palmitoylation reduces fatty-acid uptake and protects against steatohepatitis in mice [22, 23]. Moreover, palmitoylation enzymes function as disease modifiers—hepatic ZDHHC3 activity exacerbates steatohepatitis, whereas ZDHHC3 deficiency perturbs hepatic lipid handling and mitochondrial metabolism [24]. These observations position palmitoylation as a lipid-sensing regulatory axis with direct relevance to MASLD pathogenesis.

Against this backdrop, the relationship between palmitoylation and MASLD remains unclear, especially its impact on the immune microenvironment. We therefore conducted a comprehensive, multi-dimensional, integrative study to test whether palmitoylation serves as an upstream organizer of MASLD biology. We mapped the distribution of palmitoylation-related genes (PRGs) across distinct molecular subtypes. We identified candidate hub genes and validated their expression in public datasets. Among these candidates, COX6A1, a complex IV subunit, emerged from screening as consistently upregulated and biologically relevant. We then examined how these hubs, including COX6A1, interface with the MASLD immune landscape. We used single-cell transcriptomic data to probe possible immunoregulatory functions. Taken together, these analyses outline a coherent framework that links palmitoylation to metabolic and immune dysregulation in MASLD and provide a conceptual basis for palmitoylation-targeted interventions. Building on these findings, we hypothesize that a PRG program connects mitochondrial dysfunction in hepatocytes with immune activation and thereby shapes MASLD heterogeneity and progression. Within this framework, COX6A1, identified through our screening, may function as a key node that couples complex IV dependent oxidative stress to inflammatory signaling.

Methods

Study design

Flowchart of this study

Dataset source

In this study, we performed an integrative analysis of multiple publicly available datasets, including one single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset and four bulk RNA-seq datasets, as detailed in Supplementary Table S1. All MASLD-related bulk RNA-seq datasets were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), specifically: GSE126848 (n = 45) [25], GSE130970 (n = 78) [26], GSE135251 (n = 216) [27], and GSE213621 (n = 368) [28]. Expression matrices were mapped to platform-specific annotations, converted to Transcripts Per Million (TPM), log2-transformed, and batch-corrected using ComBat (R package sva) [29]with study/batch specified as a covariate; the effectiveness of batch correction was evaluated by PCA before and after correction (Supplementary Fig. S3A–S3B). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between MASLD and control samples were identified using limma with an explicit design matrix including group and batch factors; empirical Bayes–moderated t-statistics were used, and multiple testing was controlled with the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR). Unless otherwise stated, genes meeting FDR < 0.05 and |log2FC| >0.5 were considered significant, and all p-values were two-sided. DEGs between MASLD molecular subtypes—defined by palmitoylation-related gene (PRG) expression patterns—were analyzed using the same framework. Volcano plots were generated using ggplot2 to visualize differential expression patterns. To assemble a PRG candidate set, we queried GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) with the keyword “palmitoylation” and selected genes with a relevance score >12, yielding 50 palmitoylation-related candidates for further analysis (Supplementary Table S2).

Consensus clustering analysis

In this study, we used the GSE213621 bulk RNA-seq dataset for preliminary analyses. Consensus clustering (R package ConsensusClusterPlus) was performed on the expression profiles of palmitoylation-related genes. The procedure was repeated for 50 resampling iterations, subsampling 80% of samples per iteration. The optimal number of clusters was determined by inspecting the cumulative distribution function (CDF) curves of the consensus scores and the consensus matrix heatmap, to ensure robust and stable sample stratification.

Functional and pathway enrichment analysis

In this study, functional enrichment analyses of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were conducted using the R package “clusterProfiler” [30], focusing on Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways and Gene Ontology (GO) terms. GO analysis encompasses three categories: biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC). To control for false-positive results, multiple hypothesis testing was adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, with adjusted P-values below 0.05 considered statistically significant to ensure result reliability. Additionally, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed to investigate pathway differences between high- and low-expression groups. Annotated gene sets from the MSigDB database served as the reference background, and enriched pathways between clusters were identified. Results were ranked by adjusted P-values (< 0.05) and normalized enrichment scores (NES), providing insights into the biological mechanisms underlying distinct disease states.

Analysis of gene co-expression networks using weights (WGCNA)

In this study, Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) [31] (version 1.70.3) was conducted on bulk RNA-seq data using the WGCNA R package to identify co-expression modules significantly correlated with palmitoylation-related genes. To enhance analytical robustness, the top 25% most variable genes—based on variance—were selected for downstream analysis. Initial quality control involved hierarchical clustering of samples and exclusion of those with excessive missing values. The optimal soft-thresholding power (β) was determined to construct a scale-free network reflective of underlying biological characteristics. An adjacency matrix was used to compute the topological overlap matrix (TOM), which facilitated module detection via the dynamic tree cut algorithm. Subsequently, highly similar modules were merged to improve clustering accuracy. For each gene, Module Membership (MM) and Gene Significance (GS) were calculated, and correlations between individual modules and clinical traits (e.g., MASLD subtypes) were evaluated. The module exhibiting the strongest association with palmitoylation was selected for further functional enrichment analysis and hub gene identification.

Machine learning-based identification and diagnostic analysis of palmitoylation-related hub genes in MASLD subtypes

To identify palmitoylation-related hub genes across distinct molecular subtypes of MASLD, we employed a previously established methodology [32], normalizing gene expression matrices from all datasets into z-scores to ensure cross-cohort comparability. Immune infiltration analysis

As previously described, six distinct algorithms—xCell [33], EPIC, MCP-counter [34], QUANTISEQ, CIBERSORT [35], and TIMER [36]—were employed to comprehensively evaluate immune cell infiltration associated with palmitoylation-related genes across MASLD subtypes using the same bulk RNA-seq dataset. These computational approaches estimate the relative abundance or enrichment scores of immune cell subsets within each sample based on predefined immune marker gene sets. Intergroup differences in immune infiltration were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. To visualize the immune landscape generated by the different algorithms, heatmaps were constructed to display infiltration profiles across MASLD subtypes. Additionally, seven categories of immunomodulatory factors [37], seven predefined immune subtypes, and immunophenoscores (IPS) [38] were integrated to further characterize the immunological heterogeneity among MASLD subgroups, particularly in relation to clustering based on palmitoylation-related gene expression.

Single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis

In this study, a publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset (GSE136103; see Table S1) was used to characterize the cellular composition and gene expression landscape of the liver microenvironment in MASLD-induced cirrhosis [39]. Data preprocessing was conducted using the Seurat R package. Raw expression data were initially normalized using the NormalizeData function, followed by identification of the top 2,000 highly variable genes (HVGs) via FindVariableFeatures. Dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis (PCA) through the RunPCA function. To correct for potential batch effects, the Harmony R package was applied (Supplementary Figs. 3 C and 3D). Cellular neighborhoods were computed with FindNeighbors, and optimal clustering was achieved using FindClusters. Two-dimensional visualization was performed using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Cell type annotation was based on canonical marker genes corresponding to each cluster, differential expression was tested using Seurat FindMarkers with the two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test, restricting to genes detected in ≥ 10% of cells in either group (min.pct = 0.10) and applying an effect-size threshold logfc.threshold = 0.25. Multiple testing was controlled by Benjamini–Hochberg FDR; unless otherwise specified, genes with FDR < 0.05 were considered significant. Additionally, pseudotime trajectory analysis was carried out using the Monocle R package to order cells along developmental trajectories and identify key branching points, thereby revealing dynamic changes in cellular lineages. Intercellular communication was further explored using the CellChat R package, which inferred and visualized signaling networks based on scRNA-seq data [40].

Cell culture and small interfering RNA transfection

The HepG2 cell line was obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Gibco, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco), and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. To establish an in vitro steatotic liver model, logarithmically growing HepG2 cells were treated with palmitic acid (PA; 200 µM; KunChuang Technology Development Co., Xi’an, China) for 48 h. Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) were purchased from Haixing Biosciences and transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded onto glass coverslips in six-well plates and transfected the following day. Cells were harvested 24–48 h post-transfection for downstream experiments. Each experiment included a blank control and a negative control, and all procedures were performed in triplicate. Knockdown efficiency of the target gene was evaluated by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). The siRNA sequences targeting COX6A1 are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells and tissue samples using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed by UV–Vis spectrophotometry, with acceptable A260/A280 ratios of 1.8–2.0. For reverse transcription, 2 µg of total RNA was used to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA) with a commercial kit (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the SYBR Green method (Sevenbio, China); primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S4. Each 20 µL reaction contained 10 µL SYBR Green Master Mix, 1 µL forward primer (10 µM), 1 µL reverse primer (10 µM), 2 µL cDNA template, and 6 µL RNase-free water. β-Actin was used as the internal control to normalize gene expression. All samples were run in technical triplicates, and each experiment was independently repeated at least three times. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method.

Western blot

Following the collection of cells and mouse liver tissues, proteins were extracted with RIPA buffer (Solarbio) combined with ultrasonic disruption. Lysates were centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected for subsequent analyses. Protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were resolved on 12.5% SDS–PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked at room temperature in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) containing 5% nonfat dry milk.Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies (manufacturer and catalog number): anti-COX6A1 (PS03703, Abmart), anti-NDUFA4 (PA1787, Abmart), anti-p53 (ET1601-13, HUABIO, Hangzhou, China), anti-BCL2 (381702, ZEN-BIOSCIENCE), anti-BAX (R22708, ZEN-BIOSCIENCE), anti-caspase-3 (R23315, ZEN-BIOSCIENCE), anti-caspase-9 (R22844, ZEN-BIOSCIENCE), anti-cleaved caspase-3 (#9661, CST), anti-cleaved caspase-9 (#9505, CST), anti-GAPDH (P60037, Abmart), and anti-β-actin (AC026, ABclonal), at manufacturer-recommended dilutions. GAPDH and β-actin served as loading controls. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (AS014, ABclonal). Bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and imaged with a chemiluminescence detection system.

Mitochondrial membrane potential

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was assessed using the JC-1 staining assay. HepG2 cells were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 8 × 10^5 cells per well and cultured for 24 h. After the indicated treatments, cells were incubated with 1 mL JC-1 working solution (2 µM; C2003S, Beyotime, China) at 37 °C for 20 min, protected from light. Cells were then washed three times with PBS to remove excess dye. Fluorescence images were acquired using an inverted fluorescence microscope. JC-1 aggregates (indicative of high/normal ΔΨm) emitted red fluorescence, whereas JC-1 monomers (indicative of depolarized/low ΔΨm) emitted green fluorescence.

Measurement of mitochondrial ROS

HepG2 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells/well and cultured for 24 h before treatment. Mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were assessed using the mitochondria-specific superoxide indicator MitoSOX™ Red (S0061S, Beyotime, China). Cells were incubated with 1 µM MitoSOX Red and Hoechst 33,342 at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by three washes with PBS to remove residual dye. Fluorescence images were acquired using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Lipid accumulation detection

Cells or tissue samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and dehydrated using 60% isopropanol. Lipid accumulation was assessed with Oil Red O working solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were stained for 15 min, briefly rinsed 2–3 times with 60% isopropanol, and then washed with distilled water. Coverslips were mounted, and images were captured using a light microscope. For Nile Red staining, cells were rinsed with PBS and incubated with Nile Red staining solution (Solarbio) at 37 °C for 10 min. Following incubation, cells were washed again with PBS, and lipid fluorescence was visualized using a fluorescence microscope.

Flow cytometry-based cell apoptosis analysis

Apoptosis was evaluated using an Annexin V–allophycocyanin (APC)/propidium iodide (PI) dual-staining kit (Procell, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, HepG2 cells were harvested by trypsinization, washed, pelleted by centrifugation, and resuspended to obtain a single-cell suspension. An aliquot of 100 µL suspension was transferred to a flow cytometry tube and mixed with 500 µL binding buffer containing 5 µL Annexin V–APC and 5 µL PI. The mixture was incubated for 15 min at room temperature, protected from light. Samples were analyzed on a BriCyte E6 flow cytometer (Mindray, China). All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the apoptosis index was calculated as the percentage of Annexin V–positive cells (Annexin V+/PI– plus Annexin V+/PI+) relative to the total cell population.

Transmission electron microscopy

Cells were initially fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Sigma) for 2 h at room temperature, followed by washing with 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4; Solarbio). Post-fixation was carried out using 1% osmium tetroxide (Sigma) for 1.5 h. After a graded ethanol dehydration series, samples were infiltrated with butanone-acetate (Sigma), embedded, and sectioned. Ultrathin sections were sequentially stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate (Sigma). Mitochondrial ultrastructures were visualized using a JEOL-1230 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan).

Animals and treatment

A total of 28 male C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old) were obtained from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and acclimated for one week under standard laboratory conditions (23 ± 2 °C, 12-hour light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. Mice were then randomly assigned to three groups: a normal chow diet group (NCD, n = 14), a high-fat diet group (HFD, n = 7), and a methionine- and choline-deficient diet group (MCD, n = 7). The HFD provided 60% of calories from fat (Xiao Shu You Tai, Beijing, China), while the MCD group received a diet lacking methionine and choline (Xiao Shu You Tai, Beijing, China). The NCD group was fed a standard laboratory chow. After 8 weeks of MCD feeding, all MCD mice and 7 mice from the NCD group were sacrificed via cervical dislocation under 2% isoflurane anesthesia for liver and serum collection. The remaining NCD and HFD mice were euthanized after 20 weeks using the same protocol. Serum levels of total cholesterol (TC) and triglycerides (TG) were measured using commercial assay kits (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.), while aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels were determined using kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). Liver tissues were collected for further analysis. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Harbin Medical University.

Histological analyses

Liver tissues were rapidly embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound (Sakura Finetek, Torrance, CA, USA), snap-frozen, and sectioned at a thickness of 10 μm using a cryostat (Leica). Frozen sections were stained with Oil Red O for 15 min, followed by a brief rinse with 60% isopropanol. Lipid droplets were subsequently visualized under a light microscope. The remaining liver samples were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned at 4 μm using a microtome (Leica) and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Solarbio) for general histological evaluation, and Masson’s trichrome staining (Solarbio) to assess collagen fiber deposition and hepatic fibrosis.

Immunohistochemistry analysis

Immunohistochemistry was performed on paraffin-embedded liver sections following standard protocols. The tissue sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber with primary antibodies, including anti-rabbit COX6A1 (PS03703, Abmart) and anti-rabbit NDUFA4 (PA1787, Abmart). To ensure objectivity and minimize bias, a blinded assessment was conducted, and three representative fields were randomly selected from each mouse specimen for observation and analysis.

Multiple immunofluorescence (mIHC) staining

Multiplex immunohistochemistry was performed using the AlphaTSA Multiplex IHC Kit (AXT37100031, Alphaxbio) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Paraffin-embedded liver Sect. (4 μm) from control, HFD, and MCD mice were sequentially deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and subjected to antigen retrieval and blocking. Sections were then incubated sequentially with primary and secondary antibodies, followed by fluorescent staining. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min, and coverslips were mounted using an antifade mounting medium. Image acquisition was conducted using the Pannoramic MIDI II scanner, and image processing was performed with SlideViewer software (version 2.6). The primary antibodies used included anti-rabbit COX6A1 (PS03703, Abmart), anti-rabbit NDUFA4 (PA1787, Abmart), and anti-mouse ONECUT1 (MG520196, Abmart).

Results

Expression patterns and co-expression modules of palmitoylation-related genes in MASLD

In the discovery cohort GSE213621, transcriptomic profiles of 368 individuals—including 69 healthy controls and 299 patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD)—were analyzed. A total of 371 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified (Supplementary Table S5), comprising 249 upregulated and 122 downregulated genes (Fig. 2A). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses revealed that these DEGs were primarily involved in energy metabolism, lipid biosynthesis, and glucose metabolic processes (Supplementary Table S6 and Table S7). Notably, significant enrichment was observed in pathways such as ECM–receptor interaction, focal adhesion, and TGF-β signaling, indicating roles in extracellular matrix remodeling and fibrosis progression. Several DEGs were also associated with cellular stress responses, inflammation resolution, and cell cycle regulation, highlighting the multifaceted molecular pathology of MASLD (Fig. 2B, C). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further demonstrated that MASLD samples were significantly enriched in pathways related to interferon-γ response, Myc target genes, and protein secretion, suggesting potential immune activation and metabolic reprogramming. Upregulation of peroxisome and oxidative phosphorylation pathways may reflect compensatory responses to lipid metabolic imbalance and mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Table S8).To validate the robustness of these findings, differential expression analysis was repeated in an independent cohort (GSE130970), with results visualized in Supplementary Figs. 1 A and 1 F. GO enrichment analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1C) indicated that DEGs were enriched in chemokine activity, immune cell migration, and membrane structure maintenance. KEGG analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1D) highlighted enrichment in cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, chemokine signaling, and PPAR signaling pathways. GSEA (Supplementary Fig. 1E) revealed sustained upregulation of oxidative phosphorylation, lipogenesis, and PI3K–Akt–mTOR, and TGF-β signaling pathways in MASLD samples, consistent with observations from the primary cohort. Overall, MASLD exhibits hallmark features of inflammatory activation, secretory stress, and metabolic reprogramming. To further investigate the expression patterns of palmitoylation-related genes (PRGs) in MASLD, we extracted PRGs with known human expression profiles from the bulk RNA-seq dataset. The results revealed significant upregulation of DGAT1, DGAT2, PNPLA3, PNPLA4, MOGAT2, PLIN4, LPL, CD36, and ZDHHC5 in MASLD samples, whereas CPT1A, CPT1B, CPT2, ACOX1, HADHB, and MTTP were significantly downregulated (Fig. 2E, G). Other genes, including LIPE, LIPA, and PLSCR1, showed no significant expression differences between groups. Co-expression analysis demonstrated strong positive correlations among CPT1A, CPT1B, and CPT2, suggesting their involvement in a coordinated metabolic module. Similar co-expression patterns were observed between DGAT1 and DGAT2, and between PNPLA3 and PNPLA4. In contrast, CD36 exhibited strong negative correlations with CPT1B, HADHB, and other mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation genes, indicating potential antagonistic regulatory relationships. Collectively, PRGs formed distinct positively correlated clusters that interacted with negatively correlated gene groups, implying cooperative or opposing roles in MASLD pathogenesis (Fig. 2G).

Molecular and functional heterogeneity of palmitoylation-based subtypes in MASLD (Cluster 1 vs. Cluster 2)

To investigate the heterogeneity of MASLD, consensus clustering analysis was performed based on the expression profiles of palmitoylation-related genes (PRGs). The optimal number of clusters was determined to be k = 2, as indicated by the consensus cumulative distribution function (CDF) and delta area plot, reflecting optimal stability and clear separation between groups (Fig. 3A–C). Accordingly, MASLD samples were stratified into two molecular subtypes: Cluster 1 and Cluster 2. Differential expression analysis between the two clusters identified 255 upregulated and 154 downregulated genes in Cluster 1 relative to Cluster 2 (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Table S9). GO enrichment analysis revealed that these differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were mainly associated with synaptic regulation, cell adhesion, and calcium ion homeostasis (Fig. 3G). GSEA further demonstrated that Cluster 1 was enriched in metabolic pathways such as androgen response and cholesterol homeostasis, while Cluster 2 exhibited significant enrichment in KRAS signaling, Wnt/β-catenin signaling, and mitotic processes (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Table S10), indicating distinct differences in signal transduction and cell cycle regulation between the subtypes. Expression pattern analysis showed that more than two-thirds of PRGs were differentially expressed between the clusters. Specifically, genes such as PNPLA4, DGAT2, MGLL, LIPE, ATP8B1, CETP, PNPLA3, LIPG, LIPA, CPT1A, APOE, CD36, CPT2, ACOX1, MOGAT2, PLIN4, LPL, PNPLA2, and DGAT1 exhibited highly significant differences (p < 0.001), while LYPLA1, SLC27A1, MPP1, PPT1, HADHB, GPAT3, MTTP, LPCAT1, and ZDHHC5 showed moderate differences (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3E). In contrast, PRGs such as PLB1, LIPC, PLSCR1, and PPT2 showed no statistically significant differences. These findings underscore a widespread divergence in PRG expression between the two subtypes, reflecting potential heterogeneity in palmitoylation regulation. To further characterize the functional features of each subtype, GSEA was conducted independently for each cluster, and the top 20 significantly enriched pathways were visualized (Fig. 3H, I). In the GO biological process category, Cluster 1 was enriched in pathways related to histone modification, ion channel activity, neurotransmitter transport, and chromatin remodeling, suggesting a transcriptionally active and signal-processing-oriented phenotype. Conversely, Cluster 2 was enriched in antigen processing and presentation, MHC class II complex assembly, innate immune responses, and mitochondrial protein synthesis, indicating enhanced immune activity and mitochondrial metabolism.KEGG pathway analysis supported these findings: Cluster 1 showed enrichment in circadian rhythm, ABC transporters, the phosphatidylinositol signaling system, spliceosome, and purine metabolism, highlighting a role in metabolic regulation and intracellular transport. In contrast, Cluster 2 was significantly enriched in Toll-like receptor signaling, chemokine signaling, natural killer (NK) cell activation, and autoimmune disease-related pathways, reflecting a pronounced immuno-inflammatory profile. Collectively, Cluster 1 is characterized by metabolic regulation and signal transduction, whereas Cluster 2 exhibits a predominantly immune-driven phenotype. These results suggest that the two MASLD subtypes engage distinct biological programs within the palmitoylation regulatory network.

Hub genes associated with different subtypes of MASLD

In this study, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was first applied to calculate palmitoylation-related gene set (PRG) scores for each MASLD sample within the bulk transcriptomic dataset. These ssGSEA-derived scores served as phenotypic traits for subsequent weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), aimed at identifying gene modules significantly associated with PRG activity. The constructed WGCNA network yielded 24 distinct co-expression modules, each annotated by a unique color (Fig. 4B). Correlation analysis between module eigengenes and PRG scores identified the yellow module (MEyellow) as most strongly associated (Pearson correlation coefficient = 0.65; Fig. 4C).To further prioritize candidate genes with potential regulatory roles, the 511 genes within MEyellow were intersected with two sets of differentially expressed genes (DEGs): (1) DEGs between MASLD and normal samples; and (2) DEGs between Cluster 1 and Cluster 2. This intersection identified 13 candidate hub genes showing both differential expression and strong module membership. To refine biomarker selection among these candidates, a comprehensive panel of machine learning models was constructed and evaluated. Using GSE213621 as the training cohort, 10-fold cross-validation was employed to assess 113 model combinations, followed by external validation in three independent datasets (GSE135251, GSE130970, and GSE126848). The model combining Stepwise Generalized Linear Modeling (Stepwise GLM) with Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) demonstrated robust and consistent performance across cohorts, achieving an average AUC of approximately 0.77 (Fig. 4A). Further performance evaluation revealed high sensitivity for MASLD identification (ranging from 79.61% to 97.66%), although specificity varied, with lower values observed in some datasets (e.g., 10.0% in GSE135251). Nonetheless, the model attained an accuracy of 89.13% in the training set and F1-scores exceeding 78% across all validation cohorts, underscoring its discriminative power (Fig. 4G). Feature importance analysis identified COX6A1, COX7A2, and NDUFA4 as key hub genes. ROC curve analysis further supported their diagnostic potential, with AUC values exceeding 0.75 in the GSE213621 dataset (Fig. 4D). A logistic regression model incorporating these three genes was developed for individualized risk prediction and visualized as a nomogram (Fig. 4E), which predicted an example case with a risk probability of 83.2%. Additionally, decision curve analysis (DCA) demonstrated that the model conferred a greater net clinical benefit compared to “treat-all” or “treat-none” strategies across a threshold probability range of 0.1 to 0.75 (Fig. 4F), suggesting its potential utility in clinical decision-making.

Immune infiltration characteristics of the MASLD subtypes

To elucidate the immune microenvironmental differences between MASLD subtypes defined by palmitoylation-related gene (PRG) expression, we conducted a comprehensive immune-related analysis comparing Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 (Fig. 5A–D). Immune cell infiltration profiling revealed that Cluster 2 was notably enriched with various immune cell populations, including multiple subsets of T cells (CD4⁺/CD8⁺ naive, memory, and effector), B cells (naive, memory, and plasma), natural killer (NK) cells, dendritic cells, mast cells, and monocytes. Cluster 2 also exhibited a particularly high proportion of M0 and M2 macrophages, which are indicative of an enhanced immune response and inflammation-modulating activity (Fig. 5A). Further immune function scoring showed significantly elevated scores in Cluster 2 across several pathways, including CCR signaling, T cell co-stimulation, HLA expression, para-inflammation, and tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) abundance, suggesting a globally activated immune state (Fig. 5B). Regarding immunoregulatory molecules, key factors such as CD274 (PD-L1), CD276 (B7-H3), TGFB1, and CXCL10 were significantly upregulated in Cluster 2. These molecules are known to participate in immune checkpoint modulation, TGF-β-mediated immunosuppression, and chemotactic signaling, highlighting the complexity of immune activation and regulation within this subtype (Fig. 5C). In contrast, Cluster 1 displayed generally lower expression of immune-related genes, suggesting a relatively “immune-silent” phenotype. Correlation analysis further revealed that the identified hub genes (COX6A1, COX7A2, and NDUFA4) were significantly negatively associated with various immune function scores (Fig. 5D), implying that heightened metabolic activity may be linked to immunosuppressive features. Collectively, these findings underscore the immunological heterogeneity between the two PRG-based MASLD subtypes. While Cluster 2 is characterized by immune activation and inflammatory engagement, Cluster 1 exhibits a metabolically active but immunologically quiescent profile, suggesting distinct metabolic-immune coupling patterns across MASLD subtypes.

Validation of hub genes at the single-cell level

Given the pivotal roles of hepatic cell types—including parenchymal cells, endothelial cells, and immune cells—in the pathogenesis and fibrotic progression of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), we analyzed the single-cell RNA sequencing dataset GSE136103 to delineate cell subtype-specific transcriptomic features within liver tissue. Following standard normalization and processing using the Seurat workflow, a total of 11 distinct cell types were identified (Fig. 6A), including macrophages, monocytes, plasma cells, endothelial cells, NKT cells, epithelial cells, hepatocytes, B cells, T cells, smooth muscle cells (SMCs), and dendritic cells. Figure 6B illustrates the top five representative marker genes and the proportion of positively stained cells within each cell type. Furthermore, UMAP visualization (Fig. 6C) reveals the spatial distribution of canonical marker genes [41] such as EPCAM, DLGAP5, TAGLN, CALD1, and CD3D/E, which align well with their respective cell clusters. To investigate the potential functional roles of each cell population in MASLD and cirrhosis, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (Fig. 6D). The results highlight pronounced functional heterogeneity among the cell types. Specifically, macrophages were enriched in pathways associated with MHC class II receptor activity, antigen binding, and immunoglobulin receptor binding, suggesting critical roles in immune regulation and inflammatory response. Monocytes were enriched in cytokine binding and immune response modulation pathways, underscoring their involvement in shaping the hepatic immune microenvironment. Epithelial cells showed significant enrichment in cell–cell adhesion and enzyme inhibitor activity, indicating a role in barrier function and tissue repair. Endothelial cells were enriched in chemokine receptor binding and immune receptor activity, potentially contributing to angiogenesis and immune cell trafficking. Hepatocytes were associated with immune receptor activity and MHC-II-mediated antigen presentation, suggesting a broader role beyond metabolism. We further examined the expression patterns of the key genes NDUFA4, COX6A1, and COX7A2 across different liver cell types. As shown in Fig. 6E, these genes were highly expressed in hepatocytes, macrophages, and endothelial cells, and their expression levels were significantly upregulated in MASLD patient samples (Fig. 6F). Statistical analysis confirmed that all three genes were significantly elevated in the disease group compared with controls, implicating their potential involvement in MASLD progression.

Expression characteristics of hub genes in hepatocyte subpopulations and their functional validation in MASLD progression

Given the markedly elevated expression of the identified hub genes in hepatocytes, we further investigated their potential roles in MASLD progression by performing dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis within the hepatocyte population. Since both COX6A1 and COX7A2 encode subunits of the cytochrome c oxidase (COX) complex, we focused on the expression of COX6A1 and NDUFA4. The results confirmed that NDUFA4, COX6A1, and ONECUT1 were all markedly upregulated in the livers of both MCD and HFD mice (Fig. 7E–H), supporting a functionally activated role of these three hub genes in the pathogenesis and progression of MASLD.

Validation of optimal hub genes in mouse and cell models

To evaluate the potential diagnostic value of hub genes in MASLD, we established MCD and HFD mouse models to simulate different stages of MASLD (MASL and MASH). After treatment with a high-fat diet (HFD) for 20 weeks and a methionine-choline-deficient diet (MCD) for 8 weeks, we assessed liver tissue morphological changes through H&E, Oil Red O, and Masson staining. The results revealed significant hepatic steatosis and focal inflammation in the HFD group, while the MCD group exhibited more severe liver damage, including lipid accumulation, cellular structure disruption, and prominent fibrosis (Fig. 9A). Body weight and liver weight analyses showed a significant increase in body weight and liver weight in the HFD group, suggesting that a high-fat diet induces obesity and fatty liver formation (Supplementary Figs. 2G, H). In contrast, the MCD group showed limited weight gain, with a decrease in both body weight and liver weight in later stages (Supplementary Figs. 2 A, B), reflecting more extensive liver damage. Serum biochemical analysis indicated a significant increase in ALT and AST levels in both models (Supplementary Figs. 2E, F, K, L), suggesting liver function impairment; meanwhile, total cholesterol (TC) and triglycerides (TG) levels were elevated (Supplementary Figs. 2I, J), but the MCD group exhibited lower levels compared to the HFD group (Supplementary Figs. 2 C, D), indicating lipid metabolism abnormalities in both models, with HFD being more prone to causing hyperlipidemia. Further examination through RT-qPCR (Figs. 9D, E), immunohistochemistry (Fig. 9F), and Western blot (Figs. 9B, C) revealed a significant upregulation of NDUFA4 and COX6A1 expression in liver tissues of both the HFD and MCD groups, consistent with the results from the previous bioinformatics analysis and validation set GSE130970 (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Additionally, in vitro experiments were conducted in HepG2 cells using palmitic acid (PA) to induce lipid accumulation. After 48 h of treatment, Western blot (Fig. 9G) and RT-qPCR (Fig. 9H) results demonstrated a marked increase in NDUFA4 and COX6A1 expression, further supporting their upregulation during hepatic steatosis.

The role of COX6A1 in hepatocyte lipid metabolism and disease progression

Cytochrome c oxidase (COX), the terminal enzyme of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV (RCC IV), is a critical transmembrane complex that maintains hepatic homeostasis. Previous studies have demonstrated that hepatocytes deficient in COX are more susceptible to apoptosis under stress conditions [42]. Similarly, mice lacking the nuclear-encoded subunit COX6A1 exhibit pronounced hepatic dysfunction, underscoring its essential role in liver physiology [43]. To investigate the mechanistic involvement of this hub gene, COX6A1 was silenced in HepG2 cells using siRNA, achieving significant knockdown efficiency (Fig. 10A, B). Under palmitic acid (PA) treatment, Oil Red O and Nile Red staining revealed a substantial reduction in both the number and size of lipid droplets in COX6A1-depleted cells (Fig. 10C), indicating a pivotal role in lipid metabolism. Pseudotime analysis of hepatocytes delineated a trajectory from healthy to diseased states, accompanied by distinct transcriptional transitions (Fig. 10D). COX6A1 expression increased sharply along the disease branch (Fig. 10F), implicating it in cell-state progression. CytoTRACE analysis suggested that the majority of hepatocytes occupied a late-differentiation status (Fig. 10G). A heatmap of dynamic gene expression along the trajectory highlighted Cluster 3 genes—enriched for p53 signaling and programmed cell death—as tightly associated with disease development (Fig. 10E). Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses pointed toward p53-dependent apoptotic signaling, suggesting that COX6A1, as a key component of mitochondrial respiration, may influence p53-mediated cell fate decisions by modulating cellular energy homeostasis. In our system, COX6A1 appears to promote lipotoxic, p53-dependent apoptosis; accordingly, disruption of COX6A1 acts cytoprotectively by attenuating, not activating, p53 signaling. Western blot analysis supported this hypothesis: PA-treated control HepG2 cells showed a robust increase in p53 protein levels, whereas COX6A1 knockdown significantly suppressed PA-induced p53 upregulation (Fig. 10H). These findings suggest that COX6A1 silencing attenuates fatty-acid-induced p53 activation, likely through alterations in mitochondrial metabolism, thereby reducing apoptosis under lipotoxic conditions.

COX6A1 knockdown alleviates PA-Induced mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis

To evaluate apoptosis under different treatment conditions, flow cytometric analysis was conducted to compare apoptotic levels among the si-NC, si-COX6A1-2, and si-COX6A1-3 groups following palmitic acid (PA) induction. The results demonstrated that PA significantly induced apoptosis in HepG2 cells, whereas COX6A1 knockdown markedly attenuated this pro-apoptotic effect (Fig. 11A). Although COX6A1—a key subunit of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV—is generally considered essential for maintaining mitochondrial integrity, with its deficiency typically associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and cellular stress [44], our fatty acid-induced model revealed a paradoxical cytoprotective effect following COX6A1 silencing. We hypothesize that this protective outcome may result from COX6A1 knockdown-induced metabolic reprogramming, which reduces mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and suppresses activation of the p53 signaling pathway, thereby mitigating PA-induced oxidative stress and downstream apoptotic cascades. To test this hypothesis, we assessed mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) using JC-1 staining. PA treatment caused a notable decrease in the red/green fluorescence ratio, indicating ΔΨm depolarization, whereas COX6A1 knockdown significantly restored membrane potential integrity (Fig. 11B). In addition, mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) levels, measured using MitoSOX Red, were markedly increased by PA and significantly suppressed by COX6A1 silencing (Fig. 11C). Transmission electron microscopy further confirmed that COX6A1 knockdown alleviated PA-induced mitochondrial swelling and structural disruption, supporting its role in preserving mitochondrial homeostasis (Fig. 11D). Building on these observations, we analyzed the expression of key proteins involved in the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway, including BCL2, BAX, Caspase-9, and Caspase-3. Western blot analysis revealed that PA exposure upregulated pro-apoptotic proteins (BAX, Caspase-9, and Caspase-3) and downregulated the anti-apoptotic protein BCL2. Conversely, COX6A1 knockdown reversed these effects, decreasing BAX, Caspase-9, and Caspase-3 expression while increasing BCL2 levels (Fig. 11E). Collectively, these findings indicate that COX6A1 depletion mitigates PA-induced hepatocellular injury by suppressing mitochondrial apoptosis, likely through reduced ROS generation and preservation of mitochondrial function.

Discussion

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a prevalent metabolic disorder with a steadily increasing global incidence [45–47] and is closely linked to heightened risks of obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease [48–50]. Despite extensive research, the underlying mechanisms of MASLD pathogenesis remain incompletely understood. Palmitoylation, a reversible post-translational modification, plays a critical role in regulating protein trafficking, localization, stability, and interactions—processes fundamental to cellular homeostasis and signaling [14, 51, 52]. In the discovery cohort, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) mapped predominantly to energy and lipid metabolic programs (e.g., oxidative phosphorylation and peroxisome) alongside extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling and TGF-β/focal-adhesion signaling, while GSEA highlighted interferon-γ response, Myc targets, and secretory stress; these signals were recapitulated in the validation cohort with enrichment of cytokine–cytokine receptor and chemokine pathways together with PPAR signaling. This convergence of metabolic and inflammatory pathways is consistent with contemporary models of MASLD pathogenesis that place mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid handling, and innate immune activation at the core of disease biology [53–57].

In this study, we combined bioinformatics analysis with experimental validation to systematically explore palmitoylation-associated molecular subtypes in MASLD. Using integrated transcriptomic datasets, we identified two distinct MASLD subtypes defined by divergent expression patterns of palmitoylation-related genes (PRGs), and further pinpointed hub genes with potential regulatory significance. These findings were validated in multiple external datasets and experimental models. Additionally, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was used to relate hub-gene expression to immune-cell infiltration, providing cell-type–resolved mechanistic clues. Notably, enrichment patterns aligned with subtype biology: Cluster 1 showed stronger signatures of oxidative phosphorylation, cholesterol homeostasis, circadian/transport pathways, and androgen response, whereas Cluster 2 was characterized by Toll-like receptor/NF-κB axes, chemokine signaling, antigen presentation, and cell-cycle programs—an immune-activated, proliferative phenotype. These data suggest that palmitoylation-linked variation helps partition MASLD into metabolically biased versus immune-inflammatory states with potential therapeutic implications [56–58].

COX6A1 (cytochrome c oxidase subunit 6A1) is a nuclear-encoded component of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase, RCC IV) and plays a crucial role in sustaining oxidative phosphorylation and maintaining metabolic homeostasis. As the terminal enzyme of the electron transport chain, dysfunction of RCC IV has been widely implicated in the pathogenesis of various metabolic diseases and the initiation of apoptosis [42, 59, 60]. Previous studies have shown that COX6A1 is highly expressed in metabolically active tissues such as the liver, nervous system, and skeletal muscle. Loss or dysfunction of COX6A1 impairs mitochondrial respiratory efficiency, increases susceptibility to oxidative stress, and promotes programmed cell death and tissue damage [44]. Building on these foundations, our multi-level analyses highlight a pivotal role for COX6A1 in MASLD progression. We found that COX6A1 expression is significantly elevated in hepatocytes from MASLD patients and is predominantly enriched in the metabolically active yet immunologically “silent” Cluster 1 subtype, suggesting that COX6A1 may participate in disease pathogenesis through metabolism–immunity coupling. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis coupled with ligand–receptor inference revealed that COX6A1⁺ hepatocytes exhibit markedly enhanced interactions with macrophages, B cells, and T cells in the disease state. These interactions are primarily mediated through inflammation-associated signaling pathways, including MIF and TNF axes, indicating that COX6A1 may exert dual regulatory roles in modulating both immune activation and inflammatory signaling in MASLD [61, 62]. Given that mitochondrial stress can release danger signals and mitochondrial DNA that engage cGAS–STING and downstream NF-κB pathways, the oxidative-phosphorylation bias associated with COX6A1 provides a plausible link between hepatocellular redox imbalance and the immune-pathway activation observed in Cluster 2 [63].

Furthermore, in an in vitro model of steatosis, silencing COX6A1 significantly attenuated palmitic acid (PA)-induced lipid accumulation, mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) depolarization, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. These alterations collectively suppressed activation of the p53-mediated apoptotic pathway. Although COX6A1, as an essential subunit of the mitochondrial respiratory chain complex, is conventionally associated with maintaining cellular energy metabolism, its overexpression under MASLD-related pathological conditions may paradoxically exacerbate metabolic stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. The cytoprotective changes observed after COX6A1 knockdown—lower oxidative stress and reduced apoptosis—support a model in which dampened respiratory activity can mitigate lipotoxic injury and favor cell survival [59]. Comparable phenomena of metabolic “functional reprogramming” have been described in other metabolic disorders [64, 65], potentially reflecting adaptive cellular strategies to counteract pathological metabolic environments [66].

Notably, to our knowledge, this study delineates a distinct palmitoylation regulatory landscape in MASLD. Subclassification based on PRG expression profiles identified two molecularly distinct clusters. Cluster 2 displayed immunologically activated features, suggesting that palmitoylation may contribute to immune cell recruitment and activation by modulating the localization and stability of key signaling proteins and membrane-associated molecules during immune dysregulation. In contrast, Cluster 1 was defined by heightened metabolic activity and immune quiescence, revealing the existence of pathophysiologically divergent MASLD subtypes with potentially distinct mechanistic underpinnings and therapeutic susceptibilities. The elevated expression of COX6A1 in Cluster 1 further underscores its critical role in maintaining metabolic homeostasis under stress conditions [67].

In summary, this study integrates multi-omics analysis, cross-cohort validation, and mechanistic experiments to identify COX6A1 as a central regulator of metabolic dysfunction in MASLD and a plausible molecular bridge between metabolism and immune regulation. Concordant signals across bulk and single-cell datasets, together with in-vitro lipotoxicity assays and in-vivo HFD/MCD models, converge on a mitochondrial–immune axis in which COX6A1 heightens redox stress, perturbs lipid homeostasis, and shapes immune communication [59, 61]. These data position COX6A1 as a promising, subtype-relevant target and suggest that therapeutic strategies aimed at palmitoylation dynamics and mitochondrial function may offer translational value in MASLD [52, 67, 68].

Several considerations merit note. First, the use of public transcriptomic cohorts and a modest validation set can introduce heterogeneity and batch effects; we applied uniform preprocessing and cross-dataset replication to mitigate these issues, but prospective, multi-center studies are needed to confirm robustness. Second, although single-cell analyses provided high-resolution views of cellular states and interactions, the available datasets did not fully span the MASLD spectrum, limiting temporal inferences. Third, in vitro tests were performed in HepG2 cells, which may not fully recapitulate primary hepatocyte biology. Finally, while we focused on COX6A1, the broader palmitoylation regulatory network warrants systematic interrogation [52, 67]. Collectively, our findings outline a coherent framework linking palmitoylation to mitochondrial and immune dysregulation in MASLD and define practical next steps toward clinical translation.

Conclusion

This study identifies COX6A1 as a crucial regulator in the progression of Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD) and proposes it as a potential therapeutic target. Through comprehensive multi-omics analysis, we highlight the essential role of palmitoylation-related genes (PRGs) in modulating the metabolic and immune landscapes of MASLD. Two distinct molecular subtypes, Cluster 1 and Cluster 2, were delineated, with Cluster 1 primarily involved in metabolic regulation and Cluster 2 exhibiting heightened immune activation. Noteworthy hub genes, such as COX6A1, COX7A2, and NDUFA4, demonstrated significant diagnostic potential and impacted immune cell infiltration and mitochondrial dysfunction. Notably, COX6A1 knockdown in vitro resulted in reduced lipid accumulation, improved mitochondrial function, and decreased apoptosis, indicating its therapeutic potential.
