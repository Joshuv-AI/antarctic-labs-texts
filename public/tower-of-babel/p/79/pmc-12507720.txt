Viewpoint: The Use of Antimycobacterial (Anti‐MAP) Therapies in the Treatment of Active Luminal Crohn's Disease
Authors: Paul Pavli, Peter R Gibson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis (MAP) as the infectious cause of Crohn's disease and the use of antimycobacterial (anti‐MAP) therapies in its treatment remain topics of controversy. A major limitation accepting this view is that irrefutable evidence that MAP causes Crohn's disease—the demonstration that long‐term remission follows clearance of the infection with antibiotics—has been lacking. While several centers continue to advocate their use, most authoritative guidelines do not recommend anti‐MAP regimens. This viewpoint aims to evaluate current data, in particular, the results of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of anti‐MAP therapy published in 2007 and in 2024. We conclude that, similar to several other antibiotic regimens, the benefit of anti‐MAP therapy in active Crohn's Disease (CD) is likely to be modest. Continuing treatment is unlikely to be of benefit if there is no significant objective improvement in disease activity by 16 weeks. Any beneficial effect of anti‐MAP therapy is not maintained after its discontinuation. There is still no convincing evidence that the causative microorganism of Crohn's disease is Mycobacterium avium ssp. paratuberculosis in significant numbers of patients.

Introduction

Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis (MAP) as the infectious cause of Crohn's disease and the use of antimycobacterial (anti‐MAP) therapies in its treatment remain topics of controversy. A major limitation accepting this view is that irrefutable evidence that MAP causes Crohn's disease—the demonstration that long‐term remission follows clearance of the infection with antibiotics [1]—has been lacking. While several centers continue to advocate their use based on four claims (Table 1), most authoritative guidelines do not recommend anti‐MAP regimens. This viewpoint aims to evaluate current data, in particular, the results of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of anti‐MAP therapy.

Results of Randomized Controlled Trials

Case reports or case series of patients with Crohn's disease describing responses to standard antimycobacterial therapy [2, 3, 4, 5] have to be matched to three fully published negative RCTs involving 188 patients [6, 7, 8, 9]. Antimycobacterial regimens that have specific activity towards MAP, utilizing clarithromycin, rifabutin, and clofazimine, alone or in different combinations, were reported to have clinically impressive short and long‐term results in 145 adult and pediatric patients for intestinal inflammation, clinical disease activity, strictures, and perianal disease, although some patients had other therapies concomitantly [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Such data warrant RCTs in order to minimize the potential influence of selection bias, non‐protocolized assessment, and other problems associated with retrospective analyses. There are now two randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled clinical trials of dedicated anti‐MAP treatment involving 534 patients with active intestinal Crohn's disease. The results of the two trials are, in fact, remarkably similar, although the interpretations differ.

The Australian APT (Anti‐Paratuberculosis Trial) Trial [18]

Selby et al. (2007) undertook a prospective, parallel, placebo‐controlled, double‐blind, randomized trial of clarithromycin (750 mg/d), rifabutin (450 mg/d), and clofazimine (50 mg/d) in active Crohn's disease. The study design—2 years' therapy of triple antibiotics followed by a further year of follow‐up on no therapy—addressed the first three claims (Table 1). The characteristics of MAP and evidence from published studies of antibiotic treatment of Crohn's disease determined the choice, dose, and duration of the antibiotics used. This trial did not assess mycobacterial status (Claim 4). The focus of the study was the maintenance of established remission on anti‐MAP therapy, and the co‐primary end points were the proportion of patients experiencing at least one clinical relapse at 12, 24, and 36 months.

Enrolment was between September 1999 and September 2001 before the availability of anti‐tumor necrosis factor (TNF) antibodies in Australia. Two hundred thirteen patients were randomized to anti‐MAP therapy or placebo, in addition to a 16‐week tapering course of prednisolone. Those in remission at week 16 continued their study medications in the maintenance phase of the trial.

The combination of anti‐MAP therapy with steroids for induction of remission was associated with better outcomes than the use of steroids alone. The absolute difference was 16% (66% vs. 50%, p = 0.02) at week 16 (Figure 1, Table 2). The maximum benefit was achieved in the first 16 weeks of treatment and remission rates steadily declined in both groups over the subsequent 18 months of active treatment. There was no evidence that prolongation of therapy resulted in a “late” response (Claim 2).

Finally, 12 months after stopping 2 years of continuous anti‐MAP therapy, there was no difference in remission between those who had received anti‐MAP therapy (14%) and those who had been administered placebo (9%). In other words, there was no sustained benefit or likely “cure” (Claim 3).

The Multi‐National multinational anti‐ Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis Trial (MAPUS) Trial [19]

A second double‐blind, randomized, placebo‐controlled clinical trial (RCT) examining dedicated antimycobacterial treatment (NCT01951326) was conducted between 2013 and 2018, and published in 2024.

This international study from 10 countries recruited 331 subjects who were randomized to either a fixed‐dose combination capsule (RHB‐104) (total daily dose building up to clarithromycin 950 mg, rifabutin 450 mg, and clofazimine 100 mg) or placebo. This antibiotic combination is identical to, and the doses similar to, those used in the APT trial (2007) [clarithromycin 750 mg/day, rifabutin 450 mg/day, clofazimine 50 mg/day]. Subjects were stratified according to the use of anti‐TNF agents.

Again, there was some initial improvement reported using anti‐MAP therapy: clinical remission at week 26 (the primary endpoint) was 61/166 (37%) in the treatment group and 37/165 (22%) in the placebo group (p = 0.005) (Table 2). The difference of 15% is very similar to the 16% at week 16 seen in the anti‐MAP trial. Remission at week 52 was 47/166 (28%) and 32/165 (19%) in the treatment and placebo groups, respectively (p = 0.06). In the APT trial, the remission rates at week 52 were 40% and 22% for the anti‐MAP therapy and placebo, respectively. The clinical outcomes following cessation of anti‐MAP therapy were not reported in the multinational anti‐ Mycobacterium avium subspecies paratuberculosis trial (MAPUS) trial (Claim 3 was not tested).

An analysis of the NCT01951326 (MAPUS) trial results indicated that the rates of remission were best at week 16 (70/166, 42%) compared with those at week 26 (61/166, 37%) and week 52 (47/166, 28%), indicating that those who may have benefited would have done so in the first 16 weeks of treatment. Those who had not responded by week 16 were unlikely to do so if the therapy was continued to 26 weeks (Claim 2). Participants not in remission at 26 weeks were withdrawn and entered into an open‐label study.

An analysis of maintenance of remission at week 52 alone, ascribing failure to treatment withdrawals, demonstrated that, of those in remission at week 16, 43/70 (61%) on RHB‐104 versus 20/48 (42%) on placebo maintained remission at week 52 (p = 0.036). However, of those in remission at week 26, the benefit of ongoing therapy diminishes: by week 52, 40/61 (66%) of RHB‐104‐treated patients maintained remission while 24/37 (65%) on placebo remained in remission (p = 0.95). Viewing these data from a different perspective, 29 of 70 patients (41%) who were in remission at 16 weeks lost response over the next 36 weeks, which was more common than 26% in the Australian trial.

Further insight into outcomes of patients who did not reach clinical remission at week 16 or 26 derives from the results of an unpublished sister study in the RHB‐104 development program [NCT03009396 Open Label Efficacy and Safety of Anti‐MAP ( Mycobacterium avium ssp. paratuberculosis) Therapy in Adult Crohn's Disease]. The results available online (ClinicalTrials.gov) indicate that those who were not in remission after 26 weeks of active RHB‐104 in the initial blinded study and who continued open‐label anti‐MAP therapy subsequently had a 19% (3/16) chance of being in remission after a further 16 weeks of anti‐MAP therapy and a 6% (1/16) chance of being in remission from Week 16 through to Week 52 (Table 2). In other words, patients who fail to respond to antimycobacterial agents over the first 26 weeks may respond over the ensuing 36 weeks, but the chance is low.

Predictors of Response/Non‐Response

Both studies examined associations with concomitant immune‐targeted therapy. There was some evidence for a benefit for those on a thiopurine in the APT trial (2007), although this was not sustained beyond 12 months, and remission at 26 weeks was numerically but not statistically significantly greater in those on thiopurines in the MAPUS trial. This possible benefit may relate to the synergistic effect of a thiopurine with macrolides and rifamycin derivatives against MAP [20]. Of perhaps greater contemporary importance is the effect of concomitant anti‐TNF agents on the efficacy of anti‐MAP therapy. This was examined in the MAPUS trial. Patients entering the study with active disease despite anti‐TNF therapy were continued on the anti‐TNF while they received RHB‐104. The remission rate at 26 weeks was 11/31 (36%) on concomitant anti‐TNF, which was similar to 50/135 (37%) in those not on anti‐TNF agents. This contrasts with the generally reduced rates of remission for subsequent therapies when anti‐TNF has failed. However, the role of the anti‐TNF in achieving the antibiotic‐associated remission is uncertain as the anti‐TNF was not withdrawn in any patients. Nevertheless, remission rates at Week 26 favored anti‐MAP therapy over placebo in patients (who entered the trial with active disease) whether or not they were taking anti‐TNF agents, immunomodulators, or corticosteroids (Table 2b, MAPUS study).

Clinical versus Inflammatory Remission

A major limitation of both studies was the objective assessment of active inflammation. In the current era, a major target for anti‐inflammatory therapy is to reduce or heal intestinal inflammation. Active inflammation at recruitment was confirmed in both studies—by colonoscopic findings in all patients in the APT trial (2007) and by an elevated CRP, fecal calprotectin > 250 μg/g, or colonoscopy in the MAPUS trial (2024). As was recommended at the time of the design of both studies, response and remission were defined by the CDAI. The CDAI is largely dictated by symptoms, which are now known to be a poor indicator of intestinal inflammation. For example, in a post hoc analysis of the SONIC trial, in which the efficacy of azathioprine and infliximab, alone or in combination, was evaluated, clinical remission defined by a CDAI ≤ 150 did not correlate well with endoscopic changes of inflammation: the positive predictive value and negative predictive value of a CDAI ≤ 150 to detect mucosal healing were 65% and 53%, respectively [21]. Moreover, in a retrospective analysis, cross‐sectional imaging by intestinal ultrasound in 202 patients with Crohn's disease in clinical remission (Harvey‐Bradshaw Index < 4—equivalent of CDAI < 150) indicated that 6 out of 10 patients had unequivocal features of intestinal inflammation and that this was associated with poorer outcomes over 12 months than in those in sonographic remission [22].

However, there were some objective endpoints applied in the two RCTs. In the APT trial, the proportion of patients who had elevated serum CRP concentrations at therapy initiation was not stated. It was not surprising then that CRP did not differ between the treatment groups since it was likely many had normal values initially and highly sensitive assays that enable accurate quantification within the normal range were not performed. Colonoscopy was performed and scored according to the Crohn's Disease Endoscopic Index of Severity (CDEIS) [23], prior to entry and yearly in those remaining in the study. The baseline CDEIS was not reported, but improvements of CDEIS in the minority who reached the yearly colonoscopy did not differ statistically between the antibiotic and placebo groups. The APT trial predated the application of fecal calprotectin in clinical IBD trials.

In the MAPUS trial, CRP levels were not different between treatment groups. Fecal calprotectin was reported by the proportion of patients with an elevated fecal calprotectin (> 162.9 μg/g, according to the normal values of the local laboratory). The difference in the proportion of patients with continuing high calprotectin was higher in the placebo than in the anti‐MAP group. Colonoscopy was optional and performed at baseline and during therapy. The Simplified Endoscopic Activity Score for Crohn's Disease (SES‐CD), a simplified version of CDEIS [24] was reported. Only 35 patients (14 on active therapy) took up this option, and improvement tended to be greater with the active therapy, but no actual data were shown. Post hoc, the authors identified just over one half of the patients as having ‘more active disease’ on the basis of mostly unconventional cut‐off values CRP (≥ 2.87 mg/L) or calprotectin (> 250 μg/g) or SES‐CD score (≥ 6), but individual patient details were not provided. More achieved clinical remission with ≥ 50% reduction of calprotectin or CRP at weeks 16, 26, and 52, and the proportion with a calprotectin ≤ 162.9 mg/g was less in those receiving antibiotics compared with placebo in this active group (p < 0.08 for all).

In summary, objective biomarkers of active inflammatory disease were not a primary focus of either study, and the results were hard to interpret: there were no convincing signals of improvement in the MAPUS study, and they were presented and analyzed in subgroups defined post hoc using unconventional criteria and without showing individual patient results. This challenges the interpretation of the findings, which should be considered suggestions worthy of further study.

Relationship of Response to the Detection of MAP

The mycobacteria that are thought to be pathogenic in Crohn's disease are cell wall‐deficient (spheroplasts or L forms) and have very slow rates of growth. Unique genomic sequences are limited and generally target a marker that is found specifically in MAP, the insertion sequence 900 (IS900). In the absence of validated tests for the detection of MAP and reproducible techniques for its culture, it is difficult to correlate responses to anti‐MAP therapy with MAP status. Issues related to the fastidiousness of the organism and the challenges in culturing and detecting MAP are reviewed in Dane et al. (2023) [25] and include: for culture—preparation of the sample matrix, technique for decontamination, choice of culture media, and other culture conditions; and for identification—choice of sequence for polymerase chain reaction (PCR).

Only the MAPUS study reported application of a specific, nested PCR assay for detecting a DNA insertion sequence, IS900, unique to MAP from DNA extracted from blood (buffy coat) and, in an unspecified number of patients, colonic tissue at baseline and after 26 and 52 weeks of therapy. The proportions of patients who had positive detection at the three time points were very similar in the anti‐MAP and placebo groups, and did not reduce quantitatively with antibiotic therapy. The authors stated that the assay was inadequate as it did not directly analyze MAP DNA, despite the original description and validation of the assay [26]. Indeed, a comparison of different methodologies to detect MAP failed to show convincing cross‐validation [27]. The lack of any impact of the anti‐MAP therapy on the proportion of patients with detectable MAP DNA was disconcerting and suggested that there were questions about the accuracy of the assay or that anti‐MAP therapy did not achieve its goals of eliminating MAP, or a combination of both. However, specimens have been stored pending the development of a validated MAP diagnostic test.

Risk–Benefit Considerations

The antibiotic regimen seldom led to withdrawal of participants. In the APT trial (2007), the adverse events were not listed, but abnormal liver function tests, vaginal candidiasis, abdominal distension, arthralgia, myalgia, and discoloration of the urine and teeth occurred in 2%–4% of patients receiving anti‐MAP therapy and were reported 3 to > 4 times more frequently than with placebo. The MAPUS study (2024) listed the adverse events that occurred more frequently than in the Australian study for both antibiotics and placebo. Discolored urine was noted in one in 4 (vs. 1.2% placebo), arthralgia in one in ten patients (vs. 4.2%), and nausea leading to discontinuation of therapy in 2.4% (vs. 0.6%) taking antibiotics. The only adverse event more common with placebo was Clostridioides difficile infection that occurred in 8.5% (vs. 2.4% on anti‐MAP). On electrocardiogram, QT prolongation was documented in 66 patients on anti‐MAP therapy (vs 6 on placebo), but in none was this considered clinically significant.

An important consideration for the clinician and patient alike is the risk: benefit ratio. From the results of the RCTs, the symptomatic benefits of prolonged therapy are modest, and the benefits in terms of cure are small. The risks involve symptoms that can impair quality of life, such as arthralgia and nausea, and the risk of discoloration of teeth that some patients may not wish to take.

Other Antibiotics in Crohn's Disease

Studies of the use of other antibiotics in Crohn's disease (Claim 1) show conflicting results, although some show a benefit of the same magnitude as in this trial, as most recently reviewed [28]. This Cochrane Systematic Review, published in 2019, assessed the efficacy and safety of antibiotics for induction and maintenance of remission in Crohn's disease in 13 RCTs using various agents including metronidazole, ciprofloxacin, clarithromycin, cotrimoxazole, rifaximin, and anti‐MAP agents (N = 1303 participants) [29]. All antibiotics were pooled as a class versus placebo, and antibiotics with anti‐TNF agents versus placebo with anti‐TNF agents. When antibiotics as a class were pooled, 55% (289/524) of antibiotic participants failed to achieve remission at 6 to 10 weeks compared with 64% (149/231) of placebo participants (RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.76 to 0.98; 7 studies; high certainty evidence). At 10 to 14 weeks, 41% (174/428) of antibiotic participants failed to achieve a clinical response compared to 49% (93/189) of placebo participants (RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.64 to 0.93; 5 studies; moderate certainty evidence). The effect of individual antibiotics on Crohn's disease was generally uncertain due to imprecision, but it was concluded that there was moderate‐ to high‐quality evidence suggesting that any benefit provided by antibiotics in active disease is likely to be modest and may not be clinically meaningful. In any case, it appears that several antibiotics, either alone or in combination, showed some benefit in luminal Crohn's disease (Claim 1).

Opinion

Although the interpretations differ, the results of the two trials were similar in that the benefits of anti‐MAP therapy in both studies were modest and loss of response was high. The APT trial (2007) additionally showed no evidence for remission of Crohn's disease over the 12 months after cessation of 2 years of anti‐MAP therapy; this outcome was not reported in the MAPUS (2024) study. In the MAPUS trial, ‘late’ response after 26 weeks was observed in a minority of patients (< 20%) after a further 16 weeks' therapy in the sister open‐label study to the MAPUS trial, but this was not examined in the APT trial. The lack of correlation between MAP status at the initiation of therapy and the outcomes of the MAPUS RCT raises questions regarding the general pathogenic role of MAP in Crohn's disease, although concern regarding the specificity of the assays used has been raised. If MAP is important in a subset of patients, detecting this was not factored into the design of the MAPUS trial. This can only be addressed by defining predictors of response and would be best served by stratifying patients according to the detection of MAP prior to instituting therapy. Since MAP status, as applied to the MAPUS trial (2024), did not predict response to anti‐MAP therapy, the notion that MAP is the causative microorganism in Crohn's disease, even in a proportion of patients, is not supported.

Independent advocates of the use of anti‐MAP therapy made the following recommendations prior to the publication of the second RCT [30]:

Patients with CD should be treated with conventional therapies as per international guidelines. However, there is a subset of patients that may benefit from antimycobacterial therapy when standard therapy is contraindicated or there is a lack of efficacy.

‘The Guy's and St Thomas’ approach has been to use a prolonged three‐drug antibiotic regimen… [clarithromycin, clofazimine, rifabutin]… Patients are assessed clinically following 3 months of treatment, and if there is evidence of objective improvement in disease activity, treatment is continued for a total of 2 years.

With the publication of the results of the MAPUS trial, such advice would not change. However, as more effective conventional therapies become available with greater persistence of initial response, it seems less likely that the use of anti‐MAP therapy will find a place as a first‐line therapy, especially in view of its limited effectiveness, high rate of loss of response, and the risk of adverse events during long‐term exposure. Continuing treatment beyond 16–26 weeks in those who have not demonstrated a significant objective improvement may benefit a small proportion of patients who cannot at this stage be identified prospectively.

The current RCTs were not designed to identify the small proportion of patients who may indeed have MAP as a major driver of Crohn's disease, as suggested by excellent outcomes reported in some case series [12, 16]. The ideal scenario is that such patients be identified and targeted for anti‐MAP therapy. As outlined above, current microbiological techniques are believed to be inadequate to do this. Novel methods, such as the use of a panel of antibodies and cytokines, have been proposed to select patients for anti‐MAP therapy [31], but therapeutic trials using such measures are required.

An alternative strategy to explore the role of MAP in the causation of Crohn's disease is to test the use of therapeutic vaccines against MAP. The results of a phase 1b clinical study using two therapeutic vaccines, each expressing four MAP antigens, appear to justify further trials [32].

Conclusions

Similar to several other antibiotic regimens, the benefit of anti‐MAP therapy in active CD is likely to be modest and may not be clinically meaningful in the majority of patients treated (Claim 1). Furthermore, the chance of loss of response after 16 weeks is high. While ongoing treatment is associated with a small chance of remitting if not in remission by 16 weeks, persistence of such a response has not been investigated (Claim 2). Any beneficial effect of anti‐MAP therapy is not maintained after its discontinuation (Claim 3). There is still no convincing evidence that the causative microorganism of Crohn's disease is Mycobacterium avium ssp. paratuberculosis in a significant number of patients (Claim 4).
