Knowledge and Attitudes of Ontario Pediatricians Related to Existing and New Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) Prevention Products: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Adria Rose, Rohini R. Datta, Costanza Di Chiara, Pierre‐Philippe Piché‐Renaud, Shaun K. Morris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionNew RSV prevention products show strong potential, but their success depends on wide acceptance and use. We assessed Ontario pediatricians' knowledge and attitudes about these products before their introduction to highlight opportunities and barriers.MethodsIn March−April 2024, Pediatricians Alliance of Ontario newsletter subscribers (n = 1566) were invited to complete an online survey on RSV knowledge and attitudes. Descriptive analyses with a complete‐case approach assessed responses.ResultsOf 117 responses (7% response rate), we retained 104 confirmed as from pediatricians with complete demographic data, including 81% (84/104) general pediatricians and 19% (20/104) subspecialists. Most worked in primary care (40%; 42/104), community hospitals (32%; 33/104), and urban centers (63%; 65/104), and had ≥ 10 years' experience (81%; 83/104). They had high awareness of provincial recommendations (91%; 94/103) and knowledge of severe RSV disease risk factors (Figure 2). While 83% (82/99) always recommended palivizumab to eligible patients, only 34% (34/99) reported that all eligible patients always received it. Over 90% were likely to recommend nirsevimab (91%; 89/98), but 66% (65/98) expected to administer it. Top anticipated barriers included the introduction of a new product (69%; 25/36) and concern for adverse effects (69%; 41/59). The likelihood of recommending a maternal vaccine over nirsevimab was 45% (44/98), while 44% (43/98) were neutral.ConclusionOntario pediatricians were knowledgeable and supportive of RSV prevention products. However, they have experienced barriers with palivizumab, and they anticipated barriers with nirsevimab and the maternal RSV vaccine. As other jurisdictions look to implement new RSV prevention products, acknowledging and addressing anticipated barriers may promote acceptance and use.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a major cause of hospitalization among children globally, including in Canada [1, 2], and causes significant morbidity, notably among those younger than 90 days [3]. Palivizumab, a short‐acting monoclonal antibody (mAb), has been in use in Canada since 2003 [4]. It is effective at reducing RSV‐associated hospitalizations [5] but has a short half‐life and requires monthly dosing during RSV season, which imparts a heavy burden on families, providers, and the healthcare system.

Nirsevimab, a longer‐acting mAb approved by Health Canada in April 2023, has been proven to be effective as a single dose per season in reducing the risks of medically‐attended and severe RSV among healthy preterm and full‐term infants [6, 7, 8]. Additionally, an RSV vaccine was approved for use for maternal vaccination during pregnancy in January 2024 [7] to protect newborns in the first 6 months of life. Nirsevimab has demonstrated real‐world effectiveness in reducing RSV‐related hospitalizations, lower respiratory tract infections (LRTI) requiring oxygen, and all‐cause LRTI [9]. In addition, the maternal RSV vaccine has shown significant reductions in medically attended severe RSV‐associated lower respiratory tract illness in infants [10].

Ontario initiated a new RSV strategy in the fall of 2024, which recommended nirsevimab to all children born in Ontario during the 2024–2025 season and made the maternal RSV vaccine available to pregnant residents of Ontario from 32 to 36 weeks' gestational age who were to deliver near the start of or during the 2024–2025 season [11]. The success of this campaign required strong promotion among healthcare providers with parallel campaigns to promote public awareness of these immunizations [12]. Previous studies showed that acceptance of vaccines and vaccine uptake by patients relies heavily on healthcare provider recommendations [13], as well as other factors such as vaccine safety assessments and strong national recommendations [14, 15]. Providers who are knowledgeable about vaccine‐preventable diseases are more likely to recommend the related vaccines to their patients [16, 17]. Mechanisms that support healthcare providers' recommendations of these immunizations and make it feasible for families to receive them will promote their successful implementation [12].

This study describes Ontario pediatricians' knowledge and attitudes towards palivizumab, nirsevimab, and the maternal RSV vaccine. Notably, this survey was done during a critical transition period when the two new products had been approved, but National Advisory Committee on Immunization (NACI) and Ontario Ministry of Health recommendations had not yet been released, so the products were not yet in use [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. As other jurisdictions prepare to implement these new RSV prevention products, acknowledging and addressing pediatricians' embedded knowledge, experience and attitudes towards existing and new RSV prevention products—including anticipated barriers—may improve acceptance and use of these products overall.

Methods

Study Setting

This cross‐sectional survey was conducted in Ontario, Canada, in March–April 2024, after nirsevimab and the maternal RSV vaccine had been approved by Health Canada but before the release of provincial recommendations on their use in May 2024 [18].

Study Design and Population

The Pediatricians Alliance of Ontario (PAO) provides content “designed for Ontario pediatricians” and is affiliated with the Pediatric section of the Ontario Medical Association [19]. Collaboration with PAO allowed distribution of an online self‐administered survey through an email listserv (n = 1566) on March 6, 2024, with weekly reminders sent over the course of 5 weeks (March 6 to April 12, 2024). Anonymity of the survey respondents prevented targeted outreach of non‐responders to boost engagement. Other methods of survey recruitment (e.g., social media, phone, or other outreach) beyond the initially approved strategy were not attempted due to resource constraints. Survey participation was voluntary and anonymous, with implied consent attained upon survey submission. Survey questions asked respondents to report their professional roles and demographic information; only pediatricians who responded to all key demographic questions were included in the analysis. To promote engagement, we provided the opportunity to enter a random draw for one of three gift cards, each valued at CAD$100. Entry in this draw was not linked to survey responses to maintain anonymity. This study received approval from the Research Ethics Board of the Hospital for Sick Children (#1000081077). The questionnaire, which included structured survey multiple‐choice and Likert scale questions with some open text “other” response options offered, was validated through pilot testing with two pediatricians and two pediatric infectious disease specialists prior to distribution (survey in Online Resource 1).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted to understand the following outcomes: pediatricians' self‐rated knowledge of RSV and prevention products, guidelines, and risk factors; experience with recommending palivizumab and barriers to its use; attitudes towards use of nirsevimab and anticipated barriers; and attitudes towards use of the maternal RSV vaccine and anticipated barriers. A complete‐case approach was used for each section of the analysis to maximize responses despite some partially incomplete surveys (Figure 1).

The Participant flow chart for descriptive analysis of online survey responses of Pediatricians Alliance of Ontario (PAO) newsletter subscribers on RSV prevention products.

These analyses explored frequency distributions of categorical data. The survey includes prompted and open‐ended “other” responses on barriers to administration and to acceptance of these prevention products. Response options for barriers to administration included logistics of managing related storage and staffing, as well as lack of prioritization by providers and parental hesitancy. Barriers to acceptance included reasons why they thought patients may choose not to get the immunizations. Any open text comments were grouped thematically according to the original multiple‐choice options included, when appropriate, or kept separate if deemed to be reflecting a different concept. The five response options for the likelihood of recommending nirsevimab over palivizumab and of recommending the maternal vaccine over nirsevimab were each collapsed into three categories of very unlikely/unlikely, neutral, and very likely/likely for the descriptive analysis. This manuscript complies with the STROBE checklist for cross‐sectional studies.

Results

Demographics

In total, 117 people responded to the survey (7% response rate). After excluding 4 non‐pediatricians (2 family physicians, 1 clinical pharmacologist, 1 unspecified) and those with incomplete demographic information, 104 respondents remained: 81% (84/104) general pediatricians and 19% (20/104) pediatric subspecialists (Table 1). Most had more than 10 years of clinical experience (80%; 83/104) and worked in either primary care practices (40%; 42/104) or community hospital settings (32%; 33/104). All primary practices were located in Southern Ontario, and most were in urban locations (63%; 65/104), reported managing bronchiolitis very often or always (39%; 41/104% and, 40%; 42/104, respectively) in their practices and 72% (75/104) noted that they provided immunizations to children at their primary practices.

Knowledge Assessments

We collected 103 complete responses to the knowledge assessment section of the survey. Responses demonstrated understanding of the key individual risk factors for severe RSV, which mirrored the indications for recommending palivizumab in Ontario at that time [20] (Figure 2).

Knowledge assessment of the underlying conditions* that increase risk for severe RSV disease and justify recommendation of palivizumab, as per a survey of Ontario pediatricians. *Living in a remote area is mentioned in Ontario recommendations for palivizumab administration but is limited to those born at specific gestational ages. As gestational age was not specified in the survey question, this response option was counted as correct when selected as a risk factor for severe RSV.

The survey assessed knowledge among the pediatricians of the population‐level group that represents the largest proportion of severe RSV disease. The majority (62%; 64/103) understood that, despite acknowledged individual risk factors for severe disease, the largest proportion of those with severe RSV are healthy infants and children under the age of two (Online Resource 2). Respondents showed strong awareness of provincial recommendations (91%; 94/103) and the Ontario Risk Assessment Tool for RSV Prophylaxis for High‐Risk Infants Program (84%; 86/103). Self‐rated knowledge of palivizumab, which was in use in Ontario at the time of the questionnaire, was high; however, self‐rated knowledge of nirsevimab and maternal RSV vaccine, which were only recently approved and not yet in use in Canada, was mostly very low/below average (Figure 3).

Experience With Palivizumab

Most of the 99 respondents who answered questions on their experience with palivizumab had patients receiving palivizumab within the past 5 years (97%; 96/99). Palivizumab was most commonly administered in their practices by nurses (57%; 56/99).

While 83% (82/99) of respondents always recommended palivizumab to eligible patients, only 34% (34/99) reported that all patients to whom they recommended palivizumab, received it 100% of the time (Online Resource 3). Of the 38% (38/99) that identified specific barriers to administration, the most common responses were parental hesitancy (40%; 15/38), frequency of appointments (37%; 14/38), and cost to the system (29%; 11/38). Barriers to acceptance of palivizumab, noted by 31% (31/99) respondents, included multiple doses requiring multiple visits (77%; 24/31) and concerns for potential adverse effects (61%; 19/31) (Online Resource 4).

Preferences and Expectations Regarding Nirsevimab

The majority of respondents (91%; 89/98) were likely or very likely to recommend nirsevimab over palivizumab (Table 2). Notably, while 57% (56/98) of respondents considered themselves to have below‐average or very low knowledge of nirsevimab, this group was still generally likely or very likely to recommend nirsevimab (88%; 49/56), with 6/56 (11%) being neutral and 1/59 (2%) being unlikely or very unlikely to do so. Those with average knowledge of nirsevimab were all likely or very likely to recommend it. Of the respondents with high levels of knowledge of nirsevimab, 88% (15/17) were likely or very likely to recommend it, 6% (1/17) were neutral, and 6% (1/17) were unlikely or very unlikely to recommend it.

Respondents generally supported the idea of administering nirsevimab during birth hospitalization (52%; 51/98); fewer preferred the first well‐baby visit (25%; 25/98) or at a specific vaccine clinic (17%; 17/98). A majority (73%; 72/98) thought it should be offered to all infants in the first year of life, with the remaining 27% (26/98) suggesting it should only be offered to high‐risk infants (Online Resource 5).

Despite 91% (89/98) of respondents being likely or very likely to recommend nirsevimab over palivizumab, only 66% (65/98) expected to be likely or very likely to actually administer it. Respondents identified anticipated barriers for administration (37%; 36/98) and acceptance (60%; 59/98) of nirsevimab. The most commonly anticipated barriers for administration were the introduction of a new product (69%; 25/36), storage concerns (61%; 22/36), and the availability of professionals to administer it/lack of sufficient pay for this service (58%; 21/36). Anticipated barriers to acceptance of nirsevimab centered on concern for adverse effects (69%; 41/59), lack of awareness (59%; 35/59), giving a healthy baby a monoclonal antibody (37%; 22/59), and lack of real‐world data (36%; 21/59) (Online Resource 6).

Table 3 presents pediatricians' characteristics distributed by their likelihood of recommending maternal RSV vaccine over nirsevimab (n = 98). Across all characteristics, 45% (44/98) of respondents were very likely or likely to recommend maternal RSV vaccine over nirsevimab, 44% (43/98) were neutral, and 11% (11/98) were unlikely or very unlikely. Respondents were divided over anticipated patient acceptance of the maternal RSV vaccine, with 40% (39/98) expecting it to be higher or much higher than acceptance of nirsevimab, 37% (36/98) expecting it to be no different, and 23% (23/98) expecting it to be lower or much lower (Online Resource 7).

Discussion

RSV prevention products have shown strong real‐world effectiveness in reducing severe RSV disease [9, 10, 21]. Countries like Spain and Chile that have already implemented nirsevimab or maternal RSV vaccination successfully have benefited from coordinated distribution, strong provider and public engagement, consistent messaging across sectors, and reliable supply [9, 10]. These countries can serve as models for other jurisdictions in earlier stages of implementation, but the specific strategies will need to be adapted to meet the needs of individual countries and contexts.

This need for tailored implementation planning for individual contexts can be demonstrated by comparing the attitudes of pediatricians towards these new RSV products in similar time periods as this study, but across different areas. For example, a 2023 study in Croatia found 71% of pediatricians agreeing/totally agreeing on the use of monoclonal antibodies and 67% agreeing/totally agreeing on the use of RSV maternal vaccine [22]. Studies in Italy and Spain from this time period found over 80% of pediatricians favored using monoclonal antibodies [23, 24]. Meanwhile, a study of pediatricians in Turkey in 2024, similarly done before the use of these new RSV products, noted only 38% intending to recommend nirsevimab and 26% intending to recommend the maternal RSV vaccine to all pregnant women [25]. This variation across studies from similar time periods highlights that pediatricians' knowledge of and attitudes towards RSV prevention products are not universal but rather are context‐specific.

Our study of Ontario pediatricians found strong, consistent support for nirsevimab over palivizumab, but the responses regarding the maternal RSV vaccine were more complex. The self‐rated level of knowledge of the maternal RSV vaccine was similar to that of nirsevimab, and just below half (45%) were likely or very likely to recommend the maternal RSV vaccine over nirsevimab, with 44% remaining neutral. Anticipated barriers to acceptance of the maternal RSV vaccine were noted among 68% of respondents, which was similar to the 60% who were anticipating these types of barriers for nirsevimab. However, survey respondents were more likely to anticipate barriers to the administration of maternal RSV immunization (58%) than to nirsevimab (37%). These concerns about administration may reflect anticipated logistical and role‐related issues, particularly given the limited opportunity pediatricians may have to counsel families about all RSV prevention products at the time maternal vaccination decisions are made.

While pediatricians may have had mixed impressions about the maternal RSV vaccine, other studies highlight that pregnant patients generally preferred the maternal RSV vaccine over nirsevimab at that time [26]. For example, a survey of pregnant patients in Quebec in 2023 found that 69% preferred vaccination during pregnancy over infant immunization [27], and a study from 2023 to 2024 of pregnant and post‐partum individuals in Canada found that 77% preferred maternal RSV vaccine to nirsevimab [28]. These discrepancies in preferences highlight the complex context impacting recommendations and barriers anticipated for the maternal RSV vaccine even before it was in use in Ontario.

After completion of our study, NACI released recommendations in May 2024 that were based on data available at the time and expert opinion, providing some clarity. They named nirsevimab as the preferred first‐line agent for RSV prevention due to its higher efficacy and possible longer duration of protection and recommended the maternal RSV vaccine in response to patient preference and/or in situations where nirsevimab may not be readily available to the infant [18]. The Society of Obstetricians and Gynecologists of Canada (SOGC) released a similar statement in July 2024 [29]. While this aligned messaging is helpful, it still does not lay out an ideal practical strategy for the coordination of care between maternal and child care providers that will be required to put these recommendations into practice across multiple contexts. In order to address these more practical concerns, jurisdictions will need to track how and by whom these recommendations are implemented across various contexts and identify practices that best respond to the attitudes, logistical constraints, needs, and preferences of pediatric and maternal health providers as well as their patients.

This study offers a first glimpse of the factors in Ontario that will need to be tracked and followed over time before best practices for this and other provinces can be confirmed [30, 31, 32], including awareness of RSV tools and policies as well as how, by whom, and where they are most successfully implemented. The high awareness of the existing Ontario RSV tools and policies may help explain the strong support for use of nirsevimab across 91% of respondents, even among those with self‐rated low levels of knowledge of the product. This finding aligns with literature showing that strong recommendations from trusted experts will boost provider confidence [14]; other jurisdictions may find benefit in initiating and actively promoting policies and expert guidance when introducing new products.

Pediatricians in our study generally supported administering nirsevimab during the birth hospitalization (52%). Similarly, a survey of Ontario providers (not just pediatric providers) done at the same time as this study found that 74.6% of respondents supported nirsevimab administration in the birth hospital prior to discharge, with the remainder preferring administration by the infant's primary care provider within 3 days of birth [12]. Studies in Europe show successful administration of nirsevimab as part of the birth hospitalization [33], and this aligns with subsequent Ontario Ministry of Health recommendations [34]. Therefore, this mode of implementation likely will be well‐accepted, but this will need to be followed over time to confirm it leads to successful and widespread implementation of RSV prevention products.

Limitations

This study provides valuable insights into pediatricians' knowledge, experience, and attitudes towards RSV prevention but has important limitations. First, the convenience sampling strategy likely resulted in selection bias, with overrepresentation of pediatricians more interested in and knowledgeable of RSV prevention, which potentially inflated estimates of knowledge, awareness of guidelines, and support for these new products. Conversely, non‐responders may have had less engagement with RSV prevention programs, estimated knowledge and awareness of the guidelines. Therefore, these data likely reflect attitudes and behaviors of an engaged subset of Ontario pediatricians.

Furthermore, these data only include responses of pediatricians, so they may not generalize to other maternal and child health care providers, such as family physicians, obstetricians, nurse practitioners, public health nurses, and other primary care specialties that have been and will continue to be directly involved in RSV prevention. The low response rate reflects the challenge of engaging busy practitioners with voluntary online surveys and further limits generalizability. A more intensive outreach strategy might have boosted the response rates but may have risked being misaligned with the scope of this project and even perceived as promotional. Even with these limitations, this study found high levels of knowledge and receptivity to the new products, as well as notable concerns among pediatricians with direct implications for their acceptance and use.

Implications of These Findings

This study explores the knowledge, experience, and attitudes related to RSV among Ontario pediatricians at a critical moment just before expert guidance on nirsevimab and the maternal RSV vaccine was released. It highlights the ways that these practitioners were not a “clean slate” awaiting recommendations but instead harbored knowledge as well as concerns even before these new products were put into practice. Therefore, these findings offer a guide for engaging Ontario pediatricians now that these products are in use and for jurisdictions implementing the products for the first time. Our findings underscore the need to assess and acknowledge providers' existing expertise and concerns right from the start as new products for RSV prevention are introduced.

Ongoing assessment of providers' attitudes, concerns, and practices over time will be important as maternal and pediatric providers gain experience. Engaging with what these professionals already know and what they may be uncertain about can help communication and implementation strategies be tailored in a way that supports uptake and builds trust. Specifically, tracking of implementation strategies (such as rates of administration during birth hospitalization vs. other administration procedures in both maternal and child health care settings), knowledge and attitudes of providers and patients (with specific attention to anticipated/reported barriers), real‐world uptake of both nirsevimab and the maternal RSV vaccine across the population, and RSV outcomes over time will enable illumination of best practices across various contexts.
