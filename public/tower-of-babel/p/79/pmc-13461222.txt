A Physical Exercise Program Significantly Boosts the Impact of Cognitive Training on Attention Deficits After a First Episode of Schizophrenia
Authors: Keith H Nuechterlein, Joseph Ventura, Kenneth L Subotnik, Juliane Nguyen, Emily A McGraw, Margaret G Distler, Dennis Dacarett Galeano, Michael F Zito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and HypothesisOur initial smaller study and other recent research suggest that adding a physical exercise program to cognitive training has promise for enhancing the impact on attention and overall cognition. We hypothesized that this would be the case even compared to adding a healthy living training program.Study DesignIn a randomized clinical trial of individuals with a recent first episode of schizophrenia, we provided cognitive training, 4 h/week for 6 months for all participants. Half were randomized to a physical exercise program and half to a healthy living group. The MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery was administered at baseline, 3, and 6 months, with a focus on Attention/Vigilance. Exercise was measured by the International Physical Activity Questionnaire and the number of exercise sessions completed.Study ResultsAttention/Vigilance was differentially enhanced by cognitive training and physical exercise compared to cognitive training and healthy living group (T score gain of 4.6 vs. −0.2 over 6 months, P < .01). Improvement in Attention/Vigilance by 3 months was significantly associated with number of exercise sessions attended (P = .02) and exercise intensity (P < .03) during that period. Attention/Vigilance gain by 6 months was significantly predicted by total metabolic energy expended during the first 3 months (P = .04) and proportion of group exercise sessions attended (P < .01) over 6 months. In the first 3 months, brain-derived neurotropic factor tended to increase in the exercise condition but decrease in the comparison condition.ConclusionAdding an exercise program to cognitive training enhances the impact on the core deficit in attention in schizophrenia. The gains in attention are significantly related to the amount and intensity of exercise, consistent with the view that physical exercise is driving the attention improvements beyond the effect of cognitive training.Keywords: aerobic exercise, cognitive remediation, first-episode psychosis

Introduction

Deficits in attention have been recognized as a core feature of schizophrenia.1–3 Investigations of sustained focused attention in schizophrenia were a major theme in the research program of Ba LISTNUM rbara Cornblatt,4,5 to whom this theme section is dedicated. Deficits in sustained focused attention have been shown to be present in individuals with schizophrenia during psychotic and remitted states, indicating that they are not secondary to psychotic symptoms.6–8 Furthermore, these deficits have been detected to a less severe degree in parents and siblings of schizophrenia probands, suggesting that they may be indicators of genetic vulnerability to the disorder.9–12 A meta-analysis of cognitive deficits in unaffected first-degree relatives of schizophrenia probands concluded that, when studies were restricted to those with symmetrical exclusion criteria and age-matched comparison groups, the sustained attention deficits have the largest effect size of 24 variables.13 Sustained attention also shows at least moderate heritability in both schizophrenia and community samples.14–17 Evidence from the New York High-Risk Project, a longitudinal study of children born to mothers with schizophrenia, found that deficits in sustained attention could be detected years before onset of schizophrenia spectrum disorders in a subgroup of these high-risk children.18 Finally, the attentional deficits in individuals with schizophrenia have been shown to be associated with functional capacity19 and everyday functioning.20 Thus, multiple lines of evidence support the view that sustained attention deficits are a persisting core feature of schizophrenia that make them an important target for intervention.

A prominent measure of sustained focused attention in the schizophrenia literature was developed by Barbara Cornblatt and her colleagues.14,21 An initial Continuous Performance Test—Identical Pairs (CPT-IP) involved a computerized task in which 4-digit numbers appeared every 1.0 s and the task was to press a button each time identical numbers occurred in sequence.14 A version of the CPT-IP that varies the cognitive load by including 2-, 3-, and 4-digit conditions was selected as the Attention/Vigilance measure for the MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB), which has been endorsed by the US Food and Drug Administration as the gold standard for measuring cognitive change in clinical trials of interventions to improve cognition in schizophrenia.22

Improving cognitive performance in schizophrenia has proven to be quite difficult, and no pharmacological treatment has thus far been approved for this purpose.23 Cognitive remediation24,25 and aerobic exercise26 have been demonstrated to have significant benefits for cognition in schizophrenia, but each alone tends to show small to medium effect sizes.

A relatively recent development has been attempts to combine systematic cognitive training and aerobic exercise to boost the impact on cognition. Animal research indicates that physical exercise can prime hippocampal neuroplasticity, increasing neurogenesis and synaptogenesis when cognitive stimulation is added.27 One mechanism by which exercise improves synaptic plasticity, learning, and memory is by boosting brain-derived neurotropic factor (BDNF).28,29

Studies of the combination of physical exercise and cognitive training in individuals with schizophrenia and related disorders have tended to be promising. Most involve short-term interventions (4-12 weeks) and they differ substantially in the form of cognitive training employed and the nature and intensity of physical exercise. An initial 2014 study by Oertel-Knochel and colleagues suggested that only 4 weeks of cognitive training plus aerobic exercise led to more improvement in working memory in patients with schizophrenia than in those with major depressive disorder.30 A 2015 study by Malchow et al. found no improvement in cognition during the first 6 weeks of bicycle aerobic exercise but short-term memory improved when Cogpack cognitive training was added for another 6 weeks.31 Our University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) group found in a 2016 small non-randomized pilot study that 10 weeks of aerobic exercise plus Posit Science cognitive training produced a larger effect size on the MCCB overall cognitive composite than the cognitive training alone.32

Studies published in the 2020s have expanded on these early results but have been inconsistent in their conclusions. A 10-week study that added moderate intensity aerobic activity of the individual’s choice to Cogpack cognitive training found that the addition of aerobic exercise did not improve cognition more than Cogpack training alone.33 Resting BDNF did not change for either group, raising the possibility that the exercise program was not sufficiently intensive. A 3-month study with only 18 h of intervention found that both exercise alone and the combination of exercise and cognitive training improved working memory at 3 months but only the combination led to continued improvement at a 2-month follow up.34 Thus, the combination benefited persistence of working memory effects. An 8-week intervention of treadmill exercise plus cognitive training led to improved processing speed than either exercise alone or treatment as usual, but exercise alone did not improve processing speed so the possibility that the gain was due to cognitive training alone cannot be ruled out.35 A recent 3-month study of stationary bike aerobic exercise plus Cogpack training found that this combination produced larger improvements in working memory and verbal learning than either cognitive training or exercise alone and larger attention effects than cognitive training alone.36

The only prior randomized controlled trial that examined a longer intervention period is the initial one from our UCLA group, which used relatively intensive dosages of Posit Science cognitive training (4 h/week) and aerobic exercise (150 min of moderate intensity per week) over 6 months in patients with a recent first psychotic episode.37 We demonstrated large gains in the MCCB overall composite within 3 months and large improvements in work/school functioning by 6 months in patients in the combined cognitive training and exercise (CT&E) intervention compared to cognitive training alone. This clinical trial remained relatively small (N = 47) and did not include a comparison treatment of equal contact time, so the current study doubled the sample size and included a didactic healthy living group as a control for the amount of group treatment time provided to each group. In this report we also particularly focus on the impact on attention/vigilance improvement based on the CPT-IP. We hypothesized that the combination of CT&E would improve attention/vigilance, overall cognition, and functional outcome more than cognitive training and a didactive healthy living group. We also examined whether the addition of exercise differentially raised serum BDNF, a potential mechanism for its impact.

Methods

Participants

Individuals who recently experienced a first psychotic episode of schizophrenia, schizophreniform disorder, or schizoaffective disorder were recruited from local public and private psychiatric hospitals and clinics. We will refer to these individuals as having first-episode schizophrenia for brevity. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were (1) a first episode of a psychotic illness that began within the past 2 years; (2) a DSM-5 diagnosis of schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, mainly depressed type, or schizophreniform disorder; (3) between 18 and 45 years of age; (4) no evidence of significant head injury or a known neurological disorder (eg, epilepsy); (5) no evidence of moderate or severe substance use disorder within the 6 months prior to the first episode and no evidence that substance use accounts for the psychotic disorder; (6) premorbid IQ not less than 70; (7) sufficient acculturation and fluency in the English language to avoid invalidating research measures; and (8) residence within commuting distance of the UCLA Aftercare Research Program. Diagnosis was established through a combination of the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 (SCID-5)38 and information from medical records, treatment providers, and family members. Substance use was evaluated through self-report, family reports, and medical records. Raters were trained to reliability standards.39 The UCLA IRB approved the research; all participants gave written informed consent. This randomized clinical trial (RCT) was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov as NCT02823041.

Treatment for all Participants

Upon entry into the UCLA Aftercare Research Program, a coordinated specialty care clinic for recent-onset schizophrenia patients, all participants were provided a psychiatrist and a psychologist. Psychiatric medication was managed by an Aftercare psychiatrist, while weekly individual case management, recovery-oriented psychotherapy, and family psychoeducation were coordinated by the psychologist. Participants were prescribed second generation antipsychotic medication with dosage individually optimized. Adjunctive medications (eg, antidepressants, antiparkinsonian medications) were used when clinically indicated.

Given the difficulties of public transportation for substantial distances in Los Angeles, prior to the COVID-19 pandemic UCLA van transportation was provided to patients who could not drive themselves to UCLA or use public transportation. The pandemic occurred in the middle of this clinical trial, which forced all treatment which could be completed remotely to be done by HIPAA-compliant Zoom videoconferencing starting in March 2020.

Randomization to Cognitive Training and Exercise vs. Cognitive Training and Healthy Living Group

For the first 3 months after entry into the Aftercare Research Program, patients were stabilized on antipsychotic medication to resolve any acute psychotic symptoms and provided weekly case management and group psychoeducation about their symptoms, contributing factors, and treatment. Then the baseline assessments were completed. Although we considered whether acute symptoms would prevent group participation, remission of psychotic symptoms was not required for randomization. Immediately following baseline assessment, participants were randomized using a random number table in a 1:1 ratio to CT&E vs. Cognitive Training and Healthy Living Group (CT&HLG). The group treatments described below lasted 6 months. The group treatments occurred on Tuesday and Thursday, with 2 CT sessions separated by either an exercise or healthy living group on each day and a bridging group on Tuesday (CT&HLG) or Thursday (CT&E). The major points of repeated assessment were 3 and 6 months after randomization. Figure 1 provides the recruitment and enrollment CONSORT diagram.

Cognitive Training

All participants were provided identical systematic cognitive training program using web-based Posit Science programs, with the first 12 weeks being neurocognitive training and the second 12 weeks being social cognitive training. One hour of computerized cognitive training sessions occurred twice a day on 2 days/week, thereby providing 48 h of neurocognitive training and 48 h of social cognitive training. CT session attendance was encouraged by providing $2 per session. Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, the CT sessions were held in an Aftercare Research Program room, with 4-6 participants on separate computers with one cognitive coach. After onset of pandemic restrictions, CT sessions occurred via group Zoom videoconferences, with 4-6 participants on their home computers with one cognitive coach.

Neurocognitive Training

Posit Science BrainHQ modules for neurocognition emphasized attention, auditory discrimination, speed of processing, working memory, verbal memory, and verbal reasoning. The modules started with basic cognitive processes and progressed to more complex processes. The BrainHQ exercises adapt difficulty level within modules to match the participant performance to maximize engagement while gradually increasing cognitive skill. Progress was visible to the participant on the computer screen and to cognitive trainers. The cognitive coach provided verbal reinforcement and suggested strategies if patients became stuck at a given level.

Social Cognitive Training

The Posit Science social cognitive modules focus on facial emotion recognition, social perception, processing of emotion, self-referential style, and interpretation of social information. Participants start with simple emotion identification and gradually move to more difficult emotion discrimination and social cue interpretation tasks. The difficulty level is constantly adjusted based on performance for maximum engagement and learning. The more complex tasks involve application of neurocognitive skills to social situations, such as memory for information about several individuals. Dynamic visual stimuli and a social reward system encouraged continued motivation.

Bridging Group

All patients participated in a 1-h weekly Bridging Group with other members of their own treatment group. This group focused on generalization of cognitive training to everyday life situations. Patients discussed how the neurocognitive and social cognitive skills they learned in the computerized training programs could apply to their specific challenges. Patients practiced specific strategies for applying cognitive skills to improve their functioning in key domains of independent living, school/job, and social situations. Group leaders asked patients about challenging work and school situations and provided structured feedback on ways to improve work habits and work quality. To encourage group engagement, patients took turns making presentations about a topic in which they had particular interest. We fostered peer-to-peer support in a safe environment to facilitate generalization of training to everyday functioning.

Aerobic Exercise Program

In addition to the cognitive training, participants randomized to the CT&E group participated in an aerobic exercise and strength conditioning program. The group exercise sessions occurred twice a week, and individual exercise sessions were assigned for another 2 sessions/week. Our aerobic conditioning program used the same exercises as our earlier RCT.37 The 45-min group exercise sessions involved 5-7 participants led by a certified fitness trainer and a clinical psychology practicum trainee. They included a dynamic warm-up (7.5 min) and cool-down after exercise (7.5 min). Each exercise session involved 30 min of moderate-intensity aerobic conditioning (1-min intervals) and moderate-to-high-intensity strength and calisthenic conditioning (1-min intervals), alternating to maintain heart rate in the aerobic zone as much as possible. At the start of each session, participants were instructed on the proper technique and form for 5 different exercises. They then completed 3 rounds of the 5 sets of aerobic and strength training exercises. The goal was 150 min/week of moderate aerobic activity, which included 2 group exercise sessions (each 45 min) and 2 completed individually (each 30 min), as recommended by the American College of Sports Medicine.40 The exercise intensity started at 50% of individually calibrated aerobic capacity and increased as quickly as possible to 60%-80% of aerobic capacity, using the Karvonen formula. To measure heart rate and activity data, each participant was given a Scosche Rhythm+ heart rate monitor. During the group exercise sessions, participants checked their heart rate on 3 occasions and worked with the trainer as needed to adjust their exercise intensity. Participants were instructed to wear their heart rate monitor while doing the individual exercise sessions to allow a measure of adherence to assigned exercise. To encourage completion of the individual exercise sessions, participants chose their own form of exercise, ranging from hiking, basketball, and Zumba classes to use of YouTube videos of the exercises from the group sessions.

Exercise was rewarded by providing $5 for every individual session and $2 for each group session. The greater reward for individual sessions was designed to overcome the tendency for participation to be somewhat lower for those sessions. Each CT&E participant was also paired with an exercise text buddy, a clinical psychology practicum trainee sent a text reminder and motivational message on the chosen exercise days.

Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, the group exercise sessions were held at the clinic twice a week. Once pandemic restrictions began, all group exercise sessions were conducted by Zoom videoconference, again with 5-7 participants and led by a certified fitness trainer and clinical psychology practicum trainee.

Healthy Living Group

To equate the weekly duration of interventions for the 2 treatment conditions, a didactic healthy living group met twice weekly for those not randomized to the aerobic exercise program. Each session was 60 min in duration, led by clinical psychology practicum or internship trainees with 5-7 participants. Application of healthy living habits outside of the sessions was encouraged. A series of semi-structured modules were used for presentation and discussion. Topics included wellness, nutrition, insight, recovery goals, independent living skills, social skills, coping skills for stressors, and leisure and hobbies. These group sessions were held at the clinic until the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, and then by Zoom videoconference after that point.

Measures

The major assessments occurred at baseline and 3 months and 6 months after starting the randomized group sessions.

Cognitive Performance

The MCCB22 was administered at baseline and 3 and 6 months later. The cognitive domain of Attention/Vigilance, measured by the CPT-IP,14 was of particular interest for this report. The T score for the Overall Composite Score was also examined to assess the breadth of any cognitive impact.

Functional Outcome

The Global Functioning Scale41 was the primary functional outcome measure. The mean of the 10-point Role and Social ratings was the overall functional measure. Criterion levels of interrater reliability were established. Raters used their weekly contact with the participants and information from family members, employers, and teachers to rate work/school and social performance.

Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor

Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) is known to be a mediator of the effects of exercise in the brain.42 We collected fasting serum BDNF via venipuncture upon clinic arrival in the morning at baseline and after 3 months and 6 months. The UCLA Center for Pathology Research Services completed assays using the ELISA immunoassay kits from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN).

Amount and Intensity of Exercise

For participants randomized to CT&E, the number of group and individual exercise sessions completed was recorded. In addition, all participants were administered monthly the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ).43 The IPAQ is a structured self-report questionnaire, completed in an interview with a staff member, that assesses physical activity habits over the previous 7 days. Amount of time walking, doing moderate exercise (eg, carrying light loads, bicycling at a regular pace, or easy swimming), and doing vigorous activities (eg, aerobics, heavy lifting, digging, or fast bicycling) is converted into a weekly total metabolic equivalent of tasks (METs). A MET is the amount of oxygen consumed during an activity compared to the resting metabolic rate.

Data Analyses

Analyses were completed with SPSS Statistics v30 GLM and Correlation modules. Because we needed to switch from in-person to remote group treatments in the middle of this RCT due to the COVID-19 pandemic, we included this factor in our analyses. We calculated repeated measures ANOVAs with Group (CT&E, CT&HLG) and Telehealth (in-person, telehealth) as between-subjects factors, Time (baseline, 3 months, 6 months) as the within-subjects factor, and Group by Time and Group ×Telehealth ×Time interactions. For cognitive performance, we covaried the baseline cognitive T score because cognitive training gains have sometimes been found to be affected by baseline performance. All available outcome data were examined, without regard to the number of treatment sessions that the patients completed.

The relationships of amount and intensity of exercise to cognitive improvement were examined through correlational analyses.

Results

Sample Characteristics and Treatment Participation

Table 1 provides the demographic and clinical characteristics of patients in each treatment condition. The 90 participants whose data are presented in this article have a mean age of 22.9 years and a mean of 13.4 years of education. Most are male (66%) and single (94%). Their racial and ethnic backgrounds are diverse, as is typical of the Los Angeles area. Their first psychotic episode of at least one week duration started a mean of 8.5 months prior to project entry. At the point of randomization, their mean MCCB Attention/Vigilance T score was 37.8 and their mean Overall Composite T Score was 31.5. The mean rating on the Global Functioning Scale: Role was 3.9 (of 10) and on the Global Functioning Scale: Social was 5.3. Their mean BPRS Thought Disturbance and Anergia factor scores44 were both 2.0 (of 7). These cognitive, global functioning, and symptom levels are typical of clinically stabilized outpatients after a first episode of schizophrenia, with low symptom levels but substantial impairment in cognition and role functioning. None of the demographic or clinical characteristics differed significantly between treatment conditions. The levels of prescribed second-generation antipsychotic medication were also not significantly different for the 2 treatment conditions based on Daily Defined Dosage risperidone equivalents45 for the 6-month treatment period (CT&E: mean 5.89, SD = 3.77; CT&HLG: mean 5.29, SD = 2.76; P = .58).

Cognitive Performance

The mean gains for Attention/Vigilance were substantial for the CT&E group (4.6 T) and absent for the CT&HLG group (−0.2 T), Group × Time effect, F(2,164) = 4.78, P < .01, Cohen’s f = .21, as illustrated in Figure 2. A directional tendency for Attention/Vigilance to improve more with telehealth than in-person sessions was observed across groups over the 6 months but this was not significant (telehealth: 3.2 T; in-person, 1.2 T, F(2, 164) = 1.97, P = .14) and did not interact significantly with Group. The MCCB Overall Composite T score improved very significantly for both groups over the 6-month clinical trial (Time effect, F(2, 166) = 26.30, P < .001, Cohen’s f = .55), but the gains did not differ significantly between treatment groups (CT&E: 5.1 T; CT&HLG: 4.7 T) (see Table 2).

To examine further whether the improvement in Attention/Vigilance was associated with physical exercise, we correlated the Attention/Vigilance gain with the amount and intensity of exercise. Within the CT&E group, the gain in Attention/Vigilance in the first 3 months was significantly associated with the number of group exercise sessions attended (r = 0.32, P = .02) and with a higher activity category based on the IPAQ (r = 0.33, P < .03). Similarly, the Attention/Vigilance gain by 6 months was predicted by total amount of metabolic energy (METs) expended during exercise in the first 3 months for the entire sample (r = 0.22, P = .04) and was significantly correlated with the proportion of group exercise sessions attended in the first 6 months (r = 0.38, P < .01).

Functional Outcome

The mean of the Role and Social ratings on the Global Functioning Scale improved substantially in both treatment groups over 6 months (Time effect, F(2, 172) = 45.07, P < .001, Cohen’s f = .71), but the gains did not differ significantly between groups (CT&E: 1.4; CT&HLG: 1.3) (see Table 2).

BDNF

The changes in serum BDNF levels were expected to be particularly apparent in the immediate months after starting a regular physical exercise program. After 3 months the changes were in the expected direction (CT&E: 3-month gain 1095 pg/mL, CT&HLG: 3-month decrease 868 pg/mL), but this tendency for a differential change was not maintained by 6 months (CT&E: 6-month gain 700 pg/mL; CT&HLG: 6-month gain 1351 pg/mL). The Group × Time interaction was not significant (F(2,100) = 1.01, P = .37). The large standard deviations in BDNF levels within groups (5621-8038 pg/mL) made it difficult to detect treatment group differences (see Table 2). The sample size for BDNF was also smaller (N = 54) due to blood draw refusals and restrictions during the COVID-19 pandemic. Analyses showed no significant differences in severity of anxiety, depression, delusions, or hallucinations for participants with versus without BDNF data, but statistical power was more limited for BDNF analyses. Analyses showed that baseline BDNF levels for participants who did not have BDNF data at 3 and 6 months tended to be lower than for participants with follow-up BDNF data (22 900 vs. 25 902 pg/mL, t(70.7) = 1.964, P = .054), so low baseline BDNF participants might have contributed further BDNF gains with exercise had their blood draws been available.

IPAQ METs

Given that BDNF levels showed expected directional changes after 3 months that were not maintained at 6 months, we explored the total metabolic energy expended on physical exercise for the 2 groups for the first 3 months and the second 3 months. The sum of monthly total METs from the IPAQ yielded a significant Group × Time interaction (F(1,72) = 4.28, P = .042). Follow-up contrasts revealed that the CT&E group expended significantly more energy on exercise than the CT&HLG group in the first 3 months (CT&E: 3449 METs (SD = 2246); CT&HLG: 2041 METs (SD = 1736); P < .004) but not in the second 3 months (CT&E: 2407 METs (SD = 2027); CT&HLG: 2014 METs (SD = 1947); P = .47).

Discussion

This 6-month RCT demonstrated that the addition of an aerobic exercise program to systematic cognitive training produced significantly greater gains in attention/vigilance in recent-onset schizophrenia patients than an equally intensive combination of cognitive training and didactic healthy living training. Given that the attention deficit in schizophrenia has been shown to occur before psychosis onset in high-risk children, after psychosis remission in individuals with schizophrenia, and among first-degree relatives of schizophrenia probands, reduction of this persistent core deficit provides further support for the efficacy of the combination of CT&E. The fact that the amount of attention gain is significantly associated with the amount and intensity of physical exercise that participants completed supports the view that the exercise is leading to the attentional improvement. The improvement in attention/vigilance continued over the 6-month CT&E intervention, which suggests that it is not a transient gain. The differential improvement in attention with the addition of aerobic exercise to cognitive training occurred compared to a treatment condition with comparable group intervention time and which also focused on healthy behaviors, supporting the hypothesis that the source of gain was active physical exercise.

Most prior clinical trials of a combination of cognitive training and physical exercise have shown benefits for some domains of cognition (except see McGurk et al33) but have been shorter in treatment duration30–36 and smaller in sample size.30–33,36,37 The most frequent cognitive domain impacted has been working memory although one recent study reported attention gains relative to cognitive training alone.36 Most of the prior studies, like the current one, have compared the combination of physical exercise and cognitive training to a treatment that did not include exercise rather than to an exercise-alone condition, raising the possibility that physical exercise alone might produce the same benefits. However, one recent study35 found that processing speed was significantly improved by the combination but not by exercise alone. Another study34 reported similar working memory results for the combination and for exercise alone during treatment but the combination produced persisting gains 2 months later that did not occur with exercise alone. Thus, while more research is needed to evaluate the impact of exercise alone vs. the combination of exercise and cognitive training, evidence supporting the combination is emerging.

The impact of adding exercise to cognitive training was not as broad in the current RCT as it was in our prior smaller RCT. In the Nuechterlein et al. RCT,37 the combination of CT&E led to differential gains in the MCCB overall cognitive composite by 3 months and in work/school functioning by 6 months. The cognitive training intervention and the aerobic exercise sessions followed the same protocol in both studies, but other factors may contribute to the difference in results. First, the addition of the healthy living group in the comparison treatment might have led to a smaller difference in cognitive effects across domains. However, both treatment groups achieved notable cognitive gains in the current study, similar to that of cognitive training alone in the prior RCT, so the somewhat smaller magnitude of the overall cognitive composite gain in the current CT&E group compared to the prior study is the more likely source of difference. A second factor is that baseline MCCB overall cognitive performance was lower in the 2023 study than in the current study (mean T scores of 24.9 vs. 31.5), which might influence the range of benefits from these interventions. Third, the finding that total metabolic energy expended in physical exercise was significantly higher in the CT&E group than in the CT&HLG group in the first 3 months but not the second 3 months suggests that the impact of the exercise program on amount of exercise tended to decrease over time. This difference between groups in metabolic energy expended in exercise in the first but not the second 6 months parallels the tendency toward BDNF group differences in the first but not the second 6 months. The exercise adherence rates of about 62% for group exercise sessions and 44% for individual sessions are in the range of exercise studies of other clinical populations,46 but indicate that exercise adherence rates have notable room for improvement. Examining ways to boost motivation to exercise and to provide other exercise facilitators over time will be important to strengthen the impact of exercise in individuals with schizophrenia. Finally, the COVID-19 pandemic hit in the middle of the 5-year enrollment period for this RCT (2017-2022) and dominated treatment conditions for the latter half of the study. All group treatments needed to be converted to remote videoconferences and opportunities to seek work and have social gatherings were severely restricted. Thus, it is not surprising that the functional outcome benefits seen in the earlier RCT37 did not generalize to this RCT.

The limitations of this study follow from some of the points just made. First, to demonstrate broader sustained impact on cognition, the exercise program needs to maintain a higher level of physical exercise than the comparison intervention throughout the active treatment period. Further support for motivation to exercise and other incentives to exercise are indicated.47,48 This study nevertheless revealed notable improvement in the core attentional deficit in schizophrenia due to exercise that was not present from cognitive training. Second, the conversion from live in-clinic group interventions to remote videoconference interventions was disruptive and limited the ability to standardize treatment conditions throughout the study. Finally, this study does not provide a strong test of the impact of the combination of cognitive training and exercise on everyday functioning, as the COVID-19 pandemic strongly restricted work and social functioning during the latter half of the study.
