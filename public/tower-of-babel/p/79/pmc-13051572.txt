Surgical findings and timing in re-exploration of free flaps in extremity reconstruction
Authors: Kubilay Erol, Özgün Barış Güntürk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study aims to evaluate the salvage rates of re-explored free flaps in extremity reconstruction, focusing on the timing of re-exploration, types of vascular compromise, and related factors that affect outcomes.Methods:A retrospective review of 527 free flap surgeries conducted between January 2010 and July 2024 was performed. Cases requiring re-exploration within seven days postoperatively (n=78) 14.8% were analysed. Data collected included demographics, flap types, timing of vascular compromises and re-explorations, and surgical outcomes. Statistical analyses were performed to identify relationships between variables.Results:Among the re-explored cases, a salvage rate of 71.7% (n=56) was achieved. Venous thrombosis emerged as the leading cause of re-exploration (44.9%) (n=35). Significant findings included a median time to re-exploration of 12.2 hours from the initial surgery and 2.5 hours from the first sign of compromise. The first signs of flap compromise were observed in 91.1% (n=71) of cases within the first 24 hours postoperatively and no instances of flap compromise were recorded after 72 hours.Conclusion:Timely intervention was found to be strongly associated with improved flap salvage rates. These findings underscore the critical importance of early monitoring and prompt surgical management in free flap reconstructions, particularly for extremity cases. The study supports re-exploration within 2.5 hours of detecting vascular compromise as essential for maximizing salvage success. High re-exploration and survival rates demonstrate that effective and timely management protocols are vital for optimizing outcomes in microsurgical procedures.Level of Evidence:Level IV, Therapeutic Study.

Introduction

Free flaps represent an optimal surgical choice for the reconstruction of tissue defects resulting from trauma, oncological resections, and infections. Previous studies have indicated that free flaps have been performed successfully in 95% to 99% of cases over the past decades.1-3 Nonetheless, 6% to 16% of the free flaps necessitate early re-exploration due to vascular compromise.4-7 Flap loss contributes to increased hospital stay, higher costs, patient morbidity, and may negatively impact aesthetic outcomes, often requiring revision surgery. The precision of surgical techniques is paramount for achieving success in microvascular procedures.8,9 Despite advancements, flap compromise remains a challenge, even in highly experienced hands or specialized centers.

Flap monitoring, early detection of initial signs of compromise, and prompt re-exploration are critical for compromised flaps. This approach allows for flap salvage before the onset of the no-reflow phenomenon.10 The literature contains limited studies on improving the survival rates of free flaps requiring re-exploration, with most research focusing on head, neck, and breast reconstructions.9,11 Extremity reconstruction differs from other sites due to variations in vascular patterns and recipient vessels. Additionally, extremity defects typically result from traumatic injuries, complicating the identification of the injury zone. This study on extremity reconstruction aims to investigate the salvage rate of re-explored flaps and examine associated factors such as timing, type of vascular compromise, and strategies for avoiding and managing complications.

Material and Methods

After obtaining approval from the Institutional Board of EMOT Hospital, Approval Number: 2024/006 Date: 16.09.2024), a retrospective review was conducted on the free flap surgeries performed between January 2010 and July 2024. Flaps that were re-explored within the first 7 days post-surgery were included in the study. Patient information was accessed using operative notes, flap follow-up forms, and the hospital digital archive.

The data collected included patients’ age, gender, type of flap, type of vascular compromise, and the timing of the first signs of flap compromise and revisions following the initial surgery. The survival rates of the re-explored flaps were analyzed. Additionally, the surgical interventions performed were recorded by reviewing the surgical notes.

All free tissue transfers were conducted by a team of surgeons, each possessing a minimum of 5 years of experience in microsurgery. A total of 8 consultant surgeons participated in these procedures, all of whom are affiliated with a private local hospital specializing in hand and microsurgery, orthopedics, and traumatology. The reconstructions targeted either the upper or lower extremities.

During the execution of free flap surgery, all microvascular anastomoses were performed manually, without the use of coupler devices. Arterial anastomoses were based on the compatibility of diameters between the recipient vessel and the flap artery, as well as the condition of the recipient artery, determined to be either the primary or secondary artery of the extremity. Extremity vascularization was always checked by temporarily clamping the artery if a major artery was planned to be used. These arterial anastomoses were conducted using either an end-to-end or end-to-side technique, depending on the aforementioned conditions. All venous anastomoses were executed in an end-to-end manner. For procedures involving the lower extremities, the deep venous system was mostly utilized as the recipient vein. Conversely, for upper extremity procedures, the selection of the recipient vein was made at the surgeon’s discretion, taking into consideration the length of the pedicle.

Following the completion of the microvascular anastomoses, the flap was monitored in the operating room for a duration of 20-30 minutes. During this monitoring period, the flap’s color, turgor, and capillary refill were assessed. Additionally, the integrity of the pedicles and anastomoses was evaluated. If any kinking was observed in the pedicle, tension sutures were employed to provide a curved but smooth pathway.

Following the initial free flap procedure, all patients were transferred to a dedicated microsurgical unit staffed with specialized nursing personnel for flap monitoring. During the first 6 hours post-procedure, flaps were monitored at 30-minute intervals; subsequently, they were examined hourly for the next 72 hours. Flaps that exhibited no complications during this period were then monitored at 2-hour intervals until the fifth day. From days 5 through 7, monitoring was conducted at 4-hour intervals throughout the day. The minimum hospitalization duration was 7 days. Monitoring parameters included capillary refill time, turgor, surface color, temperature, and the pinprick test. All findings regarding flap condition were meticulously documented in flap follow-up forms. Should any issues be detected during monitoring, nursing staff were instructed to promptly notify the surgical team of the compromised flap to facilitate timely planning for re-exploration. Before the re-exploration procedures, patients were observed for a short period for possible vasospasms. During that period, extremity was elevated, and dressings were released if tight bandages were suspected. If the findings of compromise continued, a re-exploration procedure was performed.

In the re-exploration procedure, sutures are removed at the site of the anastomosis, and a hematoma is drained if present. The site was observed for active bleeding, vessel kinking, and thrombosis. The anastomosis is cut, and thrombectomy is performed, after which the vessel lumens are irrigated with heparinized saline solution and checked for any damaged vessel walls. If the lumens are adequate, short segment resection and reanastomosis are performed. If the lumens are not adequate, long segment resection and reanastomosis with vein grafts were performed. The types of procedures performed are noted from the operation notes.

Postoperatively, acetylsalicylate (1 to 5 mg/kg/day) and Voluven® (10 mL/kg/day) were administered. For acetylsalicylate, 100 mg/day usually provides the antiaggregant dose for adults, and the tablets were split/cut in half for children. The initial dose of the intravenous fluid regimen was given in the operating theater. For postoperative analgesia, a continuous peripheral brachial plexus or epidural catheter was utilized. Antibiotic prophylaxis was provided with cefazolin; patients weighing less than 70 kg received a preoperative dose of 1 g, while those over 70 kg received 2 g. The antibiotic dose was adjusted for children to 40 mg/kg/day. Postoperatively, cefazolin was administered at a dosage of 1 g every 8 hours for the first 24 hours. If a specific pathogen is identified in the patient’s culture, the antibiotic treatment is adjusted based on the sensitivity of the pathogen. Voluven was continued for 5 days and acetylsalicylate for 3 weeks.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as n (%), mean ± SD, and median (minimum-maximum values). The Pearson chi-square test was used in the analysis of relationships between categorical variables. In the normality test, when the number of samples in the group was greater than 50, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and Student’s t-test were used to analyze the difference between the measurement values of the 2 groups, and P values less than .05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Between the specified dates, a total of 527 free flap procedures were performed. The overall survival rate for free flaps was 95.9% (n = 505). Seventy-eight cases (14.8%) required re-exploration postoperatively. Out of the 78 re-explored flaps, 56 (71.7%) were successfully salvaged. There were no cases of failure without re-exploration. The average age of patients who underwent re-exploration was 34 years (min 7, max 67), and 67 patients were male. A total of 9 different types of free flaps were utilized in the procedures, with the anterolateral thigh flap being the most frequently applied. The highest re-exploration rates were observed in the osteocutaneous vascularized fibula flap (35.7%), (5/14), and the superficial circumflex iliac artery perforator flap (31%), (9/29) (Table 1). There were 3 cases with partial skin necrosis. One of them was healed by debridement and dressing changes, while the other 2 required small skin grafts.

Venous thrombosis was the most common cause of re-exploration in this study, accounting for 35 cases (44.9%) (Figure 1). Arterial thrombosis occurred in 19 cases (24.4%) (Figures 2 and 3), while hematoma formation was found in 12 cases (15.4%). Additionally, 12 cases (15.4%) showed no evidence of pedicle thrombosis or hematoma, resulting in negative explorations. Successful salvage rates were observed to be 54.3% (n = 19) for venous compromise and 63.2% (n = 12) for arterial compromise, though these differences were not statistically significant (P = .529).

The mean time to flap re-exploration after the initial surgical procedure was 24 hours, with a range spanning from 2 to 72 hours. The mean interval from the detection of flap compromise to the re-exploration was recorded at 4.5 hours, with a range of 1 to 12 hours. Furthermore, the mean time until the first signs of flap compromise were observed was 19.5 hours, with a range from 1 to 70 hours.

The first signs of flap compromise were observed in 91.1% (n = 71) of cases within the first 24 hours postoperatively. The times when flaps display initial findings of circulatory compromise within the first 24 hours are given in Figure 4. In 5.1% (n = 4) of cases, signs appeared between 24 and 48 hours, while 3.8% (n = 3) of cases exhibited signs of compromise between 48 and 72 hours. Notably, no instances of flap compromise were recorded after 72 hours.

In cases with flap loss, both the time from the initial surgery to re-exploration (12.2 vs 19.6 hours) and the time from the first clinical finding of compromise to re-exploration (2.5 vs 5.6 hours) were significantly longer compared to those flaps that survived (P = .02 and P < .001, respectively). Although the time to the first clinical finding after surgery was also higher in patients with flap loss (9.6 vs 14.2 hours), this difference was not statistically significant (P = .12).

In the cases of flap failure despite re-exploration, 22 patients underwent various procedures: for 4 patients with defects in their fingers, stump closure was performed; for 2 patients, finger shortening and skin grafting were applied. For 3 patients, debridement followed by negative pressure wound therapy and skin grafting was conducted. One patient underwent below-knee amputation, and 12 patients received new free flap procedures.

Discussion

The current study demonstrates that flap salvage rates are increased by early detection, early re-exploration, and effective management of compromised flaps. Smith and colleagues showed that the time from the initial operation to the re-examination was inversely proportional to the rate of flap salvage. They found that salvaged flaps were re-examined at a mean of 46.5 hours, and failed flaps were re-examined at a mean of 82 hours after the initial operation.4 However, they did not describe the time from detection of a compromised flap to re-exploration. In the case of a compromised flap, it is believed that the time from detection to re-exploration is very important. These findings support those of Bui and colleagues, who found that the median time from detection to re-exploration was 4 hours for salvaged flaps and 9 hours for failed flaps.5 It was found that the mean time from detection to re-exploration was 2.5 hours, and it was 12.2 hours from initial surgery in successfully salvaged cases. Based on these data, early re-exploration is recommended for compromised flaps, preferably within 2.5 hours after the first clinical finding.

The re-exploration rate was 14.8%, which was comparable to the previous reports in which the re-exploration rate was 6%-16.5%.4-6,12 The overall flap survival rate of 95.9% was similar to flap survival rates reported in other studies using different flap types.1,2,6,13 The salvage rate in compromised re-explored flaps is 71.7%, whereas this rate is reported between 33% and 95% in the literature.5,7,11,14,15 This salvage rate means that re-exploration surgery is beneficial to increase the overall survival rate of free flaps, in the authors’ opinion.

In this series of limb reconstructions, the majority of microvascular complications occur within the first 24 hours following the initial flap surgery. Vascular complications have not been observed after 72 hours post-procedure in the current series, as cited in certain reports.5,7 However, it has been documented that in head and neck reconstructions, vascular complications due to pedicle kinking or compression may emerge as a result of neck movement after the fifth postoperative day.5,16 These findings may prompt a discussion regarding the necessity of monitoring extremity flaps for a duration of 7 days.

The predominant cause for re-exploration in this study was identified as pedicle thrombosis. Specifically, flaps that required re-exploration due to pedicle thrombosis accounted for 10% of the total patient population. This finding aligns with existing literature, which reports a range of 4% to 9.9% for similar cases.5,13,17,18 Within this series, the salvage rate for cases of pedicle thrombosis was determined to be 57.4%, which is comparatively similar to the 34% to 73% salvage rates documented in the literature.5,13,17,18 In the observations, venous thrombosis was noted in 44.9% of the re-explored cases, whereas arterial thrombosis was recorded in 24.4%. Literature suggests that venous thrombosis occurs with a frequency that is 2.5 times or greater than that of arterial thrombosis.5,7 It is important to note, however, that previous studies have often combined data from head and neck, breast, and extremity reconstructions, which may influence the reported rates. In contrast, the current study focused exclusively on extremity reconstructions, which may explain the elevated rate of arterial thrombosis observed. It is hypothesized that this increased incidence is related to the fact that the majority of cases in this series were attributed to trauma, suggesting that the recipient artery may have been situated within the trauma zone, thereby increasing the risk of arterial thrombosis. Bui et al5 reported that the flap salvage rate is higher in cases of venous thrombosis. This finding contrasts with the current study’s results, which show a higher salvage rate in arterial thrombosis cases. It is believed that this discrepancy may also be attributed to differences in extremity cases.

In this study, the successful salvage rate of cases with vein grafts was 61.5% for venous-compromised cases and 75% for arterial-compromised cases. Numerous experts have indicated that the use of venous grafts in primary flap surgery is linked to elevated complication rates and increased flap loss.17-19 It is advocated that in primary surgical procedures, anastomoses should be performed without the use of venous grafts whenever feasible. However, in cases of re-exploration, as noted by Bui et al,5 it is proposed that venous grafts may be utilized if the geometry of the pedicle is meticulously adjusted between intact, healthy vessels, rather than resorting to a tense repair. Another important result of this study is that performing an additional venous anastomosis in the case of compromise resulted in a salvage rate of 71.4%. This rate was higher than the salvage rates of venous reanastomosis and venous repair with vein grafts. Therefore, it is recommended to first search for an additional vein if available, and then proceed with reanastomosis, with or without a vein graft, if no additional veins are found.

Bui et al5 reported 10 negative explorations, while there are 12 cases in the current series. They reported the reasons for the explorations as being to rule out venous thrombosis, to rule out arterial thrombosis, and to rule out hematoma. The cases are also re-explored to rule out these reasons, but it is very hard to distinguish between these and thrombotic cases, which is why there is eagerness to re-explore. Vasospasm is thought to be one of the reasons behind the negative explorations.

In this clinic, postoperative flap monitoring is conducted hourly by an experienced nursing staff using conventional methods (color, capillary refill, turgor, temperature, and pinprick test). With advancements in technology, Doppler (handheld or implantable), near-infrared spectroscopy, microdialysis, and laser Doppler have begun to be utilized for flap monitoring.7,20 However, data supporting the efficacy of these methods in increasing flap salvage rates is limited.21,22 Conventional methods of flap monitoring remain widely used and are considered the gold standard.7,22 Nevertheless, monitoring flaps using conventional methods requires experienced nursing staff and can place an additional burden on nurses and hospital personnel, particularly during the first 72 hours postoperatively. The rates of free flap re-exploration and salvage are comparable to the literature.5,7,11,14 Therefore, as noted by Chen et al,23 it is believed that hourly flap monitoring by specially trained nurses remains one of the most reliable methods. With technological advancements, the role of new techniques in flap monitoring and enabling early revision could increase, highlighting the need for further studies in this area.

This study has several limitations. It is a retrospective study. Although surgeons with considerable experience performed these surgeries together, there may have been re-explorations due to technical errors. However, these factors do not affect the primary objective of the study.

In conclusion, this study underscores the critical importance of timely intervention in the management of compromised flaps, demonstrating that early detection and re-exploration significantly enhance flap salvage rates. These findings indicate that the optimal window for re-exploration is approximately 2.5 hours from the detection of compromise, which aligns with existing literature that suggests a strong inverse relationship between the time to re-exploration and flap salvage success. Notably, the re-exploration rate of 14.8% and an overall flap survival rate of 95.9% are consistent with reported outcomes in the literature, reinforcing the efficacy of the monitoring protocols. The observations regarding the incidence of venous versus arterial thrombosis highlight the unique challenges associated with extremity reconstructions, particularly in trauma cases. Ultimately, the findings emphasize that a proactive approach to flap management, characterized by vigilant monitoring and prompt surgical intervention, is essential for optimizing patient outcomes in reconstructive microsurgery.
