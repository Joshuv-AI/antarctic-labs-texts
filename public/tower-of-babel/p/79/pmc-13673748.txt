ICU-onset atrial fibrillation surrogate, thrombocytopenia, and short-term outcomes in patients with cancer requiring intensive care: a retrospective ICU cohort study
Authors: Hasan Burak Isleyen, Sevil Tugrul Yavuz, Sercan Bulut, Fatih Kizkapan, Cevahir Alioglu, Erdem Sunger, Ertugrul Okuyan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCancer and atrial fibrillation frequently coexist. Intensive care adds hemodynamic stress, organ failure, and competing bleeding risk. Whether platelet-defined thrombocytopenia modifies the association between an ICU-onset atrial fibrillation surrogate and short-term outcomes in patients with cancer remains uncertain.MethodsWe performed a secondary analysis of a retrospective ICU cohort derived from MIMIC-IV version 3.1, a de-identified critical care database with date-shifted patient timelines. Adult ICU stays with solid or hematologic malignancy were included. The exposure was an ICU-onset AF surrogate rather than adjudicated incident AF. Early thrombocytopenia was defined from the minimum platelet count within 48 h and categorized as > = 150, 100–149, 50–99, and < 50 × 10^9/L. The primary outcome was in-hospital death. Secondary outcomes were 28-day death, major bleeding identified by ICD coding, and ischemic stroke identified by ICD coding. Multivariable logistic regression tested AF-by-platelet interaction. Sensitivity models added available cancer-site, cardiovascular comorbidity, major surgery, structured anticancer medication indicators, and anticoagulation dose-class variables.ResultsAmong 9,640 cancer ICU stays, 1,788 (18.5%) met criteria for the ICU-onset AF surrogate. In-hospital death occurred in 1,724 stays (17.9%), 28-day death in 2,394 (24.8%), ICD-coded major bleeding in 1,001 (10.4%), and ICD-coded ischemic stroke in 410 (4.3%). In primary complete-case interaction models (n = 9,505), the AF-by-platelet interaction for in-hospital death was borderline (P = 0.069), with the largest mortality signal in the platelet < 50 × 10^9/L stratum (adjusted OR 1.61, 95% CI 1.09–2.37). This signal persisted after expanded adjustment (adjusted OR 1.54, 95% CI 1.04–2.27) and anticoagulation dose-class adjustment (adjusted OR 1.50, 95% CI 1.01–2.22). For ICD-coded major bleeding, the interaction was significant in the primary model (P = 0.040) and remained similar after expanded adjustment (P = 0.050); the platelet 50–99 × 10^9/L stratum showed lower adjusted odds, a finding interpreted as exploratory.ConclusionsIn this cancer ICU cohort, an ICU-onset AF surrogate was associated with short-term outcome patterns that differed across platelet strata. Severe thrombocytopenia marked the subgroup with the clearest mortality signal. Because AF, bleeding, and stroke were defined from structured data rather than adjudicated events, the findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40959-026-00548-1.

Introduction

Cardio-oncology literature recognizes atrial fibrillation as a frequent complication across the cancer continuum [1]. The relation between cancer and atrial fibrillation is bidirectional and involves inflammation, autonomic stress, structural remodeling, and treatment-related triggers [2]. Meta-analytic data suggest that cancer survivors have a higher atrial fibrillation burden than the general population [3]. Contemporary management remains heterogeneous when active cancer, competing bleeding risk, and invasive strategies are considered [4]. Current reviews emphasize that cancer type, treatment exposure, and hemorrhagic risk should modify atrial fibrillation management decisions [5]. In active cancer with grade 3–4 thrombocytopenia, anticoagulation strategies remain uncertain [6]. Registry data show that long-term anticoagulation decisions in atrial fibrillation and cancer require balance between thromboembolic and hemorrhagic risk [7]. Contemporary Turkish AF registries also support a real-world setting in which anticoagulation patterns and residual risk vary outside trial populations [8, 9].

Critical care uses a different clinical time scale. Atrial fibrillation during critical illness is linked to short-term instability, thromboembolism, and mortality, but prediction and management remain less standardized than in ambulatory AF [10]. Recent MIMIC-IV data associated new-onset AF with higher mortality in critically ill patients [11]. The international AFIB-ICU cohort and contemporary ICU studies confirm that AF is frequent in mixed ICU populations and is associated with higher illness severity and adverse outcomes [12, 13]. A national multicenter prospective cohort showed heterogeneous ICU management of new-onset AF, and recent anticoagulation data indicate that stroke-prevention decisions in this setting remain unsettled [14, 15].

Patients with cancer who require intensive care sit at the intersection of these two contexts. Thrombocytopenia, competing bleeding risk, thromboembolic risk, acute illness severity, and cancer-related comorbidity must be considered together. The specific contribution of the present study is not that AF and mortality are generally related, but that an ICU-onset AF surrogate is evaluated within platelet-defined strata in a cancer ICU cohort. We examined whether this combined phenotype was associated with in-hospital death, 28-day death, ICD-coded major bleeding, and ICD-coded ischemic stroke.

Methods

Study design and data source

We performed a secondary analysis of a retrospective ICU cohort derived from MIMIC-IV version 3.1, a de-identified critical care database with date-shifted patient timelines [16, 17]. MIMIC-IV version 3.1 was released in October 2024; the v3.x release series extends the source data through 2022, but released database dates are shifted and do not represent actual calendar admission years. PhysioNet is the hosting resource for credentialed database access [18]. The analysis unit was the ICU stay. This study was reported in accordance with STROBE and RECORD principles.

Study population

Adult ICU stays with solid or hematologic malignancy were eligible. The analytic cohort contained 9,640 cancer ICU stays. Malignancy type was modeled with non-mutually exclusive binary indicators because 160 stays carried both solid and hematologic malignancy flags. Metastatic disease was retained as a separate binary variable.

Exposure definition

The main exposure was an ICU-onset AF surrogate. AF flags were generated from structured diagnosis-based AF coding and ICU rhythm-related records, with procedure records reviewed where applicable. Prior AF was operationalized from diagnosis-based AF coding available before or at index hospitalization coding capture. AF during ICU stay was operationalized from time-stamped ICU rhythm-related records during the ICU stay. ICU-onset AF surrogate was defined as AF identified during the ICU stay in the absence of a prior AF flag. We use the term surrogate because this definition does not represent adjudicated incident AF. Exploratory timing analyses used the first AF timestamp, AF within 24 h, AF within 48 h, and near-event physiology available in the timing extraction output.

Effect modifier and outcomes

The prespecified effect modifier was early thrombocytopenia, defined from the minimum platelet count within the first 48 h. Platelet strata were > = 150, 100–149, 50–99, and < 50 × 10^9/L. The primary outcome was in-hospital death. Secondary outcomes were 28-day death, major bleeding identified by ICD coding, and ischemic stroke identified by ICD coding. Twenty-eight-day death was defined as death occurring within 28 days after ICU admission. Supportive outcomes included RBC, platelet, and FFP transfusion within 48 h.

Covariates

The primary adjusted models included age, sex, solid malignancy, hematologic malignancy, metastatic disease, SOFA score, sepsis, vasopressor use, mechanical ventilation, CRRT, hemoglobin within 48 h, creatinine within 48 h, broad anticoagulation exposure within 48 h, and platelet category. Broad anticoagulation exposure was used as an adjustment variable and not as a marker of AF-specific treatment intensity.

Sensitivity models added cancer site group, heart failure, coronary artery disease or myocardial infarction, chronic kidney disease, prior stroke or transient ischemic attack, venous thromboembolism history, major surgery during the index hospitalization, structured anticancer medication indicators during the index hospitalization, and anticoagulation dose class during the first 48 h. Dose classes were none recorded, prophylactic, therapeutic, and uncertain dose. ECOG performance status, exact cancer stage, treatment intent, and precise therapy phase were not reliably available as structured MIMIC-IV variables and were not imputed.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range. Categorical variables are presented as number and percentage. Absolute standardized mean differences were used to describe between-group imbalance.

We used multivariable logistic regression for the primary and secondary outcomes because all endpoints were binary. The main exposure was ICU-onset AF surrogate. Platelet category was evaluated as an effect modifier by including AF-by-platelet interaction terms. Missingness in variables entering the primary models was low: 0.0% for age, sex, malignancy subtype, metastatic disease, SOFA score, sepsis, vasopressor use, mechanical ventilation, CRRT, and anticoagulation exposure; 1.3% for hemoglobin within 48 h; 1.1% for creatinine within 48 h; and 1.3% for platelet category. Primary complete-case analysis yielded 9,505 stays.

INR, aPTT, and lactate were summarized descriptively and were not included in the primary adjusted models. We did not perform a formal statistical test of the missing-data mechanism. Complete-case analyses should therefore be interpreted with this limitation in mind.

Major bleeding and ischemic stroke were used as ICD-coded hospitalization indicators rather than adjudicated clinical events. We did not perform internal chart validation. Prior literature supports acceptable validity for stroke ascertainment with administrative codes in some settings, although performance depends on code position and case definition. Validation data for major bleeding codes are more heterogeneous. Therefore, these endpoints were interpreted as structured outcome indicators.

All data processing, statistical analyses, and figure generation were performed in Python using pandas, NumPy, statsmodels, and matplotlib. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals were reported. Two-sided p values were calculated. We did not apply formal multiplicity correction; interaction and sensitivity findings were interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Ethics

The source databases were approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, and informed consent was waived because all protected health information had been removed. The study plan was additionally approved by the Academic Board of the Department of Cardiology, Bezmialem Vakif University Faculty of Medicine (meeting no. 3, decision no. 11, dated 12 March 2026).

Results

A total of 9,640 cancer ICU stays were included. Solid malignancy was present in 7,856 stays, hematologic malignancy in 1,944 stays, and 160 stays carried both flags. ICU-onset AF surrogate was present in 1,788 stays (18.5%). In-hospital death occurred in 1,724 stays (17.9%), 28-day death in 2,394 stays (24.8%), major bleeding in 1,001 stays (10.4%), and ischemic stroke in 410 stays (4.3%).

Cohort assembly and analytic subsets are shown in Fig. 1. Patients with the ICU-onset AF surrogate were older and had higher illness severity than patients without the surrogate. Baseline distributions are shown in Table 1. Expanded structured covariates showed measurable cardiovascular comorbidity and treatment-context imbalance, including heart failure, CAD/MI, CKD, major surgery, cancer site group, and anticoagulation dose class. Cancer-site and anticoagulation dose-class distributions are provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2, and missingness is summarized in Supplementary Table 3.

After expanded adjustment, the platelet < 50 × 10^9/L mortality signal persisted with attenuation (adjusted OR 1.54, 95% CI 1.04–2.27; P for AF-by-platelet interaction = 0.087). A dose-class sensitivity model yielded a similar estimate (adjusted OR 1.50, 95% CI 1.01–2.22; P for AF-by-platelet interaction = 0.108). Sensitivity models are summarized in Fig. 4. Full model summaries and stratum-specific sensitivity estimates are provided in Supplementary Tables 5 and 6, and the coefficient audit is provided in Supplementary Table 7.

Sensitivity analysis summary across expanded adjustment and anticoagulation dose-class models

For 28-day death, the primary AF-by-platelet interaction did not meet the prespecified threshold (P = 0.071). The platelet < 50 × 10^9/L subgroup again had the largest point estimate in the primary model (adjusted OR 1.41, 95% CI 0.97–2.06), but the expanded adjustment estimate was imprecise (adjusted OR 1.37, 95% CI 0.94–2.01).

For ICD-coded major bleeding, platelet category modified the association in the primary model (P = 0.040). In the platelet 50–99 × 10^9/L stratum, the adjusted OR was 0.53 (95% CI 0.31–0.93). This inverse association remained similar after expanded adjustment adjustment (adjusted OR 0.54, 95% CI 0.31–0.94; P for interaction = 0.050) and dose-class sensitivity analysis (adjusted OR 0.51, 95% CI 0.29–0.89; P for interaction = 0.047). Because bleeding was ICD-coded and treatment selection was not randomized, this result was interpreted as exploratory.

For ICD-coded ischemic stroke, the primary model showed higher adjusted odds in the platelet > = 150 × 10^9/L stratum (adjusted OR 1.66, 95% CI 1.23–2.25), but the AF-by-platelet interaction was not significant (P = 0.113). Expanded adjustment stroke models had wider confidence intervals, consistent with lower event counts and sensitivity to additional covariates. Stroke findings were therefore treated as secondary and hypothesis-generating.

Exploratory timing analyses were limited to stays with a recorded first AF timestamp. Among 1,788 ICU-onset AF surrogate stays, 841 had the first AF episode within 24 h. Timing distributions and near-event physiology are shown in Supplementary Table 4.

Discussion

In this cancer ICU cohort, an ICU-onset AF surrogate was common and was associated with short-term outcome patterns that differed across platelet strata. The clearest mortality signal appeared in severe thrombocytopenia. This signal persisted after adding sensitivity cardiovascular comorbidity, cancer site group, major surgery, structured anticancer medication indicators, and anticoagulation dose class. The study therefore supports a cautious platelet-stratified interpretation of ICU AF signals rather than a claim that AF alone determines outcome.

This framing addresses an important distinction between cardio-oncology and critical care. Cardio-oncology often evaluates atrial fibrillation across the cancer continuum, including longer-term treatment effects and anticoagulation decisions. Critical care evaluates acute physiologic stress, organ failure, and immediate competing risks. These perspectives are complementary rather than conflicting. The present analysis is positioned within the ICU time scale, where AF may reflect acute severity, substrate vulnerability, treatment exposure, and hemodynamic stress.

Severe thrombocytopenia may mark a biologically plausible high-risk phenotype. In the ICU, low platelet counts can reflect marrow suppression, infection, coagulopathy, treatment toxicity, liver dysfunction, or multiorgan failure. International ICU thrombocytopenia data show that platelet transfusion practice varies, and adult ICU platelet-transfusion data support treatment-context heterogeneity in thrombocytopenic patients [19, 20]. AF-specific thrombocytopenia literature links low platelet counts with bleeding, vascular events, and treatment uncertainty, which supports the present platelet-stratified interpretation without establishing causality [21, 22]. The persistence of the severe-thrombocytopenia mortality signal after expanded adjustment suggests that the combined AF-thrombocytopenia phenotype deserves targeted validation, although residual confounding cannot be excluded.

The bleeding findings require restraint. The platelet 50–99 × 10^9/L stratum showed lower adjusted odds of ICD-coded major bleeding among stays with the AF surrogate. This pattern should not be interpreted as protection. AFIRE and DOAC registry evidence indicate that thrombocytopenia modifies bleeding-risk assessment and anticoagulation decisions in AF populations [23, 24]. In the present ICU cohort, the inverse bleeding signal may reflect administrative coding, competing mortality, treatment withholding, transfusion practice, selection into anticoagulation strategies, or unmeasured differences in bleeding ascertainment. The result is best viewed as a signal that thrombocytopenia modifies the observed relationship between AF documentation, treatment context, and coded bleeding outcomes.

The ischemic stroke findings are also secondary. The primary model showed higher adjusted odds in the platelet > = 150 × 10^9/L stratum, consistent with temporal AF-stroke literature and residual stroke risk reported among anticoagulated AF patients [25, 26]. However, stroke events were fewer than mortality events, and expanded adjustment models widened confidence intervals. The analysis cannot determine whether AF preceded stroke, whether anticoagulation decisions mediated risk, or whether coded stroke represented new clinical events.

The clinical implication is risk interpretation rather than direct treatment guidance. Short-term and longer-term outcomes after ICU admission remain heterogeneous in patients with solid and hematologic malignancies [27, 28]. CAR-T hospitalization data also identify AF as a marker of acute cardiovascular deterioration in oncology care [29]. In cancer ICU patients, an ICU-onset AF surrogate should prompt reassessment of illness severity, platelet trajectory, bleeding risk, thromboembolic risk, recent surgery, cardiovascular comorbidity, and cancer-treatment context. Contemporary AF guidance favors dynamic risk reassessment, which fits a platelet-informed but non-prescriptive interpretation of these findings [30]. Anticoagulation decisions require individualized evaluation; the present data cannot establish therapeutic benefit or harm. Cardio-oncology consultation may be most useful when AF occurs with severe thrombocytopenia, active bleeding concern, cardiotoxic therapy exposure, or competing thrombotic risk.

This study has limitations. The design was retrospective. The exposure was a structured ICU-onset AF surrogate rather than adjudicated incident AF, so misclassification is possible. Major bleeding and ischemic stroke were ICD-coded hospitalization indicators rather than adjudicated clinical endpoints. The broad anticoagulation variable and dose-class sensitivity analysis were derived from structured medication records and cannot capture AF-specific indication, treatment intent, temporary holding, adherence, or clinician reasoning. ECOG performance status, exact cancer stage, treatment intent, treatment phase, and treatment-related cytopenia mechanism were not reliably available as structured MIMIC-IV variables. Residual confounding by cancer biology, prior therapy, perioperative status, cardiovascular comorbidity severity, and indication for anticoagulation remains possible. The timing analysis was limited by sparse first-AF timestamps and was exploratory. Complete-case analyses assumed that missing covariate data did not materially alter the estimates, but we did not prove the missing-data mechanism. Multiplicity correction was not applied, and interaction findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. External transportability remains untested and should be examined in multicenter ICU resources with oncology-relevant variables.

Despite these limitations, the cohort size, event counts, and sensitivity analyses support a restrained conclusion: platelet-defined strata help contextualize ICU-onset AF signals in critically ill patients with cancer.

Conclusions

An ICU-onset AF surrogate was common in patients with cancer requiring intensive care. Platelet-defined strata differentiated short-term mortality and ICD-coded bleeding patterns. Severe thrombocytopenia marked the subgroup with the clearest mortality signal, but the findings remain observational and hypothesis-generating. Future studies should use adjudicated rhythm, bleeding, and thromboembolic outcomes and should incorporate cancer stage, functional status, and treatment phase when these data are available.
