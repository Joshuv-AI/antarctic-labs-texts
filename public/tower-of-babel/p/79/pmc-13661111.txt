Open-label placebo treatment for acute postoperative pain (OLP-POP study): a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Dilan Sezer, Matthijs de Leeuw, Andrea H. Meyer, Sarah Buergler, Cordula Netzer, Cosima Locher, Jens Gaab, Wilhelm Ruppen, Tobias Schneider
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

1. Introduction

Postoperative pain constitutes a significant concern within the field of postsurgical care, with up to 86% of patients reporting pain as a prevalent symptom. Of these, 75% describe the pain as moderate, severe, or extreme.20 Even with analgesic medication, 82% of patients suffer from pain, and 79% have at least one adverse effect.20 Consequently, there is a lack of adequate pain relief and a need for improvement of postoperative analgesia.2,22,33,50

Postoperative pain is not only a problem in its own right but is also a significant risk factor for the development of chronic pain.18 Furthermore, it significantly affects patients' quality of life19,58,65 and can cause physiological changes, contributing to higher morbidity and mortality.19,30

In the context of the ongoing opioid crisis,26,40,48 the use of multimodal analgesia and opioid-minimizing concepts37,53,62 in pain therapy is crucial. However, because of the controversial role of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs in postoperative pain therapy after spinal surgery,38 especially in the elderly population with comorbidities limiting their use,11,21,35,46,59,61,66 opioids continue to have a firm place as systemic analgesics in patients receiving dorsal lumbar interbody fusion (dLIF) surgery,24,25,59 although opioids have contraindications and dose-dependent side effects of their own.1,13

Thus, effective and safe therapeutic alternatives for multimodal analgesia, which could potentially reduce opioid dosage and thereby side effects, are warranted. Open-label placebo may represent such an option as patients benefit from the analgesic properties of placebos4,17 in an ethical manner.6,15 Several studies have shown that open-label placebos (OLPs) can elicit statistically significant and clinically relevant effects on various conditions (standardized mean difference = 0.72; standardized mean difference = 0.45–0.87 for pain),9,64 in both chronic3,10,29,32,49 as well as in acute pain.14,34,39,45,55,63

In this regard, Morales-Quezada et al.44 found significant effects of conditioned OLPs on pain control and opioid doses in patients with spinal cord injury and polytrauma, and Flowers et al.16 observed an opioid dose reduction in patients receiving conditioned OLPs after spinal surgery. With this randomized controlled trial, we examined whether OLPs reduce opioid consumption in patients suffering from postoperative pain after dLIF surgery.

2. Methods

Detailed methodology of this study has been published elsewhere.56 In the following, the applied methods in conducting this study will therefore be described in brief with any protocol deviations mentioned.

2.1. Study design

We conducted a 5-day randomized controlled trial at a single academic medical center from May 2020 to February 2023 in Switzerland. Patients were randomly assigned to 2 groups: (1) an OLP intervention group and (2) a treatment as usual (TAU) group serving as control. The recruitment ended when the prespecified N was reached. The study was carried out following the protocol and principles enunciated in the current version of the Declaration of Helsinki. It was prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04339023; 12/05/2020). The ethics committee “Ethikkommission Nordwest- und Zentralschweiz” (BASEC2020-00099) approved the study protocol.

2.2. Eligibility and recruitment

All adult patients undergoing elective dLIF surgery at the University Hospital Basel during the study period were screened for eligibility. To ensure comparability in wound surface and surgical trauma, eligible procedures were prespecified in the protocol.56 Anesthesia was performed according to the in-house standard protocol as a total intravenous anesthesia using propofol and the opioids fentanyl and remifentanil. Patients had to be German speaking and currently free of any psychological disorder (as defined by an International Classification of Diseases-10 diagnosis of chapter F) and somatic conditions (both assessed by screening of inhouse digital patient charts) that could affect pain perception or contraindicate morphine use (see Ref. 56 for more details on the in- and exclusion criteria). Those with preoperative opioid consumption exceeding 30 mg/d (oral morphine equivalent [ME]) were excluded. Eligibility was assessed through electronic hospital records and patient self-report during in-hospital or telephone screening.

2.3. Study procedure and assessments

The study timeline included assessments and interventions from the day before surgery (T-1) to the third day postsurgery (T5). At T-1, after signing informed consent (IC), patients completed questionnaires on anxiety, depression, beliefs about placebos and negative attitudes toward opioid consumption, pain, and postoperative pain expectations. On day 1 postsurgery (T0), a blinded study team member assessed baseline pain shortly before 09:00 am (ie, 5–30 minutes in advance), and patients were randomized into study groups by a specialized pain nurse. Directly afterward (ie, 0–15 minutes), the nurse checked and adjusted the patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) morphine pump, assessed baseline early postoperative morphine consumption, revealed treatment allocation, and, for the OLP group, performed the experimental intervention. Again, directly afterward, patients in both groups rated their treatment expectancy (T1), assessed by a blinded study team member. At T2 (day 1, 04:00 pm), T3 (day 2, 09:00 am), and T4 (day 2, 04:00 pm), the PCA pump was checked by a pain nurse, morphine consumption assessed, and for OLP patients, the intervention repeated. Pain and pain expectancy were reassessed by a blinded study team member immediately after each pain nurse visit. At T5 (day 3, 09:00 am), the PCA pump was checked by a pain nurse, morphine consumption was assessed, and directly afterward, questionnaires were completed (assessed by a blinded study team member) without further OLP intervention (Fig. 1).

2.4. Intervention

2.4.1. Control intervention (treatment as usual)

Following randomization, all participants of the TAU group continued with postoperatively started TAU and prestudy concomitant medication. TAU included oral analgesia (3 g of paracetamol daily), a PCA morphine pump, and rescue medication with metamizole or ibuprofen (if metamizole was contraindicated). The PCA delivered 2 mg of morphine per button press, with a 12-minute lockout, adjustable per protocol if rescue medication was ineffective or side effects occurred. To prevent disappointment and ensure comparable interaction time with pain nurses, control group patients were informed about the importance of their role (see supplementary material of Ref. 56).

2.4.2. Experimental intervention (open-label placebo)

All participants in the OLP group received TAU as described above. In addition, they received an experimental OLP intervention. This intervention consisted of 2 components: a treatment rationale and 2 OLP injections each on day 1 and day 2 postoperatively (ie, a total of 4 placebo injections), which were provided by a pain nurse. The treatment rationale was in line with previous studies10,31 and included an explanation of placebo effects and how placebos may decrease postoperative pain (for more details see Ref. 56). This rationale was provided in detail before the first injection and was repeated in brief before each subsequent injection. Placebo injections consisted of 5-mL syringes containing 5 mL of saline 0.9%. The syringes were labeled as placebos. The saline was administered intravenously. Pain nurses, who delivered the intervention and the rationale, ensured that patients watched the injection being applied.

2.5. Randomization and blinding

An independent hospital staff generated random treatment allocations using a computer-generated sequence. Sequentially numbered opaque envelopes assigned participants to the groups, with randomization performed in blocks of 10 to ensure equal distribution. As an open-label trial, participant blinding was not possible, but the primary outcome (morphine dose) was not explicitly disclosed to either group. Outcome assessors remained blinded to group allocation throughout the study, and hospital staff uninvolved in the study were also unaware of treatment assignments.

2.6. Outcome measures

The primary study outcome was assessed using the cumulative dose of self-administered morphine within 48 hours starting in the morning of day 1 postsurgery and ending in the morning of day 3 postsurgery.

Secondary outcomes comprised (1) patients' morphine demand (assessed by the total number of patients' morphine requests during the blocking time of the PCA pump), (2) back and leg pain intensity at rest (timeframe: “current pain”; assessed using two 11-point numeric rating scales [NRS])7,8 and while walking (time frame: “today”; assessed by 2 NRS if patients were up walking), (3) comprehensive pain intensity (ie, severe, maximum, or minimum pain), frequency and duration of pain, interference, satisfaction with pain therapy, as well as side effects of pain medication (time frame: “last 24 hours”; assessed by the German version of the International Pain Outcomes Questionnaire [IPO]),54 (4) amount and dosage of requested rescue analgesics, and (5) length of postsurgery hospitalization.

Other variables of interest, such as pain catastrophizing (assessed by the German version of the pain catastrophizing scale)42 and negative attitudes toward opioid consumption (assessed by a 10-item questionnaire assessing beliefs regarding opioids),36 and several other potential predictors of OLP response were assessed at T-1.56

All assessments were completed by patients themselves digitally on a tablet. Assessments were supervised by a blinded study team member, who assisted when help was needed with the understanding of the questions or for technical reasons.

2.7. Statistical analyses

We conducted a power calculation based on an F-test for an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) for 2 groups and 2 covariates (see description of covariates below), resulting in a sample size of n = 64 for a power of 0.8 to detect a medium to large effect size of d = 0.635. We decided to enroll a total sample size of 70 (ie, 35 per group) to account for 10% drop-out (see published protocol).56

Because of a small amount of missing data (N = 6 out of 71; 8.45%), we refrained from the planned data imputation and performed the analyses only with those participants who completed study participation.

To examine differences in the total amount of morphine consumed throughout the intervention period between the groups, we conducted a 1-way ANCOVA, with baseline early postoperative morphine consumption (total amount of PCA-administered morphine from surgery to first placebo application) and patients' history of morphine consumption before the study's start (ME in mg of prescribed opioids before surgery) as continuous covariates, and treatment group as a dichotomous between-subject factor. In addition, we tested whether there was a difference in morphine consumption over time by performing a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with morphine consumption as a dichotomous dependent variable (yes/no), group as a between-subjects factor, and time as a within-subjects predictor, including the interaction time × group.

Originally planned analyses of root mean square of statistical differences data were not feasible due to the unexpectedly low number of overall morphine requests.

Analyses of secondary outcomes focused on the identification of group differences by conducting t-tests and, where applicable, ANCOVAs that included relevant covariates (such as baseline variables). Initially planned self-reported bihourly assessments of pain intensity could not be analyzed, due to too much missing data. Explorative regression analyses of potential predictors of morphine consumption (eg, pain catastrophizing) and sensitivity analyses of potential other factors, such as age and gender, that could correlate with morphine consumption were performed (see supplement for the results, http://links.lww.com/PR9/A381). Because of missingness in predictors, the degrees of freedom in these analyses vary. In addition, to assess the time course of a possible OLP effect, we also conducted exploratory analyses of morphine consumption at smaller time intervals.

All analyses were performed using R, version 4.3.2.51 For all analyses, the significance level was set at α = 5%. Unless indicated, all results shown are means ± standard deviations (SD). For linear models, betas (estimates of slopes) and their respective standard errors (SE) are reported. For group differences, Cohen d was used to assess the effect sizes of categorical predictors.

3. Results

3.1. Sample characteristics and study flow

As shown in Figure 1, of the 341 screened patients, 265 did not fulfill the inclusion criteria, 63 did not want to participate, and 5 could not be included due to organizational problems. Of those who did not want to participate, 43 did not state the reason for their nonparticipation or could not be reached, 13 stated that they would be overwhelmed by study participation, and 7 stated that they did not want to consume opioids. The remaining 76 participants signed IC and completed the presurgery assessment (T-1). Before randomization, 5 participants were excluded: 4 due to medical or psychological conditions that occurred or were noticed perioperatively, and 1 participant withdrew IC. Thus, as specified by the study protocol,56 71 participants were randomized. Out of those, 6 had to stop study participation prematurely: 2 because they did not want to participate any longer and 4 due to medical or psychological complications. Thus, a total of 65 participants were included in the analyses (TAU = 32, OLP = 33).

Table 1 depicts participants' demographic and baseline characteristics. Participants' ages ranged from 32 to 88 years (mean ± SD: 68.28 ± 11.39 years). Surgical attributes, including intraoperative opioids, diagnosis leading to surgical indication, and levels of fusion, were comparable between the groups (Table 2). All surgical indications were due to degenerative pathologies; no acute herniations were included due to the nature of recruitment through planned surgeries.

3.2. Primary analyses with morphine consumption

Contrary to our expectations, the OLP group did not consume less but more morphine than the TAU group across the 48-hour intervention period (see Table 3 and Fig. 2a). Therefore, the planned 1-tailed test yielded a nonsignificant result (estimate = -6.94, SE = 5.75, t[60] = -1.21, P = 0.880, d = -0.15). For completeness, the 2-sided P value was 0.233. Because the direction of the effect was opposite to that hypothesized, the latter result should be interpreted with caution and does not support the predicted effect. However, the GLMM displayed a small linear trend for decreased morphine consumption irrespective of group allocation across time (estimate = −0.27, SE = 0.15, z = −1.78, P = 0.075).

3.3. Secondary analyses

Descriptive means of all secondary outcomes can be found in Table 3.

3.3.1. Demand (additional morphine requests during patient-controlled analgesia blocking time)

Because of the very skewed distribution of the outcome, we log-transformed the outcome before the analysis. There was no difference in the total number of morphine requests during the PCA blocking phase after a bolus delivery over the 48-hour intervention period (estimate = 0.20, SE = 0.30, t[60] = 0.68, P = 0.512).

3.3.2. Pain intensity at rest and while walking

Mean postoperative pain intensities for current back and leg pain at rest and mean postoperative pain intensity on this day while moving are displayed in Table 3 and are plotted in the supplement, http://links.lww.com/PR9/A381. There were no statistically significant differences in pain intensity between the groups, whether in the back at rest (estimate = 0.24, SE = 0.36, t[55] = 0.67, P = 0.505), in the back during movement (estimate = 0.79, SE = 0.55, t[57] = 1.46, P = 0.150), in the legs at rest (estimate = 0.04, SE = 0.27, t[55] = 0.14, P = 0.889), or in the legs during movement (estimate = −0.53, SE = 0.44, t[57] = −1.2, P = 0.234). For each of these variables, preoperative pain values and postoperative baseline values were predictors for the corresponding postoperative pain values during the 48-hour intervention period.

There was a low-to-moderate correlation between pain intensity and morphine consumption (pain at rest in the back: r = 0.48, pain at rest in the legs: r = 0.45, pain while moving in the back: r = 0.42, and pain while moving in the legs: r = 0.27).

3.3.3. Comprehensive pain assessment and patients' perception of postoperative pain management

Regarding the comprehensive pain assessment that was performed using the IPO, we did not find a group difference concerning the frequency of time within the last 24 hours the patients experienced severe (estimate = 5.64, SE = 4.96, t[56] = 1.14, P = 0.260), maximum (estimate = −0.04, SE = 0.58, t[56] = −0.08, P = 0.939), or minimum (estimate = −0.16, SE = 0.39, t[56] = −0.41, P = 0.686) pain or their satisfaction with pain therapy (estimate = 0.43, SE = 0.58, t[51] = 0.74, P = 0.464).

3.3.4. Opioid-related side effects

Baseline controlled analyses of side effect intensity (NRS 0–10) assessed by the IPO questionnaire (nausea, drowsiness, itch, and dizziness) revealed no group difference (estimate = −0.25, SE = 0.44, t[58] = −0.57, P = 0.573).

3.3.5. Hospitalization duration

No group difference in duration of hospitalization was detected in baseline early postoperative morphine consumption and preoperative opioid consumption-controlled analysis (estimate = −0.44, SE = 0.76, t[61] = −0.57, P = 0.569).

3.3.6. Rescue medication

No group difference in the number of rescue medication requests was detected in baseline rescue medication request controlled analysis (estimate = −0.35, SE = 0.38, t[62] = −0.92, P = 0.359).

3.4. Explorative analyses

To assess the correlation between the known predictors of postoperative pain (eg, pain catastrophizing) and morphine consumption, exploratory analyses were performed with different predictors to assess their association with the primary outcome of morphine consumption. Furthermore, to identify potential patient characteristics that could benefit from OLP (eg, age, gender, etc), we included interaction terms in the model to identify potential moderators. The results of these analyses can be found in the supplement, http://links.lww.com/PR9/A381. Also, results on pain expectancy are shown in the supplement, http://links.lww.com/PR9/A381.

4. Discussion

We investigated whether OLPs can reduce opioid consumption in the postoperative treatment of patients undergoing dLIF surgery. We found no significant difference between the TAU and OLP group in morphine consumption during the 48-hour intervention period. We also found no differences in the time course of consumption between the groups. Likewise, no group differences in reported pain intensity or other secondary outcomes were detected. Regarding the predictors of morphine consumption (see supplement for results, http://links.lww.com/PR9/A381), we found that higher pain catastrophizing and intraoperative remifentanil dosage led to more morphine consumption postoperatively.

The results obtained do not support our hypothesis that 4 doses of intravenously nurse-administered OLPs reduce PCA-administered morphine consumption in the first 2 days after dLIF surgery. However, our results in both groups are consistent with established research findings on the importance of psychosocial predictors for postoperative pain and analgesic consumption.43,57,60

This study may have missed a true OLP effect in the early postoperative period of dLIF patients due to population characteristics. Positive effects of OLPs on acute pain have primarily been observed in younger populations in experimental studies.5,14,34,39,45,55 Research on deceptive and open-label placebos shows that placebo responses can decrease with age.16,23,27,47,52 However, older adults can benefit from OLP treatment for chronic knee pain.49 In this study, the high mean age of 68.28 years (SD = 11.39) in combination with the cognitive challenging early postoperative phase, which could explain why there were no differences in expected pain ratings across groups (see supplement for results, http://links.lww.com/PR9/A381), may have reduced the likelihood to harvest a placebo effect. However, there was no moderating effect of age on the group effect (see supplement for results, http://links.lww.com/PR9/A381). In addition, our patients underwent surgery due to chronic degenerative diseases, meaning the early postoperative phase may be a complex mix up of acute and chronic pain also influencing the placebo effect.

Furthermore, the measurement period ended on the third day after surgery, although Flowers et al.16 found lower morphine doses in the OLP group only from the fourth day postsurgery onward. Thus, our results are consistent with those of Flowers et al.,16 showing no OLP effect on opioid consumption in the early postoperative phase. Interestingly, the 48-hour morphine consumption (37.7 mg for OLP, 33.5 mg for TAU) equated to about 6 mg every 8 hours—a relatively low dose, suggesting that pain in the early postoperative phase was tolerable. Accordingly, experienced postoperative pain intensity and demands of rescue analgesics were low, which reduced assay sensitivity to detect potential differences. Next, we used intravenous saline placebos instead of the OLP pills typical in most studies.9 Although invasive placebos often elicit stronger effects,12,41 in line with Flowers et al.,16 who used unlike us conditioned OLP pills, we found no OLP effect in the early postoperative phase. Finally, administering OLP injections at fixed times by nurses may have influenced results. Evidence suggests that placebo analgesia is enhanced when patients have control over treatment,62 particularly when viewed through the definition of health as the ability to adapt and self-manage by Huber et al.28 Fixed timing may also have misaligned placebo delivery with patients' analgesic needs. Fourth, in our study, we used a different OLP paradigm than Flowers et al.16 They employed conditioning, pairing OLP pills with analgesics to induce expectations, whereas we used verbal suggestion only—a common method in OLP research.9 We chose this approach to test its feasibility in acute postoperative settings. Although we observed no difference in OLP effectiveness compared with Flowers et al. during the first 2 postoperative days, it remains to be seen if the results of Flowers et al., who found effects starting on day 4 after surgery, can be replicated in future studies.

4.1. Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including low reporting bias, high data quality, minimal missing data, standardized procedures, and digital data collection on-site. We also demonstrated the clinical feasibility of integrating an OLP-injection protocol into standard postoperative pain management. Researcher allegiance bias was minimized by having pain nurses deliver the intervention without involvement in the study's design or publication, enhancing external validity.

Besides the brief treatment period, a major limitation is the lack of practitioner blinding, inherent to OLP studies. However, other practitioners, including outcome assessors, were blinded, reducing reporting bias. Additionally, although validated questionnaires were used, key factors influencing placebo responses, such as postoperative cognitive performance and non–pain-related morphine desires, were not assessed. Finally, although the sample size was based on available empirical findings, our overall N should be considered as a possible limitation.

5. Conclusion

This study investigated whether 4 intravenous OLP injections can reduce PCA-administered morphine consumption on the first and second day after dLIF surgery. In our elderly study sample, we did not find lower morphine consumption or pain intensity in the OLP group compared with the TAU group. With this finding, the study provides interesting insights into the use of OLP in clinical practice and into important study design features for future studies in this field. First, it is important to be aware of the population treated with OLP, as the results of experimental studies with young participants may not be transferable to older and clinical populations. Second, our data raise questions about the applied OLP methodology in this specific population (ie, conditioning vs verbal suggestion), the time frame of observation (only 2 days postoperatively), and the mode of administration (ie, self-administered vs nurse-administered and pills vs intravenous injections) to observe OLP effects in acute pain after dLIF surgery. Further research is needed to confirm the findings and investigate the methodological questions raised by this study. In particular, it would be interesting to investigate whether injected OLPs could be effective in longer observation periods, as Flowers et al.16 have shown for conditioned OLPs in a similar population.
