Case study of PEPFAR funding cuts on HIV infections and deaths: reversing hard-won gains
Authors: Tsitsi Apollo, Yao-Rui Yeo, Owen Macdonald Mugurungi, Ngwarai Sithole, Isaac Taramusi, Emmanuel Tachiwenyika, Emily Prisca Gwavava, Wanzirai Makoni, Anesu Chimwaza, Kudakwashe Collin Takarinda, Getrude Ncube, Mellesia Jeetoo, Dyanna Charles, R Scott Braithwaite
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUsing a validated HIV transmission model for Zimbabwe, we simulated January 2025 U.S. President's Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR) funding cuts’ impacts on HIV incidence and HIV-related deaths. We found extending funding cuts through 2030 would increase HIV incidence by 78% and add 85 000 infections, producing 25 000 additional HIV-related deaths. However, if PEPFAR reinstated funding within 12 months, much or all of this harm could be offset through more efficient resource allocation, specifically, reallocating funds for oral PrEP towards long-acting PrEP.

Introduction

The U.S. President's Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR) has saved millions of lives globally, reducing HIV incidence in Zimbabwe by 80% since 2010, and raising life expectancy from 46 years in 2000 to 62 years in 2023 [1]. Over the past 5 years, the authors have conducted Participatory Policy-guided Modeling (PPM) to inform resource allocation across a broad suite of HIV treatment and prevention programs by Zimbabwe Ministry of Health and Child Care (MOHCC) and its major external funders. PPM is a structured, implementation-focused adaptation of decision analytic modeling in which decision-makers and other stakeholders frame model questions, select model inputs, and iteratively review model results [2–5]. Unanticipated PEPFAR funding cuts starting January 25, 2025 [6] required an urgent modeling update to project their impact on future HIV incidence and deaths, and extent to which funding restoration could mitigate this impact.

Methods

Using a validated HIV transmission model developed with MOHCC and its partners [7], we predicted HIV outcomes through 2030 with versus without PEPFAR funding cuts. Outcomes of interest were increase in HIV incidence rate, number of infections added, and expected increases in mortality from added infections. Model inputs regarding effects of interventions on HIV incidence, detection, treatment initiation, and viral suppression; and intervention coverage levels prior to PEPFAR funding cuts, were similar to prior published work [7], and were based on published studies assessed through the PPM process as meeting criteria of acceptable internal validity and transportability to Zimbabwe. As per standard practice in allocative efficiency analysis, costs of pharmaceuticals and other interventions were assessed based on opportunity costs rather than charges (e.g. negotiated price rather than asking price).

We explored various restoration scenarios for PEPFAR funding regarding timing (3 months, 12 months, and beyond 2030) and extent (USAID-implemented, CDC-implemented, or both). In scenarios in which PEPFAR funds were reinstated within 12 months, we also explored whether a more efficient re-allocation of those resources could mitigate the harm from funding interruption. We focused on one particular high-value reallocation identified in prior modeling simulations, reallocating funds for oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) towards long-acting injectable PrEP, because in “real world” settings, oral PrEP often has low daily adherence and a 12-month retention of 10%-30% [8–12], whereas injectable PrEP does not require daily adherence and its 12-month retention is 60%-90% [13], consistent with Zimbabwean programmatic data.

We made two other simplifying assumptions: funding cessations and resumptions would be instantaneous, ignoring stockpiles of equipment and frictional costs for deactivating and activating labor, and treatment effectiveness would exhibit constant returns to scale (e.g. 80% funding produces 80% effect).

We stratified results by funding cuts involving USAID- versus CDC-implemented interventions, using programmatic data and expert opinion gathered during an in-person workshop in Harare, Zimbabwe on March 3–4, 2025 (Fig. 1a). In circumstances where USAID funds and CDC funds are both required for program implementation, we assumed that discontinuation of either or both would diminish coverage by the same amount. We assumed that other funding would be maintained (e.g. the Global Fund) and that domestic resources would be redeployed whenever possible to minimize gaps in funding and implementation.

Results

Persistent PEPFAR funding cuts would increase HIV incidence by 78% in 2030 and add 85 000 additional infections through 2030, resulting in 25 000 additional deaths from those infections (Fig. 1b). Resuming PEPFAR funding cuts after 12 months would increase HIV incidence by 23% in 2030 and add 35 000 additional infections through 2030, resulting in 10 000 additional deaths. Resuming PEPFAR funding cuts after 3 months would increase HIV incidence by 13% in 2030 and add 15 000 additional infections through 2030, resulting in 4000 additional deaths. Approximately 60% of added infections are attributable to decreases in ART supply, and approximately 80% of added infections are attributable to USAID cuts alone (Fig. 1c).

We estimate PEPFAR funding cuts reduce programmatic funding by approximately $221 M per year, with USAID contributing $178 M, CDC contributing $48 M, plus a $5 M overlap due to shared costs in VMMC. If the refunding process permits reallocation of resources from lower efficiency to higher efficiency interventions, reallocating funds for oral PrEP towards long-acting PrEP (Fig. 1d) could potentially offset the increase in HIV incidence that would occur from funding pauses of 12 months or less, as long as supply and dispensing costs for long-acting PrEP were no more than 1.5 times of those of oral PrEP. This offset would be partial with a 12-month pause (7500 additional infections would still be added) but would be complete with a 3-month pause.

Discussion

Sustained PEPFAR funding cuts would threaten Zimbabwe's hard-won gains against HIV, nearly doubling HIV incidence, adding nearly 100 000 infections through 2030, and adding tens of thousands of deaths. However, resuming PEPFAR funding within 12 months could mitigate this threat, and could even offset this threat if funds could be reallocated from less efficient to more efficient interventions.

We find greater proportional impact of PEPFAR funding cuts on HIV infections than Ghandi et al. s modeling study in South Africa [14] (78% versus 50%), but that study did not assess how funding cuts differentially impacted particular interventions. Conversely, we found lesser impact than the range (100–350%) predicted for sub-Saharan African countries in aggregate by a global HIV modeling platform (OPTIMA) [15]. Our mortality predictions (added HIV mortality per infection of 30%) are lower than both Ghandi et al. (110%) and OPTIMA (40%), perhaps because Zimbabwe's HIV control baseline is among the best in sub-Saharan Africa.

Limitations of this analysis include simplifications from assuming instantaneous intervention scale-down and scale-up, constant returns to scale, and assuming stability of Global Fund donations to Zimbabwe although diminishing U.S. contributions may reduce future donations. However, these limitations are offset by important strengths, including modeling observed changes in intervention coverage rather than hypothetical across-the-board cuts, considering the mitigating effects of task-shifting and reallocating domestic resources, using a Zimbabwe-specific model, and examining alternative funding resumption scenarios regarding timing, extent, and opportunities for resource reallocation.

One of the greatest success stories in global health is under threat in Zimbabwe and elsewhere. Our results suggest mitigation of this threat by restoring PEPFAR funding quickly, and allocating resumed resources more efficiently.
