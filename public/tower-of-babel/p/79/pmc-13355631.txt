Bridging the Healthcare Financing Gap: The Role of Financial Inclusion in Improving Access to Emergency Healthcare Financing in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Tarek Sadraoui, Sameh Zarai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFinancing emergency healthcare remains a major challenge for households worldwide considering the issue of uncertain income levels and increasing healthcare costs. Financial limitations may result in delayed use of healthcare services. Saudi Vision 2030 emphasizes the need for enhancing financial inclusion alongside healthcare delivery. However, little research done on financial inclusion impacts on household access to emergency healthcare financing.MethodologyThis study uses a panel dataset comprising 13 regions within Saudi Arabia spanning from 2015 to 2023 (117 observations). We use the fixed effects approach, system GMM model, and the panel ARDL model. Indicators of financial inclusion comprise formal finance, digital financial services, and savings indicators while controls are income stability and healthcare infrastructure.FindingsFinancial inclusion increases household access to emergency healthcare financing both in short run and long run. Digital finance and savings are strong predictors of emergency healthcare finance capacity (FI=0.287, p<0.001). Income stability and healthcare infrastructure reinforce the effect of financial inclusion. The error correction measure indicates that convergence to equilibrium happens rapidly (−0.412, p<0.001).ConclusionImprovement of financial inclusion via adoption of digital financial services and savings can enable households to deal with health emergencies effectively. Access to finance in combination with income stability and health infrastructure development is essential in ensuring that health financing is sustainable in Saudi Arabia. The above findings have significant policy recommendations concerning financial-sector reform and health system resilience in line with Vision 2030.Jel ClassificationG21; I13; I18; O16; O53.

Introduction

The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia is showing great progress in building a universal public system of healthcare. Since the funding and main providers in universal health systems are the government, the concerns are in the rapidly shifting demographic and epidemiological profile—characterized by an increasing population and rising rates of non-communicable diseases—which increases pressure on public finances. Emergency care, which is unpredictable and usually expensive, is a great risk in the area of the “healthcare financing gap”, or the difference between the costs of a medical emergency and the cash available to individuals or households to pay those costs.

Such a gap can force households into vicious cycles of poverty, as they can forced into using unproductive coping strategies, which might have negative effects, including the depletion of savings, selling of productive assets, borrowing money with high interest rates, and, in the worst cases, discontinuing treatment. Even though the Saudi Government is responsible for paying most of the costs, some costs, such as transport costs, shared costs in cases of uncovered costs, expatriate costs, and post-discharge costs, can be substantial.1

The adoption of digital financial solutions in Saudi Arabia has increased tremendously because of three reasons: firstly, the National Digital Transformation Strategy of Saudi Arabia, part of the Vision 2030 initiative, which aims for a minimum of 70% of all transactions to be cashless by 2025. Secondly, the coronavirus pandemic has sped up the transition towards digital transactions by more than 300% between 2020 and 2021. Lastly, a youthful population that is tech savvy, with more than 60% of people aged below 35 in Saudi Arabia.

Apart from this, another important agenda of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia is to uphold the assemblage of economic and social transformation through Vision 2030, where financial inclusion espoused as a cornerstone for individual enablement and a robust economy. Therefore, it is perceived that financial inclusion may allow individuals to manage financial shocks through several instruments, including bank accounts, credit, and micro-insurance products. However, the role of financial instruments in mitigating health shocks has yet to acquire the attention of researchers and scholars in Saudi Arabia.

This research, therefore, seeks to address this nexus. Its major aim is to study, through empirical investigation, the role played by financial inclusion in addressing the financing access for emergency care and therefore bridging the healthcare financing gap identified in Saudi Arabia. The key questions, therefore, include: What is the relationship between the level of financial inclusion and the household’s ability to cope with emergency health expenses without catastrophe?What are the most critical aspects of financial inclusion – access, usage, or quality?What are the behavioral and systemic barriers to using formal financial tools in addressing financing for emergency care?

The significance of this study is its potential to make interventions, considering a cross-sectorial approach, while taking into consideration the objectives and goals set by the Saudi Arabian Central Bank (SAMA), the Ministry of Health, and the Financial Sector Development Program accordingly. It seeks to contribute to the development of critical products and financial policy to enhance the health and well-being of the Saudi Arabian population, developing health resilience according to the goals set by Vision 2030.

Access to timely care is the very foundation of social welfare and economic security. Unexpected health expenditure is the cause of high levels of financial burden faced by the majority of families of almost any economy.2

The paper we wrote examines the issue of how, despite heavy investment by the government of Saudi Arabia in health care, disparities still exist regarding emergency healthcare financing for the low- and middle-income sectors of the population, particularly those in the informal economy.

It has been established that, and this has opened our eyes, financial inclusion, that is access to and use of formal financial services, can be a powerful driver of the resilience of households. Financially included households use various financial services, like saving, credit, insurance, and electronic payment tools, in order to cope with pressing healthcare demands. This situation is highly relevant in Saudi Arabia owing to the economic, digital, and healthcare reforms, as discussed in Vision 2030.3,4

While Saudi Arabia’s healthcare system is largely government-funded, it continues to expose families to substantial risk financially: indirect costs (transportation, expatriate co-payments, follow-up costs post-discharge) may amount to 15–25% of the total cost of an emergency event.3 Globally, at least 1.3 billion individuals experience the phenomenon of catastrophic health spending each year,4 and even wealthy nations of the Gulf region are prone to such vulnerability due to structural financial disparities brought about by the Vision 2030 model.5

The study of financial inclusion-healthcare financing relationship offers a very interesting example with regard to Saudi Arabia. With the implementation of the Vision 2030 project, financial inclusion has been elevated into a national developmental strategy that aims at having 85% formal bank account ownership by 2025, from 67% in 2015 (SAMA, 2022). On the other hand, Saudi Arabia stands out due to its high digitalization rate, with 98% smartphone usage and a highly advanced e-government system in the Middle East region.6

The current research paper adds value to the literature on novelty from three different perspectives. Firstly, the current study is one of the first studies that quantitatively analyzes the relationship between financial inclusion and emergency healthcare financing in a high-income Gulf state by employing dynamic panel estimation techniques, thus closing the geographic coverage gap of the existing literature, which is largely limited to Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. Secondly, the use of the Panel ARDL/PMG approach in the study highlights the short- and long-run implications of the relationship, while taking into consideration the differing orders of integration of variables, thus improving upon the existing methodologies.

Timely access to healthcare is the very building block for social and economic security. The reason for the large amount of financial burdens that most families in any economy face is unexpected health expenditure.

It has established that this may open our eyes: Indeed, financial inclusion, or access to and use of formal financial services, may be a significant driver of the resilience of households. This is all very relevant to the stage that Saudi Arabia is in, given the economic and digital transformation, as well as the healthcare reform agenda that is currently under discussion.

Nevertheless, whereas the Middle East, as a whole, as well as Saudi Arabia, as a specific case, still constitutes a field which is under-explored as a study issue within this specific field of literature, currently carried out research mainly focused on aspects of healthcare provision, insurance, as well as expenditure. There is a lack of specific research regarding financing for emergency health care, per se, as opposed to regular healthcare expenditure.

This research connects these two research streams as we incorporate our model of financial inclusion variables and outcomes related to healthcare financing within a dynamic econometric model. This extends the research in these literature streams both geographically and conceptually.7,8

The healthcare sector of Saudi Arabia has seen great progress in recent years owing to Vision 2030, whereby the nation invests in its healthcare sector for SAR 200 Billion each year, and the Ministry of Health manages 2300 primary healthcare facilities and 280 hospitals around the country. Despite all these developments, disparities in the level of income among various economic classes remain a problem, even though they are lesser when compared to other nations. The Healthcare Financing Gap and Catastrophic Expenditure

Literature, the HC Fin Gap recognized as one of the significant hurdles to clear in developed and developing systems. The consensus arrived at by the WHO is that financial risk protection is a basic target that needs to be achieved. Research carried out worldwide, including in the GCC region, has confirmed that even systems perceived as highly subsidized are not able to escape the ill effects of indirect expenditure on the financial condition of populations. Research in Saudi Arabia focuses on the contents of financial systems and patient satisfaction, with a growing recognition of the financial burden incurred in treating some diseases, indicating the emergence of a clear HC Fin Gap.

Financial Inclusion as a Tool for Risk Mitigation

There is a well-established body of literature that establishes the connection between financial inclusion and the resilience of people to economic shocks.9–11 Financial savings, for instance, allow people to smooth consumption, while access to credits allows people to temporarily smooth income shocks. Digital financial services harnessed for faster receipt of remittances via social networks in response to shocks.12 However, as has been noted before, these studies only touch on shocks other than those concerning health.

The Nexus of Financial Inclusion and Health Financing

The research is continually expanding with regard to intersectional research. More recent research undertaken in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia indicates that mobile money and micro-savings can reduce the incidence of catastrophic health expenditures.13 The insurance link is obvious; however, the financial inclusion body of literature reveals links to pre-displacement savings and post-risk borrowings. One of the emerging research needs was the lack of context-specific research in high-income transforming nations such as Saudi Arabia, where the financial systems were highly developed, yet the usage and appropriateness for purpose was not obvious to address the health needs of the population.14

Synthesis and Research Gap

Evidently, even as the concept looks promising, there is no empirical research on the case of the Gulf, and particularly Saudi Arabia, as most research on healthcare financing and financial inclusion are conducted as two separate entities. There has obviously been a gap in literature – there is no empirical econometric research to assess the potential to leverage the ambitious and well-institutionalized process of financial inclusion to address the urgent societal need – emergency healthcare finance.

Common panel cointegration estimations procedures assume identical order of integration of I (1), while common fixed effects estimation procedures assume stationarity. The Panel ARDL-PMG method is perfectly applicable in this case since it is equally applicable when the regressors are either I (0) or I (1).

Variable Definitions & Data Sources

The variable definition and measurement table presents a good framework on which to base a theoretically grounded study into the determinants of emergency healthcare financing access in the regions of Saudi Arabia. The dependent variable, Emergency Healthcare Financing Access (EHF), appears particularly well thought out as a composite 0–1 index variable generated by Principal Component Analysis to concretely represent the multi-faceted nature of healthcare financial resilience. In terms of the study’s principal variables, it appears to focus on a multi-faceted Financial Inclusion Index (FI) variable created by reference to the Sarma method, supported by individual dimensions of financial inclusion such as bank account access, digital payments, and credit access.17,18

All of which have positive-expected signs grounded in the theoretical premise that greater financial inclusion enhances households’ capacity to mobilize resources during health shocks. Health insurance coverage is included both as a constituent element of EHF and as a separate independent variable, with its obvious dual role as both an outcome and a determinant of healthcare financing behavior. The methodology ensures that methodological consistency is applied throughout by setting the study duration to be 2015–2023 (“2010–2024” incorrectly stated in abstract). This period is consistent with the availability of sub-national data on the 13 regions within the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (Riyadh, Makkah, Eastern Province, Madinah, Qassim, Asir, Tabuk, Hail, Jazan, Najran, Al-Bahah, Al-Jawf, and the Northern Borders Region). The study region is defined as 13 regions of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia while the study population consists of a balanced panel of 117 observations (13 regions x 9 years)—that is, a census of all administrative regions—thus eliminating any possibility of sampling bias. Data gathered from secondary sources, such as Saudi General Authority for Statistics (GAStat), SAMA Annual Statistics, the Ministry of Health Statistical Yearbook, and World Bank World Development Indicators. Econometrics were done using Stata 17.0 (Stata Corp LLC, College Station, TX, USA), whereby xtpmg for PMG estimator, xtabond2 for GMM estimation, xtunitroot for Panel Unit Root test, and xtpedro.

The data on digital payments reveals tremendous variation, as evidenced by a coefficient of variation of 71.6%, while the standard deviation is nearly three-quarters the size of its own mean. This reveals the phenomenon of a digital finance revolution progressing at vastly different speeds in different regions of the country. Perhaps the most revealing figures are those on the decomposition of variance by between-region and within-region effects.

For nearly all variables represented in the sample dataset, the between-region standard deviation dwarfs the within-region standard deviation, illustrating that structural effects observed between Riyadh and the Northern Borders remain more significant than panel effects observed over the nine-year period. This naturally substantiates our approach to frame panel econometric measures that control for regional fixed effects.

Economic development, as represented by log GDP per capita, reveals a striking stability as evidenced by a coefficient of variation of just 4.1%, while healthcare infrastructure reveals wider disparities: hospital beds per 1000 people vary considerably (CV 33.3%), which indicates that the infrastructure has not been distributed evenly.

Furthermore, the average urbanization rate stands at 78.4%, thereby confirming the urbanized status of Saudi Arabia. Additionally, the range of urbanization stands between 51.2% and 95.6%, indicating obviously distinct stages of development in the different regions of the Kingdom. These descriptive statistics not only describe the dataset but also are likewise instrumental in providing justification for the need to conduct this analysis given the noticeable regional variations in both FI and HF results, which are sufficient to establish cause-and-effect links over time against a backdrop of obviously uneven infrastructure growth more details.22–24

The temporal trend visualization of the Emergency Healthcare Financing Access index from 2015 to 2023 shows an obvious yet constructive pattern of increase in the various regions of Saudi Arabia, in line with the prevailing levels of regional variability. Starting from a base of 23.0 in 2015, the EHF index has shown an increase in each year, rising to 24.0 in 2016, followed by steady growth in each successive year until 2018.

What this suggests is that these variables are stationary, mean reverting, and possess stable statistical properties for the period 2015–2023. On the contrary, digital payments usage and GDP per capita fail to reject the null hypothesis of a unit root in their level forms, and with all test statistics falling below the respective threshold values; we further reminded that the series are stochastically trending and hence non-stationary. (Table 4) attempts to address the non-stationarity of the series by re-running the unit root tests for the first-differenced versions of the non-stationary variables.

The evidence is unambiguous: all three variables—digital payments, GDP per capita, and credit access—are found to be strongly stationary after first-differencing, with the test statistics being significant at the 1% level and, more importantly, with the magnitudes being significantly greater, for example, the LLC test statistic for the variable GDP per capita improves from −1.45 to −6.23. This confirms that all the variables are classified as Integrated Order One, denoted by I (1). This brings us to an important methodological insight covering the presence and admixture of differently ordered integration across the variables under consideration.

It definitively eliminates traditional approaches such as Panel Estimators – Fixed Effects or GMM, which call for all variables to be stationarity. At the same time, it rules out traditional cointegration techniques, which call for uniform I (1) integration. The presence of the I (0) and I(1) mix, as found by these unit tests, represents the precise empirical rationale for employing the Panel Autoregressive Distributed Lag approach, as estimated by the Pooled Mean Group estimator – which explicitly allows for the presence of mixed integration orders and simultaneously estimates both the short-run dynamics as well as the long-run relationship. These unit root tests therefore actively inform and validate our modeling strategy.

The long-run coefficients show that the Financial Inclusion Index has a powerful and highly significant positive impact on the access to finance healthcare emergencies, with a coefficient of 0.287***. This forms our main result: a one-unit rise in the multidimensional financial inclusion index is associated with a 0.287-unit rise in healthcare financing access in the long run, an economically meaningful magnitude considering that both indices vary along the 0–1 scale. As would be expected, all constituent dimensions of financial inclusion have significant long-run effects, out of which health insurance coverage has the largest coefficient, 0.203, followed by account penetration, 0.124, and digital payments, 0.0032.

Among the control variables, “GDP per capita” has a positive impact in the long run with a coefficient of 0.102; the poverty rate has a significant negative coefficient of −0.234, reinforcing the argument of the previous regression that “poverty” indeed plays the role of a structural constraint on the resilience of healthcare financing.

The coefficient for the error correction term, which defined as the speed of adjustment coefficient, found to be −0.412 and is significant at a 1% significance level. The negative and highly significant coefficient not only provides theoretical confirmation for the existence of a cointegration relationship based on the earlier results but also used to deduce an important policy implication from its absolute value; namely, 41.2% changes are corrected within a year. A rate as high as this reveals a remarkably efficient correcting mechanism, reminiscent of the efficient correcting properties of regional economies in Saudi Arabia.

The short-run results show that financial inclusion has immediate positive impacts too, as the coefficient on the change in FI Index is 0.089 at 0.411, proving that the gains from enhanced access to finance start to build up even before they continue to grow. The result of the diagnostic tests shows that the model has an excellent fit as well as appropriateness, as the results of the Hausman Test of the PMG vs. unrestricted MG estimator have χ2 = 8.23, p-values = 0.411, failed to reject the null of long-run slope homogeneity, thereby validating the efficiency benefits of the PMG estimator. The Log Likelihood is 245.67, and the AIC and BIC values of 2.89 and 3.45, respectively, signify that the model is an excellent fit.

Table 8 assists us in validating the appropriateness of our model selection by directly comparing our models with the three discussed models. It is seen that the MG model, which allows for the free variation of all the coefficients across regions, provides a slightly higher estimate of the long-run FI coefficient (0.312) but at the cost of lower information criteria (AIC=3.12, Log-Lik=241.23).

The DFHE model with homogeneity in both the long run as well as short-run coefficients results in the lowest value of the coefficient (0.256). However, more importantly, the failure of the Hausman test serves to establish the PMG as the preferred model, combining the theoretical benefits of long-run homogeneity across the Saudi regions with the inherent heterogeneity in the short-run effects.

(Table 9) offers the dynamic story of how these effects occur over time through the lens of cumulative dynamic multipliers. As seen in the current period (t=0), a financial inclusion shock has a corresponding effect on EHF of 0.089 units. This builds up progressively to 0.156 in one year, 0.201 in two years, and 0.234 in three years, eventually settling to the long-run value of 0.287. From this adjustment function plot, it seen that 90% of the long-run impact of a financial inclusion shock is realized after approximately 2.1 years. The effects of health insurance emerge even quicker, reaching the same point in 1.9 years, while digital payments reach the 90% mark after 2.3 years.

This notion of temporal heterogeneity has strong policy implications: health insurance investments provide the fastest returns in financing emergencies, whilst overall financial inclusion has slightly slower but overall larger effects. Discussion of Results and Policy Implications

Our results directly speak to the three research questions asked in our Introduction section. First, in terms of the link between financial inclusion and households’ abilities to withstand emergencies associated with healthcare costs, our study shows a strong positive correlation (coefficient of FI = 0.287; p<0.001). Second, when identifying the most important components of financial inclusion, it seen that dynamic multipliers show that it takes the least amount of time for health insurance to kick in (90% of impact after 1.9 years); the impact of financial inclusion (on average) takes slightly longer (2.1 years). Finally, in terms of behavioral vs systemic obstacles to healthcare accessibility, we observe the importance of systemic barriers via a negative and statistically significant coefficient of poverty (−0.234; p<0.001) and Granger causality.

The half-triangle correlation matrix offers strong preliminary evidence of the relationships between emergency healthcare financing (EHF), financial inclusion, and some of the significant socioeconomic variables considered. The analysis hints at a moderately strong positive correlation between emergency healthcare financing (EHF) and financial inclusion (FI), supporting that there is an increased ability to provide formal financial services that, in turn, helps in mobilizing adequate resources to cover emergency healthcare issues, a theory that aligns with advancing financial inclusion to smoothen healthcare shocks through savings, insuring, and providing credit mechanisms to households and governments.

Similarly, other financial inclusion measures disaggregated into smaller components, such as “account ownership”, “digital financial services”, and “access to credit”, have recorded some level of correlation with EHF, pointing to a multifaceted nature of financial inclusion. “Digital finance”, in turn, has recorded significant correlation with EHF as well as other financial inclusion measures, suggesting a crucial role in minimizing transaction costs in health emergencies. From this perspective, digital finance can actually contribute to the rapid mobilization of funds, thereby minimizing liquidity risks in health emergencies.

Health insurance coverage shows up as one of the variables with the highest correlation to EHF, thus giving significant importance to the role of insurance in enhancing the resilience of healthcare finance systems. Notably, GDP per capita and health expenditures also show a positive correlation to EHF; thus, giving significant importance to the role of economic potentials in the sustainability of emergency health finance systems. Poverty rate shows a systematic negative correlation to EHF and other finance indicators; thus, giving more importance to the role of poverty in undermining the efficiency of health finance systems.

Of critical note, although correlations for financial inclusion variables are substantial, not any of the values transcend the thresholds that denote the likelihood of severe multicollinearity. This verifies the correctness of adding diverse financial inclusion aspects to the econometric equation and justifies the applicability of the PMG-ARDL technique to obtain both short-run effects and long-run equilibrium relationships.

These findings have a number of important policy implications. First, policies of financial inclusion should be framed as part of overall health financing strategies rather than as freestanding financial-sector reforms. Improving access to bank accounts, digital payment systems, and formal credit can significantly improve the capacity of households and governments to respond to healthcare emergencies.

The second key message is that, given the strong linkage between digital finance and emergency healthcare financing, there is a need for investments in digital financial infrastructures, especially in developing and emerging economies. Policymakers should foster interoperable payment systems, mobile banking, and digital identification frameworks to ensure the speed and inclusiveness of financial access in the face of health shocks.

Third, the leading role of health insurance coverage suggests that linking financial inclusion initiatives with UHC reforms can yield considerable complementarities. Expanding insurance enrollment through digital and inclusive financial channels has some scope to improve risk pooling and reduce out-of-pocket health expenditure in emergencies.

In turn, the negative relationship between poverty and EHF underscores the importance of social protection and financial inclusion initiatives, especially for disadvantaged groups. In this regard, there are conditional cash transfers, insurance subsidies, digital welfare payments, which can address issues of inequality and increase overall vulnerability differently.

Conclusion

This paper offers theoretical contributions regarding the link between emergency financing for healthcare services, financial inclusion, and socioeconomic factors. The relationship between financial inclusion and emergency healthcare financing using correlation analysis shows that financial inclusion variables such as digital finance systems, credit availability, and insurance services correlated with emergency financing requirements. On the other hand, poverty acts as a major constraint. Consequently, the dynamic panel ARDL approach adopted.

In all, the findings emphasize the contribution that financial system development can make as an essential element in well-prepared healthcare financing systems. By improving financial inclusion and linking this with health policy, countries can improve their preparedness for health crises, as well as limit financial risk in households. In future, additional studies can conducted with the inclusion of institutional measures, fiscal decentralization, and even indicators specific to shocks in order to develop this research.

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, our regional-level analysis may mask important within-region heterogeneity, particularly between urban and rural populations. Second, data limitations prevent disaggregation by expatriate versus citizen status, which may be relevant given that expatriates constitute approximately 40% of Saudi Arabia’s population. Third, our study period (2015–2023) captures the early and middle stages of Vision 2030 implementation; longer-term follow-up studies will be valuable as reforms mature. Future research should incorporate institutional quality measures, fiscal decentralization indicators, and health shock-specific variables to develop this research agenda.
