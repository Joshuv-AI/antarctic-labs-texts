STXBP6-driven signaling cascade alters lipid peroxidation in tumor-associated macrophages to facilitate ovarian cancer progression
Authors: Min Wang, Jinlong Wang, Hang Xu, Siying Zhang, Fengzhen Jin, Qinke Li, Zhu Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ovarian cancer (OC) is highly lethal, with late diagnosis and frequent treatment resistance. Tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) are key immunosuppressive cells whose metabolic state critically influences redox homeostasis and immune function in the tumor microenvironment (TME). Here, we show that STXBP6 expression is elevated in OC TAMs. Mechanistically, STXBP6 promotes cPLA2-mediated arachidonic acid release and PKCβ-dependent nuclear translocation and activation of arachidonate 5-lipoxygenase (ALOX5), driving alterations in arachidonic acid metabolism and downstream 5-HETE production. Lipidomics, transcriptomics, and functional assays demonstrated that STXBP6 regulates lipid droplet formation, lipid peroxidation, and TAM polarization, linking redox-regulated lipid metabolism to immunosuppressive phenotypes. Using in vitro TAM models and in vivo OC models, including TAMs-OC co-implantation, we further confirmed that the STXBP6/cPLA2/ALOX5/5-HETE axis enhances tumor growth, metastasis, and the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized mechanism by which STXBP6 drives TAM metabolic reprogramming and immune modulation in OC, highlighting STXBP6 as a potential target for immunometabolic therapy.

Background

Ovarian cancer (OC) is one of the most prevalent and lethal malignancies among women worldwide [1]. Due to its insidious early symptoms and the lack of effective screening strategies, approximately 80% of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages (III–IV), with five-year survival rates of only 27% and 13%, respectively [2,3]. Although OC initially responds to platinum- and taxane-based chemotherapy, 70–80% of patients experience relapse within two years, and recurrent tumors are often more resistant to treatment, contributing to the high mortality rate [1]. The global burden of OC is projected to rise substantially in the coming decades [4,5].

The tumor microenvironment (TME), composed of tumor cells, infiltrating immune cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts, blood vessels, and extracellular matrix, plays a critical role in cancer initiation, progression, and metastasis [[6], [7], [8]]. Tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) are among the most abundant immune cells in the TME and can be broadly classified into pro-inflammatory, anti-tumor M1 and immunosuppressive, pro-tumor M2 phenotypes [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. In most tumors, TAMs predominantly exhibit an M2-like phenotype, promoting tumor growth, angiogenesis, and immune evasion through the secretion of cytokines and growth factors (e.g., VEGF, TGF-β) or by directly suppressing T and NK cell functions via immunosuppressive molecules such as ARG1, IL-10, and PD-L1 [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. The dynamic balance between M1 and M2 states is tightly regulated by microenvironmental cues, shaping an immunosuppressive TME and contributing to tumor progression and therapy resistance [[21], [22], [23]].

Beyond signaling pathways, TAM function is closely linked to their metabolic state, with lipid metabolism reprogramming emerging as a key regulator of M2 polarization and immunosuppressive activity [[24], [25], [26], [27], [28]]. Altered lipid metabolism, including increased lipid uptake and storage, lipid peroxidation (LPO), and accumulation of oxidized lipid products—can drive TAMs toward a pro-tumor phenotype. In OC, ascites-enriched TAMs expressing high levels of PLIN2 accumulate lipids and activate the HIF-1α/SPP1 pathway, thereby promoting tumor migration, invasion, and vascular permeability [29]. These findings underscore the critical role of TAM lipid metabolism in tumor progression and suggest that targeting this pathway may provide novel therapeutic opportunities.

Emerging evidence indicates that STXBP6 (also known as Amisyn) exerts context-dependent roles in cancer [30,31]. In certain tumor types, such as colorectal cancer, the STXBP6/IRF1 axis suppresses tumor progression via PD-L1 regulation [32], and similar tumor-suppressive effects have been observed in lung adenocarcinoma [33,34]. Conversely, STXBP6 can exhibit pro-tumorigenic properties in other contexts: in hepatocellular carcinoma, high STXBP6 expression correlates with tumor development and may serve as a diagnostic marker [35]; in bladder cancer, elevated STXBP6 promotes malignancy, and multi-gene prediction models incorporating STXBP6 effectively identify high-risk patients [36]. Functionally, STXBP6 has also been implicated in regulating cellular metabolism, including lipid metabolism, which may contribute to its context-dependent roles in tumor progression.

Notably, our previous study demonstrated that STXBP6 promotes OC progression, suggesting a potential role in modulating lipid metabolism within tumor cells [37]. However, this work primarily focused on cancer cell-intrinsic mechanisms, without addressing the contributions of TAMs or the broader TME. Given the central role of TAMs in shaping OC progression through lipid metabolic reprogramming, it remains unclear whether and how STXBP6 modulates TAM function and the immunosuppressive microenvironment in OC.

In summary, although the immunoregulatory mechanisms and metabolic reprogramming of TAMs have been partially elucidated, significant gaps remain regarding lipid metabolism-mediated functional remodeling of TAMs in OC. While our previous work showed that STXBP6 promotes OC progression via tumor cell-intrinsic lipid metabolism, its role in the TME, particularly in TAMs, remains unexplored. Therefore, this study aims to elucidate the role of STXBP6 in OC by: (1) characterizing its expression within the TME and association with TAMs; (2) investigating the molecular mechanisms through which it regulates TAM polarization and lipid metabolic reprogramming; and (3) evaluating its impact on OC growth, metastasis, and the immunosuppressive microenvironment using both in vitro and in vivo models. Collectively, this study seeks to provide mechanistic insights into STXBP6-driven TAMs regulation and to assess its potential as a diagnostic and therapeutic target in OC.

Materials and methods

Online database

The GEPIA database was used to analyze the mRNA expression levels of ALOX5 in OC and normal tissues. The TIMER2.0 database was employed to evaluate the immunological relevance of ALOX5 in OC.

Clinical samples

This study included 20 tissue specimens collected from the Department of Pathology, the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (CQMU), comprising 10 high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma samples and 10 normal ovarian tissue samples. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of CQMU, and all experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and regulations.

scRNA-seq dataset reanalysis

Publicly available scRNA-seq data from OC patients was downloaded from GSA-Human (accession code: PRJCA005422). Data normalization, scaling, and unsupervised clustering of the global cell population were performed using the Seurat and scater packages within the R environment. To minimize potential patient- and site-specific batch effects, harmony was applied for dataset alignment and batch correction. Major cellular lineages and macrophage sub-lineages were resolved via unsupervised clustering and visualized using ggplot2 and scCustomize pipelines. To profile the compartment-specific expression of STXBP6, cell-type-specific differential expression and statistical analyses across different anatomical sites were performed using presto and MAST, with data wrangling facilitated by dplyr and data.table.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide at 37°C for 25 min, followed by blocking with 3% BSA for 30 min. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C and subsequently with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at 37°C for 50 min. Signals were developed using DAB, counterstained with hematoxylin, and examined under a light microscope after dehydration and mounting.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Sections were air-dried, outlined with a hydrophobic barrier pen, and blocked with 3% BSA at 37°C for 30 min. Sections were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C in a humidified chamber. After PBS washes, sections were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies at 37°C for 50 min in the dark, followed by nuclear counterstaining with DAPI for 10 min. Autofluorescence was quenched for 5 min, and sections were mounted with an anti-fade medium and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

Cell culture

Human OC cell lines SKOV3 and A2780, mouse OC cell line ID8, human monocytic leukemia cell line THP1, and mouse monocyte–macrophage leukemia cell line RAW264.7 were obtained from Shanghai Jinyuan Biotech Co., Ltd. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and confirmed to be mycoplasma-free. SKOV3 cells were cultured in McCoy's 5A medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, while A2780, ID8, and RAW264.7 cells were maintained in DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. THP1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and 0.05 mM 2-mercaptoethanol. For macrophage differentiation, THP1 cells were treated with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) for 24 h to generate THP1-derived macrophages (THP1-MΦ), which were used in subsequent experiments unless otherwise specified. All cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) isolation and culture

Bone marrow cells were flushed from the femurs and tibias of 6-to-8-week-old mice. The cell suspension was subjected to red blood cell lysis and filtered through a 70 μm strainer. The cells were initially seeded and allowed to adhere for 4–8 h to deplete mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs). The non-adherent supernatant was then collected, re-seeded, and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 25 ng/mL recombinant mouse M-CSF (PeproTech, USA) for 7 days to differentiate into mature BMDMs.

Flow cytometry and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)

For ascites samples, harvested cells were treated with RBC lysis buffer and passed through a 70 μm nylon strainer to obtain single-cell suspensions. Cells from either in vitro cultures or in vivo ascites were collected, washed twice with flow cytometry staining buffer, and adjusted to a density of 1×10∧6 cells/mL. Aliquots of 100 μL were incubated with the corresponding fluorochrome-labeled antibodies according to the manufacturers' protocols. Following incubation, the cells were washed with staining buffer, centrifuged at 300g for 5 min, and resuspended in 500 μL of staining buffer. For phenotypic analysis, data were acquired on a flow cytometer. For cell purification, the stained single-cell suspensions were subjected to FACS on a BD FACSAria III cell sorter. Target TAM populations were isolated based on specific fluorescence gating strategies and harvested immediately for downstream experiments. All flow cytometry antibodies utilized for analysis and sorting are detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

Preparation of tumor-conditioned medium (TCM)

When tumor cells reached 70% confluence, the culture medium was replaced with serum-free basal medium, and cells were incubated for an additional 24 h. The supernatant was collected, centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min and then at 2000 × g for 10 min at 4°C to remove cell debris. The resulting TCM was aliquoted and stored at −80°C until use. For co-culture experiments with macrophages, TCM was mixed 1:1 with basal medium supplemented with 2% FBS.

Lentiviral transduction

Adherent cells were seeded into six-well plates to reach 20–30% confluence the next day. Suspension cells were prepared at 1 × 105 cells/mL in 2 mL complete medium and infected with lentivirus. Cells were incubated at 37°C for 12–16 h before replacing the medium. For adherent cells, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium; for suspension cells, cells were centrifuged at 200 × g for 5 min, the supernatant discarded, and the pellet resuspended in fresh medium. Stable infection was achieved via puromycin selection for 48 h. Surviving cells were considered stably transduced. The sequences of all shRNAs used for knockdown are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated using the RNA-Quick Purification Kit (EScience, China). Reverse transcription and qRT-PCR were performed using the RT Reagent Kit and TB Green (Takara, Japan). Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Western blot

Proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China), separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at 37°C, incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 1) overnight at 4°C, and then with corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h at 37°C. Protein bands were detected using the ChemiDoc Imaging System.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) of cell culture supernatants

Secretion levels of human TNF-α, ARG-1, LTB4, and mouse TNF-α, ARG-1, LTB4, arachidonic acid (AA), and 5-HETE were measured using the corresponding ELISA kits (Bioswamp, Meimian Bio, China) according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Transwell assay

Transwell migration and invasion assays were conducted using 24-well Transwell chambers. For invasion assays, Matrigel was diluted 1:8 in serum-free medium and added to the upper chamber (50 μL), followed by gelation at 37°C for 1 h. Cells were seeded at 1 × 105 cells/mL (200 μL per upper chamber) and incubated at 37°C for 16 h (SKOV3, ID8) or 36 h (A2780). Non-migrated/non-invaded cells were removed, and remaining cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and quantified using ImageJ.

Oil Red O staining and Nile Red staining

Lipid droplets were stained using a Modified Oil Red O Staining Kit (Beyotime, China) or Nile Red (MCE, China) following manufacturer instructions. For Oil Red O staining, images were acquired under bright-field microscopy. For Nile Red staining, cells were counterstained with DAPI-containing antifade mounting medium and imaged using fluorescence microscopy.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

RNA-seq was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 platform (LC-Bio Technology). Genes with fold change ≥2 and P < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. Functional enrichment analyses were conducted using Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG databases.

Detection of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) level

Intracellular ROS levels were detected using a ROS Assay Kit for Superoxide Anion with DHE (Beyotime, China) and measured by flow cytometry.

Measurement of malondialdehyde (MDA) and ATP levels

MDA and ATP concentrations were determined using the Lipid Peroxidation MDA Assay Kit and ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime, China), following the manufacturer's protocols.

BODIPY 581/591C11 staining

Cellular lipid peroxidation levels were evaluated using the Lipid Peroxidation Assay Kit with BDPY 581/591C11 (Beyotime, China). Following staining according to the manufacturer's instructions, cellular lipid oxidation levels were analyzed via flow cytometry and visualized under a fluorescence microscope.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Cells were lysed in buffer containing protease inhibitors, incubated with specific antibodies, and bound to Protein A/G magnetic beads (MCE, China). Immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted with SDS-PAGE buffer and analyzed by Western blot.

Isolation of nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins

Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were prepared using a commercial extraction kit (KeyGene BioTech, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

siRNA transfection

Cells at 30–50% confluence were transfected with siRNA using Lipofectamine 2000 in Opti-MEM. After 6 h, the medium was replaced with complete medium, and cells were cultured for 24–72 h before downstream experiments. siRNA sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Untargeted lipidomics

Untargeted lipidomics analysis was performed by Hangzhou LC-Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Lipids were extracted using cold isopropanol/acetonitrile/water (2:1:1, v/v/v) and profiled by UHPLC–MS/MS. Data were processed for peak detection, alignment, normalization, and lipid identification.

Mouse model

Female C57BL/6 mice aged 4–6 weeks were used for all in vivo tumor xenograft experiments. For the syngeneic subcutaneous and intraperitoneal tumor models, 1 × 106 ID8 cells were suspended in PBS and injected either subcutaneously into the right flank or directly into the peritoneal cavity, respectively. For co-inoculation experiments, ID8 cells were mixed with RAW264.7-TAM cells at a 1:1 ratio and injected either subcutaneously or intraperitoneally. All procedures involving animals were conducted according to the ethical policies approved by the Institutional Animal Protection and Utilization Committee of CQMU.

In vivo intratumoral delivery of siRNA

Once subcutaneous tumors became palpable, the tumor diameters were systematically monitored every 6 days. When the tumors reached an average size of approximately 4 mm × 4 mm, the mice were anesthetized and immobilized for therapeutic intervention. Pre-diluted siRNA was directly administered into the tumor mass via multi-point and multi-site microinjections to ensure homogeneous diffusion. Each injection carried a volume of 50 μL, delivering a dosage of 2.5 nmol of siRNA per mouse per injection, whereas the vehicle control group received an equal volume of PBS. This dosing regimen was maintained at a frequency of twice weekly for a total of 5 consecutive injections. At 6 weeks post-inoculation of the tumor cells, the mice were humanely euthanized; the tumor grafts were subsequently excised, photographed, and harvested. Finally, the tumor tissues underwent IHC staining to evaluate STXBP6 expression levels, thereby verifying the in vivo genetic knockdown efficiency.

TUNEL staining

Paraffin sections were treated with Proteinase K, incubated with TUNEL reaction mixture, counterstained with DAPI, and imaged under a fluorescence microscope.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired Student's t-test, and multiple-group comparisons were analyzed by one-way ANOVA. All experiments included three independent biological replicates. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.

To investigate the cellular distribution of STXBP6 within the TME, we reanalyzed a publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset of OC patients (GSA-Human, accession code: PRJCA005422), which comprised samples from multiple sites, including peripheral blood, pelvic lymph nodes, primary ovarian tumors, omental metastases, and ascites. Unsupervised clustering of the global cell population successfully identified distinct major cellular lineages within OC tissues and their related anatomical compartments (Fig. 1C). To provide a higher-resolution landscape of the TME, these major cell types were further annotated into more detailed sub-clusters, which are comprehensively presented in Supplementary Fig. 1A. To gain deeper insights into the myeloid lineage, macrophages were sequentially isolated and re-clustered for downstream analysis (Fig. 1D), which confirmed the presence of transcriptionally distinct macrophage subpopulations across different anatomical sites. We next characterized the expression profile of STXBP6 within these macrophage compartments. Notably, STXBP6 expression was substantially enriched in macrophages derived from the primary tumors, metastatic lesions, and ascites, whereas its expression remained markedly lower in those from lymph nodes and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) (Fig. 1E). To further examine the expression frequency and distribution characteristics of STXBP6 within these individual groups, a group-split density analysis was performed. This complementary analysis reinforced the prominent shift toward higher STXBP6 expression in tumor-associated and ascites-derived macrophages compared to their peripheral or lymph node counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Taking together, these lines of evidence demonstrate a compartment-specific enrichment of STXBP6 in TAMs within the OC microenvironment. Given that malignant ascites plays a key role in driving peritoneal metastasis and representing the unique metabolic landscape of OC, we subsequently focused on this fluid compartment to validate our transcriptomic findings in vivo. In an OC mouse model, TAMs were isolated from the ascites of tumor-bearing mice using a strict FACS gating strategy (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Subsequent qRT-PCR analysis revealed that STXBP6 mRNA levels were significantly higher in these sorted TAMs compared with those in naive, successfully characterized BMDMs (Supplementary Fig. 2B and C) (Fig. 1F). This empirical validation substantiates our scRNA-seq observations, reinforcing that STXBP6 in localized TAMs actively drives OC malignancy within the fluid microenvironment.

Spurred by these in vivo findings, we next established in vitro culture systems to dissect the precise cell-intrinsic impact of STXBP6 on macrophage behavior, employing human THP-1, murine RAW264.7 cells, and primary BMDMs as macrophage models. THP1 monocytes treated with 100 ng/mL PMA for 24 h underwent morphological changes from suspension to adherent growth (Supplementary Fig. 2D). Flow cytometry and qRT-PCR analyses confirmed significant upregulation of the macrophage marker CD68 in THP1-MΦ compared with parental THP1 cells (Supplementary Fig. 2E and F), indicating successful differentiation.

To mimic the pathological educational signaling within the TME, TCM harvested from SKOV3, A2780, and ID8 cells (designated as CM-SKOV3, CM-A2780, and CM-ID8, respectively) were employed to induce TAM-like differentiation. Strikingly, both mRNA and protein levels of STXBP6 were significantly increased in TCM-treated THP1-MΦ, RAW264.7 cells, and primary BMDMs (Fig. 1G and H), corroborating our in vivo data and highlighting its prominent role within the TME.

STXBP6 drives M2-like polarization of TAMs

To examine the functional significance of STXBP6 in shaping TAM phenotypes, THP1 and RAW264.7 cells were transduced with STXBP6 lentiviruses targeting STXBP6 for knockdown or overexpression (shSTXBP6/shNC and OE-STXBP6/Vector for THP-1; sh-stxbp6/shNC for RAW264.7), followed by puromycin selection. Stable knockdown or overexpression was confirmed by qRT-PCR and Western blot (Supplementary Fig. 3A and B). In parallel, primary BMDMs were transiently transfected with specific STXBP6-targeting siRNAs (si-stxbp6/siNC), and the transient knockdown efficiency was thoroughly validated at both transcript and protein levels via qRT-PCR and Western blot (Supplementary Fig. 3A and B). Transfected THP1 cells were differentiated into THP1-MΦ. THP1-MΦ, RAW264.7 cells, and BMDMs were then co-cultured with the respective TCM to generate educated TAMs (designated as THP1-TAM, RAW264.7-TAM, and BMDMs-TAM, respectively).

Flow cytometric profiling revealed that STXBP6 knockdown distinctly decreased the M2 marker CD206 while increasing the M1 marker CD80, conversely, ectopic overexpression of STXBP6 exerted the opposite effect, reinforcing an M2-biased landscape (Fig. 2A). To consolidate these phenotypic findings at the functional secretory level, ELISA were performed on the culture supernatants. Consistently, STXBP6 deficiency markedly reduced the secretion of the M2-associated pro-tumorigenic cytokine ARG-1 while boosting the M1-associated pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α; meanwhile, STXBP6 overexpression significantly elevated ARG-1 levels and suppressed TNF-α release (Fig. 2B). STXBP6 in TAMs promotes OC cell migration, invasion, and survival

To investigate whether changes in STXBP6 expression in TAMs influence OC progression, SKOV3, A2780, and ID8 cells were co-cultured with THP-1-, RAW264.7-, or BMDMs-derived TAMs, followed by Transwell migration and invasion assays. A schematic of the co-culture system is shown in Fig. 3A. Transwell assays revealed that STXBP6 overexpression in TAMs significantly enhanced OC cell migration and invasion, whereas STXBP6 knockdown inhibited these abilities (Fig. 3B). In parallel, conditioned media from THP-1-, RAW264.7-, and BMDMs-derived TAM cultures were applied to OC cells for 48 h. OC cell apoptosis was reduced by STXBP6-overexpressing TAMs and increased by STXBP6-silenced TAMs, as assessed by flow cytometry (Fig. 3C). Based on these enrichment results, lipid metabolism–related DEGs were further analyzed to identify potential downstream targets of STXBP6 in TAMs. Heatmap analysis revealed that arachidonic acid 5-lipoxygenase (ALOX5) was among the most significantly downregulated genes following STXBP6 silencing (Fig. 4C). As a key enzyme in AA metabolism, ALOX5 catalyzes the conversion of AA into bioactive lipid mediators and is critically involved in LPO and inflammatory regulation. Consistent with this observation, KEGG analysis demonstrated significant enrichment of the AA metabolic pathway upon STXBP6 knockdown (Fig. 4B), suggesting that STXBP6 may regulate TAM function through modulation of the AA–ALOX5 metabolic axis.

To further evaluate the clinical relevance of ALOX5 in OC, its expression was analyzed using the GEPIA database, which revealed significantly elevated ALOX5 levels in OC tissues compared with normal controls (Fig. 4D). This upregulation was further confirmed by IHC staining of OC tissues (Fig. 4E). Moreover, TIMER database analysis demonstrated a positive correlation between ALOX5 expression and macrophage infiltration, particularly M2 macrophages (Fig. 4F), indicating a potential role for ALOX5 in TAM polarization within the OC microenvironment.

Multiplex IF staining of OC tissues further revealed pronounced co-localization of ALOX5, STXBP6, and the macrophage marker CD68, indicating that ALOX5 and STXBP6 are concurrently expressed in TAMs in situ (Fig. 4G). In addition, Co-IP assays confirmed a direct interaction between STXBP6 and ALOX5 in TAMs (Fig. 4H), supporting the notion that ALOX5 functions as a key downstream effector of STXBP6. Collectively, these findings identify ALOX5 as a critical mediator through which STXBP6 regulates lipid metabolism and functional remodeling of TAMs in the OC TME.

STXBP6 promotes ALOX5 nuclear translocation via PKCβ

Given that nuclear translocation of ALOX5 is essential for its full enzymatic activation, we next investigated whether STXBP6 regulates this process. Western blot and IF analyses demonstrated that STXBP6 markedly promoted the nuclear localization of ALOX5 (Fig. 5A and B). The protein kinase C (PKC) family is a well-established upstream regulator of ALOX5 activity and subcellular localization. Activation of specific PKC isoforms promotes ALOX5 translocation from the cytoplasm to the nucleus and nuclear membrane, whereas inhibition of PKC signaling impairs this process and reduces downstream metabolite production. To determine whether STXBP6 regulates ALOX5 nuclear translocation via PKC signaling in TAMs, we examined the expression of major PKC isoforms (PKCα, PKCβ, and PKCδ). qRT-PCR analysis revealed that STXBP6 specifically regulated PKCβ expression, whereas PKCα and PKCδ remained unchanged (Fig. 5C). This selective regulation was further confirmed at the protein level by Western blot analysis (Fig. 5D). Mechanistically, Co-IP assays demonstrated direct interactions between STXBP6 and PKCβ, as well as between PKCβ and ALOX5 (Fig. 5E), suggesting that STXBP6 may act as a scaffold to facilitate PKCβ-mediated ALOX5 nuclear translocation. To functionally validate the involvement of PKCβ, TAMs were transfected with PKCβ-specific siRNA, and efficient knockdown was confirmed by qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses (Fig. 5F and G). Importantly, PKCβ silencing markedly reduced the nuclear localization of ALOX5, as evidenced by Western blot and IF analyses (Fig. 5H and I). To firmly establish whether STXBP6 functions through an ALOX5-dependent mechanism, TAMs were treated with the ALOX5 inhibitor, Zileuton. To validate the lipidomic alterations, lipid droplet accumulation in TAMs was assessed by Oil Red O and Nile Red staining. STXBP6 knockdown markedly reduced lipid droplet content, whereas STXBP6 overexpression significantly increased lipid droplet accumulation (Fig. 7D), which was in close agreement with the lipidomics findings.

Given the prominent enrichment of AA-containing phospholipids revealed by our lipidomics profile, we next quantified intracellular free AA levels to assess functional substrate availability. STXBP6 knockdown significantly decreased free AA abundance, while STXBP6 overexpression led to a pronounced increase in intracellular free AA (Fig. 7E).

To unveil the precise enzymatic driver behind this altered free AA pool, we turned our attention to cytosolic phospholipase A2 (cPLA2), the primary rate-limiting enzyme responsible for hydrolyzing host membrane phospholipids to mobilize functional lipid substrates. Strikingly, Western blot analysis demonstrated that the protein expression levels of total cPLA2 were dynamically and positively controlled by STXBP6; ectopic overexpression of STXBP6 robustly elevated cPLA2 abundance, whereas its genetic silencing precipitated a distinct down-regulation of cPLA2 (Fig. 7F and G). These findings provide compelling mechanistic evidence that STXBP6 dictates upstream AA metabolic flux and substrate availability directly by modulating cPLA2 expression, thereby fueling the catalytic machinery of downstream lipometabolic pathways.

As ALOX5 typically catalyzes AA to generate bioactive lipid mediators, we subsequently measured the major ALOX5-derived metabolites, LTB4 and 5-HETE. Pharmacological inhibition of ALOX5 markedly reduced 5-HETE production, whereas LTB4 levels were largely unaffected. Consistently, STXBP6 knockdown significantly decreased 5-HETE levels, while STXBP6 overexpression substantially increased 5-HETE abundance (Fig. 7H and I). These findings indicate that 5-HETE is the principal downstream metabolite regulated by the STXBP6/ALOX5 axis in TAMs.

To further confirm the absolute dependence of 5-HETE production on ALOX5 enzymatic activity, the specific ALOX5 inhibitor Zileuton was applied to STXBP6-overexpressing TAMs. Zileuton treatment effectively abolished the STXBP6-induced surge in 5-HETE levels (Fig. 7J), demonstrating that STXBP6 orchestrates upstream substrate mobilization via cPLA2 to ultimately drive 5-HETE production in a strictly ALOX5-dependent manner.

5-HETE rescues the effects of ALOX5 inhibition on OC cell growth and metastasis via TAMs

To determine whether altered 5-HETE levels in TAMs influence OC cell growth and metastatic potential, TAMs were co-cultured with OC cells, followed by analyses of apoptosis, migration, and invasion. Inhibition of ALOX5 in TAMs significantly increased OC cell apoptosis and impaired migratory and invasive capacities. Notably, supplementation with exogenous 5-HETE effectively reversed these effects, resulting in reduced apoptosis and restoration of OC cell migration and invasion (Supplementary Fig. 6A and B). These results indicate that 5-HETE functionally rescues the pro-tumorigenic effects of TAMs under conditions of ALOX5 inhibition, thereby promoting OC cell growth and metastatic potential.

Discussion

In the present study, we demonstrated that STXBP6 is significantly upregulated in OC and acts as a critical pro-tumorigenic factor, particularly within the unique OC TME. While OC remains, a lethal gynecologic malignancy characterized by late diagnosis and frequent recurrence [4,40,41], our findings highlight STXBP6 as a promising therapeutic target. Previous studies, including our own, have noted that STXBP6 displays pleiotropic roles, serving as a tumor suppressor in colorectal cancer but functioning as an oncogene in other contexts. Crucially, our data suggests that this functional divergence may be driven by the unique, lipid-rich environment of the peritoneal cavity in OC, where STXBP6 potentially modulates lipid metabolism and peroxidation pathways, contrasting sharply with its suppressive roles in non-lipid-dominated malignancies.

Given that macrophages are central executioners of lipid metabolism and immune remodeling within this lipid-rich cavity, we focused on the function of STXBP6 in TAMs in OC. We observed that STXBP6 is highly expressed in TAMs and exhibits significant co-localization with the macrophage marker CD68, suggesting a potential role in TAMs-mediated tumor regulation. Functional assays further demonstrated that STXBP6 promotes M2-like TAM polarization, whereas its knockdown favors an M1 phenotype. Consistently, modulation of STXBP6 in TAMs markedly influenced OC cell apoptosis, migration, and invasion, indicating that STXBP6 contributes to tumor progression through the lipid-dependent reprogramming of the immune microenvironment.

To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, RNA-seq analysis of STXBP6-deficient TAMs revealed extensive transcriptional alterations involving signal transduction, immune regulation, and inflammatory responses. Notably, KEGG enrichment analysis highlighted lipid metabolism–related pathways, particularly AA metabolism, as prominently affected. These findings provided an initial mechanistic link between STXBP6 expression and immunometabolic reprogramming of TAMs.

Subsequent analyses identified ALOX5 as a key downstream effector of STXBP6. ALOX5 expression in OC tissues closely mirrored that of STXBP6 and was strongly associated with M2-like TAM infiltration. Moreover, Co-IP assays confirmed a direct interaction between STXBP6 and ALOX5, supporting its role as a central mediator of STXBP6-dependent TAM function. As ALOX5 enzymatic activity critically depends on its translocation from the cytoplasm to the nucleus or nuclear membrane [42], we hypothesized that STXBP6 may regulate ALOX5 function by modulating its subcellular localization. Indeed, STXBP6 overexpression markedly promoted ALOX5 nuclear translocation, whereas STXBP6 knockdown restricted ALOX5 to the cytoplasm, indicating spatial regulation as a key mode of ALOX5 activation.

ALOX5 nuclear translocation is tightly controlled by kinase signaling pathways. Our data implicate PKCβ as a critical upstream mediator, as STXBP6 selectively regulated PKCβ expression, and PKCβ interacted with both STXBP6 and ALOX5. Importantly, PKCβ silencing significantly impaired ALOX5 nuclear localization, supporting a PKCβ-dependent mechanism through which STXBP6 facilitates ALOX5 activation.

Functionally, enhanced ALOX5 activation was accompanied by increased LPO in TAMs, as evidenced by elevated MDA and ROS levels, indicative of metabolic reprogramming. Conversely, pharmacological inhibition of ALOX5 using Zileuton suppressed LPO and shifted TAM polarization toward an M1-like phenotype. Notably, Zileuton effectively abolished the pro-oxidative and pro-M2 effects induced by STXBP6 overexpression, reinforcing the conclusion that ALOX5 is the principal downstream effector mediating STXBP6-driven TAM reprogramming.

Beyond regulating ALOX5 activation, Western blot and lipidomics analyses revealed that STXBP6 also reshapes lipid metabolic programs in TAMs. Our protein expression data demonstrated that STXBP6 significantly upregulates cPLA2, the premier phospholipase responsible for cleaving and releasing AA from membrane phospholipids. Concurrently, lipidomics profiling validated that STXBP6 modulates lipid droplet accumulation and alters the levels of PE (P-16:0/20:4), a specific AA-containing phospholipid substrate, thereby driving cPLA2-mediated intracellular free AA release and securing the essential substrate supply for ALOX5-mediated LPO. This dual regulatory mechanism—coordinating ALOX5 activation through PKCβ-dependent nuclear translocation and ensuring substrate availability via cPLA2-driven AA release—resulted in the enhanced production of the bioactive lipid mediator 5-HETE, which in turn promoted M2 polarization of TAMs.

In vivo, STXBP6 knockdown markedly suppressed subcutaneous tumor growth and peritoneal dissemination, reduced malignant ascites formation, and shifted TAM polarization toward an M1 phenotype, without significantly affecting CD4+ or CD8+ T cell infiltration. These findings indicate that STXBP6 promotes OC progression predominantly through TAM reprogramming rather than direct modulation of adaptive immunity, and the strong concordance between in vitro and in vivo results underscores the physiological relevance of the STXBP6/ALOX5/5-HETE axis in shaping the OC immune microenvironment.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, technical constraints precluded the direct quantification of free AA using targeted lipidomics; this limitation was partially addressed by ELISA-based measurements to indirectly estimate AA availability. Second, while the in vivo use of systemic siRNA targeting STXBP6 in the ID8 model demonstrated potent anti-tumor efficacy, this approach inherently lacks cell-type precision. Consequently, we cannot entirely exclude the potential systemic effects of STXBP6 inhibition on tumor cells or other stromal components within the TME. Although our co-inoculation experiments (Fig. 10) successfully mitigated this concern by specifically evaluating the tumor-promoting capacity of educated TAMs, the absence of a macrophage-specific STXBP6 knockout mouse model (e.g., Lyz2-Cre; Stxbp6∧fl/fl∧) remains a limitation. Future studies employing such cell-type–specific genetic models will be required to definitively dissect the cell-intrinsic role of STXBP6 in TAMs and further validate its therapeutic window within the complex OC microenvironment.

In conclusion, our study identifies STXBP6 as a master regulator of TAM metabolic reprogramming and M2-like polarization within the unique, lipid-rich ovarian cancer TME. Mechanistically, STXBP6 drives a dual-regulatory program by promoting cPLA2-mediated cleavage of PE (P-16:0/20:4) to release free AA, while simultaneously facilitating PKCβ-dependent ALOX5 nuclear translocation. This coordinated activation of the STXBP6/cPLA2/ALOX5/5-HETE axis fuels lipid peroxidation and shapes an immunosuppressive microenvironment, thereby accelerating OC growth and peritoneal dissemination. These findings significantly deepen our understanding of TAM-driven immunometabolic regulation in OC and highlight the STXBP6/cPLA2/ALOX5/5HETE pathway as a promising therapeutic target for lipid-metabolic remodeling and limiting disease progression.
