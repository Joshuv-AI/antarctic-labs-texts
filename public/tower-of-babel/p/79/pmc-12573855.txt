Prevalence of imposter syndrome among Saudi ophthalmology residents: urgent need for support
Authors: Jawaher Alwatban, Yazen Bajaeifer, Saeed Alamoudi, Nouf Alzendi, Sara AlHilali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImposter syndrome is a psychological pattern that involves persistent self-doubt and fear of being exposed as a fraud despite evident competence. It has been reported among medical trainees, including ophthalmology residents, who face high academic and clinical demands. The primary aim of this study is to assess the prevalence of imposter syndrome among ophthalmology residents in Saudi Arabia and to evaluate its impact on professional and personal well-being.MethodsThis is a cross-sectional survey conducted among 101 ophthalmology residents across various residency programs in Saudi Arabia. Clance Imposter Phenomenon Scale was utilized to assess imposter syndrome severity. Sociodemographic data, coping strategies, mental health status, and quality of life indicators were analyzed for potential associations with imposter syndrome severity.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 27.5 ± 1.4 years with nearly equal gender distribution. Most participants were single (66.3%; n = 67) and enrolled in the Central Program (60.4%; n = 61). Imposter syndrome was mild in 11.9% (n = 12 [95% CI: 5.57–18.19]), moderate in 50.5% (n = 51 [95%CI: 40.74–60.25]), significant in 30.7% (n = 31 [95%CI: 21.70–39.69]), and intense in 6.9% (n = 7 [95%CI: 1.98–11.88]). Higher IS scores correlated with Self-criticism (P < 0.001), minimizing achievements (P = 0.015), and avoidance of challenges (P = 0.014. Anxiety (P = 0.036), burnout (P = 0.023), and seeking professional help (P = 0.009) were significantly associated with imposterism severity. This study highlights the association of imposter syndrome with mental health and academic performance. Structured support programs, such as mentorships programs and counselling services, may help to address Imposter syndrome and support professional development among residents.Keywords: Imposter syndrome, Imposterism, Behavioral health, Resident well-being

Background

Imposter syndrome (IS), first described by Clance and Imes in 1978, is a psychological internal experience of intellectual fraudulence. Individuals who suffer from this phenomenon believe their accomplishments and success are due to luck or error despite external evidence of competence [1]. This often results in procrastination or overworking, followed by temporary relief upon recognition, but the cycle soon repeats, potentially leading to chronic stress, burnout, and reduced self-esteem [2]. IS can affect individuals at any stage of their medical career, from students to experienced physicians [3, 4]. It also affects other professions beyond healthcare, such as marketing and software engineering, undermining confidence, and performance [5, 6].

IS is linked to anxiety, depression, and burnout, which can impair decision-making and overall well-being. In medical settings, these psychological effects may lead to a higher risk of medical errors and decreased patient care quality, as self-doubt and stress affect clinical judgment and performance [7–9]. Addressing this condition is crucial for ensuring clinician well-being and patient safety.

The ophthalmology residency program is a transformative phase in the training of physicians where graduated doctors undergo intensive clinical education, specialized skills, and hands-on experience necessary to excel in the specialty. This period, often lasting 4 years, is marked by rigorous schedules, high-pressure situations, demanding workloads, and a steep learning curve, all of which can be overwhelming and contribute to the development of imposter syndrome. In this study, we aim to determine the prevalence of Imposter syndrome among ophthalmology residents in Saudi Arabia, explore the coping strategies taken by the residents to manage IS, and examine the impact of IS on their professional and personal lives.

Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted among ophthalmology residents in Saudi Arabia. Data were collected through an online survey distributed to residents across multiple regions, including the Central, Southern, Eastern, Western, and Taif programs. Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality was ensured to minimize response bias. The study targeted all actively enrolled ophthalmology residents in accredited Saudi residency programs. Of 109 eligible residents, 101 responded (93% response rate), which reduces but does not completely eliminate the possibility of selection bias.

Data collection

A structured, self-administered questionnaire was used to collect data, consisting of three sections. The first section gathered demographic information, including age, gender, marital status, residency program, and postgraduate year (PGY) level. The second section assessed imposter syndrome using the Clance Imposter Phenomenon Scale (CIPS), a validated tool designed to evaluate the imposer feeling. The final section explored coping strategies, the perceived impact of imposter syndrome on mental well-being and quality of life, and treatment-seeking behaviors.

Assessment tools

The Clance Imposter Phenomenon Scale (CIPS) is a widely used, validated, self-reported questionnaire designed to measure the severity of imposter syndrome [10, 11]. Developed by Pauline Rose Clance, it consists of 20 items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all true) to 5 (very true). The scale assesses various dimensions of imposterism, including self-assessment of competency, response to praise, and interpretation of success. The cumulative CIPS score ranges from 20 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater imposter feelings. Respondents scoring 40 or below are classified as having few imposter characteristics, scores between 41 and 60 indicate moderate imposter experiences, scores between 61 and 80 reflect frequent imposter feelings, and scores above 80 indicate intense imposter experiences [11].

Coping strategies were assessed through self-reported behaviors, including seeking validation, engaging in physical activities, overworking, avoiding challenges/risks, and minimizing achievements. Mental health impact was evaluated using structured questions regarding self-reported experiences of anxiety, depression, social isolation, and burnout. Additionally, participants were asked about treatment-seeking behaviors, including whether they had sought professional help or considered medications in response to imposter syndrome-related distress. The impact of imposter syndrome on quality of life was also examined, specifically whether it interfered with daily life, affected academic performance, or had no perceived impact. The study further analyzed the associations between CIPS scores and these variables, with statistical significance assessed through mean scores and p-values to determine potential relationships.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered and stored in Microsoft Excel 365 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington, USA), where data cleaning, management, and coding were also performed. The statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0 (IBM Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages [No. (%)], while continuous variables were reported as mean (± standard deviation, SD) and range [minimum – maximum]. For inferential analysis, the Chi-square (χ²) test was used to assess associations between categorical variables, with the Fisher’s exact test applied when appropriate. A Likert scale analysis approach was utilized to evaluate the impact of different factors based on a developed scoring system. The mean Imposter Syndrome score was compared across various demographic, socioeconomic, educational, and regional categories. A 95% confidence interval (CI) was applied, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. As this study was designed as a descriptive, cross sectional study, multivariable analysis was not performed, and reported association should be interpreted as exploratory.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the institutional review board at King Khaled Eye Specialist Hospital and performed according to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki (RAC 2070023). All participants provided informed consent prior to involvement in the study. In order to protect research participants, the confidentiality and anonymity of participants and their data were preserved.

Results

A total of 109 residents were approached, yielding 101 responses and a 93% response rate. Demographic data of study participants are shown in Table 1. The mean age of participants was 27.5 years (SD ± 1.4, range: 25–31 years), with a nearly equal gender distribution (males: 51.5%, females: 48.5%). Most residents were single (66.3%). Regarding residency program distribution, the Central Program had the highest representation (60.4%), followed by the Western (15.8%), Eastern (14.9%), Southern (6.9%), and Taif Program (2.0%). Residents were evenly distributed across training levels, with PGY-1 having the highest proportion (28.7%), followed by PGY-2 (26.7%), PGY-3 (24.8%), and PGY-4 (19.8%).

Regarding IS severity, 11.9% (12 residents [95% CI: 5.57–18.19])) experienced mild imposterism, while 50.5% (51 residents [95%CI: 40.74–60.25]) reported moderate imposterism. Across age, gender, or marital status no significant differences in CIPS scores were observed as shown in Table 2. Residents aged ≤ 27 years had an average score of 57.5, while those older than 27 years scored 57.3 (P = 0.939). Gender differences were negligible, with males scoring 57.5 and females 57.0. Similarly, marital status showed no significant variation, as single participants had a mean score of 56.8, while married participants scored 57.1. In terms of residency programs, the Central Program had the highest mean CIPS score (58.3), followed by the Western (57.8), Eastern (57.3), Southern (50.9), and Ta’if (44.0). However, these differences were not statistically significant. Among training levels, PGY-2 residents had the highest mean score (61.4), followed by PGY-3 (57.2), PGY-4 (55.1), and PGY-1 (54.9), with no significant differences.

Residents with higher CIPS scores were more likely to engage in maladaptive behavioral and cognitive responses. Table 3 demonstrates association of CIPS score with coping strategies, mental health, treatment-seeking, and quality of life. A significant association was found between higher imposter syndrome scores and behaviors such as avoiding challenges or risks (P = 0.014), minimizing achievements (P = 0.015), and engaging in self-criticism or negative self-talk (P < 0.001). However, no significant relationships were observed between CIPS scores and seeking validation from others, engaging in physical activities, overworking or over-preparing, or smoking/vaping. As shown in Fig. 2, higher CIPS scores were significantly associated with anxiety and burnout. Residents experiencing anxiety had a mean score of 59.3 (P = 0.036), while those with burnout had a mean score of 60.6 (P = 0.023). However, no significant relationship was found between CIPS scores and depression or social isolation.

Residents with higher CIPS scores were also more likely to seek professional help or consider medication. Those who sought professional help had an average score of 64.9, compared to 55.5 for those who did not (P = 0.009). Similarly, residents who considered medication had a mean score of 73.7, while those who did not had a mean score of 56.0 (P = 0.001). In contrast, no significant relationship was found between imposter syndrome scores and talking to a friend or using self-help strategies.

Higher CIPS scores were significantly associated with academic struggles, as residents reporting academic difficulties had a mean score of 63.8, compared to 55.0 for those who did not (P = 0.007). Conversely, those who did not report imposterism affecting any life areas had significantly lower CIPS scores (54.4) than those who did (60.5, P = 0.033). No significant correlation was found between CIPS scores and interference with daily life or impact on relationships.

Discussion

Imposter syndrome (IS), characterized by persistent feelings of inadequacy despite achievement, is a prevalent phenomenon among medical trainees across specialties [12–15]. Studies have reported prevalence rates ranging from 9% to 82% [16]. Likewise, this research highlights its significant presence in Saudi ophthalmology residents, with 89% experiencing moderate to intense imposterism. The combination of surgical and medical aspects in ophthalmology, along with technical skills and a steep learning curve, may contribute to the observed prevalence of IS in this population.

Other studies report demographical factors such as gender and marital status as risk factors for IS, where women and single individuals tend to report higher levels [17–21]. However, our study found no significant correlation between IS and gender, marital status, or other demographic variables, which reinforces the idea that IS is an internalized psychological experience [22]. PGY-2 residents experienced higher levels of imposter syndrome. One possible explanation is that they are in a transitional phase of training. They are no longer PGY-1s but are also not yet senior residents with extensive experience. This “in-between” status creates pressure to take on greater responsibilities while still feeling uncertain about their competence. As a result, they may struggle with self-doubt and perceived inadequacy, making them more vulnerable to imposter syndrome.

Imposter syndrome arises from factors such as fear of failure, fear of success, and low self-esteem [23]. It is strongly linked to self-criticism, avoidance of career advancement, people-pleasing, and overworking [2, 23–25], aligning with our finding on IS association with self-criticism, avoidance of challenges, and minimizing achievements. While over-preparing and seeking validation were not significant in our study, prior research suggests they may still contribute to IS [2, 25]. Additionally, despite the link between smoking, physical activity, and stress, we found no direct association with IS [26, 27]. Beyond individual behaviors, cultural and systemic factors such as hierarchical training environments, stigma surrounding mental health, and limited institutional support may reinforce imposter feelings and discourage residents from seeking timely help.

This syndrome significantly affects mental health, often leading to anxiety, depression, and in severe cases, suicidal ideation [3, 21, 24]. These burdens impair physician well-being, and clinical performance, ultimately affecting patient care quality [8, 28]. Our analysis confirms IS’s correlation with anxiety and burnout, consistent with existing literature, although we found no significant correlation with depression [16, 29, 30]. Additionally, our results confirm that IS negatively influences clinical performance, aligning with previous studies [21, 23]. This may lead to avoidance of clinical opportunities, reinforcing cycles of self-doubt, and impairing career advancement.

The higher IS scores observed among participants who sought professional help or considered medication suggest two key points: First, individuals with severe imposter syndrome may demonstrate insight into their condition by actively seeking mental health support. Second, such awareness tends to emerge only at more advanced stages, underscoring the importance of early identification and intervention. Moreover, it is worth noting that stigma and systemic barriers may prevent trainees from seeking psychological support [31]. These findings reinforce the need for proactive measures and early intervention.

Managing imposter syndrome requires effort from the individual experiencing it. Overcoming self-doubt involves actively challenging negative thoughts and replacing them with a more balanced perspective. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) and Logotherapy (LT) help reframe self-criticism and negative thoughts into constructive thinking [3]. At the program level, residency programs can support residents through peer-mentorship programs that provide guidance and normalize self-doubt. Faculty workshops can train supervisors to identify imposter feelings early and help residents navigate challenges. Accessible counseling services allow residents to seek help safely without fear of stigma. Complementary initiatives, such as cognitive-behavioral skill sessions, further enhance coping strategies, professional growth, and overall well-being. Together, these measures promote belonging, normalize self-doubt, and improve trainees’ well-being, self-efficacy, and patient care.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes establishing causal relationships between imposter syndrome and observed outcomes and limits inferences regarding changes in IS over time. Additionally, participation was voluntary, introducing the potential for self-selection and recall bias. Moreover, observed associations are unadjusted and may be influenced by unexplored confounding variables. Finally, the lack of longitudinal follow-up limits our ability to evaluate the persistence or progression of imposter syndrome during residency. This highlights the need for more comprehensive and longitudinal assessments of imposter syndrome and coping strategies in future research.

Conclusion

Imposter syndrome is widespread among ophthalmology residents in Saudi Arabia. Factors such as self-criticism, minimizing achievements, and avoiding challenges were strongly linked to higher IS severity scores. This study highlights the detrimental impact of IS on mental health, and academic performance. Given these challenges, the need for structured psychological support is urgent. Addressing IS is critical for improving individual well-being, professional growth, and optimal patient care. Recognizing and addressing IS can help reshape residency culture and promote confidence in emerging ophthalmologists.
