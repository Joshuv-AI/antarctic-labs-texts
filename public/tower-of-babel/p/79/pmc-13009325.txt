DJ-1 promotes necroptosis of chondrocytes by the PTEN/PI3K-AKT signaling pathway
Authors: Xiangyu Zu, Wenhan Fu, Hao Tang, Di Yang, Yi Ren, Yetian Li, Wei Huang, Qingli Luo, Jun Chang, Zongsheng Yin, Minmin Wu, Kunpeng Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteoarthritis (OA) is a common degenerative joint disease characterized by progressive cartilage degradation. Necroptosis has been recognized as a process closely associated with OA progression; however, its precise underlying mechanisms remain elusive. In this study, we found that the expression levels of DJ-1 and p-MLKL were significantly elevated in human OA cartilage tissue. Transmission electron microscopy revealed typical necroptotic morphology in OA chondrocytes. In vitro experiments demonstrated that DJ-1 overexpression exacerbated TSZ + LPS-induced chondrocyte necroptosis and disrupted extracellular matrix (ECM) metabolism. The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway can be involved in TNF-mediated necroptosis, while PTEN, as a negative regulator, inhibits the activation of this pathway primarily by dephosphorylating phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)-trisphosphate (PIP3). Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays showed that DJ-1 directly binds to and inhibits PTEN, thereby relieving its suppression of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Treatment with the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 significantly alleviated DJ-1-induced chondrocyte necroptosis and ECM dysregulation. Furthermore, in vivo studies confirmed that DJ-1 knockdown attenuated DMM-induced OA progression and improved cartilage and subchondral bone structure. Collectively, these findings indicate that DJ-1 promotes chondrocyte necroptosis and ECM degradation through regulation of the PTEN/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, thereby accelerating the onset and progression of OA. This may provide a new therapeutic target for the treatment of OA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06122-3.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a common chronic degenerative joint disease that primarily affects weight-bearing joints such as the knees and hips [1–3]. With the aging of the population, the incidence of OA continues to rise, significantly compromising patients’ quality of life. The main pathological features of OA include articular cartilage degradation, synovial inflammation, subchondral bone remodeling, and loss of joint function, among which the progressive degeneration of cartilage is considered the central event in OA progression [4]. The pathogenesis of OA is highly complex, influenced by various factors including biological processes such as cell death and extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, genetic susceptibility, biomechanical stress, and chronic inflammation [5, 6]. Therefore, studying the molecular mechanisms of cartilage metabolism disorders may provide an idea for the treatment and prevention of OA.

Programmed cell death of chondrocytes plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis (OA). Necroptosis, a newly recognized form of programmed cell death, has attracted widespread attention due to its pronounced pro-inflammatory characteristics [7, 8]. A series of molecular interactions drives the signaling cascade of necroptosis. Upon activation, RIPK1 forms a complex with RIPK3, promoting the autophosphorylation and activation of RIPK3. Activated RIPK3 then phosphorylates the downstream effector MLKL, triggering its oligomerization and translocation to the plasma membrane. There, MLKL forms pores in the membrane, ultimately leading to cell rupture and damage to the surrounding tissue [9]. Necroptosis contributes to OA progression by causing chondrocyte dysfunction and promoting extracellular matrix degradation, thereby accelerating cartilage degeneration. The elevated expression of necroptosis-related molecules in OA cartilage, especially in the late stages, highlights the pivotal role of necroptosis in the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis [10]. PI3K is widely recognized for its critical role in activating protein kinase B (AKT), thereby regulating various physiological processes such as cell development, differentiation, proliferation, survival, and migration. Studies have reported that inhibitors of PI3K and its downstream target AKT can block tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-induced necroptosis, suggesting that the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway is involved in this process [11–13]. However, the mechanism by which PI3K triggers necroptosis remains unclear.

DJ-1 (also known as Parkinson’s disease protein 7) is a multifunctional protein that plays a pivotal role in maintaining cellular homeostasis. It is particularly well known for its antioxidant properties, protecting cells from damage and apoptosis by scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) [14, 15]. However, studies have shown that DJ-1 may exert effects contrary to its well-established antioxidant role under certain pathological conditions. For instance, in models of liver injury, the absence of DJ-1 was found to alleviate oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, thereby reducing hepatocellular damage [16]. Similarly, in Parkinson’s disease, N-homocysteinylation of DJ-1 impairs its antioxidant and mitochondrial protective functions, which in turn exacerbates neuronal cytotoxicity [17]. These findings highlight the context-dependent nature of DJ-1’s function, suggesting that its role may vary across different diseases or tissues due to differences in pathological conditions and cellular microenvironments.DJ-1 also plays a critical role in skeletal physiology. By scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS), it inhibits osteoclast differentiation and helps maintain metabolic homeostasis in skeletal muscle through the regulation of mitochondrial function. Moreover, literature indicates that ROS is associated with necroptosis [18]. However, in rheumatoid arthritis, DJ-1 is involved in the regulation of osteoclast formation, thereby affecting bone tissue homeostasis. These findings suggest that in cartilage degenerative diseases such as osteoarthritis (OA), DJ-1 may exert a counter-regulatory effect, potentially serving as a key modulator in the development and progression of OA.

PTEN (phosphatase and tensin homolog), a crucial tumor suppressor gene, has been shown to negatively regulate the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway by dephosphorylating phosphatidylinositol-3,4,5-trisphosphate (PIP3), thereby controlling multiple cellular signaling pathways [19, 20]. Zhang and colleagues found that PTEN deficiency led to sustained activation of Akt signaling, triggering oxidative stress and senescence in chondrocytes, which ultimately induced osteoarthritis-like lesions [21]. Additionally, PTEN loss was demonstrated to exacerbate LPS-induced inflammation and apoptosis in chondrocytes [22]. DJ-1 can directly interact with PTEN and act as its negative regulator by inhibiting PTEN’s phosphatase activity [23, 24], thereby indirectly activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and participating in the regulation of various cellular functions [25, 26]. However, the role of DJ–1–mediated PTEN regulation in the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis (OA) remains unclear. It remains unclear whether this pathway exerts a chondroprotective effect via PI3K/AKT activation or contributes to cartilage degeneration by promoting inflammatory responses, highlighting the need for further investigation.

In this study, we identified DJ-1 as a key factor in the onset and progression of osteoarthritis (OA). Our findings demonstrate that DJ-1 regulates PI3K/AKT-mediated chondrocyte necroptosis through its interaction with PTEN, suggesting that the DJ-1/PTEN/PI3K/AKT signaling axis may serve as a potential therapeutic target for OA intervention.

Materials and methods

Patients and tissue samples

We obtained samples of human OA cartilage from individuals who underwent total knee replacement surgery at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (No PJ20230131). The hospital’s ethics committee approved the sample collection, and all patients provided written informed consent. Healthy human cartilage samples were obtained from donors.

Primary culture of human chondrocytes isolation and culture

Cartilage tissues harvested from human femoral condyles and tibial plateaus were initially washed three times with sterile PBS containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin (HyClone, USA). The cartilage was thoroughly cut into pieces of about 1mm3, and transferred into a 50 mL centrifuge tube, Afterward, 0.25% trypsin was added, and the mixture was incubated for 30 min before being centrifuged at 1000 rpm. After centrifugation, trypsin was removed, and 0.2% type II collagen, which was dissolved in DMEM/F12, was digested at 37 °C for 10 h. Isolated chondrocytes were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (HyClone, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. The medium was refreshed every 48 h, and only first-passage cells were utilized for subsequent experiments. To establish an in vitro model of chondrocyte necroptosis, human articular chondrocytes were treated with increasing concentrations of TSZ (1, 2, 2.5, and 5 µM) in combination with LPS. Western blot analysis showed that the expression of phosphorylated MLKL (p-MLKL) was most markedly elevated following treatment with 2.5 µM TSZ and 10 µg/mL LPS (Supplementary Fig. 1). Therefore, the chondrocytes were exposed to TSZ 2.5µM + LPS 10 µg/mL (Beyotime, China) for 24 h.

Quantitative Real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT–PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from both cartilage tissue and human primary chondrocytes using TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After RNA extraction, 500ng of RNA was used to synthesize complementary DNA with an accurate reverse transcription kit (Accurate Biotechnology, China). The qRT-PCR assays were performed using qRT-PCR Master Mix (Accurate Biotechnology, China), with 40 cycles of 15 s at 95 °C followed by 30 s at 60 °C. The expression levels of the genes were normalized to those of Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and quantified using the ΔΔCT method. Western blot assay

Proteins were extracted using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Invitrogen, USA). Protein levels were calculated using the BCA protein assay. Approximately 20 µg of proteins were separated on 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) gels and transferred onto Millipore polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, USA). Membranes were then blocked with 5% fat-free milk (Sangon, China) in Tris-buffered saline/Tween solution for 2 h at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight. Following overnight incubation, the membrane was incubated for an hour with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies diluted to 1:10000. The Chemo Dox XRS system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA) was used to view the images from the blots. The optical density of each band was calculated using the ImageJ program (MD, USA). Analyses were performed as described previously. The antibodies used included rabbit anti-p-RIP3(Affinity, AF7443, China), rabbit anti-RIP3(Affinity, AF7942, China), rabbit anti-p-MLKL(Affinity, AF7420, China), rabbit anti-MLKL(Affinity, DF7412, China), rabbit anti-RIP1(Affinity, AF7877, China), rabbit anti-p-PI3K(Affinity, AF3242, China), rabbit anti-PI3K (Affinity, AF6241, China), rabbit anti-p-AKT(Affinity, AF0016, China), rabbit anti-AKT(Affinity, AF0836, China), rabbit anti-Aggrecan(Affinity, DF7561, China), rabbit anti-CollagenⅡ (Affinity, AF0135, China), rabbit anti-MMP3(Affinity, AF0217, China), rabbit anti-MMP13(Affinity, AF5355, China), rabbit anti-DJ-1(Abcam, ab76008, UK), rabbit anti-PTEN(Abcam, ab267787, UK), and mouse-anti-DJ-1 (Abcam, ab119767, UK), mouse-anti-β-actin(Affinity, AF7018, China).

Immunofluorescence assays

Human primary chondrocytes were treated with TSZ (2.5 µM) and LPS (10 µg/mL) for 24 h and then seeded on a slide in a 12-well plate (1 *105 cells/well). The cells were fixed for 15 min with freshly prepared 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by three washes with PBS. The cells were then blocked with 5% (w/v) bovine serum albumin for 1 h at room temperature, treated with 0.5% (v/v) Triton X-100 for 10 min, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The cells were subsequently incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies (1:500) for 1 h in a dark room. DAPI (Beyotime, China) was added, and the cells were visualized after 5 minutes. Finally, the samples were examined and photographed using an inverted fluorescence microscope (LEICA, Germany).

Plasmids and SiRNA

The open reading frame encoding DJ-1 (GenBank ID: GU175984.1) was amplified using qRT-PCR of chondrocyte RNA. The full-length DJ-1 was then subcloned into the pSin-3HA vector. siRNA against DJ-1 was purchased from RiboBio Co, Ltd. (Guangzhou, China) and transfected into human knee chondrocytes according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Three DJ-1 siRNAs were used: DJ-1-siRNA#1: GGTTCTACCAGGAGGTAAT (sense); DJ-1-siRNA #2: AGACGGTCATCCCTGTAGA (sense); DJ-1-siRNA#3: GGTCATTACACCTACTCTG (sense). Before transfection, cells were seeded into a 12-well plate without antibiotics and cultured to 30–50% confluency for 24 h. Cells were transfected with 50 µM siRNA using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). After 24 h of transfection, the complexes were removed, and a fresh culture medium containing 10% FBS was added. Cells were collected for RNA and protein extraction 24 h later.

Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

Lysed human primary chondrocytes (6*105) were statically lysed on ice, and pre-cooled lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl,2 mM EGTA, and 1% Triton X-100) containing Complete™ protease inhibitor (Roche Applied Science, USA). Blown and mixed, and static lysed on ice for 30 min. Centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 degrees Celsius, and the supernatant was collected. Subsequently, the supernatant samples were incubated with anti-DJ-1 (Abcam, ab76008, USA), anti-PTEN (Abcam, ab170941, USA), and normal IgG antibody-binding protein A/G magnetic beads were incubated for 4 h at 4 °C on a rotating device. The immunoprecipitants were washed 3 times with pre-cooled 0.1% Triton X-100 in lysis buffer and then washed 3 times with PBS. Bead complexes were collected, and the proteins were denatured by boiling at 100 degrees for 10 min and analyzed by western blotting.

Flow cytometry analysis

Human primary chondrocytes (approximately 1*106 cells/ml) in each well were washed twice with cold PBS and collected using trypsin-free Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution diluted in the buffer. The cells were washed twice and then resuspended in 500 µL of staining buffer. Five microliters of PI staining solution were added to the cell suspensions and mixed gently. The cells were incubated in the dark at 4 °C for 10 min. After incubation, cells were then washed with PBS and resuspended in fresh PBS. Flow cytometry (BD, USA) was used to detect necroptosis of chondrocytes, and the cell necrosis rate was expressed as a percentage of propidium iodide-positive to propidium iodide-negative cells.

Live-Dead staining assay

Human primary chondrocytes were treated with or without TSZ (2.5µM) + LPS (10 µg/mL) for 24 h. After treatment, the cells were washed gently three times with PBS, calcein-AM (100µL) was added to cover the slide, and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. The cells were then washed twice with PBS, and PI (100 µL) was added to the slide. The cells were incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark, followed by washing with PBS. The cells were examined and imaged using an inverted fluorescent microscope (LEICA, Germany).

Transmission electron microscopy

Human primary chondrocytes were cultured in 25 cm2 culture flasks, washed in cold PBS, and digested using 0.05% trypsin-free EDTA. The cell was fixed with glutaraldehyde at a concentration of 2.5% at room temperature The cells were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at room temperature for 2 hours, and then overnight at 4 °C. After fixation, the cells were dehydrated using ethanol at different concentrations (50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%), followed by further dehydration with 100% acetone and embedding in epoxy. The fixed cell aggregates were sliced into ultrathin sections using an ultramicrotome and stained. The ultrastructure of the cells was examined using a transmission electron microscope (Talos L120C G2, USA). Tissue samples were prepared using the same method as for cells.

Mouse OA model and treatment

Forty male C57/BL mice, aged 10–12 weeks and weighing 20–22 g, were utilized in this study. Osteoarthritis (OA) was induced in mice by surgically destabilizing the medial meniscus (DMM). To anesthetize the mice, pentobarbital sodium (40–50 mg/kg) was intraperitoneally injected. The area surrounding the knee joint was clipped and sterilized. A longitudinal incision was made along the medial side of the patellar ligament to expose the area between the femoral condyles of the knee. The medial meniscal tibial ligament was cut, and the joint was flushed with sterile saline. The articular cavity was sutured with 7 − 0 surgical sutures, and the skin wound was closed using topical antibiotics. This revision reduces repetition and improves clarity by breaking down the procedure into steps and providing more detail where necessary. The control group of C57/BL mice (n = 6) underwent arthroscopic surgery on the right knee joint without DMM. To knock down DJ-1 expression, a recombinant adenovirus expressing short hairpin RNA targeting DJ-1 (Ad-shDJ-1) was constructed and purchased from [General Biol, China]. A non-targeting control adenovirus (Ad-shCtrl) was used as a negative control. The sequences of the shRNA were as follows: shDJ-1: 5′-ATCTGGGTGCACAGAATTTAT-3′, shCtrl: 5′-ttCtCCGAACGtGtCACGt-3′. The mice were then randomly divided into four groups: sham, DMM, DMM + Ad-shCtrl, and DMM + Ad-shDJ-1 groups. Mice in the DMM + Ad-shDJ-1 group received an intra-articular injection of 10 µL of 1013 µg/mL of Ad-shDJ-1, while mice in the DMM + Ad-shCtrl group received an intra-articular injection of 10 µL of 1013 µg/mL of Ad-shCtrl.

Micro-computed tomography

Knee joints were scanned with micro-computed tomography (CT) (Sky Scan 1176, USA), conducted. For 120 min at 800 µA and 50 kV with a resolution of 12 μm. Analysis of micro-CT data was performed using CTAn (SkyScan, USA) in conjunction with Medical 21 (Materialize, Belgium). In this study, 3D structural parameters were analyzed for the subchondral region of the tibial plateau, along with the following morphological parameters: (1) trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) (mm); (2) Bone volume/total tissue volume (BV/TV) (%); (3) trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) (mm); and (4) trabecular mesh factor (Tb.Pf) (1/mm). and (5) trabecular number (Tb.N) (1/mm).

Histology

The experimental mice in all groups were sacrificed 8 weeks after surgery, and their knee joints were dissected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Decalcification was then performed using 10% EDTA for 2–3 weeks. The tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned coronally at 5 mm thickness, and stained with safranin O fast green according to standard protocols. Hematoxylin-eosin staining experiments were performed. The severity of osteoarthritis was determined using the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) scoring system.

Immunohistochemical analysis

The expression levels of DJ-1, PTEN, p-MLKL, MMP13, and Aggrecan were detected using immunohistochemical staining. Cartilage tissue was fixed with paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5 mm thick slices. The sections were deparaffinized and treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min, then sealed with 5% normal serum and blocked for 30 min. Primary antibodies (DJ-1, PTEN, p-MLKL, MMP13, and Aggrecan) were used to incubate the sections, followed by secondary antibodies. The sections were then imaged using light microscopy, and ImageJ version 6.0 was used to analyze each image. The levels of DJ-1, PTEN, p-MLKL, MMP13, and Aggrecan were determined using integral absorbance values.

Statistical analysis

Three independent experiments were represented in all the data. All results were presented as mean ± standard deviation. GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software, USA) was used to perform all statistical analyses. Two groups were compared using unpaired t-test, and three or more groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The DJ-1 is upregulated in OA cartilage and associated with chondrocyte necroptosis

The incidence of osteoarthritis (OA) increases with age, and numerous studies have shown that necroptosis is closely associated with the progression of OA [7, 27] Although DJ-1 is considered to have antioxidant and cytoprotective roles, recent studies have revealed that it may exert opposite effects under certain pathological conditions [28, 29]. To investigate the role of DJ-1 in cartilage degeneration, western blotting was performed on cartilage from OA patients, and the results revealed that DJ-1 expression was significantly upregulated in OA cartilage compared to the control group (Fig. 1A-B). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) results showed that DJ-1 and p-MLKL expression was markedly increased in the cartilage of OA patients (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, transmission electron microscopy showed that chondrocytes in OA cartilage exhibited hallmark morphological features of necrosis, including compromised membrane integrity and disorganized intracellular structures, while healthy cartilage maintained normal morphology with no signs of damage (Fig. 1D). Next, we established an in vitro osteoarthritis model of human primary chondrocytes necroptosis by treating cells with LPS + TSZ. Live/Dead staining showed a significant increase in the number of dead cells in the LPS + TSZ-treated group (Fig. 1E), indicating the successful establishment of the chondrocyte necroptosis model. Consistent with the tissue findings, DJ-1 protein levels were significantly elevated in LPS + TSZ-treated human primary chondrocytes compared to untreated controls (Fig. 1F). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that DJ-1 promoted the phosphorylation of MLKL (Fig. 1G). Collectively, these findings indicate that DJ-1 is upregulated during OA progression and may serve as a critical regulator of chondrocyte necroptosis.

DJ-1 promotes necroptosis in human primary chondrocytes

To elucidate the role of DJ-1 in chondrocyte necroptosis, we transfected DJ-1-HA fusion protein into the LPS + TSZ-treated human primary chondrocytes, and western blot analysis exhibited a significant upregulation of RIP1, p-RIP3, and p-MLKL, key proteins involved in necroptosis, compared to the control group (Fig. 2A-B). Immunofluorescence staining showed that the fluorescence intensity of p-MLKL was significantly enhanced in DJ-1-overexpressing human primary chondrocytes (Fig. 2C). ATP assays demonstrated that DJ-1 overexpression exacerbated the excessive ATP consumption, leading to a further reduction in intracellular ATP levels and cell viability (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed ultrastructural hallmarks of necroptosis in human primary chondrocytes treated with TSZ + LPS. These included plasma membrane rupture, mitochondrial swelling, organelle disintegration, and nuclear histone release. Overexpression of DJ-1 significantly exacerbated these necroptotic morphological alterations (Fig. 2E). Flow cytometry revealed a significantly higher percentage of PI-positive cells in the DJ-1-overexpressing group, providing further evidence that DJ-1 promotes necroptosis (Fig. 2F).

To further investigate the regulatory role of DJ-1 in necroptosis, we employed siRNA-mediated knockdown to reduce DJ-1 expression in human primary chondrocytes and explored its function in the necroptosis process. We designed three siRNAs targeting DJ-1 and evaluated their knockdown efficiency through Western blot analysis. The results showed that siRNA3 exhibited the highest silencing efficiency among the three siRNAs (Fig. 3A), and therefore, siRNA3 was selected for subsequent experiments. Further analysis revealed that silencing DJ-1 significantly reduced the expression levels of RIP1, accompanied by a decrease in RIP3 and MLKL phosphorylation in human primary chondrocytes (Fig. 3B-C). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that the fluorescence intensity of p-MLKL was significantly attenuated in DJ-1-knockdown chondrocytes compared with controls (Fig. 3D). These findings establish DJ-1 as a potent activator of the necroptosis signaling cascade in human primary chondrocytes.

DJ-1 Binding to PTEN promotes PI3K/AKT pathway activation in human primary chondrocytes

Previous studies have shown that downregulation of PTEN can induce oxidative stress and exacerbate inflammation in human primary chondrocytes [30], and DJ-1 has also been reported to directly bind to PTEN and inhibit its phosphatase activity [31]. We performed molecular docking simulations (PyMOL) and identified several high-affinity binding interfaces between DJ-1 and PTEN (Fig. 4A). To confirm the interaction between DJ-1 and PTEN, we conducted co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays in primary human chondrocytes. In the forward Co-IP, immunoprecipitation of endogenous DJ-1 followed by immunoblotting revealed co-precipitation of PTEN, indicating an association between the two proteins (Fig. 4B, left panel). Conversely, in the reverse Co-IP, immunoprecipitation of endogenous PTEN successfully pulled down DJ-1 (Fig. 4B, right panel). Additionally, we employed si-DJ-1 and the PTEN inhibitor VO Ohpic trihydrate (2.5 µM, Selleck) to knock down the expression of DJ-1 and PTEN respectively, the binding of DJ-1 and PTEN was markedly reduced (Supplementary Fig. 2). Immunofluorescence analysis showed that DJ-1 co-localized with PTEN and suppressed PTEN expression in human primary chondrocytes following LPS + TSZ treatment (Fig. 4C). To validate this observation, we overexpressed DJ-1 in human primary chondrocytes and found that PTEN protein levels were markedly decreased (Fig. 4D-E). PTEN is a negative regulator of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [32]. human primary chondrocytes were treated with various concentrations of PTEN activators Oroxin B (0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20µM). CCK8 assay revealed Oroxin B exhibited no significant cytotoxicity at concentrations of 0.5 µM or below (Supplementary Fig. 3). Therefore, 0.5 µM Oroxin B was chosen for further experiments. Western blot analysis showed DJ-1 overexpression led to a significant increase in phosphorylated of PI3K and AKT, while PTEN activator Oroxin B (MCE, 0.5 µM) upregulates PTEN expression, reversing these effects (Fig. 4F-G). Collectively, these results suggest that DJ-1 promotes PI3K/AKT pathway activation by binding to and inhibiting PTEN.

DJ-1 promotes chondrocyte necroptosis by inhibiting pten and activating the PI3K/AKT Pathway

To further elucidate the regulatory role of DJ-1 in chondrocyte necroptosis through PTEN interaction and PI3K/AKT pathway activation, we pretreated DJ-1-overexpressing human primary chondrocytes with the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 (10 µM, MCE). As expected, LY294002 abolished DJ-1 overexpression-induced PTEN downregulation and the upregulation of RIP1, p-RIP3, and p-MLKL (Fig. 5A-B). Consistent with the above findings, immunofluorescence analysis showed that LY294002 pretreatment abolished the activation of p‑MLKL induced by DJ‑1 (Fig. 5C). Additionally, ATP assays revealed that DJ-1 overexpression exacerbated TSZ + LPS-induced intracellular ATP depletion, which were significantly reversed by LY294002 pretreatment (Fig. 5D). Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that the increase in PI-positive cells caused by DJ-1 overexpression was alleviated following LY294002 treatment (Fig. 5E). We employ AKT inhibitors A-443,654 (2 µM, MCE) to suppress DJ-1 overexpression-induced upregulation of p-RIP3 and p-MLKL (Supplementary Fig. 4). Collectively, these results indicate that DJ-1 promotes chondrocyte necroptosis by inhibiting PTEN and activating the PI3K/AKT pathway.

DJ-1 promotes TSZ + LPS-induced dysregulation of chondrocyte matrix metabolism

Subsequently, we explored the effect of DJ-1-mediated necroptosis regulation via the PI3K/AKT pathway on extracellular matrix (ECM) metabolism in human primary chondrocytes. Western blot results showed that DJ-1 overexpression significantly increased the protein levels of MMP3 and MMP13, while markedly decreasing the protein expression of Collagen II and Aggrecan (Fig. 6A-B). RT-qPCR also confirmed that DJ-1 overexpression increased the mRNA levels of MMP3 and MMP13, while decreasing Collagen II and Aggrecan (Fig. 6C). In addition, immunofluorescence staining showed that compared with the control group, DJ-1 overexpression markedly enhanced the fluorescence intensity of MMP13 and reduced the fluorescence intensity of Aggrecan (Fig. 6D-E). In subsequent experiments, we explored whether DJ-1 regulates chondrocyte anabolic metabolism via the PTEN/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. By using the PTEN activator Oroxin B (MCE, 0.5 µM), we found that Oroxin B treatment significantly reversed the DJ-1 overexpression-induced upregulation of MMP3 and MMP13 as well as the downregulation of Aggrecan and Collagen II (Fig. 6F-G). Collectively, these results indicate that DJ-1 promotes ECM degradation in human primary chondrocytes by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway through PTEN inhibition, thereby contributing to the dysregulation of chondrocyte matrix metabolism.

DJ-1 knockdown attenuates DMM-induced osteoarthritis and improves cartilage and subchondral bone structure

To evaluate the role of DJ-1 in cartilage damage in the mouse model, we established an osteoarthritis (OA) model by performing destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) surgery, which compromises the stability of the medial meniscus in the right knee joint. Western blot analysis revealed a significant increase in DJ-1 expression in the mouse arthritis model at the eighth week post-modeling (Supplementary Fig. 5).DJ-1 expression was suppressed in OA mice using a DJ-1 knockdown lentiviral vector. Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining and Safranin O/Fast Green staining revealed that the cartilage surface of sham-operated mice remained intact with strong proteoglycan staining, whereas OA model mice showed marked loss of proteoglycans, decreased chondrocyte density, significant surface fibrillation, and structural disruption of the cartilage. Notably, DJ-1 knockdown significantly alleviated cartilage degeneration and improved cartilage structure in OA mice (Fig. 7A). This was further confirmed by Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) scoring, which demonstrated that DJ-1 knockdown effectively attenuated cartilage damage in OA mice (Fig. 7B). Micro-CT analysis showed that, compared with the sham group, OA mice exhibited a significant increase in bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), along with a notable decrease in trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) and the trabecular pattern factor (Tb.Pf). However, DJ-1 knockdown effectively improved trabecular architecture, reduced cartilage degeneration and subchondral bone sclerosis, and significantly increased bone mineral density (Figs. 7C–D). Furthermore, immunohistochemical analysis indicated that, compared with the OA + sh-NC group, DJ-1 knockdown significantly upregulated PTEN expression in cartilage tissue, while expression of the necroptosis marker p-MLKL and the matrix-degrading enzyme MMP13 was notably reduced. Meanwhile, the expression of aggrecan was significantly increased (Fig. 8). Taken together, these results suggest that DJ-1 can accelerate osteoarthritis progression by promoting chondrocyte necroptosis and extracellular matrix degradation through regulation of the PTEN/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

Discussion

Osteoarthritis (OA), an age-related metabolic disorder, is characterized by low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress as its final common pathological pathways [33]. The clinical manifestations of OA, including pain, joint stiffness, and impaired mobility, progressively lead to severe disability and significantly reduced quality of life. However, the precise pathogenic mechanisms remain elusive [34]. Chondrocytes, the primary cellular components of articular cartilage, play a crucial role in maintaining extracellular matrix (ECM) homeostasis, and their functional impairment directly contributes to cartilage degeneration [35, 36]. Necroptosis, a form of inflammatory programmed cell death, can be triggered by various environmental stressors such as chemical insults, mechanical injury, inflammatory stimuli, and pathogenic infections [7, 37]. Increasing evidence indicates that chondrocyte necroptosis plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of OA.

DJ-1 is a highly conserved homodimeric protein that plays a critical role in a variety of pathological processes, including Parkinson’s disease, liver dysfunction, and several inflammatory disorders [38, 39]. DJ-1 is widely recognized for its cytoprotective and anti-inflammatory effects, but recent studies suggest that under certain pathological conditions, it may exert opposite effects. For instance, in hepatocytes, DJ-1 deficiency significantly enhances the accumulation and protein stability of PARKIN in the mitochondria, thereby alleviating ischemia/reperfusion (I/R)-induced liver injury and inflammation [40]. Additionally, DJ-1 knockout reduces pro-inflammatory cell infiltration and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in the liver of mice, offering protective effects against acetaminophen (APAP)-induced acute liver injury [16]. Despite these findings, the specific regulatory role of DJ-1 in cartilage injury remains largely unexplored. Our results demonstrate that DJ-1 is highly expressed in human osteoarthritic cartilage and a TSZ + LPS-induced chondrocyte necroptosis model. DJ-1 overexpression led to increased levels of key necroptosis-related proteins, including RIP1, p-RIP3, and p-MLKL, whereas DJ-1 knockdown suppressed the expression of these necroptotic markers. These findings suggest that DJ-1 plays a critical role in regulating necroptosis during osteoarthritis progression.

PTEN is a well-characterized tumor suppressor gene encoding a protein/lipid phosphatase that plays a pivotal role in maintaining cellular homeostasis [41]. Its primary molecular function involves the dephosphorylation of PIP3, thereby inhibiting activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [42]. Through this mechanism, PTEN negatively regulates various cellular processes, including cell survival, metabolism, and apoptosis [43]. Studies have shown that deficiency of PTEN has been shown to result in sustained activation of the Akt signaling pathway, triggering oxidative stress-induced chondrocyte senescence and causing OA [21]. Additionally, PTEN deficiency aggravates LPS-induced inflammation and apoptosis in human primary chondrocytes [22]. The PI3K-AKT signaling pathway plays a critical role in TNF-induced necroptosis, with PI3K acting as a partner of RIP3 to initiate the activation of the RIP1-RIP3-MLKL cascade, ultimately leading to necroptotic cell death [44]. Our study revealed that DJ-1 can directly interact with PTEN and act as a negative regulator of its expression, thereby activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and promoting the expression of necroptosis-related marker proteins RIP1, p-RIP3, and p-MLKL in human primary chondrocytes. Additionally, DJ-1 overexpression promoted the expression of extracellular matrix (ECM)-degrading enzymes MMP3 and MMP13, while inhibiting the expression of ECM-synthesizing proteins such as aggrecan and Collagen II in human primary chondrocytes. Furthermore, treatment with the PTEN activator Oroxin B suppressed the expression of these DJ-1-induced catabolic markers related to ECM synthesis and degradation. These findings indicate that DJ-1 can regulate necroptosis and ECM metabolism by modulating the PTEN-PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

In the OA mouse model, DJ-1 knockdown effectively delayed cartilage degeneration, accompanied by a significant upregulation of PTEN expression in cartilage tissue. Meanwhile, the expression levels of p-MLKL and MMP13 were reduced, while Aggrecan expression was increased. These findings further support that DJ-1 promotes chondrocyte necroptosis and extracellular matrix degradation by regulating the PTEN/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, thereby accelerating the development and progression of osteoarthritis.

Recent studies increasingly highlight the pivotal role of necroptosis in cartilage degeneration during OA [45]. Unlike immunologically silent apoptosis, necroptosis triggers the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and proinflammatory cytokines, exacerbating local inflammation and extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation [46, 47]. DJ-1, widely recognized for its antioxidant and cytoprotective functions in Parkinson’s disease and oxidative stress-related disorders [27, 48]. However, our study reveals a functional shift of DJ-1 in OA, a condition characterized by persistent low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress. Rather than serving a protective role, DJ-1 acts as a key pathogenic regulator that promotes chondrocyte death and matrix breakdown. Mechanistically, we further demonstrate that DJ-1 directly binds to PTEN and suppresses its expression, thereby relieving PTEN’s inhibitory effect on the PI3K/AKT pathway. This disinhibition activates downstream necroptotic signaling, ultimately leading to chondrocyte necroptosis.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that the critical role of DJ-1 in the pathogenesis of OA expands our understanding of OA mechanisms and opens new avenues for the development of targeted therapies. By delving into the molecular basis of OA, future interventions targeting the DJ-1-PTEN-PI3K/AKT axis hold the potential to improve patient outcomes and offer new hope for disease modification in OA (Fig. 9).
