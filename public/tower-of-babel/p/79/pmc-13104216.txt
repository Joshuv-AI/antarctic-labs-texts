Heavy metals and prevalence of sexually transmitted infections: a cross-sectional study using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2003–2016
Authors: Brandie DePaoli Taylor, Akaninyene Noah, Victor Adekanmbi, Yuanyi Zhang, Marybeth Ingle, Abbey Berenson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeavy metals can impact immune system function by increasing susceptibility and progression of infections. Few studies have considered if heavy metals are associated with sexually transmitted infections (STIs). Our aim was to examine the relationship between heavy metals measured in urine and STIs.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). We examined Chlamydia trachomatis and high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) genotypes, two of the most common STIs. Nine heavy metals measured in urine were included in the analyses. Poisson regression with robust error variance was used to determine the prevalence ratio (PR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for the association between the heavy metals and STIs. All models adjusted for age, smoking, race and ethnicity, sex, and the poverty-index ratio.ResultsUrinary levels of uranium (PR 1.20, 95% CI 1.05–1.36) and antimony (PR 1.20, 95% CI 1.01–1.44) were associated with chlamydia. Urine barium (PR 1.17, 95% CI 1.06–1.29) was associated with HPV 16/18 genotypes. Similar patterns were observed for urine antimony, cadmium, cesium, lead, mercury, tungsten, and uranium. When stratified by sex, these associations were only observed among female participants.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that heavy metal exposure was associated with STIs. Because this was a cross-sectional study, we cannot determine temporality, and additional research is needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13046-7.

Background

Chlamydia trachomatis and human papillomavirus (HPV) are two of the most common sexually transmitted infections (STIs) in the United States [1]. Both infections pose significant public health concerns due to their asymptomatic nature, associated long-term health sequelae, and substantial racial and socioeconomic disparities in rates [2, 3]. Chlamydia trachomatis is associated with pelvic inflammatory disease, ectopic pregnancy, infertility, and adverse pregnancy outcomes in women [4], while in men, it can lead to urethritis, epididymitis, and prostatitis [5]. High-risk HPV genotypes can lead to cancers of the cervix, vagina, penis, anus, and oropharynx [6].

Environmental pollution, particularly heavy metals, has become a growing area of concern in public health [7]. Heavy metals can be defined as metal or metalloid compounds with a density greater than 5 g/cm3 [8]. Heavy metal pollution refers to exposure from essential and non-essential metal ions such as lead or cadmium, which oxidize in the body to yield toxic by-products and subsequent oxidative stress [8]. Heavy metal exposure can negatively impact innate and adaptive immune system function [9], increasing the susceptibility and/or severity of infections [10]. Despite the widespread nature of these exposures and potential for long-term health damage, few studies have examined the impact of heavy metal exposure on STIs, underscoring the need for more research on this topic.

We hypothesize that heavy metal exposure is associated with STIs, specifically focusing on Chlamydia trachomatis, the most common bacterial STI, and HPV, the most common viral STI. Using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), we examined the associations between several heavy metals (barium, cadmium, cobalt, cesium, mercury, molybdenum, lead, antimony, thallium, tungsten, uranium) and each of these STIs.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a cross-sectional study using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a continuous survey program of the non-institutionalized population of the United States. NHANES is a program of the National Center for Health Statistics, which is part of the Centers for Disease Prevention and Control (CDC). To ensure the survey sample population is representative of the US resident population, NHANES uses a complex multistage, clustered sampling design with differential selection probabilities for different groups [11]. Survey data are collected in two stages. Participants undergo interviews and complete questionnaires during the first stage and physical and laboratory testing in the second stage. Informed consent is collected from all survey participants. Detailed information on NHANES recruitment, questionnaires, sample design, specimen collection, and processing techniques can be found at the CDC website [11].

Due to variations in the availability of data for heavy metal measurements, chlamydia, and HPV test results, we used different data sets for our analyses. To investigate the relationship between heavy metals and chlamydia, we used data from seven NHANES cycles (2003–2004, 2005–2006, 2007–2008, 2009–2010, 2011–2012, 2013–2014, and 2015–2016). For the association between heavy metals and high-risk HPV, we analyzed data from five survey cycles (2007–2008, 2009–2010, 2011–2012, 2013–2014, 2015–2016). Unfortunately, STI data were not publicly available for later cycles (2017–2018 and 2019–2020). The University of Texas Institutional Review Board reviewed the protocol (IRB #22–0112) for this study and determined it to be exempt.

Exclusion criteria

We restricted our study population to individuals ages 18 to 39 years who had complete sociodemographic data, urine metal concentration measurements, and either a chlamydia or HPV infection test (Fig. 1). NHANES STI data are only publicly available for those 18 years of age and older. Pregnant women were excluded from our analysis based on a positive urine pregnancy test or a self-reported pregnancy, as this can influence immunology and susceptibility to STIs [12]. After all exclusions, the study population included 4,370 individuals for the heavy metals and chlamydia analysis and 4,363 individuals for our heavy metals and HPV analysis.

Primary exposure

Our study exposures were heavy metals reported in NHANES, including barium, cadmium, cobalt, cesium, mercury, molybdenum, lead, antimony, thallium, tungsten, and uranium. An inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (ICP-MS, PerkinElmer, Norwalk, CT) was used to determine the concentration of each metal in urine samples [13]. Urine samples were obtained during the medical examination portion of the survey and shipped on dry ice to the National Center for Environmental Health, Atlanta, GA. Samples were stored at -20 °C until analysis. NHANES urine samples are obtained at one time point during the physical examination (i.e., as spot samples). Regression-based creatinine adjustment was applied to account for differences in urinary dilution, with all urinary metal levels calculated as µg/g creatinine [14]. The lower limit of detection (LLOD) for each metal can be found in Supplemental Table 1. Values below the LLOD were imputed by dividing the LLOD by the square root of 2. Detailed information on urine sample storage and processing techniques can be found in the NHANES laboratory procedures manual [13].

Primary outcome

Our primary outcomes were chlamydia and high-risk HPV infections. We categorized high-risk HPV infections into two groups by genotype: HPV 16/18 and other high-risk HPV genotypes (31, 33, 34, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68, and 7). These genotypes have been associated with HPV-related cancers and are important to consider in studies examining HPV prevalence [15].

NHANES detects chlamydia by processing urine samples collected during the medical examination portion of the survey. Between 2003 and 2008, NHANES used a BD Probe Tec amplified DNA assay (McKesson, Irving, TX, USA) to determine if a participant had a positive chlamydia infection [16], and from 2009 to 2016, they used an APTIMA Combo 2 assay test (Hologic, Marlborough, MA, USA) [17]. To test for HPV infections and determine their genotype, NHANES processed DNA samples, obtained from self-collected penile or vaginal swabs, using a Roche linear HPV DNA test (Roche Molecular Systems Inc., Branchburg, NJ, USA). Further details on sample collection and processing methods can be found in the NHANES laboratory procedures manual [13].

Covariates

We used directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) to conceptualize confounders and identify a sufficient adjustment set [18]. We considered several covariates, including the participant’s age (18–25 years and 26–39 years), self-reported sex (male or female), race and ethnicity (Hispanic, non-Hispanic (NH) Black, NH White, and other), family poverty-index ratio [≤ 1.3 (low), > 1.3–3.5 (middle), and > 3.5 (high)], and serum cotinine concentrations (ng/mL) as a proxy for smoking. Race and ethnicity are not biological variables, but we included this variable in our models as a proxy for exposure to structural racism [19]. These variables were selected as they are associated with STIs [1, 3, 20, 21] and may be disproportionately affected by heavy metal exposure [22–24]. Educational attainment (less than high school diploma, high school diploma, some college, college graduate) was also considered. Education tends to operate as a social determinant [25], and associations between education and STIs are potentially mediated through poverty, age, and behavior. For the relationship between education and heavy metals, we theorize that this may be mediated by poverty, housing, and potentially occupation [26]. As a sensitivity analysis, we initially include education in the models, but removing this variable had little impact on our estimates or precision. Although sexual behaviors such as multiple sex partners and lack of condom use are associated with STIs, we could not find evidence that sexual behavior is associated with heavy metal exposure. While body mass index (BMI) has been associated with heavy metals [27], there is no consistent direct association between BMI and STIs [28, 29]. To avoid unnecessary adjustment, we did not consider sexual behavior or BMI as potential confounders [18].

Statistical analyses

We utilized rescaled sample weights to account for biases that could arise from the NHANES clustered sampling design and differential non-response rates across demographic groups. These weights incorporate the post-stratification adjustments conducted by NHANES to align the survey population with population estimates from the US Census Bureau. This approach minimizes potential biases and enhances the generalizability of our results.

To compare metal concentrations between chlamydia-positive and chlamydia-negative study participants, we calculated the geometric mean and standard error for each metal. The potential association between each metal and chlamydia was evaluated using a modified Poisson regression model with robust error variance [30]. This is a widely used analytic method in epidemiological research to determine the point prevalence ratio (PR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for cross-sectional data [31, 32]. This method is often employed when working with NHANES data [33]. High-risk HPV genotypes were common in our study population (> 10%), but the prevalence of both chlamydia and HPV 16/18 was < 10%. As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated our analysis using a logistic regression model and obtained similar results (Supplemental Table 2). The concentrations of each metal were log2-transformed in our analyses to mitigate the impact of outliers. Models were adjusted for age, sex, smoking, race and ethnicity, and family income-to-poverty ratio. Identical analyses were performed for HPV 16/18 and other high-risk HPV genotypes.

To determine the effect of a mixture of our four most correlated heavy metals on STI prevalence, we used a quantile g computation model. Heavy metal exposure commonly occurs as a mixture of two or more metals, and it is essential when assessing their health-related effects to account for individual and joint exposures. Quantile g computation is a modeling technique to assess the joint impact of a mixture, where the exposure is divided into quantiles, and the effect of each quantile increase on STI prevalence is determined [34]. These models were also adjusted for age, sex, race and ethnicity, and family income-to-poverty ratio. As a sensitivity analysis, we stratified all models by sex, as heavy metal concentrations and metabolism differ between males and females, and there are immunological differences between males and females [35]. Stratified analysis is the primary method for effect modification, and this allows us to understand the potential differences in the magnitude and direction of the prevalence ratios. As a sensitivity analysis, we tested interaction on the multiplicative scale by including an interaction term in the models, and on the additive scale using Relative Excess Risk due to Interaction (RERI) [36]. RERI is an epidemiologic metric used to determine the additive risk due to the joint effect of two binary exposures [37]. A RERI value of 0 indicates little synergistic or antagonistic interactive effect between the two exposures, while a non-zero value signifies the presence of interaction [37]. Lastly, to assess the effect of unmeasured confounding, E-values were calculated [38]. All analyses were conducted using R software, version 4.3 for Windows.

Results

Study population and demographics

Males had higher concentrations of barium, cesium, lead, and molybdenum compared to female participants. We also observed differences in the concentrations of barium (1.48 ng/mL vs. 1.29 ng/mL) and molybdenum (44.02 ng/mL vs. 37.57 ng/mL) between younger participants (18–25 years) and older participants (26–39 years) (Table 1). Except for barium being higher in NH Whites, NH Blacks had the highest concentrations of cadmium, cobalt, cesium, mercury, molybdenum, lead, antimony, thallium, and tungsten. Uranium concentrations were similar across race and ethnicity categories. Concentrations of metals were similar across each poverty-index tertile (e.g., low, medium, high), with a few exceptions. Those in the highest tertile of the poverty-income ratio also had the highest concentrations of mercury but the lowest concentrations of lead compared to the low and middle tertiles.

Correlation across metals

Correlation across metals can be found in Fig. 2. Most urinary heavy metals demonstrate moderate to strong positive correlations. The strongest associations were observed between thallium and cesium (r² = 0.82), molybdenum and cesium (r2 = 0.66), and lead and cesium (r2 = 0.61). Metals such as antimony, cadmium, cobalt, lead, and uranium also showed several moderate correlations (r2 = 0.4–0.6) with other metals. Mercury generally exhibited weaker correlations (r2 ≤ 0.4), suggesting more distinct exposure. Overall, the correlation pattern suggests partial co-exposure among multiple metals, particularly those in the cesium, lead, and thallium clusters.

Association between individual metals, chlamydia, and HPV

Among our study population, the overall prevalence of chlamydia was 2.4%, while HPV 16/18 was 5.1% and other high-risk HPV genotypes were 23%. After adjustments, we found that increased concentrations of urine uranium (PR 1.20, 95% CI 1.05–1.36) and antimony (PR 1.20, 95% CI 1.01–1.44) were associated with chlamydia (Fig. 3a). However, cadmium (PR 1.14, 95% CI 0.96–1.34), tungsten (PR 1.14, 95% CI 0.98–1.32), and lead (PR 1.10, 95% CI 0.94–1.28) showed no association with chlamydia. Barium, cesium, cobalt, mercury, and thallium exhibited similar patterns of non-association.

We observed moderate associations between metals, HPV 16/18, and other high-risk HPV genotypes after adjustments (Fig. 3b). Urine barium (PR 1.17, 95% CI 1.06–1.29), cadmium (PR 1.17, 95% CI 1.01–1.29), cesium (PR 1.28, 95% CI 1.06–1.55), mercury (PR 1.23, 95% CI 1.10–1.36), lead (PR 1.19, 95% CI 1.03–1.39), antimony (PR 1.27, 95% CI 1.11–1.46), tungsten (PR 1.17, 95% CI 1.04–1.31), and uranium (PR 1.14, 95% CI 1.01–1.28) were associated with HPV 16/18 genotypes (Fig. 3b). There were fewer associations with other high-risk HPV genotypes (Fig. 3c). Barium (PR 1.06, 95% CI 1.01–1.10), cadmium (PR 1.08, 95% CI 1.03–1.13), lead (PR 1.09, 95% CI 1.02–1.17), and antimony (PR 1.07, 95% CI 1.02–1.13) showed modest associations with other high-risk HPV genotypes.

Each quintile increase in the urine concentration of our heavy metal mixture was associated with chlamydia (PR 1.30, 95% CI 1.29–1.30), HPV 16/18 (PR 1.40, 95% CI 1.40–1.41), and other high-risk HPV genotypes (PR 1.14, 95% CI 1.14–1.14) after adjustments (Table 2).

Sensitivity analyses

After stratifying our analyses by sex, we did not observe a significant association between heavy metals and chlamydia for males in our adjusted models (Supplemental Table 2). However, among females, antimony (PR 1.30, 95% CI 1.02–1.67) and uranium (PR 1.24, 95% CI 1.05–1.46) were associated with chlamydia. For high-risk HPV 16/18 genotypes, we observed a similar pattern where females were driving most of the associations (Supplemental Tables 3–4). Among female participants, barium (PR 1.17, 95% CI 1.06–1.29), cadmium (PR 1.14, 95% CI 1.01–1.29), cesium (PR 1.28, 95% CI 1.06–1.55), mercury (PR 1.23, 95% CI 1.10–1.36), lead (PR 1.19, 95% CI 1.03–1.39), antimony (PR 1.27, 95% CI 1.11–1.46), thallium (PR 1.26, 95% CI 1.04–1.53), tungsten (PR 1.17, 95% CI 1.04–1.31), and uranium (PR 1.14, 95% CI 1.01–1.28) were associated with HPV 16/18, while male-stratified models showed no associations (Supplemental Table 3).

Fewer associations were observed among females for other high-risk HPV genotypes. These included barium (PR 1.06, 95% CI 1.01–1.12), cadmium (PR 1.06, 95% CI 1.01–1.12), and lead (PR 1.14, 95% CI 1.04–1.18) (Supplemental Table 4). No associations were observed for males with other high-risk HPV genotypes.

Lastly, among females, heavy metal mixtures were associated with increased prevalence of chlamydia, HPV 16/18, and other high-risk HPV genotypes (Supplemental Table 5). In contrast, among males, there was an inverse relationship with HPV 16/18 (PR 0.98, 95% CI 0.98–0.98), and no association with chlamydia or other high-risk HPV genotypes.

We formally tested for interaction (Supplemental Table 6) but observed few significant interactions. There was a significant interaction between sex and cadmium (p = 0.0127) and cobalt (p = 0.0381) on the association with other high-risk HPV. Supplemental Table 7 shows the RERI analyses, which found no significant additive interactions.

Discussion

We explored the relationship between environmental metal exposure and two common STIs, Chlamydia trachomatis and high-risk HPV, using NHANES data. We found an association between elevated urine concentrations of uranium and antimony and chlamydia. Most metals were associated with HPV genotypes 16/18, while fewer associations were observed for other high-risk HPV genotypes. Heavy metal mixtures were associated with both STIs. Because the data are cross-sectional and the metal exposures are moderately to strongly correlated, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and should not be considered evidence of causation.

Prior studies have reported associations between individual metals, metal mixtures, and a range of bacterial, viral, and parasitic infectious diseases (e.g., Staphylococcus aureus, cytomegalovirus, hepatitis C, herpes simplex virus, malaria) [10, 39, 40]. One proposed explanation is that metals may impact immune function [41, 42], although empirical evidence remains limited. Metals play diverse roles in biological systems, with some being essential and others non-essential. Environmental exposure to these metals can occur through various routes, including air, water, and food. Essential transition metals, such as cobalt, carry out important biological functions including regulating the pathogen-host interface, and any dysregulation could have negative impacts on human health [43]. There is broad scientific consensus regarding the immunotoxic implications of metals in both humans and animals [8, 44]. This has been linked to multiple mechanisms: altered innate immune responses through interactions with Toll-like receptors (TLR), changes in adaptive immune cell populations, and the generation of oxidative stress via increased production of reactive oxygen species [9]. For example, elevated blood lead levels have been inversely associated with CD4+ helper T cells [45], cadmium exposure has been linked to reduced lymphocyte proliferation [44], and uranium has been associated with cytotoxicity, DNA damage, and oxidative stress in immune cells [46]. While these mechanisms have been described in prior investigations, we cannot infer them from the cross-sectional NHANES data.

Immune dysfunction and oxidative stress are implicated in C. trachomatis and HPV pathogenesis. In C. trachomatis, TLRs are essential for innate immune defense, yet their dysregulation can worsen inflammation and tissue damage [47]. Similarly, T-cells play an important role in susceptibility and disease progression of C. trachomatis, and reductions in CD4 T-cell activity have been associated with persistent infection [4]. Inflammation and oxidative stress have also been linked to tissue edema and increased vascular permeability, which may influence susceptibility to HPV [48]. Although data remain limited, Pal et al. have suggested that metal toxicity is correlated with HPV infection and oral carcinoma [49], and other studies have suggested that certain metals may influence cellular proliferation relevant to cancer biology [50]. While these pathways may offer biological plausibility, our analysis cannot establish whether metal exposure occurred before STI acquisition.

There are several strengths of using NHANES, including the availability of physical measurements and laboratory specimens. NHANES publishes detailed records on its specimen collection and laboratory validation protocols, ensuring high replicability and validity. However, several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design prevents establishing temporality between metal exposure and chlamydia or high-risk HPV infections. Because many metals were moderately to strongly correlated, our observed associations may reflect shared exposure sources rather than independent effects of specific metals, underscoring the exploratory nature of this investigation.

Urinary metal concentrations represent acute or recent exposure for many metals (e.g., cadmium, thallium, cesium) [29], whereas infections such as HPV and Chlamydia trachomatis are often asymptomatic and can have persistent forms [51, 52]. Thus, for some participants, infection could have preceded the urinary metal measurements. Moreover, NHANES publicly available STI data excluded adolescents, who have a high burden of chlamydia [1], limiting the generalizability of our findings. Unmeasured confounding is also possible. Our E-value analyses indicated that an unmeasured confounder of moderate strength could explain most of the observed associations, after accounting for measured covariates (E-values: 1.40 to 1.88 for the point estimates and 1.11 to 1.26 for the confidence intervals). Some associations, such as those involving barium and other high-risk HPV genotypes, were less robust to unmeasured confounding. Finally, although multiple comparisons increase type I error, adjusting for these comparisons may inflate type II error [53, 54]. Consistent with recommendations from Rothman and others [53, 54], we reported point estimates and confidence intervals rather than p-values.

Conclusions

In this exploratory cross-sectional investigation, we observed an association between heavy metal exposure and common STIs. This remains an understudied area of research, and given the public health significance of STIs, disparities in STI rates, and increasing relevance of environmental justice, prospective studies are needed to examine whether environmental metal exposures are associated with STIs. Additionally, mechanistic studies are necessary to better understand if metal exposure could influence susceptibility to reproductive tract infections.
