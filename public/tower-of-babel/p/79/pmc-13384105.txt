Fe-induced stabilization of corundum α-(FexGa1−x)2O3 thin films: phase transformation and thermodynamic insights
Authors: Minh-Tan Ha, Mai Khanh Pham, Nguyen Hong Hai, Quoc Tiep Do, Ngoc Thi Bich Nguyen, Manh Trung Tran, Ho Anh Tam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gallium oxide (Ga2O3) is an ultrawide-bandgap semiconductor with strong potential for electronic and optoelectronic applications. The metastable corundum α-phase is particularly attractive for alloying and bandgap engineering but suffers from limited thermal stability. In this work, the phase stability of α-(FexGa1−x)2O3 thin films grown by mist chemical vapor deposition is systematically studied using experiments and density functional theory. Increasing Fe content suppresses the α → β transformation, shifts the transition to higher temperatures, and reduces the bandgap due to electronic hybridization. DFT results show that Fe incorporation lowers the energy difference between α- and β-phases, stabilizing the corundum structure through strain effects, defect interactions, and reduced cation mobility. These findings provide insight into phase control in Ga2O3-based alloys and a pathway to stabilize metastable oxide phases for high-temperature applications.

Introduction

Gallium oxide (Ga2O3) has attracted increasing attention in recent years owing to its broad potential for advanced optical, electronic, and optoelectronic applications, including solar-blind ultraviolet photodetectors,1,2 high-power electronics,3,4 photovoltaic systems,5,6 phosphors,7 transparent conductive coatings,8 and related optoelectronic technologies.3,9 Ga2O3 is known to crystallize in four well-established polymorphs, namely α, β, γ (cubic), and ε(κ). Among these polymorphs, β-Ga2O3 is the thermodynamically stable ground-state phase, whereas the other forms are metastable and irreversibly transform into β-Ga2O3 upon thermal annealing.10 Although β-Ga2O3 has been extensively studied for power electronics and optoelectronic devices,3 its monoclinic structure gives rise to pronounced anisotropy in the optical, electrical, and thermal properties.11,12 This limitation has stimulated growing interest in the metastable α-, ε(κ)-, and γ-Ga2O3 as alternative functional platforms. In particular, ε(κ)-Ga2O3 is highly attractive because its non-centrosymmetric crystal symmetry (orthorhombic Pna21 or hexagonal P63mc) enables spontaneous polarization, strong piezoelectricity, and even ferroelectric-like behavior.13 Likewise, γ-Ga2O3 has shown promise for luminescence, photocatalysis, and room-temperature ferromagnetism upon appropriate transition-metal doping.14 Among the metastable polymorphs, α-Ga2O3, which adopts the corundum structure (rhombohedral, space group R̄3c), is particularly notable because of its ultrawide band gap of approximately 5.3 eV and its theoretically higher critical breakdown field than β-Ga2O3.15 More importantly, the corundum framework is structurally compatible with a wide range of isostructural sesquioxides, such as Al2O3, In2O3, Fe2O3, and Cr2O3, thereby providing a versatile platform for alloying, band-gap engineering, and lattice-mismatch reduction across a broad compositional space.16,17

Within this context, the (FexGa1−x)2O3 system is of particular interest because it inherits rich polymorphism from its parent oxides, β-Ga2O3 and α-Fe2O3, and may additionally form intermediate phases such as spinel-like γ-(Ga,Fe)2O3 or orthorhombic ε-GaFeO3 depending on synthesis conditions. Bulk (FexGa1−x)2O3 has been prepared by various techniques, including the float-zone method, sol–gel processing, ball milling, and solid-state reactions. In contrast, studies on Fe-containing Ga2O3 thin films have been dominated by deposition routes such as pulsed laser deposition (PLD),18–20 laser molecular beam epitaxy,21 polymer-assisted deposition,22 and mist chemical vapor deposition.23 However, the thin-film phase space reported thus far remains strongly biased toward the thermodynamically stable β phase, γ (spinel-related) phase, and orthorhombic GaFeO3, rather than the corundum α phase. In PLD-grown (Ga1−xFex)2O3 films on sapphire, Fe incorporation has been shown to enlarge the lattice and induce a composition-dependent structural evolution from monoclinic β to γ/spinel-like phases, while oxygen-deficient growth conditions similarly promote the β-to-γ transformation through changes in Fe valence state.19,20 These findings indicate pronounced phase competition and only a limited single-phase stability window in (Fe,Ga)2O3 thin films. In parallel, a substantial body of work has focused on orthorhombic GaFeO3-related thin films grown mainly by PLD on SrTiO3, MgO, Al2O3, quartz, YSZ, and buffered conductive substrates, where the orthorhombic phase is largely retained and the emphasis has been placed primarily on magnetic, dielectric, and optical functionalities rather than on alloy phase stability itself.18,24,25 By contrast, reports on corundum-structured α-(Ga1−xFex)2O3 thin films remain extremely scarce. To the best of our knowledge, the only dedicated thin-film study is that of Kaneko et al.23 who demonstrated highly crystalline α-(Ga1−xFex)2O3 alloy films over the full composition range by mist-CVD on c-plane sapphire. Nevertheless, that work mainly established epitaxial growth and band-gap tunability, without addressing how Fe incorporation affects the thermal stability of α-(FexGa1−x)2O3 during post-annealing. This issue is especially important because α-Ga2O3 itself is a metastable corundum polymorph; accordingly, whether Fe stabilizes or destabilizes the α phase during thermal treatment remains an unresolved question.

Despite these advances, a fundamental understanding of phase stability in corundum-structured α-(FexGa1−x)2O3 thin films remains lacking. In particular, the role of Fe incorporation in governing the thermal stability of the metastable α-phase during post-deposition annealing has not been systematically investigated. This knowledge gap is critical, as controlling phase stability is essential for enabling reliable device integration and for exploiting the unique properties of corundum-structured sesquioxide alloys.

In this work, we combine experimental and theoretical approaches to elucidate the phase stability and transformation behavior of α-(FexGa1−x)2O3 thin films grown by mist-CVD. By systematically varying Fe composition and annealing temperature, we investigate the evolution of crystal structure and optical properties. Furthermore, density functional theory (DFT) calculations are employed to provide atomistic insights into the thermodynamic driving forces governing phase stability. This integrated approach aims to clarify whether Fe incorporation stabilizes the α-phase and to establish a comprehensive understanding of phase competition in the (Fe,Ga)2O3 system.

Method

Experimental

In this study, thin films were deposited on c-plane sapphire substrates using the narrow channel type mist chemical vapour deposition (mist-CVD) technique,26 as illustrated in Fig. 1. This method enables the formation of metastable α-Ga2O3 under atmospheric pressure conditions, as previously reported.27,28 The precursor solution consisted of a mixture of Ga(acac)3 and FeCl3·6H2O dissolved in distilled water.

Schematic of the narrow channel type mist-CVD system.

The nominal Fe precursor ratio, RFe, was defined from the molar concentrations of the starting chemicals in the precursor solution as RFe = [FeCl3]/([Ga(acac)3] + [FeCl3]). This quantity represents the molar fraction of Fe-containing precursor supplied to the mist-CVD solution and should not be interpreted as the ionized Fe fraction in the vapor phase or as the incorporated Fe fraction in the deposited film. The corresponding nominal compositions are listed in Table 1. A small amount of HCl was added to adjust the solution pH = 1 to maintain the solubility of the precusors.

Sample designation and as-grown conditions

The precursor solution was placed in a 1.7 MHz ultrasonic atomiser to generate mist droplets. The generated mist was transported into a hot-wall furnace by O2 carrier gas with a flow rate of 8 L min−1. Film growth was carried out at 400 °C for 1 h. The as-grown samples were denoted as Cn-AG, where n represents the nominal Fe concentration RFe. Post-deposition annealing was performed in air at 600, 700, and 800 °C for 1 h. The annealed samples were labelled as Cn-AT, where T denotes the annealing temperature.

The structural properties of the films were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Malvern PANalytical Aeris) using Cu-Kα radiation. The diffraction profiles contain both Kα1 and Kα2 components, but only the Kα1 peak position was used for subsequent analysis.

Diffuse reflectance spectra were collected using a JASCO V-770 UV-vis spectrophotometer to evaluate the optical absorption behavior of the films. Because XRD and UV-vis measurements were not repeated independently, these spectra are presented as representative measurements.

Local chemical compositions were examined by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). Five point spectra were collected for each sample, and the point-composition values are reported as mean ± standard deviation.

Computational details

All density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out using the QUANTUM ESPRESSO package.29 The exchange–correlation functional was described within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional.30 The projector augmented-wave31 (PAW) pseudopotentials form pslibrary1.0.0 (ref. 32) were employed to represent the ion–electron interaction.

The computational models for α- and β-(FexGa1−x)2O3 were constructed based on their conventional cells, containing 30 and 20 atoms, respectively. In these cells, 12 cation sites (α phase) and 8 cation sites (β phase) are available for Fe/Ga substitution. Since the number of possible substitutional configurations increases with system size, the cluster expansion (CE) method33 was adopted to efficiently capture the configurational energetics of the system, providing near-ab initio accuracy with significantly reduced computational cost. The CE method was carried out using the maps code implemented in the Alloy Theoretic Automated Toolkit (ATAT).34,35 In this method, the configurational energetics of the alloy system are represented by a generalized Ising Hamiltonian,36,37 whose effective cluster interactions were fitted to a set of ab initio calculated energies.

For the DFT calculations used to construct the CE, Monkhorst–Pack38k-point grids of 5 × 5 × 2 and 2 × 7 × 4 for the α- and β-phases, respectively. Plane-wave kinetic energy cutoffs of 50 Ry for the wavefunctions and 400 Ry for the charge density were adopted. The electronic self-consistency convergence threshold was set to 10−6 Ry. Structural optimisations of both lattice parameters and atomic positions were carried out using the Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS) algorithm. The convergence criteria for ionic relaxations were set to 10−4 Ry for total energy, 10−3 Ry Bohr−1 for atomic forces, and 0.5 kbar for pressure. The lowest-energy configurations of α- and β-(FexGa1−x)2O3, identified by combining DFT-calculated energies and CE predictions. To obtain more accurate total energy for mixing enthalpy, these selected structures were recalculated using denser k-point meshes of 10 × 10 × 4 and 3 × 11 × 6, and it was increased plane-wave cutoffs of 90 Ry for the wavefunctions and 1080 Ry for the charge density, with electronic convergence threshold of 10−8 Ry.

Fig. 2(c) depicts a significant discrepancy between the nominal precursor ratio, the EDS-derived chemical composition, and the XRD-derived corundum-lattice Fe fraction. That indicates Fe incorporation during mist-CVD is not governed simply by the starting solution stoichiometry. EDS measures the local chemical composition within the electron-interaction volume, including possible surface-enriched, segregated, or poorly crystalline Fe-containing regions. In contrast, the Vegard-law analysis probes only the Fe fraction incorporated coherently into the corundum lattice that contributes to the resolved (0006) diffraction peak. Possible contributors to the observed differences include precursor chemistry, droplet transport, surface reaction kinetics, Fe segregation, and growth-temperature limitations. These factors were not directly quantified in the present study and are therefore discussed only as possible contributors.

Phase evolution of annealed FGO

The phase evolution of (FexGa1−x)2O3 upon post-annealing is investigated by XRD and is represented in Fig. 3. For pure Ga2O3 (Fig. 3(a)), the α-Ga2O3(0006) peak dissapeared by annealing at 700 and 800 °C, and introduced the β-Ga2O3(4̄02) reflection at 2θ ≈ 38.4°, indicating the onset of the α → β phase transition. This behavior is consistent with previous reports showing that metastable α-Ga2O3 on sapphire transforms into thermodynamically stable β-Ga2O3 upon annealing near 600 °C.10

XRD patterns of samples (a) C000, (b) C025, (c) C050, (d) C075, and (e) C100 as-grown and annealed at temperatures ranging from 600 to 800 °C.

In contrast, pure Fe2O3 (Fig. 3(e)) retained the α-Fe2O3(0006) reflection at 2θ = 39.6° even after annealing up to 800 °C, confirming the high thermal stability of hematite. Furthermore, increasing the concentration of Fe in the alloy increase the temperature required to transform the corundum to monoclinic phase as shown in Fig. 3(b–d). Particularly, the sample C025 transform from corundum to monoclinic after annealed at 800 °C. The sample C050 and C075 can not transform to the monoclinic even after annealed at 800 °C. Moreover, the α-(Ga,Fe)2O3(0006) diffraction peak almost dissapeared in the sample C000-A600, C025-A700 and C050-A800 but the β-Ga2O3(4̄02) have not appearred. We therefore describe these samples as XRD-poorly crystalline intermediate states rather than assigning a specific crystalline phase.

To examine the kinetic contribution associated with annealing time and rule out the possibility that the annealing time was insufficient for ionic rearrangement within the lattice, we further annealed the C000-A600, C025-A700, and C050-A800 samples for up to 5 hours to ensure sufficient diffusion time. The XRD patterns of these samples are shown in Fig. 4. The extended annealing time led to the nearly complete disappearance of the alpha-phase peaks; however, no new beta-phase peaks were detected. These results suggest that the phase transformation boundary is not primarily governed by annealing-time kinetics, but instead depends mainly on Fe concentration and annealing temperature (indicated by the pink background in Fig. 3).

XRD patterns of samples (a) C000, (b) C025, and (c) C050 annealed at temperatures of 600, 700, and 800 °C, respectively, for 5 hours.

To further elucidate the role of Fe concentration in the phase transformation, it is important to consider the diffusion mechanism in Ga2O3. Previous studies have shown that impurity diffusion in Ga2O3 is primarily mediated by native defects such as vacancies.40–42 Experimental results indicate that Fe exhibits a very low diffusion coefficient in β-Ga2O3,43 while first-principles calculations reveal high migration barriers for Fe3+ diffusion.41,44 In contrast, Ga vacancy-mediated diffusion is expected to be comparatively faster.43 Therefore, under typical conditions (e.g., oxidizing annealing and low-to-moderate Fe concentrations), cation transport is likely governed by Ga3+ mobility, which becomes the rate-limiting factor for the α → β phase transformation. In the present FGO system, where the Fe concentration remains relatively low, Ga3+ ion mobility is expected to dominate the phase transformation kinetics, while Fe3+ remains largely immobile and primarily influences the process indirectly through defect formation and lattice effects.

However, as shown in Fig. 3, the phase transformation temperature clearly depends on the Fe concentration in the alloys, with higher Fe content requiring higher temperatures for the α → β transition. The microscopic origin of this Fe-dependent stabilization has not been directly verified experimentally in the present study. Therefore, we propose the following thermodynamic picture as a working hypothesis to rationalize this behavior. First, Fe incorporation may introduce a strain-related term, Estrain, because Fe3+ substitution in the corundum lattice can stabilize the α framework while also producing local lattice distortion. Second, Fe-related defects may contribute to an interfacial term, Einterface, by hindering the migration of the α/β transformation front.45 Third, Fe-vacancy association can be represented by a binding term, Ebinding, which reduces the effective mobility of vacancies involved in cation transport.45 Zener-type pinning may also contribute, although its contribution is expected to be secondary in the present system.45 Overall, within this proposed thermodynamic framework, the increase in transformation temperature with Fe concentration is attributed to the combined effects of corundum-lattice stabilization, local strain, interface hindrance, and reduced cation mobility.

The total activation energy required for the phase transformation can be expressed as: Etrans = EGa + Estrain + Einterface + Ebinding, where EGa represents the intrinsic activation energy associated with Ga3+ diffusion, while Estrain, Einterface, and Ebinding arise from Fe-induced lattice strain, interface pinning, and defect clustering, respectively. Although it is difficult to quantitatively separate each contribution, the overall activation energy can be estimated from experimental observations.

Assuming that the phase transformation is diffusion-controlled and that 1 h of annealing is sufficient for complete transformation once the critical temperature is reached, the temperature at which the α-phase disappears can be approximated as the onset temperature for transformation. By assuming that the transformation rate at the onset condition is approximately constant, the activation energy can be estimated using a proportional relationship:Here, Etrans(0) corresponds to the activation energy of undoped α-Ga2O3 (sample C000), which is dominated by Ga diffusion. Based on reported values, EGa is in the range of 3.9–5.0 eV,45 and a representative value of 4.0 eV is adopted for estimation. Using this approach, the activation energies for Fe concentrations of 2.15% and 17.21% (corresponding to samples C025 and C050) are estimated to be 4.46 eV and 4.92 eV, respectively. This increase in activation energy with incoporated Fe concentration indicates that defect-related contributions, Edefect = Estrain + Einterface + Ebinding, become increasingly significant, demonstrating that Fe-induced defects and trapping effects strongly influence the phase transformation kinetics.

Using this phenomenological estimate and extrapolating the same trend up to an incorporated Fe fraction RFe ≈ 30%, the values of Etrans are estimated to be 5.42 eV and 7.98 eV for Etrans(0) = 4.0 and 5.0 eV, respectively, as seen in Fig. 5. These correspond to transformation temperatures of approximately 910 °C and 1120 °C. Although this estimation does not uniquely determine the microscopic rate-limiting mechanism, it indicates that increasing Fe concentration significantly raises the activation barrier, thereby requiring higher thermal energy to drive the α → β phase transformation. Thus, increasing Fe content appears to enhance the stability of the α-phase, suggesting the potential for improved thermal stability of α-Ga2O3-based materials at elevated temperatures. In addition, the optical properties of α-(FeGa)2O3 alloys should be further investigated, as discussed in the following section.

Optical properties of FGO

The as-grown and annealed samples with different nominal Fe concentrations were characterizby by wavelength-dependent diffuse reflectance measurements using UV-vis spectrophotometry. The measured reflectance R was converted using the Kubelka–Munk function F(R).46,47

For the present thin films on sapphire, F(R) is used as a comparative absorption-like quantity rather than an absolute absorption coefficient. The Tauc plots, expressed as (F(R)hν)2versus hν, are provided in Fig. S3 of the SI. However, because the linear fitting region is not clearly defined for several samples, especially those near the phase-transformation boundary, the bandgap extraction from the Tauc plots is subject to considerable uncertainty. Therefore, for clearer comparative analysis, the first derivative of the Kubelka–Munk function, dF(R)/d(hν), is presented in Fig. 6. The peaks in these spectra correspond to strong absorption at specific energy levels.

First derivative of α with respect to photon energy dF(R)/d(hν) derived from diffuse reflectance spectra of (a) the sapphire substrate, (b) C000 (as-grown and annealed), (c) C100 (as-grown and annealed), and (d)–(f) C025 to C075 (as-grown and annealed).

At the first glance, in Fig. 6(a), a peak is observed at 3.77 eV, which can be attributed either to oxygen vacancy-related defects or to contributions from the sapphire substrate. For the thin film samples, this peak consistently appears at ∼3.77 eV across all samples, indicating that it is primarily associated with absorption from the sapphire substrate.

For the C000-AG sample (Fig. 6(b)), a distinct absorption peak at 5.22 eV is observed, corresponding to the band-to-band transition of α-Ga2O3. In contrast, the annealed samples C000-A700 and C000-A800 exhibit peaks at 5.00 and 4.94 eV, respectively, which are consistent with the reported bandgap values of β-α-Ga2O3.48–50 Notably, C000-A600 does not show a clear absorption peak in the ∼5.0 eV region, suggesting a disordered structure or a high density of traps and defects. This observation is in good agreement with the XRD results shown in Fig. 3(a).

For the C100 samples (Fig. 6(c)), a characteristic absorption peak at 2.24 eV is observed, corresponding to α-Fe2O3. Furthermore, the intensity of the 2.24 eV peak increases monotonically with annealing temperature, while the intensity at 3.76 eV decreases. This indicates an improvement in the crystallinity of the α-Fe2O3 phase at higher annealing temperatures, which is consistent with the decrasing of the FWHM of the (0006) reflection at 39.6° at elevated temperatures.

For the α-(FeGa)2O3 (FGO) alloy samples shown in Fig. 6(d–f), samples grown or annealed below the phase transformation temperature exhibit distinct absorption peaks at 3.36 eV (C025-AG and C025-A600), 3.28 eV (C050-AG, C050-A600, and C050-A700), and 2.85 eV (C075-A600, C075-A700, and C075-A800). These values are assigned to characteristic optical transition energies of the α-FGO alloys, which decreases with increasing Fe content. This trend is consistent with the gradual shifting of the film color observed visually in Fig. 2(a). The pronounced red-shift in transition energy can be attributed to the hybridization of Fe3+ 3d orbitals with O-2p and Ga-4s states, leading to band-edge shifts. In particular, the Fe3+-3d states raise the valence band maximum and partially mix with the conduction band, analogous to an sp–d exchange interaction that narrows the bandgap.51

For samples annealed near the phase transformation temperature, such as C025-A700 and C050-A800, no prominent absorption peaks are observed. This suggests the presence of significant disorder and a high density of trap states, which is consistent with the corresponding XRD results. In contrast, samples annealed above the transformation temperature, such as C025-A800, exhibit a clear absorption peak at 4.69 eV, corresponding to the bandgap of β-Ga2O3, while no distinct absorption peak associated with hematite is observed. This indicates that, at relatively low Fe concentrations, it is difficult to form sufficiently large and well-crystallized Fe2O3 phases to be detected by XRD. Additionally, compared to the annealed C075 samples, the as-grown C075-AG sample does not exhibit a clear absorption peak at 2.85 eV, indicating that higher Fe concentrations degrade crystallinity and introduce a high density of defects and trap states. Therefore, post-growth annealing is necessary to improve the crystalline quality of the films.

However, Fe incorporation significantly modifies the optical response of Ga2O3 and reduces the characteristic optical transition energy of α-(FeGa)2O3 alloys. Therefore, the suitability of these films for device applications should be assessed only after direct electrical characterization and device-level measurements are available.

Thermodynamic point of view

In order to better understand the stabilization of the α-phase induced by Fe in the FGO system, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were employed to evaluate the formation enthalpy and mixing enthalpy of the FGO alloys in both α and β phases. Formation enthalpy (ΔHl) of the single-phase (FexGa1−x)2O3 structures was calculated according to:ΔHl = E[(FexGa1−x)2O3]l − E[Ga2O3]l − E[Fe2O3]l,where [(FexGa1−x)2O3]l, E[Ga2O3]l and E[Fe2O3]l denote the DFT total energies per cation of the mixed and end-member compounds within the same lattice type l (corundum for α and monoclinic for β phase). The calculated ΔHl values were used to determine the energetically favourable cation configurations at each composition x.

As shown in Fig. 7(a), for α-(FexGa1−x)2O3, a total of 179 structures were calculated by DFT to identify the lowest-energy configurations, while the remaining CE-predicted configurations were found to have higher energies. In addition, the correlation between the CE-predicted and DFT-calculated energies is presented in Fig. 7(b). The very small cross-validation score of 0.00075 eV per unit cell indicates the good predictive capability of the CE model. For the β-Ga2O3 phase, all configurations were directly calculated by DFT to determine the lowest-energy structure without relying on CE-predicted configurations, as shown in Fig. 7(c).

As shown in Fig. 8, the lowest-energy configurations of α- and β-(FexGa1−x)2O3 were identified. In the corundum α phase (Fig. 8(a)), the FeO6 and GaO6 octahedra progressively develop adjacent Fe-rich layers as the Fe concentration increases. At x = 0.5, the most stable configuration is composed of stacked Fe2O3 and Ga2O3 trilayers along the c-axis. This behavior differs from that of other corundum sesquioxide alloys, such as Al2O3–Ga2O3 and In2O3–Ga2O3, for which the energetically preferred structure at x = 0.5 consists of alternating monolayers. Nevertheless, phase-separated Fe2O3- and Ga2O3-rich layers have also been reported previously, and were attributed to the small energy cost of Ga/Fe site swaps being compensated by strong magnetic coupling between adjacent Fe3+ ions.52 In contrast, monoclinic β-(FexGa1−x)2O3 (Fig. 8(b)) differs from other sesquioxides in that it shows no distinct cation preference for octahedral or tetrahedral sites. At x = 0.5, both Fe and Ga are distributed over the two coordination environments, with each cation occupying two tetrahedral and two octahedral sites among the eight available cation sites in the conventional cell, including four octahedral and four tetrahedral positions.

Conventional cells of (a) α-(FexGa1−x)2O3 and (b) β-(FexGa1−x)2O3. Each cell contains 12 and 8 cation sites for α- and β- phases, respectively. For each configuration of the α- phase, n/12 Ga3+ ions at the octaheral sites (brown color) are replaced by n/12 Fe3+ ions. For each configuration of the β- phase, m/8 Ga3+ ions at the octaheral or tetrahedral sites (brown color) are replaced by m/8 Fe3+ ions.

Mixing enthalpies of α and β phases of (FexGa1−x)2O3 alloys.

At low Fe concentrations (from x = 0 to x = 0.25), the mixing enthalpy of the α phase is higher than that of the β phase, indicating that the corundum alloy is energetically less favorable in the Ga-rich region. However, the enthalpy difference decreases substantially from 0.076 to 0.0038 eV per cation with increasing Fe content, suggesting that Fe incorporation progressively reduces the energetic disadvantage of the α phase. In the intermediate composition range (from x = 0.25 to x = 0.5), the mixing enthalpy of the α-phase remains slightly higher than that of the β-phase, but the difference remains small, reaching only 0.0078 eV per cation at x = 0.5. This behavior indicates the onset of strong phase competition and suggests that the relative stability of the corundum phase is significantly improved in this composition range. In the Fe-rich region, the mixing enthalpy of the α phase becomes clearly lower than that of the β phase. The energy difference is much larger than that in the low-Fe regime, reaching 0.0756 eV per cation at x = 0.75 and increasing further toward the Fe-rich end. These results demonstrate that the corundum phase becomes energetically favorable at high Fe concentrations. Therefore, the DFT calculations clearly show that Fe incorporation enhances the stability of the α phase in (FexGa1−x)2O3 alloys, providing a rational explanation for the experimental observation that increasing Fe content suppresses or delays the corundum-to-monoclinic phase transformation during post-annealing.

Conclusions

In this study, the phase stability and transformation behavior of α-(FexGa1−x)2O3 thin films were systematically investigated using a combination of mist-CVD growth, post-annealing experiments, and first-principles calculations. The results demonstrate that single-phase corundum α-(FexGa1−x)2O3 alloys can be successfully synthesized over a wide composition range, although the actual Fe incorporation is significantly lower than the nominal precursor composition due to kinetic and chemical limitations during growth.

Post-annealing studies reveal that Fe incorporation strongly influences the α → β phase transformation. While pure α-Ga2O3 readily transforms into the thermodynamically stable β-phase upon annealing, increasing Fe content progressively suppresses this transition and raises the required transformation temperature. This behavior is attributed to multiple Fe-induced effects, including lattice strain, defect-mediated interface pinning, and vacancy trapping, all of which hinder cation diffusion and increase the activation energy for phase transformation.

Optical measurements further show that Fe incorporation significantly reduces the bandgap of α-(FexGa1−x)2O3 alloys due to electronic hybridization effects, while phase transformation and structural disorder introduce additional defect-related absorption features.

Complementary DFT calculations confirm that Fe incorporation thermodynamically stabilizes the corundum phase, particularly at higher Fe concentrations, by reducing the energy difference between the α- and β-phases and eventually favoring the α-phase in the Fe-rich regime.

Overall, this work demonstrates that Fe alloying is an effective strategy to enhance the thermal stability of metastable α-Ga2O3, providing important insights into phase control in ultrawide-bandgap oxide systems. These findings offer a basis for tailoring material properties of α-Ga2O3-based alloys for high-temperature applications. Although the detailed mechanisms of the α → β phase transformation are not fully resolved in the present study, future studies combining depth-resolved composition analysis with TEM/SAED or high-resolution XRD would further clarify the transformation pathway and the role of Fe incorporation in α-phase stabilization.
