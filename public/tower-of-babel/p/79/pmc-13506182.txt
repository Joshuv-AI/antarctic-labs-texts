Sex Differences in Screening for Large Vessel Occlusion and Thrombectomy: A Population-Based Cohort Study
Authors: Yasodara P.S. Siddharthan, Moira K. Kapral, Jiming Fang, Peter C. Austin, Anahita Carbonneau, Michael D. Hill, Albert Jin, Jennifer Mandzia, Frank L. Silver, Amy Y.X. Yu, Jemini Abraham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Population-based sex differences in large vessel occlusion (LVO) screening and thrombectomy use for acute ischemic stroke are not well understood. We compared the detection of LVO, thrombectomy use, and long-term clinical outcomes in female versus male patients with cerebral ischemia in Ontario, Canada.METHODS:This retrospective cohort study used data from the Ontario Stroke Registry linked with health administrative data. We included adult patients hospitalized for cerebral ischemia within the first 24 hours of last seen normal time during 2 fiscal years (2019/2020 and 2022/2023). We used modified Poisson regression models to evaluate sex differences in intracranial neurovascular imaging, thrombolysis, or thrombectomy. Among patients with LVO, we used Cox proportional hazard and cause-specific hazard models to compare female versus male patients long-term mortality, readmission, or nursing home admission until 2025, stratified by thrombectomy treatment.RESULTS:Among 16 935 eligible patients (47% female patients; median age, 76 [interquartile range, 65–84] years), female patients were less likely than male patients to receive intracranial neurovascular imaging (83.6% female versus 87.8% male; P<0.01). Among those who underwent imaging, 19.3% of female patients had an LVO compared with 15.9% of male patients (P<0.01). Thrombectomy use was higher in female patients than male patients in the overall cohort (adjusted relative risk, 1.08 [1.01–1.16]), but, once restricted to those with LVO, thrombectomy use was similar (adjusted relative risk, 0.98 [0.93–1.03]). Adjusted hazard of the composite outcome was lower in female patients with LVO treated with thrombectomy compared with their male counterparts (adjusted hazard ratio, 0.88 [0.77–0.99]).CONCLUSIONS:Female patients with acute cerebral ischemia were less likely than male patients to receive intracranial neurovascular imaging but more likely to have an LVO, and long-term mortality was lower in female patients with LVO treated with thrombectomy. Stroke outcomes’ research must account for neurovascular imaging and LVO status to accurately identify sex-based disparities in stroke care and outcomes.

Female patients with ischemic stroke present with higher stroke severity and experience poorer outcomes compared with male patients.1 Older age and poorer baseline functional status contribute to these differences, but knowledge on modifiable risk factors is lacking. Recent studies suggest that the proportion of intracranial large vessel occlusion (LVO) may be higher in female patients compared with male patients,2,3 raising the question whether sex differences in the incidence or management of ischemic stroke with LVO may be contributing to sex differences in stroke severity and outcomes.

This is important to understand because stroke with LVO is associated with higher severity and worse outcomes than other types of strokes, but endovascular thrombectomy is highly effective if LVO is diagnosed and treated promptly.4,5 Some population-based analyses suggest that female patients with ischemic stroke were more likely than male patients to undergo thrombectomy,6–8 while some suggest otherwise,9,10 but most studies did not account for the presence of LVO. The Recovery by Endovascular Salvage for Cerebral Ultra-Acute Embolism-Japan Registry reported that female patients with LVO were less likely to undergo thrombectomy,11 but the registry only included patients with confirmed intracranial occlusions across selected participating hospitals. Thus, sex differences in the detection of LVO and access to thrombectomy in the population remain poorly understood. In addition, clinical trials and most observational studies have focused on 90-day outcomes, but little data exist on the sex differences in long-term outcomes in this population.12

We aimed to determine potential sex differences in the use of intracranial neurovascular imaging in patients with cerebral ischemia, the prevalence of LVO by sex, the use of thrombectomy among those with confirmed LVO, and the long-term clinical outcomes of such patients in Ontario, Canada. We hypothesized that outcomes after thrombectomy will be similar in female and male patients, but there may be differences in LVO prevalence or treatment with thrombectomy when LVO is present.

Methods

Data Availability Statement

The data set from this study is held securely in coded form at formerly Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences (ICES). While data sharing agreements prohibit ICES from making the data set publicly available, access may be granted to those who meet the criteria for confidential access after an application process.

Cohort Creation

We conducted a retrospective population-based cohort study using data from the Ontario Stroke Registry, a clinical stroke registry that has been extensively used for public reporting on stroke care and for health services research in the province of Ontario, Canada.13 Detailed registry methods and data management have been previously reported, and the case report forms for data collection are publicly available. We used data from the latest data collection phase where consecutive patients aged 18 to 105 years hospitalized through an emergency department for ischemic stroke or intracerebral hemorrhage to an acute care hospital in Ontario with at least 70 annual stroke admissions during the fiscal years 2019/2020 and 2022/2023 were included. The registry is housed at ICES, which holds the designation of a Prescribed Entity under the Ontario Personal Health Information Protection Act. Thus, data collection took place under Section 45 of the Act without the requirement of individual patient consent, minimizing selection bias.14 Case lists were generated using validated International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Canadian codes, I63 and I64 for ischemic stroke and I61 for intracerebral hemorrhage.15 Patients with an invalid Ontario health insurance number (nonresidents of Ontario), admission date preceding January 1, 2019, in-hospital stroke events, and those with a most responsible diagnosis of transient ischemic attack (G45 excluding G45.4) and subarachnoid hemorrhage (I60) were excluded from data collection. Unique episodes of care were created to avoid double-counting interhospital transfers as separate events. This sampling strategy included over 90% of stroke hospitalizations in Ontario. Given that the focus of the current study was the prevalence of LVO and thrombectomy treatment, we only included patients with cerebral ischemia who presented within 24 hours of last seen normal. For patients with >1 stroke episode during the accrual period, only the first episode of care was included.

Exposure and Outcomes

The primary exposure variable was biological sex, female or male. First, we described measures of stroke care by sex, including interhospital transfers, treatment at a comprehensive stroke center at any point during the episode, neuroimaging and intracranial neurovascular imaging, and use of thrombolysis or thrombectomy. Second, among those who had intracranial neurovascular imaging, we reported the prevalence of LVO, defined as any occlusion of the M1 segment of the middle cerebral artery or basilar artery, because the standard clinical practice is to treat such patients with thrombectomy.4,16 Isolated intracranial internal carotid artery occlusion without ipsilateral M1 occlusion was not considered an LVO as thrombectomy is not standard practice for such patients across Ontario. Finally, among patients with LVO, we reported sex differences in thrombectomy use, the reasons for nontreatment, and long-term clinical outcomes stratified by treatment. The primary clinical outcome was the composite of all-cause mortality, all-cause readmission, or nursing home admission. Secondary outcomes included all-cause mortality, and among patients discharged alive, we estimated the hazard of all-cause readmission and nursing home admission as separate secondary outcomes. Clinical outcomes were obtained from linkage with governmental administrative data, including the Registered Persons Database for Vital Statistics, Discharge Abstract Database for Readmission, and Continuing Care Reporting System for Nursing Home Admission (Table S1). Patients were followed until an outcome or until March 31, 2025.

Privacy and Ethics

Data sets were linked deterministically using unique encoded identifiers and analyzed at ICES. The use of data in this project was authorized under Section 45 of Ontario’s Personal Health Information Protection Act and did not require research ethics board approval. As per ICES privacy standards, cell counts <6 were suppressed.

Statistical Methods

We compared baseline patient characteristics and stroke care measures by sex using the χ2 test for categorical variables and the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables with P<0.05 indicating statistical significance. We compared intracranial neurovascular imaging, thrombolysis, or thrombectomy by sex using modified Poisson regression models to account for baseline differences and reported the adjusted relative risk (RR) and 95% CIs. We adjusted for clinically relevant covariates obtained from the Ontario Stroke Registry, including age (continuous), history of dementia, current smoking, prestroke independence (baseline modified Rankin Scale score, 0–2), prestroke living situation (home versus retirement home versus nursing home versus other), stroke severity (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale as a continuous variable), whether stroke onset was witnessed, time from last seen normal to first emergency department arrival (continuous), time of the day at first arrival (0800–1959 versus 2000–0759 hours), and stroke center type at first presentation (comprehensive stroke center, primary stroke center, or nondesignated stroke center). Additional covariates from linked administrative data using validated case definitions17,18 included hypertension, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, dyslipidemia, prior stroke, coronary artery disease, peripheral artery disease, frailty risk score (low frailty with score 0–<5, intermediate frailty with 5–≤15, and high frailty with >15), rurality (large urban with population >100 000, medium urban if 10 000–100 000, and small town if <10 000), and neighborhood-level income quintiles (see Table S1 for case definitions). Continuous variables were modeled using restricted cubic splines.

Registry data were collected by trained chart abstractors. The data collection software was programmed with internal quality and logic checks with forced entry for key variables. Internal quality monitoring was additionally performed through regular data downloads, ensuring minimal missing data. When the stroke severity score was missing from the chart, abstractors calculated the Canadian Neurological Scale score, which was transformed into the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale using previously published methods.19 Other missing data were handled using multiple imputation. The imputation model included all baseline variables in addition to the outcome for the analysis models. A total of 20 imputed data sets were generated. The modeling results were pooled using the Rubin rules to account for within- and between-imputation variabilities.20

Among patients with LVO, we used modified Poisson regression models to evaluate the association between sex and thrombolysis or thrombectomy, adjusting for the same covariates as above. We used Cox proportional hazard models to evaluate the association between sex and clinical outcomes, and we reported the adjusted hazard ratio (HR) and 95% CI for all patients with LVO and stratified by thrombectomy use. For the secondary outcomes of all-cause readmission and nursing home admission, we accounted for the competing risk of death by using cause-specific hazard models.21 This article follows the STROBE reporting guidelines (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Cohort Studies in Epidemiology). All data analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise Guide, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc, Cary, NC).

Results

Overall Cohort Within 24 Hours of Last Seen Normal

Of 22 794 patients admitted with cerebral ischemia, 16 935 (74.3%) presented to the first emergency department within 24 hours of their last seen normal time and were retained for full analyses. The cohort creation flowchart is shown in Figure S1. Compared with male patients, female patients were older, more likely to have hypertension, atrial fibrillation, dementia, higher frailty, or be living in a retirement home or nursing home prestroke (Table 1). Female patients were less likely to be current smokers or have diabetes, dyslipidemia, coronary artery disease, peripheral artery disease, or prestroke independence (modified Rankin Scale score ≤2). The National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score at presentation was also higher in female patients compared with male patients. Female patients were more likely to present during daytime hours but less likely to have witnessed stroke onset.

Stroke center type at first presentation, care at a comprehensive stroke center, and use of interhospital transfers were similar between female and male patients (Table 2). Although over 99.8% of patients received noncontrast neuroimaging, only 83.6% of female patients received intracranial neurovascular imaging compared with 87.8% of male patients (unadjusted RR, 0.95 [95% CI, 0.94–0.96]). This difference persisted after adjustment for baseline covariates (adjusted RR, 0.98 [95% CI, 0.97–0.99]). Fewer female patients received thrombolysis compared with male patients (20.6% female versus 22.3% male; unadjusted RR, 0.92 [95% CI, 0.87–0.98]), but this difference did not persist after adjustment (adjusted RR, 0.97 [95% CI, 0.93–1.02]). Thrombectomy was similar between the 2 groups (12.6% female versus 12.4% male; unadjusted RR, 1.02 [95% CI, 0.94–1.11]), but, after adjustment, female patients were more likely to be treated with thrombectomy (adjusted RR, 1.08 [95% CI, 1.01–1.16]).

Cohort With LVO

Among 14 533 patients who had intracranial neurovascular imaging, female patients had a statistically significantly higher proportion of intracranial vessel occlusion in any location (39.0% female versus 34.2% male), LVO (19.3% female versus 15.9% male), or middle cerebral artery M1 occlusion (17.9% female versus 14.1% male) with P<0.01 for all comparisons (Table 3). Sex differences in the baseline characteristics of the 2540 patients with LVO followed a similar pattern as the differences observed in the overall study cohort, except there were no sex differences in dyslipidemia, frailty risk score categories, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, or whether the stroke onset was witnessed (Table S2). Female patients with LVO were less likely to receive care at a comprehensive stroke center compared with male patients (80.8% female versus 84.8% male; P=0.01; Table S3).

Treatment with thrombolysis (38.5% female versus 44.3% male; unadjusted RR, 0.87 [95% CI, 0.79–0.95]) or thrombectomy (60.1% female versus 65.8% male; unadjusted RR, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.86–0.97]) was lower in female patients compared with male patients, but this difference was no longer present after adjustment (adjusted RR, 0.94 [95% CI, 0.87–1.02] for thrombolysis and 0.98 [95% CI, 0.93–1.03] for thrombectomy). Table S3 also lists the reasons for not treating patients with LVO with thrombectomy by sex. The most common reason for nontreatment was that the established infarct was deemed to be too extensive. Female patients were more likely than male patients to have characteristics and comorbidities that led to a decision to not pursue thrombectomy (23.3% female versus 17.2% male; P=0.02), while male patients were more likely to have the occlusion deemed not suitable or inaccessible as a reason for nontreatment (10.1% female versus 14.7% male; P=0.03).

With regards to long-term clinical outcomes, patients with LVO were followed for a median of 0.33 person-years for the primary composite outcome. After adjusting for covariates, there were no sex differences in the hazards of the composite primary outcome in the overall cohort of patients with LVO (adjusted HR, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.82–1.00]) or in those with LVO and not treated with thrombectomy (adjusted HR, 0.93 [95% CI, 0.79–1.09]), but female patients with LVO and treated with thrombectomy had a lower hazard of the composite outcome compared with male patients (adjusted HR, 0.88 [95% CI, 0.77–0.99]; Figure 1).

When we evaluated the individual components of the composite outcome, we found no sex differences in all-cause readmission or nursing home admission across all groups but made some interesting observations with all-cause mortality (median follow-up was 2.33 person-years for the mortality outcome). The hazard of death was higher in female patients with LVO compared with male patients in unadjusted comparisons (HR, 1.16 [95% CI, 1.04–1.30]), but this association was reversed after adjusting for covariates (adjusted HR, 0.85 [95% CI, 0.76–0.96]). Similar observations on the attenuation of the associations of sex and death were found when stratified by thrombectomy (Figure 1). Discussion

In this population-based cohort of patients hospitalized with cerebral ischemia within the first 24 hours of last seen normal, we found that female patients were less likely to receive intracranial neurovascular imaging compared with male patients, even after adjusting for baseline differences. However, among those who were imaged, female patients were more likely to have any intracranial occlusion and more likely to have an LVO.

Prior work on sex differences in the provision of thrombectomy in the population has yielded inconsistent results.6–11 A systematic review of this topic concluded that there were no sex differences in thrombectomy,22 but most studies did not account for whether an LVO was confirmed, which may explain why different studies reached different conclusions. Our study brings new information to this topic because our data reflect the broader continuum of care and include information on sex differences in the use of intracranial neurovascular imaging and detection of LVO before assessing differences in thrombectomy use and outcomes. We found that thrombectomy use was 8% higher among female patients presenting within 24 hours of last seen normal compared with male patients, but, once we restricted to patients with a confirmed LVO, there was no difference. Ongoing efforts evaluating access to thrombectomy and health equity by sex or other social determinants of health must account for the presence and location of vascular occlusion. Unfortunately, information on the presence of LVO is currently not routinely collected in administrative data, but the Ontario Stroke Registry used a combination of primary data collection and health administrative data linkage to obtain this information.13

Our study identified an important care gap: only 86% of patients had imaging of their intracranial vessels. In comparison, a French population–based registry study of patients with ischemic stroke reported that 93% of patients had arterial imaging.23 Moreover, it is not clear why female patients were less likely than male patients to have neurovascular imaging in Canada. Female patients had higher stroke severity, were more likely to arrive during daytime hours, and were equally likely to arrive at a comprehensive or primary stroke center compared with male patients. Although female patients were more likely to be older, frail, and have prestroke dependence, intracranial neurovascular imaging is the standard of care in patients with ischemic stroke,5,16 and the association persisted despite adjustment for baseline differences. Few prior population-based studies have evaluated sex differences in intracranial neurovascular imaging, and our findings identify this as a potential modifiable source of sex-based inequities in stroke care.12 Improving access to intracranial neurovascular imaging will likely require leadership at the system level, rather than at the time of the individual patient and healthcare professional interaction. A recent scientific statement from the American Heart Association stated that primary stroke centers should have 24/7 access to computed tomography angiogram with imaging interpretation within 20 minutes of completion.24 However, in Canada, only comprehensive stroke centers have a 24/7 computed tomography angiogram access requirement without guidance on time to imaging interpretation goals,25 and Accreditation Canada does not include any measurement of intracranial neurovascular imaging when awarding Stroke Distinction.26

With regards to long-term outcomes, crude mortality was higher in female patients compared with male patients, but the hazard of death after adjustment for baseline differences was lower in females in patients with LVO, in the overall cohort and among those treated with thrombectomy. Prior multicenter observational studies,27,28 exploratory analysis of clinical trials,29,30 and pooled meta-analysis31 on sex differences in thrombectomy outcomes have reported no sex differences in 90-day mortality. Some of the observational studies included patients undergoing thrombectomy for all occlusions, but the patient population was, nevertheless, predominantly people with LVO. Our study brings additional information about sex differences in long-term outcomes after thrombectomy in a population treated in routine clinical practice.12,22 The observation that the direction of the association between sex and long-term mortality changed after adjustment for baseline differences suggests that prestroke factors are accounting for the higher crude mortality in female patients. Our findings showed new information on sex differences in outcomes in patients with LVO who do not undergo thrombectomy and reasons for nontreatment.

Our study has several limitations that warrant discussion. Our sampling strategy included over 90% of stroke hospitalizations across Ontario, but patients admitted to hospitals with fewer than 70 annual stroke admissions were excluded. Such hospitals are often in more rural areas with limited stroke expertise, and we acknowledge that the gaps in screening for LVO may be even higher than what we report. Nevertheless, the Ontario hub-and-spoke stroke systems of care facilitate transfer of patients with hyperacute stroke or severe symptoms to a higher-level stroke center. Thus, we expect that most patients presenting acutely with stroke symptoms are redirected to hospitals included in our sampling strategy. The Ontario Stroke Registry only collected data on whether intracranial vascular imaging was completed during the index hospitalization, so we could not describe any delays in imaging and LVO diagnosis. The data were collected during fiscal years 2019/2020 and 2022/2023, before the publication of clinical trials showing efficacy of thrombectomy for stroke with basilar occlusions or with a large infarct core.32,33 As the treatment for stroke continues to evolve, our findings highlight the need for ongoing effort to optimize intracranial vascular imaging in clinical care and the importance of monitoring stroke care and outcomes based on intracranial occlusion status.

Conclusions

We showed that female patients with cerebral ischemia and presenting within 24 hours of last seen normal were less likely to receive intracranial neurovascular imaging compared with male patients, but, among those imaged, female patients were more likely to have an LVO. Although long-term crude mortality was higher in female patients than in male patients, adjusted hazards of death were lower in female patients. Our study identifies sex differences in intracranial neurovascular imaging as a potential modifiable factor for sex-based disparity in stroke care and outcomes. In addition, our findings suggest that research on stroke health services provision must account for screening and identification of intracranial occlusion to reach accurate conclusions.
