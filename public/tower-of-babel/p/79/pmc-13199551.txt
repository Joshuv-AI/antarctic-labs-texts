Primary small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma of the liver: a case report
Authors: Shuyue Yin, Shuping Li, Lijuan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a case of primary hepatic small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (PHNEC). The patient was a 74-year-old male with a history of hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection, who presented with persistent right upper quadrant and lower back pain for two weeks. Imaging studies revealed multiple nodules and masses in the left hepatic lobe, accompanied by enlargement of lymph nodes at the hepatic hilum and in the retroperitoneum. Subsequent liver biopsy was performed, and to rule out potential origins from the gastrointestinal tract or lungs, gastrointestinal endoscopy and chest computed tomography (CT) scans were conducted. These evaluations confirmed the diagnosis of hepatic small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma. Following one cycle of chemotherapy combined with localized radiotherapy, a reduction in the size of both lymph nodes and hepatic lesions was observed, achieving a partial response (PR). However, follow-up imaging one month later showed significant enlargement of the hepatic masses, indicating progressive disease (PD). Subsequent chemotherapy was withheld due to progressive hepatic dysfunction and superimposed infection. The patient ultimately succumbed to multi-organ failure, with an overall survival of 4 months. The imaging features of PHNEC are nonspecific, making diagnosis relatively challenging. Improving detection rates and establishing standardized treatment guidelines remain critical challenges for clinicians. We hope this case may serve as a reference for clinicians in the field and recommend close monitoring of serological markers of liver function throughout the entire course of treatment and follow-up.Keywords: Hepatic neoplasms, Primary neoplasms, Small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma, Radiotherapy, Chemotherapy

Introduction

Neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) arise from cells of the diffuse endocrine system and constitute a heterogeneous family of neoplasms. The most common sites of origin include the gastrointestinal tract, lungs, thymus, and pancreas [1]. Neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are classified into well-differentiated NETs and poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs) [1]. Analysis of the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database shows that 9% of neuroendocrine carcinomas originate from extrapulmonary sites [2], with the gastrointestinal (GI) tract being the most frequent location for extrapulmonary NECs [3]. The liver is a common site for tumor metastasis but a rare origin for primary carcinomas [4]. Primary hepatic neuroendocrine carcinomas (PHNECs) are extremely rare [5]; accounting for only 0.3% of all neuroendocrine neoplasms and approximately 0.46% of primary hepatic tumors [6]. The diagnostic challenge of PHNEC stems from its nonspecific early manifestations, including vague symptoms, lack of definitive serological markers, and indeterminate imaging features. PHNEC is characterized by high aggressiveness and short survival; however, no consensus treatment guidelines exist, and current therapeutic approaches are solely derived from case-based evidence. Herein, we report a case of primary hepatic small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma and conduct a review of relevant literature.

Case report

A 74-year-old male was admitted to the hospital due to pain in the right upper quadrant of the abdomen and the chest/back region. The pain was described as a persistent, dull ache severe enough to disrupt sleep. Initial evaluations at a local hospital, including electrocardiography (ECG) and chest CT, revealed no significant abnormalities. However, abdominal CT demonstrated patchy, low-density lesions at the hepatic dome and beneath the hepatic capsule (Fig. 1). Subsequent contrast-enhanced abdominal CT further confirmed these patchy, low-density lesions (3.4 cm × 2.9 cm) in the same locations, suggesting a neoplastic lesion with associated hemorrhage and mild dilatation of the portal vein (Fig. 1). Following symptomatic treatment with analgesics and antispasmodics, the patient’s condition improved, and he requested discharge for home recovery.

Three days prior to admission to our institution, the patient presented with worsening pain and was admitted to our department with a preliminary diagnosis of “hepatic malignancy”. The patient reported fair appetite and sleep quality, normal urination and defecation, and no significant recent weight changes. His medical history included HCV infection for over two decades and a subtotal gastrectomy in 2003 for perforated gastric ulcer. There was no history of smoking or alcohol consumption. Physical examination revealed a conscious and alert patient with a Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) score of 90 and a Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) pain score of 6. Hepatic percussion tenderness was positive, with pain radiating to the chest and back. No other notable abnormalities were identified.

Upon admission, laboratory tests showed significantly elevated tumor markers: alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) at 234.73 ng/mL (reference range: <8.78 ng/mL) and neuron-specific enolase (NSE) at 35.65 ng/mL (reference range: <20 ng/mL). Other notable findings included an activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) of 42.60 s, normal transaminase levels, urine urobilinogen (1+), HCV antibody titer of 852 AU/mL (reference range: <5 AU/mL), and negative HCV RNA. Contrast-enhanced abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated: (1) Multiple nodular and mass lesions (6.4 cm×4.3 cm) in the left hepatic lobe, suggestive of a malignant neoplasm (with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma included in the differential diagnosis); (2) Multiple lymph nodes at the hepatic hilum and in the retroperitoneum, highly suspicious for metastasis; (3) Multiple hepatic cysts and a small right renal cyst (Fig. 2).

Subsequent liver biopsy confirmed small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (Fig. 3). Immunohistochemical staining yielded the following profile: Glypican-3 (-), HepPar-1 (-), CK19 (-), CK7 (-), CD34 (-), CEA (-), CgA (-), Syn (+), Arginase-1 (-), AFP (-), Ki-67 (90%), HSP70 (+), P53 (missense mutation), CD45 (-), and CD56 (+). Gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed no abnormalities. Further improvement of enhanced chest CT and gastrointestinal endoscopy was performed, and the results indicated no abnormalities in the lungs, mediastinum, and digestive tract.

Pathological image by Hematoxylin-Eosin (H&E) staining (magnification ×200). The tumor cells were round, oval, or short spindle-shaped, with granular nuclear chromatin. The cell nuclei were deeply stained, with obvious cellular pleomorphism, and necrosis and mitotic figures were easily seen

Following a multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussion, the patient was diagnosed with metastatic hepatic small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma, staged as cT3N1M0 and China Liver Cancer Staging (CNLC) stage IIIb. Given the large tumor size with intratumoral hemorrhage and extensive lymph node involvement at the hepatic hilum and in the retroperitoneum, surgical resection was deemed unfeasible. The recommended treatment was systemic chemotherapy combined with local radiotherapy targeting the involved lymph nodes.

The patient had a KPS score of 90, Child-Pugh grade A liver function (Fig. 4), and no other comorbidities. After confirming the absence of contraindications, the first cycle of chemotherapy with the EC regimen (carboplatin 300 mg on Day 1 and etoposide 0.1 g/day on Days 1–3, administered intravenously) was initiated on September 17, 2025. Due to the bulky retroperitoneal lymph nodes, image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) targeting the retroperitoneal mass was administered from September 22 to October 17, 2025 (Fig. 5). The regimen comprised IGRT with 6 MeV X-rays, delivering a total dose (DT) of 45 Gy in 30 fractions (1.5 Gy per fraction) to the planning target volume (PTV) on a twice-daily (BID) schedule.

During chemoradiotherapy, the patient experienced significant nausea and vomiting and developed Grade II myelosuppression (leukocyte count: 2.5 × 10⁹/L), accompanied by a weight loss of approximately 7 kg. Supportive care included human granulocyte colony-stimulating factor injections (300 µg) to elevate white blood cell counts, metoclopramide tablets (5 mg TID) for antiemetic purposes, and sustained-release hydromorphone hydrochloride tablets (16 mg QD, later adjusted) for analgesia. Upon completion of treatment, follow-up assessments showed normalization of AFP and NSE levels (Fig. 6). Comparison of cone-beam CT (CBCT) images obtained in the radiotherapy room with pre-radiotherapy scans demonstrated reduced sizes of both hepatic and retroperitoneal lesions. Per RECIST criteria, the treatment response was assessed as partial response (PR).

One month after discharge, the patient returned for a scheduled follow-up evaluation (November 11, 2025). Laboratory investigations revealed the following key findings: NSE elevated to 34.60 ng/mL; aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 71.03 U/L (reference range: 15–40 U/L); gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) at 171.23 U/L (reference range: 10–60 U/L); alkaline phosphatase (ALP) at 216.88 U/L (reference range: 45–125 U/L) (Fig. 4); prolonged APTT at 47.50 s; and fibrinogen level at 4.28 g/L. Contrast-enhanced abdominal MRI demonstrated: (1) Multiple hepatic nodules and masses (11.9 cm×7.7 cm), showing significant interval growth compared to the previous scan on September 13, 2025, with a new lesion identified in Segment IV—findings consistent with intrahepatic progression of primary hepatic neuroendocrine tumor, requiring close follow-up; (2) Multiple lymph nodes at the hepatic hilum and in the retroperitoneum with reduced size and corresponding decreased signal on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI); (3) Persistent multiple hepatic cysts and a small right renal cyst (Fig. 7).

Based on the observed deterioration in liver function, rising tumor markers, and radiological evidence of disease progression with new hepatic lesions. Per RECIST criteria, the patient was assessed as having progressive disease (PD). During hospitalization, the patient developed yellow watery stools for one day (more than 10 episodes in total) due to improper diet, and additionally presented with pulmonary infection in the context of an influenza outbreak. Symptomatic treatment was administered, leading to improvement in the patient’s respiratory symptoms; however, transaminase levels remained markedly elevated (Fig. 4). Given the patient’s poor physical status and inability to tolerate further anti-tumor therapy, the patient opted for discharge for rest and continued oral hepatoprotective medications on an outpatient basis. The patient was advised to enhance nutritional intake with adequate protein supplementation in the diet, abstain from tobacco and alcohol, and avoid spicy, greasy and cold foods. Furthermore, regular daily routines and moderate physical activity were recommended.

Half a month after rest at home, the patient gradually developed apathy and progressive generalized jaundice. Therefore, the patient was readmitted to the hospital on December 29, 2025. Physical examination revealed obvious generalized cutaneous and scleral jaundice, as well as significant edema of the lower extremities. The patient was in poor mental state with markedly increased sleep duration and slowed response to external stimuli. After admission, the patient gradually progressed to somnolence. The Performance Status (PS) score was 3. Laboratory tests showed significantly elevated transaminase and total bilirubin levels (Fig. 4), along with decreased albumin level (Child-Pugh class C, CNLC stage Ⅳ). The patient maintained a persistent hypotensive state with blood pressure fluctuating between approximately 69–94/48–66 mmHg. After a Multidisciplinary Team (MDT) discussion, the patient was diagnosed with end-stage malignant tumor and could no longer tolerate anti-tumor therapy. Upon being informed of the condition, the patient and their family decided to refuse all resuscitative measures and requested only palliative care to relieve suffering. Despite treatment with magnesium isoglycyrrhizinate, glutathione, and bicyclol tablets for hepatoprotection, as well as human albumin for albumin supplementation, the therapeutic effect was poor, and no significant improvement was observed in serological indicators. Eventually, the patient died of multiple organ failure (hepatic failure, renal failure, and cardiac failure) on January 5, 2026. The overall survival (OS) was approximately 4 months (Fig. 8).

Discussion

PHNEC is extremely rare, and three main hypotheses have been proposed regarding its origin [7]: (1) Derivation from neuroendocrine cells within the intrahepatic biliary epithelium; (2) Origin from ectopic pancreatic or adrenal tissue with neuroendocrine function located in the liver; (3) Development from malignant hepatic stem cell precursors. Most researchers favor the first two theories.​ Several studies have indicated that this may be attributed to genetic deletions and mutations. Researchers have explored the application of gene sequencing technology for PHNEC and recommended genetic testing for all patients with this condition, which may expand our understanding of PHNEC and enable targeted therapy to serve as one of the treatment options for inoperable patients [8].

Viral hepatitis is a major etiological factor for the development of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [9]. The patient had a history of hepatitis C infection for more than 20 years, with abnormally elevated levels of NSE and AFP. Initially, we considered the possibility of a mixed lesion of hepatocellular carcinoma and neuroendocrine carcinoma. However, the punctured tissue suggested neuroendocrine carcinoma. In addition, the patient’s liver function indicators and coagulation indicators were normal in the early stage, while the lesion progressed rapidly, which was consistent with the manifestations of small-cell neuroendocrine carcinoma. Therefore, the patient was diagnosed with neuroendocrine carcinoma. However, the absence of autopsy samples precluded a definitive exclusion of this possibility. Notably, the synchronous occurrence of HCC and PHNEC is extremely rare [10].

The imaging manifestations of hepatic NENs are heterogeneous [11]. To date, no specific CT/MRI imaging features are available to establish a definitive diagnosis of PHNEC. PHNEC is prone to being misdiagnosed as HCC; however, some PHNEC lesions exhibit intralesional cystic change, hemorrhage and necrosis on MRI and CT images, a feature that allows for its differentiation from HCC [12]. The imaging manifestations of the patient’s lesion were as follows: mild enhancement began in the arterial phase, followed by progressive enhancement, with no obvious emptying phenomenon. The lesion had a clear boundary, contained cystic degeneration and necrosis, and showed progressive enhancement on contrast-enhanced imaging. In contrast, the most typical feature of hepatocellular carcinoma is a “fast-in and fast-out” enhancement pattern on contrast-enhanced imaging, with mostly unclear boundaries.

The distinction between primary and secondary NEC can be achieved via imaging examinations and gastroenteroscopy. In the present case, initial contrast-enhanced abdominal CT at our institution raised suspicion for intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma. However, the final diagnosis of hepatic small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma was confirmed based on histopathology and immunohistochemistry. To determine whether the tumor was primary or metastatic, the patient underwent contrast-enhanced chest and abdominal CT as well as gastrointestinal endoscopy, which ruled out pulmonary, mediastinum, and gastrointestinal origins. Immunohistochemistry plays a crucial role in the diagnosis of NETs. Chromogranin A (CgA) and synaptophysin (Syn) are considered highly sensitive immunohistochemical markers for NETs [6]. It has been reported that Syn is typically positive in neuroendocrine neoplasms, whereas CgA may be negative in some cases [13]. In our case, CgA was negative and Syn was positive, which is consistent with the findings of this report. Unfortunately, we did not assess thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF-1) and insulinoma-associated protein 1 (INSM1) to further support the diagnosis. Although no extrahepatic primary lesion was found in the existing examinations, the possibility of an occult primary tumor cannot be completely ruled out. Due to the rapid progression of the disease and the patient’s economic constraints, the PET-CT examination was not completed. Since no abnormalities were found on enhanced chest CT, further bronchoscopy was not performed. Instead, diagnosis and treatment were carried out based on the existing examination results.

No globally accepted consensus exists for the management of PHNEC. Surgery remains the first-line option when feasible. In a series of 73 patients with primary hepatic neuroendocrine neoplasms (PHNENs) reported by Zha et al., the 5-year and 10-year postoperative survival rates were 80% and 75%, respectively [14]. For inoperable patients, transcatheter arterial chemoembolization (TACE), platinum-based chemotherapy (EP/EC regimens), radiofrequency ablation (RFA), liver transplantation, and targeted therapy have been used in previous cases for the treatment of PHNEC [15–20]. Our patient presented with retroperitoneal lymph node metastasis at initial diagnosis, making him ineligible for surgery. Neuroendocrine tumors are generally hypervascular and sensitive to ischemia; thus, transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) may serve as an effective alternative for patients who are not candidates for surgery [21]. However, current evidence indicates that TACE primarily provides favorable short-term outcomes [22]. Over time, patients may exhibit minimal lipiodol retention, leading to diminished therapeutic efficacy. In this case, given the large tumor size, TACE posed a considerable risk of major hemorrhage. The European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) guidelines recommend EP or EC as the first-line chemotherapy regimens for advanced neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) [23]. Additionally, a retrospective study demonstrated no significant difference in survival rates between patients receiving EP and EC chemotherapy [24]. Furthermore, neuroendocrine tumors with a Ki-67 index ≥ 55% typically exhibit a better response to platinum-based chemotherapy [3].

This was an elderly patient with poor physical status and a Ki-67 index of 90%, presenting with rapidly progressive disease. During the 10-day transfer from a local hospital to our institution, the intrahepatic lesions had enlarged significantly. In addition, the retroperitoneally metastatic lymph nodes were large, and the pathological type was small-cell carcinoma. Based on a comprehensive assessment, we selected the less toxic EC regimen for systemic chemotherapy combined with radiotherapy for the enlarged retroperitoneal lymph nodes (The dose limits of organs at risk were all within the normal range). After local radiotherapy, the liver lesion was slightly reduced compared with the previous one, and we speculate that this was the “abscopal effect” induced by radiotherapy. And the overall therapeutic response was evaluated as PR after the completion of the first cycle of chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Radiotherapy is conventionally regarded as a local treatment modality; however, a growing body of clinical research has demonstrated that it can trigger systemic innate and adaptive immune responses and, in some cases, induce tumor regression outside the irradiated area—a phenomenon known as the “abscopal effect” [25, 26].

Hepatic small-cell neuroendocrine carcinoma is characterized by rapid progression. A retrospective study identified Ki-67 index, ECOG PS score and treatment regimen as independent prognostic factors for overall survival (OS) in patients with advanced PHNEC [24]. Nevertheless, this patient had a Ki-67 index of 90%, indicating an aggressive biological behavior and high malignancy. A follow-up examination one month later revealed disease progression of the intrahepatic lesions, while the retroperitoneal lymph nodes showed no progression due to the excellent local control achieved by radiotherapy, resulting in an overall evaluation of progressive disease (PD). The patient was unable to tolerate further chemotherapy due to poor physical status, and hepatoprotective therapy failed to control the rapid deterioration of liver function caused by the tumor. The patient subsequently developed hepatic failure and eventually died, with an OS of approximately 4 months. The limited treatment options contributed to the poor prognosis of this patient.

Conclusion

Primary hepatic neuroendocrine carcinoma (PHNEC) is a rare malignancy with a poor prognosis. It presents no specific clinical symptoms or imaging features; however, intralesional cystic change, hemorrhage, and necrosis observed in some PHNEC lesions may facilitate differentiation from hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). The final diagnosis mainly relies on pathological and immunohistochemical findings. Equally importantly, comprehensive imaging examinations and gastroenteroscopy should be performed to rule out the possibility of metastasis. Individualized treatment regimens for PHNEC should be selected based on the tumor location, size, pathological characteristics, and the patient’s specific clinical status. Surgical resection is the mainstay of treatment. For inoperable patients, comprehensive treatment modalities such as systemic chemotherapy, transcatheter arterial chemoembolization (TACE), radiotherapy, biological therapy, liver transplantation, and molecular targeted therapy are available.

Herein, we present a case of PHNEC with rapid disease progression. The subsequent cycle of chemotherapy was delayed due to deterioration of liver function complicated by infection, and the patient eventually died 4 months after diagnosis. However, there were still some limitations in the diagnosis and treatment process of our case: the pathology was based on puncture specimens rather than gross specimens, and no more immunohistochemical examinations or more comprehensive tests were performed to distinguish whether the liver lesion was primary or secondary; in addition, the limited treatment options led to a poor prognosis. We hope this case provides valuable insights for clinicians in this field. We also recommend close monitoring of serological indicators of liver function during treatment and follow-up. If abnormalities occur, early intervention and symptomatic treatment should be initiated promptly to strive for the opportunity to continue anti-tumor therapy.
