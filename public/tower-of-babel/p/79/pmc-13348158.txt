Comparison of overactive bladder symptoms and toileting behaviors between imams and male teachers: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Anil Erdik, Haci Ibrahim Cimen, Kemal Demirhan, Deniz Gul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundToileting behaviors and occupational constraints are increasingly recognized as contributors to lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) and overactive bladder (OAB). Religious practices involving ritual ablution require pre-emptive voiding multiple times daily, yet no study has examined whether such behaviors influence OAB symptoms. This study compared urinary habits, occupational stress, and OAB severity between imams, among whom ritual pre-ablution voiding was commonly reported, and male teachers, whose work may restrict timely restroom access.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, 75 imams and 83 male teachers from Karasu, Türkiye completed validated questionnaires: the OAB-V8 for bladder symptoms, the Short Index of Job Satisfaction (SIJS), and the Turkish version of the General Work Stress Scale. Exclusion criteria included diabetes mellitus, previous lower urinary tract or prostate-related urological surgery, and medical treatment for benign prostatic hyperplasia. Group comparisons were performed using appropriate non-parametric tests.ResultsTeachers demonstrated significantly higher OAB-V8 scores than imams (median 8 vs. 6, p = 0.022). Imams reported more frequent pre-emptive voiding before ablution and predominantly seated voiding, whereas teachers reported toileting patterns shaped by work schedule and break structure. Stress scores were significantly higher among teachers, aligning with prior evidence linking occupational strain to OAB symptoms. Fluid and caffeine intake did not differ meaningfully between groups. Post-void dribbling was less common among imams (16.0% vs. 31.3%, p = 0.024).ConclusionRitually motivated pre-emptive voiding among imams may be associated with a lower OAB symptom burden. Conversely, restricted toileting opportunities and higher occupational stress may contribute to increased symptom burden among teachers. Prospective studies are needed to clarify causal pathways and evaluate ritual-based voiding as a behavioral correlate of OAB symptom patterns.

Introduction

Overactive bladder (OAB) is a prevalent condition defined by the International Continence Society (ICS) as “urinary urgency, usually accompanied by frequency and nocturia, with or without urgency urinary incontinence, in the absence of urinary tract infection or other pathology” [1]. The global prevalence of OAB ranges from 10% to 20% among adults and tends to increase with age [2, 3]. OAB negatively impacts quality of life, sleep, social functioning, and psychological well-being, and contributes substantially to healthcare costs worldwide [4, 5].

The pathophysiology of OAB is multifactorial, involving neural, myogenic, and behavioral mechanisms [6]. Beyond intrinsic physiological factors, behavioral and occupational conditions—such as chronic urine retention or delayed voiding—have been shown to influence bladder function. Occupational stress and unhealthy toileting behaviors, particularly delayed voiding, have been reported to mediate the relationship between job strain and OAB among operating room nurses [7]. Similarly, occupational stress has been identified as an independent risk factor for OAB in female healthcare workers, even after adjusting for demographic and obstetric factors [8]. In addition, restricted toilet access and infrequent voiding behaviors have been linked to increased urinary urgency and frequency in various professional groups, including teachers and nurses [9].

However, little is known about occupational or cultural routines that may have the opposite—protective—effect on bladder health. Religious practices involving ritual cleansing (ablution) and regular voiding before prayers may promote healthy bladder emptying habits.

Imams are responsible for leading daily prayers five times a day, and many may void before prayer as part of religious preparation. This behavior may prevent prolonged bladder filling and thus reduce the risk of detrusor overactivity. Conversely, teaching professionals often experience restricted toilet access during lessons, a condition previously associated with increased lower urinary tract symptoms [9].

We aimed to explore whether differences in toileting behaviors between imams and teachers are associated with differences in OAB symptoms.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted among male imams and teachers working in the Karasu district of Sakarya Province, Türkiye. Participants were reached through their respective institutions with official permissions obtained from relevant administrative authorities. Individuals who expressed interest in the survey were contacted by the study coordinator via telephone or e-mail to assess eligibility. To be eligible, participants had to self-identify as currently working as an imam or a male teacher. Those meeting inclusion criteria were informed about the purpose of the study and provided consent prior to participation. Because the occupational role of imam in our study setting is male-only, the study was restricted to male participants. To ensure comparability between occupational groups, only male teachers were included.

Participants were approached through their institutions rather than through symptom-based recruitment. Both occupational groups were invited using the same administrative pathway and were screened according to the same eligibility criteria. Individuals were enrolled regardless of the presence or absence of urinary symptoms in order to reduce symptom-driven recruitment bias.

A total of 171 individuals initially responded to the survey (84 imams and 87 teachers). Exclusion criteria included medical conditions or treatments known to influence lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), such as a history of diabetes mellitus (DM), previous lower urinary tract or prostate-related urological surgery, or the use of medical therapy for benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), including alpha-blockers or 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors (5-ARIs). No participants were found to have used medical treatment for OAB. Among the imams, nine participants were excluded (6 due to DM, 2 due to previous lower urinary tract or prostate-related urological surgery, and 1 due to the use of BPH-related medical therapy). Among the teachers, four individuals were excluded (3 due to DM and 1 due to the use of BPH-related medical therapy). Following these exclusions, 158 participants were included in the final analysis, consisting of 75 imams and 83 teachers (Fig. 1).

Participants were not included or excluded on the basis of LUTS status. Instead, urinary symptom burden was assessed after enrollment using the Turkish version of the OAB-V8, which was used to quantify OAB-related symptoms across the full study sample. We did not apply a separate predefined exclusion criterion for neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction; instead, eligibility was based on the available self-reported medical history obtained during participant screening.

Ethics statement / study approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Sakarya University Ethics Committee on 10 October 2025, under decision number E-523,247. All participants were informed about the aims and procedures of the study, and written or electronic informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. Participation was voluntary, and all responses were collected anonymously to ensure confidentiality.

Data collection tools

Overactive bladder questionnaire (OAB-V8)

OAB symptoms were assessed using the Turkish version of the Overactive Bladder Questionnaire-V8 (OAB-V8), an 8-item symptom-based screening tool evaluating urgency, frequency, nocturia, and urge incontinence. Each item is scored from 0 to 5, yielding a total score of 0–40, with higher scores indicating greater symptom burden. The Turkish version has demonstrated good validity and reliability, and a cut-off value of 11 has been proposed for probable OAB [10]. These psychometric properties confirm that the Turkish OAB-V8 is a valid and reliable tool suitable for epidemiological and occupational health studies. In the present study, the OAB-V8 was used as a symptom quantification tool rather than as a recruitment or screening criterion.

Job satisfaction – short index of job satisfaction (SIJS)

Job satisfaction was measured using the Turkish version of the Short Index of Job Satisfaction (SIJS), originally developed by Brayfield & Rothe [11] and later condensed into a 5-item form by Judge et al. [12]. The SIJS is a 5-item instrument scored on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater job satisfaction. The Turkish adaptation has shown satisfactory psychometric validity and internal consistency [13].

General work stress scale (GWSS)

Occupational stress was measured using the Turkish version of the General Work Stress Scale (GWSS), a 9-item unidimensional instrument scored on a 5-point Likert scale. Higher scores indicate greater perceived occupational stress. The Turkish version has demonstrated strong reliability and validity [14].

Procedure

Participants completed the survey during non-working hours, either in person or electronically, under supervision of the research team to ensure completeness. For imams, particular attention was given to avoid potential influence of religious duties on response timing. The survey included demographic questions (age, education, years of professional experience, daily fluid intake, and caffeine consumption) in addition to the validated scales. To minimize missing data and item-level misunderstanding, questionnaires were completed under supervision of the research team; however, no bladder diary or frequency-volume chart was collected.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 23, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between imams and teachers were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A multivariable logistic regression model was constructed using OAB-V8 ≥ 11 as the dependent variable and age, occupation, SIJS, and GWSS scores as independent variables.

Results

A total of 158 male participants were included in the final analysis, comprising 75 imams (47.5%) and 83 teachers (52.5%). There were no significant differences between groups regarding the presence of comorbidities or regular medication use (p > 0.05). The prevalence of OAB-V8 ≥ 11 was 16.0% among imams and 21.7% among teachers, with no statistically significant group difference (p = 0.363).

Demographic characteristics

Although there was no statistically significant difference in age between teachers and imams (39 vs. 38 years, [IQR 16 vs. IQR 14], p = 0.310), age-adjusted analyses were performed to minimize potential confounding. Furthermore, although teachers appeared to have longer professional experience, there was no difference (17 vs. 13 years, [IQR 16 vs. IQR 17], p = 0.054).

Overactive bladder symptoms & occupational stress and job satisfaction

Contrary to the initial hypothesis that imams would be more prone to OAB due to delayed voiding requirements, teachers demonstrated higher symptom burden as measured by OAB-V8. Teachers reported significantly higher job satisfaction but higher perceived occupational stress compared with imams. Marked differences were observed in urination-related behaviors between the two groups. A significantly higher proportion of imams reported voiding in a seated position (93.3%) compared with teachers (69.9%) (p < 0.001). Furthermore, 61.3% of imams reported performing ritual ablution before each prayer, and 54.7% reported voiding immediately prior to ablution, suggesting the presence of frequent pre-emptive voiding patterns. Among teachers, 68.6% reported using the restroom before going to work, 56.5% reported voiding during breaks between classes, and 28.9% reported using the restroom at school before returning home, indicating that toileting opportunities were at least partially structured by the work schedule.

Additionally, 60% of imams reported using a specific technique to prevent residual urine dribbling after voiding (p < 0.001). Consistent with this finding, post-void dribbling was less commonly reported among imams than teachers (16.0% vs. 31.3%, respectively; p = 0.024).

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate whether occupational voiding restrictions influence OAB symptom severity among imams compared to male teachers. Contrary to the initial hypothesis suggesting that imams would exhibit higher OAB symptom burden due to potential limitations in accessing restroom facilities during religious duties, the results demonstrated the opposite.

Imams reported significantly lower OAB-V8 scores than teachers. This finding challenges the traditionally accepted notion that occupationally induced voiding delay consistently increases LUTS risk and suggests that behavioral patterns specific to religious practices may be associated with a lower symptom burden.

Previous research has demonstrated that occupational stress and voiding postponement are associated with an increased risk of OAB and LUTS. Xu et al. found that job strain affected urinary symptoms through delayed voiding behavior and elevated stress response among nursing staff [7]. Similarly, Zhang et al. reported that occupational stress was a significant independent predictor of OAB symptoms among healthcare professionals, even after adjustment for demographic and obstetric variables [8].

In alignment with these findings, teachers in our study exhibited significantly higher levels of occupational stress compared to imams. This may partially explain their greater OAB symptom burden, as psychosocial stress is known to influence detrusor overactivity through central nervous system mechanisms [4, 8].

Specifically, heightened sympathetic activation may suppress normal voiding signals and precipitate urgency and frequency, features commonly associated with OAB. Interestingly, when adjusted for age, occupation, job satisfaction, and perceived work stress, only work stress remained independently associated with OAB symptom severity. This suggests that the observed group differences may be driven more by psychosocial factors than by occupation itself. This finding supports previous evidence indicating that psychological well-being and perceived job strain play a significant role in lower urinary tract symptom perception.

Conversely, imams’ toileting behaviors appeared to favorably modulate bladder dynamics. Ritual ablution (wudu), performed before each of the five daily prayers, is commonly preceded by voluntary voiding to maintain ritual purity. In our study, 61.3% of imams reported voiding before ablution, and 93.3% reported voiding in a seated position—behaviors which promote complete bladder emptying and may reduce residual urine accumulation. Furthermore, 60% of imams reported using a specific post-void maneuver to prevent urine dribbling, compared with only 14.5% of teachers (p < 0.001). This suggests increased awareness and intentional urinary control practices among imams, likely derived from religious obligations.

Johari et al. demonstrated that ritual behaviors such as ablution may promote controlled bodily practices and influence daily behaviors beyond spiritual intentions [15]. Thus, routine voiding prior to prayer may reflect a behavioral pattern that could be associated with reduced bladder overdistension and urgency episodes.

Previous qualitative studies suggest that chronic habitual holding or infrequent voiding may be detrimental to bladder health [16], although the exact physiological mechanisms remain unclear. Standard bladder training for OAB generally aims to progressively increase voiding intervals and improve bladder capacity rather than encourage frequent pre-emptive voiding [6].

Therefore, the ritual pre-emptive voiding observed in the present study should not be interpreted as equivalent to guideline-based bladder training, but rather as a cross-sectional behavioral pattern identified in a specific occupational-cultural context. Existing literature on behavioral interventions indicates that pelvic floor muscle training can suppress urgency and support bladder control [17]. In this context, the frequent pre-emptive voiding observed among imams prior to ritual ablution may represent a behavioral pattern associated with less prolonged bladder filling. Furthermore, behavioral techniques such as timed voiding are considered first-line management approaches in OAB treatment guidelines to promote improved bladder control rather than reactive urgency suppression [18]. Although no study to date has demonstrated a direct link between voiding suppression and increased intravesical pressure, prolonged delay in voiding may reinforce unfavorable toileting behaviors, potentially contributing to symptom persistence. Consequently, the frequent early voiding behavior seen in imams may be associated with a lower symptom burden; however, whether this pattern is beneficial, neutral, or potentially maladaptive cannot be determined from the present cross-sectional data and requires confirmation in longitudinal and interventional studies.

Although no study directly links voiding posture with detrusor function or complete bladder emptying, it may be hypothesized that seated voiding facilitates better relaxation of the pelvic floor muscles in men. In our study, seated voiding was significantly more prevalent among imams than teachers, which may be related to the lower rates of post-void dribbling observed in this group (16.0% vs. 31.3%). There is no evidence from current literature to support a direct physiological relationship between voiding posture and urinary leakage; therefore, this interpretation should be considered hypothesis-generating. Similarly, while no published research has established associative mechanisms between behavioral conditioning and detrusor regulation, increased awareness of urinary control in individuals who routinely void prior to ablution may reflect consistent toileting habits that could positively influence symptom perception. Further urodynamic and behavioral studies are warranted to explore the impact of postural and ritual voiding patterns on bladder function.

In the present study, lifestyle-related factors such as total fluid intake and caffeine consumption did not differ significantly between imams and teachers. Previous evidence does not demonstrate a statistically significant association between overall fluid or stimulant beverage intake and OAB symptom severity [5]. However, Qudah et al. reported that daily fluid intake exceeding 2,500 mL may be associated with a potential increase in OAB risk, although this finding did not reach statistical significance and requires cautious interpretation.

Furthermore, qualitative research suggests that individuals may intentionally regulate fluid intake to avoid urinary urgency, particularly in situations where access to toilet facilities is limited [16]. Taken together, these findings indicate that lifestyle habits alone are unlikely to explain the differences observed in OAB symptoms between groups in the current study.

Instead, occupational and behavioral patterns may play a more prominent role in shaping bladder symptomatology in this population. Future studies should incorporate bladder diaries or frequency-volume charts to better characterize the timing, frequency, and functional context of ritual and occupational voiding behaviors.

Behavioral therapy strategies such as scheduled voiding and urgency suppression aim to improve bladder control by modifying voiding habits and altering the patient’s behavioral response to urinary urgency, as supported by prior literature on behavioral management of OAB [19]. Although these approaches are theoretically well justified, evidence regarding their long-term effectiveness remains inconclusive [20]. In this context, the frequent pre-emptive voiding practiced by imams before ritual ablution may resemble elements of bladder training strategies by preventing prolonged bladder filling; however, this interpretation is hypothesis-based and has not been directly evaluated in religious populations. Qualitative findings also indicate that some individuals engage in voiding in the absence of strong urgency due to situational or habitual factors [16], which may conceptually align with such pre-emptive voiding behavior.

Therefore, it may be proposed that ritual-driven voiding represents a culturally integrated, low-cost behavioral approach that could potentially contribute to symptom control, although this remains speculative and requires investigation in future prospective studies.

The potential influence of occupational stress on bladder function should also be considered. Job-related constraints, including limited bathroom access during working hours, have been identified as possible contributors to voiding dysfunction in occupational health studies [9]. In the present study, teachers demonstrated higher OAB-V8 scores and elevated stress levels compared with imams, consistent with evidence linking occupational strain to increased OAB symptom burden [8]. While the precise physiological pathway has not been fully elucidated in this context, stress has been associated with alterations in central processing of urinary signals and may contribute to urgency-related symptoms. These findings suggest that workplace modifications such as improving restroom accessibility and incorporating stress management strategies may be beneficial in mitigating OAB-related symptoms among individuals in high-demand professions, including educators.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any inference regarding causality or temporal directionality between toileting behaviors and OAB symptoms. Accordingly, the observed associations may reflect reverse causation, residual confounding, or bidirectional relationships rather than a true causal effect. Second, the use of self-reported questionnaires may be subject to information and recall bias, particularly in populations with strong cultural or religious identity, where social desirability may influence how participants report toileting habits or urinary symptoms. Third, objective urofunctional assessments were not obtained. Specifically, bladder diaries, frequency-volume charts, post-void residual urine measurements, and urodynamic testing were not performed. As a result, pre-emptive voiding could not be objectively quantified and the functional significance of the observed behavioral patterns remains uncertain. Neurogenic conditions affecting bladder function were not systematically screened as a separate exclusion category, and therefore residual confounding related to neurological disease cannot be fully excluded. Moreover, variability in occupational roles (e.g., primary vs. secondary school teachers, mosque leaders vs. supporting staff) was not evaluated and may influence stress-related and voiding behaviors.

Additionally, the study included only male participants. This design choice reflected the fact that the imam group was inherently male-only in our study setting, and male teachers were selected to preserve comparability between occupational groups. Nevertheless, this limits the generalizability of the findings to women, including female teachers, among whom LUTS prevalence and toileting behaviors may differ. Religious toileting behaviors were partially characterized through self-report, but the exact consistency, context, and objective frequency of ablution-related voiding habits were not fully measured in either group. Although 61.3% of imams reported performing ablution before each prayer and 54.7% reported voiding immediately before ablution, these data were based on self-report and were not objectively verified. In addition, comparable information on the frequency of religiously related voiding practices in teachers was not systematically collected, which may have resulted in residual behavioral confounding. Several potentially relevant covariates were not assessed, including anxiety/depression, sleep quality, body mass index, prostate-related factors, and physical activity. These unmeasured variables may have influenced both urinary symptoms and perceived occupational stress. Finally, the study was conducted in a single district (Karasu, Sakarya), and occupation-related stress was assessed via self-report rather than objective biomarkers (e.g., physiological stress indicators), which may further limit external validity. In addition, participation was voluntary after institutional contact, which may have introduced self-selection bias. Individuals who were more health-conscious or more willing to discuss urinary symptoms may have been more likely to participate, whereas some individuals with more severe symptoms or privacy concerns may have declined participation. No formal a priori sample size or power calculation was performed because the study was designed as an exploratory cross-sectional comparison. Therefore, non-significant findings should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, multiple behavioral comparisons were performed, which may have increased the risk of type I error. Future prospective, multicenter studies incorporating objective urofunctional assessment and behavioral monitoring are warranted to confirm these findings and strengthen causal interpretation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, despite initial assumptions that delayed voiding during religious duties would predispose imams to higher OAB symptomatology, our findings suggest that frequent pre-emptive voiding associated with ritual purification may be associated with a lower OAB symptom burden. By contrast, higher stress levels and occupational voiding limitations among teachers may contribute to increased OAB burden. These results highlight the potential role of culturally driven habitual voiding behaviors in supporting urinary health and support the need for occupational health policies that facilitate adequate restroom access. Further longitudinal research is warranted to clarify causality and to determine whether such behavioral patterns have relevance beyond this specific occupational-cultural context.
