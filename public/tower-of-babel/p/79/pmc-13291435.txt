Engineering Pseudomonas protegens as a Targeted Antifungal‐Effector Delivery Chassis via Heterologous Type III Secretion System Reconstitution
Authors: Yuyuan Peng, Simei He, Guofan Zhang, Jiatao Yang, Rong Fan, Chen Li, Jiajie Xie, Xinran Long, Linger Qian, Jiayi Wang, Yanmin He, Hui Cai, Yushan Zhang, Qiuqian Zhang, Shaopeng Pan, Shengbiao Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study addresses the core challenge of Fusarium wilt control in agricultural production. We successfully reconstituted a functional heterologous type III secretion system (T3SS) from Photorhabdus luminescens in the biocontrol bacterium Pseudomonas protegens Pf‐5, creating an engineered molecular syringe for targeted delivery of antifungal effectors. The system is activated under low‐calcium conditions, achieved by cultivation in calcium‐limited medium followed by EGTA‐mediated chelation of residual Ca2+, enabling conditional secretion of effector proteins. By fusing the antifungal protein Bg9562 to the N‐terminal secretion signal of the T3SS effector LopT and co‐expressing it with the cognate chaperone SlcT, we obtained fluorescence‐based evidence for T3SS‐dependent delivery of Bg9562 into the hyphae of multiple Fusarium species. The engineered strain exhibited enhanced rhizosphere colonization, promoted plant growth and conferred improved protection against tomato Fusarium wilt, restoring plant height to levels approaching healthy controls. We further demonstrated the modularity of this platform by successfully transferring it into Pseudomonas koreensis D26, a strain known for its plant growth‐promoting properties, indicating broad applicability across biocontrol‐relevant pseudomonads. This work establishes a versatile T3SS‐based delivery platform for precision biocontrol, offering a generalizable strategy for engineering beneficial rhizobacteria.Keywords: antifungal effector, biological control, Fusarium wilt, protein delivery, Pseudomonas protegens Pf‐5, type III secretion system (T3SS)

Introduction

Plant fungal diseases, particularly those caused by soil‐borne pathogens like Fusarium oxysporum, pose a severe threat to global agricultural productivity and food security (Gordon 2017; Wang et al. 2024). The use of plant growth‐promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) as biocontrol agents has emerged as a sustainable alternative to chemical fungicides (Lugtenberg and Kamilova 2009; Taheri et al. 2025). Among these, Pseudomonas protegens Pf‐5 is a well‐characterized biocontrol strain known for its ability to produce antifungal secondary metabolites and to induce systemic resistance in plants, with its regulatory networks extensively studied in the context of rhizosphere colonization (Heeb et al. 2005; Ramette et al. 2011; Loper et al. 2012; Takeuchi et al. 2023; Dobrzyński and Jakubowska 2025; Takeuchi and Seo 2025). Previous engineering efforts have further demonstrated that P. protegens Pf‐5 can be genetically modified to enhance agriculturally relevant functions, including antifungal activity and nitrogen fixation (Jing et al. 2020). However, like many other fluorescent pseudomonads, Pf‐5 lacks a type III secretion system (T3SS)—a specialised molecular apparatus that enables bacteria to directly deliver effector proteins into the cytoplasm of eukaryotic host cells (Diepold and Armitage 2015; Kuhlen et al. 2018). This absence limits the potential of Pf‐5 to deploy proteinaceous effectors as precise weapons against fungal pathogens.

The T3SS, often described as a molecular syringe, is a complex nanomachine commonly found in Gram‐negative bacterial pathogens, where it plays a pivotal role in establishing infection by translocating virulence effectors (Li et al. 2023). Recent advances in synthetic biology have demonstrated that entire T3SS gene clusters can be heterologously expressed in naive bacterial hosts, thereby conferring upon them the ability to secrete and deliver effector proteins (Widmaier et al. 2009; Huang et al. 2025; Yi et al. 2025). For instance, the 32‐kb T3SS gene cluster from Photorhabdus luminescens TT01 has been successfully transferred into a T3SS‐deficient Xenorhabdus strain, enabling enhanced insecticidal activity. However, the functional transfer of an entomopathogenic T3SS into plant‐beneficial pseudomonads for targeting fungal pathogens has remained largely unexplored. The activity of T3SS is tightly controlled by environmental cues, including calcium concentration, temperature and pH (Yadav et al. 2020; Pratama et al. 2023; Huang et al. 2024, 2025). Understanding these regulatory parameters is crucial for optimizing engineered T3SS platforms.

Beyond the T3SS itself, the choice of effector proteins determines the functional output of the delivery system. Recent studies have uncovered a wealth of antifungal effectors deployed by various bacterial secretion systems, and bioinformatics tools have accelerated the prediction of such effectors from genomic data. Particularly relevant is the prophage tail‐like protein Bg9562 from Burkholderia spp., which exhibits potent antifungal activity, facilitates endophytic colonization, triggers plant immunity in tomato, and is regulated by calcium (Yadav et al. 2020; Chandan et al. 2023). Pfam analysis revealed that Bg9562 and its orthologs harbour a conserved phage_TAC_7 superfamily domain (Pfam: PF10109; InterPro: IPR019289), generally associated with bacteriophage tail protein Gp41, consistent with a membrane‐penetrating antifungal mechanism (Swain et al. 2017). These properties make it an ideal candidate for loading onto a heterologous T3SS delivery platform. The broader exploration of bacterial effector biology has been greatly facilitated by computational approaches. For example, the machine‐learning tool Effectidor II enables pan‐genomic prediction of type III secretion system effectors, thereby expanding the repertoire of potential antifungal cargoes. Meanwhile, studies on other secretion systems have revealed diverse effector functions: type IVA secretion system effectors have been implicated in quorum quenching (Liao et al. 2023), while effectors delivered by the type VI secretion system can bind lipopolysaccharide and recruit outer membrane vesicles to mediate exploitative competition and horizontal gene transfer. Furthermore, effector‐mediated transcriptional rewiring has been shown to resolve interbacterial conflict through conserved c‐di‐GMP antagonism (Xie et al. 2025). Together, these advances highlight the vast functional diversity of bacterial effectors and support the rationale for engineering the T3SS to deliver proteins such as Bg9562 for targeted biocontrol applications.

Bacterial–fungal interactions are mediated by both chemical mediators and protein effectors (Leveau and Preston 2008; Kobayashi and Crouch 2009; Scherlach and Hertweck 2020). In this study, we aimed to engineer a protein delivery platform in the biocontrol agent P. protegens Pf‐5 by heterologously expressing the entire T3SS gene cluster from P. luminescens TT01. We further optimized the system by deleting a prophage‐associated lysis cassette and characterized its functionality using the model effector LopT (Huang et al. 2025). Subsequently, we harnessed this platform to deliver Bg9562 directly into F. oxysporum hyphae, thereby enhancing biocontrol efficacy against tomato Fusarium wilt. We also extended the platform to Pseudomonas koreensis D26, a species with documented biocontrol potential (Wang et al. 2025), and assessed whether the heterologous T3SS platform could be functionally reconstituted in a second biocontrol‐relevant Pseudomonas background. To our knowledge, this is the first study to functionally transfer a heterologous T3SS into plant‐beneficial pseudomonads for direct delivery of antifungal effectors into fungal pathogens, establishing a versatile T3SS‐based delivery system and providing a new paradigm for developing next‐generation biocontrol agents through synthetic biology.

Materials and Methods

Strains, Plasmids and Culture Conditions

Bacterial strains and plasmids are listed in Tables S1 and S2. For routine small‐volume cultures used for plasmid construction, strain verification and molecular assays, Pseudomonas protegens Pf‐5, Pseudomonas koreensis D26 (Kwon et al. 2003), and their derivatives were cultured in LB medium (1% tryptone, 0.5% yeast extract, 1% NaCl) at 30°C in 1.5‐mL tubes using an Eppendorf ThermoMixer F1.5 at 800 rpm. Escherichia coli strains DH5α, MFDpir and HGB283 were cultured under the same conditions at 37°C. For preparation of bacterial inocula used in rhizosphere colonization and greenhouse experiments, cultures were grown in flasks at 180 rpm using a ZQZY‐CS8V shaking incubator (Shanghai Zhichu Instrument Co. Ltd.). Pseudomonas strains were cultured at 30°C, whereas Escherichia coli strains were cultured at 37°C. Diaminopimelic acid (DAP) was added at 30 μg/mL for E. coli MFDpir.

Fusarium oxysporum and Saprolegnia sp. were cultured on PDA medium (1.2% potato extract, 2% glucose, 1.8% agar) at 28 C with shaking at 180 rpm for liquid cultures.

Construction of Recombinant Plasmids and Strains

Conjugal Transfer of T3SS Gene Cluster

The conjugative plasmid pEVS107‐T3SS (harbouring the Photorhabdus luminescens TT01 T3SS cluster) (Bai et al. 2018; Virolle et al. 2020) and its derivative pEVS107‐T3SSΔphage (with the phage lysis cassette deleted) were introduced into P. protegens Pf‐5 or P. koreensis D26 via triparental conjugation using E. coli MFDpir (donor) and HGB283 (helper) (Datsenko and Wanner 2000). Transconjugants were selected on LB with appropriate antibiotics and verified by colony PCR of three T3SS segments and 16S rDNA.

Effector Expression Vectors

Effector genes were cloned into pBBR1MCS2, a medium‐copy‐number broad‐host‐range plasmid (~20–30 copies/cell), with FLAG or EGFP tags. For secretion assays, lopT was fused to a C‐terminal 3 × FLAG tag under the control of the constitutive Tac promoter, generating pBBR1‐LopT‐FLAG. The construct was introduced into Pf‐5 and Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage by electroporation (1250 V) and selected on LB agar containing gentamicin. Other effector genes (hopJ, bg9562, sachib, nhk5, n7u7) were similarly cloned into pBBR1MCS2 derivatives. A co‐expression vector fusing bg9562 with the LopT signal peptide and SlcT chaperone was synthesized (Tsingke). All constructs were verified by PCR, sequencing and electroporated into recipient strains. Engineered strains are listed in Table S1.

Deletion of the Lysis Cassette

The phage lysis cassette (plu3747–plu3750) was deleted using Red/ET recombination (Zhang et al. 1998). The chloramphenicol resistance cassette was amplified and recombined into pEVS107‐T3SS in E. coli ECD100pir expressing Red/ET recombinases, generating pEVS107‐T3SSΔphage.

In Vitro T3SS Induction and Protein Analysis

Induction by Ca2+ Chelation

To evaluate the functionality and regulatory features of the heterologous T3SS, overnight bacterial cultures were inoculated (5%) into BHI medium with 0.2–2 mmol/L CaCl₂ and grown at 30°C for 3 h (Horsman et al. 2012). EGTA was added to 5 mmol/L to chelate Ca2+, and cultures were shifted to 37°C for 2–3 h. Supernatants were filtered (0.22 μm) and proteins precipitated with 1/6 volume 100% TCA overnight at 4°C. Pellets were washed with acetone, dissolved in PBS, and stored at −80°C.

Western Blot

Proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked with 5% non‐fat milk in TBST, and incubated with primary antibodies (anti‐FLAG, anti‐GFP, anti‐RNA polymerase, 1:2000) overnight at 4°C, followed by fluorescence‐conjugated secondary antibodies (1:2000). Signals were detected using an Odyssey system.

qRT‐PCR Analysis of T3SS Gene Expression

To assess transcriptional activation of T3SS‐related genes under inducing conditions, Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐LopT was cultured as described above. Following EGTA induction, bacterial cells were collected at 0, 0.5, 1, 2 and 3 h. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, and genomic DNA was removed. RNA (2 μg) was reverse‐transcribed into cDNA using a qPCR reverse transcription kit. qPCR was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR PCR Master Mix on an ABI 7500 system. The target genes included sctW (gatekeeper), sctV, htpG (chaperone) and hslU (ATPase subunit), with 16S rRNA as the internal reference. Relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with the 0 h time point serving as the calibrator. Each reaction was performed in triplicate.

Bacterial and Fungal Assays

Growth Curve and Morphology of P. protegens Pf‐5 and Its Derivatives

To assess whether introduction of the heterologous T3SS imposes a fitness burden on Pf‐5, single colonies of Pf‐5, Pf‐5‐T3SS and Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage were inoculated into LB liquid medium and cultured overnight at 30°C for 12 h. Cultures were diluted to the same OD600 and inoculated into fresh LB medium at a 1% (v/v) ratio. OD600 was measured every 2 h over a 36 h period.

For morphological observation, single colonies were inoculated into LB liquid medium and cultured at 30°C for 12 h. Bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation at 8500 rpm for 5 min, washed 15 times with sterile distilled water (8000 rpm, 2 min each), and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4°C overnight in darkness. After fixation, cells were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min, the fixative was discarded, and the pellet was dehydrated sequentially with 30%, 50% and 70% ethanol (1 min each). The dehydrated cells were resuspended in absolute ethanol, and morphology was examined by cold field emission scanning electron microscopy (Hitachi Su8010) after gold sputtering.

Growth Curve and Morphology of P. koreensis D26 and Its Derivatives

To assess whether T3SS introduction affects the growth and morphology of D26, single colonies of D26 and D26‐T3SS were inoculated into LB liquid medium (with appropriate antibiotics) and cultured at 30°C for 12 h. Cultures were diluted to the same OD600 and inoculated into fresh LB medium at a 1% (v/v) ratio. OD600 was measured every 2 h over a 24 h period.

For morphological observation, cells from overnight cultures were collected by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 5 min, washed 1–2 times with sterile water, and resuspended in 10 μL sterile water for preliminary observation under a light microscope. For scanning electron microscopy, cells were collected by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 5 min, washed 15 times with PBS (9000 rpm, 2 min each), and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4°C overnight in darkness. Fixed cells were centrifuged and dehydrated sequentially with a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, 95% and 100%; 1 min per concentration, 8000 rpm, 2 min). The dehydrated cells were resuspended in absolute ethanol, and 10 μL of the suspension was placed on a glass slide for cold field emission scanning electron microscopy observation after gold sputtering.

For observation of the T3SS needle structure, a single colony of D26‐T3SS was inoculated into BHI medium containing erythromycin (50 μg/mL) and cultured overnight at 30°C. The overnight culture was diluted 1:20 into fresh BHI medium containing appropriate antibiotics supplemented with CaCl₂ to a final concentration of 0.2 mmol/L and incubated at 30°C for 1.5 h to prime T3SS expression. EGTA was then added to a final concentration of 5 mmol/L to chelate Ca2+, and the culture was shifted to 37°C for an additional 1.5 h to induce secretion. Induced cells were collected, washed 2–3 times with PBS, and sent to Saiwei Biotechnology for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging.

Antifungal Activity

To evaluate the antifungal efficacy of T3SS‐delivered Bg9562, for plate inhibition, induced bacterial cultures (108 CFU/mL) were added to wells in PDA plates pre‐inoculated with fungal spores.

Fluorescence Microscopy

To visualize T3SS‐mediated effector translocation into fungal cells, for effector translocation, bacterial strains expressing EGFP fusions were co‐incubated with fungal hyphae in PDB for 24 h and observed under a fluorescence microscope (Khang et al. 2010). For rhizosphere colonization, mCherry‐labelled bacteria were inoculated onto seedling roots; after 48 h, roots were washed and imaged by confocal microscopy.

Microscopic Observation of Hyphal Morphology

To assess the impact of T3SS‐delivered Bg9562 on fungal cellular integrity, original microscopic images of F. oxysporum hyphae co‐cultured with Pf‐5‐Bg9562‐EGFP or Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562‐EGFP from three independent experiments were visually examined. Hyphae exposed to Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562‐EGFP consistently exhibited pronounced morphological abnormalities, including vacuolisation, cytoplasmic contraction, and discontinuous cytoplasmic organization, whereas hyphae exposed to the T3SS‐deficient control strain Pf‐5‐Bg9562‐EGFP largely retained normal morphology. Representative unedited micrographs are provided in Figure S5.

Proteomics Analysis

To assess global changes in the extracellular protein profile associated with T3SS introduction, extracellular proteins from induced cultures were precipitated with TCA and analysed by LC–MS/MS. Proteins with fold change ≥ 1.2 and *p < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed (Ye et al. 2019). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using standard bioinformatics tools.

Plant Experiments

Greenhouse Biocontrol and Growth Promotion (Zhang et al. 2024). Biocontrol Assay

To assess the protective efficacy of engineered strains against Fusarium wilt, tomato seeds (Solanum lycopersicum) were surface sterilized with 2% sodium hypochlorite for 5 min, rinsed three times with sterile distilled water, and germinated on moist filter paper in Petri dishes at 28°C in darkness for 5–7 days. Seedlings at the three‐leaf stage were transplanted into plastic pots (diameter 12 cm, height 10 cm) containing sterile potting soil composed of peat moss, vermiculite and perlite (3:1:1, v/v/v), autoclaved twice at 121°C for 30 min with a 24 h interval. Plants were divided into five groups: (1) uninoculated control (Control), (2) F. oxysporum alone (FO), (3) FO + Pf‐5, (4) FO + Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage (+T3SS) and (5) FO + Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562 (+T3SS‐Bg9562). Bacterial treatment was applied twice by soil drenching with 10 mL of bacterial suspension (108 CFU/mL, prepared from overnight cultures grown with appropriate antibiotics to maintain plasmid selection, then washed twice and resuspended in sterile PBS) prior to pathogen inoculation. No antibiotics were added to the soil to avoid introducing confounding variables into the rhizosphere environment and to maintain experimental consistency across all treatment groups. For pathogen inoculation, F. oxysporum spore suspension was prepared from 7‐day‐old PDA cultures by flooding with sterile water, filtering through four layers of sterile cheesecloth, and adjusting the concentration to 106 spores/mL using a haemocytometer. Roots were gently wounded with a sterile scalpel and immersed in the spore suspension for 30 min before transplanting. After transplanting, a third bacterial treatment (10 mL, 108 CFU/mL) was applied by soil drenching.

Plants were maintained in a controlled greenhouse at 25°C–28°C under continuous light, with 60%–70% relative humidity, and watered with sterile water every 2–3 days. Plant height (measured from the soil surface to the apical meristem), fresh weight, root length and rhizosphere pH were measured at 21 days post‐inoculation. Disease severity was evaluated by measuring plant height as the primary quantitative indicator, as preliminary experiments established that height reduction is the most consistent and objective symptom of Fusarium wilt in tomato seedlings. The experiment was performed three times with 3, 6 and 9 biological replicates per treatment, respectively. All three independent experiments were conducted under the same controlled greenhouse conditions to minimize environmental variation. Data from the third experiment (n = 9) are presented as a representative replicate, and pooled analysis of all three experiments is provided in Figure S6.

Growth Promotion Assay

To evaluate the plant growth‐promoting effects of engineered strains under pathogen‐free conditions, tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) seeds were surface sterilized and germinated as described above. Seedlings were inoculated by soil drenching with 10 mL of bacterial suspension (108 CFU/mL) of Pf‐5, Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage, Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562, or sterile water (Control). Plants were grown in sterile potting soil (peat moss: vermiculite: perlite = 3:1:1, autoclaved twice) under controlled conditions at 25°C with a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod and 60%–70% relative humidity. Root length was measured at 14 days post‐inoculation. Sample sizes were n = 17, 18, 20 and 19 for Control, Pf‐5, Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage and Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562, respectively.

Rhizosphere Colonization Dynamics (Liu et al. 2024)

To monitor the persistence and colonization dynamics of engineered strains in the plant rhizosphere, tobacco seedlings were treated by soil drenching with 10 mL of bacterial suspension (108 CFU/mL). Roots were sampled at 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 14 and 16 days post‐inoculation. At each time point, whole root systems were carefully excised, gently shaken to remove loosely adhering soil, and weighed. Roots were then placed in sterile PBS (10 mL per gram of fresh root) and vortexed vigorously for 2 min to dislodge rhizosphere bacteria. Serial dilutions of the resulting suspension were plated on LB agar containing appropriate antibiotics and incubated at 30°C for 48 h before colony‐forming units (CFU) were counted. Colonization density was expressed as CFU per gram of fresh root tissue.

Pathogen Inoculation

Tomato roots were wounded and immersed in F. oxysporum spore suspension (106 spores/mL) for 30 min, then transplanted into soil. Disease severity was assessed 21 days later by measuring plant height, fresh weight and root length.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates. Data are presented as mean ± SD for measurements of individual biological replicates (e.g., cell length, plant height per replicate), and as mean ± SEM when data were pooled across independent experiments (as specified in each figure legend). Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.2). For multiple group comparisons (e.g., plant height, fresh weight, root length, disease index), one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test was used. The third independent biocontrol experiment (n = 9 per treatment) served as the representative dataset for main figures, and compact letter display (a, b, c…) was used to indicate significant groupings at p < 0.05. For pairwise comparisons between specific treatment groups (e.g., colonization density differences between engineered and control strains at individual time points), Student's t‐test with Bonferroni correction was applied. Significance levels were defined as: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001; ns, not significant (p > 0.05).

Results

Establishment and Functional Validation of a Heterologous T3SS Platform in Pseudomonas protogens Pf‐5

To confer targeted protein‐delivery capacity on the biocontrol strain Pseudomonas protegens Pf‐5, the complete type III secretion system (T3SS) gene cluster from Photorhabdus luminescens TT01 was introduced into Pf‐5 by triparental mating, generating Pf‐5‐T3SS. Because the intact Pf‐5‐T3SS construct retained a prophage‐associated lytic cassette and showed instability under inducing conditions in preliminary assays, this cassette was deleted by homologous recombination to generate the optimized Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage strain. Therefore, subsequent functional assays were performed using the Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage background unless otherwise indicated.

Under non‐inducing conditions (LB medium, 30°C), introduction of the heterologous T3SS imposed only a minor fitness burden on Pf‐5. Growth‐curve analysis showed that Pf‐5‐T3SS and Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage displayed growth dynamics comparable to those of wild‐type Pf‐5 (Figure S1), although both recombinant strains showed a slight delay of approximately 2 h before entering stationary phase. Quantification of the maximum specific growth rate and final colony‐forming units after 24 h revealed no significant differences among the three strains (one‐way ANOVA, *p > 0.05), indicating that the heterologous T3SS was stably maintained without markedly compromising bacterial proliferation under vegetative growth conditions. Given that T3SS assembly occurs constitutively but secretion requires induction, these results primarily reflect the cost of maintaining the membrane‐spanning apparatus, not the additional energetic demand of active protein translocation.

Cold‐field scanning electron microscopy revealed that T3SS introduction was associated with a pronounced alteration in cell morphology. Wild‐type Pf‐5 cells appeared as typical short rods with an average length of 3.25 ± 0.21 μm (mean ± SD, n = 50). In contrast, both recombinant strains exhibited elongated morphologies. The average cell length increased to 5.00 ± 0.35 μm in Pf‐5‐T3SS, representing a 53.8% increase relative to the wild type (p < 0.001), and further increased to 5.87 ± 0.42 μm in Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage, corresponding to an 80.6% increase (p < 0.001) (Figure S1B). These results suggest that the introduced secretion machinery remodels cell morphology while exerting minimal effects on overall growth.

To determine whether the introduced T3SS was expressed and functionally assembled, extracellular proteins from Pf‐5 and Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage were analysed by comparative secretome proteomics. In total, 121 extracellular proteins were identified (Figure 1A; Tables S3 and S4), among which 105 proteins were shared by both strains. Relative to wild‐type Pf‐5, Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage exhibited 16 newly detected extracellular proteins. Among the 105 shared proteins, 7 were significantly upregulated (fold change ≥ 1.2 or ≤ 1/1.2, *p < 0.05). Thus, a total of 23 differentially expressed proteins were identified in the engineered strain. Signal‐peptide prediction further identified PyrG, which contained both a signal peptide and a transmembrane domain, and CarB, which contained a signal peptide alone, as candidate secretion‐associated proteins. Functional enrichment analysis indicated that proteins enriched in the Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage secretome were associated with biological processes including de novo CTP biosynthesis, protein folding and phosphorylation (p < 0.01), whereas enriched molecular functions included ATP binding and metal ion binding. KEGG analysis showed significant enrichment in pyrimidine metabolism, purine metabolism, ansamycin biosynthesis and multiple amino acid metabolism pathways (q < 0.05) (Figure 1B). Together, these data indicate that the introduction of the T3SS reshaped the extracellular protein profile of Pf‐5.

The secretion competence and regulatory features of the engineered system were then examined using FLAG‐tagged LopT as a model effector. As described above, the lopT‐3 × FLAG construct was expressed in both Pf‐5 (Pf‐5‐LopT) and Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage (Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐LopT). Western blotting confirmed intracellular expression of LopT in both strains (Figure S2A). Under low‐calcium induction with EGTA, LopT was specifically detected in the culture supernatant of Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐LopT but not in the supernatants of Pf‐5‐LopT or uninduced controls. RNA polymerase was not detected in any supernatant fraction (Figure 1C), excluding nonspecific protein release caused by cell lysis. These results demonstrate that the heterologous T3SS in Pf‐5 is functional and mediates regulated effector secretion. Secretion was strictly dependent on EGTA induction: in medium containing Ca2+, LopT was detected in the supernatant only after EGTA‐mediated Ca2+ chelation, whereas the uninduced control showed no detectable secretion (Figure 1C). This recapitulates the native regulatory logic of the P. luminescens T3SS, in which EGTA‐triggered calcium depletion serves as the activation signal for effector export. Such tight environmental control provides a desirable ‘sense‐and‐respond’ capability that is ideal for spatially and temporally controlled biocontrol applications.

Further optimisation showed that LopT secretion was tightly dependent on calcium availability and reached its highest level when the medium was supplemented with 2 mmol/L Ca2+ prior to chelation with 5 mmol/L EGTA (Figure 1C). Efficient secretion occurred at 37°C (Figure 1D). Time‐course analysis showed that secretion peaked at 2 h after induction (Figure 1E). Secretion was favoured by slightly alkaline conditions, with the strongest signal observed at pH 8.0–9.0 (Figure 1F). To further assess the substrate specificity of the heterologous T3SS, we tested whether the Pf‐5 endogenous protein HopJ—annotated as a putative T3SS effector in the Pf‐5 genome but not secreted under our assay conditions—could be recognized and secreted by the introduced P. luminescens system. HopJ was expressed intracellularly in both Pf‐5 and Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage, but was not detected in the supernatant of either strain following EGTA induction. These results demonstrate that the heterologous T3SS does not promiscuously secrete non‐cognate proteins, confirming that effector export is governed by specific secretion signals recognized by the P. luminescens T3SS sorting platform (Figure S2C). To confirm that the heterologous T3SS is transcriptionally active, we monitored the expression of key T3SS structural and regulatory genes by qRT‐PCR following EGTA induction. The gatekeeper protein gene sctW, which controls the ordered secretion of T3SS substrates, was rapidly upregulated by approximately 7.6‐fold at 0.5 h post‐induction relative to the uninduced control (0 h). Transcript levels of the ATP‐dependent protease ATPase subunit gene hslU and the chaperone gene htpG increased by approximately 6.0‐ and 3.5‐fold, respectively, at 3 h post‐induction (Figure S3). These results demonstrate that the core T3SS operon is transcriptionally responsive to low‐calcium/EGTA induction, providing molecular evidence that the introduced secretion system is actively expressed under secretion‐permissive conditions.

Targeted Delivery and Functional Validation of the Antifungal Effector Bg9562

After establishing a functional heterologous T3SS platform in Pf‐5, we next evaluated whether this system could be used to deliver an antifungal effector into phytopathogenic fungi. To construct the Bg9562 delivery vector, the bg9562 coding sequence was fused to the N‐terminal 150‐bp region of lopT (encoding the T3SS secretion signal) by homologous recombination, replacing the remainder of the lopT open reading frame. The fusion construct was co‐expressed with the cognate chaperone SlcT from the same plasmid to ensure proper recognition and secretion. Unlike the native effector LopT, whose N‐terminal secretion signal is naturally recognized by the T3SS sorting platform, Bg9562 is a heterologous protein that requires both the LopT signal peptide and its chaperone SlcT for efficient engagement with the secretion machinery. For secretion validation of the native effector, the lopT‐3 × FLAG reporter was expressed from pBBR1MCS2, a medium‐copy‐number broad‐host‐range plasmid (~20–30 copies/cell), under the control of the constitutive Tac promoter. The resulting Bg9562 plasmid was introduced into Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage, generating Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562. Under low‐calcium induction with 5 mmol/L EGTA, Western blotting detected Bg9562 in the culture supernatant of Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562, but not in that of the T3SS‐deficient control strain Pf‐5‐Bg9562 (Figure S2B). This indicates that Bg9562 fused to the LopT signal peptide is recognized and secreted as a bona fide substrate of the heterologous T3SS.

The antifungal activity conferred by T3SS‐mediated Bg9562 secretion was evaluated against three agriculturally important Fusarium species: Fusarium oxysporum, Fusarium proliferatum and Fusarium fujikuroi. Before co‐cultivation, Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562 was induced under low‐calcium conditions for 3 h to activate T3SS secretion. After 72 h of co‐cultivation on PDA plates, Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562 produced clear inhibition zones of 24.8 ± 2.1 mm, 22.6 ± 1.8 mm and 18.9 ± 1.7 mm against F. oxysporum, F. proliferatum and F. fujikuroi, respectively. In contrast, neither the T3SS‐only strain Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage nor wild‐type Pf‐5 produced detectable inhibition under the same conditions (Figure 2A and Figure S4). These results indicate that the antifungal phenotype depends on both the heterologous T3SS and the delivered Bg9562 effector.

Microscopic observation further supported the antifungal activity of T3SS‐delivered Bg9562. Hyphae of F. oxysporum co‐cultured with Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562‐EGFP showed pronounced morphological abnormalities, including vacuolisation, cytoplasmic contraction and discontinuous cytoplasmic organization. In contrast, hyphae exposed to the T3SS‐deficient control strain Pf‐5‐Bg9562‐EGFP largely retained normal morphology (Figure 2B). Visual comparison of the original microscopic images showed more frequent and more obvious hyphal abnormalities in the Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562‐EGFP treatment than in the Pf‐5‐Bg9562‐EGFP control. Additional unedited representative raw micrographs are provided in Figure S5. These results support that T3SS‐mediated delivery of Bg9562 disrupts fungal cellular integrity.

The biochemical robustness of the Bg9562‐containing supernatant was then assessed under different temperature and pH conditions. Antifungal activity was retained after exposure to a broad temperature range from 15°C to 60°C and across pH 5.0–10.0, with activity maintained under neutral to alkaline conditions (Figure 2C). This stability profile suggests that Bg9562 has favourable properties for potential application in variable rhizosphere environments.

To directly visualize effector translocation into fungal cells, two reporter strains were constructed: one expressing LopT‐EGFP and the other expressing Bg9562‐EGFP. Each strain was co‐cultivated with fungal or oomycete targets. Following low‐calcium induction and 24 h of co‐incubation, strong green fluorescence was observed within the hyphae of Saprolegnia sp. and F. oxysporum when these organisms were co‐cultured with Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐LopT‐EGFP. No fluorescence was detected in hyphae exposed to the corresponding T3SS‐deficient control strain. Similarly, Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562‐EGFP delivered detectable fluorescence into the hyphae of all four tested targets, including Saprolegnia sp., F. oxysporum, F. proliferatum and F. fujikuroi, whereas no fluorescence was observed in any T3SS‐deficient control treatment (Figure 3). These results provide fluorescence‐based evidence that the engineered T3SS can mediate effector delivery into fungal and oomycete cells.

T3SS Engineering Enhances Rhizosphere Colonization, Plant Growth Promotion and Biocontrol Efficacy Against Fusarium Wilt

The performance of the engineered strain in the plant rhizosphere was next examined using tobacco seedlings. Root‐associated bacterial populations were quantified over a 16 day period after inoculation. Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562 consistently exhibited higher rhizosphere colonization than the T3SS‐deficient control strain Pf‐5‐Bg9562 throughout the observation period, with significant differences observed at 2, 4, 6 and 8 days post‐inoculation (*p < 0.05, Student's t‐test with Bonferroni correction) (Figure 4A). At early time points, particularly 2–4 days post‐inoculation, Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562 reached densities above 106 CFU/g of root tissue, approximately one order of magnitude higher than those of the control strain. Although bacterial populations gradually declined over time in both treatments, Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562 maintained higher densities of 104–105 CFU/g through 16 days post‐inoculation, whereas Pf‐5‐Bg9562 declined below 104 CFU/g after day 14 (Figure 4A).

Statistical and trajectory‐based analyses confirmed the improved colonization capacity of the engineered strain. Boxplot analysis showed a higher overall median colonization level for Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562 than for Pf‐5‐Bg9562 (Figure 4B). Individual sample trajectories further revealed that the engineered strain achieved higher peak colonization densities, reaching up to 106 CFU/g, and remained detectable throughout the 16 day monitoring period (Figure 4C). Hierarchical clustering based on colonization abundance separated the engineered strain from the control and showed consistently stronger root‐associated persistence across most time points (Figure 4D). Fluorescence microscopy at 48 h post‐inoculation revealed dense colonization of tobacco root surfaces by Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562, particularly around root hair zones and epidermal cell junctions (Figure 4E). A similar enhancement of rhizosphere colonization was also observed in tomato seedlings (Figure S7, left and right panels), indicating that T3SS engineering promotes root colonization in more than one plant host.

In addition to improving colonization, the engineered strain promoted plant growth under pathogen‐free conditions. Tobacco seedlings inoculated with Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562 developed visibly more extensive root systems than uninoculated controls and wild‐type Pf‐5‐treated plants (Figure 5A,D). Under the tested pathogen‐free conditions, strains carrying the heterologous T3SS showed increased root elongation, with the Bg9562‐expressing strain showing the strongest effect. The mean root length of seedlings treated with Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562 reached 12.4 ± 1.1 cm, representing a 30% increase relative to the untreated control group (9.3 ± 0.8 cm, p < 0.001). Because the T3SS‐only strain lacks an antifungal effector cargo, this phenotype should be interpreted as an association under the tested conditions rather than evidence that the injectisome itself directly stimulates plant growth.

The protective efficacy of Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562 was then assessed in greenhouse assays against F. oxysporum. Three independent experiments were conducted with increasing sample sizes of 3, 6 and 9 plants per treatment. Data from the third experiment are presented as a representative replicate, and pooled analysis of all three experiments is provided in Figure S6A–D. Infection with F. oxysporum severely inhibited plant growth, reducing plant height by approximately 70% relative to healthy controls (2.78 ± 0.28 cm vs. 9.17 ± 1.33 cm, p < 0.001). Co‐inoculation with wild‐type Pf‐5 provided partial but statistically limited protection (3.56 ± 0.41 cm, p = 0.082 versus pathogen‐only treatment). The T3SS‐only strain showed a similar level of protection to wild‐type Pf‐5 (2.44 ± 0.81 cm vs. 3.56 ± 0.41 cm, p > 0.05). In contrast, Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562 substantially alleviated disease symptoms and restored plant growth, producing a mean plant height of 6.44 ± 1.21 cm, which was significantly greater than the pathogen‐only treatment (p < 0.01, Tukey's HSD). Notably, plant height in the Bg9562‐treated group did not differ significantly from that of the uninfected healthy controls (*p = 0.063), suggesting a trend toward growth recovery that approached the level of healthy plants, although the difference was marginally above the conventional significance threshold.

Pooled analysis across the three independent experiments confirmed the reproducibility of this protective effect. Plant‐height distributions pooled across all experiments are shown in Figure S6A, and experiment‐wise distributions, mean plant heights, and treatment‐level comparisons are shown in Figure S6B–D. The Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562 treatment consistently ranked as the most effective treatment and restored plant height to 76% of the healthy control level. Fresh weight and root length showed substantial recovery following treatment with Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562, reaching 79.8% and 98.9%, respectively, relative to healthy controls. Plant height was restored to 70.3% of the control level (Figure 5E). Soil pH remained stable across all treatments, ranging from 5.11 to 5.56, indicating that disease suppression was not attributable to rhizosphere acidification (Figure 5B,E and Figure S6). Visual inspection of root systems further showed that Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562‐treated plants developed extensive, well‐branched roots comparable to those of healthy controls, whereas pathogen‐infected plants displayed severe root reduction and browning (Figure 5B). Longitudinal dissection of roots further showed that Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562‐treated plants retained relatively intact internal root tissues, whereas pathogen‐infected roots showed extensive browning and necrotic symptoms (Figure 5C). Together, these results demonstrate that T3SS‐mediated delivery of Bg9562 markedly enhances the biocontrol efficacy of Pf‐5 against Fusarium wilt.

Extension of the Heterologous T3SS Platform to Pseudomonas koreensis D26

To evaluate whether the heterologous T3SS platform could be transferred to another biocontrol‐relevant Pseudomonas background, the same T3SS gene cluster was introduced into Pseudomonas koreensis D26, generating D26‐T3SS. Unlike Pf‐5‐derived strains, Live/Dead staining using SYTO 9 and propidium iodide showed that D26‐T3SS had fewer detectable intact cells than wild‐type D26 under control conditions (Figure S8, left panels), and this reduction remained evident after low‐Ca2+/EGTA induction (Figure S8, right panels), consistent with cell lysis or loss of intact bacterial cells. To improve strain stability, the prophage‐associated lytic cassette plu3747–plu3750 was deleted, generating the optimized strain D26‐T3SSΔphage, which was used for all subsequent analyses.

Two key differences between the Pf‐5 and D26 backgrounds were observed. First, whereas Pf‐5 cells became significantly elongated upon T3SS introduction, D26‐T3SS retained a typical short‐rod morphology indistinguishable from wild‐type D26 (Figure S1C), suggesting species‐specific responses to the heterologous injectisome. Second, comparative secretome proteomics revealed that, although the heterologous T3SS also remodelled the extracellular protein landscape of D26 (Figure 6A–C; Tables S5–S7), the affected pathways differed markedly from those in Pf‐5. While Pf‐5 showed enrichment in nucleotide and amino acid metabolism, D26 exhibited enrichment in central carbon metabolism, including glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism and the tricarboxylic acid cycle (Figure 6C).

Expression of LopT in D26‐LopT and D26‐T3SSΔphage‐LopT was first verified by whole‐cell immunoblotting (Figure S2D). The functionality of the optimized D26‐T3SSΔphage platform was then confirmed by regulated LopT secretion under different induction conditions (Figure 6D–G), with growth kinetics comparable to those of the parental D26 background (Figure S1D). These results support the transferability of the heterologous T3SS platform across Pseudomonas species, but that strain‐specific optimization—such as deletion of the lytic cassette—is essential for functional reconstitution.

Discussion

In this study, we successfully engineered a functional heterologous T3SS in the plant‐beneficial bacterium P. protegens Pf‐5, transforming it into a targeted protein delivery vehicle capable of translocating antifungal effectors directly into fungal pathogens. This work represents a significant advance in the synthetic biology of biocontrol agents and aligns with the growing trend of repurposing bacterial secretion systems for biotechnological applications (Widmaier et al. 2009; Huang et al. 2025; Yi et al. 2025).

Functional assembly was corroborated at the molecular level by proteomic identification of T3SS structural components and the tightly regulated, Ca2+‐dependent secretion of the model effector LopT. This strict calcium dependency recapitulates the canonical regulatory patterns of native T3S (Yadav et al. 2020; Pratama et al. 2023), but critically, it endows the engineered strain with a desirable ‘sense‐and‐respond’ strategy that is absent from conventional biocontrol systems. Under the calcium‐replete conditions typical of bulk soil and the rhizosphere, the secretion machinery remains quiescent, minimizing metabolic burden and off‐target effects; activation occurs only upon sensing low‐calcium microenvironments, such as those encountered at the pathogen–host interface. The additional regulation by temperature and pH further highlights the importance of environmental tuning for optimal in vivo performance (Yi et al. 2025). Moreover, the failure of the endogenous Pf‐5 effector HopJ to be secreted demonstrates that the heterologous T3SS retains stringent substrate selectivity, a crucial safety feature that likely reflects the strict requirement for cognate chaperones and specific N‐terminal secretion signals (Huang et al. 2025). Together, these properties establish Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage as a low‐leakage, conditionally activated delivery platform that addresses a longstanding bottleneck in biocontrol—namely, the inability to deliver proteinaceous effectors in a spatially and temporally controlled manner. It is worth noting that the comparative secretome analyses, while informative for assessing the global physiological impact of T3SS introduction, do not constitute direct evidence for T3SS needle assembly. The differentially detected extracellular proteins may originate from indirect effects such as altered outer membrane vesicle (OMV) biogenesis or envelope stress responses accompanying injectisome assembly (Kulp and Kuehn 2010). Nevertheless, qRT‐PCR analysis confirmed that key T3SS structural and regulatory genes—including sctW (gatekeeper, ~7.6‐fold), hslU (ATPase, ~6.0‐fold) and htpG (chaperone, ~3.5‐fold)—are significantly upregulated under inducing conditions (Figure S3), providing molecular evidence that the T3SS is actively expressed. Future studies employing fluorescence microscopy with GFP‐tagged T3SS structural proteins will be valuable to directly visualize needle assembly on the bacterial surface.

The introduction of the T3SS into Pf‐5 induced noticeable morphological changes, with cells becoming significantly elongated. This phenotype may reflect the metabolic burden associated with assembling the large injectisome complex or perturbed cell division. Similar alterations have been reported in other bacteria upon expression of large secretion systems (Jousset et al. 2017). The marked cell elongation observed in Pf‐5 may reflect physical interference of the membrane‐spanning T3SS injectisome with normal cell division. The T3SS injectisome is a large trans‐envelope complex that spans both the inner and outer membranes. Assembly of such large complexes can compete for membrane space and locally perturb peptidoglycan remodelling, thereby affecting cell shape maintenance (Typas et al. 2012). Furthermore, the divisome—the multi‐protein machinery responsible for septal peptidoglycan synthesis and cell constriction—requires precise spatial coordination at the division plane (den Blaauwen et al. 2017). The presence of a bulky foreign complex such as the T3SS injectisome near the division site could physically delay FtsZ ring constriction or septal peptidoglycan synthesis, leading to delayed cell division and consequent elongation. Intriguingly, this morphological response was specific to Pf‐5; D26 cells retained normal dimensions despite carrying the same T3SS cluster (Figure S1C). This species‐specific difference likely reflects variations in peptidoglycan crosslinking density, membrane lipid composition, or the intrinsic capacity of the cell envelope to accommodate foreign membrane complexes. The two species differ in their native secretion system complements and cell envelope architecture, which could fundamentally influence their response to the heterologous injectisome. Similar host‐dependent morphological responses to heterologous secretion systems have been documented (Jousset et al. 2017). Although a direct causal link was not established in this study, it is plausible that the observed cell elongation increases the surface area available for root attachment, or promotes microcolony formation, thereby contributing to the enhanced rhizosphere persistence. Notably, our colonization assays revealed that Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562 exhibited enhanced root attachment and persistence compared to the T3SS‐deficient control strain Pf‐5‐Bg9562, suggesting that the T3SS, despite its burden, may confer competitive advantages in the rhizosphere environment. Root exudates act as molecular architects shaping rhizobacterial communities (Shafi and Shahid 2025), and modifications to bacterial surface structures could influence attachment patterns. This interpretation is consistent with recent work showing that improvement of root‐associated traits, including bacterial attachment and persistence, is an important strategy for enhancing the performance of plant‐beneficial bacteria (Charron‐Lamoureux et al. 2024). The observed changes in the metabolite profile of Pf‐5‐T3SS, as indicated by proteomic analysis (enrichment of purine/pyrimidine and amino acid metabolism pathways), hint at a metabolic rewiring that could affect the production of native biocontrol compounds. Primary metabolites such as glutamate are known to regulate biocontrol efficacy in P. protegens (Takeuchi et al. 2023; Shafi and Shahid 2025). In addition, siderophore‐mediated resource acquisition can shape the ecological fitness of Pf‐5 in iron‐limited microbial communities, as shown by the fitness benefit of pyoverdine cross‐feeding in P. protegens Pf‐5 (Smith and Schuster 2024). We note that the growth and morphology analyses were conducted under non‐inducing conditions in nutrient‐rich medium. The T3SS injectisome assembles constitutively, and the observed minor fitness cost likely reflects the burden of maintaining this membrane‐spanning structure. However, the additional metabolic demand associated with active effector secretion under inducing conditions remains to be quantified. Furthermore, the use of rich LB medium may mask subtle fitness defects that could become apparent under nutrient‐limiting conditions. Competitive co‐culture experiments or growth assays in minimal media (e.g., M9) or soil extracts will be valuable to rigorously quantify the fitness cost. Despite this caveat, the engineered strains maintained detectable rhizosphere colonization and conferred improved disease protection in greenhouse trials, demonstrating that any fitness burden, if present at higher levels during secretion, does not preclude their biocontrol utility. The loading of the Burkholderia effector Bg9562 onto the T3SS platform proved highly effective. Bg9562 was specifically secreted in a T3SS‐dependent manner and, importantly, was visualized inside F. oxysporum hyphae, providing fluorescence‐based evidence supporting T3SS‐dependent translocation. This finding supports the use of the heterologous T3SS as a molecular‐syringe‐like platform for delivering proteins into fungal cells, a capability that wild‐type Pf‐5 lacks. Although full‐length EGFP fusions were detectable in hyphae under our assay conditions, T3SS translocation reporters can be influenced by cargo size and folding. Future studies using split‐GFP or smaller epitope‐based reporters will be useful for quantifying delivery efficiency and reducing potential reporter‐related artefacts. The antifungal activity of Bg9562 is consistent with previous reports describing prophage tail‐like proteins as potent antifungal weapons. The remarkable stability of Bg9562 across a wide range of temperatures and pH further supports its suitability for field applications where environmental conditions fluctuate (Swain et al. 2017; Chandan et al. 2023).

The greenhouse trial results showed that plants treated with Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562 had stronger suppression of Fusarium wilt than plants treated with wild‐type Pf‐5 or the T3SS‐only platform. This protective effect likely reflects the combined contribution of Bg9562 delivery and the enhanced root‐associated persistence observed in the Bg9562‐expressing T3SS background. It is also plausible that Bg9562, once delivered, may trigger plant immune responses, as has been shown for its native producer B. gladioli . The combination of direct antifungal activity and potential induction of systemic resistance may contribute to the improved protection observed (Pieterse et al. 2014). Although T3SS‐containing strains showed increased root elongation under pathogen‐free conditions, we do not interpret the empty injectisome as a direct plant growth‐promoting factor. Growth promotion in P. protegens is primarily mediated by secreted secondary metabolites such as 2,4‐diacetylphloroglucinol and phenazines, whose effective range depends on the spatial distribution of the producing bacteria along the root surface. The T3SS‐containing strains consistently achieved higher colonization densities than wild‐type Pf‐5 (Figure 4A), likely enabling their secondary metabolites to reach a broader surface area of the developing root system. One possible explanation is that changes in colonization behaviour or surface association indirectly altered the spatial distribution of native Pf‐5 metabolites along the root surface. However, this interpretation remains hypothetical and will require dedicated experiments comparing metabolite production, competitive fitness, and root‐associated persistence under matched conditions. Consistent with this interpretation, the Bg9562‐expressing strain, which achieved the highest colonization levels, also showed the strongest growth promotion effect. We note that plasmid stability was not directly quantified in this study. However, several lines of evidence suggest that the Bg9562‐encoding plasmid was functionally retained throughout the greenhouse trials. First, the Pf‐5‐T3SSΔphage‐Bg9562 treatment conferred significantly greater disease suppression than both the wild‐type Pf‐5 and the T3SS‐only strain (lacking the Bg9562 plasmid) (Figure 5E). If substantial plasmid loss had occurred, the protective efficacy would have deteriorated to the level of the T3SS‐only control, which was not observed. Second, bacteria recovered from root samples at the end of the colonization assays retained their antibiotic resistance markers. Nevertheless, future strain development will prioritize chromosomal integration of the effector gene to eliminate reliance on plasmid‐based expression and ensure complete genetic stability under field conditions. The availability of a gap‐free genome assembly for F. oxysporum f. sp. conglutinans provides a foundation for future studies aimed at understanding effector–fungus interactions at the molecular level (Wang et al. 2024).

The successful transfer of the T3SS platform to P. koreensis D26 demonstrates its broader applicability. Functional assays with LopT confirmed Ca2+‐, temperature‐ and pH‐dependent secretion, mirroring the regulatory patterns seen in Pf‐5. The D26 results further support the transferability of the heterologous T3SS platform across biocontrol‐relevant Pseudomonas backgrounds. The observed changes in the extracellular protein profile should be interpreted as host‐background‐dependent physiological responses associated with T3SS introduction, rather than as direct evidence for endogenous effector translocation. The lysis issue encountered in D26‐T3SS was resolved by deleting the phage lysis cassette, underscoring the importance of strain‐specific optimisation when transplanting large gene clusters (Huang et al. 2025).

The multifunctionality of effectors is an emerging theme in interbacterial and host–microbe interactions (Wang et al. 2022; Xu et al. 2026). In our context, Bg9562 delivered into fungal cells may exert its antifungal effect through multiple mechanisms, including disruption of membrane integrity or interference with intracellular signalling. Microbial‐based biofungicides have been shown to mitigate damage caused by F. oxysporum in various crops (de Lamo and Takken 2020; Izquierdo‐García et al. 2024).

In conclusion, we have successfully engineered a heterologous T3SS‐based protein delivery platform in two plant‐beneficial pseudomonads and demonstrated its utility in enhancing biocontrol against Fusarium wilt. This synthetic biology approach opens new avenues for engineering rhizosphere bacteria with enhanced antifungal activity and targeted effector‐delivery capacity. By combining the native biocontrol traits of strains like Pf‐5 with the targeted delivery of potent effectors, we can create more robust biopesticides. The platform also provides a tool for functional screening of effector candidates from diverse sources, accelerating the discovery of novel antifungal proteins. This work lays the foundation for a new generation of ‘designer’ biocontrol agents tailored to combat specific pathogens with precision and efficacy.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully established a functional heterologous Type III Secretion System (T3SS) in the biocontrol bacterium Pseudomonas protegens Pf‐5 and demonstrated its utility as a versatile platform for targeted delivery of antifungal effectors. By loading the antifungal protein Bg9562 onto this T3SS platform, we achieved tightly regulated, Ca2+‐dependent secretion and obtained fluorescence‐based evidence supporting T3SS‐dependent delivery of the effector into fungal and oomycete hyphae.

The engineered T3SS‐Bg9562 strain exhibited significantly enhanced rhizosphere colonization on both tobacco and tomato roots, as well as improved plant growth‐promoting activity under pathogen‐free conditions. More importantly, in greenhouse trials, this strain conferred improved protection against tomato Fusarium wilt, achieving disease suppression comparable to healthy controls. The protective effect was specifically attributed to T3SS‐mediated delivery of Bg9562, as the T3SS platform alone provided only marginal benefit.

Furthermore, the modular nature of this system was demonstrated by its successful transfer to another biocontrol strain, Pseudomonas koreensis D26, indicating its broad applicability across different bacterial hosts.

Collectively, our work establishes a versatile T3SS‐based ‘molecular syringe’ for precision delivery of antifungal effectors, offering a promising strategy for developing next‐generation biocontrol agents with enhanced colonization, growth‐promoting and disease‐suppression capabilities. Tshis approach not only addresses key limitations of conventional biocontrol strategies but also provides a generalisable platform for engineering beneficial bacteria to combat a wide range of phytopathogens.
