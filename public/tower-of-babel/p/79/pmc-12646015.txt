Guided by Culture, Fueled by Goals: Evaluating the Patnubay sa Pagtatagumpay Mentorship Journey
Authors: Priscilla L Sagar, Mary Dioise Ramos, Mizpah Amados, Gloria Lamela Beriones, Warly Remegio, Marlon Garzo Saria, Lorraine Evangelista
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEffective mentorship is fundamental to professional development in nursing; however, there are limited initiatives that cater to the unique cultural and contextual needs of Filipino American nurses. To bridge this gap, the Philippine Nurses Association of America launched its Patnubay sa Pagtatagumpay Program (P3). This innovative initiative offers a culturally tailored approach to mentorship, incorporating features such as mentor-mentee pairings, structured goal setting, and year-long cohort support. This paper evaluates the effectiveness of the P3 program, with initial outcomes indicating high overall satisfaction from both participants. By demonstrating that culturally focused mentorship can effectively meet the specific needs of a diverse nursing population, the P3 model presents a valuable framework for enhancing professional development and satisfaction within the health care field.

To bridge this gap, the Philippine Nurses Association of America (PNAA) launched its Patnubay sa Pagtatagumpay Program (P3). This innovative initiative offers a culturally tailored approach to mentorship, incorporating features such as mentor-mentee pairings, structured goal setting, and year-long cohort support. By demonstrating that culturally focused mentorship can effectively meet the specific needs of a diverse nursing population, the P3 model presents a valuable framework for enhancing professional development and satisfaction within the health care field.

Mentorship is crucial for all nurses, but it is particularly critical for those from underrepresented cultural backgrounds.1,2 Filipino Americans (FA), along with other Asian Americans and Pacific Islanders aspiring to enter the nursing profession, may encounter distinct social and cultural challenges.3 The “bamboo ceiling” refers to the unseen barriers that women face in their pursuit of advancement to leadership roles.4 A “model minority” [myth or stereotype] would suggest that Asian Americans and Pacific Islanders do not require much assistance, which may result in unsupported efforts to advance in their careers. Nurses often experience feelings of isolation or a lack of appreciation, highlighting the need for culturally competent mentors who can address issues specific to their cultural context.5 As a health care community, we must comprehend and empathize with these unique issues.

Filipino American nurses have historically been referred to as the “backbone” of the American health care system due to their significant representation in the U.S. nursing sector.6 In 2021, the Philippines was the source of approximately 27% of the foreign-born nurses in the United States.7 This demonstrates that Filipino nurses are making significant contributions to the workforce by filling vacant positions, while individuals continue to migrate to the United States. It may be challenging for them to become nurses in the United States due to the complex immigration processes and the need to adjust to a new cultural and clinical environment.8 Upon their arrival, they must acquire the necessary skills to navigate the regulations, communication methods, and procedures of the U.S. health care system. Filipino nurses who have pursued their education abroad have reported experiencing feelings of isolation, fear, and exclusion as they adjust to their work environment.9 These experiences emphasize the significance of establishing support mechanisms, such as mentorship, that are tailored to the cultural and situational needs of FA nurses.

The advantages of mentorship in nursing are well-documented.10–12 Nevertheless, there is a lack of research and organized programs that address the specific needs of FA nurses. This lack of research is particularly concerning given the historical scarcity of mentorship programs designed through a Filipino cultural perspective, resulting in a significant gap in support for this demographic. Consequently, numerous individuals commenced their careers in the U.S. without mentors familiar with their backgrounds. This sometimes resulted in inadequate integration and employee dissatisfaction.

Numerous minority nursing mentorship programs exist; however, they often aggregate Asian subgroups or employ a uniform approach, thereby overlooking the diversity within the Asian American and Pacific Islander category.1 There is limited understanding of how mentors and mentees from diverse cultural backgrounds align their expectations. We have not thoroughly explored the impact of varying training levels on mentors and mentees. FA nurses constitute a heterogeneous group, with specific individuals receiving their education in the Philippines and others in the United States. Various expectations between mentor and mentee can strain their relationship and complicate the learning process.13 Individuals exhibit diverse levels of clinical training and professional socialization. Differences in educational background or prior nursing experience may impact the required level of assistance and the effectiveness of communication.14 However, the effects of these variations on the mentoring process remain ambiguous.

In recent years, the FA nursing community has begun to acknowledge and address these issues. The PNAA initiated the Patnubay sa Pagtatagumpay (P3) Mentoring Program in February 2023 exclusively for its members. It was among the inaugural structured mentorship initiatives designed explicitly for FA nurses at PNAA’s 56 chapters throughout the U.S. P3, translating to “Guidance for Success” in Filipino, was developed to provide individuals with a structured and culturally relevant means of obtaining assistance. The P3 program is a mentee-focused, year-long mentorship initiative designed explicitly for FA nurses, collaborating with various other programs. This initiative links beginner FA nurses with experienced FA nurses who serve as mentors. P3 asserts that mentors who have faced similar cultural and professional challenges are most effective in supporting FA mentees. The program aims to foster the development of professional and culturally relevant connections between mentors and mentees by leveraging shared language, values, and experiences.

The purpose of this article is to present the impact and effectiveness of the PNAA’s (P3) Mentoring Program. This investigation explores the effectiveness of mentorship within the FA nursing community by analyzing the data from the P3 program’s culturally tailored strategies. Specifically, the study evaluates the influence of its culturally appropriate matching process, SMART goal setting, and year-long cohort support to on mentor-mentee relationships. It examines the consistency of mentor and mentee expectation, with a focus on how cultural concepts of trust, leadership, and professional development influence these dynamics. Ultimately, this initiative contributes to the development of culturally relevant and accessible mentorship models that enhance the visibility, job satisfaction, and career advancement opportunities for FA nurses and other underrepresented groups in health care.

METHODS

Study Design and Participants

We conducted a cross-sectional survey of P3 program participants from April to May 2025. Eligible participants included all 21 mentees and 25 mentors enrolled in P3’s cohort 2. Invitations and survey links were distributed via SurveyMonkey, yielding usable responses from 14 mentees (a 67% response rate) and 25 mentors (a 100% response rate).

Instrumentation

To assess mentor competencies and mentee observations, we adopted the validated Mentoring Competency Assessment (MCA) tool developed by the University of Wisconsin–Madison’s Institute for Clinical and Translational Research and its Center for the Improvement of Mentored Experiences in Research, with a Cronbach’s alpha above 0.81 for each sub-scale.15 The MCA measures self-reported proficiency across 6 core domains—maintaining effective communication, aligning expectations, assessing understanding, fostering independence, promoting professional development, and addressing diversity—using a five-point Likert scale (1 = “Not at all proficient” to 5 = “Extremely proficient”). Mentees completed a parallel version of the MCA to rate the observed behaviors of their mentors. In addition to the MCA, we integrated the bespoke P3 Tool, explicitly designed for the P3 program, which captures program-specific elements, including culturally congruent mentor–mentee matching, joint SMART goal development, year-long cohort support, and overall satisfaction. Items were drafted in consultation with PNAA leadership and pilot-tested with a subgroup of 5 mentors and 5 mentees to ensure clarity and cultural relevance.

Data Collection and Measures

Participants reported demographic characteristics, primary expectations or roles in mentorship, total hours of formal mentor/mentee training, and overall satisfaction (1 = “Very dissatisfied” to 5 = “Very satisfied”). The MCA provided domain-level scores for both mentor self-assessments and mentee observations. The P3 Tool yielded quantitative ratings on cultural congruence and goal-achievement supports, as well as open-ended feedback on program strengths and areas for improvement.

Data Analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed in SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics [Version 27], Chicago, IL). Descriptive statistics summarized participant characteristics, training hours (median, IQR), and satisfaction ratings. Paired t-tests compared mentor self-ratings versus mentee ratings across MCA domains. Mann–Whitney U tests were used to assess differences in training hours. Qualitative responses from the P3 Tool were thematically coded in NVivo 12 by 2 independent reviewers; discrepancies were resolved through consensus.

RESULTS

Participant Characteristics

Table 1 presents the demographics of 14 mentees and 25 mentors enrolled in the P3 program. Mentees predominantly held master’s degrees (90.9%) and served as nurse managers/educators (36.3%) or advanced practice nurses (27.3%). In comparison, mentors were more likely to hold doctoral degrees (76.0%, including PhD, EdD, and DNP) and occupy academic posts, such as professorships (28.0%) or clinical instructor positions (20.0%). Research interests showed complementary patterns: behavioral research was common among both mentees (27.3%) and mentors (24.0%), whereas 36.4% of mentees did not specify a research focus (Table 1).

Training Disparities

A significant training gap emerged between mentors and mentees: mentors completed a median of 10 h (IQR, 8–14) of formal mentorship preparation, whereas mentees completed just 3 h (IQR, 2–5) of formal mentorship preparation. Figure 1’s boxplots illustrate this disparity (p < .001), highlighting the need for balanced training curricula to empower mentees as active participants in the mentorship process.

Mentors consistently rated their proficiency as higher than that of mentees across all 6 MCA domains (Table 2). Cultural and Relational Observations

Less than half of mentees reported observing key relational competencies: 42% noted cultural-competence behaviors, and 47% observed leadership modeling (Table 3). These under-observed domains suggest that, despite high self-rated diversity-addressing skills (mean 4.2 ± 0.7), mentors may need to demonstrate cultural affirmation and leadership more explicitly to meet the expectations of their mentees.

Program Satisfaction and P3-Specific Supports

Overall satisfaction with the P3 mentorship experience averaged 4.1 (±0.8), with 63.6% of mentees describing themselves as “extremely satisfied.” Table 4 and Figure 3 show that culturally congruent matching (mean 4.3 ± 0.6) and joint SMART goal development (mean 4.4 ± 0.5) received particularly high endorsements, with over 80% rating these elements as “very” or “extremely” supportive. Year-long cohort engagement also garnered strong support (mean 4.2 ± 0.7). This feedback highlights the P3 program’s success in establishing culturally aligned, goal-driven mentorship structures.

DISCUSSION

The P3 mentorship program produced positive outcomes for both participants. Mentees experienced improved academic and career skills, while mentors developed stronger leadership abilities and a greater sense of purpose, aligning with prior research on the mutually beneficial nature of mentorship.1,16–18 Despite these shared benefits, the study identified a key difference in participant priorities. Mentees tended to focus on tangible results, such as academic and career guidance, whereas mentors prioritized the mentoring process and the relationship-building aspect. This aligns with findings from other programs, where mentors and mentees seek distinct yet equally valuable outcomes.19 While the P3 program’s success corroborates the general finding that mentorship yields significant gains for all involved, it also highlighted common challenges.20–22 Some mentors reported role ambiguity and felt unprepared to handle complex situations, and some mentees were initially hesitant to seek help.23,24 These issues underscore the necessity of a well-designed program with proper training and support to address these challenges and ensure a positive experience for everyone.25

Implications for Nurse Leaders

Nurse leaders should view mentorship programs as a strategic investment in professional development and talent retention. Findings from the P3 program, consistent with broader research, indicate that mentorship enhances job satisfaction, promotes professional growth, and reduces turnover among both new and experienced nurses. This means that a well-structured mentorship program is not just an added benefit but a crucial tool for building a stable, skilled, and engaged workforce.26 The P3 data revealed that mentors often need more support and training. Nurse leaders must provide comprehensive resources to address role ambiguity and equip mentors to handle diverse situations. Training should include relationship-building and cultural competence, as well as clear guidelines on a mentor’s roles and responsibilities to prevent stress and inconsistent support.23,24 Furthermore, offering ongoing support through regular check-ins or peer support groups can help mentors navigate challenges and share best practices.10,25

Nurse leaders should move beyond simple pairings and use a more thoughtful approach that considers shared academic backgrounds, professional fields, and cultural identities. Diversifying the mentor pool is also critical, as mentees are more likely to be inspired and confident when they can connect with mentors who share similar experiences. Leaders should recognize that mentorship is a reciprocal process. It benefits not only the mentee’s growth but also the mentor’s development of leadership and communication skills. By acknowledging and rewarding the contributions of mentors, leaders can cultivate a culture that values this two-way learning dynamic, ultimately boosting engagement and fostering a more supportive work environment.

The study’s strengths include its dual perspective, gained from both mentors and mentees, and its use of real-world data, employing a combination of quantitative and qualitative methods. However, it’s limited by its focus on a single program, which affects generalizability. Other limitations include the absence of a control group, reliance on self-reported data, and a focus on short-term effects, which leaves the sustainability of the outcomes unknown. To improve, the P3 program should implement follow-up studies to track the long-term sustainability of participant gains. The program could also explore AI-assisted matching and investigate the effectiveness of mentoring across diverse populations to create more tailored resources. Expanding the program’s reach through partnerships and hybrid mentoring models that combine in-person and digital platforms would make it a more adaptable and equitable model.

CONCLUSION

The P3 mentorship program demonstrated its value as a culturally responsive and beneficial program for both mentors and mentees. Mentees reported improvements in both their academic and personal development, while mentors enhanced their communication skills and gained a deeper understanding of their mentees’ growth. Mentees prioritized tangible outcomes and direct assistance, while mentors focused more on relationship-building and self-reflection. To improve, the P3 program should invest in better mentor training, enhance the matching process to align professional backgrounds, and track the long-term impacts on participants.
