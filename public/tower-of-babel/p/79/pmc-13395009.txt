MiR‐375 in CAFs‐Derived EVs Promotes HCC Progression via Regulating RASA1 and May Potentially Serve as a Biomarker for HCC
Authors: Wei Qin, Li Wang, Beiyuan Hu, Cuicui Xiao, XiaoYing Wu, Huan Tian, Songlin Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAFs) in the tumor microenvironment play an important role in cancer initiation and progression through mediating crosstalk between CAFs and cancer cells. In our study, we unveiled that CAFs‐derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) could deliver miR‐375 to endothelial cells (EC) and further contribute to tumor angiogenesis by regulating Ras GTPase Activating Protein 1 (RASA1) in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). We also found that HCC patients with more advanced disease showed higher levels of miR‐375 in plasma EVs, which were preferentially expressed in the EVs of CAFs. These data provided direct evidence supporting the pivotal role of the tumor suppressor miR‐375, which is selectively sorted into CAF‐derived EVs, in promoting angiogenesis during HCC progression and revealed that genetically modified CAF‐derived EVs may provide a potential therapeutic strategy for HCC. Furthermore, our study indicated that miR‐375 in plasma EVs could serve as a potential predictive and prognostic biomarker in HCC patients.

Introduction

Cancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are one of the most abundant stromal components in many types of malignancies [1]. Accumulating evidence indicates that the alterations of extracellular matrix (ECM) structure, metabolic and immune reprogramming of cancer cells or other stromal cells triggered by CAFs have profound effects on the tumor microenvironment (TME), all of which contribute to the establishment and maintenance of the tumorigenic state and resistance to anticancer therapies [2, 3]. And our previous studies indicated that CAFs could mediate crosstalk between cancer cells and tumor stroma by secreting proteins, such as HGF, IL‐6 and PEG2, resulting in the processes of cancer initiation and progression [4, 5]. Significantly, recent studies demonstrated that the therapeutic strategies that target CAFs could pave the path to develop a beneficial anticancer therapy in multiple types of cancers [3]. Nevertheless, the roles of CAFs in cancer development through controlling the tumor‐stromal interactions are still far from elucidated.

Emerging evidence has indicated that exosomes/extracellular vesicles (EVs) play a key role in tumor‐host crosstalk by delivering their “cargo” (proteins, nucleotides, deoxynucleotides, miRNA and so on) to recipient cells, thereby impacting tumor cell growth, metastasis, angiogenesis and modulating the cancer microenvironment [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Notably, cancer cells could employ exosomes as vehicles to get rid of tumor suppressor miRNAs, which indicates that selective sorting of miRNAs into exosomes may underlie many biological processes related to cancer development [11]. These findings may provide evidence to support the notion that CAFs could shape their phenotype via selective sorting of miRNAs into exosomes/EVs, thereby affecting the TME. Similarly, Chen et al. have showed that miRNAs derived from human mesenchymeal stem cells (MSCs) played an important role in regulating the function of endothelial cells (ECs) and vitally participating in embryonic angiogenesis [12]. Herein, we identified the miRNA expression profiles in CAF‐derived EVs, and examined the effect of miR‐375 (a tumor suppressor miRNA) in CAFs‐derived EVs on ECs angiogenesis, thereby promoting HCC progression. Additionally, we further explored the underlying molecular mechanisms and its clinical values.

Materials and Methods

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded tissues were sliced into 4.0 μm thickness. The paraffin sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval with 0.01 M sodium citrate buffer (PH 6.0), followed with 3% hydrogen peroxide incubated for 20 min at room temperature to block endogenous peroxidase, next with 10% goat serum blocking for 30 min. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4°C in a humidified chamber, followed by secondary antibody [GTVision III Detection System/Mo&Rb (GeneTech, China)] incubation for 30 min at 37°C in a humidified chamber. The sections were pigmentized in DAB under the monitoring of microscopic observation. Tissue sections were counterstained by hematoxylin, dehydrated in graded ethanols, and mounted. Appropriate positive and negative controls were included for each run of IHC. The antibodies were listed in the Table S1. Positive mouse CD34 in vascular endothelial cells was used for quantifying MVD. The area with the highest density of microvessels was selected to count microvascular numbers at a magnification of ×400 in three sections. Median values were used as the MVD in subsequence analyses.

RNA Isolation and Quantitative Real‐Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT‐PCR)

Isolation of EVs RNA was performed using the mirVana PARIS Kit (Life Technologies, USA). RNA from cells, CM and solid tissues was prepared using TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies, USA). Prior to isolation of extracellular RNA (EVs or CM), a C. elegans miRNA, Cel‐miR‐39 was spiked into the samples with a final concentration of 0.05 nM as a reference. Total RNA was isolated from plasma using the exoRNeasy Serum/Plasma Midi Kit (Qiagen, USA). Complementary DNA synthesis was performed using Transcriptor First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Roche Life Sciences, Switzerland). Amplication was performed using the PrimeSTAR Max DNA. RNA extracted from the samples was reversely transcribed with miRcute Plus miRNA First‐Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (TIANGEN, China). Subsequently, qRT‐PCR with SYBR Green master mix (TIANGEN, China) was performed with a LightCycler 480 thermocycler (Roche Diagnostics, Germany). All kits were used according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cel‐miR‐39 and U6 snRNA were used as an internal control to normalize RNA input. To analyze the mRNA levels of related genes, total RNA was reversely transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, Japan), followed by qRT‐PCR using SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Takara, Japan). Standard curves were generated and the relative amount of target gene mRNA was normalized to GAPDH. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. The change in miR‐375 or mRNA expression was calculated as fold change using the comparative threshold cycle (Ct) method. The sequences of primers were shown in the Table S2.

Lentiviral and Plasmid Package and Cell Transfection

Lentiviral plasmids encoding miR‐375 or NC, plasmid encoding RASA1 and the RASA1 NC plasmid were designed and produced by Guangzhou Fitgene Biotechnology Co. LTD (Guangzhou, China). Viruses were harvested 72 h after transfection and viral titers were 3.0 × 108 TU/mL. HUVECs were transfected with lentivirus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 20. Cy3‐miR‐375 and siRNA for RASA1 were purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The siRNAs and NC plasmid were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. The siRNAs sequences are shown in the Table S3. The siRNAs and miRNA‐NC plasmid were transfected using Lipofectamine2000 (Invitrogen, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol.

Other Materials and Methods are described in the Supporting Information and Methods Doc S1.

Results

CAFs Contribute to HCC Growth and Metastasis by Promoting Tumor Angiogenesis

To functionally assess the impact of CAFs on HCC progression in vivo, we isolated the CAFs and non‐cancerous fibroblasts (PAFs and NLFs) (Figure S1), and then subcutaneously co‐implanted them with human HCC cells (MHCC97‐L) into nude mice. Our results showed that CAFs and NLFs affected the growth of MHCC97‐L xenografts (Figure S1). Histologically, xenografts in CAFs and NLFs groups exhibited more specific α‐SMA+ fibroblasts and higher microvessel density (MVD) count (Figure S1). The mice bearing MHCC97‐L/CAFs or MHCC97‐L/NLFs xenografts had significantly increased intrahepatic and pulmonary metastases compared with those bearing MHCC97‐L xenografts (Figure S1). These data suggest that at least part of the NLFs are transformed into CAFs and then facilitate the tumor growth and spontaneous metastatic dissemination in vivo of HCC.

The Isolation and Identification of CAF‐Derived EVs

To better explore the roles of EVs derived from CAFs, we isolated and cultured fibroblasts derived from HCC tissues. Then, we isolated nanoparticles from the culture supernatant of CAFs by multistep ultracentrifugation. Transmission electron microscopy showed a homogenous pattern of spheroid membrane‐sorting particles. The size of these particles ranged from 40 to 120 nm (Figure S2A). The purity and the size of CAF‐derived EVs were confirmed by Nanosight analysis (Figure S2B). Further, Western blot proved that the small membrane vesicles expressed EVs marker proteins CD63, CD81, Flotillin‐1, TSG101, and Hsp90α/β, and were depleted from cellular markers Calnexin and GM130 (Figure S2C). All results above suggested that these nanoparticles derived from CAFs are EVs.

CAF‐Derived EVs Accelerate EC Angiogenesis

In this study, CAFs were found to employ EVs to communicate with HUVECs (Figure 1A). Upon treatment with EVs from CAFs ex vivo, rather than that from NLFs, the proliferation, migration, invasion abilities of HUVECs (Figure 1B–G) and the number of branch points within the capillary‐like structures (Figure 1H,I) were increased. Morphologically, HUVECs treated with CAF‐derived EVs presented long spindle shapes, a typical sign of cytoskeleton arrangement via activation of the RhoA pathway (Figure 1J–M). Additionally, a significant dose‐dependent response was observed in the effect of CAF‐derived EVs on angiogenesis (Figure 1N). In vivo, the xenograft tumor of HCC cells co‐implanted with CAF‐derived EVs displayed a significantly higher MVD (Figure 1O). Collectively, these data indicate that CAF‐derived EVs could promote tumor angiogenesis in HCC.

Differential miRNA Profiles Between CAF‐Derived EVs and CAFs

To explore the effect of miRNAs in CAFs‐derived EVs on endothelial cell angiogenesis, we sequenced small RNAs (18‐30 nt) sequencing from CAFs and CAF‐derived EVs. An overview of miRNA expression profiles was shown in Figure S2D,E. The miRNA profiles between CAFs and CAF‐derived EVs were very similar, and nearly 200 miRNAs were found to be co‐expressed. There were abundant miRNAs in both CAFs and CAF‐derived EVs, such as let‐7a‐5p, miR‐100‐5p, miR‐125b‐5p, miR‐21‐5p, miR‐29a‐3p, miR‐22‐3p, and miR‐143‐3p (Table S4).

However, some miRNAs were significantly up‐regulated in EVs, suggesting that the packaging of miRNAs into the EVs was a selective process. A total of 6 miRNAs (fold change ≥ 1.5) were differentially expressed between CAF‐derived EVs and CAFs, including miR‐4488, miR‐375, miR‐4508, miR‐1273 g‐3p, miR‐4492 and miR‐6087.

KEGG Pathway Analysis by the Target Genes of Differentially Expressed miRNAs

A Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis was performed with the target genes of the top ten differentially expressed miRNAs identified in the CAF‐derived EVs by DIANA mirPath V3.0 software. Among them, the ECM‐receptor interaction pathway was prominent with the smallest p‐value (4.83 × 10−51). In addition, we further analyzed specific KEGG pathways targeted by the up‐regulated miRNAs in CAF–derived EVs, which included miR‐4488, miR‐375, miR‐4508, miR‐1273 g‐3p, miR‐4492, and miR‐6087. The details of target pathways were listed in Table S5. Undoubtedly, the miRNAs involved in axon guidance signaling could affect ECs and further induce angiogenesis. A previous study had indicated that formononetin promoted ERα‐positive cell (HUVECs, MCF‐7) proliferation through miR‐375 activation [13]. Consistently, our data demonstrated that miR‐375 was significantly up‐regulated in CAF‐derived EVs as compared to NLF‐derived EVs and their parental cells (Figure S2F,G). Therefore, miR‐375, the one revealing the most evidenced correlation, was chosen for detailed investigation.

MiR‐375 in CAFs‐Derived EVs Could Be Transferred to HUVECs

Next, we further verified the effect of miR‐375 in CAFs‐derived EVs on tumor angiogenesis. Our results showed that Cy3‐tagged EVs‐miR‐375 could be up‐taken by HUVECs (Figure 2A). An elevated level of miR‐375 was observed in HUVECs after EVs treatment, which was not affected by RNA polymerase II inhibitor (Figure 2B). These suggest that the increased miR‐375 results from the EVs‐mediated miRNA transfer rather than the induction of its endogenous expression.

RASA1 Is a Direct Target of miR‐375 in HUVECs

We searched for candidate target genes of miR‐375 using public databases such as TargetScan, miRBase, miRanda, and microInspector. The complementary sequence of miR‐375 was confirmed in the 3′‐UTR (untranslated region) of RASA1 (Figure 2C), which had been reported to promote angiogenesis in some previous studies [14, 15]. Our data demonstrated that the up‐regulation of miR‐375 or EVs treatment could dramatically reduce the mRNA and protein levels of RASA1 in HUVECs (Figure 2D–F). Consistently, miR‐375 significantly suppressed the luciferase activity of RASA1 only when wild‐type 3′‐UTRs were present, whereas anti‐miR‐375 treatment increased the luciferase activity of RASA1 (Figure 2G). Taken together, these results indicate that RASA1 is a direct target of miR‐375 in HUVECs. In agreement, there were more infiltrating α‐SMA+ CAFs in RASA1‐negative HCC tissues than in RASA1‐positive HCC tissues (Figure 2H).

miR‐375 Promotes EC Angiogenesis by Activating Ras/Erk Signaling Pathway

To further explore the effect and potential mechanisms of miR‐375 on angiogenesis, we knocked down the expression of RASA1 in HUVECs with short interfering RNA (siRNA). qRT‐PCR and western blotting results confirmed that RASA1 was significantly down‐regulated in the si‐RNA3 group compared with that in the control group (Figure 3A). Indeed, down‐regulation of RASA1 significantly increased the proliferation, capillary tube formation, invasion, and migration abilities of HUVECs, and reduced the apoptosis of HUVECs (Figure 3B–F). Conversely, reintroduction of RASA1 expression significantly inhibited the increased angiogenesis of overexpressed miR‐375‐HUVECs back to the similar levels of control cells (Figure 3G–K).

As RASA1 is a well‐recognized negative regulator of Ras/Erk signaling pathway [16, 17], we further investigated whether miR‐375 could regulate Ras/Erk signaling pathway in HUVECs. Our results showed that overexpression of miR‐375 in HUVECs induced the activation of Ras/Erk pathway (Figure 3L). In contrast, the effects of up‐regulation of miR‐375 on HUVECs could be partially attenuated by overexpressing RASA1 ectopically. Overall, these results indicate that miR‐375 in CAFs‐derived EVs promotes tumor angiogenesis through modulation of Ras/Erk pathway.

miR‐375 in CAFs‐Derived EVs Promotes HCC Progression by Downregulating RASA1 in Vitro and in Vivo

Subsequently, we further verify whether miR‐375 in CAF‐derived EVs could promote tumor angiogenesis by regulating RASA1 in vivo. Our results showed that the xenograft tumor of Huh7 cells co‐implanted with CAF‐derived EVs displayed a significantly lower RASA1 expression (Figure 4A). On the other hand, inhibiting the miR‐375 in CAFs‐derived EVs dramatically abrogated the ability of CAFs to induce tumor angiogenesis and down‐regulate RASA expression in HUVECs (Figure 4B,C), and promote HCC progression in vivo (Figure 4D–H). Mechanistically, similar results indicated that miR‐375 in CAFs‐derived EVs dramatically down‐regulated RASA1 expression and then induced the activation of the Ras/Erk pathway in vivo (Figure 4I). Collectively, these in vivo data provide further evidence to support that miR‐375 in CAF‐derived EVs promote HCC progression by down‐regulating RASA1.

MiR‐375 in Plasma EVs Is a Potential Diagnostic Biomarker for HCC

Based on these above findings, we next sought to evaluate whether our findings were clinically relevant. We recruited 197 participants overall, 133 in the training (ZSSY) cohort and 55 in the validation (FDHS) cohort. We detected the levels of miR‐375 in plasma EVs from healthy controls, patients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) or hepatitis B virus (HBV)‐induced liver cirrhosis, and HCC patients by qRT‐PCR (Figure 5A). The demographic characteristics of HCC patients in the ZSSY and FDHS cohorts were summarized in Table S6.

In the training cohort, miR‐375 levels in plasma EVs were significantly up‐regulated (relative expression > 1.25) in 97/106 HCC cases (91.5%) as compared to healthy controls (Figure 5B). Importantly, the levels of miR‐375 in plasma EVs were significantly increased in HCC patients as compared to patients with CHB or HBV‐induced liver cirrhosis (Figure 5C). Thus, we examined the potential diagnostic value of miR‐375 in plasma EVs. Our results indicated that miR‐375 in plasma EVs contributed to 0.702 area under the ROC curve (AUC) (p < 0.01), 90.9% specificity and 57.5% sensitivity in discriminating individuals with HCC from non‐HCC controls (healthy controls, patients with CHB and HBV‐induced liver cirrhosis), whereas AFP only resulted in 0.689 AUC (p < 0.01), 96.0% specificity and 50.9% sensitivity. ROC analysis showed that testing of both miR‐375 in plasma EVs and AFP increased the diagnostic accuracy for HCC compared with either test alone (AUC 0.811, p < 0.0001), 94.7% specificity and 68.0% sensitivity (Figure 5D,E). 94 (88.7%) of 106 HCC patients had positive results when miR‐375 in plasma EVs and AFP were tested together. Furthermore, 22 (66.1%) of 36 AFP‐negative HCC patients had positive AFP results.

In view of our preceding data with the training cohort demonstrating the value of miR‐375 in plasma EVs as a biomarker for HCC, we further verified the potential diagnostic value of miR‐375 in the FDHS cohort. In the FDHS cohort, miR‐375 levels in plasma EVs were significantly up‐regulated (relative expression > 1.25) in 28/34 HCC cases (82.8%) as compared to healthy controls (Figure 5F). As expected, the levels of miR‐375 in plasma EVs were significantly increased in HCC patients as compared to patients with CHB or HBV‐induced liver cirrhosis (Figure 5G). When HCC patients in the FDHS cohort were compared with non‐HCC controls, ROC curve analysis validated the diagnostic ability of miR‐375 in plasma EVs to discriminate between non‐HCC controls and HCC cases with an AUC of 0.736 (p < 0.01), 86.4% specificity and 55.0% sensitivity, whereas AFP showed an AUC of 0.691 (p = 0.01), 87.5% specificity and 47.5% sensitivity. ROC analysis showed that testing of both miR‐375 in plasma EVs and AFP increased the diagnostic accuracy for HCC compared with either test alone (AUC 0.790, p < 0.0003), 95.5% specificity and 57.6% sensitivity (Figure 5H,I). Additionally, the validation cohort confirmed the ability of miR‐375 in plasma EVs to diagnose HCC in AFP‐negative patients (10/19).

Higher Levels of miR‐375 in Plasma EVs Lead to More Aggressive Disease

To examine whether miR‐375 in plasma EVs is up‐regulated in aggressive HCC, we investigated the correlations of miR‐375 in plasma EVs with clinicopathological parameters of HCC patients in the ZSSY cohort (Table S7). Significantly, we observed that HCC patients with BCLC B/C stage displayed higher levels of miR‐375 in plasma EVs as compared to that of HCC patients with BCLC A stage (Figure 5J, Table S4). Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that the patients with a high miR‐375 level in plasma EVs had poorer recurrence‐free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) than those with a low level (Figure S3A,B and Table S8). Subsequently, we asked if miR‐375 could be used to predict the recurrence of HCC after hepatic resection. As shown in Figure 5K, our clinical data with an 18‐month follow‐up after hepatectomy in the FDHS cohort further confirmed that HCC patients with a high level of miR‐375 in plasma EVs have a higher risk of early recurrence.

To further identify the origin of miR‐375 in plasma EVs, we isolated the EVs from the normal liver cell (L02), HCC cells, CAFs, para‐cancer fibroblasts (PAFs), LX‐2 and human umbilical vascular endothelial cells (HUVECs), and found that miR‐375 expression was low in HCC cell lines and CAFs. Furthermore, the miR‐375 level in EVs derived from CAFs was significantly higher than that from the other cells (Figure 5L). And the EVs miR‐375 from CAFs accounted for over two thirds of that in whole culture supernatants (Figure 5M). To verify whether selective sorting of miR‐375 into EVs is mainly responsible for its loss in CAFs, we treated CAFs with GW4869, an EVs secretion inhibitor, to modulate the EVs miR‐375 levels. As expected, the GW4869 treatment induced significant decreases in the EVs miR‐375 (Figure 5N).

Discussion

Recently, many studies have indicated that the “dialog” between stromal cells and cancer cells in the TME could mediate tumor invasion and drug resistance. Exosomes/EVs have been considered as an important “messenger” between cells. Our previous study also indicated that CAFs‐derived Gremlin‐1‐rich exosomes promote EMT and decrease the sensitivity of hepatoma cells to sorafenib by Wnt/β‐catenin and BMP signaling [18]. In this study, we identified that miRNAs in CAF‐derived EVs were involved in angiogenesis‐related pathways, including Notch, mTOR, and VEGF signaling pathway (data not shown). Most strikingly, our results showed that up‐regulated miR‐375 in CAF‐derived EVs could promote ECs angiogenesis by regulating axon guidance signaling pathway. These findings were consistent with the general view that axon guidance signaling pathway was essential for normal vascular patterning [19, 20] and might regulate guidance events controlling vascular sprouting.

Another novel finding of the present study was that EVs miR‐375 derived from CAFs could affect the proliferation and cytoskeleton of HUVECs. Current studies have identified the relationships between ECs senescence and cytoskeleton change [21, 22], we, therefore, explored whether CAF‐derived EVs could induce a senescence‐like EC phenotype in vitro and found that CAF‐derived EVs have no effect on the expression levels of the typical senescence markers (Figure S4A–C), further supporting the notion that CAF‐derived EVs are closely linked to tumor angiogenesis, but not EC senescence.

miR‐375 has been reported as a well‐known tumor‐suppressive miRNA in human cancer [23, 24, 25]. As reported previously, miR‐375 is significantly down‐regulated in HCC tissues and liver cancer cells, and exerts an antitumor effect during HCC progression [26, 27, 28]. Consistently, our data showed that HCC tissues exhibited the down‐regulated miR‐375 expression, compared to that in ANLTs. And a Kaplan–Meier plot (http://www.kmplot.com) showed that HCC patients with a low miR‐375 level in tumor tissues had a higher risk of OS than those with a high miR‐375 level (Figure S5A,C). Importantly, our findings first demonstrated that miR‐375 in CAF‐derived EVs could be horizontally transferred to ECs and directly target RASA1, thereby promoting tumor angiogenesis and HCC progression. These results were in agreement with other studies [13].

RASA1 is a Ras GTPase–activating protein (RasGAP), which acts as a negative regulator of Ras and its function as a potent tumor suppressor gene in cancer [15, 29, 30, 31]. Lapinski PE et al. recently uncovered that RASA1 catalytic activity was essential for the function and development of lymphatic vessel (LV) valves, which provided a partial explanation for LV leakage defects and potentially other LV abnormalities observed in CM‐AVM [32]. Indeed, our work may provide initial evidence in support of the conclusion that EVs miR‐375 from stromal cells affected ECs angiogenesis and the morphology of neovascularization by targeting RASA1. Furthermore, we provided the evidence that miR‐375 was a super‐controlled for Ras–Erk cascade by directly targeting RASA1. These results were consistent with the findings that RASA1 and SPRED1 were determined to be direct targets of miR‐182 in regulating the Ras/Erk signaling pathway [17].

A great interest has been generated in EVs miRNAs as cancer alternative biomarkers that can be used as liquid biopsies for cancer screening, patient stratification and monitoring. Up to date, a few studies have examined exosomes/EVs miRNA profiles in plasma of HCC patients. Given our above results showing that there is an inverse correlation between the expression level of miR‐375 in HCC tissues and corresponding level of miR‐375 in plasma EVs from HCC patients, we speculate that miR‐375, a tumor suppressor miRNA in HCC, could be specifically sorted into EVs leading to its high levels in plasma (Figure S5B,D–I). Significantly, we demonstrated that CAFs selectively sorted tumor suppressor miRNAs out of cells, which ultimately promoted HCC progression in this particular way. This confirms a previous concept that cancer cells employ exosomes as vehicles to get rid of tumor suppressor miRNAs [11, 33]. Nowadays, the mechanism of RNA‐binding proteins (RBPs) in the sorting and packaging of miRNAs into EVs has become a key area of investigation [34]. The recognition motifs within miRNAs, known as EXOmotifs (associated with exosomes enrichment) and CELLmotifs (related to intracellular enrichment), play a pivotal role in this sorting process [34]. RBPs that recognize these motifs are essential for the selective loading of miRNAs into EVs. For instance, two RBPs, Alyref and Fus, play a role in the export of miRNAs that carry the potent EXOmotifs, CGGGAG [35]. Moreover, previous studies showed that several members of the heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein (hnRNP) family have been identified as key regulators of post‐transcriptional RNA metabolism and vesicle loading. These proteins could bind specific RNA motifs, thereby leading to mediating transport, stabilization, and subcellular localization of miRNAs. For instance, hnRNPA2B1, hnRNPK, and hnRNPH1 have each been implicated in facilitating the sorting of miRNAs into exosomes through motif recognition or interaction with vesicle biogenesis pathways [36]. Excitingly, the role of selective sorting miR‐375 into CAF‐derived EVs has been investigating in our team.

Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying the loading of specific miRNAs into EVs is critical for advancing their clinical applications. These conclusions also provided a theoretical basis for further seeking for new biomarker in HCC. Here, our data indicated that miR‐375 in plasma EVs helped to improve the diagnostic efficacy of HCC in the training and validation cohorts. Higher EVs miR‐375 expression was specifically observed in BCLC B/C stage HCC patients as compared with BCLC A stage HCC patients. These data highlighted the potential of this exosomal marker to diagnose HCC and predict the recurrence of HCC after hepatectomy. Until now, exosomal miR‐375, as a blood‐based liquid biopsy, is used for detection of lymph node metastasis in patients with high risk early gastric cancer [37], and locally advanced rectal cancer patients [38]. Taken together, these findings and our results support the notion that miR‐375 in circulating EVs may reflect cancer aggressiveness and accordingly be candidate biomarkers for further investigations.

Although miR‐375 tissue upregulation has been associated with inhibition of the PI3K‐Akt pathway and often exhibits a protective effect against metastasis, Meltzer S et al. indicated that these conflicting findings were observed as the seemingly complicated and inexplicable, attributing to the presence, aggressiveness, and treatment responsiveness in several malignancies [38]. In the present study, we demonstrated the reasonable explanation for these similar findings in a particular way.

Additionally, we detected the changes of miR‐375 level in postoperative plasma EVs. As shown in the Figure S6, our data showed that the miR‐375 level was upregulated in postoperative plasma EVs about post‐operation 1 week. Similarly, Li Q et al. compared plasma LINC00152 levels in paired preoperative and postoperative plasma samples to observe the changes after resecting gastric neoplasm. The results showed that the plasma LINC00152 levels were further increased in postoperative plasma samples. We speculated that operations may stimulate cells to release an amount of miRNAs to the circulation [39], but it needs larger and different timepoints samples to verify. Thus, we further investigated the correlation of miR‐375 in plasma EVs with recurrence of HCC patients in this study. Although the application of plasma EVs‐miR‐375 in precision medicine still remains unclear, we anticipated that future investigation on the level of plasma EVs‐miR‐375 in HCC patients would be helpful for choosing the best therapy for these patients.

Overall, our findings enhanced our understanding of the miR‐375/RASA1/Ras/Erk axis function as a positive mediator in tumor angiogenesis, leading to worse prognosis. More importantly, miR‐375 in plasma EVs was detected in HCC patients, suggesting its potential application in predicting HCC recurrence and choosing precision therapy (Figure 6).

CAFs could transfer EVs miR‐375 into endothelial cells with a low level of RASA1 and further promote EC angiogenesis. Plasma EVs miR‐375 have the potential values as a novel diagnostic and prognostic biomarker for HCC.
