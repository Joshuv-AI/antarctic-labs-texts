A qualitative study on tobacco- use associated behavior and major influencing factors among youths from indigenous communities of Odisha, India
Authors: Jagatdarshi Badamali, Kanhu Charan Satapathy, Prasanna Kumar Patra, Subhendu Kumar Acharya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTobacco use is a significant public health concern globally. In India, tobacco-related behaviors are increasing in a worrying pattern and such burden is highly prevalent among tribal and indigenous populations. This present study, conducted in Odisha, the state with the highest number of tribal communities in India, examines the factors that explain tobacco use behavior, its initiation, and continuation among two of the largest tribes in Odisha: the Santal and Bhumij communities.MethodologyAn exploratory cross-sectional qualitative study was conducted using ethnographic approaches such as in-depth interviews and participant observation among individuals from Santal and Bhumij indigenous communities aged 10 years and older and up to 30 years. A total of 13 in-depth interviews were conducted and used for the final analysis for this study.ResultsFindings of the present study indicates excitement to experience, family influences, and lack of family control were key factors influencing the initiation of tobacco among children and youths. Peer pressure was observed of playing a significant role among the young adults. Individual perceptions and affordability were reasons for continuing with local tobacco products like bidi, Dukuta, Khaini. Cultural practices and festivals occasions were found to be major circumstances for tobacco use initiation and heavy consumption.DiscussionThe present research highlights the tobacco use associated with major social and cultural factors by discussing the scope for tobacco control among tribal youths of two major tribes in India. The findings also indicate the high scope for developing tobacco control-associated implementation design and tobacco control interventions in these indigenous communities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26837-z.Keywords: Smokeless Tobacco, Anthropology and addiction, Substance use; Addiction & Tribe, Cultural aspect of tobacco use

Introduction

Tobacco use and its impact on public health have emerged as a major preventable cause of disease around the globe [1]. Tobacco consumption is leading to premature mortality in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including India [2]. India is the second largest producer and consumer of tobacco in the world and accounts for around one-sixth of tobacco-related deaths [3]. Evidence shows that 1.3 billion people are using tobacco products globally, and the annual death rate is around six million [4]. In India, according to the World Health Organization's factsheet published in 2018, tobacco abuse and addiction killed more than one million people [5]; this is one-sixth of tobacco use-associated deaths worldwide, accounting for 9.5% of all deaths in India.

The prevalence of tobacco consumption in both smoked and smokeless forms is though getting higher in several major states in India, smokeless forms is highly predominant; the most common forms include Khaini, gutka, betel quid with tobacco, and zarda (flavoured chewing tobacco). On the other hand, bidi, hookah and cigarettes are used in smoked tobacco forms [6]. India reports one of the highest rates of oral cancer cases, particularly among males in the world [7–10], along with low birth weight, premature birth, and other lethal conditions, including neonatal deaths due to parental tobacco uses particularly among the mothers [11, 12].

It has been found that tobacco use is significantly higher among vulnerable populations and the poor, particularly among the scheduled tribe populations [13–16]. However, most studies are quantitative, and there is scant evidence around the reasons, pathways, and processes of addiction to tobacco use in individual or community settings in India. Major national-level large surveys as well as other primary studies reported common tobacco-associated risk factors like gender, age, education, and employment [17, 18]. Therefore, it is necessary to explore the qualitative aspects of different tobacco-associated behaviors to gain a deeper understanding of the cultural and social underpinnings. The present study provides some of the earliest qualitative evidence from the state of Odisha and tribal populations in India. So, the findings and observations of the study carry a lot of significance.

The objectives of this present study are: 1) To understand the tobacco use behaviour in the study populations; 2) To qualitatively explore the various socio-cultural, economic and behavioral influencing factors defining tobacco consumption in the selected populations.

Materials and methods

Study design, study setting and area and people

This cross-sectional study was conducted in two ethnic groups, the Bhumij and Santal tribes, in selected districts, such as Balasore and Mayurbhanj of Odisha (Fig. 1). Odisha, the present state of interest, is a major and economically backwards state in Eastern India. The state has significant populations of Scheduled Castes (17.13%), Scheduled Tribes (22.84%) and other vulnerable groups [13]. Odisha has been grappling with the challenges of high tobacco use, and as per the GATS-Odisha factsheet 2017, the state stands at the top in overall tobacco use among all the states in major state categories, with > 45% population having some form of tobacco consumption habit [14]. The state reports a typical double burden of high poverty and extensive tobacco use in the general population. Paradoxically, there is very little evidence available about tobacco use among various communities in the state.

Location map indicating the selected study area

The selected communities are two major tribes of Odisha state and live in large populations in the northern part of the state. These two tribal populations of Odisha mostly maintain their livelihood by adopting various traditional means [19]. The studied communities primarily depend on various forest products and cultivation. Beyond this, the Bhumij community depends on khadan (local stone mine work), while Santals work as migrant labourers as a secondary income source to earn their livelihood during non-agricultural seasons.

The study focuses on identifying perceptions of the study participants around tobacco habits by defining major factors influencing the tobacco habits of the tribes’ men and women.

Badamali et al., 2022, in their study, reported heavy tobacco consumption among these two tribes, with 54.2% of youth having a tobacco habit, primarily in smokeless form [20]. Both the studied populations were found to use traditional and locally made tobacco; people collect tobacco leaves from their gardens or the local Haat (Local daily market). The tobacco leaves were available in the local market in processed form; after procuring, the leaves were crushed by adding lime before being consumed. On the other hand, the leaves collected from their garden were fermented first by using mahuli (a liquor prepared from a local flower called Mahula—Madhuca indica and Madhuca longifolia). Then, those fermented leaves are dried. Before consuming, they add lime and grind it on their palm or in a stone grinder. This practice was the most traditional or indigenous tobacco use in the study area.

Study sample

Examining the quantitative findings, field notes, ethnographic accounts from fieldwork, and preliminary observations, several important research focuses emerged that require a qualitative investigation. We undertook a pilot study around the emerging qualitative interests and research questions. As a result, the present qualitative study was designed with the defined objectives. The study samples were randomly derived from the initial quantitative study involving a total of 210 participants (Table 1) from the selected two communities [20]. The participants for the study belonged to the age group of 10 years old to 30 years. Data collection continued until saturation was reached. A total of 13 in-depth interviews were randomly undertaken for the present study. Given the sociocultural similarities due to the cohabiting nature of the selected study population and the exploratory qualitative design, this sample size adequately achieved the data saturation.

Data collection

All the in-depth interviews were conducted in the field through direct participation. The first author J.B. conducted the interviews. Before undertaking the in-depth interviews, all the authors undertook fieldwork and collected accounts related to tobacco behaviour in the selected two tribes; this activity involved participant observation and key informant consultation regarding the tobacco use practices among the community members. The investigators participated in the community festivals, local markets and rituals and documented the tobacco use practices on such occasions. The ethnographic explorations regarding tobacco use helped generate independent explanations for the selected qualitative purposes regarding tobacco use. The present study was also backed by the identified quantitative indicators influencing tobacco use; these factors were considered while preparing for the qualitative investigation. So, all the authors shared their findings, followed by a rigorous discussion. Finally, all these activities helped us to develop the in-depth interview guide.

The interview guide included a set of open and closed-ended questions on various aspects, including tobacco use types, patterns and history of tobacco use, and factors that reinforced such use (Table 1). The use of in-depth interviews allowed robust findings, combining the personal insights from interviewees, which further enhanced the validity and richness of the study findings; additionally, probes helped to delve into the further details of the experience and possible factors that influenced tobacco use among the participants. The interviews were recorded on the field itself, and field notes were taken. The fieldwork and in-depth interviews were undertaken between 15th Nov 2019 and 12th Feb 2020. Individual cases were explored through a total of 13 in-depth interviews with consenting participants and were documented. A thematic approach was applied to explain specific qualitative research questions.

Following the initial briefing and assent/consent, the in-depth interviews were conducted in the local language (Odia). Each interview was conducted privately with the individual participants, allowing them to freely discuss their understanding, knowledge, concerns and care practices around tobacco habits. The funnelling technique helped in obtaining the data from the participants. After each interview, the researchers debriefed the participants to find out if they had any queries. Each interview spanned from 45 min to one hour. Saturation was attended early due to probable homogeneous socio-cultural patterns and the cohabiting nature of the two tribes.

Inclusion criteria:

The following inclusion criteria were followed while undertaking the qualitative in-depth interviews:

Both male and female participants in the age group of 10 to 30 years and belonging to the Santal and Bhumij tribes

Both male and female participants in the age group of 10 to 30 years and belonging to the Santal and Bhumij tribes

Those who had the habit of taking either or both of the smoked and smokeless tobacco at the time of fieldwork.

Those who had the habit of taking either or both of the smoked and smokeless tobacco at the time of fieldwork.

Data analysis

The recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim and translated into English, coded and analyzed for emerging themes (Table 2). Two authors, namely JB and SKA transcribed and translated the interview verbatim after the interview was completed. Transcripts were analyzed using thematic content analysis. We also analysed the observations from our field notes. All the authors participated in the data analysis. After reading each transcript, the authors reviewed and analyzed the findings. A two-stage process was followed to capture the meaning behind the transcribed text to develop major themes. The researchers reviewed the transcribed verbatim to generate the initial codes with an open coding process while additional codes emerged from the interview guide. The researchers followed a deductive approach to explore the new themes. In the second stage, the themes were reviewed by the researchers and grouped into categories. We triangulated the data sources where the findings of the interviews were validated by field notes and independent ethnographic findings by the researchers. During data analysis, intercoder agreement between two independent investigators was attended through details discussion helping consistency and reduced subjectivity. The reporting of this study follows the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist for reporting qualitative research [21].

Ethical statement

The study obtained ethical approval from the Department Review Board, P.G. Department of Anthropology, Utkal University, Bhubaneswar, Odisha. Initially, we established rapport with the community by engaging with local leaders, including village council members and political representatives like ward members. Later, after a detailed consultation, informed consent was obtained from them to undertake the study in the community. We also utilized the service of a local language translator when required. We later discussed with individual community members for their support and participation in the study. The objectives of the research and methods and outcomes were explained in the first step. We took the consent of individual participants. All the participants who voluntarily participated were considered for the study. Informed consent was taken from the participants before the original commencement of the study. Assent was obtained from children/adolescents less than 18 years of age along with their parents' consent orally/in written form as applicable.

While knowing the sociodemographic determinants [20] is important, the present qualitative study tried to explore how these factors act along with subjective aspects such as curiosity about tobacco use, peer group influence, lack of family control, in the community setting. Such understanding will also help explain the pathway of initiation and continuance of the tobacco use habit. We have discussed such qualitative factors around tobacco habit in the following section.

Factors leading to tobacco consumption initiation among the participants in the study areas

In the present study, we found several factors causing the initiation of tobacco consumption. In 7 of the 13 or more than half of the cases, teenage (between the ages of 12 years to 16 years) was observed as the primary age for initiation of tobacco consumption. However, the family situation and peer group have exclusive roles to play in initiation of tobacco habit among adolescents and youths. The early experiences due to the lack of family control were observed leading these people to habituate to tobacco habit early. Peer pressure was also observed as the other major and crucial factor in this process which happened through the influence of close friends and older people in the peer group. Evidence also suggested that local festivals were the major hubs encouraging tobacco consumption habits; on the other hand, the continuation of tobacco use increased with the availability at home. In a few instances, we made an important observation during this qualitative exploration, where we understood that tobacco use was an outcome of efforts to cope with hunger (Table 4).

Triggering factors for initiating tobacco consumption

There were several factors observed to trigger the use of both smoked and smokeless forms of tobacco; some of such factors are a) Social initiation and experimentation b) Peer Influence and social belonging c) Lack of parental guidance d) Community-level and gender difference in tobacco use among the tribes e) Local game and festival as Cultural spaces as point of initiation of tobacco. These are the major factors influencing tobacco use initiation in both the studied populations.

Social initiation and experimentation

Personal interview conducted in the studied area reflected that many of the students consumed tobacco as they had seen the elderly people in the village consuming it. So, when they got a chance to explore this, mainly while going to school or college, they consumed tobacco regularly. This was also to exhibit self as an adult or mature person. The present observation among young adults showed that they smoked cigarettes in their group and they did it experimentally initially; this excitement for the experience of smoking and chewing tobacco later became a habit with repeated experimentation with different groups. Most of the students expressed that the feeling of experiencing and experimenting with tobacco ended up forming the addictive habit.

I don’t use tobacco always, but sometimes I smoke cigarettes with my friends, so we will be in a group there. Sometimes I also use alcohol as it is prepared at home, but nobody in my family has the information.

(21-year-old Bhumij male).

When our friends are in a group during a festival or in college, all will feel bad and teased if someone is not consuming tobacco .

(Two Santal students (of 18–20 years) of a college-going group).

The participants primarily used the packed form of tobacco as it was readily and easily available and did not smell or taste pungent and unpleasant like the traditional form of tobacco. However, the elderly people of the village took the smokeless form of tobacco frequently like 15 to 20 times a day and keep it in their mouth for longer duration.

Peer influence and social belonging

Some of the respondents in the studied communities replied that they started tobacco use, as family members and community peers were using tobacco to feel better. The respondents started the tobacco consumption practice in smokeless form with the twig of the tobacco leaf. Later, they got habituated to this with nicotine dependency behavior and addiction.

My sister-in-law has advised me to chew dukuta, so I can do more work constantly in the agricultural field.

(A 30-year-old Bhumij female).

Some of my family members were using and encouraged me to chew tobacco so that I can get some relief from my pain .

(A 27-year-old Santal male).

Tobacco use was also observed as an occupational exposure. Particularly, respondents in the driving profession consumed all forms of tobacco along with alcohol. According to them, the fellow senior drivers drank alcohol and took tobacco. They convinced them that this habit helped them not to get any pain and exertion, allowing better sleep and attentiveness on duty. Additionally, some typical practices promoting increased use of tobacco and alcohol were seen with betting practice with peers; further, the challenges like finishing a cigarette in one puff or a bottle of alcohol in one go, were observed to be leading causes of addiction.

My friends bet with me; can you finish the entire bottle? So, I took it as a challenge and many a time did do it.

(A 25-year-old Bhumij male tractor driver).

We bet in our cab stands to complete one round of carom game without spitting tobacco, and it feels good when we complete the game without spitting .

(A 22 years driver from the Santal tribe).

Lack of parental guidance

Tobacco consumption among tribal males starting from childhood is quite highly prevalent. Elders were observed of putting these children in the habit of tobacco in several instances. Several adolescent girls were reported with smokeless tobacco habits and in some other instances, they had both tobacco and alcohol habits as both were available at their homes. They initiate the consumption with the occasional use of tobacco, and later, such behavior is converted into a regular habit. So, the children were also observed of using tobacco for longer duration with higher frequency. Our observation indicates, the tobacco habit is also having a inherent past experience of the participant where tobacco was introduced to him/her to cope with hunger and food scarcity.

I feel well by chewing dukuta, khaini, pana; also, I don’t feel hungry and feel free to play with friends.

(A 10-year-old child from Santal tribe).

She is consuming tobacco as it is available at home.

(The Bhumij mother aged 26 years of an 11 years child).

Her mother herself gives her dukuta (home-prepared tobacco) to consume.

(The Bhumij aunt aged 29 years of a Santal child (11 years) with tobacco habit).

Nicotine dependency and the studied community

Around one-sixth of the Indian population exhibits substance use habits [22]. In the studied context, we observed that tobacco use starts as an occasional happening in the participant’s life, primarily as part of social sharing, friendship, peer activity, cultural adherence, or celebration; all these occasional uses later got converted into the habit in the majority of the cases. In the studied area, the FTND scale (Fagerstrom Test for Nicotine Dependence) for smokeless tobacco was administered, showing the high nicotine dependence in the studied population. It showed that 35.2% of the studied population consumed tobacco-dip within the first 5 min after waking up. In the studied populations, significant sections were found with the moderate to high dependency category; such participants reported that they kept the tobacco in their mouth the whole night during sleep. Community-level gender difference in tobacco use among the tribes

The present study found a gender-based difference in tobacco habits; the young Bhumij females were observed using traditional forms of smokeless tobacco regularly, while their counterparts in the Santal community mainly reported avoiding consuming tobacco. Furthermore, within the Santal community, elderly females of > 40 years consumed both tobacco and alcohol, while the younger females avoided any such consumption habit. On the other hand, the Bhumij females were found using tobacco in all age groups. Here, it may be highlighted that the Santals are among the most modernized tribes in India. There is an ongoing large-scale lifestyle change and adaptation to nontribal and caste cultural practices, mostly influenced by the local caste communities [22, 23]. This ‘Sanskritisation’ [24] is seen as the response to the stimuli to fulfil the aspiration to look at par with ‘modern’ society or those societies perceived to be advanced; such aspiration, in many cases, is influenced by the emerging stigma of tribal identity in several contexts. In nontribal, caste and traditional societies, tobacco use among younger women is considered a stronger taboo than that of boys. Additionally, occupational opportunities in the case of younger Santal women were observed to be restricted to household and owned agricultural activities in a majority of families, which limits their independent access to money and buying capacity. Their Bhumij counterparts, on the other hand, do not face such social control, and they participate in daily wage activities, which ensures their buying capacity, allowing their tobacco habits to continue.

Not that much of females are chewing tobacco in our community. Females shouldn’t use tobacco.

(A 29-year-old Santal female).

Local game and festival as Cultural spaces as point of initiation of tobacco

In the Santal community, cockfighting is a significant entertainment activity; places facilitating cockfighting and community gambling were observed as major sites for tobacco initiation. Respondents replied that they usually went to these places with their friends and in groups. They get involved in betting, get money on winning, and pay the contestants for losses. They consume alcohol on loss to avoid loss-associated anxiety and on winning to celebrate. In both cases, the alcoholic behavior triggers tobacco use. Some respondents replied that they consumed only traditional forms of tobacco as they did not have much money.

Yes, I used to go bet in cockfighting to pandupani festival and the earnings from there are used in my family.

(A 28-year-old Santal male).

Honestly, we don’t have money with us for that we don’t consume tobacco other than dukuta khaini (homemade smokeless type).

A 20 years Bhumij male

I never smoke there, the only thing I chew dukuta ( traditionally home prepapred ) , because smoking leads to T . B .

(A 30-year-old Bhumij male)

Discussion

The present study, conducted among the Indian tribal youth communities, specifically the Santal and Bhumij, aimed to explore the status of tobacco use-associated behaviors. The study also examined the major risk factors associated with the initiation and continuation of this behavior. In the Indian context, it has been observed that determinants like poor education, age, occupation, and alcohol consumption have remained the major associated factors for tobacco use in India [25–27]. The present study also found that these risk factors have a significant association with tobacco use among the youths in the two studied tribal communities. Beyond, most of the tribesmen suffered from chronic poverty. Previous studies suggested that the scheduled tribe youths are at a major risk for tobacco use disorder behavior in India than other communities, which was also reported during the present study [13, 26, 28]. Our ethnographic observations and informal discussion explained a large-scale tobacco use among the youths of the selected communities. Similar findings were also observed in a previous report [29].

The present study shows that the traditional form of tobacco consumption (mostly smokeless tobacco) is highly prevalent among the Santal and Bhumij communities. However, the pattern of tobacco consumption indicates that the usage of the traditional form of tobacco is more prevalent among Bhumijs than the Santals; we observed the economic/occupational perspective and lifestyle change are among the major influencing factors. The Bhumij community was found to be economically weaker and mostly agricultural labourers, while a section of them simultaneously worked in local pathar khadan (stone mines). In stone mines, they mostly sit and work for extended periods. To avoid hypersalivation during working hours and to prevent exertion, they often chew tobacco. Santals were found to be both agricultural and industrial laborers, and also engage in seasonal migration for employment. They go to the nearby towns in search of work in the non-agricultural season, so the packet forms of tobacco available in the market and shops were more visible among them. Though the traditional types of tobacco like dukuta and khaini are also used by the Santals, such use is largely limited to the members from the older age group. Furthermore, the exposure of Santals to modern lifestyle was observed to be higher than any other tribe in Odisha. The critical concern is that the studied populations are largely unaware of the impact of tobacco consumption on their health, and their poor educational status and high dropout rates are further adversely affecting their habits, leading to increased tobacco consumption.

Gambling habits are largely prevalent among tribal people from the selected tribes, and particularly among men. The gambling places were observed to become the major points of tobacco use initiation in the selected tribes. As there is large-scale alcohol abuse in these places, such behavior is also a leading factor in the rise of tobacco use. In the present study area, it is observed that Santal participants went to gambling places for cockfighting; some responded that they, for the first time, started using alcohol and tobacco there only. Due to the high cost of cigarettes, they consume mostly locally made smoked (bidi) and smokeless forms of tobacco (dukuta). While participating in betting, the Bhumijs also used alcohol and tobacco. From sex specific perspectives, it was seen that though elderly tribal women usually took tobacco, followed by alcohol, tribal young women particularly from Santal ethnicity avoided tobacco which was found to be a result of mainstreaming. This, in a way, as Brady (2020) indicates, tobacco as a bad habit is slowly becoming mainstream among tribal females, particularly of the Santal tribe, causing public stigma [30]. Among youths in general, the habit of tobacco consumption was observed to occur in both smokeless and smoked forms primarily at home. In other contexts, it takes place outside the village to avoid being identified by peers [31].

During rituals, local festivals, death or birth ceremonies, the custom of excessive tobacco use, along with alcohol, is prevalent among the selected tribes. The smokeless tobacco was also observed to be rubbed on roosters to increase their performance, which indicates the strong cultural association of tobacco in the communities’ traditions; this practice has also been reported among African tribal communities [32].

We found different patterns in tobacco use in various age groups. It was observed that in the children age group, some started with mouth fresheners and ended up with a tobacco chewing habit, while others began with a small amount of tobacco occasionally and then got habituated over a short period. The tobacco use practices, in both chewing and smoked forms, were different among students and early youths than the adults. The younger section was mostly got influenced by the possible recreational character of tobacco, mostly the packed forms as reported previously [33]; adults were found to consume only the traditional form of tobacco, both in smoked (like Bidi) and in chewed form (like dukuta, khaini). GYTS fourth round report (2019), though indicated the median age at initiation for smoked tobacco is 10.5 years and for smokeless tobacco, it is 9.9 years [34], it was observed to be much lower in the studied populations.

Tribal society is primarily characterized by a subsistence economy, along with strong social dependency, cultural bonding, commitment to shared values, and emotional connection, which are important community characteristics. Such practices inherently pressure members of these groups to remain committed to group activities. As we observed during the field research, such community values emerged as important peer pressure in tobacco habits. Though sporadic, evidence in a few contexts also evolved, indicating food insecurity and hunger escalating the triggers for such tobacco habits.

Tobacco cessation is essential to reduce its associated mortality and morbidity. While we briefly explored institutional facilities, it was found that there are only 13 tobacco de-addiction centers in the whole of the studied state, i.e., Odisha [35] and are highly limited in their presence and functionality. Further, lack of knowledge on the adverse health effects of tobacco use along with this scarcity of healthcare infrastructure are the other major factors. It needs further detailed investigation regarding the health system and associated tobacco control practices, followed by necessary intervention strategy.

Indian tobacco control policy and the vulnerable tribal population

There is an immediate need for intervention to prevent the high tobacco consumption habits, particularly of SLTs, among the tribal communities, especially the vulnerable young tribal populations, considering their high dependency. Similar observation was also reported by another study among tribal communities in Kerala [36]. McKey et al., 2015 in their systematic review highlighted the need for adolescent and youth-based intervention and cessation programs [37]. The WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC) was the first international agreement on tobacco control accepted by the World Health Assembly in 2003. Along with this, India has also formulated the Cigarette and Other Tobacco Product Act (COTPA) to control excessive tobacco use [38]. An additional program, NTCP (National Tobacco Control Program), is also there that aim to decrease the production and supply of tobacco in the market [39]. The Act has rules and provisions to prohibit the production, supply, trade, distribution, and commercial advertisement of tobacco products.

In this context, there is a need for an assessment of public policy to control tobacco abuse among the tribal youths [37]. The findings of NFHS 5 in comparison to NFHS 4, though indicated a limited declining trend in the overall tobacco consumption (Men: 45.5%, NFHS 4 to 40.8%, NFHS 5 and women; 6.8%, NFHS 4 to 4.0%, NFHS 5), the prevalence is still huge [40]. A contrastingly high prevalence among vulnerable tribal communities indicates substantial challenges and low implementation outcome of the tobacco control program [41]. A recent systematic review (Nath et al., 2025) on tobacco habits among Indian tribal communities (involving 28 studies and 56,883 individuals) indicated a strikingly high tobacco prevalence (overall, 60%; Males, 66%; and Females, 42%) [42]. In this context, it is worth noting that Odisha ranks first in the prevalence of tobacco, particularly the SLT, among major states in India (GATS-2). The Government of Odisha enforced COTPA in 2005, while the National Tobacco Control Program (NTCP) piloted it in 2007–08 in 42 districts of 21 states, including Odisha [43, 44]. The NTCP currently has 13 tobacco cessation centers (TCCs) operational in the state, with a coverage of only three tribal dominated districts (Mayurbhanj, Nabarangpur and Koraput) out of at least 14 tribal dominated districts. The TCCs would motivate tobacco users to quit and, side by side, will provide therapeutic support to overcome their habit and addiction [45]. These facilities would conduct an intensified campaign at the community level to educate people on tobacco's effects and how tobacco consumption is associated with other comorbid conditions and diseases like cancer, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and diabetes [46].

In the above context, we introduce some of the suggested measures. Tobacco being a foremost adverse practice among tribal individuals across gender and age, it is important to prioritise the design and implementation of culturally appropriate and adoptable tobacco control measures among them with a priority focus on children and youth. An aggressive approach is required in this direction [35]. For example, there is a need for establishing additional TCCs in tribal dominated areas with intensified efforts for tobacco control at the community level. It is highly important that steps are taken to ensure and strengthen the implementation of the various tobacco control policies discussed above in tribal-dominated areas [46–48]. Effective behavioural change models with target group interventions like schools, colleges, and workplaces carry higher potential for successful outcomes than simple community outreach and mass media campaigns [49]. Furthermore, addressing various undue promotions, noncompliance with the proper presentation of tobacco-associated risks on tobacco packs, and various harmful marketing strategies employed by both formal and local-informal tobacco-producing industries are additional concerns in these underprivileged settings, which need to be addressed [48]. School-based interventions have high potentials for tobacco control and such activities may also include parents/guardians and school teachers for successful, rapid and sustainable outcomes. By ensuring control over production, supply and easy accessibility, such aims can be achieved to a significant extent [35, 37]. Considering the excess use and its health adversities as observed in the present study and in previous evidence, the above points will be a highly effective way to protect these vulnerable groups.

Significance and limitations of the present study

Tobacco use is the most significant cause of morbidity and mortality globally. India is the second-largest tobacco consumer, and such behavior is highly prevalent in Odisha. As we find, tobacco use among tribal communities has strong socio-behavioral and cultural roots. Screening and raising awareness regarding the association of poor oral hygiene and tobacco consumption with precancerous lesions and potentially malignant disorders are important. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first such study that brings in the qualitative findings of triggering factors on tobacco consumption initiation and its continuation in tribal children and youths. Due to the limited period, it was not possible to cover aspects like the detailed tobacco consumption pattern, and details pathways due to the cross-sectional nature of the study. The sample selection is also a limitation; it was restricted to specific geographic locations. The findings, therefore, also cannot be generalized, though they give important inputs to explore tobacco use habits in community settings in future studies. However, we would mention here the risks of translation bias, which could potentially affect the nuanced responses of participants. Therefore, we believe that further longitudinal investigation may help to understand the various determinants, including beliefs and perceptions about continuing tobacco use and such pathways, among indigenous communities.
