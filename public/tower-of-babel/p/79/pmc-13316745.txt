Nontuberculous Mycobacteria Pulmonary Disease Prevalence, New York City, 2012 to 2020
Authors: Christopher S. Semancik, Ettie M. Lipner, Doreen J. Addrizzo-Harris, D. Rebecca Prevots
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) pulmonary disease (PD) has been increasing in the United States, and New York City is an important hotspot, with a high burden of disease.Research QuestionHow do neighborhood-level risk factors influence NTM PD prevalence in New York City?Study Design and MethodsWe used outpatient claims data from hospitals participating in the Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute network, compiled by the INSIGHT Clinical Research Network at Weill Cornell Medicine. NTM PD period prevalence (referred to here as prevalence) was estimated by New York City neighborhood for the study period 2012 through 2022. A case was defined as someone with at least 1 claim for NTM PD. Deidentified NTM PD case data and demographic data were analyzed by neighborhood of residence at diagnosis. Using prevalence estimates, we detected high- and low-prevalence regions within New York City and associated these estimates with demographic-, clinical-, socioeconomic-, and environmental neighborhood-level factors using Poisson regression and backward elimination of covariates.ResultsOverall, 6,169 NTM PD cases were identified among people receiving care across 17 private New York City hospitals. The mean age was 67.5 years; 68.7% were female, and 57.3% were White. Over the study period, NTM PD prevalence increased throughout New York City, and median year built of housing units, median income, and median age of residents were significant neighborhood-level risk factors. The highest prevalence neighborhoods were in Manhattan, whereas the lowest prevalence neighborhoods were in Brooklyn and Staten Island.InterpretationOur results show that neighborhood-level access to care may explain the heterogeneity in NTM PD prevalence among New York City neighborhoods, because higher income and newer neighborhoods exhibited the highest NTM PD prevalences. Future studies should examine the extent of undetected NTM PD in New York City, particularly in low-income areas.

Take-Home Points

Study Question: Is nontuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) pulmonary disease (PD) associated with clinical, demographic, socioeconomic, and/or environmental factors in New York City neighborhoods?

Results: We found that median year built of housing units, median income, and median age of residents were all significant predictors of NTM PD prevalence; Manhattan neighborhoods showed the highest prevalence, whereas Brooklyn and Staten Island neighborhoods exhibited the lowest prevalence, and NTM PD prevalence increased throughout the study period.

Interpretation: We report that these neighborhood-level risk factors for NTM PD are indicators for access to care, and there is likely a burden of undetected NTM PD in New York City, particularly in low-income neighborhoods.

Nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) are bacteria ubiquitous in the environment and may cause chronic lung disease. Incidence and prevalence of NTM pulmonary disease (PD) have been increasing in the US and globally.1, 2, 3 The Northeastern region of the US has a high burden of NTM PD, particularly for Mycobacterium avium complex.3,4 This burden is particularly high in New York City and New York State.5 Two previous studies have identified NTM PD clusters in New York City in both cystic fibrosis and older adult populations.6,7 The costs of NTM PD in New York are estimated to be approximately $48.6 million per year, posing a large-scale health and economic challenge.4 The availability of data relevant to NTM PD from the Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute, specifically the INSIGHT Clinical Research Network (CRN), which includes patients from across New York City, allowed us to study neighborhood-level demographic and environmental factors associated with NTM PD to better understand patterns and trends of this condition in New York City.

Study Design and Methods

Study Population

For this retrospective cohort study, the study population comprised patients receiving health care at hospitals which participate in the INSIGHT CRN through the Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute.8 The INSIGHT CRN aggregates deidentified data from medical records for patients receiving care at any of the 17 participating private hospitals throughout New York City. The study population was further restricted to patients with International Classification of Diseases (ICD), Ninth and 10th Revision codes for NTM PD (ICD-9 code 031.0 or ICD-10 code A31.0) for the study period 2009 through 2023. Demographic and clinical information was extracted for all patients with an NTM PD code, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, zip code of residence, and BMI. These patient-level data were extracted for descriptive purposes, but only neighborhood-level data were used in modeling analyses. Data regarding mycobacteriology results were not available in this data set. If patients had multiple claims, only the first claim was used for calculating period prevalence and annual point prevalence. We were limited by record availability in this descriptive study of a fixed set of patients, and because we used all patient records available, sample size calculations were not relevant. Data were deidentified, and were therefore classified as not human patients research, under the 2018 Health and Human Services Common Rule (45 CFR 46.102(e)[1](ii), 45 CFR 46.116 (b)[9](i)), exempt from institutional review board review.

Data Sources

Our primary supplemental data set was the Social Determinants of Health database from the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality. This data set contains data by zip code with median values for the following variables: year built of housing units, income, Gini index (a measure of income inequality that describes deviation of incomes from a perfectly geometric distribution), age, and distribution of race/ethnicity, sex, and total population in each zip code from 2012 through 2020. This period corresponds to the visit years for most NTM PD cases in the data set (about 72%). These data were extracted from annual surveys conducted by the US Census Bureau.9

Because race/ethnicity is generally a risk indicator rather than a risk factor, we diagrammed the way in which environmental risk or access to health care could be mediated through self-described race/ethnicity. Namely, racial/ethnic group is highly correlated with income (r = 0.71, P = 0.00000017 in the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality Social Determinants of Health data set), which is in turn associated with neighborhood of residence. Therefore, the association with environmental factors is confounded and differentially modulated by socioeconomic status factors. A second data source was OpenData NYC. We used multiple data sets from this large data repository, including the following: the Department of Health and Mental Hygiene’s data set describing air quality of particulate matter concentration < 2.5 μg/m3 by zip code,10 (2) the Department of Health and Mental Hygiene’s data set describing the number of water tank violations by zip code,11 and (3) the Department of Buildings’ data set describing the total number of boiler safety violations by zip code.12

Data Analysis and Risk Factor Assessment

Zip codes were grouped into 1 of 42 New York City United Hospital Fund (UHF) neighborhoods, as defined by the New York City Department of Health (e-Fig 1).13 Prevalence by UHF was estimated by dividing cases from each UHF identified within INSIGHT by the INSIGHT-identified catchment area population for a given UHF. The catchment area denominator was obtained by estimating the number of people living in each New York City zip code who had visited an INSIGHT CRN hospital throughout the duration of the study period (2009-2023). We then aggregated all zip code data to the UHF level by adding corresponding estimates for all zip codes comprising each UHF. Rather than assuming people from each neighborhood visited INSIGHT CRN hospitals with frequencies proportionate to their neighborhood’s population, using catchment area estimates as our denominators provided a more representative estimate of neighborhoods where people were truly coming from to seek treatment for NTM PD or other conditions at one of the 17 INSIGHT CRN hospitals. This reduced potential bias that may result from the fact that INSIGHT CRN hospitals are located primarily in Manhattan.

To identify significant risk factors for pulmonary NTM PD at the neighborhood level, Poisson regression models were built including covariates with sufficient neighborhood-resolution data and biological plausibility. We standardized all covariates and included the median values for the following neighborhood-level variables in the model: year built of housing units, income, age, percent White, percent female, and Gini index. If any component zip code had missing data, that zip code was assigned the overall neighborhood median for that variable. To estimate prevalence, we included a logged offset variable for UHF catchment population. After including all relevant covariates in a Poisson model, we used backward elimination to select significant covariates. ORs, 95% CIs, and P values were estimated from the adjusted Poisson regression models.

We also estimated annual and average annual prevalence for the years 2012 to 2020, representing 9 of the 15 years for which we had NTM PD diagnoses from INSIGHT CRN hospitals (2009-2023) and a window during which 72% of the NTM PD cases were represented. Because we did not have yearly catchment area data (only overall catchment area data for the overall study period), we used US Census-derived yearly zip code populations to obtain a denominator for this prevalence calculation. We again aggregated zip code populations to the UHF level by adding each zip code’s population with each of the component zip codes’ populations in a particular UHF. We then estimated prevalence per 100,000 population by dividing the NTM PD cases in a particular year from INSIGHT CRN hospitals (using the year of the ICD code from each UHF to derive each yearly prevalence numerator) by a UHF’s population in a given year. Because we did not have catchment area data by year, these yearly prevalences may underrepresent the true annual burden of NTM PD throughout New York City. R (Version 4.3.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) was used for analysis. Choropleth maps were created using ArcGIS Pro. (Redlands, CA: Esri Inc.)

During the study period, the annual NTM PD prevalence increased from 5.7 per 100,000 in 2012 to a peak of 11.1 per 100,000 in 2018 and 8.2 per 100,000 in 2020. Overall, the average annual prevalence was 8.9 per 100,000 (Fig 2). Our final model included 3 significant predictors of New York City neighborhood prevalence: median year built of housing units, income, and age of residents. Each SD increase in median neighborhood year built of housing units (SD, 11.47 years) was associated with a 12% increase in NTM PD prevalence odds (OR, 1.12; 95% CI, 1.07-1.18). Furthermore, each SD increase in median neighborhood income (SD, $27,928.93) was associated with a 9% increase in NTM PD prevalence odds (OR, 1.09; 95% CI, 1.04-1.14). Discussion

Our study found that median income was among the most important predictors of neighborhood NTM PD prevalence. This finding is likely explained by neighborhood-level access to care. The medical centers represented in the INSIGHT CRN are all private hospitals, and typically do not have a robust network of ambulatory clinics or providers that accept Medicaid programs or uninsured patients. Thus, people living in lower-income neighborhoods are less likely to be diagnosed with NTM PD and treated at these hospitals. Notably, NTM PD prevalence estimated in this study likely underestimates true neighborhood prevalence because these cases represent only those who were diagnosed at an INSIGHT CRN hospital and not those individuals with NTM PD who remain undiagnosed. Our finding is consistent with prior literature reporting higher income and education as risk factors for NTM PD.7

Another significant predictor of NTM PD prevalence in our model was the median year built of housing units, where higher (more recent) median construction years were associated with higher NTM PD prevalence. Although we hypothesized that neighborhoods with older buildings (with earlier median construction years) may have older pipes, plumbing, and facilities that could promote the growth of NTM, we found instead that neighborhoods with newer buildings (with later or larger median construction years) experienced higher NTM PD prevalence. This association may also be a proxy for income. In addition, areas with newer buildings may have more ongoing construction, potentially contributing to higher particulate matter concentrations. High particulate matter concentrations in these locations could increase the presence of NTM and other expelled particles, leading to higher rates of lung disease and symptoms that lead people to seek treatment and develop conditions predisposing them to NTM PD. Finally, as expected, we observed that median neighborhood age of residents was the most significant risk factor, reflecting the importance of older age in the risk of NTM PD.14 The spatial variation in NTM PD prevalence by neighborhood was strongly associated with income. The neighborhoods with the highest prevalences of NTM PD are among the highest income neighborhoods in New York City, whereas the lowest prevalence neighborhoods had median incomes below New York City’s median.

Overall, NTM PD prevalence increased during the study period, consistent with trends in the US and globally for similar time periods.1, 2, 3 The slight decrease from 2018 onward is attributable to declining outpatient visits and health care utilization during the COVID-19 pandemic, not an underlying decrease in NTM infections. The highest annual prevalences in our study are somewhat lower than the estimated incidences presented by Bodle et al15 in New York City in 2008 (17.7 per 100,000 people), perhaps because data from this study were limited to only 1 hospital that saw high volumes of NTM PD cases at that time, and because the authors used a microbiologic case definition which is more sensitive. The increasing burden of NTM PD through increasing incidence and prevalence rates has also coincided with increasing awareness and diagnostic techniques.16,17 However, even despite increasing awareness of NTM PD in previous years, the clinical practice of testing and diagnosing patients continues to vary widely, even in high-income countries like the US.18 Although higher-income areas have been reported to have higher prevalence of NTM PD,7,19 another study found that social and economic disadvantage in the state of Wisconsin was associated with increased risk of NTM pulmonary infections.20 Limited access to NTM-specific care in lower-income groups may hinder diagnosis and treatment in these communities, resulting in underestimates of NTM PD prevalence in these low-income neighborhoods.21

This study has multiple strengths. First, we used catchment area data as our denominator when estimating the neighborhood period prevalence rates in Poisson regression models. Because the 17 private CRN hospitals were clustered in Manhattan and Brooklyn, it was important to use an estimate of how many people from each neighborhood were being seen in 1 of these hospitals and not referred from other hospitals. This approach minimizes the amount of bias from not having hospital data from every New York City hospital, and represents a less biased approach than simply using the population of each neighborhood as our denominator.

Our study also has several limitations. The INSIGHT CRN represents data from only 17 private hospitals within New York City, most of which are in Manhattan and higher-income areas. Even though we used catchment area data to reduce bias in referral patterns, the study population represents primarily a higher-income population, rather than all hospitals, both public and private, from throughout New York City. Nonetheless, because using ICD codes to identify NTM PD cases has a low sensitivity,14 we are likely underestimating the true NTM PD prevalence and estimating prevalence only of those cases which are diagnosed at an INSIGHT CRN hospital.

Interpretation

We found that neighborhood-level socioeconomic status is a significant predictor of NTM PD prevalence, likely because it is an indicator for access to care. Because our study area represented a small geographic area with little variation of environmental factors, we were not able to fully incorporate factors related to the built or natural environment. Our results lead us to hypothesize that there may be some burden of undetected NTM PD in New York City, particularly in low-income neighborhoods. Therefore, the confirmed NTM PD cases from INSIGHT CRN hospitals reflect only those cases which have been diagnosed in these hospitals and not the true underlying NTM PD prevalence in New York City neighborhoods. Future studies should specifically examine older adult populations in low-income areas to identify the burden of NTM PD. Although much work remains to expand clinical care, including diagnosis and treatment, progress has been made recently. Formed by the Bronchiectasis and NTM Association, the US Bronchiectasis and NTM Care Center Network currently has 33 sites which aim to standardize practices and education on bronchiectasis and NTM PD diagnosis among providers, identify gaps and unmet needs, increase access to care, and serve underrepresented and underdiagnosed communities throughout the country.22
