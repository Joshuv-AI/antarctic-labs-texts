A new SNP comparison (SnpC) method for detecting unique/enriched gene-SNP variants and comparing population gene mutation diversity
Authors: Zhanshan (Sam) Ma, Lianwei Li, Ya-Ping Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComparing mutation patterns and population-specific genetic signatures, such as single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), is of fundamental importance in genetic and genomic studies today. The problem is challenging because it falls into the category of NP-hard problems, meaning that the computational time increases exponentially, or even faster, as the problem size grows. Additionally, existing approaches often fails to account for the exact number of SNPs per gene (gene-SNP abundance) nor their distribution across populations, hindering the detection of genes that are uniquely or richly mutated in specific groups.ResultsTo bridge this gap, by harnessing gene-SNP abundance and distribution information as well as their heterogeneity across individuals and populations, we develop the SnpC (SNP comparison) method (framework) for detecting genes with unique or enriched SNPs, referred to as unique genes (UGs) and enriched genes (EGs) for specific population. The power of SnpC lies in two novel metrics: Gene-SNP Heterogeneity (GSH), which identifies genes with unique or enriched number of SNPs in one population compared to others, and Population Gene-SNP Diversity (PGSD), which measures the population-level diversity of gene mutations including SNP. These metrics are coupled with robust permutation tests to provide statistical significance.ConclusionsWe demonstrate SnpC on data from the 1000 Genomes Project, where it successfully catalogs population-specific (unique/enriched) genes and quantifies their inter-population mutation diversity pattern. SnpC delivers a unique, actionable output for gene prioritization in association studies and comparative population genomic analysis. It is easily extended to other variation types, and the R code is provided in the article's supplements.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12859-026-06381-8.

Background

Structural variants (SVs) and single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are fundamental to human genetic diversity, playing critical roles in various diseases and accounting for the majority of nucleotide differences between individuals. Sudmant et al. [38] demonstrated this by analyzing SVs across 26 populations, creating a comprehensive catalogue that revealed population-specific variants and their enrichment in disease-associated haplotypes. The accurate detection of these variants is therefore a cornerstone of genetic research. SNP arrays have evolved into versatile platforms for this purpose due to their cost-effectiveness [16], and significant efforts have been made to improve SNP calling across different contexts, from polyploid plants [9] to single-cell sequencing data with tools like Monopogen [10].

The applications of SNP analysis are vast and critical for advancing biomedical science. Researchers have focused on developing SNP-based diagnostic panels for early disease detection in specific populations [3], automated tools for extracting SNP-disease associations from literature [39], and methods for SNP enrichment analysis in post-GWAS research [31]. Furthermore, the unique roles of rare genetic variants in complex diseases underscore the need for improved functional impact estimation [28]. This has been facilitated by increasingly sophisticated variant callers, from early Bayesian inference engines like POLYBAYES [25] to modern deep learning approaches like DeepVariant [32]. The scope extends to pathogen genomics [13], fine-mapping functional variants [37], identifying splicing quantitative trait loci (sQTLs) [44], reviewing miR-SNPs in cancer [4], and understanding the role of noncoding variants in disease [29]. Tools like iGDA also address challenges in detecting minor variants from long-read data [12], and pathway-driven methods like GSEA-SNP combine SNP analysis with gene set enrichment [15].

A persistent challenge within this field is the systematic identification of genes with unique or enriched SNP profiles across populations. Despite the long-standing attention given to identifying differential genetic variations, an effective approach to this specific problem remains elusive due to inherent technical challenges. This task is computationally formidable, falling into the category of NP-hard problems, where the resources required for an optimal solution grow exponentially with problem size, akin to the multiple sequence alignment (MSA) problem [11, 40, 41]. This exponential growth makes it impractical even for supercomputers to solve moderate-sized problems optimally. Most computer scientists and mathematicians agree that NP-hard problems are equivalent; solving one in polynomial time implies all are solvable, but a polynomial-time solution for NP-hard problems is generally thought not to exist.

Given the equivalence among NP-hard problems, another more intuitive NP-hard problem, the traveling salesman problem (TSP), illustrates this complexity well. The problem, which seeks the shortest possible path visiting each town once and returning to the start, becomes exponentially harder with more towns. In the 1950s and 1960s, RAND Corporation held the record for solving TSP with 49 towns; today, the record stands at 85,900 towns. These records represent exact solutions without approximations. When small deviations are acceptable (e.g., 2–3% from the optimal solution), modern supercomputers can handle millions of towns. This suggests that while there are no efficient algorithms to solve NP-hard problems perfectly, heuristic algorithms can provide reasonably good solutions for practical applications [18, 19]. Back to biology, numerous heuristic approaches and software packages developed for MSA [5, 8, 17, 33, 36] are so effective that most biologists are satisfied with their speed, except when dealing with extremely large genomes. Thus, while NP-hard problems pose significant theoretical challenges, practical, approximate solutions often suffice for moderate-sized instances in real-world applications.

In the case of SNP variant identification, a powerful yet underutilized heuristic is to leverage the distribution information of SNPs alongside their abundance. The SNP distribution or heterogeneity across genes, individuals, and populations may contain varying levels of signals and significance, which are crucial for detecting unique or enriched gene SNPs. To the best of our knowledge, no existing approaches for comparing SNP patterns have comprehensively considered both gene-SNP abundance and distribution to fill this specific research gap.

Inspired by the species specificity concept introduced by Mariadassou et al. [27] and its various applications (e.g., [20, 22–24]), we have developed the SNP Comparison (SnpC) methodology (framework). This framework is designed to detect genes with unique SNPs within a population (referred to as UG or unique genes) and to differentiate genes with enriched SNPs (referred to as EG or enriched genes). The SnpC framework employs a duo of metrics: gene-SNP heterogeneity (GSH) and population gene-SNP diversity (PGSD), along with two associated permutation tests, the GSH permutation (GSHP) test and the PGSD permutation (PGSDP) test.

The GSH metric synthesizes SNP abundance and distribution, providing signals for heuristically identifying UGs and EGs. The GSH permutation (GSHP) algorithm functions as a computational random search, constrained by statistical rigor in terms of the P-value (significance level). These permutation tests and the associated P-value define the stop point for the random search, ensuring that the detected genes indeed have unique or enriched SNPs while minimizing the computational workload required to complete the task effectively and efficiently.

Furthermore, the PGSD metric and the associated PGSD permutation (PGSDP) test differentiate SNP mutation patterns across populations holistically. Together, this framework provides a tool for differentiating SNP patterns at the gene, chromosome, and population levels. The approach can be extended to differentiate other structural variants such as insertions and deletions. We demonstrate the SnpC framework using extensive SNP datasets from the 1000 Genomes Project [38].

The proposed SnpC (SNP comparison) framework comprises a duo of metrics—gene-SNP heterogeneity (GSH) and population gene-SNP diversity (PGSD)—along with their corresponding permutation tests: the GSH permutation test and the PGSD permutation test (Fig. 1). The GSH metric and its associated permutation test are designed to detect and catalog unique genes (UGs, genes with unique SNPs) and enriched genes (EGs, genes with a significantly higher number or richness of SNPs). The PGSD metric and the PGSD permutation test aim to holistically compare groups (such as populations or chromosomes) in terms of their mutation (SNP) diversity. This aspect is considered one of the most important properties of gene mutation patterns.

Gene-SNP heterogeneity (GSH)

Mariadassou et al. [27] characterized species specificity within a spatial framework, such as different habitat locations, as a metric for the uneven distribution (heterogeneity) of a species or taxon across various habitats. This concept of species specificity is significant in demonstrating the variations among habitats concerning their species abundance distributions (i.e., abundance-rank distributions). The specificity continuum can be viewed as a revised form of the specialist-generalist paradigm, which has long been a focus of ecological research. According to the specificity continuum proposed by Mariadassou et al. [27], there are two extremes corresponding to the traditional specialist-generalist classification: (i) taxa that are uniformly abundant across many habitats, referred to as generalists, and (ii) taxa that are exclusively and consistently found in a single habitat, known as specialists.

Gene SNP heterogeneity (GSH) permutation (GSHP) test

To identify treatment effects in the presence of individual differences within treatments (or within-treatment heterogeneity), we developed the Gene SNP Heterogeneity Permutation (GSHP) test algorithm, which distinguishes gene SNP patterns among various populations (treatments). The GSHP test is designed based on standard permutation test principles [30]. It enables the blending of 'false' groups that include different populations, thus reducing the impact of variability within the same population (within-treatment heterogeneity). Additionally, it takes into account the inherent heterogeneity among individuals across diverse populations, and consequently detect the truly altered SNPs across populations.

The following steps describe the GSHP test designed for assessing the significance of the difference between treatments A and B regarding gene SNP heterogeneity (Hi), where A and B represent different populations, such as AFR (African) and AMR (American).

Step (i) Consider that the gene SNP matrix for population A consists of m individuals, while the matrix for population B contains n individuals. Calculate the gene SNP heterogeneity for gene i for both A and B separately using Eq. (1). Next, determine the actual gene SNP heterogeneity difference between A and B, represented as Δ(i) =|Ai − Bi|.

Step (ii) Randomly shuffle the combined (m + n) individuals. Assign the first m individuals as a simulated group A′ and the remaining n individuals as a simulated group B′. Calculate the heterogeneity of gene i for both A' and B', respectively, using Eq. (1). Then, determine the gene SNP difference between A' and B' as Δ′ (i) =|A′i − B′i|.

Step (iii) Perform Step (ii) 1000 times to obtain 1000 sets of Δ′(i). Calculate the P-value for gene i by counting the number of times (n) where Δ(i) ≤ Δ′(i), indicating that the actual gene SNP difference does not exceed the simulated difference. Determine a pseudo-Pi value for gene i as Pi = n/1000. If Pi ≤ 0.05, it implies that the actual difference surpasses the simulated differences, indicating a statistically significant event (i.e., not a small probability event). Conversely, if Pi > 0.05, the difference is considered statistically insignificant.

These categorized gene lists will facilitate a deeper understanding of the differential gene SNP profiles and the molecular mechanisms underlying the differences between populations A and B. Naturally, the implementation of these rules relies on the previously defined GSHP test.

The following steps detail the process of conducting a randomization test to assess the significance of the difference in population gene SNP diversity (PGSD) between populations A and B, where A and B represent distinct populations such as AFR and AMR. The PGSD permutation (PGSDP) test can be applied to each of six gene categories: UG in A, UG in B, EG in A, EG in B, all genes with significant differences in GSH, and all genes.

For each of the six gene categories, calculate the PGSD for groups A and B separately, denoted as PGSD(A) and PGSD(B). Next, determine their difference as follows: Δ = ∣PGSD(A) − PGSD(B)∣. This step computes the observed or actual difference in PGSD between populations A and B.

Step (ii) Utilizing the same permutation procedure as in the previous GSHP test, calculate the PGSD difference =|PGSD′(A) − PGSD′(B)|.

Step (iii) Repeat Step (ii) 1000 times between groups A and B for each of the 1000 simulated permutations, denoted as Δ′ to generate 1000 sets of Δ′. Calculate the P-value for each gene category by counting how many times (n) Δ is less than or equal to Δ′, indicating that the actual PGSD difference does not surpass the simulated difference. Compute a pseudo-P value for each gene category as P = n/1000. If P ≤ 0.05, this indicates that the actual difference exceeds the simulated differences and signifies a statistically significant event. If P > 0.05, the difference is considered statistically insignificant.

It is important to note that the previous PGSD permutation (PGSDP) test can be applied to each of the six previously identified gene categories, with identification based on the previously defined gene SNP heterogeneity permutation (GSHP) test. These six PGSDP tests collectively provide a comprehensive differentiation between treatments A and B.

Results and discussion

GSH (gene-SNP heterogeneity) and GSHP (GSH permutation) test

A comparison of gene single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) heterogeneity (GSH) was conducted among five groups: AFR, AMR, EUR, EAS, and SAS, resulting in a total of 10 pairwise comparisons (see Tables S1–S10). In each comparison, genes showing significant differences in GSH (P < 0.05, FDR-adjusted) were identified alongside those with insignificant differences. The genes with significant differences were then classified into four categories: unique genes (UG)—genes with distinct SNP mutations—in the first group, unique genes in the second group, SNP-enriched genes (EG)—genes with a significantly higher number of SNPs—in the first group, and EGs in the second group. For example, in the AFR vs. AMR comparison, a UG in the AFR population indicates that the gene only exhibited SNP mutations in AFR and not in AMR. Conversely, an EG in the first group suggests that it had significantly more SNPs in AFR than in AMR. The UG/EG in the latter population is defined in the opposite manner compared to those in the former population.

Discussion

As briefly summarized in the previous introduction, our proposed SnpC framework differs from existing approaches for computationally identifying unique and enriched SNPs by relying on the concept of gene-SNP heterogeneity (GSH). This metric can be viewed as a modern iteration of species specificity in ecology [20, 22–24, 27]. The advantages of GSH are twofold: first, it takes into account both the abundance of SNPs and their heterogeneous distribution across individuals and populations. Second, it establishes robust theoretical rules for identifying unique genes (UGs), which have distinct SNPs, and enriched genes (EGs), which have significantly higher SNP counts. Additionally, the GSH permutation test is designed to rigorously enforce these rules by providing statistically validated P-values. These permutation tests effectively eliminate the spurious effects of random noise in SNP mutation patterns.

While the GSH and GSH permutation tests are designed to detect and catalog unique and enriched gene lists, the newly defined PGSD (population gene-SNP diversity) and its associated permutation test are intended to provide a holistic comparison of gene-SNP mutation patterns, specifically through Hill numbers at various diversity orders (q = 0, 1, 2, …). Essentially, PGSD serves as a high-level synthesis of GSH for all genes within a group or population, based on Renyi entropy [34]. However, there is a conceptual distinction between GSH and PGSD, aside from their definitions at different levels (individual genes versus the total genes of a group or population). This distinction lies in the difference between heterogeneity and diversity.

Both terms are commonly used in ecology, genetics, and many other disciplines, and they are often conflated (https://harvardforest.fas.harvard.edu/news/bullard-spotlight-comparative-investigation-heterogeneity-across-natural-sciences-social). One key difference is that heterogeneity must consider interactions that typically determine the spatial and temporal distributions of entities (such as SNPs), whereas diversity is usually defined in terms of the quantity of entities (like Hill numbers). Measuring diversity as discrete numbers presents challenges, and many established metrics in ecology and genetics, including Shannon entropy and Simpson’s index, do not fulfill these requirements. Hill numbers [14] possess the desired properties, transforming some of the most widely used diversity metrics in ecology into a unified “metric system,” analogous to the relationship between U.S. dollars and gold under the Bretton Woods system [21, 23, 24]. In a previous study [21], we proposed measuring SNP diversity using Hill numbers. The distinction between the prior individual-level SNP diversity and the population genetic structure diversity (PGSD) lies in the scale at which SNP diversity is assessed. To illustrate this difference, we can compare individual-level SNP diversity to alpha diversity, while population-level PGSD can be compared to gamma diversity.

The relationship between alpha diversity and gamma diversity in ecology is that alpha diversity measures the diversity within individual communities, while gamma diversity quantifies the total diversity of a metacommunity composed of multiple local communities. Due to overlaps among these local communities, directly summing all species (or genes) across them is not a valid approach for calculating gamma diversity. The newly proposed population genetic structure diversity (PGSD) provides an effective metric for assessing population gene-SNP diversity. Analysis of the 1000 Genomes Project data showed that PGSD permutation tests identified nearly 100% holistic differences in PGSD values among populations (Table S11–S14), except for q = 0).

In summary, our approach is unique in three key aspects: (i) Existing methods do not account for the number of SNPs per gene, or gene-SNP abundance, while we use this as a component of GSH (gene-SNP heterogeneity) metric to identify unique or enriched SNPs. (ii) Existing approaches also fail to consider gene-SNP distribution across populations, whereas we integrate both gene-SNP distribution and abundance into the GSH metric to identify unique or enriched genes. Consequently, no existing methods are designed to detect unique genes (UGs) or enriched genes (EGs) in the manner as we have defined them. A UG refers to a gene that carries a unique SNP within the local population, while an EG refers to a gene with a significantly higher number of SNPs in the local population. (iii) The Population Gene-SNP Diversity (PGSD) metric introduced in this study, together with our previous individual-level SNP diversity metric [21]—both using Hill numbers, but on different scales—provides a comprehensive framework for measuring SNP diversity from individual to population levels. To the best of our knowledge, no similar metrics exist in the literature. These distinctive features also make it difficult to directly compare the proposed SNP comparison framework (SnpC) with existing approaches.

A limitation of our study is its reliance on the statistical rigor (P-value) of the GSH permutation tests. While our approach for identifying Unique and Enriched Genes (UG/EG) is grounded in the theoretical rules of GSH metrics, further validation would be ideal. Specifically, comparative studies using simulated SNP datasets with known, ‘implanted’ mutations would be highly valuable. However, developing a simulation program that accurately reflects the complexity of human genome SNP patterns is a non-trivial undertaking.

An anonymous expert reviewer generously suggested a simpler method to sanity-check our methodology: using the classic fixation index (Fst). Following established practices [2, 35, 42, 43], we used an Fst threshold of > 0.05 to identify loci with significant population divergence. For each comparison, we then quantified the number of genes containing at least one diverged SNP. As shown in Table 3, we report the counts of genes exhibiting either significant GSH differences (Total column in Table 1) or Fst-based divergence. The strong correlation between the gene sets identified by these two independent methods (Fig. 6) provides compelling validation for the sanity of our approach.
