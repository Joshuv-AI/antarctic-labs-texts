SLIT2 modulates NMIIA to regulate mitophagy and suppress hepatocellular carcinoma progression
Authors: Yong Qin, Junbin Zhou, Shimiao Li, Xiaofeng Liu, Shengqian Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is characterized by aggressive progression and metastasis, driven by complex molecular interactions. This study elucidates the functional roles of slit guidance ligand 2 (SLIT2) and non-muscle myosin IIA (NMIIA) in HCC and explores their mechanistic interplay. Immunofluorescence and quantitative PCR (Q-PCR) analyses demonstrated significant downregulation of SLIT2 and upregulation of NMIIA in HCC tissues, with SLIT2 expression inversely correlating with tumor stage and metastatic propensity, as validated by The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset. Functional assays revealed that SLIT2 overexpression attenuated HCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, whereas NMIIA overexpression markedly enhanced these oncogenic properties. Mechanistically, NMIIA facilitated epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and mitophagy, potentiating tumor progression. Conversely, SLIT2 overexpression inhibited myosin regulatory light chain (MRLC) phosphorylation, thereby suppressing NMIIA activity, EMT, and mitophagy. SLIT2 also diminished cell adhesion, while NMIIA enhanced adhesion and colony-forming capacity. In vivo xenograft studies corroborated that SLIT2 depletion accelerated tumor growth. These findings establish the SLIT2/NMIIA axis as a critical modulator of HCC progression and a promising therapeutic target.Keywords: Slit guidance ligand 2, Non-muscle myosin IIA, Hepatocellular carcinoma, Epithelial-mesenchymal transition, Mitophagy

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1], with its lethality largely attributed to early metastasis and therapeutic resistance [2, 3]. The silent onset of HCC and the absence of specific biomarkers pose significant challenges for early diagnosis and effective treatment strategies [4].

Mitophagy, a selective autophagic process that degrades dysfunctional mitochondria, plays a pivotal role in maintaining cellular homeostasis and regulating tumorigenesis. Dysregulated mitophagy has been implicated in cancer progression, where excessive mitophagic activity can contribute to tumor metastasis by mitigating oxidative stress and promoting survival in hostile tumor microenvironments [5, 6]. Unc-51 like autophagy activating kinase 1 (ULK1), a central regulator of mitophagy [7], is activated in response to EMT (pithelial-mesenchymal transition) stimuli, thereby augmenting cancer cell invasiveness [8, 9]. Suppressing EMT-related pathways, including key markers such as E-cadherin and Slug [10], has been proposed as a viable strategy to attenuate HCC progression [11].

NMIIA (Nonmuscle myosin Ⅱ A, NMⅡA), encoded by the MYH9 gene, is a fundamental cytoskeletal component that governs cellular architecture, motility, and adhesion [12]. Increasing evidence indicates that NMIIA overexpression contributes to tumor progression across various malignancies [13]. In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) [14], NMIIA upregulation correlates with advanced disease stages and poor survival outcomes, acting via the Wnt/β-catenin axis to promote EMT [15]. A similar oncogenic role has been identified in ovarian cancer, where NMIIA enhances cell invasion and metastasis by counteracting miR-6089-mediated tumor suppression [16].

SLIT2 (Slit Guidance Ligand 2), a secreted extracellular matrix protein [17], has been widely studied for its involvement in axonal guidance [18, 19], angiogenesis [20], and organogenesis [21]. Beyond its developmental functions, SLIT2 has been recognized as a tumor suppressor in multiple cancer types [22, 23]. In breast cancer, SLIT2 restrains tumor growth and dissemination via Robo1 receptor signaling [24, 25]. Likewise, in lung adenocarcinoma, inhibition of SLIT2/ROBO1 signaling enhances cancer cell proliferation and invasiveness [17].

This study investigates the hypothesis that SLIT2 modulates NMIIA expression to suppress EMT-driven mitophagy, thereby restricting HCC progression. Our findings offer insights into a novel regulatory axis that could be exploited for therapeutic intervention.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

HCC cell lines SNU-387 and HLF used in this study were purchased from the Cell Resource Center of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. The culture medium consisted of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Cat# 10099141 C, Australia) and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin solution (Gibco, Cat# 15140-122, Australia). To ensure experimental reliability and cellular stability, all experiments were conducted using cells at passages 4 to 8.

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cells or tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Cat# 15596026, USA). RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop ND-2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Model ND-2000, USA). For reverse transcription, 1 µg of RNA was used as a template in a 20 µL reaction system with HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, Cat# R223-01, China). The reaction was performed at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by enzyme inactivation at 95 °C for 5 min.Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, Cat# RR820A, Japan) on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Model 4376600, USA). The reaction conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of amplification (95 °C for 5 s, 60 °C for 34 s). Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. β-actin was used as an internal control for normalization, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Protein extraction and western blot analysis

Protein extraction from cells and tissues was performed using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Cat# P0013B, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Extracted proteins were separated using SDS-PAGE on 10–15% polyacrylamide gels, depending on the molecular weight of the target proteins. Electrophoresis was conducted at 100 V for 1 h, followed by protein transfer onto PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore, Cat# IPVH00010, USA). After transfer, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies specific to target proteins (The antibody are listed in Table 2). The membranes were then washed three times with TBST (containing 0.1% Tween-20) for 10 min each to remove unbound antibodies. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated at room temperature for 1 h with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody, Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (HRP) (1:10,000, Abcam, Cat# ab205718, UK), followed by three additional washes with TBST (10 min each). Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system, and images were captured with a fully automated gel imaging system (Thermo Scientific, iBright FL1000, USA). Band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software, with β-Actin serving as the internal control to ensure data accuracy and reproducibility.

Cell proliferation and viability assay

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a predetermined density. After treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime, Cat# C0038, China) was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1–4 h. After incubation, optical density (OD) values were measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, ELx800, USA) to assess cell proliferation and viability. Each experiment was performed in triplicate to ensure data accuracy and reproducibility.

Cell invasion assay (transwell assay)

Matrigel was diluted at a 1:8 ratio with serum-free medium or PBS and maintained at 4 °C. A total of 60 µL of the diluted Matrigel was evenly spread on the bottom of the upper chamber of a Transwell insert and incubated at 37 °C for 3 h to allow polymerization into a thin membrane. After incubation, excess liquid was removed, and 100 µL of serum-free medium was added to hydrate the basement membrane for 30 min. Cells were cultured in medium containing 10% FBS, digested with 0.25% trypsin for 2–3 min, centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 3 min to remove the supernatant, and washed 1–2 times with PBS. The cells were then resuspended in serum-free medium at a final concentration of 1 × 10⁵ to 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. The lower chamber of a 24-well plate was filled with 500 µL of medium containing 5–10% FBS as a chemoattractant. The Transwell insert was placed into the well, and 100 µL of the prepared cell suspension was added to the upper chamber. The system was incubated at 37 °C for 12 h to allow cell invasion. After incubation, the inserts were removed, and residual liquid in the upper chamber was aspirated. A cotton swab was used to gently remove non-invading cells and Matrigel. The inserts were then fixed in 600 µL of 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min. After fixation, the inserts were dried and transferred to 800 µL of 0.1–0.2% crystal violet staining solution for 15–30 min. Excess dye was removed by gently wiping with a cotton swab, and the number of invaded cells on the lower membrane surface was randomly counted under a microscope for statistical analysis.

Wound healing assay

Cells were seeded into a 24-well plate and cultured in complete medium for 24 h until a confluent monolayer was formed. A 10 µL pipette tip was used to gently and consistently scratch two parallel straight lines in one direction across the monolayer. The wells were then washed twice with PBS to remove detached cells. Fresh culture medium was added, and cells were incubated for an additional 48 h. Images were captured at 0 h and 48 h using a microscope. The gap distance was measured and analyzed using ImageJ software to evaluate cell migration ability.

Immunofluorescence assay

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, Cat# P0099, China) for 10–15 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Beyotime, Cat# ST795, China) for 30 min. After permeabilization, cells were incubated with primary antibodies specific to target proteins (The antibody are listed in Table 2). Following primary antibody incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS and then incubated with Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated secondary antibodies, either Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (Abcam, Cat# ab150077, UK) or Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L (Abcam, Cat# ab150113, UK), for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Fluorescence images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica, ICX41, Germany), and fluorescence intensity was quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

Soft agar colony formation assay

Agar solutions of 1.2% and 0.7% were prepared and autoclaved, then maintained in a liquid state in a 42 °C water bath. Meanwhile, a 20% FBS + 2× basal medium mixture was prepared. For the base layer, the 1.2% agar solution was mixed with the prepared culture medium at a 1:1 ratio, and 1.5 mL of the mixture was added to each well of a 6-well plate. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to allow the agar to solidify. Cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin, centrifuged, and resuspended in culture medium at a concentration of ≥ 4.0 × 10⁵ cells/mL. The 0.7% agar solution was then mixed with culture medium at a 1:1 ratio, combined with the cell suspension, and quickly added to the 6-well plate (1 mL per well). The plate was left at room temperature for 30 min until the upper layer solidified, then incubated at 37 °C for 2–3 weeks. At the end of the culture period, colonies were observed and imaged under a microscope. Colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted for statistical analysis.

Autophagy detection via fluorescent reporter assay

Cells were seeded into culture plates and transfected with the SensGFP-StubRFP-LC3 plasmid using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# L3000015, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After transfection, cells were incubated under 37 °C, 5% CO₂ conditions for 24–48 h to ensure sufficient expression of the reporter proteins. Fluorescent signals within the cells were observed using a confocal microscope, and image data were analyzed using ImageJ software to quantify fluorescent puncta. Statistical results were presented as mean ± standard error (SE), and appropriate statistical methods were applied to evaluate differences between groups.

Establishment of HCC xenograft model in nude mice

An 8-week-old female Balb/C-nude mouse subcutaneous xenograft model was established to study HCC tumor growth. Experimental animals were purchased from Shanghai Bikai Keyi Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Human HCC cells in the logarithmic growth phase were digested with 0.25% trypsin, resuspended as a single-cell suspension, and adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 10⁷ cells/mL [26]. Each mouse was injected subcutaneously in the right flank with 100 µL of the cell suspension to establish the tumor model. Mice were housed in a temperature-controlled, sterile environment with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and had free access to food and water. Tumor length and width were measured regularly using calipers, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V=(Length×Width2)/2;When tumors reached the predetermined volume (2 × 103 mm3 max) or at the experimental endpoint, mice were euthanized by CO₂ asphyxiation, and tumor tissues were collected for further analysis. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Lishui People’s Hospital (Animal License No.: SCXK-2023-00009; Ethics Approval No.: LSPH-2024XS068).

Results

SLIT2 and NMIIA expression in HCC tissues

To assess the clinical relevance of SLIT2 and NMIIA in HCC, we performed Q-PCR analysis on tumor tissues. SLIT2 mRNA levels were significantly lower in advanced-stage HCC patients (G3/G4) compared to healthy controls, whereas NMIIA mRNA expression was elevated (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, metastatic HCC samples exhibited reduced SLIT2 and increased NMIIA expression relative to non-metastatic counterparts (Fig. 1B). TCGA dataset analysis corroborated these findings, confirming SLIT2 downregulation and NMIIA upregulation in HCC samples (Fig. 1C).

Expression levels of SLIT2 and NMⅡA in tumor tissues of HCC patients. A Expression levels of SLIT2 and NMⅡA in tumor tissues of patients at different cancer stages; (B) Expression levels of SLIT2 and NMⅡA in primary and metastatic tumor tissues; (C) Expression levels of SLIT2 and NMⅡA in tumor tissues of HCC patients from the TCGA database; *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01 compared between the two groups

SLIT2 inhibits HCC cell oncogenicity in vitro

We generated stable SLIT2 knockdown and overexpression HCC cell lines (SNU-387, HLF) via lentiviral transduction. Western blot analyses validated successful gene modulation (Fig. 2A). Functional assays demonstrated that SLIT2 depletion enhanced cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, whereas SLIT2 overexpression significantly suppressed these phenotypes (Fig. 2B-D), reinforcing its tumor-suppressive function.

SLIT2 modulation in SNU-387/HLF cells and its inhibitory effects on HCC progression. A Western blot analysis of SLIT2 expression in different experimental groups. B CCK-8 assay assessing cell viability. C Wound healing assay evaluating cell migration. D Transwell assay measuring cell invasion. * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01 vs. control

NMⅡA overexpression promote HCC tumorigenic phenotypes in vitro

In HCC clinical samples, we observed not only a decrease in SLIT2 expression but also an increase in NMⅡA expression. To investigate the role of NMⅡA in HCC, we constructed lentiviral siRNA and overexpression vectors for NMⅡA and established stable NMⅡA-knockdown and NMⅡA-overexpressing human HCC cell lines (SNU-387 and HLF). Western blot confirmed successful knockdown and overexpression of NMⅡA (Fig. 3A). Functionally, NMⅡA knockdown reduced cell viability, migration, and invasion compared to control HCC cells, whereas NMⅡA overexpression enhanced these malignant properties (Fig. 3B-D). These findings suggest that NMⅡA promotes HCC progression and plays a crucial role in tumor invasion.

C Wound healing assay evaluating cell migration. D Transwell assay measuring cell invasion. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01 vs. control

NMⅡA overexpression promotes EMT-mediated mitophagy

EMT-mediated mitophagy plays a crucial role in tumor progression and metastasis, with excessive mitophagy promoting HCC metastasis. To investigate the effect of NMⅡA on mitophagy, we analyzed EMT- and mitophagy-related markers in stable NMⅡA-knockdown and NMⅡA-overexpressing HCC cell models. Results showed that NMⅡA overexpression enhanced EMT and mitophagy, characterized by decreased E-cadherin and increased Slug expression. Additionally, mitophagy markers, including phosphorylated amp-activated protein kinase (P-AMPK), PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1), and microtubule-associated protein 1 A/1B-light chain 3B (LC3B), were significantly upregulated, while mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) expression was reduced (Fig. 4A-C), indicating increased autophagy (Fig. 4D). Conversely, NMⅡA knockdown resulted in increased E-cadherin, decreased Slug expression, attenuated EMT, reduced P-AMPK, PINK1, and LC3B levels, and increased mTOR expression (Fig. 4A-C), leading to mitophagy suppression (Fig. 4D). These findings suggest that NMⅡA overexpression promotes mitophagy through EMT activation.

NMⅡA overexpression enhances EMT-mediated mitophagy. A Western blot analysis of EMT-related markers E-cadherin and Slug in different experimental groups. B-C Western blot analysis of P-AMPK/mTOR and mitophagy-related proteins PINK1 and LC3B. D Confocal microscopy analysis of NMⅡA’s effect on autophagy. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01 vs. control

NMⅡA overexpression enhances cell adhesion

To investigate the role of NMⅡA in HCC metastasis, we assessed the adhesion ability of stable NMⅡA-knockdown and NMⅡA-overexpressing HCC cells.

Results showed that NMⅡA knockdown reduced the expression of focal adhesion markers vinculin and paxillin, whereas NMⅡA overexpression significantly increased their levels (Fig. 5A). Immunofluorescence and AGS soft agar colony formation assays further confirmed that NMⅡA overexpression enhanced cell adhesion and colony formation ability (Fig. 5B-D), reinforcing its role in promoting HCC metastasis.

NMⅡA overexpression enhances HCC cell adhesion. A Western blot analysis showing increased expression of focal adhesion markers vinculin and paxillin in NMⅡA-overexpressing cells. B Immunofluorescence analysis of vinculin and paxillin expression. C Soft agar colony formation assay evaluating the effect of NMⅡA on cell adhesion. D Quantification of colony formation data. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01 vs. control

SLIT2 overexpression reduces NMⅡA activity and inhibits HCC vell migration and adhesion

Previous studies have suggested that SLIT2 inhibits NMⅡA activity by reducing MRLC phosphorylation [27]. To explore whether SLIT2 overexpression can reduce NMⅡA activity and suppress HCC cell migration and adhesion, we examined stable NMⅡA knockdown and overexpression models in HCC cells. The results showed that compared to the control group, SLIT2 knockdown led to increased MRLC phosphorylation, enhanced migration, and adhesion abilities, as indicated by reduced SLIT2 and MRLC expression and increased p-MRLC, NMⅡA, vinculin, and paxillin levels (Fig. 6A-C), as well as enhanced soft agar colony formation (Fig. 6D-E). In contrast, SLIT2 overexpression resulted in elevated SLIT2 and MRLC expression, and decreased levels of p-MRLC, NMⅡA, vinculin, and paxillin (Fig. 6A-C), along with reduced colony formation (Fig. 6D-E). These findings suggest that increased SLIT2 expression inhibits NMⅡA activity and suppresses HCC cell migration and adhesion.

B Western blot analysis of changes in focal adhesion markers vinculin and paxillin. C Immunofluorescence analysis comparing vinculin and paxillin expression following SLIT2 overexpression. D Soft agar colony formation assay evaluating the effect of SLIT2 on cell adhesion. E Quantification of colony formation data. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01 vs. control

SLIT2 overexpression suppresses EMT-mediated mitophagy

To investigate the impact of SLIT2 on mitophagy, we analyzed stable SLIT2-knockdown and SLIT2-overexpressing HCC cell models.Our results showed that, compared to the control cells, SLIT2 knockdown led to increased expression of NMⅡA, P-AMPK, PINK1, and LC3B, accompanied by decreased mTOR expression (Fig. 7A-D), indicating enhanced mitophagy (Fig. 7E). In contrast, SLIT2 overexpression resulted in reduced levels of NMⅡA, P-AMPK, PINK1, and LC3B, with an increase in mTOR expression (Fig. 7A-D), leading to a significant reduction in mitophagy (Fig. 7E). These findings provide strong evidence that SLIT2 overexpression inhibits EMT-mediated mitophagy, highlighting its role as a potential suppressor of mitophagy in liver cancer.

SLIT2 suppresses HCC progression: evidence from a xenograft mouse model

To investigate the role of SLIT2 in HCC progression, we established a subcutaneous xenograft model using SLIT2-knockdown HLF cells and evaluated tumor growth (Fig. 8). The results demonstrated that, compared to the control group, SLIT2 knockdown significantly accelerated tumor growth (P < 0.05). By the end of the experiment, both tumor volume and weight were markedly higher in the SLIT2-knockdown group than in the control group (P < 0.05). These findings suggest that SLIT2 downregulation promotes HCC progression, further supporting its role as a potential tumor suppressor in liver cancer.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that SLIT2 significantly suppresses hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) progression by negatively regulating NMIIA-mediated epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and mitophagy. Clinical sample analysis revealed that SLIT2 expression was markedly reduced, whereas NMIIA was upregulated in HCC tissues, particularly in patients with metastatic disease. Functional assays consistently showed that SLIT2 inhibited cell proliferation, migration, invasion, adhesion, and anchorage-independent growth, whereas NMIIA exhibited opposing effects, promoting these tumorigenic phenotypes.

Mechanistically, our results indicate that SLIT2 decreases NMIIA activity by suppressing MRLC phosphorylation, which is essential for NMIIA-driven cytoskeletal remodeling and subsequent invasive behaviors. This finding aligns with previous studies highlighting NMIIA’s role in cytoskeletal dynamics, EMT, and tumor aggressiveness across various malignancies [13, 14, 16]. Moreover, we established a crucial link between NMIIA activation and mitophagy induction, as evidenced by increased expression of mitophagy markers (PINK1, LC3B) and enhanced autophagic flux in NMIIA-overexpressing cells. Consistently, SLIT2-mediated NMIIA inhibition attenuated mitophagy, further supporting the critical role of SLIT2/NMIIA signaling in modulating mitochondrial quality control and cellular invasiveness.

Notably, the exact molecular intermediates through which SLIT2 regulates NMIIA activity warrant further clarification. Our co-immunoprecipitation assays did not detect direct physical interactions between SLIT2 and NMIIA (data not shown), suggesting an indirect regulation through upstream receptors or signaling intermediates. SLIT2 is known to exert its biological functions primarily through binding to the Robo receptor family, especially Robo1, in various tumor types (24, 25, 28). Therefore, it is reasonable to speculate Robo1 as a key mediator connecting extracellular SLIT2 signals to intracellular NMIIA activity modulation. Future investigations specifically targeting Robo1 using genetic knockout or receptor-specific agonists will be important to comprehensively elucidate this mechanistic axis.

Furthermore, although our current data strongly support the functional antagonism between SLIT2 and NMIIA, one experimental limitation noted was the absence of SLIT2/NMIIA co-transfection assays. Such experiments would indeed provide definitive evidence that SLIT2 directly counteracts NMIIA-induced phenotypes. However, the reciprocal effects we observed—SLIT2 suppressing NMIIA activity and related phenotypes across multiple experimental conditions—firmly support our conclusion that SLIT2 negatively regulates NMIIA-driven tumor progression. We recognize this as an important next step and are planning co-transfection rescue experiments in our ongoing research.

Translationally, the SLIT2/NMIIA axis presents compelling therapeutic potential for HCC treatment. Although our current study focused on elucidating mechanistic interactions, the translational implications are promising. Potential therapeutic strategies include delivery of SLIT2-derived peptides or gene constructs aimed at restoring SLIT2/ROBO signaling, siRNA-mediated NMIIA silencing, and small molecule modulators such as ROCK inhibitors that indirectly suppress NMIIA activation via MRLC phosphorylation blockade. In addition, exploring Robo1 agonists, pending validation of receptor involvement, may represent another valuable therapeutic approach. Our group is actively engaged in preclinical evaluation of these strategies using immunocompetent and humanized HCC models, and the outcomes of these translational studies will be presented in forthcoming publications.

Collectively, our findings significantly advance the understanding of the SLIT2/NMIIA axis in hepatocellular carcinoma metastasis and provide a robust scientific foundation for future studies aimed at developing targeted therapeutic interventions.
