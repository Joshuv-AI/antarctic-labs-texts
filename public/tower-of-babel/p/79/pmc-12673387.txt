Social Determinants of Health Influence Brain and Cognitive Function in Youth
Authors: Lucina Q Uddin
Published: 2025

Social determinants of health (SDOH) are the social and environmental conditions surrounding an individual that can contribute to the emergence of health disparities (1). SDOH frameworks explicitly acknowledge how neighborhood context can influence human development and life outcomes.

SEE CORRESPONDING ARTICLE NO. 100533

The recent study by Zhou et al. (2) in Biological Psychiatry: Global Open Science examined associations between neighborhood opportunity as indexed by the Childhood Opportunity Index (COI), cognition assessed by the NIH Toolbox Cognition Battery, and indices of brain structure in 10,463 9- to 10-year-old children participating in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study (3). Domains of cognition assessed were verbal ability, attentional control and cognitive flexibility, working memory, processing speed, episodic memory, and reading ability. Brain structural metrics assessed were whole-brain and regional measures of cortical gray matter volume, cortical surface area, and mean cortical thickness.

The authors found that youths living in neighborhoods that afforded higher opportunities performed better on all cognitive measures and had greater whole-brain gray matter volume, surface area, and cortical thickness. Furthermore, using structural equation modeling, they found that whole-brain gray matter volume and surface area mediated the relationship between the COI and cognitive outcomes (general ability) 2 years later (2).

A considerable strength of this study is that it is a registered report with a preapproved protocol and a priori hypotheses. A novel aspect of the study is that the authors relied on a measure of opportunity rather than a measure of deprivation to examine the effects of neighborhood on the brain and cognition in youths. This stands in stark contrast to the large literature documenting the adverse effects of low neighborhood socioeconomic status (SES) on children’s cognitive development, instead taking a positive framing and an assets-based approach with strong implications for policy.

The COI includes 29 indicators measuring neighborhood-based opportunities in the domains of education, health and environment, and social and economic resources; higher scores indicate higher resources (Figure 1). Therefore, it goes beyond single measures such as SES or measures that combine income and home values such as the Area Deprivation Index (ADI). The authors were also able to capitalize on measures of household-level factors such as parental education, family material hardship, and family conflict measured by the ABCD Study to tease apart effects of immediate family circumstances and broader neighborhood-level resources.

The Childhood Opportunity Index (COI) permits understanding the effects of neighborhood resources on health outcomes. The COI quantifies opportunity within a given neighborhood in the United States in a single metric (1–100). The measure includes 29 neighborhood-level indicators in 3 domains: 1) education (e.g., advanced placement course enrollment), 2) health and environment (e.g., access to green space and toxic exposure), and 3) social and economic resources (e.g., homeownership and employment rates).

Some results are particularly noteworthy. The COI domains were found to explain ∼2% to 14% of the variance in cognitive function, with the socioeconomic domain contributing the most, followed by the health/environment and education domain—independent of household-level factors. Similarly, the COI domains explained ∼1.5% to 4.6% of the variance in brain volume and surface area measures, with the socioeconomic domain contributing the most.

A very interesting sensitivity analysis revealed no significant moderation of results by race/ethnicity. This underscores the important point recently made by other ABCD researchers that when discussing race and ethnicity in research, there is a risk that these demographic categories will be misconstrued as innate characteristics (4). For this reason, researchers have recently recommended that to avoid misrepresenting constructs such as race/ethnicity as proxies for social and environmental forces, it is better to directly measure the social and environmental variables of interest (5), as the authors have done in the current study. In that case, observed differences between groups (in this case, in cognition) can be accurately attributed to the likely source (neighborhood opportunity) rather than being misconstrued as inherent biological differences between racial and ethnic groups.

Another sensitivity analysis revealed that the ADI minimally contributed to explaining variance in cognitive function and brain structure compared with the COI measures, underscoring the importance of adequately measuring neighborhood-based opportunities in SDOH research.

Nevertheless, these intriguing new results leave several unresolved questions. What are the mechanisms that underlie the observed effects of COI scores on brain and cognitive function in youth? There is a large literature in animal models examining how enriched environments can improve cognitive function and lead to increased brain size through processes including enhanced dendritic arborization, synaptogenesis, gliogenesis, and neurogenesis (6). It is quite possible that similar mechanisms are at play in humans, although it is not possible to examine these types of microstructural alterations using noninvasive neuroimaging techniques. It is also important to consider that measurements of cortical thickness and surface area from structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can be biased in samples with suboptimal image quality, as was recently demonstrated in the ABCD dataset (7). Therefore, extra caution with respect to data quality control is warranted when trying to relate these structural MRI–derived measures to cognitive phenotypes.

Other open questions relate to the influence of childhood opportunity on the development of subcortical brain structures that were not examined in the current analyses. It would also be of great interest to conduct similar analyses using brain functional connectivity as the neuroimaging metric. One might reasonably expect to find more profound longitudinal changes in functional brain measures than structural brain measures across a 2-year interval. As both resting-state and task functional MRI data are available in the ABCD Study, these are entirely feasible analyses to conduct in follow-up studies.

SDOH and health disparities research using the ABCD dataset has the potential to impact life outcomes for individuals as well as policies that broadly impact society. The work by Zhou et al. (2) represents an exciting first step toward realizing the potential for large population neuroscience datasets to reveal the impact of environment and opportunity on brain and cognitive development. In particular, the assets-based approach adopted here demonstrates how supportive early-life environmental factors can positively influence future trajectories of youth.
