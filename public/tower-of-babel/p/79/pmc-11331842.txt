Risk perception regarding social determinants of health among women with breast cancer in Iran: a qualitative study
Authors: Arezou Ashari, Neda SoleimanvandiAzar, Marzieh Nojomi, Hadi Ranjbar, Kamran Mirzaei, Nahid Nafissi, Mahshid Roohravan Benis, Zahra Rampisheh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesBreast cancer is the most common cancer among women all around the world. Today, in addition to factors including hormones and genetics that are involved in the occurrence of breast cancer, special attention is paid to the role of social and non-medical determinants of health. This study aims to explore the perception of Social Determinants of Health (SDH) in women with breast cancer.DesignQualitative study design with a conventional thematic analysis approach.SettingThe study was conducted in Tehran, Iran, between December 2021 and February 2023.Participants19 women with breast cancer were selected through purposeful and snowball sampling with maximum variation. Sampling continued until data saturation was reached.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe study categorised the extracted codes from interviews into three main categories and 12 subcategories related to SDH in women with breast cancer.ResultsThe study identified factors such as personal and family health records, health behaviours and lifestyles and medical screening and follow-up as key themes in the perception of SDH among women with breast cancer. The main categories were also categorised into 12 subcategories, ‘including family history’, ‘environmental factors’, ‘hormonal and medicinal changes’, ‘metaphysical factors’, ‘traditional medicine-related factors’, ‘stress’, ‘body weight’, ‘physical activity’, ‘nutrition’, ‘smoking and tobacco use’, ‘screening’, ‘self-examination’ and ‘barriers to medical follow-up’. ‘Near the telecommunications tower’, ‘Improper use of supplements’, ‘Being subjected to the evil eye regularly’, ‘Eating cold nature foods’, ‘Breast weight’ and ‘Being ashamed of the doctor’ were some examples of the codes.ConclusionsParticipants identified a number of environmental, personal and cultural factors as contributing to the disease. Woman’s screening behaviours were influenced by factors such as fear of disease and death, disbelief in getting sick and shame, depending on cultural context. The study suggests the need for further research to explore the impact of these factors on screening behaviours and outcomes in different cultural contexts.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common cancer among women. Additionally, it is the most lethal type of malignancy among them.13 According to the reports of the American Health Organisation, every year, more than 1.6 million new cases of breast cancer are detected among women globally, accounting for 10% of all new cancer cases and 23% of all cancer cases in women.4 5 Each year, the incidence of breast cancer increases by 1%–2% in developed countries and by 5% in developing countries.58

After receiving a cancer diagnosis, the patient may experience anxiety, depression and fear of sudden death.5 9 These symptoms can in turn lead to physical, psychological, economic and familial issues.10 11 Women with breast cancer are also severely isolated and their social relations are disrupted, which leads to a further decrease in their quality of life.12

The American Cancer Society has stated that about a quarter of breast cancer cases are caused due to known risk factors. These risk factors in women include old age, living in urban areas, high social class, being single, white race, history of ovariectomy, early menstruation, post menopause, history of breast cancer in one breast, history of fibrocystic breast, family history of breast cancer, family history of uterine and ovarian cancer and history of chest radiation, while other factors are not yet known.13

Health-related theories have recently gained wide attention, and in addition to the medical causes of breast cancer, special attention is being paid to the role of non-medical determinants of health. There is strong scientific evidence that suggests social factors have many effects on health and its aspects.14 15 These can include marginalisation, psychological pressure, job insecurity, unemployment, psychosocial work environment and health, social support and solidarity, addiction, food, transportation, urbanisation and migration of minorities

The importance of Social Determinants of Health (SDH) cannot be overlooked if we aim to improve the health of society. Merely providing healthcare is not sufficient.16 According to existing estimates, about 50% of an individual’s health is influenced by social factors such as poverty, literacy, housing conditions, jobs and the level of respect for human rights. In contrast, only 25% of an individual’s health depends on the healthcare system, 15% on genetic factors and 10% on the environment. These statistics highlight the importance of social factors determining an individual’s overall health.17 18

Prior research on factors affecting breast cancer has predominantly focused on medical and physiological factors associated with breast cancer.1923 However, the significant role that SDH plays in an individual’s health, which has been emphasised by health theorists in recent years,18 has been barely investigated, especially concerning cancers such as breast cancer.24 Thus, the present study aims to address this gap by exploring the risk perception of SDH in women with breast cancer in Tehran, Iran.

Methods

Design and sample

This qualitative study was conducted between December 2021 and February 2023. Data were collected from 19 in-depth semistructured interviews with women suffering from breast cancer. Conducting individual interviews was chosen for data collection to ensure equal opportunities for all participants to share their viewpoints and perceptions. We engaged an obstetrics and gynaecology clinician who worked across both private and public sectors to facilitate our sampling process. With the support of the clinician, we approached participants face-to-face, ensuring personalised engagement and depth in the discussions. The clinician informed her female patients aged 18 and above about the project’s objectives and extended invitations to participate. Prior to enrolment, she diligently obtained informed consent from interested patients, emphasising ethical considerations. Sampling was carried out in Tehran, aiming for maximum variation in economic status and education level. Participants were recruited from various areas of Tehran, both urban and suburban, utilising purposive sampling techniques. Sampling was conducted at two hospitals, one private and one public, to ensure diversity in income levels among participants. Recruitment spanned from 11 December 2021 to 9 March 2022.

Measures and data collection

The interview guide (online supplemental appendix) was developed by the research team using relevant literature and expanded after initial interviews. The sequence of questions varied based on the interview process. The guide underwent pretesting for reliability and timing in two individual interviews. Interviews were conducted by AA, a qualified researcher with an MD degree, experienced in qualitative research methods. Interviews took place in private and convenient locations for participants, such as their homes or workplaces. Only participants and researchers were present during interviews.

Recognising the sensitive nature of the topic and the emotional vulnerability of our participants, we implemented the following practices to ensure empathy and respect towards participants throughout the study: training our interviewer by techniques for active listening, showing genuine concern and responding appropriately to emotional expressions, creating a safe and comfortable environment, respecting emotional boundaries and empathetic questioning. If a participant became visibly upset or uncomfortable, the interviewer paused the discussion, provided reassurance and offered to take a break or reschedule the interview. Moreover, we framed our questions in an open-ended and non-judgmental manner, allowing participants to express their thoughts and feelings freely.

At the end of each interview, participants were provided with information on support resources, including counselling services and support groups for breast cancer patients. This ensured that participants had access to additional emotional support if needed. We also provided opportunities for participants to ask questions or seek further clarification about the study.

The interviews began with introducing the interviewer after obtaining written informed consent from the interviewees. Participants were informed about the researcher’s professional background and the study’s objectives, without any disclosure of personal goals or reasons for conducting the research. They were also briefed on the study’s procedures, including details such as the anticipated duration of the interview sessions, typically lasting approximately 1 hour, aimed at eliciting their experiences.

The participants were also ensured about the confidentiality of personal information and permission was obtained from them about the recording of their voices. No pre-established relationships were established with participants before the study commenced. Then, demographic information, including age, marital status, employment status, education level, understanding of economic status, history of diseases, issues related to fertility, and the time elapsed since diagnosis were collected from the participants.

Interviews were guided by participants’ content and experiences, lasting between 62 and 90 min. Interviews were transcribed verbatim and analysed, with additional questions developed for subsequent interviews. Sampling continued until data saturation was reached.

As mentioned earlier, immediately after each interview, we transcribed the voice of the participant. As we progressed with 17 interviews, we noticed that the information being shared was becoming repetitive. It was during the final two interviews that we realised no new information was being provided beyond what we had already collected. Consequently, we decided to conclude the interview stage of the study with a total of 19 participants.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: being at least 18 years old, willing to participate in the study, having a stable haemodynamic condition, having the ability to speak the Persian language, and not suffering from psychological disorders confirmed by a psychiatrist (based on the statement of patient’s companions), living in the city of Tehran for the past 10 years and having a confirmed diagnosis of breast cancer for at least a year before the interview. The exclusion criteria included refusing to continue with the interview. At each stage of the study, if the participant was not willing to cooperate with the researcher, she was removed from the study, and no one refused to continue.

Analysis

Thematic analysis was employed due to the lack of studies on SDH related to breast cancer. Graneheim and Lundman’s method was used, involving multiple stages of coding to extract themes. Data trustworthiness was assessed using Braun and Clarke.25

Patient and public involvement

Regrettably, it was not feasible to engage patients or the public in the various stages of our research project. This decision was primarily due to constraints such as time, resources and logistical challenges. Despite our inability to involve patients or the public, we recognise the value of their perspectives and contributions in research, and we aim to prioritise their involvement in future studies where feasible.

Results

In this study, 19 women were enrolled, and there were no instances of refusal to participate or dropouts during the study. The mean age of the participants was 44.68 (± 8.77) years. Most of the participants (73.68%) had a high school diploma or an academic degree. Nine participants (47.36%) were housewives, and also nine participants (47.36%) had ‘medium’economic status. Almost about half of the participants were married, and on average they had 2.05 (± 2.71) children. Also, the mean age of participants at their first pregnancy was 23.08 (± 5.33) years, while on average, their menstruation started at the age of 14.11 (± 1.18). Out of the 19 participants, two (10.5%) had a history of smoking, while none reported alcohol consumption (table 1).

The 864 extracted codes from interviews were classified into three main categories, including ‘personal and family health records’, ‘health behaviours and lifestyles’ and ‘medical screening and follow-up’. The main categories were also categorised into 12 subcategories (online supplemental table 1).

Personal and family health records

Individual and family health records were the first category obtained in the study. Participants believed that a set of factors are associated with breast cancer. These include several subcategories, namely family history, environmental factors, hormonal and medicinal changes, metaphysical factors, and traditional medicine-related factors.

Family history

According to the participants, the family history of cancer indicated that the cancer gene is present in the body and will be activated at a specific time. However, some participants did not value this feature highly. The lack of family history was the determining factor in not performing breast cancer screening.

Participant 7: It’s not because of jealousy. It is genetic and comes from a person’s body or…

Participant 15: It is genetic. My job was not particularly stressful, and I didn't have anything special. My only concern was my mother’s illness. I didn't have anything else. I think it is genetic.

Participant 9: Well, when I was examining myself, I didn't see anything special. Perhaps I missed it, and maybe I was so busy with my mother’s illness. I can say that I have forgotten about it.

Environmental factors

The participants believed that environmental factors had caused cancer in them. They considered factors, such as air pollution, TV jamming, working in environments with radiation pollution, being close to the telecommunication tower, unhealthy diet, use of chemicals in the living area, and having pets as potential cancer-causing agents.

Participant 6: They recently installed one of these telecommunications towers near our home

Participant 19: I believe that TV jamming is one of the factors that cause breast cancer

Participant 12: Tehran’s air pollution always causes allergic symptoms and migraine headaches in me, so I also wonder what destructive effects it can have on my immune system.

Hormonal and medicinal changes

The participants referred to factors such as the use of hormonal drugs, drugs that disturb the body’s hormonal balance, treatments such as laser treatment of the skin, as well as not breastfeeding the child after delivery, or using special coverings that affect the body as the causes of cancer.

Participant 2: Another thing I thought about at that time was the laser treatment I had; because I went for a laser treatment and had my whole body treated. I had some unwanted hairs around my breast, so I asked the laser operator if it is safe to remove them and she said there is no problem and then, treated my armpit and breast with a laser.

Participant 3: They say we should not use vitamin D, vitamin E, multivitamins, etc. The laboratory physician said that as normal body cells need these vitamins, cancerous cells also need them, so you should not use these vitamins as much as possible.

Participant 7: I did not breastfeed my child, and my daughter did not take any colostrum, so it remained in my breast. Since the colostrum is very thick milk, I thought the hardness in my breast might be colostrum.

Participant 8: I took pregnancy pills for eighteen years, so I think that is the reason for my disease.

Participant 12: For 6 months, I was taking minocycline tablets without a doctor’s prescription to get rid of small subcutaneous acne.

Participant 18: Since my first pregnancy was done by IVF method and I used a series of hormonal drugs, it could have made me susceptible to this disease.

Metaphysical factors

Metaphysical beliefs were among the determinants of health in the view of participants. They also referred to factors such as the evil eye, punishment for being bad to oneself and not paying attention to oneself, bad luck, others’ malice, divine test, punishment from God, fate, and negative energy as causes of cancer.

Participant 19: I thought to myself that I have been subject to the evil eye for having three children. When I was going to different places, everyone used to tell me that I have three children. You know, you managed to have three children in this hard economic time. Everyone was telling me something. Maybe it’s a divine test. For example, maybe God wants to see how I go through this situation, how much I can tolerate it, have patient am I, etc.

Participant 5: I said to myself I am done this time. God is punishing me for the bad things I have done. By bad things, I mean attempting to take suicide pills.

Traditional medicine-related factors

Some participants referred to factors, which are usually regarded in complementary and traditional medicine as pathogenic or preventive factors, as determinants of health-related to breast cancer.

Participant 18: For example, they say hot nature is better, or food should be used this way, etc.

Participant 5: I used to drink green tea and ginger a lot for weight loss, but I didn't think it would be good for this treatment. This is a hot nature disease, but they say that chemotherapy drugs are cold. They say cancer is a hot nature disease… I don't know… When my sister-in-law had this disease, I did not know much about it. For example, she said this disease is a hot nature disease, but I did not know.

Health behaviours and lifestyle

The second category obtained in this study was health behaviours and lifestyle. This category included four subcategories of negative effects of stress, body weight, physical activity, smoking, and nutrition.

Negative effects of stress

Stresses of daily life such as work stress and family conflicts along with a sensitive spirit were introduced by the participants as determinants of health-related to breast cancer.

Participant 19: Only these stresses in society cause cancer.

Participant 17: I think I have got it because of the very sensitive spirit I have. Of course, there may be other factors involved, but as I have had a very sensitive spirit since childhood, I have got this disease. I think this cancer mostly has a nervous origin.

Participant 16: I think there is a lot of greed and jealousy in my work environment, and it seems they all went hand in hand. I think that is why I got this issue.

Participant 13: I think it is because of unemployment and lack of money. After all, the children grow up, the girl is getting married, and the boy has to do the university entrance exam. They also need school tuition fees. We have to buy groceries and clothes, and the children have a series of demands, but they can't tell their father at home, so they push me instead.

Body weight

The participants identified overweight, weight loss or gain, diets, and low birth weight as determinants of health-related to breast cancer.

Participant 3: I think maybe I got this disease because I was born prematurely… I don't know whether it has anything to do with it.

Participant 5: If I gain five kilos, lose five kilos, gain seven kilos, lose seven kilos; I don’t know……

Participant 9: Maybe one of the causes of cancer is obesity. Once I lost lots of weight… For example, I was almost 100 kilos and then lost a lot of weight and became 74–75 kilos.

Physical activity

According to the participants, the level of physical activity was one of the determinants of health-related to breast cancer. Also, some participants considered exercise as the cause of cancer or a factor that hides its symptoms and leads to late diagnosis.

Participant 19: Because of my busy schedule, I didn't walk or exercise. I didn't have any exercise in this sense, and sometimes I think that maybe this is the reason for my disease.

Participant 18: After my children were born, all these things stopped altogether. One of my main problems is that my husband always used to tell me that I don't exercise at all.

Participant 6: I've been exercising very hard for almost a year now… I'm a little scared… I mean, I think about it. For example, I might have put too much pressure on myself, or too much pressure on my body.

Participant 3: I used to go to the gym all the time, but I stopped for two or three months because of my work schedule. Maybe exercise was preventing my tumor from growing and being detected.

Nutrition

Participant 3: I used to eat cold nature food. I mean for two years I was having cucumber, tomato, and cheese for breakfast at my workplace.

Participant 12: Since one year before my diagnosis, I had a very bad diet. I used to regularly consume ready-made and canned foods or go to restaurants. I used to eat lots of red meat and fast food.

Participant 13: I was a great meat eater, a great lover of barbecue, and a great lover of “kaleh-pacheh” and other fatty foods.

Participant 15: We don't eat fried and fatty food or salty food. We mostly eat vegetables… My diet is very different from my friends.

Smoking and tobacco use

Few participants identified smoking and tobacco use as factors that cause breast cancer.

Participant 2: I used to smoke from morning to night. This is one of the factors that I keep thinking about.

Participant 7: These are stuff that has become too much for me. For example, I used to get up in the morning and smoke one cigarette before breakfast or sometimes two cigarettes.

Medical screening and follow-up

Screening and follow-up are of special importance in breast cancer. These factors facilitate faster diagnosis and more effective treatment of the disease.

Self-examination and screening

Self-examination and performing screening tests are important for the timely diagnosis of the disease, and the participants did not understand the importance of these factors before their diagnosis. They did not take them seriously or perform them regularly as recommended.

Participant 2: Sometimes, when I was in a bathroom or lying down, I was looking and checking my breasts very superficially.

Participant 3: For example, I knew that at least once a month, I should perform a self-examination. My doctor told me that you should examine your armpits with these four fingers… But I didn't do it.

Participant 4: Everyone knows that they have to go for an ultrasound and mammography either once a year or once every six months. It depends on the doctor’s orders.

Obstacles to medical follow-up

The participants referred to factors such as embarrassment, high cost, time-consuming doctor visits, crowded public health centres and not being interested in the examination as the reasons for not visiting the doctor.

Participant 2: When I had a cold or ate something, I was not visiting the doctor. I wasn't used to going to the doctor at all.

Participant 3: You know, when my period was irregular, I was saying to myself that you should visit the doctor… I see other patients too… It is very rare that, for example, a gynecologist would tell you to go and perform a breast ultrasound. She just orders a uterus ultrasound.

Participant 5: About the disease… I should have followed it up earlier. I regret that didn't follow it up earlier. My fear prevented me from visiting a doctor.

Participant 13: I didn't like this checkup and examination at all. I was ashamed because I was very fat. Yes, that is what kept me from following it up.

Discussion

The present study was conducted in Tehran with a qualitative approach to explain the understanding of women with breast cancer about the SDH related to breast cancer. According to the results of the present study, the participants considered a set of factors including family history, environmental factors, hormonal and medicinal changes, metaphysical factors and traditional medicine-related factors to be effective in the development of breast cancer.

The participants believed that family history was one of the factors affecting the development of breast cancer. Similar to this finding, a large amount of research implies that family history is one of the factors influencing cancer development. In another qualitative study in Indonesia,26 the participants considered the family history of cancer as one of the factors influencing the development of cancer.26The results of a study conducted on African Americans showed that those who had a family history of cancer believed that they were more susceptible to cancer and were more likely to develop cancer in the future.27 The results of a study in Kenya in 2017 revealed that although Kenyan women had limited understanding about the role of genetics in cancer, they were aware that family history could be a risk factor.28

The participants believed that environmental factors contributed to their cancer development. Harmful radio waves, chemicals and living environment are factors that cause cancer from the participants’ point of view. Consistent with the findings of the present study, a study conducted on American firefighters in 2017 showed that they considered exposure to smoke and chemicals during their missions as a contributing factor to their development of lung cancer.29 The results of a study in Thailand in 2020 also showed that cancer patients believed a variety of issues, including environmental pollution, to be the causes of their cancer.30

The participants expressed that jealousy and evil eyes were among the factors that contributed to their developing cancer. In line with this finding, in a study conducted in 2014 in Bahrain, women participating in the study considered cancer to be a product of evil eyes and God’s punishment.31 In another study in the United Arab Emirates in 2016, the female participants identified the evil eye and God’s will as the causes of their breast cancer.32 In a study carried out in Africa in 2017, female participants considered demonic spirits to be the cause of breast cancer in them. In another study in Kuwait in 2019 on women with cancer, jealousy and the evil eye were cited as contributing factors.33

According to the women participating in the present study, stress from family, work and daily life is the most common cause of breast cancer. The findings of earlier studies also supported this finding. In a systematic review in 2018, the qualitative analysis of 17 retrospective and 35 prospective studies showed a relationship between stress, stressful life events and cancer.34 The results of a systematic review and meta-analysis in 2019 also showed that the onset of breast cancer is associated with a history of stressful life events.35

Some of the lifestyle factors cited as cancer-causing by the participants included body weight, physical activity, nutrition and smoking. These factors were investigated in several studies. The results of a study on 2761 Austrian women in 2017 showed a relationship among alcohol consumption, smoking, being overweight and breast cancer.36 The results of a review study on 14 cohorts with 934 681 samples showed that smoking before to the birth of the first child was associated with an increased incidence of cancer.37 The results of a qualitative study in Iran in 2020 showed that women referred to factors such as eating fast foods, foods with additives, an unhealthy lifestyle, not eating natural foods, anxiety and depression as causes of breast cancer.38

According to the results of the present study, the fear of developing cancer, not believing in the possibility of developing cancer, the high costs of screening tests, as well as personal and cultural issues such as being ashamed of the examiner, prevented the participants from performing screening tests. In most of the participants in the present study, the cancerous mass was discovered by chance, which showed that the diagnostic and screening procedures had not been done properly. A review study of screening barriers in 2015 showed that the most important barriers to screening include lack of knowledge and awareness, lack of access (economic, geographical and cultural), fear of cancer and pain during the examination, the performance of service providers, women’s beliefs, embarrassment, verbal and language problems, long waiting time and previous negative experiences.39

One of the most important limitations of the present study was recall bias. Nevertheless, an attempt was made to extract the factors that women knew as the causes of breast cancer by recalling their previous thoughts and memories during the diagnosis and treatment. Moreover, the number of participants in our study may have restricted the diversity of perspectives and experiences captured, potentially limiting the richness and comprehensiveness of the data. Future studies with more participants should be done in order to explore more SDH related to breast cancer.

Conclusion

The present study explained the factors as SDH for breast cancer from the perspective of woman with this disease in Tehran. Some of the factors introduced by the participants were close to the scientific evidence and some of them were close to the mythical beliefs related to this disease. Participants identified a number of environmental, personal and cultural factors as contributing to the disease. Woman’s screening behaviours were influenced by factors such as fear of disease and death, disbelief in getting sick and shame, depending on cultural context. The combination of these factors was in line with previous models of health determinants in diseases, including breast cancer. New factors were also found depending on the cultural context of Iran.
