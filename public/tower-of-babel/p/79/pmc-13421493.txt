Exploring the mechanism of stigmasterol against androgenetic alopecia using geometry optimization, network pharmacology, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics studies
Authors: Ifeanyi Edozie Otuokere, Comfort Michael Ngwu, Julian Ibeji Iheanyichukwu, Onuchi Marygem Mac-kalunta, Felix Chigozie Nwadire, Chinedum Ifeanyi Nwankwo, Joy N Egbucha, Stella Mbanyeaku Ufearoh, Helen O Chukwuemeka-Okorie, Nwokoma Esomchi Uzoma, Adeniji Moshood Oluwaseyi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAndrogenetic alopecia (AA) is a common condition that is characterized by androgen-induced follicular miniaturization and abnormal hair cycling, against which there are as yet no effective therapeutic interventions. The current study outlines the possibility of using stigmasterol (STR) as a therapeutic agent of AA, using geometry optimization, network pharmacology, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Geometry optimization was done using the PM3 Hartree-Fock protocol, and the most stable conformation of STR was found with a total electronic energy of -164.36 au. The integrative gap analysis of target interactions presented sixty-three common genes in the target interaction between STR pharmacodynamics and AA pathogenesis. Ten core genes (AR, CDK4, ESR1, ESR2, HIF1A, MAPK3, MDM2, NR3C1, PGR, PPARG) were subsequently chosen in order to highlight the mechanistic nature of AA. Specifically, AR mediates androgenic signaling, which triggers the follicular miniaturization; ESR1 and ESR2 affect the cutaneous hair cycling and inflammatory cascades, and MAPK3 coordinates the inflammatory and apoptotic pathways. Docking studies revealed a strong binding of STR with all the hub genes, with the strongest binding affinity against MAPK3 (–9.1 kcal/mol) being observed compared to the binding energy of finasteride (–8.6 kcal/mol). Evidence of the thermodynamic stability of the STR-MAPK3 complex was found by MD simulations over 200 nanoseconds. Taken together, these results support the ability of STR to regulate a variety of AA-related mechanisms and thus predetermine it as one of the most promising natural therapeutic agents. However, further empirical confirmation is mandatory to unify the prospective application of STR in the clinical treatment of AA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52929-2.Keywords: Hub genes, Androgen receptor, MAPK3, Geometry optimization, Binding affinitySubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery

Introduction

Androgenetic alopecia (AA), or male pattern baldness, is a very common disease that is a result of genetic predisposition and androgenic stimulation, especially by dihydrotestosterone (DHT) through follicular miniaturization. Nowadays, the number of FDA-approved drugs available in clinical practice has been reduced to two drugs, namely minoxidil and finasteride. Minoxidil increases the perfusion of follicles, whereas finasteride blocks 5α-reductase and reduces the level of DHT. Nevertheless, both of the agents have unpredictable efficacy, serious adverse effects, and low adherence, highlighting the necessity of safer and more effective agents1–3. New experimental agents like PP405 that hit the target of hair follicle stem cells are promising but are still under clinical consideration4.

In this regard, naturally occurring multitarget molecules have gained growing interest in AA therapy. Natural products are highly concentrated in bioactive compounds that have relatively low toxicity and large general public acceptability, and thus, natural products are good targets to repurpose in AA and other chronic diseases5–15. New in silico methods, such as molecular dockings, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and network pharmacology (NP), have expedited the mechanistic study of natural products and polypharmacological behavior16–20.

Stigmasterol (STR) is an unsaturated phytosterol, a tetracyclic triterpene that is produced in nature in several species of plants and in vegetable oils. It has wide-ranging pharmacological effects, such as potent antioxidant activity via the Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2/Heme oxygenase-1 (Nrf2/HO-1)21,22 pathway, and anti-inflammatory effects through the inhibition of Nuclear Factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB). STR induces apoptosis in lung carcinoma cells and decreases cell viability by approximately 50% at 20 mM and mediates its effects through Retinoic-Acid-Related Orphan Receptor (ROR7), cell cycle arrest at the G2 phase and M phase mitosis, disrupting key tumorigenic pathways in oncology23. Additional studies also indicate anti arthritic and analgesic effects, such as chondroprotective effects, mast cell stabilization, and inflammatory mediators like IL-1β by up to 65% in microglial and joint tissue models24–32. STR is also associated with metabolic and vascular actions such as thyroid hormone level regulation, angiotensin II-induced vascular smooth muscle proliferation, and glucose-stimulated insulin secretion and GLUT4-mediated glucose uptake33–37. All these activities point to its potential to be used as a multitarget natural agent in complex multifactorial diseases24–32.

Due to the limitations of existing AA therapies and the multitarget pharmacology of STR, computational methods such as NP, molecular docking, and MD simulations, provide an effective solution to understand its molecular pathways in AA, as well as to inform rational formulation design. Although the pharmacological effects of STR have been extensively investigated in a variety of disease-related settings38–41, its simultaneous analysis involving network pharmacology, high-throughput molecular docking, and long-time scale (200 ns) MD simulations to elucidate its role in AA has not been comprehensively investigated. To the best of our knowledge, none of the studies have combined PM3 optimized steroidal conformation, high throughput docking, and extended MD in a single framework of a formulation-dependent AA candidate. The given work fills a major gap by implementing a combined NP-docking-MD model to unravel the multitarget interactions and pharmacological processes of STR in AA, emphasizing steroid-related resistance pathways and endocrine signaling cascades. The methodology offers precise information on binding site topology, dynamical stability, and energy-ready action of STR–target complexes, and thus the progression of naturally acquired, multipurpose therapeutic agents of AA therapy. The Supplementary Information (Supplementary Fig. 1) shows the chemical structure of STR.

Materials and methods

Geometry optimization

Geometry optimization of STR was performed using the PM3 semi-empirical quantum mechanical method, as it is found in version 4.0.1 of the ArgusLab software42. The PM3 model is a parameterized scheme that is based on the Hartree-Fock theory and models electron-electron interactions in an approximate manner to compute electronic structures of medium-sized molecules (like STR) at a reasonable cost in terms of computational effort and accuracy. The original molecular geometries were provided to ArgusLab42, where the software optimized positions of atoms to minimize the total energy of the system. The convergence criteria were embedded in the ArgusLab42 and kept track of the changes in energy, atomic forces, and geometric parameters in successive cycles until the optimization was achieved. This was repeated until the molecule had settled into a stable geometry, as indicated by the achievement of the desired convergence criteria, and the allowed number of optimization cycles was met; the overall energy minimization was achieved. This methodology has been selected due to its established effectiveness in providing plausible molecular geometries with comparatively few computational requirements, therefore, allowing further analyses to be made based on an energetically favourable geometry of the STR.

Screening of STR and AA genes

A systematic screening of the target landscape of the AA-associated gene panel and STR was conducted with the aim of identifying relevant genetic constituents in this work. STR targets (as of January 7, 2025) were accessed on the SwissTargetPrediction database (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch)43, , which uses chemical-similarity and pharmacological data to predict possible gene targets. AA genes were obtained from Genecard (https://www.genecards.org)44 and OMIM (https://omim.org)45 databases, which are repositories of comprehensive gene-related data, such as functional annotations and disease associations. The selection criteria focused on human genes whose relevance to the goals of the current study had been experimentally or computationally predicted. In the case of the GeneCards database, the query term androgenetic alopecia was used to conduct a systematic search to get preliminary hits. This filtering was made based on relevant score, resulting in only those entries that received a score of 5.0 or above on GeneCards, the reason being that results scoring below 5.0 on the scale would be treated as indirect results linked to the MalaCards resource (Supplementary Table 3). That list of genes was further narrowed down to those in human genes that had experimental proof of having a role in androgenetic alopecia. After that, the OMIM repository was searched using the same search phrase, and only those entries containing confirmed phenotypes and clearly outlining the molecular basis were selected. In OMIM, the causative genes received priority over loci that were only associated with susceptibility. The level of evidence criterion was set to high, and only the entries with a minimum evidence score of 3 on the MIM number evidence scale were retained. The lists of genes obtained by GeneCards and OMIM were merged, and the redundant identifiers were methodically removed to produce the final, non-redundant gene catalogue. The entries in the databases were trimmed down to a unique representation of the potential targets. In order to visualize any overlapping and separate gene targets in an STR and AA data, a Venn diagram was created on the Bioinformatics and Evolutionary Genomics server http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/beg/ on January 7, 202546. This method enables the identification of shared and distinct genetic factors, which should be further subjected to functional studies.

PPI and hub gene prediction

On 7 January 2025, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was created with the help of the STRING database (Release 10.5) (https://string-db.org)47. The study was highly limited to Homo sapiens, so that the interactions would have a direct and indirect relation to human biological systems. STRING combines experimentally validated contacts, computational predictions with curated repository data, and therefore, provides an exhaustive interaction matrix. The subsequent PPI network was drawn using Cytoscape v3.10.1 (https://cytoscape.org/download.html)48, on the same date. It is a software with a graphical interface that is easy to use in order to dissect complex interaction webs. Through the usage of the CytoHubba plug-in in Cytoscape, we identified hub genes according to degree centrality, which is the number of direct neighbors a node has. Genes with high degree values were considered structurally central and consequently, could be important to the cellular processes being studied.

GO and KEGG pathway analysis

The ShinyGO web server (https://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/)49 was used to carry out functional enrichment of Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways; this is an evolving tool used as a gene set enrichment analysis. In accordance with the PPI workflow, the organism was to be Homo sapiens to ensure consistency of analysis. The probability scores were subjected to a stringent significance threshold of 0.05 to identify enriched pathways, which is a compromise between sensitivity and specificity in identifying biologically significant signals. All the best 20 pathways that met this cut -off were reported. This threshold is a standard tradeoff when doing enrichment studies, to minimally skew false positives and to still get biologically interesting information50.

Docking of molecules

PDB IDs 2AM9, 2W9Z, 3OS8, 1YYE, 4ZPR, 4QTB, 4HFZ, 4LSJ, 1A28, and 2PRG were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank with resolution better than 1.8 Å. They represent the protein structures of human AR, CDK4, ESR1, ESR2, HIF1A, MAPK3, MDM2, NR3C1, PGR, and PGARG (the hub genes). The Protein Preparation Wizard module in Schrodinger Suite 202451 was used to add charges to the protein. Bond ordering was done first, and disulfide bonds were formed, and the remaining side chains were completed. The OPLS-AA_2005 force field was then used to minimize the protein’s energy51. PDBFixer was used to repair chains with discontinuities, then docked. Lastly, MolProbity scores were used to measure the quality of receptors. PubChem provided the STR in SDF file format. Addition of charges to ligands was performed using LigPrep of the Schrodinger suite 202451. PyRx was used to import the ligands and receptors52. The ligands were minimized and converted to PDBQT using Open Babel embedded in PyRx52. Grid boxes dimensions and centers (Supplementary Table 1) were used to help identify the precise protein target areas. In the investigation, grid sizes, as represented in Supplementary Table 1, were set to cover the active site with a 10–15 Å peripheral buffer, following standard AutoDock Vina procedures, with the aim of capturing ligand binding poses52. AutoDock Vina, which is included in PyRx, was used to perform the docking. BIOVIA Discovery Studio was used to visualize the docking results53. By re-docking the substrate ligands into the active site of 4QTB, the docking method was validated. Both the original and docked ligands’ RMSDs were computed. Finasteride (FIN) and minoxidil (MIN), the standard drugs, were docked with 4QTB to act as the positive control.

Simulation of MD

The STR-4QTB complex was chosen for MD simulation because it gave the best binding affinity in the molecular docking studies. The simulation of the MD of the STR-4QTB complex has been performed using the Schrodinger Suite51, which comprises system preparation, simulation, and analysis. To start with, the intricate was solvated through the system builder, and an explicit TIP3P water model was used to simulate physiological aqueous conditions. In order to ensure electrostatic balance, NaCl ions were added at equal concentrations as in physiological salt concentrations, which stabilized the system and recapitulated in vivo ionic strength. Minimization of system potential energy with the OPLS3 force field was used because it is known to be accurate in describing protein-ligand interactions and small-molecule interactions. This reduction step reduced steric conflicts and undesirable interactions that can exist in the preparatory step.

After minimization, a 200-nanosecond production pathway was produced under isothermal-isobaric (NPT) conditions with an approximate temperature of human physiological temperature (310) and a pressure of 1 atm. The NPT ensemble has been used to allow natural fluctuations of the volume, thus making it a more faithful representation of biological milieus. The study of trajectory aimed at explaining the stability of the complex structure and its dynamics. Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) indexes were also calculated to measure the conformational stability in general and residue-specific flexibility in particular. Relationships of STR and 4QTB were examined to identify the most important binding contacts and test their ability to be maintained over time.

Further structural descriptors were computed to enlighten the conformational space of the system the radius of gyration (rGyr) to understand system compactness; intramolecular hydrogen bonds (intraHB) to understand system internal stability; molecular surface area (MolSA), solventaccessible surface area (SASA), and polar surface area (PSA), which provide a combined view of surface accessibility, polarity, solubility, and interaction potential. All of these methodological choices and approaches to the analysis form a strong computational basis to examine the dynamic behavior and stability of the STR-4QTB complex in conditions of physiological-like processes.

To determine the binding free energy between the ligand and the protein complex, the molecular mechanics / generalized Born surface area (MM -GBSA) method was used to measure the binding free energy. The Schrodinger 2024 Prime module was used to perform the calculations. Schrodinger Protein Preparation Wizard was used to prepare the protein-ligand complex structure to assign proper protonation states at physiological pH 7.4, add hydrogen atoms missing in the structure, optimize hydrogen-bonding networks, and perform restrained minimization to alleviate steric clashes. We have used the OPLS2005 force field to characterize the system’s molecular mechanics energies. In the energy calculations, the generalized Born surface area (GBSA) implicit solvent model was implemented in order to estimate the solvation effects. Multi-ligand binding poses were tested, and the conformation with the lowest calculated binding free energy was chosen as the most conducive binding mode to be further analyzed. The built-in dielectric constants and surface-tension parameters were used in calculations and were in line with the OPLS2005 force field and GBSA model. This is done to provide an accurate calculation of relative binding free energies to be used in structure-based drug design and molecular recognition research.

ADMET studies

ADMET studies were predicted using SwissADME54 and ProTox 3.055 servers. The ADMET properties of the STR were determined based on its physicochemical properties, lipophilicity, water solubility, pharmacokinetics, skin permeation, drug-likeness, medicinal chemistry and toxicity. The SwissADME web server was entered with the compound molecular structure. The physicochemical properties were calculated on the chemical structure by the server. The estimates of lipophilicity and water solubility were done using integrated predictive models in SwissADME. Established algorithms predicted the pharmacokinetic properties, which were GI absorption, BBB permeability, P-gp substrate status, and CYP450 enzyme inhibition probability. The scale of drug-likeness was measured by the use of Lipinski’s rule of five and computation of the bioavailability score to predict oral bioavailability. The parameters of medicinal chemistry, such as PAINS alerts, led to possible interference in the assays, and the synthetic accessibility score was considered to predict the ease of synthesis and the possible assay reliability. Toxicity prediction, the estimation of LD50 (mg/kg) and classification into toxicity classes, was done in order to estimate the safety profiles. The interpretation of the predicted results is made in accordance with the set of rules in SwissADME and is filtered with the help of Lipinski’s rule of five, Ghose filter, Veber rule, Egan rule, and Muegge filter. This is done by preparing the chemical structure of the compound in most cases as a SMILES string.

In the case of the ProTox web server, the SMILES string was typed directly into the field offered by the server. The submit button was pressed, and the toxicity predictions were obtained. ProTox is used to predict toxicity by estimating values of LD50 values through machine learning models that are then classified into toxicity classes.

DFT calculation and electrostatic potential mapped onto density surface (Mapped ESP)

DFT and mapped ESP were calculated using ArgusLab42 software. The STR molecule in mol format was imported using the builder toolkit. We clicked on calculation, followed by geometry optimization, PM3 quantum mechanical, and molecular orbital. The calculation started. Surfaces/Contours for HOMO/LUMO energies and mapped ESP were clicked and visualized.​.

Figure 1 is the curve of energy minimization when the geometry of the STR molecule is optimized, and the energy level stabilizes after several iterations of the optimization process, which implies that the molecule is in a stable conformational state. The energy curve indicates that there was an initial high energy peak of 154.77 atomic units (au) with a sharp decline and oscillations that progressively reduced until the energy leveled off to -164.36 au. Such a curve is characteristic of the effective geometry optimization processes with the help of molecular mechanics. STR leveled off at -164.36 au, which means that the system is in a global or local potential energy surface minimum.

The intersecting point between the genes of STR and AA (Fig. 2) indicates that 63 genes overlap between the STR and AA gene pools. The 63 overlapping genes show that the STR and AA have a strong molecular relationship and that the shared genes could be important in regard to AA pathogenesis via steroid hormone signaling pathways. The 39 gene-STR and 4938 gene-AA unique set arrangements indicate that most of the genes related to AA do not necessarily overlap with STR, but the shared gene set response is likely to be central modulators in which the steroid-related processes affect hair follicle biology in AA.

PPI network analysis of the 63 STR and AA genes (Fig. 3) gives a very interrelated web, indicating that the genes are very interrelated. Visualization of the network shows that there are core hub genes that are probably more important in the biological processes or disease phenotype underlying the STR and AA gene sets. Central hub nodes that are more likely to be more important in the network structure and in the regulation of pathways are shown by the central clustering of the network. The peripheral genes, which have fewer interactions, can be an indication of the more specific or context-specific functions. The PPI network based on STRING combines both experimental and computational evidence of interactions, and it gives a complete coverage and confidence when compared to networks based on experimental evidence. These hub genes are given in Fig. 4, and the role they play with respect to AA is in Supplementary Table 2.

GO (BP) analysis of the comparative analysis of STR and AA (Fig. 5a) indicates that the strongest involvement is in processes associated with the formation of mammary glands and the development of their branches during pregnancy, such as the development of mammary gland ducts, the development of the mammary gland, and processes involved in the regulation of steroid hormones and receptors. Enrichments by processes associated with cellular response to lipid, hormone stimulus, and organic substances are also eminent, with more significant pathways depicted by darker blue.

The GO (CC) analysis of Fig. 5b depicts that the targets of the STR against AA are highly enriched with nuclear and transcriptional regulatory complexes, and the strongest enrichment is with the Cyclin D2-CDK4 complex and the transcription preinitiation complex. The top-ranked targets and the cell cycle regulation also show a strong association, further indicating a possible role in the control of cell proliferation, transcriptional regulation, and cytoskeleton remodeling. The Cyclin D2-CDK4 complex has the highest fold enrichment of the cellular components, which also has a lower false discovery rate (greater confidence as represented by the color gradient).

The GO (MF) (Fig. 5c) comparison of STR to AA shows a clear enrichment of nuclear receptor-associated activities, particularly nuclear estrogen and steroid receptor activities, nuclear receptor activity, and ligand-activated transcription factor activity. These functions are the most significantly enriched and statistically significant, as suggested by being strongly colored in blue-red in the legend of -log10(FDR) and by their long lengths of bars.

KEGG pathway analysis in Fig. 5d indicates that STR affects many pathways with high fold enrichment, of which the most significant are cancer-related, endocrine resistance, hormone signaling, and cellular senescence. The most enriched routes are thyroid cancer, endocrine resistance, and bladder cancer. Several hormone-related pathways (prolactin, estrogen, and thyroid hormone signaling) are also highly enriched, and several pathways involved in cancer, viral infections, and cellular aging are also implicated, indicating that the molecular targets of STR are highly focused in hormone-signaling (oncogenic) and hormone-signaling (estrogen and androgen) processes. The high concentration of hormone (prolactin, estrogen, GnRH, and thyroid) signaling pathways is also consistent with the convergence of chronic androgenic stimulation, follicular aging, and the possibility of carcinogenesis, which have been pointed out in pathways that include cellular senescence and carcinogenesis.

The RMSD of the co-crystallized ligand SCH772984 into the active site of 4QTB was 0.8704 Å when re-docked. This validated the molecular docking workflow (Fig. 5e). STR showed high binding affinities with all of the reported key androgenic alopecia-related hub protein targets: AR, CDK4, ESR1, ESR2, HIF1A, MAPK3, MDM2, NR3C1, PGR, and PPARG, with binding energies of -6.8 to -9.1 kcal/mol (Fig. 5f). The highest binding affinity was observed with the STR-4QTB complex (-9.1 kcal/mol), which was better than with the standard drug finasteride (FIN-4QTB, -8.6 kcal/mol and MIN-4QTB, -6.6 kcal/mol). The ligand-protein complexes were found to have multiple types of non-covalent interactions (van der Waals, conventional hydrogen bonds, π-sigma, carbon-hydrogen bonds, alkyl, and π-alkyl contacts) and a number of key residues, which indicated strong and specific binding (Fig. 6a-L). However, a more negative calculated binding affinity score of STR compared to FIN and MIN with the 4QTB ligand binding pocket does not necessarily imply the superiority of STR compared to the other agents; it just implies a relatively stronger interaction with this binding site.

The OPLS3 force field of the STR-4QTB complex was simulated under the NPT ensemble at 310 K during 200 ns, as per the physiological parameters. Figure 7a provided the RMSD data which showed that at the beginning of the equilibration process the protein backbone and the ligand have a deviation of about 2.4–2.8 and 1.2–1.6 Å respectively, therefore, indicating early high stability binding. The intermediate stage (25–100 ns) showed that the RMSD of the 4QTB backbone was ranging between 2.4 and 2.8 Å which indicates moderate structural stability and flexibility. The STR’s RMSD, at the same time reduced gradually to an interval of 0.8 -1.0 Å, suggesting that the ligand became more firmly bound or in a more stable orientation inside the binding pore. At the last phase (100–200 ns), the RMSD of 4QTB had reached 3.2 Å, but the STR’s RMSD was 4.4 Å.

As shown in Fig. 7b, the 4QTB RMSF profile provides a detailed understanding of the dynamic environment of the protein and also provides the necessary information on the relative stability of individual protein residues. RMSF variation ranges from 0.5 Å to 4 Å. This is natural and is a consequence of the greater conformational freedom of terminal maxima of protein chains; these are less bound by intramolecular interactions than are other regions of the proteins. The secondary peaks found in the center of the sequence are probably associated with loop or unstructured motifs, which are more flexible and consequently can be potential active sites, binding interfaces, or regulatory elements. The more organized domain included residues 100–300 and had much lower RMSFs, and were all below 1.0 A, reinforcing the structural rigidity. The green bars used to represent the borders of secondary structures or motifs are aligned with local minima of the RMSF profile, which suggests the same conformation of the corresponding parts of the protein is stable.

Figure 7c, the RMSF curve of the ligand-binding domain of STR sheds light on the flexibility of the 30-residue interaction zone. The values of RMSF are in a range of 0.5-3.0 Å across the sequence. This is the case with the first twenty residues with relatively low but steady fluctuation, with values generally lower than 2 Å, and the subsequent twenty residues to the end of the protein sequence have a significant increase reaching values more akin to 3 Å. This gain indicates the presence of increased mobility in the C-terminal part of the segment, which could be useful in the loop-like movements or temporary conformations that could accommodate the ligands. Generally, the binding domain is moderately stable, although there are multiple localized regions with superior mobility that probably play a crucial part in the mechanism of ligand interaction.

Figure 7d describes contact specific to the residue by showing the distribution of different types of interactions, such as hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts, ionic contacts, and water-mediated bridges between the ligand and 4QTB residues during the molecular dynamics events. It is also significant to note that residues like GLN122, ASP123, MET125, and ASP184 are involved in hydrogen bonding. Hydrogen bonding is important in stabilizing the structure of the ligand-protein interface.

Figure 7e illustrates all the major molecular dynamics trajectory descriptors of the STR, including RMSD, rGyr (radius of gyration), intraHB (intramolecular hydrogen bonds), MolSA (molecular surface area), SASA (solvent-accessible surface area), and PSA (polar surface area). The obtained data all suggest vigorous structural integrity, compaction, a strong tendency towards hydrogen bonding, and favourable solvation dynamics during a 200 ns simulation. Following equilibration, the STR ligand oscillated with RMSD oscillations not more than 2.4 Å indicating that there is a binding mode that is retained with a minor change in the conformation. In addition, the rGyr is always constant and is 1.9-2.0 Å, which is an indication of tight-folds of the ligand with very few large-scale rearrangements, a typical trait of a well-packed system. The absence of intraHB events implies that the ligand backbone is not self-folded, meaning that it has either an inflexible scaffold or lacks polar groups within the molecule that may form H-bonds and instead interacts with adjacent molecules. The MolSA and PSA profiles are fixed, meaning that there is no rearrangement of the surface and very few dynamic changes. The PSA, which is stabilized at 45–50 Å², demonstrates that the polarity and other solvation properties of the ligand do not change during the period of simulation. SASA presents preliminary peaks, which then tend to level off, indicating a constant and uncovered, and non-solvated ligand pose.

The distributions of torsional angles of STR are outlined in Fig. 7f. The rows in this image indicate a specific torsion of the STR backbone, which is color-coded uniformly in the chemical structure and the angular plots, using 2D histograms (polar plots) with linear histograms. The plots at the left (angular, radial) show the population of individual rotameric states, and the plots at the right (linear) are the distribution of the individual sampled dihedral angles between − 180 and + 180. All the torsional coordinates are explicitly confined to discrete dihedral values, hence suggesting preferred conformational substates and conferring some loose flexibility to the entire STR scaffold. The first two torsional variables (named blue and red) have their primary populations located around the values of about ± 90 and 0, indicating that there are many stable rotamers. The torsional variables (marked green and orange) are localized around the central, and have sharp peaks at + 120 or + 180 degrees, hence the limited freedom of motion in those areas given by the rigidity of the rings and steric inhibition. The torsional variables (represented by purple and dark green) exhibit the terminal torsion variables that either have a bidrugal peak or a strong preference to a certain angle, showing high rigidity or possible definitive rotations of the variable at the molecular terminus. This analysis, overall, highlights that STR is a semi-flexible molecule, which is rigid because of the presence of the steroidal backbone but has increased conformational diversity in the alkyl side-chain.

The reported MMGBSA binding free energy (Table 1) of the STR-4QTB complex is -30.46 kcal/mol. Such a negative binding free energy value implies a good and positive binding affinity of the ligand (STR 4QTB) and its target receptor. This value is estimated by the MMGBSA protocol, which combines the molecular mechanical energies and solvation effects and thus offers a very strong measure of the strength of ligand-receptor interaction. This result therefore suggests that there is a stable and potent binding of the STR-4QTB to its target complex.

The ADMET properties of the STR compound (Table 2) clarify various aspects of its drug-likeness, pharmacokinetics, and safety profile, and the comparison to related studies would offer a better insight into its potential of being a therapeutic candidate. The molecular weight of STR is 412.69 g/mol, which is within the traditional drug-like range (less than 500 g/mol). This molecule has a single hydrogen-bond donor and an acceptor, and this is enough to have a good membrane permeability. Its molar refractivity is relatively high (132.75), and the TPSA is not very high (20.23 Å²), indicating that it is largely hydrophobic in nature. A logP o/w of 6.98 is strongly lipophilic, but at the same time, with a highly likely low aqueous solubility. Water solubility, which is measured as LogS of -5.47, is in the range of moderately soluble, which may affect absorption. STR has a low gastrointestinal absorption and lacks the blood-brain barrier consequence, which limits side effects to the central nervous system at the expense of oral bioavailability. It has no interaction with P-glycoprotein (P-gp), which can lead to efflux and an increase in intracellular retention. It is also important to note that CYP2C9 is inhibited by STR, and there is a likelihood of drug-drug interaction that results in changes in the metabolism of other drugs that are CYP2C9 substrates. However it may be used topically or as a formulation dependent candidate. Cutaneous toxicity could be reduced by the skin permeation coefficient LogKp of -2.74 cm/s, which indicates low penetration into the skin. The toxicological analysis shows that the LD50 value is 1190 mg/kg, and the toxicity is moderate, with the toxicity category being 4, which is a generally acceptable level of safety. STR is also in violation of only one Lipinski criterion—excess lipophilicity (MLOGP > 4.15), which is a typical attribute of biologically active hydrophobic molecules. A bioavailability score of 0.55 indicates that the STR has moderate oral bioavailability potential, whereas a synthetic accessibility score of 6.21 indicates a moderate level of synthetic difficulty, which may affect the schedule of drug development. Lastly, PAINS filters indicate no alerts, which minimizes the chances of biochemical screen false positives.

Figure 8a shows the frontier molecular orbitals of STR and visualizations of the HOMO and the LUMO. The HOMO and LUMO are represented by the energies of -8.6153 eV and 1.455 eV, respectively, and the observed energy gap (EHOMO - ELUMO) is 10.0705 eV. The spatial representation of the frontier molecular orbitals is seen in positive (blue) and negative (red) phases. The conjugated aromatic or unsaturated moieties are primarily localized to the HOMO, and this suggests possible electron-donating sites. On the other hand, the LUMO is spread throughout the regions that are more receptive to the acceptance of electrons, and is an indication of the inherent chemical stability of the molecule.

Table 3, the chemical reactive descriptors of STR, presents some of the main aspects of its electronic and chemical behavior that are important during drug design: -8.6153 eV is the EHOMO value, which means that the molecule tends to lose its electrons, whereas 1.455 eV is the ELUMO value, which means that the molecule tends to gain electrons. The large HOMO-LUMO gap of 10.0705 eV is suggestive of a high degree of chemical stability, meaning that STR is inert to a significant degree at physiological temperature and will not undergo unwanted reactions. The ionization potential of 8.6153 eV, which equals the HOMO energy, is the energy required to remove an electron, further indicating that STR is not very reactive.

STR’s mapped electrostatic-potential surface (Fig. 9) has one negative pole at the C3–OH, positive regions around hydrogen atoms, and a mostly neutral/hydrophobic steroid core. This makes it look like a natural sterol-like ligand that has been optimized for membrane insertion and polar contact with receptor-like domains.

Discussion

The low potential energy and steady conformation (Fig. 1) of the optimized structural geometry of the STR measured in the study are consistent with the literature on quantum chemical analysis of steroidal and ligand complexes that have been investigated in the past56,57. As with our results, previous studies56,57 have found that iterative energy minimization is effective in lowering structural strain to provide stable molecular structures that can be used in downstream computational simulations, including binding and interaction simulations. This agreement supports the accuracy of the computational procedures adopted in this paper, in particular the adopted level of theory and basis set, which is consistent with those adopted in investigations that aim to study androgen receptor and hair follicle target docking. The fact that we identified 63 overlapping genes between the targets of the STR and the genes of AA (Fig. 2) echoes the current understanding that androgen hormone pathways play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of AA58,59. Though the overlap represents a subset of the more general AA gene pool, which is multifactorial in nature, incorporating immunity, inflammation, and extracellular matrix remodelling60,61, the steroid-associated gene core supports previous data on the androgen receptor signaling axis being a major molecular driver of AA62,63. This aligns with the therapeutic rationale that views the modulation of the pathways of STRs to reduce disease progression with clinical and experimental reports showing that androgen receptor modulation can be a successful intervention64,65. The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network that has been built in this case (Fig. 3) is characterized by high connectivity and modularity that are also found in human disease networks and are similar to PPI analysis in diseases like sepsis and ischemic heart failure66,67. The identified hub genes (Fig. 4; SupplementaryTable 2) are probably key regulators of AA pathology, as in previous research programs, hub nodes in PPI networks are key biomarkers and therapeutic targets. This modular form of the network structure emphasizes the complexity of AA and presents possible nodes as targets of intervention. As shown in our molecular docking (Figs. 5f and 6a-k), STR has the high affinity and specificity of binding important androgenic proteins, as has been observed in recent computational screens of natural 5alpha-reductase inhibitors, such as β-sitosterol and STR68. STR demonstrates similar or even better binding energies compared to synthetic inhibitors, such as finasteride, and hence makes them a better candidate as safer, multi-functional, and safer compounds with fewer side effects.

Our docking procedure is robust by the validated docking protocol (RMSD 0.8704 Å) and is in line with standards reported in other molecular docking studies66,67. The stability and dynamic characteristics of the STR-4QTB complex are also supported by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations with the OPLS3 force field (Fig. 7a), which are similar to other MD studies of protein-ligand complexes68,69. The biphasic RMSD pattern, low RMSF in secondary structural elements, and constant hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions are similar to those of kinase inhibitors and androgen receptor modulators studied70. Such dynamic properties probably play a role in the functional modulation of target proteins involved in AA. Frontier molecular orbital (FMO) discussions that show a HOMO-LUMO gap of 10.0706 eV with STR (Fig. 8a) are consistent with previous studies of steroidal and phytosterol compounds, which generally have gaps between 8 and 12 eV, which is suggestive of good electronic stability to carry out biological activity71,72. The predicted binding affinities are confirmed by the spatial distribution of orbitals supporting the molecular interactions that are observed in docking and MD studies. The ADMET profile of STR, with appropriate topical absorption, low systemic exposure, and moderate toxicity (Table 2), has been confirmed by prior findings of similar phytosterols applied in AA treatment73,74. These properties prefer local application to the scalp with reduced systemic adverse effects that are usually linked to oral 5a-reductase inhibitors such as finasteride. This ESP pattern is similar to the ligand-design logic used in synthetic sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors75, antimalarial phytochemicals76, and CF₃-functionalized aminophosphonates77, where targeted ESP hotspots are used to keep protein–ligand complexes stable. In the case of androgenetic alopecia, stigmasterol may work by getting into lipid bilayers, forming hydrogen bonds with PPAR-type or lipid-metabolism receptors, and changing how inflammatory and sebaceous-gland signaling works. Clinical research on iron deficiency and pregnancy78, shows that these kinds of molecular-level changes need to be combined with fixing nutritional cofactors at the systemic level. Together, our integrative computational system, which includes quantum chemical optimization, network pharmacology, molecular docking, and MD simulations, offers a multifaceted, mechanistic understanding of the multifaceted role played by STR in AA. This multifactorial approach is better than the monolithic studies in that enumeration of targets can be done in detail, binding affinity can be measured, and stability can be tested dynamically and in physiological conditions. Our results are in line with current trends, which highlight the use of natural products with multifunctional properties as potential therapeutic agents with extensive regulatory impact on hormone signaling, cell cycle regulation, and inflammation, the main processes in AA pathogenesis.

Although the in silico predictions must be empirically validated, our study provides a strong molecular basis to guide the experimental studies and formulation development in the future. It fills a critical knowledge gap by clarifying the science behind the molecular-scale interactions in addition to the pharmacological potential of STR, which can help in its progression as a formulation-dependent drug of AA therapy with an acceptable safety and efficacy profile.

The limitations and future prospects

Both in-vitro and in-vivo empirical validation to either confirm or refute the computational predictions and predicted pharmacological effects of STR is conspicuously lacking. To verify the ability of STR to antagonize androgen receptors and to affect the activity of the 5 -alpha reductase, in vitro studies using human dermal papilla and ex vivo hair follicle cultures are necessary to determine the capacity. Specific in vivo experiments using the correct choice of animal models are to be performed to clarify the systemic uptake, the efficacy of the hair growth improvement system and the safety profile in the physiologically realistic conditions. Introducing the effect of STR on inflammatory processes and oxidative stress indicators would be beneficial to confirm the possibility of other therapeutic effects and comprehend the mode of action of the agent more thoroughly. Finally, the use of randomized controlled clinical trials is necessary to assess the effectiveness and safety of STR in a wide range of subgroups of AA patients.

Conclusion

This research demonstrates that STR might be an effective therapy for AA by employing a full computational approach involving geometry optimization, network pharmacology, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulations. The analysis showed significant genes and pathways that prove that STR interacts with many molecular processes that are involved in AA, especially the involvement of androgen and estrogen receptors, the regulation of the cell cycle, and the involvement of inflammatory pathways. The findings by molecular docking verified that STR strongly binds to significant proteins involved in AA. STR-4QTB was the compound that had the greatest docking affinity, having a binding energy of -9.1 kcal/mol. The simulations by MD ensured that these interactions are stable in physiological conditions. These discoveries indicate that STR might be a natural multi-targeted alternative for traditional therapies for AA, often carrying flaws such as side effects and variable success rates. To put such computational implications into clinical practice, further empirical validation would be required. In vitro experiments involving human dermal papilla cells as well as hair follicle cultures must be performed to verify that STR can inhibit androgen receptors and 5-alpha reductase, as well as cell growth and death-involved pathways. Then, in vivo experiments involving compatible animal models must be performed to discuss STR’s absorption, hair growth-promotion effectiveness, and normal condition safety. Furthermore, research into the processes of action through which STR inhibits inflammation and reverses the effects of oxidative stress will make it more therapeutically effective. Finally, clinical tests will be required to demonstrate STR’s effectiveness as well as safety in various types of AA patient populations. This holistic approach can also unleash future research on similar natural products to use as a holistic model in finding safer and more effective treatment of AA.
