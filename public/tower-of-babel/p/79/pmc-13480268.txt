The CT colonography radiology and data system: history, updates, and future directions
Authors: Emily Calabria, Maha Ahmed, Courtney C Moreno, Kevin J Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractComputed tomography colonography, also known as virtual colonoscopy, is a minimally invasive imaging technique developed in the early 1990s to evaluate the colon for polyps, cancer, and other abnormalities. Advances in multidetector computed tomography, bowel preparation protocols, and three-dimensional reconstruction rapidly improved diagnostic performance. Landmark trials demonstrating sensitivity for clinically significant adenomas comparable to optical colonoscopy led to its acceptance as a colorectal cancer screening option. Standardized reporting further accelerated clinical uptake. The CT Colonography Reporting and Data System (C-RADS), introduced in 2005, provided a structured lexicon for colonic and extracolonic findings, allowing more consistent communication and management. Prior to structured reporting, early clinical implementation of computed tomography colonography was challenged by wide variability in terminology and follow-up thresholds. C-RADS addressed these issues by categorizing colonic findings according to adequacy of evaluation and likelihood of neoplastic disease and stratifying extracolonic observations based on clinical significance. The system enhanced clarity, increased reproducibility, and supported longitudinal research efforts by enabling systematic outcome tracking. Experience gained over the next decade and a half led to the C-RADS 2023 update. Notable changes included subdivision of intermediate colonic findings to distinguish likely benign diverticular-associated soft tissue thickening from indeterminate lesions, and simplification of extracolonic categories to reduce over-reporting of clinically unimportant findings. Future directions include broader adoption across health systems, automated detection and computer-assisted interpretation, and integration with artificial intelligence to improve polyp identification and extracolonic assessment. Together, these advances are expected to expand access, improve efficiency, and further solidify computed tomography colonography within evidence-supported screening practice. Keywords: CT colonography, Virtual colonoscopy, Colon cancer, Colonic polyps

Introduction: C-RADS history, background, and early landmark studies

CT colonography (CTC), also known as virtual colonoscopy, is a minimally invasive imaging technique used to evaluate the colon and rectum for polyps, cancers, and other abnormalities. The advent of CTC dates to the early 1990s when advances in spiral and helical CT with 3D image rendering made endoluminal visualization of the colon feasible [1]. In 1994, Dr. David J. Vining and Dr. A. Mark Gelfand were among the first to demonstrate this technique using thin-slice helical CT images to reconstruct 3D views of the colonic lumen [1]. Around the same time, Robert Coin received a U.S. patent for CT virtual colonoscopy [1]. The concept evolved from prior abdominal CT work in the late 1980s, which showed that colonic air distention and thin-section imaging could aid in evaluating colorectal carcinoma.

Multiple landmark trials successfully demonstrated CTC’s comparability to optical colonoscopy for colorectal screening. Pickhardt et al. (2003) were among the first to demonstrate convincingly that CTC, when performed with updated techniques such as multidetector CT, rigorous bowel preparation, fecal/fluid tagging, and primary 3D polyp detection, could achieve diagnostic performance comparable to optical colonoscopy. In their landmark screening study of over 1,200 average-risk adults, CTC achieved 93.8% sensitivity and 96% specificity for polyps ≥ 10 mm, establishing that modern CTC could reliably detect clinically significant polyps [2]. These pivotal findings challenged long-held assumptions about CTC’s inferiority and directly prompted the initiation of the multicenter ACRIN 6664 trial, which was designed to validate Pickhardt’s results across multiple sites and reader groups [2, 3].

Published in 2008, the ACRIN 6664 trial enrolled 2,600 asymptomatic adults aged 50 years or older at 15 study centers to compare the performance of CTC with standard optical colonoscopy. All participants underwent both procedures on the same day, following standardized bowel preparation, colonic insufflation with carbon dioxide, and thin-section multidetector CT imaging protocols [3].

The trial demonstrated that CTC achieved 90% sensitivity and 86% specificity for detecting adenomatous polyps ≥ 10 mm, findings that were statistically comparable to conventional colonoscopy for identifying clinically significant lesions [3]. These results provided robust evidence supporting the use of CTC as a less-invasive, cost-effective alternative for colorectal cancer screening, particularly for patients unwilling or unable to undergo colonoscopy. The success of ACRIN 6664 led to CTC’s inclusion in multiple professional society guidelines, including those by the American Cancer Society (ACS), the U.S. Multi-Society Task Force (USMSTF), and the United States Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF), as a validated screening option [4–6].

However, despite USPSTF endorsement, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) had declined to cover screening CTC for many years until this decision was finally reversed on January 1, 2025. Now that Medicare now covers screening CTC, increased usage is anticipated in the coming years [7].

The standardization of CTC reporting was developed to improve diagnostic consistency, communication, and clinical decision-making across institutions. Early in CTC’s adoption, reporting practices varied widely, leading to challenges in comparing results and determining appropriate follow-up for detected lesions. To address this, the CT Colonography Reporting and Data System (C-RADS) was proposed by the Working Group on Virtual Colonoscopy at the 4th International Virtual Colonoscopy Symposium and published in Radiology in 2005, providing a structured lexicon and categorical framework for reporting colorectal and extracolonic findings. Modeled after the Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) in mammography, C-RADS is the second-oldest RADS system and standardized terminology, lesion measurement, and management recommendations, facilitating uniformity in clinical practice. Subsequent updates have refined the system to reflect imaging advances and evolving screening guidelines [8–10].

C-RADS categorizes findings into two main domains: colonic (C) and extracolonic (E). The colonic categories (C0–C4) confirm the diagnostic adequacy of the examination and the likelihood of clinically significant colorectal neoplasia, ranging from incomplete or inadequate studies (C0) to findings highly suspicious for malignancy (C4). The extracolonic categories (E0–E4) classify incidental findings outside the colon according to their potential clinical importance, from incomplete assessment (E0) to clinically significant abnormalities requiring further evaluation (E4) [8]. A 2016 opinion update clarified polyp size stratification, follow-up recommendations, and the role of CTC in post-polypectomy and incomplete colonoscopy settings. It also provided structured guidance for reporting extracolonic findings using the E-categorization system, improving reproducibility, and management uniformity [9].

After 12 years of increasing adoption and use of C-RADS v2005, a committee was formed in 2017 to review the clinical experience to date, as well as formally incorporate C-RADS under the auspices of the American College of Radiology (ACR) reporting and data system structure (RADS). This committee included a subset of the ACR Colon Cancer Committee and selected international experts who identified areas for improvement and clarification in the original C-RADS 2005 proposal. Three iterative online surveys were conducted to address points of contention, and multiple teleconferences culminated in the circulation of a draft document for consensus building [10]. The 2023 update to C-RADS, further described in Tables 1 and 2, incorporated additional clarifications aimed at streamlining interpretation and minimizing ambiguity. A new subcategory, C2b, was added to classify mass-like strictures in segments of diverticulitis, which are typically benign findings that previously lacked a clear category. Additionally, the extracolonic classification was simplified by merging E1 and E2 into a single E1/E2 category, encompassing findings that require no further follow-up. These revisions enhance clarity, improve interobserver consistency, and strengthen the integration of CTC into standardized colorectal cancer screening workflows [10].

First version of C-RADS 2005

C-RADS was first introduced in 2005 to standardize the interpretation and reporting of CTC examinations [8]. Prior to the establishment of C-RADS, variability in reporting terminology and diagnostic thresholds hindered both clinical communication and the development of evidence-based screening protocols. The inaugural version of C-RADS was designed to mirror the success of other structured reporting systems, such as BI-RADS in mammography, by providing a consistent framework to categorize findings and guide clinical management [8, 12].

The 2005 version of C-RADS focused primarily on two key domains: colonic findings (C0 to C4) and extracolonic findings (E0 to E4), which are detailed in Table 3 [8]. This standardized lexicon enabled more precise communication between radiologists, gastroenterologists, and referring clinicians, improving patient management and facilitating outcome tracking across institutions [2, 13].

The extracolonic findings of C-RADS were particularly important given the broad anatomic coverage of CTC which frequently reveals findings beyond the colon. The system thus proposed a limited classification of incidental lesions, minimizing unnecessary workup while ensuring clinically significant abnormalities were appropriately addressed [14–16].

The introduction of the first iteration of C-RADS in 2005 marked a pivotal advancement in CTC practice. By providing a reproducible and structured approach to image interpretation, C-RADS enhanced consistency, improved data collection for research and quality assurance, and laid the foundation for future updates that would incorporate emerging data on lesion size thresholds, morphological features, and patient outcomes [8, 17].

The European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) and the European Society of Gastrointestinal and Abdominal Radiology (ESGAR) released their own guidelines in 2014 to address clinical indications for the use of CTC. Key takeaways include: (1) CTC is the preferred radiologic test for diagnosing colorectal neoplasia. (2) CTC is recommended on the same or next day following an incomplete colonoscopy. (3) CTC is an appropriate, equally sensitive alternative when colonoscopy is contraindicated. (4) Patients with polyps ≥ 6 mm detected on CTC should be referred for endoscopic polypectomy or undergo continued surveillance with CTC if polypectomy is not performed (which differs from C2 management in C-RADS). 5.) CTC should not be used as a primary screening tool at a population level or for individuals with a first-degree family history of colorectal cancer (which also differs from ACS, USMSTF, and USPSTF guidelines) [18].

Screening guideline recommendations and USPSTF endorsement

In 2008, the ACS, in collaboration with the USMSTF and the ACR, formally recognized CTC as an acceptable option for colorectal cancer screening in average-risk adults [19]. This consensus reflected growing evidence that CTC offered comparable sensitivity to optical colonoscopy for the detection of clinically significant adenomas and cancers [2, 3]. The 2008 joint guideline update marked the first time that CTC was placed alongside established screening modalities, such as colonoscopy, flexible sigmoidoscopy, and stool-based testing, as a recommended approach for population-based screening.

The decision to include CTC was grounded in a series of prospective, multicenter studies demonstrating high diagnostic accuracy for polyp detection using low-dose, multidetector CT techniques [2, 3, 20]. These studies showed that CTC could detect advanced neoplasia with sensitivities approaching those of optical colonoscopy while offering a less invasive and more widely acceptable procedure for patients [20, 21]. Importantly, CTC requires no sedation, allows for same-day return to normal activity, and poses minimal risk of perforation, making it a compelling alternative for individuals unwilling or unable to undergo colonoscopy [22].

The ACS/USMSTF/ACR 2008 guideline recommended that CTC be performed every five years for adults beginning at age 50, provided that high-quality imaging and interpretation standards were maintained [19]. Additionally, the guideline emphasized that any positive CTC findings, defined as polyps ≥ 6 mm, should be referred for diagnostic colonoscopy for further evaluation and potential polypectomy [19, 23].

In 2008, the USPSTF reviewed the evidence for CTC but deferred inclusion at that time, citing concerns about radiation exposure, extracolonic findings, and limited access to standardization in community practice [23]. Nonetheless, the ACS and USMSTF endorsement stimulated substantial clinical and research interest, leading to broader adoption of structured reporting systems such as C-RADS, ongoing refinement of imaging protocols, and further advocacy for payer coverage [17, 24]. The 2008–2009 period thus represented a pivotal juncture, signaling the integration of advanced imaging into mainstream colorectal cancer screening paradigms and laying the foundation for the expansion of CTC utilization in subsequent years and Medicare’s eventual national coverage determination.

Several large meta-analyses conducted following the ACRIN 6664 trial (2008) further strengthened the evidence base for CTC as a reliable screening tool for colorectal cancer. In 2011, Pickhardt et al. conducted a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of over 11,000 patients from 49 studies, demonstrating pooled sensitivities of 96% for detection of colorectal cancer by CTC and 94.7% for detection by optical colonoscopy, thereby reaffirming CTC’s diagnostic equivalence to colonoscopy for clinically significant lesions [22]. Around the same time, emerging technical refinements emphasized the importance of bowel preparation, insufflation technique, and reader training in maintaining diagnostic accuracy across centers [25]. Building on this cumulative evidence, the USPSTF in 2015 incorporated CTC into its updated colorectal cancer screening recommendations, recognizing it as a validated, noninvasive screening option to be performed every 5 years for average-risk adults aged 50–75 years [23].

C-RADS revision

A 2016 C-RADS editorial underscored the importance of structured reporting not only as a quality assurance tool but also as a mechanism for research standardization and longitudinal outcome tracking. By incorporating evidence on polyp prevalence, morphology, and growth rates, the editorial reinforced CTC’s role as a reliable, minimally invasive modality for colorectal cancer screening. The enhanced clarity and reproducibility after the 2016 editorial have since contributed to improved uniformity in reporting practices, streamlined patient management, and more accurate assessment of screening outcomes across institutions [9, 17].

The C-RADS Version 2023 update, published in Radiology in 2024, marks a major revision, integrating clinical experience from large-scale screening programs and reflecting evolving diagnostic challenges, detailed in Table 1. A major addition is the C2b subcategory, designed to address interpretive ambiguity surrounding soft tissue mass or mass-like areas felt to likely be benign, such as moderate to severe diverticular myochosis coli, muscular hypertrophy, or strictures, which often appear as asymmetric soft-tissue thickening or focal narrowing in segments of chronic diverticulitis. These lesions have historically caused confusion with neoplastic processes, leading to inconsistent categorization and unnecessary colonoscopic follow-up. The new C2b category enables radiologists to designate such findings as likely benign, inflammation-related changes rather than true masses, thereby improving diagnostic specificity and patient management [10, 11]. This new category also serves to improve data collection moving forward to facilitate the development of a more concrete evidence-basis for this designation. The updated system continues to emphasize linking each diagnostic category to clear management pathways–such as surveillance intervals, referral for colonoscopy, or no further action–to ensure consistency across institutions.

The extracolonic (E) category also underwent simplification, with E1 and E2 merged into a single E1/E2 category to eliminate redundancy. Both previously indicated findings of no or minimal clinical importance, and their consolidation improves workflow efficiency without affecting management recommendations [10, 11]. Their combination also downplayed the role of incidental and clinically irrelevant E2 findings which had been criticized by referrers as a potentially harmful side effect of screening CT.

Longitudinal evidence using C-RADS v2005

A substantial body of evidence has evaluated CTC outcomes through the framework of C-RADS v2005, providing consistent benchmarks for screening performance and downstream management. Early data from a large U.S. screening cohort established reference values for C-RADS categories, adenoma detection rates, and positive predictive values, forming the foundation for quality metrics within CTC practice [17]. Subsequent systematic review and meta-analysis further quantified extracolonic findings, confirming both the frequency and clinical relevance of E-category outcomes across diverse clinical populations [26].

More contemporary evidence is now available from the largest and longest-running CTC screening program in the United States, where a 20-year, single-institution experience encompassing 11,830 patients demonstrated stable C-RADS distributions and meaningful colorectal cancer prevention metrics at scale [27]. In addition to these cross-sectional results, long-term surveillance studies have shown how colorectal polyps behave over time based on repeated CTC exams. For instance, Pooler’s 2023 study shows that most 6–9 mm polyps remain stable or shrink, and only those that grow are likely to have advanced histology, supporting selective endoscopic removal [28]. An additional study from Pooler in 2024 demonstrates that many polyps grow slowly and that stable polyps rarely have advanced histology, supporting a risk-based approach to managing small lesions [29].

Together, these studies underscore the robustness of C-RADS categorizations and provide evidence-driven follow-up recommendations.

Outcomes and data tracking for new C-RADS categories

The 2023 update to C-RADS introduced the novel C2b subcategory, designed to distinguish mass-like benign strictures from indeterminate or potentially neoplastic lesions [10]. While this modification addresses a recognized diagnostic ambiguity in prior versions, its long-term impact on management outcomes, referral patterns, and cost-effectiveness remains unquantified. Future research should focus on prospective outcome registries that capture real-world data on C2b cases, including false-positive rates, downstream colonoscopic correlation, and surgical outcomes. Linking C-RADS-coded reports with longitudinal clinical data and cancer registries would enable validation of category-specific management recommendations and assessment of their impact on screening efficiency and patient safety [17, 27]. The American College of Radiology has been collecting data for well over a decade in their National Radiology Data Registry for CTC to promote interinstitutional data-driven practices to track long-term outcomes [30].

In addition to tracking longitudinal outcomes for colorectal cancer, there is untapped potential for extracolonic screening. Several opportunistic screenings can be incorporated into CTC evaluation including evaluation for abdominal aortic aneurysm, atherosclerotic disease, hepatic steatosis, sarcopenia, and osteoporosis, among others. Furthermore, data shows that CT can be used as a tool to assess patient risk factors including body composition and other CT markers to help predict adverse cardiometabolic outcomes [31].

Potential for T- and N-staging and advanced lesion characterization

CTC has traditionally been limited to mucosal and polypoid lesion detection, but ongoing advances in neoadjuvant therapy have increased clinical interest in T- and N-staging for colorectal cancers detected by CTC. Early studies suggest CTC can provide useful information about tumor depth of invasion, degree of intestinal wall involvement, adjacent organ infiltration, and the presence of metastatic lymph nodes, potentially informing oncologic management [32–34]. Incorporating T- and N-staging within a future C-RADS version could expand its utility beyond screening to diagnostic and staging contexts, analogous to structured systems in other domains such as PI-RADS [35]. However, reproducibility and cross-modality validation with pathology and MRI will be essential before such staging elements can be standardized.

Integration of artificial intelligence (AI)

A potential future advancement for C-RADS may be its integration with AI-assisted image interpretation. Deep learning algorithms have already demonstrated high accuracy in distinguishing benign versus premalignant polyps [36–38]. AI can also automatically structure reports, standardized terminology, and perform quality assurance by cross-validating lesion measurements and classifications [39]. Future iterations of C-RADS may incorporate machine-learning-derived risk scores or automated lesion tracking to support radiologists and population health programs. Large-language model facilitated report generation can also help standardize the use of C-RADS and associated management recommendations [40].

Policy implications and Medicare coverage

The 2024 cm decision to include CTC as a covered screening test represents a landmark in the modality’s clinical integration [7, 41]. With coverage now extended to Medicare beneficiaries, utilization of CTC is projected to rise, underscoring the importance of standardized reporting through C-RADS for quality assurance, data aggregation, and cost tracking. Structured systems like C-RADS will be critical for documenting appropriate use, outcomes, and value metrics in compliance with emerging reimbursement frameworks that prioritize measurable quality indicators [24, 41, 42].

Conclusion

In conclusion, CTC has emerged as a validated, minimally invasive alternative to optical colonoscopy, offering high sensitivity for clinically significant colorectal neoplasia and the ability to assess extracolonic structures. Landmark trials and guideline endorsements by the ACS, USMSTF, National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), and USPSTF have firmly established CTC as an effective screening modality, and the recent coverage decision by Medicare is poised to expand its utilization in routine clinical practice. The structured reporting system C-RADS has played a critical role in standardizing interpretation and management, reducing interobserver variability, and facilitating consistent communication across institutions. The 2023 update further refined the system by clarifying polyp stratification, simplifying extracolonic reporting, and incorporating new subcategories such as C2b, enhancing both clinical utility and reproducibility.

Looking forward, continued refinement of C-RADS, integration with emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence, and systematic tracking of outcomes in the ACR NRDR as well as other databases and registries, will be essential to reaffirm and further evolve the clinical utility of CTC. As colorectal cancer screening options continue to expand and mature, CTC continues to hold promise as the best alternative to optical colonoscopy, with not only a proven track record for effective colorectal screening, but also a modality with clear and structured management recommendations defined in C-RADS to improve early detection and patient adherence.
