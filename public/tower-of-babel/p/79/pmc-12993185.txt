Ectopic nasal tooth: A clinical case report
Authors: Afakire Jihad, Laraj Wafae, Taha Hiba, El Zouiti Zainab, Eabdenbi Tsen Adil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEctopic eruption of a tooth into the nasal cavity is a rare odontogenic anomaly with unclear etiopathogenesis, potentially involving developmental, genetic, or environmental factors. We report the case of a 27-year-old woman presenting with unilateral nasal discomfort and foul-smelling discharge, caused by a supernumerary tooth in the left nasal cavity. Computed tomography confirmed the diagnosis and provided detailed information on tooth orientation, bony anchorage, and relationships with adjacent structures. Surgical extraction was performed via a transoral vestibular approach, chosen due to the inferior position of the tooth and its proximity to the posterosuperior hard palate. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, with complete resolution of symptoms and satisfactory healing. This case highlights the critical role of computed tomography in preoperative planning and demonstrates that, while endoscopic transnasal extraction is minimally invasive and commonly used, it may be limited or contraindicated for teeth that are inferiorly positioned, partially anchored, or adjacent to delicate structures, where the risk of mucosal injury or incomplete removal is higher. In such cases, the transoral vestibular approach provides a safe and effective alternative, allowing en bloc extraction with controlled mucosal protection and individualized surgical strategy. By combining detailed radiologic assessment with tailored surgical planning, this report provides valuable guidance for the management of rare intranasal supernumerary teeth.Keywords: Intranasal ectopic tooth, Supernumerary tooth, Computed tomography, Transoral vestibular approach, Hard palate, Surgical planning

Introduction

Ectopic tooth eruption refers to the presence of a tooth in an abnormal location outside the dental arch [1,2]. Although ectopic teeth have been reported in sites such as the maxillary sinus, orbit, and mandibular condyle, eruption into the nasal cavity is exceptionally rare [1,3].

Clinical presentation is often nonspecific, including unilateral nasal obstruction, foreign body sensation, foul-smelling discharge, or epistaxis, which can delay diagnosis [1,4,5]. The etiopathogenesis remains unclear, with proposed mechanisms including disturbances during odontogenesis, genetic predisposition, early trauma to the deciduous dentition, or associations with congenital syndromes [2,3,6].

Accurate diagnosis relies on imaging, particularly computed tomography (CT), which provides precise information on tooth orientation, degree of bony anchorage, and relationships with adjacent anatomical structures [1,4].

This case reports a supernumerary tooth in the nasal cavity of a young adult woman, emphasizing the critical role of detailed CT imaging in preoperative planning.

Case presentation

A 27-year-old woman with a history of type 1 diabetes mellitus treated with insulin therapy presented to our department with a 1-month history of foreign body sensation in the left nostril, associated with several episodes of unilateral foul-smelling nasal discharge. She reported no history of facial trauma, oral disease, or previous maxillofacial surgery. There was no relevant family medical history.

General clinical examination revealed a patient in good overall health. Anterior rhinoscopy showed a hard, whitish structure emerging from the floor of the left nasal cavity, partially covered by intact nasal mucosa. No active bleeding or purulent secretion was noted at the time of examination.

Intraoral examination, including assessment of the maxillary dental arch, revealed no morphological abnormalities or missing teeth, and no evidence of supernumerary teeth or malocclusion.

Computed tomography of the paranasal sinuses revealed an ectopic supernumerary tooth within the left nasal cavity, appearing as a well-defined hyperdense structure consistent with dental tissue (Fig. 1).

The crown projects into the nasal lumen, lateral to the nasal septum, without evidence of septal penetration, turbinate erosion, or cortical bone disruption.

The ectopic tooth demonstrated an oblique to near-vertical orientation, with the crown directed superiorly and medially, protruding into the lumen of the left nasal cavity. The root was oriented inferiorly and posteriorly, showing minimal osseous anchorage to the posterosuperior portion of the left bony hard palate, consistent with a palatal site of implantation (Fig. 3).

Coronal CT image (bone window) showing the ectopic supernumerary tooth (black arrow) projecting into the left nasal cavity. The tooth is lateral to the nasal septum, in close proximity to the inferior turbinate, with minimal osseous anchorage to the posterosuperior portion of the hard palate.

The tooth was located lateral to the nasal septum, with no evidence of septal penetration or secondary septal deviation (Fig. 1). A preserved osseous and aerated interface was noted between the tooth and the septum, indicating the absence of direct contact. The dental crown projected into the left nasal cavity and was in close contact with the inferior turbinate at the level of the inferior meatus, resulting in focal narrowing of the nasal airway without radiological evidence of turbinate erosion on the examined sections (Fig. 4).

Sagittal CT image (bone window) showing a hyperdense structure consistent with the ectopic supernumerary tooth (white arrow) within the left nasal cavity. The tooth is in close apposition to the hard palate, in intimate contact with the inferior turbinate.

The root of the tooth was in close apposition to the hard palate, specifically its left posterosuperior portion, without clear disruption of the cortical bone, consistent with an ectopic palatal implantation with secondary nasal extension.

Based on clinical and radiological findings, the diagnosis of an ectopic supernumerary intranasal tooth was established.

The patient underwent surgical extraction under general anesthesia. A left transoral sublabial vestibular incision measuring approximately 3 cm was made, centered over the radiological projection of the ectopic tooth located in the posterosuperior portion of the left hard palate, extending roughly from the region of tooth 21 to the premolar area to allow adequate surgical access. A careful submucosal and subperiosteal flap elevation was performed to expose the nasal floor while preserving the integrity of the nasal mucosa.

A submucosal plane was gradually developed toward the ectopic tooth, with meticulous protection of the mucosa using small retractors and silicone strips. Surgical instruments were oriented parallel to the mucosal plane to minimize the risk of perforation. The ectopic tooth was fully exposed, gently luxated using fine curettes, and removed en bloc without fragmentation. No osteotomy was required, as there was no intraoperative evidence of firm osseous ankylosis.

The surgical site was thoroughly irrigated with sterile saline, and the nasal mucosa was inspected, confirming its integrity. The mucosa was repositioned without tension, and closure was achieved using fine 5-0 resorbable sutures to reduce the risk of oro-nasal fistula formation or postoperative infection. Postoperative care included antibiotics, analgesics, and antiseptic mouth rinses.

At follow-up several months later, the patient reported complete resolution of symptoms. Clinical examination confirmed proper healing of the vestibular incision with no aesthetic or functional sequelae (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Ectopic eruption of a tooth into the nasal cavity is a rare odontogenic anomaly, and despite the growing number of published case reports, many descriptions remain limited in terms of detailed radiologic and anatomical analysis [1,3]. The present case contributes to the existing literature by emphasizing the role of comprehensive CT-based evaluation in diagnosis and by supporting an individualized surgical approach based on precise anatomical findings.

The etiopathogenesis of intranasal ectopic teeth remains incompletely understood. Developmental disturbances during odontogenesis may lead to abnormal migration of the tooth germ toward ectopic sites [2,3]. Genetic or syndromic conditions, such as cleidocranial dysplasia, Gardner syndrome, and cleft palate, have been associated with supernumerary or ectopically positioned teeth, and early childhood trauma has also been proposed as a contributing factor [3,6].

In the present case, the absence of trauma, syndromic features, or dental abnormalities supports the hypothesis of an isolated developmental anomaly.

Clinical manifestations of intranasal ectopic teeth are variable. While some cases remain asymptomatic, others become symptomatic once the tooth is exposed within the nasal cavity, leading to unilateral nasal obstruction, foreign body sensation, foul-smelling discharge, or recurrent epistaxis [1,4,5]. Despite the presence of foul-smelling nasal discharge in our case, computed tomography demonstrated preserved aeration of the paranasal sinuses, suggesting that the symptoms were caused by localized irritation from the ectopic tooth rather than true sinusitis. These nonspecific symptoms may mimic other intranasal conditions, such as rhinoliths or calcified foreign bodies, making imaging essential for accurate diagnosis [4,5].

CT is the reference imaging modality for intranasal ectopic teeth, allowing confirmation of dental tissue, assessment of crown orientation, root position, and relationships with adjacent structures [1,2,4]. In this case, CT demonstrated minimal osseous anchorage of the ectopic tooth to the posterosuperior palatal bone with limited root-bone contact and no radiologic signs of osseous ankylosis. The crown projected into the nasal cavity in close proximity to the inferior turbinate, while preserving the septal interface. These findings were critical in planning a transoral vestibular approach, enabling controlled en bloc extraction and minimizing the risk of mucosal injury or tooth fragmentation.

Endoscopic transnasal extraction is often preferred for its minimally invasive nature and enhanced visualization [8]. Nevertheless, recent reports indicate that this approach may be technically challenging or suboptimal for inferiorly positioned teeth, those partially embedded in bone, or those closely related to the alveolar process and nasal floor [4,5,7]. In such situations, limited working space and reduced control during tooth mobilization may increase the risk of mucosal perforation or tooth fragmentation [5]. The present case supports these observations and demonstrates that a transoral vestibular approach can provide a controlled surgical corridor, allowing en bloc extraction while preserving nasal mucosal integrity.

Additional technical considerations included subperiosteal flap elevation, parallel orientation of instruments to the mucosal plane, and soft tissue shielding with small retractors and silicone strips. These measures are particularly relevant in preventing postoperative complications such as oronasal fistula formation or persistent nasal symptoms [5,7].

Although no direct association has been established between diabetes mellitus and ectopic tooth eruption, the presence of type 1 diabetes in this patient supported early surgical management to reduce the risk of secondary infection and impaired healing. The uneventful postoperative recovery and complete resolution of symptoms confirm the safety and effectiveness of the selected surgical approach.

Conclusion

Intranasal ectopic teeth are rare entities with variable clinical presentation and unclear etiology. Computed tomography plays a pivotal role in diagnosis and surgical planning. This case highlights the importance of thorough radiological assessment and individualized surgical decision-making. The transoral vestibular approach remains a safe and effective alternative to endoscopic transnasal extraction in selected anatomical configurations, allowing en bloc removal of the tooth while preserving nasal mucosa and minimizing the risk of complications.
