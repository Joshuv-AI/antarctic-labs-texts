4-Hydroxyphenanthrene exacerbates obesity by altering gut microbiota and bile acid metabolism
Authors: Guoqiang Qin, Zhen Xu, Chao Yuan, Yang Li, Mengqian Zhang, Zhixuan Zhou, Jiahui Zhu, Xiaosong Li, Li Chen, Chenyu Li, Lehua Jiang, Shan Huang, Yarui Liu, Hongzhi Sun, Qing Wu, Zhongze Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElevated exposure to 4-hydroxyphenanthrene (4-OHPhe) is positively associated with obesity risk. However, the specific pathogenic mechanism remains unclear. Here, we use SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and logistic regression analysis to identify a strong association between 4-OHPhe and obesity. Mechanistically, exposure to 4-OHPhe significantly downregulates the expression of hydroxylamine reductase (hcp) genes in Bacteroides uniformis (B. uniformis), thereby inhibiting the removal of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reducing the abundance of B. uniformis. The decline in B. uniformis abundance reduces hyodeoxycholic acid (HDCA) production, thereby activating the intestinal farnesoid X receptor (FXR)-ceramide pathway and ultimately downregulating white adipose tissue (WAT) thermogenic genes, leading to obesity in male mice. Both B. uniformis administration and HDCA treatment promote this thermogenic effect. These findings demonstrate that 4-OHPhe exacerbates obesity via impairment of gut microbiota composition, thereby establishing a mechanistic link between environmental toxins and host metabolism.Subject terms: Risk factors, Bacterial host response, Pathogenesis

Introduction

The global prevalence of obesity continues to rise steadily, posing a significant public health challenge across the vast majority of nations1. Obesity contributes to a range of health complications, including cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes, and cancer2. Although considerable advancements have been made in obesity treatment, obesity remains a multifactorial disease with a complex etiology. Notably, the process of industrialization and urbanization has been accompanied by increasing evidence that environmental pollutants contribute to obesity3,4. However, the underlying mechanisms involved still need to be further explored.

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) represent a class of persistent organic pollutants ubiquitous in both general and occupational environments, primarily generated during incomplete combustion of carbon-containing materials such as petroleum, coal, and wood5. Among them, phenanthrene (Phe) is one of the most common PAHs, and it can be detected in almost all samples, such as drinking water, food, and air6,7. However, upon entry into the human body, Phe undergoes rapid Phase I metabolism to produce various hydroxyphenanthrene (OHPhe)8,9. This property makes OHPhe a common biomarker for assessing Phe exposure levels. Studies have reported that OHPhe is associated with the development of several metabolic diseases, such as diabetes and obesity10–12. Specifically, 4-OHPhe has been shown to have a stronger association with the development of obesity in both children and adults than other hydroxylated polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons; however, its exact mechanism of action remains unclear13–15.

As an integral component of the host, the gut microbiota is indispensable for regulating a wide range of physiological and metabolic processes16. Through extensive investigation into the role of the gut microbiota, it is becoming increasingly evident that changes in its diversity and abundance can perturb endogenous metabolic pathways, consequently contributing to the onset of obesity17–19. The intestinal microbiota composition is shaped by a wide range of factors, among which environmental pollutants have received increasing attention20–22. In particular, chronic exposure to environmental pollutants may cause gut microbiota dysbiosis, which is linked to the pathogenesis of metabolic disorders23,24. Based on existing evidence, exposure to 4-OHPhe has been associated with an increased risk of obesity, while dysbiosis of the gut microbiota has also been implicated in its pathogenesis. It is therefore crucial to examine whether 4-OHPhe contributes to obesity through modifications in the gut microbial community.

In the study, we found that among 49 common environmental chemicals, 4-OHPhe showed the strongest association with obesity. Mechanistically, exposure to 4-OHPhe exacerbates obesity in mice by downregulating the expression of thermogenic genes in white adipose tissue (WAT). Subsequently, combined metagenomic sequencing and metabolomic analysis revealed that 4-OHPhe exposure decreased the abundance of B. uniformis, resulting in reduced levels of hyodeoxycholic acid (HDCA). The reduction in HDCA levels further activates the intestinal farnesoid X receptor (FXR), an important bile acid receptor. This activation increases ceramide levels, which in turn suppresses thermogenesis. Furthermore, this study indicates that 4-OHPhe triggers oxidative stress in B. uniformis by disrupting its reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging mechanism, leading to a reduction in bacterial abundance. Our results provide important insights into the mechanisms underlying 4-OHPhe-induced obesity.

Results

4-OHPhe exacerbates obesity by impairing thermogenic capacity in adipose tissues

To investigate the effect of 4-OHPhe on obesity, we analyzed data from 539 participants in the 2011–2012 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), among whom 192 had obesity (Supplementary Table S1). SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis indicated the global contribution magnitudes of all included variables (Fig. 1a), and the top five contributing factors are race, 4-OHPhe, diabetes, BP-3, and 3-OHPhe. Among the 49 environmental chemicals associated with obesity (Body mass index ≥30 kg/m2) (Supplementary Table S2), the top five contributors were 4-OHPhe, BP-3, 3-OHPhe, MEHHP, and UCd (Fig. 1b). To further validate these associations, we used a multivariate logistic regression model to adjust for confounders. This analysis confirmed that 4-OHPhe had the strongest association with obesity among the five key chemicals, with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.47 (95% confidence interval [CI]: [1.07, 2.03]) (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Table S3). After excluding hypertension, diabetes, or both separately in the multivariate logistic regression model, the association between 4-OHPhe and obesity was still observed to be significant (Supplementary Table S4).

To further investigate the impact of 4-OHPhe on obesity development, C57BL/6J mice (n = 8 per group) were administered either 4-OHPhe or corn oil vehicle via oral gavage, in conjunction with a high-fat dietary (HFD) regimen, over an 8-week experimental period (Fig. 1d). Although no significant differences were observed in food intake between the two groups, mice exposed to 4-OHPhe exhibited significant increases in both body weight and fat mass compared to vehicle group (Fig. 1e, f and Supplementary Fig. S1a–d). Glucose tolerance test (GTT), insulin tolerance test (ITT), plasma insulin levels, and HOMA-IR index collectively demonstrated that 4-OHPhe exposure impaired insulin sensitivity (Supplementary Fig. S1e–j). Thermogenesis is closely associated with the pathogenesis and progression of obesity25. To investigate whether 4-OHPhe exacerbates obesity development by impairing thermogenic capacity, we systematically evaluated multiple thermogenesis-related parameters in mice subjected to 8-week oral gavage of 4-OHPhe or corn oil vehicle, combined with HFD feeding. Under thermoneutral conditions, rectal temperature did not differ significantly between the 4-OHPhe and vehicle groups (Fig. 1g). However, following a 48-hour cold challenge, the 4-OHPhe exposure group exhibited a significant reduction in rectal temperature (Fig. 1g). To further elucidate the impact of 4-OHPhe on adipose tissue thermogenesis, we employed infrared thermography to monitor ventral and dorsal temperature profiles in both inguinal and interscapular regions. Under the thermoneutral condition, there was no significant difference in temperature between the two regions (Fig. 1h, i and Supplementary Fig. S1k). However, under cold treatment, the temperature in the inguinal region decreased more rapidly in the 4-OHPhe exposure group compared to the vehicle group, while the interscapular region maintained a similar temperature (Fig. 1h, i and Supplementary Fig. S1k). This selective impairment suggests specific suppression of WAT thermogenic capacity following 4-OHPhe exposure.

Thermogenesis plays a pivotal role in body weight regulation through its modulation of energy metabolism. To assess the impact of 4-OHPhe on metabolic processes, we housed the mice in automatic metabolic cages for detailed energy expenditure analysis. 4-OHPhe significantly reduced the energy expenditure of the mice (Fig. 1j, k); moreover, the respiratory exchange ratio (RER) of the mice in the 4-OHPhe exposure group significantly increased, indicating a decrease in fat utilization (Fig. 1l, m). Subsequent analysis of thermogenic gene expression in WAT confirmed the suppression of thermogenic capacity in 4-OHPhe exposure group. Specifically, the expression of thermogenic genes was significantly downregulated in the inguinal and epididymal fat of 4-OHPhe-exposed mice; however, thermogenesis in brown adipose tissue (BAT) was unaffected (Fig. 1n, o and Supplementary Fig. S1l). At the same time, we also found that the expression of uncoupling protein (UCP) 1 in the inguinal fat of mice exposed to 4-OHPhe was significantly reduced (Fig. 1p, q). Through H&E staining, we found that the volume of adipocytes in the inguinal fat and epididymal fat of mice in the 4-OHPhe exposure group was significantly increased and irregularly arranged (Fig. 1r).

The gut microbiota mediates the aggravation of obesity induced by 4-OHPhe

To investigate whether gut microbiota plays a key role in the occurrence and development of obesity induced by 4-OHPhe, we treated mice (n = 6 per group) with antibiotics to deplete the intestinal microbiota. Following antibiotic intervention, 4-OHPhe exposure no longer induced significant body weight gain or impaired insulin sensitivity, and furthermore, no differences in food intake were observed among the groups (Supplementary Fig. S2a–g). Notably, antibiotic treatment abolished the differential expression of thermogenic genes in WAT between 4-OHPhe exposure and vehicle groups (Supplementary Fig. S2h, i). Furthermore, we also conducted fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) to explore whether the obesity phenotype induced by 4-OHPhe could be transferred through the gut microbiota. We found that there was no difference in food consumption among the groups, but the body weight and body fat content of mice (n = 8 per group) in the 4-OHPhe + FMT group were significantly higher than those in the Vehicle + FMT group (Fig. 2a–c and Supplementary Fig. S2j). The insulin sensitivity of mice in the 4-OHPhe + FMT group was significantly reduced (Fig. 2d–g). Meanwhile, in mice treated with 4-OHPhe + FMT, the rectal, ventral, and dorsal body temperatures, gene expression related to thermogenic heat production in WAT, and white adipocyte morphology were similar to those observed in mice directly exposed to 4-OHPhe (Fig. 2h–m and Supplementary Fig. S2k). Collectively, these findings indicate that the aggravation of obesity induced by 4-OHPhe can be eliminated by antibiotics or reproduced by FMT, establishing gut microbiota as the definitive mediator of this metabolic perturbation.

4-OHPhe exposure significantly reduced the relative abundance of B. uniformis and concurrently altered bile acid metabolism

To determine the changes in the gut microbiota induced by 4-OHPhe, shotgun sequencing of cecal contents was conducted for both 4-OHPhe exposure and vehicle mice. The α-diversity was significantly reduced subsequent to 4-OHPhe exposure. Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) revealed substantial differences in the composition and abundance of the gut microbiota between the two groups (Fig. 3a). Through linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) analysis, we found that the abundance of the bacteria (with LDA values >3) belonging to order Bacteroidales was significant reduction after exposure to 4-OHPhe, such as B. uniformis, Bacteroides acidifaciens, and Muribaculum gordoncarteri (Fig. 3b). Analysis of species-level abundances in the metagenomic data further confirmed that 4-OHPhe exposure significantly alters Bacteroidales composition (Fig. 3c).

Experimental evidence demonstrates that Bacteroidales exhibit potent bile salt hydrolase (BSH) activity, playing a pivotal role in bile acid metabolism through enzymatic cleavage of conjugated bile acids into free bile acids and amino acid residues26–28. Dysregulation of bile acid metabolism is known to promote metabolic disorders, including obesity18,19. Consequently, we focused on bile acids and microbes belonging to order Bacteroidales in the subsequent research. We quantitatively analyzed bile acid profiles in both plasma and ileum. Consistent with these findings, we observed that 4-OHPhe exposure led to the most pronounced decrease in free bile acid hyodeoxycholic acid (HDCA) levels and the most significant increase in conjugated bile acid taurohyodeoxycholic acid (THDCA) concentrations in both ileum and plasma samples (Fig. 3d, e). Given the observed metabolomic phenotype of impaired deconjugation (specifically, reduced HDCA), species decreased by 4-OHPhe (e.g., B. uniformis) were targeted for study as plausible mediators. Other increased species (e.g., B. thetaiotaomicron) were considered less likely drivers of this specific shift (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, Spearman’s correlation analysis was conducted to assess the relationships between the changes in microbial species (with LDA values > 3) belonging to order Bacteroidales and the host bile acid levels in intestines were calculated. Additionally, the distribution of BSH in the microbial species (with LDA values > 3) belonging to the order Bacteroidales was investigated. Combined with the LEfSe results, it was found that B. uniformis displayed the most substantial decrease in abundance (with the maximum LDA value) and exhibited a positive association with HDCA (Fig. 3f). To further validate the role of B. uniformis in mediating 4-OHPhe-induced bile acid alterations, in vitro culture experiments demonstrated its capacity to hydrolyze THDCA into HDCA (Supplementary Fig. S3e).

Notably, antibiotic treatment completely abolished 4-OHPhe-induced bile acid alterations (Supplementary Fig. S3a, b). In contrast, FMT recipients exhibited pronounced metabolic shifts, with 4-OHPhe + FMT mice showing maximal HDCA reduction and THDCA elevation in both plasma and ileum samples (Supplementary Fig. S3c, d). Collectively, these results substantiate that 4-OHPhe impairs HDCA production through selective depletion of B. uniformis, establishing a direct microbiota-metabolite axis in 4-OHPhe-induced metabolic disruption.

B. uniformis supplementation significantly attenuated the aggravation of obesity induced by 4-OHPhe and increased the level of HDCA

Our experimental findings demonstrated that recipient mice developed exacerbated obesity phenotypes following transplantation of cecal contents from the 4-OHPhe exposure group. Coupled with the observed specific reduction in B. uniformis abundance upon 4-OHPhe exposure, these results prompted us to investigate the potential protective role of B. uniformis in mitigating 4-OHPhe-induced metabolic dysregulation. Following 8 weeks of combined 4-OHPhe exposure and HFD feeding, C57BL/6J mice (n = 8 per group) were orally administered B. uniformis for 4 weeks via gavage (Fig. 4a). There was no difference in food consumption among the groups (Supplementary Fig. S3f). The results demonstrated that 4-OHPhe exposure mice receiving B. uniformis supplementation showed no significant difference in body weight compared to vehicle mice, and a significant reduction in body weight was observed in the 4-OHPhe exposure mice after four weeks of gavage of B. uniformis as compared to the 4-OHPhe exposure group without B. uniformis intervention (Fig. 4b). Moreover, a significant reduction in both fat mass and body fat percentage was observed in the 4-OHPhe exposure group treated with B. uniformis via gavage, compared to the group that did not receive such intervention (Fig. 4c, d). GTT, ITT, insulin levels, and HOMA-IR index demonstrated that gavage of B. uniformis ameliorated the reduced insulin sensitivity induced by 4-OHPhe exposure (Supplementary Fig. S3g–l).

Subsequently, the metabolic cage monitoring test showed that the 4-OHPhe exposure-mediated inhibition of energy expenditure in mice could be counteracted by gavage of B. uniformis (Fig. 4e, f). And the RER of the 4-OHPhe exposure group decreased after B. uniformis intervention, thereby eliminating the difference from the vehicle group (Fig. 4g, h). Analysis of thermogenic gene expression in WAT showed that B. uniformis intervention reduced the inhibitory effect of 4-OHPhe exposure on the expression of thermogenic genes in inguinal and epididymal fat (Fig. 4i, j).

Given that B. uniformis plays a significant role in influencing bile acid metabolism, we also measured the levels of various bile acids in the plasma and ileum of mice exposed to 4-OHPhe after gavage of B. uniformis. Compared to the 4-OHPhe exposure group without intervention, the group that received B. uniformis showed significant differences in the levels of HDCA and THDCA in both plasma and ileum (Fig. 4k, l). The above results indicate that the 4-OHPhe-induced decrease in B. uniformis abundance contributes to the aggravation of obesity, possibly by disrupting bile acid metabolism.

4-OHPhe reduces the abundance of B. uniformis via impaired ROS clearance, resulting from hcp gene inhibition

Exposure to 4-OHPhe most significantly reduced the abundance of B. uniformis (Fig. 3f). To explore the killing effect of 4-OHPhe on B. uniformis, we tested the abundance changes of B. uniformis. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 4-OHPhe towards B. uniformis was 2 mg/L, and all the tests were replicated three times (Fig. 5a). To explore the mechanisms through which 4-OHPhe inhibits the growth of B. uniformis, we performed RNA sequencing on bacterial cultures with and without 4-OHPhe exposure. RNA sequencing identified 319 upregulated genes and 113 downregulated genes (Supplementary Fig. S4a). Our study focused on gene expression related to the bactericidal effect of 4-OHPhe. With 4-OHPhe exposure, transcriptome data showed that amino acid transport and metabolism were the most significantly upregulated pathway (Supplementary Fig. S4b), however, we found that the gene involved in glutamine metabolism, hcp, was the most significantly downregulated in B. uniformis (Fig. 5b–d). The contradictory results suggested that the downregulated hcp genes might negatively affect the amino acid metabolism and inhibit the growth of B. uniformis. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis showed that the downregulated hcp gene altered the metabolism of ammonia and glutamine (Fig. 5b), and the concentration of ammonia and glutamine was significantly reduced in B. uniformis with 4-OHPhe exposure, even though the glnA was not affected by 4-OHPhe (Supplementary Fig. S4c–f, k). Then we want to see if adding additional glutamine could help B. uniformis to resist the killing effect of 4-OHPhe. We added additional glutamine in each well, and we found that the MIC increased to 32 µg/mL with 4-OHPhe exposure (Supplementary Fig. S4g). Notably, precipitation of 4-OHPhe was observed at and above 32 µg/mL due to its limited solubility, which further corroborates the accuracy of this endpoint. The findings support the theory that 4-OHPhe-mediated downregulation of the hcp gene might play an important role in reducing the abundance of B. uniformis by affecting glutamine metabolism.

To explore the effect of 4-OHPhe-mediated downregulation of the hcp gene, the ROS level was detected. In vitro study found that under the stress of 4-OHPhe, B. uniformis produced significantly higher levels of ROS compared to the B. uniformis without 4-OHPhe exposure (Fig. 5e). Based on the present findings, we speculated that 4-OHPhe-mediated downregulation of the hcp gene might increase ROS and contribute to the reduced abundance of B. uniformis.

To verify the important role of 4-OHPhe-mediated downregulation of hcp gene in the reduced abundance of B. uniformis, a xylP-induced promoter was employed to induce the expression of the hcp gene in B. uniformis. The combination of synthesized sequences and pLGB31 was confirmed by PCR (Supplementary Fig. S4h) and the sequencing results of clone 5 were listed in Supplementary Table S5. The counter-selection confirmation was shown in Fig. 5f, and three positive clones were determined. Clone 3 was picked for further testing. The insertion of the xylan inducible promoter in clone 3 was determined by PCR and DNA sequencing (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Table S5). qPCR results showed that hcp expression of xylP-hcp B. uniformis was not affected by 4-OHPhe exposure with the addition of xylan (Fig. 5g). The abundance of hydroxyamine reductase was significantly higher in xylP-hcp B. uniformis than wild-type B. uniformis (Fig. 5h). The MIC of xylP-hcp B.uniformis showed a 16 times increase of MIC towards 4-OHPhe (Supplementary Fig. S4i). Under the stress of 4-OHPhe, the ROS of xylP-hcp B. uniformis was significantly less than that in B. uniformis (Fig. 5e). Moreover, with 4-OHPhe exposure, the glutamine concentration of B. uniformis mutant supplemented with xylan was significantly higher than that in B. uniformis with 4-OHPhe exposure (Supplementary Fig. S4j). Moreover, with 4-OHPhe exposure, the ammonia concentration of B. uniformis mutant supplemented with xylan was also significantly increased compared with B. uniformis (Supplementary Fig. S4k). The findings corroborated the theory, and xylP-induced expression of the hcp gene could counteract the killing effect of 4-OHPhe. The mice gut colonization tests (C57BL/6J mice, n = 6 per group) showed that B. uniformis mutant supplemented with xylan could maintain the abundance under the exposure of 4-OHPhe, while the abundance of B. uniformis supplemented with xylan was decreased (Fig. 5i). Furthermore, measurements of bile acid concentrations in mice revealed that 4-OHPhe exposure no longer significantly affected bile acid levels in the B. uniformis mutant supplemented with xylan (Supplementary Fig. S4l, m).

4-OHPhe exacerbates obesity by activating the intestinal FXR-ceramide signaling pathway

Since 4-OHPhe exposure significantly altered the bile acid profile in mice, we profiled the expression of key bile acid receptors. Among these, the nuclear receptor FXR emerged as a specifically affected target. Exposure to 4-OHPhe selectively upregulated ileal FXR target genes (e.g., Fgf15, Shp); this effect was not seen in the liver or for other related receptors examined (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. S5a, b). Given the role of intestinal FXR-ceramide signaling in metabolic diseases29, we measured ceramide levels in mouse plasma and ileum and found that 4-OHPhe exposure significantly elevated them (Fig. 6b, c), suggesting that 4-OHPhe promotes obesity by activating this pathway. Subsequently, the expression of genes related to ceramide synthesis and catabolism was examined in the ileal tissues of mice. We found that 4-OHPhe exposure did not affect ceramide catabolism (Supplementary Fig. S5c) but increased ceramide levels by promoting its synthesis (Fig. 6d–f). Additionally, the study showed that the intestinal microbiota mediated the effect of 4-OHPhe on ceramide levels (Supplementary Fig. S5d–g). After supplementation with B. uniformis, there were no significant differences in ceramide levels in the plasma and ileum between the 4-OHPhe-exposed and vehicle groups (Supplementary Fig. S5h, i). Finally, we also discovered that in the B. uniformis mutant with xylan added, the exposure to 4-OHPhe no longer significantly affected the levels of ceramides (Supplementary Fig. S4n, o).

To validate the critical role of intestinal FXR in the 4-OHPhe-exposed group of mice, we placed intestinal-specific Fxr knockout (FxrΔIE) mice (n = 6 per group) and floxed control (Fxrfl/fl) mice (n = 6 per group) on an HFD and subjected them to an 8-week exposure to 4-OHPhe (Fig. 6g). The results showed that there were no significant differences in food intake, body weight, body fat, RER, EE, and insulin sensitivity in FxrΔIE mice with or without exposure to 4-OHPhe (Fig. 6h–n and Supplementary Fig. S5j–o). Furthermore, in FxrΔIE mice, thermogenesis was no longer inhibited by 4-OHPhe exposure and the expression of WAT thermogenesis-related genes was no longer suppressed (Fig. 6o–q and Supplementary Fig. S5p–r). These results suggest that intestinal FXR plays a crucial role in 4-OHPhe-induced obesity exacerbation.

HDCA ameliorates 4-OHPhe induced obesity exacerbation by inhibiting the intestinal FXR-ceramide signaling pathway

4-OHPhe exposure decreased HDCA levels in mice and activated the intestinal FXR-ceramide signaling pathway. Based on these findings, we administered exogenous HDCA by oral gavage (C57BL/6J mice, n = 8 per group) to determine if it ameliorates 4-OHPhe-induced obesity by inhibiting this pathway (Fig. 7a). After 4 weeks of HDCA treatment, there were no significant differences in food intake between the groups (Supplementary Fig. S6a); however, body weight, body fat mass, and body fat percentage were significantly lower in the HDCA-treated 4-OHPhe exposure group compared with the non-HDCA-treated 4-OHPhe exposure group (Fig. 7b–d and Supplementary Fig. S6b). Meanwhile, the improvements in GTT, ITT, and HOMA-IR index, along with a reduced insulin level, indicated enhanced insulin sensitivity in HDCA-treated mice exposed to 4-OHPhe (Supplementary Fig. S6c–h).

We then examined the expression of Fxr and its target genes in the ileum. HDCA treatment inhibited the intestinal FXR signaling pathway in 4-OHPhe-exposed mice compared to those without HDCA treatment (Fig. 7e). Similarly, the plasma and ileal ceramide pools were reduced in HDCA-treated, 4-OHPhe-exposed mice (Fig. 7f, g). Discussion

Obesity is a multifactorial condition, and environmental pollutants are increasingly emerging as significant risk factors. As a common PAH pollutant, Phe enters the body through multiple pathways to exert its toxic effects30. However, it does not persist stably within the body, as it is efficiently metabolized by both the host and the gut microbiota into a series of metabolites9,31. Among the metabolites of Phe, OHPhe has garnered greater attention, with numerous epidemiological studies establishing a positive association between its exposure and obesity risk13,14. Using SHAP and logistic regression analysis, we identified 4-OHPhe as the environmental chemical most strongly associated with obesity among 49 obesity-related compounds. These findings prompted us to investigate the mechanism of 4-OHPhe-induced obesity. Exposure to environmental pollutants can alter the gut microbiota, and such alterations are implicated in obesity pathogenesis32,33. However, whether the gut microbiota mediates 4-OHPhe-induced obesity remains unknown. In this study, we discovered a mechanism for 4-OHPhe-induced obesity: alteration of the gut microbiota composition. Exposure to 4-OHPhe exacerbates obesity by downregulating the hcp gene in B. uniformis, which impairs ROS clearance, reduces bacterial abundance, and thereby disrupts bile acid metabolism, ultimately activating the intestinal FXR-ceramide signaling pathway.

The gut microbiota plays a profound role in regulating host metabolic homeostasis16. Paradoxically, despite this critical function, the microbial community is inherently unstable and highly vulnerable to external and internal perturbations, such as environmental pollution, diet, and host genetics34–36. Pseudo-germ-free mice created through antibiotic treatment can effectively simulate the absence of intestinal flora and determine whether external factors continue to play a role in the progression of disease19,37. We found that depleting the gut microbiota abolished the obesity-exacerbating effect of 4-OHPhe in HFD-fed mice. Moreover, FMT experiments confirmed that the 4-OHPhe-triggered obesity phenotype is transmissible via the gut microbiota. These data suggest that 4-OHPhe promotes obesity not by targeting host pathways directly, but by altering the gut microbial community. Our further analysis revealed that 4-OHPhe exposure significantly reduced the abundance of the order Bacteroidales. Through integrated analysis of bile acid metabolic shifts and correlation data, we ultimately established the critical role of B. uniformis in driving obesity upon 4-OHPhe exposure. While exposure to 4-OHPhe reduced the abundance of most members within the order Bacteroidales, it increased that of specific species, including P. goldsteinii and B. thetaiotaomicron. These enriched Bacteroidales species have been reported in the literature to be associated with weight loss and bile acid metabolism. For example, P. goldsteinii is involved in the metabolism of cholic acid (CA) and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), and B. thetaiotaomicron has been linked to the metabolism of Tauro-β-muricholic acid (TβMCA)38,39. However, these reported functions are not consistent with the main conclusions of our study. While the specific functions of these enriched Bacteroidales species were not investigated in detail in this study, their increased abundance highlights a distinct microbial response, which may provide valuable insights for future research on pollutant-induced shifts in community function.

Previous reports have characterized B. uniformis as a potential beneficial bacteria, with increased abundance shown to ameliorate metabolic diseases including obesity and metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD)40–42. Given this protective role, the reduction in its abundance induced by 4-OHPhe demands explanation. Although intestinal microbial composition profoundly influences host physiology, the molecular mechanisms through which environmental pollutants alter the microbiota to induce obesity are not fully elucidated43. We therefore investigated the interaction between 4-OHPhe and B. uniformis. To survive under stress, bacteria adapt by altering their metabolism, cellular architecture, and group behavior44,45. Consistent with this adaptive response, our transcriptome analysis revealed that amino acid transport and metabolism were the most significantly upregulated pathways in B. uniformis following 4-OHPhe exposure. Remarkably, we observed that the hcp gene, which is involved in glutamine synthesis, was the most significantly downregulated. This gene encodes the hybrid cluster protein (HCP), an enzyme found in many bacteria that catalyzes the reduction of hydroxylamine to NH₃ and H₂O and is also annotated as hydroxylamine reductase46. hcp is a critical gene for defense against both oxidative and nitrosative stress46. Downregulation of the hcp gene by 4-OHPhe would likely result in the accumulation of hydroxylamine, thereby inducing the generation of ROS and reactive nitrogen species (RNS), ultimately leading to bacterial cell death47,48. Although hcp is recognized as a significant stress-related gene in bacteria, its specific role in Bacteroides has not been investigated. To address this gap, a xylan-induced promoter was inserted in front of the hcp gene (xylP-hcp), which responds to xylan exposure36. Our results demonstrated a significant increase in hydroxylamine reductase and reduced ROS levels in the xylP-hcp strain. Moreover, the MIC of 4-OHPhe for the xylP-hcp strain was 16 times higher than that of the wild-type strain. This study elucidates the molecular mechanism by which 4-OHPhe impacts the abundance of B. uniformis. Our data demonstrate that the hcp gene is essential for B. uniformis survival under 4-OHPhe stress. However, the mechanism by which 4-OHPhe suppresses hcp expression remains unclear, warranting further study to elucidate the underlying molecular events.

Exposure to environmental pollutants can disrupt host bile acid metabolism, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of metabolic diseases; however, the precise underlying mechanisms remain elusive49,50. Analysis of the bile acid profile following 4-OHPhe exposure revealed significant alterations. Notably, we observed a substantial decrease in HDCA, in contrast to a significant increase in its taurine-conjugated metabolite, THDCA. B. uniformis contributes to bile acid homeostasis by highly expressing BSH26, an enzyme that hydrolyzes conjugated bile acids such as THDCA to generate their unconjugated forms (HDCA). Collectively, our data demonstrate that the disruption of bile acid metabolism by 4-OHPhe is primarily mediated by a reduction in B. uniformis abundance.

Our results demonstrated that exposure to 4-OHPhe led to a significant decrease in HDCA levels. To determine if this reduction impacted key bile acid signaling, we examined the important receptor FXR and its downstream pathways. We observed a differential regulation of FXR signaling by 4-OHPhe. Specifically, it activated the intestinal FXR-ceramide pathway but had no appreciable effect on hepatic FXR signaling. The activation of the intestinal FXR-ceramide pathway has been implicated in metabolic disease pathogenesis51,52. This is largely attributable to ceramide, a lipid species whose elevated levels are a well-established risk factor for obesity, cardiovascular disease, and diabetes53,54. Our research reveals that 4-OHPhe promotes obesity by indirectly stimulating ceramide synthesis, a process potentially mediated by intestinal FXR. To test this, we generated intestine-specific FXR knockout (FxrΔIE) mice. In this study, we found that compared to floxed control mice, Fxr knockout mice exhibited a slightly improved metabolic phenotype, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. In stark contrast, 4‑OHPhe exposure exacerbated obesity by specifically activating intestinal FXR signaling, an effect that was completely abolished in FxrΔIE mice. These results definitively establish intestinal FXR as the critical mediator of 4‑OHPhe‑induced obesity. Although targeting intestinal FXR has long been a therapeutic strategy for metabolic diseases, numerous bile acids are known to act as FXR antagonists to exert beneficial effects55,56. Given that HDCA is a known gut FXR antagonist that ameliorates metabolic disease by inhibiting intestinal FXR signaling, we administered it to 4-OHPhe-exposed mice. HDCA treatment alleviated 4-OHPhe-induced obesity, underscoring its therapeutic potential.

In summary, our results reveal the specific mechanism by which 4-OHPhe induces obesity. 4-OHPhe exposure impairs the ROS scavenging capacity of B. uniformis. This leads to a decline in its abundance and a consequent reduction in HDCA levels. The decrease in HDCA, a known FXR antagonist, thereby activates the intestinal FXR-ceramide signaling pathway. This cascade ultimately suppresses WAT thermogenic capacity, exacerbating the obese phenotype. These findings elucidate a link between environmental pollutants and metabolic disease, mediated through regulation of the gut microbiota.

Methods

Study population and environmental chemicals

The data were obtained from the 2011 to 2012 cycle of the NHANES, conducted by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. The survey protocol was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Institutional Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to study enrollment. Comparable chemical data from other years could not be harmonized, although similar information on environmental exposures was available. All data are publicly available through the NHANES website. After excluding participants younger than 18 years and those with incomplete data on environmental chemical measures or obesity status, a total of 539 individuals remained for analysis.

A total of 49 substances across nine categories were analyzed: 11 urinary metals, 11 phthalates, 9 polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), 5 phenols, 5 polyfluoroalkyl chemicals (PFCs), 3 salts, 3 volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and 2 pesticides. Detailed information on these chemicals is provided in Supplementary Table S2. Urinary concentrations of environmental chemicals were adjusted for creatinine to account for variations in dilution. To improve normality, all environmental chemical concentrations were natural log-transformed.

Mouse models

All mice were male and housed in a specific pathogen free (SPF) environment. 8-week-old C57BL/6J mice were housed at 23 ± 1 °C, humidity (45−55%) on a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle and had free access to food and water. In animal experiments, 8-week-old male mice were randomly assigned to different groups, and no mice were excluded from the analysis. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Research Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University (TMULA-2025023).

Mice were subjected to GTT and ITT for each group seven days prior to the euthanasia, and to body fat content and fat percentage assessments by an MRI body fat meter (Echo MRI) two days prior to the euthanasia. To stimulate thermogenesis, all mice were subjected to hypothermia induction at 4 °C for 48 h prior to execution. Plasma was collected to determine bile acid levels and ceramide levels. WAT, BAT, feces, cecum and ileum were quickly frozen and stored at −80 °C.

Bacterial strains and culture

The B. uniformis strain was purchased from China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC). The strain species was confirmed by amplifying the 27f and 1492r primer and aligning them with those in the NCBI reference database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). B. uniformis was cultured in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth at 37 °C in an anaerobic chamber for 24–48 h. Cell pellets were collected by centrifugation at 8000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and oxygen-free PBS was used to resuspend cells to a final cell density of 1 × 109 colony-forming units CFU)/mL. The cell suspension (200 μL) was then used for the animals’ oral administration every other day.

The information on the chemical and biomolecular materials used in this study is provided in Supplementary Table S6.

Obesity assessment and covariates

Body mass index (BMI) was used to define generalized obesity. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared, and a BMI of ≥30.0 kg/m² was classified as generalized obesity.

Smoking status was defined as having smoked at least 100 cigarettes in a lifetime, and drinking status as consuming more than 12 alcoholic drinks per year. Calorie intake was estimated from the mean dietary energy intake recorded across two 24-h dietary recall visits. Metabolic equivalent tasks (METs) were used to quantify physical activity. The poverty income ratio (PIR), a widely used indicator of socioeconomic status, was calculated as the ratio of household income to the survey-year poverty threshold. Hypertension was defined as a physician diagnosis, a mean systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg, a mean diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive medication. Diabetes mellitus was defined as a physician diagnosis, fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥ 6.5 %, or current use of glucose-lowering medication.

Machine learning

XGBoost is an optimized gradient boosting algorithm that is well suited for structured data, offering fast and scalable performance with built-in regularization to reduce overfitting. In this study, XGBoost was applied to identify environmental chemicals most strongly associated with generalized obesity. For standardization, continuous variables were normalized to a 0–1 range using Min–Max scaling, and categorical variables were encoded using one-hot encoding. The model was trained and validated using 10-fold cross-validation, which is particularly advantageous for complex models and small datasets, as it improves stability, generalization, and data efficiency. To optimize performance, grid search was used to tune model hyperparameters. SHAP, based on cooperative game theory, were used to quantify the contribution of each feature to model predictions, thereby improving interpretability of the complex machine learning model. The XGBoost model employed SHAP-based visualizations to assess the relative importance of variables.

Mouse experiment

In the 4-OHPhe intervention study, 8-week-old C57BL/6J mice (male, n = 8 per group) were acclimatized for one week. Mice were randomized into two groups based on whether they were exposed to 4-OHPhe. All mice received a high-fat diet (HFD) (Research diets, Cat# D12492). Based on doses reported in previous literature23,24,57,58, mice in the 4-OHPhe-exposed group received a daily oral dose of 4-OHPhe (2.5 mg/kg body weight, dissolved in corn oil) for 8 weeks. The dose of the gavage reagent was adjusted according to the body weight of the mice. The control group received the same volume of corn oil intervention daily.

In the antibiotic treatment study, the antibiotic mixture was added to the drinking water of SPF 8-week-old C57BL/6J mice (male, n = 6 per group) and continued for seven days. The broad‑spectrum antibiotic combination consisted of vancomycin (50 mg/kg, Sigma), metronidazole (100 mg/kg, Sigma), kanamycin (100 mg/kg, Sigma), and ampicillin (100 mg/kg, Sigma)37,59. Mice were then treated with the 4-OHPhe intervention and superimposed on an HFD for 8 weeks.

In the FMT study, recipient mice (8-week-old, male, n = 8 per group) were pretreated with antibiotics as described above. Feces (20 mg) were collected from the cecum of vehicle or 4-OHPhe-exposed mice, homogenized in pre-diluted PBS (1 mL per sample), shaken for 3 min, and then centrifuged at 4 °C for 3 min. Then 200 µl of supernatant was collected. A suspension of 100 microliters of the precipitate was given to recipient mice by oral gavage.

In the HDCA treatment study, C57BL/6J mice (8-week-old, male, n = 8 per group) after 8 weeks of exposure to 4-OHPhe were divided into two groups, and mice were orally administered HDCA (100 mg/kg) or vehicle daily during HFD feeding. After 4 weeks of HDCA treatment, mice were cold-induced (4 °C) for 48 h and then executed.

In the B. uniformis administration study, B. uniformis strain was purchased from China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC). The strain was confirmed by comparing the 27f and 1492r gene amplification sequence with those in the NCBI reference database (htps:/www.ncbi.nim.nih.gov/). B. uniformis was cultured in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth at 37 °C in an anaerobic chamber for 24–48 h. Cell pellets were collected by centrifugation at 8000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and oxygen-free PBS was used to resuspend cells to a final cell density of 1 × 108 colony-forming units (CFU)/mL. The cell suspension (200 μL) was then used for the animals’ oral administration every other day. C57BL/6J mice (8-week-old, male, n = 8 per group) exposed to 4-OHPhe for 8 weeks were similarly divided into two groups, and mice were treated with pure B. uniformis (108 CFU/mouse) or saline orally every other day for 4 weeks during the HFD feeding period.

Eight-week-old male Fxrfl/fl and FxrΔIE mice (male, n = 6 per group) were orally administered 4-OHPhe (2.5 mg/kg) or corn oil daily for 8 weeks. The critical role played by intestinal FXR in 4-OHPhe-induced obesity was successfully evaluated.

In an administration study using the xylP-hcp mutant of B. uniformis, mice were treated with either the wild-type bacterium (108 CFU/mouse) or the mutant (108 CFU/mouse). Following xylan supplementation, all C57BL/6J mice (8-week-old, male, n = 6 per group) were gavaged with corn oil or 4-OHPhe (2.5 mg/kg body weight) for two consecutive weeks.

Metabolic assays

After fasting for 16 h, GTT were performed. First, fasting blood glucose was measured, then glucose solution (2 g/kg body weight) was injected into the abdominal cavity, and blood glucose levels were measured at 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min.

ITT were tested after fasting for 5 h. Insulin (0.6 U/kg body weight) was injected intraperitoneally, and blood glucose levels were measured at 15, 30, 45, 60, and 90 min.

Metabolic cage

Indirect calorimetry experiments, including RER and energy expenditure measurements, were conducted using an automatic metabolic cage (Sable promethion core system, USA). Before the experiment, allow the mice to adapt to the metabolic cage environment for 3 days to reduce the impact of stress on the experimental results. Set the data collection frequency according to the experimental requirements to ensure the continuity and accuracy of the data.

Temperature measurement

Rectal temperature measured with a digital thermomete. Surface temperatures around the inguinal scWAT and interscapular BAT were recorded using a thermal imaging camera, and the temperatures were quantified.

Real-time PCR and RNA-seq profiling

Take an appropriate amount of mouse tissue or fresh cultured B.uniformis from liquid nitrogen and extract total RNA from the frozen tissue using the standard phenol-chloroform method with TRIzol reagent. cDNA was synthesized from 2 µg of total RNA using a reverse transcription kit. The sequences of real-time PCR primers for the expressed genes in mice and B. uniformis are shown in Supplementary Table S7. RT-qPCR was performed on an ABI7500fast (USA) Real-Time PCR System (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, ltd) with Hieff UNICON® Universal blue qPCR SYBR green master mix (Takara, USA). Reaction mixtures (20 μL total volume) contained final concentration of forward and reverse primers: 0.2 µmol, template: 0.25 µmol and 1× master mix, and amplification conditions were as follows: 95 °C 2 min, 40 cycles of 95 °C 10 s and 60 °C 30 s. All reactions were performed in triplicate technical replicates, target-specific positive controls, and negative controls (DEPC-water substituted for template). Calculating mice’s relative gene expression level was standardized based on the 18S rRNA, while relative mRNA expression of B. uniformis was normalized to 16S rRNA using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method.

Freshly cultured wild-type B. uniformis were incubated in triplicate with 4-OHPhe (1 mg/L of commercial solution) anerobically at 37 °C for 48 h under 180 rpm shaking. Cultures were centrifuged, supernatants discarded, and bacterial RNA extracted using the Spin Column Bacteria Total RNA Purification Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai) following the manufacturer’s protocols. Purified RNA samples were transferred to Sangon (Shanghai ltd) for sequencing on the Illumina Hiseq™. Gene quality was evaluated and filtered by FastQC and quality-controlled sequencing reads were aligned to the reference genome with Bowtie2. Alignment statistics were subsequently evaluated with RSeQCDifferentially expressed genes (DEGseq) were determined by featureCounts defined as those with (log₂ (Fold Change) >1 and qValue <0.05). All analyses and visualizations were performed using IGV (Integrative Genomics Viewer). Transcriptomic data are deposited in the GSA under accession.

Western blot

Tissues were homogenized in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein extracts were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) on 10% gels and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked at room temperature for 1 h in Tris/Tween-buffered saline containing 10% BSA, followed by incubation overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies specific to UCP1 or β-actin. After washing, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies.

Metagenomic sequencing and analysis

After Sample QC, sample DNA for metagenomics was extracted using MagPure Fast Stool DNA KF Kit B (catalog no. MD5115-02B; Magen Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). 500 ng of Meta DNA was fragmented by ultrasound on Covaris E220 (Covaris, Brighton, UK), then selected to 300 bp ∼700 bp by magnet beads size-selection. The selected DNA fragments were repaired, then ligated with indexed adapter. The ligation product was amplified by PCR, hybridized with exon probe, and captured by streptavidin beads, The Captured DNA was amplified again by PCR, and circularized to get single-stranded circular(ssCir) library. The ssCir library was then amplified through rolling circle amplification (RCA) to obtain DNA nanoball (DNB). The DNB was then loaded to flowcell and sequenced by DNBSEQ Platform.

The bioinformatics analysis started with raw sequence files. To check the quality of the reads, SOAPnuke filter was used, adapters and low-quality reads were removed. The remaining reads were aligned with bowtie2 and assembled using MEGAHIT. Coding sequences were predicted with Prodigal and clustered into unigenes using CD-HIT. For metagenomic phylogenetic analysis, MetaPhlAn 3.0 was used, and then produced a table containing the detected microbes and their relative abundances. eggNOG mapper was used for functional annotation. Diversity analysis, LEfSe (Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size) and Spearman’s correlations analysis were conducted by OmicStudio cloud platform60. Visualization was performed using cladograms and bar charts generated via the R package ggplot2 (version 3.2.0).

Metabolomics analysis

Bile acid profiles were determined as previously described19. A total of 184 samples were analyzed in this study, including 92 plasma samples and 92 ileum samples. After the pretreatment of plasma and ileum samples was completed, the samples were analyzed using the Eksigent Ultra Liquid Chromatography 100 system, which is coupled with the AB 5600 Triple TOF system (AB SCIEX). The chromatographic column used was the XBridge Peptide BEH C18 column (3.5 μm, 2.1 ×100 mm; Waters Corp.). The column temperature was 40°C, the flow rate was 0.4 mL/min, and the mobile phase consisted of A: water +0.1% formic acid +10 mM acetic amine, B: 80% methanol +20% acetonitrile +0.1% formic acid. The elution gradient began at 35% B (v/v) for 0.5 min; linearly increased to 60% B over 2.5 min; then to 80% B over 7 min; then to 90% B over 6 min; before finally decreasing linearly to 35% B over 4.5 min and holding for 2.5 min for column re-equilibration. Key parameters for ESI-MS analysis in negative mode on the Triple TOF instrument were set as follows: ion source gases 1 and 2 at 50, curtain gas at 30, source temperature 550 °C, ion spray voltage −4500 V. Scans were acquired from m/z 200-800 (TOF-MS) and m/z 50–800 (auto-MS/MS) with an accumulation time of 19.993 min, collision energy of −45 ± 20 V, and declustering potential of −80 V. The internal standards used were CA-d4, LCA-d4, and UDCA-d4. First, calibration curves for each analyte were constructed using six concentration points. Then, a mixed QC sample (15 bile acid standards in a blank matrix) was analyzed after every 10 experimental samples to monitor instrument performance. Based on five replicate injections, the within-batch RSD for all analytes was below 8%, all of which demonstrated excellent method repeatability. The limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) for the bile acids are provided in Supplementary Table S8.

We determined the content of ceramides using a method as described previously59. This study analyzed 232 samples, consisting of 116 plasma samples and 116 ileum samples. The following parameters were used in negative ion mode: curtain gas, 30 psi; ion source gas 1, 50 psi; ion source gas 2, 50 psi; source temperature, 550 °C; ion spray voltage, −4500 V; collision energy, −35 ± 15 V; declustering potential, −80 V. The temperature was set at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of (A) water and (B) a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of acetonitrile and isopropanol, both containing 10 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid. A gradient elution was performed with the following profile: 35% B at 0 min, ramped to 80% B at 2 min, further to 100% B at 9 min, held at 100% B until 15 min, then decreased to 35% B at 16 min, and finally held at 35% B until 20 min. Ceramides were identified and quantified using Peak View 1.2 and Multi Quant 2.1, respectively, based on their m/z values and retention times. The internal standard was ceramide 19:0. Calibration curves for each analyte were constructed using seven concentration points. To ensure analytical performance, a mixed QC sample (seven ceramide standards in blank matrix) was analyzed after every 10 samples. Based on 5 replicates, the within-batch RSD for all ceramides was below 8%, confirming excellent repeatability. The LOD and LOQ for the ceramides are provided in Supplementary Table S9.

MIC determination

The MIC of 4-OHPhe was determined using the standard broth microdilution method described by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute. Moreover, xylan was added to the final concentration of 10 mg/L.

Glutamine, ammonia and hydroxylamine reductase detection

Glutamine was detected by the Elabscience® Colorimetric Assay Kit by following the manufacturer’s instructions. In general, 1 × 106 (or 0.1 g rat intestinal content) bacterial cells were pelleted, add homogenized in 200 μL saline. The samples were centrifuged at 4 °C and 12,000 × g for 15 min. Then, the supernatant for glutamine detection was filtered through 50 kDa ultrafiltration tube by centrifugation. The filtrate was added to an antibody-embedded 96-well plate. Meanwhile, standards were also diluted and added to a 96-well plate. The absorbance was detected at 450 nm. The standard curve and statistical analysis were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Ammonia was detected by BYSH-0436W ammonia detection kit (Leye.Bio). 1 × 106 or 0.1 g bacterial cells were pelleted and homogenized in 1 ml lysis buffer. Then, the samples were centrifuged at 4 °C and 12,000 × g for 15 min, and the supernatant for ammonia was stored on ice for use. The samples and standards were mixed with reagents in 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. The absorbance was detected at 630 nm, and the concentration of ammonia was calculated according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

An ELISA kit (Byabscience, ltd, China) was used for the quantitative detection of hydroxylamine reductase (HR). Briefly, bacterial cells were lysed by ultrasonication, and supernatant were collected for test. In general, samples and standards were added to the wells, followed by an HRP-conjugated detection antibody. After incubation and washing, TMB substrate is added for color development, which is stopped with sulfuric acid. The absorbance is measured at 450 nm. A standard curve is plotted using absorbance values of the standards, and sample concentrations are determined by interpolation.

ROS detection

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) were determined by Chemiluminescence kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, ltd). The bacterial cells were pelleted and washed by PBS. The pellete were then resuspended in 10 µM probes and incubated in the dark for 30 min. Then the pellets were washed 3 times with PBS, and the ROS were detected at excitation wavelength 488 nm and emission wavelength at 525 nm.

Genetic modification of hcp gene

pLGB31 vector was used to genetically manipulate hcp gene. In general, 1000 bp of the hcp upstream gene, xylan-inducible promoter and hcp gene with xmaI and claI restriction enzymes were synthesized and combined in the pUC57 vector. pLGB31 was purchased from Addgene. Briefly, 4 µg pUC57 and 4 µg pLGB31 were incubated with 1 unit xmaI (NEB, UK) and 1 unit claI (NEB, UK) in 50 µL reaction volume at 37 °C overnight. The digestion products were run by gel, and the target sequences were cut by scarple. The target sequences were then purified by gel purification kit. Then the sequences were combined with pLGB31 vector by T4 DNA ligase (NEB, UK) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The positive clones were determined by PCR and sequencing (Supplementary Table S10).

Escherichia coli strains were cultured aerobically at 37 °C in LB medium (supplemented with 100 μg/mL carbenicillin when required), and B. uniformis was cultured anaerobically at 37 °C on brain heart infusion (BHI) agar supplemented with 5 mg/L hemin and 2.5 μg/L vitamin K₁ (BHIS). M9S plates were used for screening, consisting of M9 minimal medium supplemented with 50 mg/L L-cysteine, 5 mg/L hemin, 2.5 μg/L vitamin K₁, 2 mg/L FeSO₄·7H₂O, and 5 μg/L vitamin B₁₂. The carbon source was 0.25% (w/v) inulin (M9S-inulin; inulin from chicory, Sigma-Aldrich).

Conjugation and screening were performed according to Leonor García-Bayona et al. with gentle modifications61. In general, B. uniformis (0.5 mL) and E. coli S17-λpir (5 mL) were grown to OD600 0.1–0.2 and 0.2–0.6, respectively. Cells were pelleted (9000 × g, 10 min) and resuspended in 100 μL BHIS. Suspensions were spot-plated onto pre-warmed BHIS plates and incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 48–72 h. Conjugation mixtures were streaked onto selective M9S-inulin plates. Single colonies were picked after 2–4 days of anaerobic culture for PCR verification. After PCR-confirmed cointegrate formation, cultures were diluted following overnight growth and plated (50/5/1/0.2 μL) onto plates containing 10 mM rhamnose. The single colonies were picked and checked by PCR.

Statistics and reproducibility

The experimental data are shown as the mean ± SEM values. The normal distribution of the data was determined by the Shapiro–Wilk normality test. For statistical comparisons, Student’s t test or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test was used to compare normally distributed variables. Non-normally distributed data were compared by the Mann–Whitney U test or the Kruskal–Wallis test. Spearman’s correlations between changes in microbial species and host bile acid levels were calculated based on species with significant differences between the two groups. Benjamini–Hochberg procedure with a cutoff of 0.1 was applied to all Spearman’s correlations. p < 0.05 was considered significant. All the bar plots in this study were generated by GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). For microbiota analysis, community composition analysis, indicator species analysis, alpha/beta diversity analysis was conducted and visualized via R Studio (version 4.2.1). BSH detection was performed using blastp (v 2.13.0+) with a threshold coverage >80% and identity >30%. We conducted logistic regression analyses to estimate the odd ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for the associations between the top five chemicals and obesity. Basic model: adjusted for age (continuous), sex (male/female), and race (Non-Hispanic White [reference], Non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, Other). Multivariable model (fully adjusted): further adjusted for education (college below [reference], college and above), poverty-income ratio (PIR, continuous), smoking status (former/never [reference], current), alcohol use (former/never [reference], current), physical activity (total MET-minutes/week, continuous), total energy intake (kcal/day, continuous), hypertension (yes/no), and diabetes (yes/no). Residual and influence diagnostics were conducted to evaluate the mode’s adequacy and to detect influential observations. The residual and Cook’s distance plots confirm that the logistic regression models were well fitted and the robust, with no evidence of outliers of influential points affecting the overall results (Supplementary Fig. S7a, b). To address potential dependencies among obesity, hypertension, and diabetes, we performed a series of sensitivity tests by sequentially excluding hypertension, diabetes, and both variables from the multivariable logistic regression model. Additionally, we assessed multicollinearity using variance inflation factors (VIF). All variables showed variance inflation factor (VIF) below 2 (range: 1.10–1.69), indicating an absence of significant multicollinearity.
