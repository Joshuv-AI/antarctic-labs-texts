Machine learning prediction and optimization of thermodynamic analysis and energy enhancement of a hybrid infrared dryer for onion slices
Authors: Hany S El-Mesery, Mansuur Husein, Ahmed H ElMesiry, Abdulaziz Nuhu Jibril, Sabah Mounir, Zicheng Hu, Patrick B Njobeh, Amer Ali Mahdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMachine Learning (ML) and Artificial Intelligence (AI) are important tools for modelling drying processes to reduce moisture and preserve food products. This study investigated the drying performance of an industrial infrared conveyor belt drying system on onion slices under different drying conditions. The effects of drying temperature, infrared intensity, and airflow rates were evaluated. The results demonstrated that increasing IR power and air temperature significantly reduced drying time by 44.23%. Effective moisture diffusivity increased from 0.238 × 10⁻¹⁰ to 0.457 × 10⁻¹⁰ m²/s, indicating enhanced internal moisture transport at elevated thermal inputs. The lowest Sect. (10.72 kWh/kg) was achieved at 600 W, 65 °C, and 0.3 m/s, while the highest (22.26 kWh/kg) occurred at low temperature and high airflow conditions. Thermal efficiency improved with increasing temperature and radiation intensity, reaching a maximum of 21.92%. However, the Artificial Neural Network model exhibited excellent predictive capability with a correlation coefficient (R) of 0.999, accurately estimating key drying parameters. Self-Organizing Map (SOM) analysis identified distinct operational clusters, revealing that higher air temperature and IR power reduced drying time and energy consumption, whereas increased airflow increased energy usage. Therefore, the study demonstrates that integrating AI and statistical tools provides a robust framework for optimizing industrial drying systems, enabling reduced energy consumption and improved process efficiency.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53110-5.Keywords: Artificial intelligence (AI), Onion slices, Hybrid dryer, SEC, ThermodynamicSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Onion (Allium cepa L.) is one of the most widely cultivated and consumed vegetable crops worldwide. It contains significant amounts of phenolic compounds, natural antioxidants, vitamins (A, B6, and C), and bioactive constituents with antimicrobial and health-promoting effects1,2. Drying is one of the most widely adopted preservation methods, enabling moisture reduction to safe levels (typically below 10%) while facilitating storage, transportation, and industrial utilization in the form of powders, flakes, or slices. Therefore, efficient drying methods are essential for converting fresh onions into value-added products, such as flakes, powders, and granules, which are widely used in the food processing industry3,4. Fresh onions’ moisture content gradually drops from 80% to less than 10%. Consequently, one of the most significant ways to extend its shelf life and maintain its healthy constituents is to dry it for5–7. It helps maintain food quality, extend its shelf life, and reduce post-harvest losses. Onions are among the most perishable agricultural products because they contain a high moisture content. This means that they need to be dried quickly for storage, transportation, and industrial use. Traditional drying methods often take a long time, consume substantial energy, and degrade product quality. So, there is a need to develop more efficient and environmentally friendly drying methods8–10.

Heat is transferred from the hot air to the material’s surface in conventional hot-air drying, followed by internal moisture migration toward the surface via diffusion. However, this method is often associated with several limitations, including long drying times, high energy consumption, uneven heat distribution, and deterioration of product quality, such as shrinkage, texture hardening, and loss of nutritional compounds11–13. Moreover, reliance on external heat transfer often results in inefficient energy use, as a significant portion of the supplied energy is lost to the surrounding environment. These challenges have prompted increasing interest in the development of advanced drying technologies that can enhance drying efficiency while preserving product quality14–16. Recently, infrared (IR) drying has emerged as a promising alternative or complementary technique to conventional convective drying. Infrared radiation transfers energy directly to the material via electromagnetic waves, which are absorbed and converted into thermal energy within the product17–19. This results in rapid volumetric heating, reduced temperature gradients, and enhanced moisture diffusion. Infrared drying offers several advantages compared to traditional hot-air drying, including shorter drying times, higher energy efficiency, improved product quality, and reduced dependence on airflow20,21. However, infrared drying alone may not always ensure effective removal of evaporated moisture from the product surface, thereby limiting overall efficiency4,22. The integration of infrared radiation with convective airflow in hybrid drying systems further enhances drying performance by combining the benefits of both internal heating and external moisture removal23,24.

Artificial intelligence is widely used for multiple nonlinear problem-solving and complicated system modeling. They are mostly used to execute activities entailing partial data sets, and when there is uncertain or limited information. The networks can process many inputs and estimate various outputs without prior knowledge of the underlying procedural linkages25–27. Several black-box models have been successfully employed for prediction and optimization in drying process modeling28. Artificial neural networks (ANNs) are among the most widely used black box models due to their ability to approximate complex nonlinear functions without requiring prior knowledge of underlying physical mechanisms. Support vector machines (SVMs) map input data to high-dimensional feature spaces using kernel functions to capture nonlinear relationships11. Random forests ensemble learning method that aggregates multiple decision trees to improve prediction accuracy and reduce overfitting26,29. Gradient boosting machines (GBM) iteratively correct prediction errors from weak learners to build robust models68. Adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference systems (ANFIS) combine neural network learning capabilities with fuzzy logic reasoning to handle uncertainty in drying parameters30. Each of these black box models offers distinct advantages depending on dataset size, nonlinearity, and required interpretability.

Recently, many researchers have successfully used artificial intelligence tools to simulate drying processes. For example, El-Mesery et al. 11 used artificial neural network models to predict energy use, drying time, thermal efficiency, and specific energy. The results agreed with R values of 0.9742, 0.9869, and 0.9963 for the testing, validation, and training datasets, respectively, using infrared drying techniques on garlic slices. Taghinezhad et al.31 reported regression coefficients exceeding 0.97 for predicting shrinkage, color, specific energy consumption, and moisture diffusivity in food products. Liu et al.32 explored the application of artificial neural networks (ANNs) in infrared–assisted convection dryers to carefully simulate the energy consumption and thermal performance of onion slices. It revealed that ANN achieved 99% prediction accuracy, while SOM achieved 97% clustering accuracy in identifying the quality characteristics of onions. El-Mesery et al.33 predicted the performance of microwave drying on garlic slices using an artificial neural network. The result demonstrated that the ANN model achieved the highest predictive accuracy for energy consumption, with a coefficient of determination (R²) of 0.9994 and a mean squared error (MSE) of 0.0026. In addition to supervised learning techniques, unsupervised and statistical methods provide valuable tools for data interpretation and dimensionality reduction. For example, the Self-Organizing Map (SOM) is widely used for clustering and visualization of high-dimensional datasets, enabling the identification of patterns and relationships among variables34,35. Similarly, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) reduces data complexity by transforming correlated variables into a smaller set of uncorrelated components, often explaining 80–90% or more of the total variance in drying studies36,37. The integration of these techniques with experimental analysis enhances the understanding of drying systems and supports multi-objective optimization38–40.

Consequently, prior research has largely focused on drying kinetics and product quality, with comparatively less attention given to energy optimization of the hybrid infrared drying system. The application of machine learning in this drying system has also been relatively limited, while energy-related parameters, such as specific energy consumption and thermal efficiency, remain underexplored. This study addresses these limitations by providing an integrated, industrial-scale evaluation of an infrared–convective conveyor-belt drying system. It combines advanced data-driven techniques, including artificial neural networks for predictive modeling, self-organizing maps for pattern recognition, and principal component analysis for dimensionality reduction, enabling both accurate prediction and deeper insight into the complex, nonlinear interactions governing drying performance and energy efficiency. The objectives of this study include; (a) to systematically examine the effects of infrared power, air temperature, and airflow velocity on drying kinetics and effective moisture diffusivity of onion slices; (b) to evaluate the energy performance of the system in terms of total energy consumption, specific energy consumption, and thermal efficiency; and (c) to develop and validate a robust predictive framework using artificial neural networks, supported by self-organizing maps and principal component analysis, for accurate estimation of drying behavior and identification of optimal operating conditions.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

Fresh onion samples were obtained from a local commercial supplier and stored at 4 °C in a refrigerator before experimentation. The onions were manually peeled and carefully sliced into uniform Sect. 5.0 ± 0.1 mm thick using a stainless-steel knife. Each slice underwent three thickness measurements using a precision calliper, and the average was used. To assess the moisture content of the sample, approximately 10 g was collected for analysis in accordance with AOAC41 guidelines, then dried at 105 °C for 24 h in an oven. The initial moisture content of the onion was approximately 88.97 ± 0.08% (wb).

Equipment

Before the experiments, all of the measuring tools used in this study were properly calibrated. A digital thermocouple (Type K, ± 1 °C accuracy, Testo SE & Co. KGaA, Germany) was used to monitor air temperature. A hot-wire anemometer (Model 405i, Testo SE & Co. KGaA, Germany) was used to measure air speed. It was accurate to within ± 0.1 m/s. A digital load cell (a HX711-based system with ± 0.01 g accuracy, SparkFun Electronics, USA) was used to measure the sample mass. Also, a digital power meter (Model DDS238-2, Zhejiang CHINT Instrument & Meter Co., Ltd., China) with an accuracy of 0.01 kWh was used to measure electricity usage. The quartz infrared lamps (Model QIH-1000 W, Philips Lighting, Netherlands) made up the infrared heating system.

Designing of the infrared hybrid dryer

The system consists of a hybrid drying unit that enhances drying efficiency by combining infrared radiation with hot-air convection. The main components include a material feeder, a belt-driven conveyor system, a drying chamber, an infrared heating unit, and a product collection section. The dryer measures approximately 3 m in length, 1.5 m in height, and 8 m in width, facilitating effective material flow and thermal treatment along the drying pathway. The drying chamber is divided into two equal compartments, each measuring 0.8 × 0.8 m. The compartments are constructed from 2 mm-thick stainless steel to ensure durability and corrosion resistance.

Drying procedure

Drying experiments were conducted under controlled conditions by varying air temperature (45, 55, and 65 °C), airflow velocity (0.3, 0.5, and 0.7 m/s), and infrared power (200, 400, and 600 W). 500 g of uniformly sliced onion samples were placed in the drying chamber for each experiment. The mass of the samples was continuously monitored using a high-precision digital load cell (Model WMS304C, Mettler-Toledo Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) (± 0.01 g accuracy). The system automatically recorded weight loss every 10 min. Drying was continued until a constant weight was achieved. All experiments were conducted in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. Activation energy

The activation energy (Ea) quantifies the energy threshold that moisture molecules must surpass to diffuse through a material. As described by the Arrhenius equation, the effective moisture diffusivity (Deff) exhibits a temperature-dependent behavior, highlighting the influence of thermal conditions on the rate of moisture migration within the sample46,47.

Energy consumption (EC) and specific energy consumption (SEC)

The energy consumed during the drying process was determined by summing the energy used by each component, including the fan, the hot-air method, and the infrared method. A digital electric counter with an accuracy of 0.01 kWh was used to measure the energy consumption of the blower, electrical heater, and infrared lamps. The energy required to remove 1 kg of moisture is expressed in kWh/kg. This energy is referred to as specific energy consumption (SEC) and was calculated using Eq. (9)48,49.

Thermal energy efficiency

Thermal efficiency was defined as the ratio of the latent heat required for water vaporization within the dried material to the total energy used to evaporate it50,51.

Machine learning modeling approaches

Artificial neural network (ANN) model

Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) are an advanced and flexible framework in artificial intelligence, crafted to capture intricate, nonlinear relationships present in datasets. Generally organized into three essential layers: input, hidden, and output, as artificial neural networks handle information by relaying signals through interconnected processing units referred to as neurones26,29. The neurons assign weights to inputs, apply activation functions, and continuously refine outputs through repeated learning cycles until the desired accuracy is achieved. Increasing the number of neurons can improve model precision by capturing complex patterns; however, it also requires more computational resources and extended training time. The design of an artificial neural network, encompassing the number of neurons and layers, is typically tailored to suit the particular issue at hand52–54. Self-organizing Map (SOM) analysis

The model architecture was advanced by incorporating a Self-organizing Map (SOM), an unsupervised learning algorithm that groups data according to similarity while preserving their topological relationships. SOM adeptly compresses high-dimensional input into a two-dimensional lattice, facilitating clear visualization and interpretation43,55. During training, neurons compete to respond to a specific input, with the neuron that provides the closest match being designated the “winner.” This mechanism is comprehensively outlined by Chokphoemphun et al.56. Utilizing SOM, the model systematically arranges outcomes into an organized grid; for instance, a 20 × 20 matrix yields 400 unique clusters or output neurons. This method facilitates the identification of ideal drying parameters and produces data patterns that are easy to interpret57. The hybrid ANN-SOM framework effectively enables reliable modeling of thermal drying behavior, supporting both predictive capabilities and exploratory analysis via clustering-based categorization58–60.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

To determine the main drivers of energy and thermal properties of onion slices, PCA was performed using Minitab 2022 LLC software to gain further statistical insights. This technique was employed to unravel the intricate relationships and variations in the energy and thermal characterization of onion slices. The PCA dataset was derived from the ANN outputs. The choice of the initial 2 PCs was guided by their contributions to variance and the magnitude of their eigenvalues, critical indicators in PCA61,62. In accordance with the Kaiser-Guttman rule, components with eigenvalues exceeding 1 were retained, yielding the identification of PC1 and PC2. These components were then used to graphically represent the relationships among various drying parameters observed in the onion experiments63,64.

ANOVA analysis

A factorial analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to evaluate the effects of air temperature, infrared (IR) power, and air velocity on the drying performance of onion slices. The response variables analyzed included drying time, effective moisture diffusivity (Deff), specific energy consumption (SEC), total energy consumption, and thermal efficiency. The analysis was performed using jamovi 2.4.11, considering all main effects and interaction effects among the independent variables65,66. Prior to analysis, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. Statistical significance was determined at a 95% confidence level (p < 0.05). Where significant differences were observed, post-hoc multiple comparison tests (Tukey’s HSD)67,68.

Statistical analysis

The artificial neural network (ANN) was trained using the back-propagation algorithm, which incorporates the sigmoid activation function to adjust weights during learning. A custom MATLAB script was developed to implement the ANN architecture. The dataset was randomly partitioned into 70% for training and 30% for testing to validate the model’s predictive capability. The optimal network structure comprised two hidden layers with a total of 12 neurons, striking an effective balance between model accuracy and generalization. Results and discussions

Drying time

The experimental findings illustrated in Fig. 2 offer a detailed insight into the interactive effects of infrared (IR) power, air temperature, and air velocity on the drying kinetics of onion slices in a dryer. The data indicate a steady decrease in drying time with increasing IR power, air temperature, and air velocity. At the minimum airflow of 0.3 m/s, the drying time was about 400 min at 200 W and 45 °C, decreasing to about 250 min at 600 W and 65 °C. A comparable yet more significant trend was observed at elevated airflows, especially at 0.7 m/s, where the drying time decreased from approximately 500 min (200 W, 45 °C) to under 300 min at 600 W and 65 °C. The observed inverse relationship between process time and drying parameters is consistent with the theoretical principles governing heat and mass transfer in convective-IR drying. Increasing the infrared power enhances the sample’s absorption of radiant energy, thereby accelerating the evaporation of surface moisture. Additionally, higher air temperatures increase the vapour pressure gradient between the product and the surrounding air, thereby increasing the drying rate30. Furthermore, increased air velocity increases convective heat transfer coefficients and facilitates moisture removal from the product surface. The interplay of these variables indicates a distinctly nonlinear drying behaviour, suggesting that conventional linear regression models may not adequately capture the intricacies of the system71.

The effect of infrared power and different air temperatures on the drying time of onion slices at various air velocities using a hybrid infrared dryer.

Drying time showed high sensitivity to fluctuations in airspeed, heating temperature, and IR intensity, with statistically significant interactions at the 1% level. As IR intensity increased, surface temperatures rose, elevating the internal vapor pressure and promoting faster moisture migration from the core to the exterior. This mechanism led to a considerable reduction in total drying time, a finding supported by multiple researchers. The observed drying behavior can be explained by the coupled heat and mass transfer mechanisms governing infrared-convective drying. Infrared radiation directly penetrates the material, generating internal heat and accelerating moisture diffusion, while convective airflow enhances surface evaporation by maintaining a vapor pressure gradient. The interaction between these mechanisms leads to nonlinear drying characteristics: increases in temperature and radiation intensity significantly enhance moisture mobility, whereas excessive airflow can induce surface cooling and reduce drying efficiency.

Effective moisture diffusivity

Figure 3 illustrates the impact of infrared (IR) power levels ranging from 200 to 600 W and air temperatures between 45 and 65 °C on the effective moisture diffusivity (𝐷eff) of onion slices, evaluated at three distinct air velocities of 0.3, 0.5, and 0.7 m/s, utilizing an infrared-convective conveyor belt dryer. The effective moisture diffusivity is a crucial parameter in mass transfer modelling, indicating internal water mobility and playing a vital role in modelling drying kinetics. Moisture diffusivities of the various systems are crucial because more complex mathematical models and connections that can provide a deeper understanding of the drying processes require information on specific mass transfer parameters. This crucial drying parameter is influenced by the geometry of the transfer system, fluid velocity, mass diffusivity, and viscosity72. The drying data-derived estimated effective moisture diffusivities show a thorough mass transfer property of moisture in the material. Using electromagnetic radiation to heat a material is known as infrared heating. Infrared radiation causes water molecules to vibrate when they enter biological materials. In a mobilized state, molecules require less energy to pass through the porous medium. The effective moisture diffusivity (Deff)) was estimated using the slopes method.

The effect of infrared power and air temperatures on Deff of onion slices at various air velocities using a hybrid infrared dryer.

The findings indicate a steady increase in 𝐷eff as air temperature and IR power increase, regardless of the air velocity. At a velocity of 0.3 m/s, the effective diffusivity increased from roughly 0.285 × 10⁻¹⁰ m²/s at a power of 200 W and a temperature of 45 °C to about 0.457 × 10⁻¹⁰ m²/s at 600 W and 65 °C. This increase is caused by the combined synergistic effects of elevated infrared power and higher air temperature31. When the air velocity was at 0.5 m/s, the effective diffusivity ranged from 0.263 × 10⁻¹⁰ m²/s at 200 W and 45 °C to 0.44 × 10⁻¹⁰ m²/s at 600 W and 65 °C. Similarly, at 0.7 m/s, 𝐷eff increased from 0.28 × 10⁻¹⁰ m²/s to 0.435 × 10⁻¹⁰ m²/s under the same extreme conditions. These comparable yet intensified trends suggest that enhanced convective airflow markedly improves internal moisture diffusion by increasing the evaporation rate and reducing resistance to moisture movement73. However, increasing IR power from 400 W to 600 W at 65 °C and 0.3 m/s yielded only a 13% improvement in 𝐷eff, confirming the onset of diminishing returns. Specifically, elevated air temperatures reduce the relative humidity of the drying air, thereby increasing the driving force for moisture migration from the product surface to the ambient air74. Notably, the peak effective diffusivity was 0.410 × 10⁻¹⁰ m²/s at 600 W, 65 °C, and 0.7 m/s. This observation is essential for improving processes and suggests diminishing returns at extreme operational settings for energy efficiency and product quality.

Activation energy

The Arrhenius equation was employed by plotting the natural logarithm of operative moisture diffusivity (ln Deff) against the inverse of total temperature (1/T) to estimate the activation energy. The linear trend from this plot provided the slope required for the (Ea) calculation. The activation energy varied between 8.00 and 12.11 kJ/mol across different experimental setups, consistent with the ranges typically observed in agricultural drying studies. Increasing airflow speed from 0.5 to 1.5 m/s increased activation energy across all infrared intensities. This pattern suggests that increased molecular agitation corresponds to a lower energy barrier for moisture migration. Furthermore, higher drying air temperatures and IR intensities tended to lower the (Ea), highlighting the importance of these parameters when optimizing the energy efficiency of drying systems74. Variations between these results and those reported in the present study can be attributed to differences in drying parameters and their influence on the effective moisture diffusivity in the material. Overcoming the energy barriers that govern moisture removal is fundamental to the drying process. Furthermore, alternative theoretical models have provided useful insights into the energetic challenges of drying kinetics73.

Energy consumption

Energy usage in a drying system is of vital consideration due to its significant influence on manufacturing costs. Figure 4 shows the energy needed to dehydrate onion slices under various drying settings. At an air temperature of 45 °C and an airflow of 0.7 m/s, the energy consumption decreased from 4.94 kWh at 200 W to 4.50 kWh at 400 W and 3.88 kWh at 600 W of IR power. This decline occurs because higher IR power accelerates volumetric heating and reduces drying time more significantly than the increase in instantaneous power draw. Under a lower airflow of 0.3 m/s at the same temperature, total energy requirements similarly dropped from 1.38 kWh (200 W) to 0.96 kWh (600 W), with the lower absolute values reflecting reduced convective heat losses. When maintaining a constant airflow of 0.3 m/s and IR power of 600 W, increasing the air temperature from 45 °C to 65 °C further reduced energy consumption from 0.96 kWh to 0.60 kWh, as the hotter, drier air enhances the vapor pressure gradient and moisture-carrying capacity, accelerating the drying process75. However, energy consumption tends to increase with rising air velocity, despite reductions associated with higher IR power and air temperature. At the lowest airflow of 0.3 m/s, energy consumption increased from approximately 1.0 kWh at 200 W to 1.4 kWh at 600 W, with the maximum energy requirement observed at 65 °C. This counterintuitive trend is attributed to greater convective heat losses at higher velocities, which reduce thermal efficiency and require longer or more intense operation to maintain drying conditions17–19. Conversely, at higher air velocities of 0.5 and 0.7 m/s, energy use increased, reaching approximately 2.6 kWh at the highest IR power and temperature combination. This promotes convective cooling of the product and increases heat losses to the surroundings, thereby requiring additional energy input to maintain the drying temperature36. Specific energy consumption

The specific energy consumption (SEC) for drying onion slices at different air velocities and varying infrared power levels across a range of air temperatures is shown in Fig. 5. The results revealed that at elevated air velocities (0.5 and 0.7 m/s), although SEC values consistently increased due to the additional energy required to maintain higher airflow and uniform drying. The lowest SEC occurred at higher radiation intensity (600 W) and a higher air temperature (65 °C), combined with the lowest airflow rate (0.3 m/s), reaching a value of 10.73 kWh/kg. For example, at 0.7 m/s and 45 °C, SEC decreased from 22.26 kWh/kg at 200 W to 18.25 kWh/kg at 600 W. These results indicate that higher airflow rates require greater energy to achieve an equivalent drying effect, likely because additional energy is needed to circulate the air and maintain a uniform drying rate. The results showed a decrease in SEC as radiation intensity increased and air velocity decreased. Since the sample surface inside the drying chamber is cooled by the airflow, this leads to overall heat loss, and the SEC increases with increasing air velocity. Onion slices can be dried more quickly with higher infrared intensities because they raise the sample’s temperature and evaporation rate22. However, the specific energy consumption reflects the energy required per unit mass of moisture removed and is therefore directly linked to the efficiency of moisture transport mechanisms. The reduction in SEC at higher IR power and air temperatures can be attributed to improved internal heat generation and enhanced moisture diffusivity25–27. Infrared radiation penetrates the material, generating volumetric heating that increases internal vapor pressure and facilitates faster moisture migration from the core to the surface. This reduces the resistance to mass transfer and ensures that a greater fraction of supplied energy is effectively utilized for moisture evaporation rather than being lost to the environment76.

Thermal efficiency

Thermal efficiency quantifies the effectiveness of energy utilization during drying and is expressed as the ratio of the latent heat required for water vaporization to the total energy consumed. Figure 6 illustrates the influence of IR power, air temperature, and airflow on the dryer’s thermal efficiency. Specifically, a maximum thermal efficiency of 21.92% was achieved at 65 °C, 0.3 m/s airflow, and 600 W IR power, while the lowest efficiency of 7.73% was observed at 45 °C and 0.7 m/s airflow. Specifically, at 0.3 m/s and 600 W, raising the temperature from 45 °C to 65 °C improved thermal efficiency from 11.5% to 16.0%; at 0.5 m/s and 600 W, the same temperature increase raised efficiency from 13.5% to 19.0%; and at 0.7 m/s and 600 W, efficiency increased from 14.5% to 21.0%. These improvements correspond to enhancements of 39%, 41%, and 45%, respectively, confirming that the positive effect of temperature on thermal efficiency becomes more pronounced at higher airflow rates31. The results show that thermal efficiency improves with increasing IR power and air temperature, reaching a maximum at the highest operational levels. This positive correlation (p < 0.05) is attributed to the increased temperature differential between the drying product and the surrounding air, which enhances heat transfer, accelerates moisture removal, reduces drying time, and lowers energy consumption77. Thermal efficiency is the proportion of supplied energy effectively utilized for moisture evaporation. The observed increase in thermal efficiency with higher IR power and air temperature is primarily driven by improved heat transfer mechanisms. Elevated temperatures increase the temperature gradient between the product and the drying air, thereby enhancing both conductive and convective heat transfer.

Machine learning modelling

An Artificial Neural Network (ANN) model was successfully developed ultimately for this purpose to predict key parameters associated with the drying process of onion slices. The model used air temperature, airflow rate, and infrared (IR) power as input variables, and its primary outputs included thermal efficiency, drying time, SEC, energy utilization metrics, and effective moisture diffusivity (Deff). The performance evaluation of the artificial neural network (ANN) model demonstrated its capability to accurately forecast critical output variables such as total energy consumption (kWh), specific energy consumption (kWh/kg), and thermal efficiency (%) as illustrated in Fig. 7. The impressive predictive performance highlights the ANN’s ability to model the intricate, nonlinear relationships among drying parameters. In contrast to conventional empirical or statistical regression techniques that frequently rely on linearity or require data transformation, artificial neural networks offer adaptability and can handle multidimensional datasets characterized by significant variability. This is especially beneficial in drying systems, where elements such as heat transfer, moisture diffusion, and energy input exhibit strong interdependencies and nonlinear relationships. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to the drying process, effectively reducing the dataset’s dimensionality while preserving its variance. PCA facilitates the identification of essential contributing variables by converting correlated inputs into uncorrelated principal components, as shown in Fig. 8. The first two principal components were chosen for interpretation based on the eigenvalues and total modification78. With PC1 and PC2 accounting for 81.87 and 14.80% of the variance, respectively, the cumulative explained variance was 89.94%, falling between 70 and 90%, the recommended range for sufficient variability explanation79. SEC is aligned with PC2 in the biplot, as seen by its placement on the positive side of that component. The positive aspect of PC2 is thermal efficiency, while that of PC1 is drying time. This study surpasses previous findings by explaining nearly 97% of the variance with the first two principal components, demonstrating the robustness and reliability of the dataset and variable selection. This validates the effectiveness of PCA in pinpointing the key variables and steering multi-objective optimisation for energy-efficient drying processes.

ANOVA (Post-hoc) analysis

To further interpret the significant effects identified by the factorial ANOVA, post-hoc multiple comparison tests using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) were conducted. The ANOVA results indicated that air temperature (p < 0.001), infrared (IR) power (p < 0.001), and air velocity (p < 0.05) had statistically significant effects on drying time. Correspondingly, significant effects were observed for effective moisture diffusivity (Deff), specific energy consumption (SEC), total energy consumption, and thermal efficiency (p < 0.05). The post hoc analysis revealed that all pairwise comparisons among temperature levels (45, 55, and 65 °C) were statistically significant (p < 0.05), with the largest mean difference observed between 45 and 65 °C. This substantial reduction in drying time at higher temperatures can be attributed to the increased thermal energy supplied to the system, which enhances moisture evaporation and diffusion rates. These findings are consistent with those reported by El-Mesery et al.80, who demonstrated that increasing the drying temperature significantly accelerates moisture removal by enhancing vapor pressure gradients.

In the same vein, IR power exhibited highly significant pairwise differences across all levels (200, 400, and 600 W; p < 0.001). The increase from 200 to 600 W resulted in a noticeable reduction in drying time, confirming the dominant role of infrared radiation in enhancing drying kinetics. This behaviour is explained by IR radiation’s ability to penetrate the material and generate internal heat, thereby increasing the effective moisture diffusivity. Comparable findings were reported by Aghbashlo et al.76, who observed that infrared-assisted drying significantly reduces drying time compared to conventional convective methods. In contrast, although air velocity showed statistically significant differences (p < 0.05), the magnitude of its effect was comparatively smaller, with mean differences typically below 40 min between extreme levels. The post-hoc results indicated that increasing air velocity from 0.3 to 0.7 m/s improved drying performance up to an optimal level, beyond which the effect diminished. This trend can be explained by the competing mechanisms of enhanced convective heat transfer and evaporative cooling at higher airflow rates. Similar observations have been reported by Doymaz81, who noted that excessive airflow may reduce drying efficiency due to surface cooling effects. Furthermore, the interaction effects between temperature, IR power, and air velocity were also statistically significant (Temperature × IR: p < 0.001; Temperature × Air Velocity: p < 0.05; IR × Air Velocity: p < 0.05). Post-hoc comparisons of estimated marginal means revealed that the reduction in drying time at higher IR power was significantly more pronounced at elevated temperatures (p < 0.001), indicating a strong synergistic interaction. This confirms that the combined application of infrared and convective heating enhances both internal moisture diffusion and surface evaporation simultaneously. Similar synergistic effects have been reported in hybrid drying systems22. For effective moisture diffusivity (Deff), post-hoc analysis showed significant increases with both temperature and IR power (p < 0.001), with the highest values observed at 65 °C and 600 W. This is attributed to increased molecular mobility and reduced internal resistance to moisture transport. These findings agree with those of73, who reported that diffusivity increases exponentially with temperature. In terms of energy performance, SEC values were significantly lower at higher IR power levels (p < 0.05), indicating improved energy efficiency. On the other hand, increasing air velocity led to a significant increase in energy consumption (p < 0.05), likely due to higher heat losses and reduced system efficiency. Similar trends have been reported by82, who found that optimized IR-assisted drying reduces energy consumption compared to conventional drying methods.

Uncertainty analysis

An uncertainty analysis was conducted to evaluate the reliability of experimental measurements. The overall uncertainty was calculated using the root-sum-square method based on the individual uncertainties of the measuring instruments. Temperature, air velocity, and mass measurement uncertainties were ± 1 °C, ± 0.1 m/s, and ± 0.01 g, respectively. The combined uncertainty in calculated parameters, such as moisture content, SEC, and thermal efficiency, was estimated to be ± 3–5%. These values indicate acceptable accuracy for experimental drying studies and confirm the reliability of the obtained results.

Conclusions

This study successfully evaluated an industrial infrared–convection conveyor-belt drying system for onion slices by integrating experimental analysis with advanced data-driven modeling techniques. The combined application of infrared (IR) radiation and convective airflow proved an effective approach for enhancing drying performance, improving moisture removal, and optimizing energy use under controlled operating conditions.
