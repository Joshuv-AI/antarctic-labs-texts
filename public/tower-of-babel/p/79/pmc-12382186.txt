Baseline tumor burden predicts the efficacy of first-line chemoimmunotherapy in patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer: results from 2 phase 3 randomized placebo-controlled trials
Authors: Xinyi He, Mengting Shi, Lin Zhang, Jianhua Zhan, Jiaqing Liu, Dongchen Sun, Zihong Chen, Shaodong Hong, Yaxiong Zhang, Gang Chen, Shen Zhao, Ting Zhou, Wenfeng Fang, Yuanyuan Zhao, Yan Huang, Caicun Zhou, Yunpeng Yang, Li Zhang, Huaqiang Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBaseline tumor burden (TB), measured radiographically, has emerged as a potential biomarker for stratifying survival outcomes with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) across solid tumors. However, the predictive value for ICI plus chemotherapy (chemoimmunotherapy) in NSCLC has not been confirmed with large cohorts that include a chemotherapy-only control group.MethodsThis post hoc analysis utilized data from ORIENT-11 trial, a phase III, randomized clinical trial of advanced NSCLC patients who were administered with either chemoimmunotherapy or chemotherapy alone. TB was measured as the sum of the diameters of target lesions at baseline following RECIST 1.1 criteria. The relationships between baseline TB and outcomes were assessed using the Cox proportional hazards model. Validation was conducted using data from ORIENT-12 trial.ResultsIn chemoimmunotherapy arm, patients with low TB demonstrated longer progression-free survival (PFS: 11.60 vs. 7.20 months, hazard ratio [HR] = 0.625, p = 0.004) and overall survival (OS: 28.77 vs. 20.10 months, HR = 0.683, p = 0.020) compared to those with high TB. In the chemotherapy-only arm, no significant survival differences were observed based on TB levels. Multivariate analyses confirmed that regardless of tumoral PD-L1 expression, low TB as an independent marker of improved survival with chemoimmunotherapy. Patients with high TB and low PD-L1 expression had the shortest survival, deriving minimal benefit from chemoimmunotherapy over chemotherapy alone (PFS: 5.53 vs. 5.07 months; OS: 10.52 vs. 13.80 months). These findings were validated in ORIENT-12 cohort.ConclusionsBaseline TB is a predictive, rather than prognostic, clinical biomarker for survival outcomes in advanced NSCLC patients treated with chemoimmunotherapy. TB assessment, integrated with PD-L1 status evaluation, may improve patient stratification for first-line chemoimmunotherapy in clinical practice and provide valuable insight for the clinical trials designs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14755-z.Keywords: Non-small cell lung cancer, Chemoimmunotherapy, Baseline tumor burden, PD-L1 expression

Introduction

The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) which target programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) or its ligand, PD-L1, has dramatically reshaped the landscape of cancer treatment. The combination of ICI with platinum-based chemotherapy has become the standard frontline therapy for various malignancies, especially for advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [1–5]. Nonetheless, clinical outcomes remain varied, with a large minority of patients responding poorly to this combination regimen [6]. Although biomarkers such as tumor mutational burden (TMB) and PD-L1 expression level have been proposed to identify those patients most likely to respond to chemoimmunotherapy, these markers have limitations in predictive accuracy and clinical applicability [7–9]. Therefore, the search for additional predictive markers for guiding patient selection remains an important focus of research.

Emerging evidence suggests that baseline tumor burden (TB) – quantified through radiological tumor volume, number of metastatic lesions or serum-based biomarkers, may serve as a potential predictor for ICI therapy outcomes [10, 11]. Studies have demonstrated that higher baseline TB is associated with poorer responses and survival outcomes in patients receiving ICI monotherapy across a variety of cancers including NSCLC, melanoma and head and neck cancers [12–15], while some retrospective studies report conflicting results that TB does not significantly impact outcomes in advanced NSCLC patients receiving ICI treatment [16, 17]. However, most studies lack a chemotherapy-only control group, making it difficult to determine whether TB acts as a predictive or prognostic indicator [10]. Additionally, while numerous studies have explored the link between TB and outcomes of ICI monotherapy, the impact of baseline TB on ICI plus chemotherapy (chemoimmunotherapy) remains poorly understood.

In this study, we investigated the association between baseline TB and efficacy of chemoimmunotherapy in patients with advanced non-squamous NSCLC, using data from the phase 3 randomized controlled trial ORIENT-11 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03607539) [3]. A validation cohort with squamous NSCLC patients was derived from a separate phase 3 trial (ORIENT-12, ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03629925) [18]. Furthermore, we explored the relationship between baseline TB and tumoral PD-L1 expressions and their combined utility in predicting the outcomes of chemoimmunotherapy.

Material and methods

Study design and population

This study included patients from the ORIENT-11 trial as the explorative cohort, and data from ORIENT-12 trial served as the validation cohort. Details of the two trials have been published previously [3, 4, 18]. Briefly, ORIENT-11 trial is a phase 3, double-blinded, randomized multicenter trial enrolling treatment-naïve patients with locally advanced or metastatic, non-squamous NSCLC lacking EGFR/ALK alteration. Eligible patients were randomly assigned in a 2:1 ratio to receive four cycles of sintilimab or placebo in combination with platinum and pemetrexed, and then maintenance therapy with sintilimab/placebo plus pemetrexed. The treatment continued until disease progression, intolerable toxicity, new antitumor treatment initiation or consent withdrawal. The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS), assessed by a blinded independent radiographic review committee. Secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), objective response rate (ORR) and disease control rate (DCR). The clinical protocol received approval from the respective institutional review boards and ethics committees. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant. In ORIENT-12 trial, eligible squamous NSCLC patients received either sintilimab or placebo combined with gemcitabine and platinum chemotherapy for four or six cycles, followed by sintilimab or placebo monotherapy as maintenance treatment. Patients without a radiological tumor burden evaluation at baseline were excluded from this post hoc study.

Assessment

TB was assessed through imaging techniques (CT and MRI), using the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 [19]. Target lesions were defined as a maximum of five measurable lesions in total (and a maximum of two lesions per organ). TB was calculated as the sum of the diameter of all target lesions (minor axis for malignant lymph nodes and major axis for non-nodal lesions) at baseline. Unless otherwise specified, TB refers to baseline tumor burden throughout this manuscript.

PD-L1 expression was assessed in tumor samples, using the PD-L1 22C3 pharmDx immunohistochemistry assay at a central laboratory (Covance, Shanghai, China). Tumoral PD-L1 expression level was assessed using the tumor proportion score (TPS) of PD-L1, which is defined as the proportion of tumor cells displaying partial or complete membranous PD-L1 staining at any intensity.

Statistical analysis

For ORIENT-11 study, the PFS data collected up to May 2020, with OS follow-up extending to September 2021. Survival outcomes were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method, with between-group comparisons performed via log-rank tests. Cox proportional hazards model was utilized to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). ORR and DCR were determined per RECIST v1.1 criteria, and Chi-square test assessed their association with TB status and PD-1 expression.

The optimal cut-off value for TB was determined independently in the two cohorts using the minimum p-value method (with Bonferroni adjustment) using the R package cutoff (version 1.3) [20], which was based on the OS of the Sintilimab plus chemotherapy (chemoimmunotherapy) cohort. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (Version 4.3.2). Throughout the study, two-sided statistical tests were used with a predefined significance level of 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics

We included 397 participants from ORIENT-11 trial with evaluable baseline TB data (Figure S1A). The median TB was 65.0 mm (interquartile range, 41.0–105.0 mm), which is consistent with previous studies [13]. Based on the cut-off value of 71.0 mm, patients were stratified into low (N = 222) and high (N = 175) TB groups. As shown in Table 1, patients in the high TB group exhibited a higher prevalence in the aged and in males and demonstrated associations with lower body mass index (BMI), poorer Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) and ever-smoking history. No significant differences were observed in terms of PD-L1 expression or treatment assignment between the two TB groups.

TB independently predicted the efficacy of chemoimmunotherapy

Based on TB classification, the chemoimmunotherapy cohort showed notable differences in survival. Notably, those with low TB achieved improved survival outcomes over those with high TB, in both PFS (11.60 vs. 7.20 months, HR = 0.625 [0.455–0.858], p = 0.004, Fig. 1A) and OS (28.77 vs. 20.10 months, HR = 0.683 [0.496–0.941], p = 0.020, Fig. 1B). In contrast, in the chemotherapy arm, no significant survival differences were observed between patients with low versus high TB (Fig. 1C, D). Although chemoimmunotherapy was superior to chemotherapy regarding PFS and OS in both TB low and TB high groups, patients with low TB seemed to benefit more from chemoimmunotherapy, considering the lower hazard ratio between chemoimmunotherapy and chemotherapy in the analysis of PFS (HR = 0.445 [0.315–0.629] in low TB group vs. 0.527 [0.370–0.751] in high TB group) and OS (HR = 0.638 [0.441–0.924] in low TB group vs. 0.698 [0.484–1.006] in high TB group) (Fig. 1E, F). Besides, low TB was significantly correlated with a higher DCR in the chemoimmunotherapy arm (91.61% vs. 79.28%, p = 0.006) but was not in the chemo arm (p = 0.243). However, no correlation was observed between TB and ORR in either chemoimmunotherapy or chemotherapy arm (Figures S2A-B, Table S1).

Multivariate Cox analyses and logistic regression models were used to assess the association between TB and clinical outcomes, adjusting for other clinical variables (including age, sex, ECOG PS and PD-L1 expression). According to the results, low TB was an independent predictive biomarker of improved PFS (HR = 0.553 [0.392–0.780], p < 0.001, Fig. 2A) and OS (HR = 0.654 [0.467–0.917], p = 0.014, Fig. 2B), and higher DCR (OR: 0.356 [0.156–0.772], p = 0.011, Figure S2C) for the chemoimmunotherapy cohort but not for the chemotherapy-only cohort (Fig. 2C, D, Figure S2D). Collectively, these findings confirmed low TB as an independent predictor of improved PFS, OS and DCR in the chemoimmunotherapy group but not in the chemotherapy group.

To assess the robustness of the TB as a predictive biomarker, we performed a sensitivity analysis using the median TB value (65.0 mm) as an alternative threshold. Consistent with the primary analysis, patients with low TB exhibited significantly longer PFS and a borderline significant improvement in OS compared to those with high TB in the chemoimmunotherapy arm, whereas no significant survival differences were observed in the chemotherapy arm (Figure S3). These findings further support the robustness of baseline TB as a predictor for chemoimmunotherapy efficacy.

TB and PD-L1 expression were independent markers

While high TB has been associated with higher tumor PD-L1 expression in some cancers [16, 21–23], we found minimal correlation between TB and PD-L1 TPS in this cohort (Spearman correlation coefficient: ρ = 0.150, p = 0.003, Figure S4). Still, when TB was stratified into TB low/high categories, and PD-L1 TPS was categorized as three-tier with the cut-off value of 1% and 50%, no significant correlation was observed between the TB levels and PD-L1 expression (p = 0.081, Table S2). Therefore, TB and PD-L1 expression were independent biomarkers in our non-squamous NSCLC cohort.

Combinatorial analysis of TB and PD-L1 expression

Patients were stratified into four subgroups according to TB (high vs. low) and PD-L1 TPS (≥ 1% vs. < 1%). In the chemoimmunotherapy arm, those with high TB and low PD-L1 expression demonstrated the shortest PFS and OS and lowest DCR (Fig. 3A, B, Table S3). The four subgroups in the chemotherapy-only arm, however, did not differ significantly from one another (Fig. 3C, D). In those with high TB and low PD-L1 expression, PFS and OS appeared to be similar between the two treatment arms (PFS: 5.53 vs. 5.07 months, HR = 1.141, p = 0.689; OS: 10.52 vs. 13.80, HR = 1.093, p = 0.785, Figures S5G-H), suggesting that adding ICI had little additional effect in this subgroup. On the other hand, regardless of PD-L1 status, patients with low TB consistently derived survival benefits from chemoimmunotherapy when compared to chemotherapy alone (Figures S5A-F).

Validation of TB as a predictive biomarker in squamous NSCLC

To further confirm the predictive effect of TB in squamous NSCLC, we examined 349 patients with available baseline TB data from the ORIENT-12 trial (Figure S1). The median TB for those patients was 89.0 mm (interquartile range: 65.3–116.9 mm). Using a similar approach, we determined a TB cut-off of 73.2 mm, categorizing patients into low TB (N = 109, 31.23%) and high TB subgroups (N = 240, 68.77%).

In the chemoimmunotherapy arm, low TB was associated with significantly longer OS (not reached vs. 14.03 months, HR = 0.301, p = 0.001, Figure S6B). Low TB patients also showed a trend towards longer PFS (6.80 vs. 5.47 months, p = 0.276, Figure S6A). Conversely, the chemotherapy-alone arm demonstrated no significant differences in either PFS (p = 0.882) or OS (p = 0.199) between TB subgroups (Figure S6C-D).

Comparing treatment types, chemoimmunotherapy improved PFS in both TB groups, with a more pronounced benefit in the low TB group (HR = 0.462 vs. 0.576 in high TB; Figure S6E). OS analysis showed a trend towards benefit in low TB patients (HR = 0.448, p = 0.061), whereas no treatment benefit was seen in those with high TB (p = 0.869; Figure S6F).

No significant correlation between TB and PD-L1 TPS (Spearman ρ = 0.009, p = 0.87, Figure S7A). Among those with high TB and low PD-L1 expression, chemoimmunotherapy failed to improve PFS compared to chemotherapy alone (5.17 vs. 5.10 months, HR = 0.617, Figure S7E). However, all other TB/PD-L1 subgroups exhibited clear PFS benefits from chemoimmunotherapy, with hazard ratios of 0.478, 0.558, and 0.467 for various TB and PD-L1 combinations (Figures S7B-D). In summary, these results support TB as a predictive marker for the treatment outcome of chemoimmunotherapy in NSCLC.

Discussion

In this study, using data from two double-blinded phase 3 trials, we, for the first time, established baseline TB as a predictive biomarker for survival outcomes in advanced NSCLC patients receiving first-line chemoimmunotherapy. Importantly, this predictive value of TB was specific to chemoimmunotherapy; in patients receiving chemotherapy alone, it showed no prognostic relevance. Furthermore, by integrated analysis with PD-L1 immunohistochemistry score, we also identified a patient subgroup (high TB and low PD-L1 expression) that derived minimal benefit from chemoimmunotherapy. These findings have practical implications for optimizing treatment selection and personalizing therapeutic strategies in advanced NSCLC management (Figure S8).

Previous studies have established baseline TB to be associated with the outcome of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor monotherapy in NSCLC. A pooled analysis of four clinical trials (OAK, POPLAR, BIRCH and FIR) involving 1461 patients receiving atezolizumab monotherapy found that high baseline TB was associated with unfavorable OS and PFS [13]. Similarly, a post-hoc analysis of KEYNOTE-010 trial revealed improved OS with pembrolizumab in patients with lower TB [24]. Other retrospective studies also supported TB’s predictive and prognostic value in immunotherapy for NSCLC [25–27], though some reported no association with ICI treatment outcome [16]. With chemoimmunotherapy becoming the first-line treatment of advanced NSCLC [7], the role of TB in chemoimmunotherapy remained elusive. In our study, using data from multicenter randomized controlled phase 3 trials, we established TB as a predictive biomarker for therapeutic efficacy in NSCLC patients treated with first-line chemoimmunotherapy. By including patients receiving placebo plus chemotherapy as a control group, we confirmed that the predictive role of TB was limited to chemoimmunotherapy and had no prognostic relevance in the chemotherapy cohort.

Various biomarkers have been identified to predict the efficacy of ICI-containing therapies, including FDA-approved markers like PD-L1 expression level (such as tumor proportional score and combined positive score), tumor mutational burden and microsatellite instability status [6]. Although these biomarkers provide valuable insights into the tumor-immune microenvironment, their assessment often involves specialized analyses that may not be widely available at many cancer centers globally. In contrast, TB is a clinical marker that can be readily assessed through routine contrast-enhanced CT and MRI imaging. This approach offers distinct advantages over molecular biomarkers in terms of accessibility and cost-effectiveness. The routine integration of TB evaluation into clinical practice could facilitate optimal patient stratification for ICI combination therapies. Particularly in situations where genomic profiling or specialized immunohistochemistry may be unavailable, TB assessment provides a valuable, financially feasible alternative to guide treatment decision-making.

Numerous studies reported inconsistent findings on the relationship and TB and PD-L1 expression in NSCLC. While some retrospective observational studies on NSCLC immunotherapy reported elevated PD-L1 expression in patients with high TB [16, 23]; others found no such correlation [25, 27]. Importantly, most studies consistently reported that TB is associated with survival outcomes from ICI-containing therapy, independent of PD-L1 expression status. Our study based on randomized phase 3 trials confirmed this independence between TB and PD-L1 expression, which minimizes potential selection bias. We also found that the high TB & low PD-L1 subgroup showed particularly poor survival with chemoimmunotherapy and derived no significant benefit from ICI addition (PFS: HR = 1.141, p = 0.689 for non-squamous NSCLC; HR = 0.617, p = 0.055 for squamous NSCLC). These findings challenged the current treatment paradigm in which immunotherapy is routinely administered along with chemotherapy, regardless of PD-L1 expression status [28]. For patients with high TB and low PD-L1 expression, conventional chemotherapy may be a more balanced therapeutic approach, considering both the marginal clinical benefit and the costs and toxicities associated with ICI regimens.

Preclinical studies have highlighted several mechanisms by which high TB may impair the efficacy of immunotherapies. As tumors progress, they are increasingly infiltrated by immunosuppressive cell populations such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), regulatory T cells (Tregs) and M2 macrophages [29–31]. In addition, large tumors often experience poor perfusion and hypoxia. In this condition, tumor cells consume glucose rapidly and produce high levels of lactate, which have been found to support the Tregs while blunting the cytotoxic activity of T cells and NK cells [32–35]. Beyond the local tumor microenvironment, the presence of tumor also reshapes the systemic immune system of the host. Elevated TB has been associated with increased circulating levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6, IL-8 and TNF [36–38], which can alter normal hematopoiesis and result in myeloid dysregulation, thereby dampening the local and systemic antitumor immunity [39]. This has been further supported by Allen et al. that tumor burden drove the global suppression of T cell response via factors such as IL-1 and G-CSF [40].

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, as this is a post-hoc analysis of two phase 3 NSCLC trials, our findings require validation through prospective, multicentered studies specifically designed to evaluate TB’s predictive effect. Second, the association between TB and TMB, another validated biomarker for predicting ICI efficacy, was not investigated due to limited tumor samples for genomic analysis. Third, it should be mentioned that no universal definition of “high tumor burden” currently exists. Previous retrospective studies using a similar TB concepts have reported cut-off values ranging from 64 to 120 mm, based on receiver operation curve analysis, minimum p-value method or the median value [13, 25–27, 41]. More recently, maximum baseline tumor size (the longest diameter of the largest target lesion) has also been proposed as a predictor of the treatment outcomes of ICI monotherapy in NSCLC [42]. While median-based cutoff values are commonly used in prognostic studies due to their simplicity, the development of predictive biomarker requires thresholds that best discriminate clinical outcomes. In our study, TB was measured using the sum of target lesions’ diameters according to RECIST, and the optimal cutoff was determined via minimum p-value method. However, this method carries a risk of overfitting due to multiple testing across a range of possible thresholds, and thus the selected cut-off may overestimate the true effect size. To address this concern, we performed sensitivity analyses using median TB value as an alternative cutoff, and found that the observed trends remained largely consistent. Nonetheless, external validation in independent cohorts is essential to confirm the robustness and clinical applicability of the selected threshold. Fourth, we observed imbalances in baseline characteristics between TB groups and acknowledge that these may lead to residual confounding. Although we conducted multivariate analyses and propensity score matching (data not shown) to assess the robustness of our findings – with the associations between TB and survival outcomes remained generally consistent – the potential influence of unmeasured confounders cannot be fully excluded.

Conclusion

This study suggests that baseline TB may be a potential predictive biomarker for chemoimmunotherapy outcomes. However, as a post-hoc exploratory analysis, its clinical utility for guiding therapeutic decisions in routine oncology practice must be prospectively validated.
