Beyond Hepatitis: A Rare Case of Multisystem Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis in a Child
Authors: Dilip Neupane, Rishi Ram Banjade, Dhiraj Joshi, Pusp Raj Awasthi, Santosh Mishra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLangerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a rare neoplastic disorder involving abnormal proliferation of dendritic cells, with clinical presentations ranging from isolated lesions to life‐threatening multisystem involvement. This case report describes a 26‐month‐old girl who presented with progressive jaundice, fever, hepatomegaly, pruritic scalp rashes with greasy scales, and maculopapular to papulocrusted skin lesions. Laboratory findings revealed severe anemia, elevated inflammatory markers, high bilirubin levels, elevated transaminases, markedly increased GGT (Gamma‐Glutamyl Transferase) and ALP (Alkaline Phosphatase) levels, suggestive of cholestatic injury, along with low albumin and a reversed albumin‐to‐globulin ratio. A provisional diagnosis of high‐risk multisystem LCH was made, and a skin biopsy was performed, demonstrating characteristic histiocytic infiltration with immunopositivity for CD1a and S‐100, thereby confirming the diagnosis. She was initiated on chemotherapy with vinblastine in combination with prednisolone at a referred center and demonstrated a favorable clinical response. This case underscores the diagnostic challenges of Langerhans cell histiocytosis, particularly in resource‐limited settings, and highlights the importance of looking beyond common diagnoses, such as acute viral hepatitis, when the clinical presentation does not fully align. Careful recognition of systemic features, especially involvement of the liver and skin, combined with early skin biopsy and a multidisciplinary approach, is essential for timely diagnosis and effective management of rare conditions like high‐risk multisystem LCH.Keywords: case report, child, Langerhans cell histiocytosis, neoplastic disorder, pediatric

Introduction

Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a histiocytic neoplastic disorder characterized by the uncontrolled proliferation of immature dendritic cells derived from the bone marrow. These cells, which express the protein langerin, can form granulomatous lesions and infiltrate multiple organs, including the skin, bones, and lymph nodes, resulting in widespread inflammation [1, 2]. LCH affects approximately 8.9 children per million under the age of 15, with the median age at diagnosis being 3 years, and males are affected more frequently than females. In adults, the disease is even rarer, occurring at a rate of about 0.07 cases per million per year [3, 4].

LCH presents with a wide clinical spectrum, ranging from mild to potentially life‐threatening disease. It most commonly affects the bones, followed by the lungs and skin. Liver involvement in pediatric LCH varies in frequency, with reported rates ranging from 15% to 60% [5]. Hepatic LCH can clinically resemble various conditions, including chronic cholangitis, metabolic or genetic liver diseases, biliary tract obstruction, and hepatitis. It may also mimic Reye syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, or hemochromatosis. In advanced stages, it can progress to sclerosing cholangitis, liver failure, cirrhosis, portal hypertension, and hypersplenism [5]. Skin involvement occurs in 40% of cases, often presenting as eczematous or papular eruptions resembling Candida, seborrheic dermatitis, or viral rashes, making diagnosis challenging. Systemic involvement is more common in children under two, while older children typically present with isolated bone lesions [6, 7]. This case report presents a 26‐month‐old girl with high‐risk multisystem LCH complicated by anemia, adding to the understanding of the diverse clinical features of this rare condition. It emphasizes the need to consider unusual symptoms to ensure timely diagnosis and effective treatment of multisystem LCH.

Case Presentation

A 26‐month‐old previously healthy and developmentally normal female child presented with a 1‐month history of progressive yellowish discoloration of the sclera, which gradually extended to involve the entire body. This was associated with dark yellow urine, mild abdominal distension, and reduced oral intake. There was also a history of intermittent fever for 2 weeks, with a maximum recorded temperature of 102.3°F, not accompanied by chills or rigors. There was no history of pale stools, vomiting, diarrhea, burning micturition, hematuria, blood in the stool, or similar previous illness. There was no known exposure to individuals with jaundice, nor any history of consuming contaminated food or water. The child had not been given any herbal or alternative medications.

The patient had developed pruritic scalp rashes with greasy scales for 3 months, which, by the eighth day of fever, had progressed to maculopapular to papulocrusted lesions spreading from the scalp to the trunk and hands (Figure 1). The rash was not associated with vesicles, pustules, mucosal involvement, or desquamation. The child was initially evaluated at a nearby tertiary care center on the third day of illness and was managed under the provisional diagnosis of infective hepatitis. However, due to a lack of improvement, she was referred to our tertiary care center for further assessment. On abdominal examination, the liver was palpable 10 cm below the xiphisternum, firm in consistency, with a deep notch. The spleen tip was also palpable.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Based on the clinical findings, differential diagnoses considered included chronic liver disease, Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH), autoimmune hepatitis, infective hepatitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, and metabolic liver disease. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed hepatomegaly (127 mm) with coarse parenchymal echotexture, without intrahepatic biliary duct dilatation or focal hepatic lesions (Figure 2).

Ultrasound image of liver showing coarse parenchymal echotexture.

Comprehensive etiological investigations were performed (Table 1), including autoimmune markers and screening for primary sclerosing cholangitis, which were negative. Hemoglobin was very low, indicating severe anemia, with a high reticulocyte count suggesting hemolysis or blood loss. Elevated white cell count and CRP indicate active inflammation or infection. Liver function is impaired, with high bilirubin levels, raised liver enzymes, and markedly elevated GGT and ALP, pointing to cholestatic or hepatocellular injury. Low albumin and a reversed A/G ratio further support liver dysfunction.

On further evaluation, due to the abnormal liver function tests and seborrheic dermatitis on the scalp, Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) was considered a possible differential diagnosis, and a skin biopsy was therefore planned. The biopsy obtained from the rash showed diffuse dermal infiltration by histiocytic cells with oval nuclei and characteristic nuclear grooves, producing a “coffee‐bean” appearance (Figure 3). Additional features included epidermotropism, parakeratosis, occasional mitotic figures, and focal epidermal erosion. Immunohistochemical staining was positive for CD1a and S‐100 (Figures 4 and 5) confirming the diagnosis of Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis.

A skeletal survey showed no evidence of osseous involvement. Given the presence of multisystem disease (cutaneous and hepatic involvement), a diagnosis of high‐risk multisystem Langerhans cell Histiocytosis (MS‐LCH) was established. The patient was referred to a specialized pediatric hematology and oncology center for further management and initiation of chemotherapy. She demonstrated a good therapeutic response to chemotherapy with vinblastine in combination with prednisolone.

Conclusions and Results

LCH's variable presentation poses diagnostic challenges in resource‐limited settings, where access to specialized pathology and targeted therapies may be limited. Early recognition, comprehensive evaluation, and tailored management are essential. This case highlights the need for increased awareness, a multidisciplinary approach involving pediatric gastroenterology, pathology, and radiology, as well as improved diagnostic infrastructure and treatment resources for rare pediatric diseases in developing countries.

Discussion

Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) in children is a rare disease characterized by abnormal proliferation of Langerhans cells, primarily affecting bones, skin, and other organs, with presentations ranging from isolated lesions to life‐threatening multisystem involvement [8]. LCH is categorized into single‐system (SS‐LCH) and multisystem (MS‐LCH) forms. SS‐LCH affects only one organ or system, such as bone (single or multiple sites), skin, nondraining lymph nodes, lungs, hypothalamic–pituitary/CNS, or others like the thyroid or thymus. MS‐LCH involves two or more organs or systems, with or without the involvement of high‐risk organs [9]. In our case, both the skin and liver were involved, presenting as pruritic maculopapular or papulocrusted skin lesions and hepatomegaly. Since the liver is considered a high‐risk organ associated with a poor prognosis, the condition was classified as high‐risk multisystem Langerhans cell histiocytosis [10]. Due to its rarity and highly variable presentation, LCH is often challenging to diagnose early.

Histologically, LCH lesions show “coffee bean” histiocytes with mixed inflammatory cells and are immunopositive for CD1a and S100 [11]. Liver involvement in LCH generally progresses through two phases: an early stage with infiltration of histiocytes, and a later stage involving fibrotic changes and sclerosis of the biliary system. Liver involvement in LCH is associated with significantly worse outcomes, with a 3‐year survival rate of only 51.8% compared to 96.7% in patients without liver involvement, and nearly a threefold increased risk of death. Moreover, liver damage may continue to progress even after the disease regresses, due to a self‐sustaining fibrotic process. Affected children are typically younger and face a higher risk of mortality [10, 12]. Hepatic LCH in a patient already diagnosed with LCH is identified by one or more of the following: (1) hepatomegaly, defined as the liver extending more than 3 cm below the costal margin at the midclavicular line; (2) liver dysfunction, indicated by abnormal liver function tests such as bilirubin levels over three times the normal limit, protein under 55 g/L, albumin below 3 g/dL, or transaminases (ALT/AST) exceeding three times normal or clinical signs like intrahepatic nodules, ascites, or edema not due to other causes; or (3) histological evidence of active disease [9, 13]. In a review of 348 cases, the French Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis Study Group reported liver involvement in 10.1% of patients at initial presentation, which increased to 14.4% in later episodes [12]. Similarly, Braier et al. documented liver involvement in 18% of patients diagnosed with multisystem LCH [14]. In our case, LCH primarily manifested as jaundice, with abnormal liver function tests showing elevated ALP and GGT, mild increases in SGOT and SGPT, and a slight decrease in total protein, along with ultrasound findings suggesting the initial stage of hepatic LCH with histiocytic infiltration. Similarly, anemia with a high reticulocyte count was attributed to bone marrow infiltration by Langerhans cells, which triggered a compensatory increase in reticulocyte production [15].

Ge et al. reported that MS‐LCH patients with liver involvement more often presented with systemic symptoms, increased skin, lung, and ear involvement, and a higher risk of hemophagocytic lymphoproliferative syndrome. Additionally, elevated levels of ALP, γ‐GGT, total bile acid, and direct bilirubin were identified as independent predictors of liver progression or relapse during treatment, underscoring the importance of regular liver function monitoring throughout therapy [16].

Once LCH is diagnosed, baseline evaluation should include a thorough history of key symptoms—such as rash, pain, organ‐specific signs, systemic features, and neurological or behavioral changes—and a detailed physical exam at diagnosis and follow‐up [9]. Langerhans cell histiocytosis treatment varies depending on the extent and location of the disease. Unifocal cases are often managed with surgical resection, corticosteroid injections, low‐dose radiation, or observation. Multifocal or multisystem disease often requires chemotherapy (vinca alkaloids with steroids, cladribine, or cytarabine). Other options include bisphosphonates, immunomodulatory drugs, hydroxyurea, methotrexate, or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. BRAF or MEK inhibitors are promising for mutation‐positive cases [3].

The patient initially presented with jaundice, fever, and abnormal liver function tests, leading to a presumptive diagnosis of acute viral hepatitis—a common diagnostic pitfall given the rarity and variable presentation of LCH. Early recognition could have been facilitated by correlating the chronic scalp rash and hepatomegaly with systemic features and performing an early skin biopsy. In resource‐limited settings, limited access to immunohistochemistry and advanced imaging poses challenges; in such contexts, skin biopsy with histopathology may serve as the most practical and decisive diagnostic tool, highlighting the importance of careful clinical evaluation and targeted investigations.
