A Prospective Observational Study of Intraoperative Regional Cerebral Desaturation and Negative Postoperative Behavioural Changes After Non‐Cardiac Paediatric Surgery
Authors: Martin Kälvesten, Ali‐Reza Modiri, Rececka Jonshult, Robert Frithiof, Peter Frykholm
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundNegative postoperative behaviour changes (NPOBC) are known to occur after general anaesthesia in children. A significantly increased risk of NPOBC has been reported in children who exhibited a reduction in cerebral regional oxygen saturation (crSO2) of as little as 5% below baseline levels. These results were unexpected, and we therefore aimed to investigate the association between degrees of regional cerebral desaturation and NPOBC after routine surgery in young children.MethodsIn this prospective cohort study, 180 healthy children between 2 and 6 years old undergoing routine surgery were enrolled. The primary outcome was NPOBC, assessed using the Post Hospitalization Behavior Questionnaire (PHBQ), reported by parents and evaluated on Postoperative Day 7. The results were stratified according to decreases in crSO2 from baseline of at least 5, 10, 15 or 20 percentage points sustained for a minimum duration of 2 min. The χ 2 or Fisher's exact test was used to analyse differences in categorical variables. Using logistic regression, outcome data were expressed as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals.ResultsThe incidence of NPOBC was 13% on Day 7, and 15% on Day 30, respectively. No significant differences were found between children with or without NPOBC for any of the thresholds of crSO2 reduction (≥ 5%: 2 [11%] vs. 2 [1.8%] cases, p = 0.09; ≥ 10%: 1 [5.6%] vs. 2 [1.8%] cases, p = 0.36; ≥ 15% and ≥ 20%: 0 vs. 0 cases). The rate of NPOBC was not associated with intraoperative reduction in crSO2 (OR 1.25, CI 0.71–2.20, p = 0.46). Older age was associated with lower odds of NPOBCs (OR 0.39, CI 0.15–0.99, p = 0.048). Using the area under the curve for quantifying crSO2 changes did not result in a statistically significant correlation between crSO2 and NPBOCs (r = 0.11).ConclusionRegional cerebral desaturation and negative postoperative behaviour may be less common than previously reported in young children undergoing uneventful general anaesthesia. We could not corroborate the strong association between these entities reported previously. Studies investigating the effects of cerebral desaturation on patient‐centred outcomes will need large sample sizes.Editorial CommentThis prospective observational study describes a low incidence and magnitude of NIRS desaturation in healthy children. In contrast to previous reporting, there was no association with new negative postoperative behaviours in children in this cohort. Introduction

The association between various aspects of general anaesthesia and negative postoperative behaviour changes (NPOBC) in children has been explored since the 1950s [1]. The most common instrument for evaluating postoperative behavioural changes is the Post Hospitalization Behavior Questionnaire, introduced by Vernon et al. [2]. The incidence varies widely between different studies, ranging from 24% to 80% during the first few days at home following surgery, decreasing to 29%–54% at 2 weeks [3, 4, 5]. Neither the mechanisms behind nor the significance of NPOBCs have been fully elucidated.

In a single centre prospective observational study, Gómez‐Pesquera et al. used near‐infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) to monitor regional cerebral oxygen saturation (crSO2) during routine non‐cardiac surgery [6]. They hypothesised that a decrease of 20% from baseline would be associated with NPOBC, because previous studies in adults and children indicated worse outcomes at this degree of cerebral desaturation [7]. Surprisingly, the authors found a significantly increased risk of NPOBCs in children who displayed a reduction in crSO2 as low as 5% below the baseline value, and they reported an odds ratio (OR) of 1.2 of NPOBC for every 1% change in crSO2 below the above threshold value [6]. The above study can be criticised for drawing conclusions based on deviations that are within the measurement precision of the NIRS device. Nevertheless, their results were alarming, possibly suggesting that even subtle changes in cerebral oxygenation during general anaesthesia could have detrimental effects on cerebral function. Since the results are unique to date, we found reason to replicate the study. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between regional cerebral desaturation and NPOBC after routine surgery. Based on the previous study, we explored the association of crSO2 changes of at least 5%–20% from the baseline value with the occurrence of NPOBC [6]. We hypothesised that reductions in crSO2 of 5%–20% from baseline would be associated with NPOBC on Postoperative Day 7. Secondary objectives were to determine whether any observed behavioural changes persisted after 30 days, and to identify other potential predictive factors for NPOBC based on routine perioperative monitoring—such as hypotension, hypoxaemia and perioperative anxiety.

Methods

This prospective, single‐centre, cohort study was conducted at a tertiary level university hospital. It was approved by the Swedish Ethics Review Authority (application 2021‐02927, date of approval: 15 June 2021, chairperson G. Bodin). Written informed consent was obtained from both parents. The study was registered prior to the start of data collection at ISRCTN.org (registration number 11799594; 26 August 2021). The Strengthening of the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) criteria were followed for reporting the results [8]. Data were collected between September 2021 and November 2022. Children aged 2–6 years scheduled for elective surgery requiring general anaesthesia were eligible for inclusion. The exclusion criteria were planned anaesthesia duration < 30 min, children with developmental delay, signs of increased intracranial pressure, autism or other severe neuropsychiatric disease, children undergoing chemotherapy or radiotherapy, and if postoperative intensive care or other hospitalisation > 3 days was planned.

Anaesthesia and Monitoring

The patients were premedicated with oral midazolam 0.3 mg kg−1 or clonidine (3–4 μg kg−1) if indicated. All children received EMLA at two sites. Anaesthesia was induced with sevoflurane, propofol and fentanyl, depending on patient preference and availability of veins for atraumatic intravenous access. Routine monitoring including SpO2, non‐invasive blood pressure, electrocardiogram (ECG) and temperature was performed. Sevoflurane at MAC 1–1.3 was used for maintenance, supplemented with intravenous clonidine 1 μg kg−1, regional anaesthesia or repeated doses of fentanyl 0.5 μg kg−1. Most cases were managed with a laryngeal mask airway (LMA) and spontaneous ventilation with pressure support. Patients requiring endotracheal intubation received atracurium or rocuronium and controlled mechanical ventilation, but were likewise switched to spontaneous ventilation after reaching a TOF ratio of 0.9 spontaneously or after reversal with neostigmine‐glycopyrrolate. Sevoflurane was washed out at the end of surgery and repeated doses of propofol 0.5–1 mg kg−1 were administered until the end‐tidal sevoflurane reached ≤ 0.04, and the child was extubated deeply. After approximately 5 min, the NIRS optode was removed and the patient was transferred to the post anaesthesia care unit (PACU), marking the end of the study protocol.

The INVOS model 5100C was used to monitor the crSO2. A paediatric optode (SPFB < 40 kg; Covidien, Mansfield, MA, USA) was placed on the left forehead before anaesthesia induction, and after a few minutes for stabilisation, the baseline value was recorded. The anaesthesia team was not blinded to the NIRS display, but the display was placed out of view behind the ventilator and the anaesthesia team was instructed to manage the case based on routine monitoring rather than changes in crSO2. NIRS data were saved on the INVOS device at a sampling rate varying from one in 14–30 s, and transferred to a server for analysis. Regional desaturation (below any of the thresholds) lasting less than 2 min were excluded from the analysis.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was NPOBCs assessed using the PHBQ, evaluated on Postoperative Day 7. The results were stratified according to decreases in crSO2 from baseline of at least 5, 10, 15 or 20 percentage points sustained for a minimum duration of 2 min. The post‐hospitalisation behavioural changes were measured with the relative version of the PHBQ on postoperative day seven and 30. In this version, parents are asked to compare the behaviour of their child before and after hospitalisation on a 5‐point scale in retrospect [2]. In the validation of the Swedish version used in the present study, two items were excluded from the questionnaire [9]. The cut‐off for NPOBCs was set to seven (out of 25) or more negative behaviour changes. The participants' PHBQ score was the number of questions they answered with more or much more (4 or 5 points on the 5‐point Likert scale). After the surgery, the parents were either given a form with a QR code linking to an online form of the PHBQ or two copies of the PHBQ together with pre‐stamped return envelopes. They were asked to fill out the forms on days 7 and 30. The phone number of one of the parents was obtained, and they received up to two text messages or a phone call on the date they were supposed to fill in the form. If a question was left unanswered in the returned questionnaire form, the response was interpreted as no change, the central point of the scale.

Secondary outcomes were hypotension, peripheral desaturation and perioperative anxiety levels. In addition, we tested an alternative definition of NPOBC, in which the total PHBQ score for each individual was used to create a continuous variable reflecting the number of behavioural changes [3, 6]. Hypotension was defined as any episode of mean arterial blood pressure (MAP) < −1 SD in the age‐appropriate nomograms for blood pressure in children reported by De Graaff et al. [10] Perioperative anxiety was recorded preoperatively by the anaesthesia nurse using the paediatric anesthesia behavior (PAB) score, as part of the local routine [11]. A score of 2 or more (sad or mad) was defined as preoperative anxiety. Postoperative delirium was recorded by the PACU nurses using the post anesthesia delirium score (PAED), also as part of the local routine [12]. All the secondary outcomes were obtained from the patient data management system (Metavision), providing a resolution of 1 min for saved data. The criteria for artifacts were MAP = 0 or > 140 mmHg, single MAP values below the threshold value and single SpO2 values under the threshold value. In a post hoc analysis, we explored whether incorporating the duration of crSO2 reduction by using the area under the curve (AUC) would enhance the ability to detect an association between cerebral desaturation and NPOBC.

Statistical Methods

The sample size calculation was based on an OR of 3.8 for NPOBC in children with crSO2 reductions ≥ 5% of baseline reported by Gomez‐Pesquera, but we chose a more conservative expected OR of 2.5, also excluding children above the age of 6 to increase power, which yielded a sample size of 180 to achieve a power of 80% with a significance level of 0.05 [6]. Nominal data were expressed as absolute numbers and percentages. Continuous demographic data were expressed as means (±SD) or medians (IQR) according to the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test of normality. Student's t test or Mann–Whitney U test was used for inter‐group comparisons as appropriate. Outcome data were expressed as ORs with 95% confidence intervals. The Chi‐square or Fisher's exact test was used to analyse differences in categorical variables between the two groups (non‐NPOBC and NPOBC). To assess the relationship between crSO2 and the occurrence of NPOBC, a logistic regression was performed, including age, duration of surgery and anxiety level according to PAB for secondary analyses. These variables were chosen because they were found to be associated with NPOBC in the study by Gomez‐Pesquera et al. and because their p values were the lowest in the initial χ 2 test of the present study [6]. The post hoc correlation between AUC and PHBQ score was calculated using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient.

Results

During a period of 15 months (September 2021 to November 2022), 180 children were included. Four cases were excluded because they met the exclusion criteria or had inadequate baseline NIRS registrations. In 39 of the remaining cases, no PHBQ forms were obtained on Day 7, and in 5 cases, there were missing data due to technical problems with the INVOS device, leaving 132 complete datasets for analysis of the primary outcome and 137 for the secondary outcomes (Figure 1). In one case, the parents preferred the English version of the PHBQ, which was abbreviated according to Swedish validation.

The mean age was 3.4 (1.5) years, and the children in the NPOBC were 1.2 years younger than those in the non‐NPOBC group. Approximately one third were female. Premedication was used in 70% of the cases, with midazolam being the most common agent. Urologic or gastrointestinal surgery accounted for 46% of the procedures, and the average duration of anaesthesia and surgery was 141 (65) and 61 (48) min, respectively.

Discussion

In this prospective observational study, the main finding was that in a population of young children without significant comorbidity undergoing uneventful elective surgery under general anaesthesia, crSO2 reduction more than 5% points below baseline was observed in only four children (2.9%) and there was no significant association between reductions in crSO2 and the occurrence of NPOBC for any of the crSO2 thresholds. This was in stark contrast to the replicated study in which deviations in crSO2 ≥ 5% points from the baseline value were common and strongly associated with negative postoperative behavioural changes on Postoperative Day 7 [6].

Regional Cerebral Oxygenation

NIRS is increasingly used in paediatric cardiac surgery and neonatal intensive care, and there is low grade evidence suggesting that neurological complications may be reduced if values less than 20% below baseline are acted upon [13]. There is a lack of randomised controlled trials, and the threshold of 20% is arbitrary even if it is frequently used [13, 14, 15]. We were therefore concerned by the report of Gomez‐Pesquera et al., indicating that even a threshold as small as 5% below the baseline may be associated with NPOBC [6]. In their regression analysis, they found that every 1% decrease in crSO2 increased the OR for NPOBC by 1.2. In essence, our study is a replica of the latter study, aiming to provide further evidence for the effects of mild changes in crSO2 on postoperative changes in non‐cardiac surgery. Gomez‐Pesquera et al. reported crSO2 changes in 167 out of 198 cases (84%), of which 17 were reduced by more than 15 percentage points and 4 by more than 20 percentage points from the baseline values respectively. In contrast, the total frequency was much lower (3.0%) in the present study, and there were no cases with crSO2 lower than 15 percentage points from baseline.

The present study may have defined crSO2 change differently from Gomez‐Pesquera et al. [6] The latter authors did not report if they used a cut‐off for the duration of crSO2 reduction, while we included only desaturations of at least 2 min duration, which could partly explain the large difference in incidence. However, in a multicentre observational study of intra‐operative cerebral desaturation in infants, the authors used a cut‐off of 3 min and reported crSO2 reduction of at least 10 percentage points in 34% of cases, which still is much higher than the 2.3% in the present study [16]. A possible explanation is the age difference in the studied populations; Gomez‐Pesquera included children of ages 2–12 years, the multicenter study focused on infants < 6 months, while we studied children 2–6 years of age.

How can we otherwise explain the striking differences in the incidence of cerebral desaturation? In theory, crSO2 is mainly influenced by the local cerebral blood flow, oxygen content and oxygen extraction. These, in turn, are dependent on cardiac output, blood pressure, oxygen delivery, cerebral metabolism, and oxygen extraction.

Variation in Physiological Variables

The Safetots (www.safetots.org) initiative suggests that deviations from normal physiological conditions could lead to negative effects on the brain, perhaps more important than the neurotoxicity potentially caused by anaesthetic agents [17]. Body temperature, ECG, blood pressure, EtCO2 and SpO2 were part of the routine monitoring in both studies. Gomez‐Pesquera et al. did not report these parameters, other than mentioning that they were not significantly different between the outcome groups. We report a small number of cases of transient desaturation, but hypotension defined according to a strict criterion of MAP < −1 SD of the blood pressure in the study by De Graaff et al., which we applied in our unit, was not uncommon (29 cases, 22%) [18]. However, this frequency was within the expected range set in the latter study, and there was no difference in the incidence between the groups.

Negative Postoperative Behaviour Changes

Reversible negative postoperative behavioural changes are not uncommon after anaesthesia in children, and the incidence varies widely between studies [3, 5, 6, 19, 20, 21, 22].

In the present study, the incidence of NPOBC at 7 days was 13%, which is in the lower range of the above‐cited studies, while the incidence in the study by Gomez‐Pesquera et al. was more than twice as high at 38.8% [6]. There were no obvious differences in the studied populations, except for the lower age of our cohort, which should theoretically have increased the incidence.

Previous studies have reported association of NPOBC with duration of anaesthesia, parental anxiety, child anxiety, length of hospital admission, difficult previous anaesthesia experience, learning problems, emotional problems, behavioural problems and young age [3]. Gomez‐Pesquera et al. did not report the duration of anaesthesia, but found that the duration of surgery was associated with increased NPOBC. The type of surgery was similar to that in our cohort except that we did not include ENT‐surgery. The age range in the present study was lower, excluding children above the age of 6 y.

Perioperative Anxiety

Other studies have reported increased NPOBC if the child experienced pain or emergence delirium during their last medical visit [23, 24, 25]. We used the simplified Paediatric Anesthesia Behavior scale to assess anxiety during induction while Gomez‐Pesquera et al. used the Yale Preoperative Anxiety Scale (YPAS). This limits the accuracy of a comparison, but the number of children with preoperative anxiety seems lower in the present study if a PAB score of two or three is considered equivalent to YPAS of > 30, which was the criterium used by Gomez‐Pesquera et al. [6].

According to our local routine, all children received propofol at the end of the procedure. We believe that this may be the main reason for the very low incidence of emergence delirium in our unit, potentially reducing the PHBQ results [26].

Premedication

There is conflicting evidence regarding the influence of premedication on NPOBC. A recent RCT comparing the incidence of postoperative behavioural changes in children randomised to midazolam, clonidine or dexmedetomidine found no association between the type of anxiolytic agent and NPOBC [21]. In the present study, a low dose of midazolam was frequently used for premedication according to local routine for this age group. There are no data on the use of premedication in the study by Gomez‐Pesquera et al., but a possible lack of use of midazolam would hardly explain the higher incidence of NPOBC compared with the present study [6].

Strengths and Limitations

The strength of this study was that crSO2 and standard monitoring were measured in a relatively large cohort of young children, which at the planning stage was adequately powered to detect significant changes in NPBOC based on the study by Gomez‐Pesquera et al. [6].

The study was observational with inherent limitations. At the analysis stage, it became obvious that it was underpowered for two reasons. Firstly, because both crSO2 reductions and NPOBCs were much less common than in the replicated study. Secondly, in several cases, we had technical problems with the INVOS device, which shut down unexpectedly. When that happened, we excluded the case. Thirdly, in the Gomez Pesquera study, PHBQ data were obtained by a research nurse calling the parents. We chose to supply parents with a QR code and let them enter PHBQ data without the help of a researcher to avoid any bias from the interviewer. Unfortunately, this resulted in additional missing data in spite of text message reminders to parents. Future studies of the possible association between physiologic and behavioural outcomes would need a much larger sample size to be meaningful.

Furthermore, both NIRS and PHBQ have limitations. Various NIRS devices use different algorithms and combinations of wavelengths to produce optimal signals, and the extracranial component of the NIRS signal has been shown to be significant in swine models and human adults [27, 28]. However, it is likely that this limitation is less relevant in children who have less thick skulls. Furthermore, while the coefficient of variation of regional oximetry with the INVOS device is less than 5%, the validation studies are based on comparing measurements of jugular venous and arterial saturation, which can at best provide an estimate of the true regional oxygen saturation. The overall uncertainty regarding the clinical relevance of minor changes in crSO2 suggests that future studies should be powered to investigate outcomes based on deeper regional desaturation as the primary outcome and consider AUC as an alternative outcome. Given the very low incidence of deep crSO2 reductions in the present study, a sample size in the thousands would be needed unless low‐risk procedures are excluded.

Finally, postoperative behaviour changes do not necessarily represent (minimal) brain damage with lasting neurological deficits and can be caused by a range of events during the perioperative period, as outlined above. However, NIRS and PHBQ measurements are non‐invasive, which permits their use in large studies as well as in clinical practice.

To conclude, in uneventful surgery with an anaesthesia protocol designed to minimise physiological stress and physiological changes during the perioperative period, regional cerebral desaturation and negative postoperative behaviour may be less common than previously reported in young children. We could not corroborate the strong association between these entities reported previously. However, given the low incidence of both reductions in crSO2 and NPBOC in the present study, a potential association between deep intraoperative crSO2 reduction and postoperative behaviour changes cannot be firmly dismissed.
