Loperamide-Induced Ventricular Arrhythmia and Seizures
Authors: Azalfa Lateef, Ufuk Vardar, Drew Sanclemente, Bridget Lee, Deepak Pasupula, Andrew S. Tseng, Christoffel J. van Niekerk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLoperamide-induced cardiotoxicity can occur when taken at high doses.Case SummaryA 42-year-old man presented with polymorphic ventricular tachycardia after taking 100 to 200 mg of loperamide daily to self-treat opioid withdrawal. The presenting electrocardiogram showed a wide complex rhythm which spontaneously converted to sinus rhythm with a prolonged QT interval. Implantable cardiac-defibrillator therapies treated polymorphic ventricular tachycardia and ventricular fibrillation. Loperamide was discontinued, and he was treated for opioid use disorder.DiscussionLoperamide can cause cardiotoxic manifestations such as acquired long QT syndrome with associated ventricular arrhythmias and neurologic manifestations such as seizures.Take-Home MessageAlthough loperamide is an over-the-counter medication, life threatening cardiotoxicity and neurotoxicity can occur with high doses.

History of Present Illness

A 42-year-old man with a past medical history of presumed long QT syndrome and prior episodes of sustained ventricular tachycardia (VT) status post dual-chamber Boston Scientific implantable cardiac-defibrillator (ICD) placement presented to the emergency department with recurrent syncopal episodes.Take-Home Message•Due to the widespread availability of loperamide over the counter, clinicians should have increased suspicion in patients with a previous history of opioids who are presenting with long QT syndromes and adverse effects such as VAs and seizures.

The patient's surgical history includes an abdominal hernia surgery a few years prior, which was complicated by severe pain. For pain management, he had a spinal pain stimulator implanted and was given a supply of oxycodone. On discontinuation of oxycodone, he reported experiencing withdrawal symptoms; therefore, the patient started taking 100 to 200 mg loperamide per day.

Previous Medical History

The patient underwent a successful supraventricular tachycardia ablation over 10 years ago. Additionally, he had an ICD placed 4 years ago for secondary prevention, because of symptomatic sustained VT, which was found on Holter monitor during work-up of syncope and presyncope. Additionally, per the patient, he was diagnosed with presumed congenital long QT syndrome (cLQTS); however, no genetic testing was done before confirm this diagnosis. He had no family history of cardiac pathology, specifically no family history of long QT syndrome. His social history revealed that he previously consumed 6 alcoholic drinks a day, 3 to 4 d/wk, but stopped drinking a year before presentation.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for his acute presentation consists of drug-induced QT prolongation from loperamide usage, cLQTS unmasked by loperamide, electrolyte derangements, arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathies, scar-mediated re-entrant VT, or myocardial ischemia.

Additional tests were performed, including thyroid stimulating hormone, ferritin, folate, hepatic function panel, complete blood cell count, comprehensive metabolic panel, and urine drug screen, all of which were unremarkable. Serological work-up for Lyme disease was negative.

To determine the etiology of the VT, a broad work-up was conducted. Serially drawn troponins remained negative. A transthoracic echocardiogram indicated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 61%, along with eccentric left ventricular hypertrophy, moderate enlargement of the left ventricle, and mild enlargement of the right ventricle, but no regional wall motion abnormalities were noted. A coronary angiogram showed no significant coronary artery disease. Cardiac magnetic resonance was unable to be performed because of the patient having an incompatible spinal stimulator. Genetic testing for cLQTS was negative.

Management

The patient was initially started on metoprolol tartrate 25 mg twice a day and received a loading dose of amiodarone of 150 mg. He was then transitioned to nadolol 40 mg daily because of the prior presumed history of long QT syndrome, but this was decreased to 10 mg on discharge because of intolerability.

His course was complicated by loperamide withdrawal which was treated with suboxone 3 times a day, baclofen 10 mg 3 times a day, and clonazepam 0.5 mg 2 times a day; however, suboxone and baclofen were stopped because of QTc prolongation. He eventually was then tapered off the clonazepam before discharge.

The patient followed-up with cardiology outpatient 2 months after his discharge. Since the hospitalization, the patient denies any syncopal episodes or seizures. The patient's ICD was briefly interrogated, revealing 23% atrial pacing, but no ventricular arrhythmias were noted. The patient has been avoiding QT prolonging drugs since his discharge and has been abstaining from loperamide. Unfortunately, the patient has been unable to get an exercise stress test to further stratify his long QT syndrome (LQTS).

Discussion

Loperamide is an over-the-counter medication that is used as an antidiarrheal medication because of its mu-opioid agonism.1 Additionally, people have also used loperamide as an agent to relieve withdrawal symptoms from opioids.1 However, because of its variable bioavailability, loperamide can be cardiotoxic and neurotoxic at higher doses.1,2 The medication is metabolized through the cytochrome P-450 system.2 In adults, the maximum recommended daily dose of loperamide is 16 mg, which does not significantly penetrate the blood-brain barrier.2 At larger doses, there is an increased absorption across the blood-brain barrier and the effects are unpredictable, leading to, in some cases, seizures.2, 3, 4 Several case studies have indicated VT or VT storm at daily doses from 200 to 600 mg of loperamide.4,5

The most observed cardiotoxic effects from loperamide are cardiac conduction disturbances which can manifest in various ways such as QT prolongation, polymorphic VT, Torsades de pointes, and VT storm.1,3 These can manifest because of inhibition of the sodium and potassium channels, specifically the hERG voltage-gated channel, leading to a Brugada ECG pattern, QT prolongation, and/or QRS prolongation.1,6

A previous review determined the electrophysiological features of loperamide cardiotoxicity.6 They noted that the median QRS duration on presentation was 160 milliseconds and the median QTc was 620 milliseconds.6 The median loperamide daily dose was 200 mg.6 Of the 36 cases that were reviewed, 67% experienced polymorphic VT or Torsades de pointes.6 Additionally, 3 of the cases had type 1 Brugada ECG pattern.6

Cardiotoxic events caused by loperamide are managed based on the manifestation. In one case study where a patient ingested 200 mg of loperamide for 7 days, developed VT storm, and his ICD had shocked him 25 times appropriately, he was started on mexiletine 200 mg 3 times a day.3 Another case study showed treatment with isoproterenol of QTc prolongation in a patient who had seizure-like activity and recurrent VT.5

The ventricular arrythmia our patient presented with on his device interrogation was predominantly polymorphic VT and ventricular fibrillation, which is consistent with ventricular arrythmias resulting from QT prolonging agents. His presenting ECG was a wide complex rhythm consistent with accelerated idioventricular rhythm which has various etiologies including reperfusion after an acute myocardial infarction, after return of circulation after cardiac arrest, electrolyte abnormalities, and increased catecholamine states.7 These rhythms are most often attributed to enhanced automaticity which are self-limiting and frequently benign.7 Our patient's rhythm can be explained by the recent cardiac arrest with subsequent ICD shock and likely high adrenergic tone.

Genetic testing in patients with drug-induced QT prolongation has been much debated. Previous studies have shown that a substantive number of patients who had been previously misdiagnosed with drug-induced acquired long QT syndrome possessed pathogenic genetic variants consistent with cLQTS, which had been unmasked by a triggering factor, such as a drug or electrolyte imbalance, notably hypokalemia.8 Genetic contributions to QT prolongation are complex and involve both monogenic and polygenic processes.9 Canonical cLQTS is considered to be a monogenic disease process, where a single genetic variant has sufficient effect size to cause disease. Modern genetic testing panels for long QT syndrome have a yield of >80%. The remaining one-fifth of patients with LQTS with a negative genetic test either have a rare yet-to-be-discovered monogenic disease variant or may in fact have a polygenic disease process. The polygenic disease mechanism involves multiple genetic polymorphisms, where more common genetic variants with small effect sizes ultimately cause disease. To determine the contribution polygenic variations are causing, a polygenic risk score can be calculated; however, none are available for clinical use in LQTS.9 To add further complexity, polygenic variants can have an additive effect on monogenic factors that exacerbate QT prolongation.9 Prior studies have investigated QTc thresholds and other variables that could serve as predictors for carrying a mutation. One study reported that patients who met 2 of the following 3 criteria were more likely to carry a mutation that warranted further genetic testing: being <40 years of age at the time of exposure, having a QTc interval >440 milliseconds without a triggering factor, and experiencing symptoms during a prolonged QTc interval.8

Based on the previous literature, our patient appropriately underwent genetic testing for a suspected underlying cardiac channelopathy, which was ultimately negative for pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants. As previously mentioned, even with a negative genetic test, cLQTS is not definitively ruled out. Additionally, the patient was unable to undergo a stress test, which could evaluate the QT interval response to exercise and help differentiate acquired LQTS vs genotype-negative cLQTS.

Conclusions

The most likely explanation for our patient's incessant VA was his ongoing loperamide misuse at doses in the range of previously described cardiotoxic effects. Our case is unique given the prolonged time to diagnosis. Our patient took high doses of loperamide for 4 years compared with the previously presented cases of just days to weeks, highlighting the variability of cardiotoxic manifestations with dosage and duration of loperamide.
