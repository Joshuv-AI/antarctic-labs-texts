Regional inequities in myocardial infarction burden in Brazil’s public healthcare system
Authors: Sophia A. Arzumanov, Andrea D. Stephanus, Joaquim Barreto, Luiz Sergio F. Carvalho, Andrei C. Sposito, Alessandra M. Campos-Staffico
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMyocardial infarction (MI) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, with an uneven burden across Brazil. We evaluated trends in emergency MI hospitalizations, in-hospital mortality, and standardized costs within Brazil's Unified Health System (SUS) from 2008 to 2023, with emphasis on regional disparities and the COVID-19 period.MethodsWe performed a retrospective observational analysis using SUS Hospital Information System (SIH/SUS) data. Age- and sex-standardized rates were calculated for emergency MI hospitalizations and in-hospital mortality; standardized costs were expressed as BRL per hospital day and adjusted for inflation and regional purchasing power. Five-year interval means, cumulative changes, and Z-tests with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were computed. To assess associations between health-system supply factors (primary care capillarity, inpatient capacity, and workforce) and MI outcomes, we estimated region-year two-way fixed-effects panel regressions using lagged supply indices derived from CNES/TABNET. Corresponding linear two-way fixed-effects trend panels are presented to visualize the model-implied within-region linear associations.ResultsEmergency MI hospitalization rates increased by 145.91% nationally from 2008 to 2023, with the greatest growth in the Central-West (+ 240.88%). The South and Southeast consistently recorded the highest hospitalization and mortality rates, while the North and Northeast – despite lower baseline rates – showed the steepest proportional growth, raising important equity concerns. In-hospital mortality increased by 166.67% nationally, with marked worsening during the COVID-19 period (2020–2022). In contrast, standardized cost per hospital day declined nationally by 7.38% across five-year means (768.69 to 663.98 BRL/day; p < 0.001), remaining highest in the South (1102.69 BRL/day in 2018–2022) and declining most in the North (684.38 to 465.70 BRL/day; p < 0.001). In supply-side panel analyses, inpatient-capacity expansion was most consistently associated with lower per-day reimbursement intensity and lower MI hospitalization rates within regions over time; supply indices showed limited association with in-hospital mortality.ConclusionMI burden in Brazil's SUS rose substantially from 2008 to 2023, with persistent regional inequities and worsened outcomes during the pandemic. Inpatient-capacity expansion was the supply domain most consistently linked to MI utilization and cost variation, though all associations are ecological rather than causal. Standardized per-day inpatient costs did not rise commensurately with the growing clinical burden, suggesting that reimbursement/resource intensity per hospital day did not expand in step with demand. These findings support region-specific efforts to strengthen timely, evidence-based acute MI care – particularly in the North and Northeast, where demand is rising fastest against a backdrop of structural resource constraints.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-026-01968-2.

Background

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of death worldwide, claiming one death every 1.5 s in 2021 [1]. Ischemic heart disease (IHD), the most lethal manifestation of CVD [2], affects roughly 200 million people globally [3] and most commonly presents as myocardial infarction (MI). While high-income countries have experienced declining IHD incidence over the past two decades [4], low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) disproportionately account for more than 75% of all CVD-related deaths [5]. Brazil, a large LMIC, faces an escalating public health challenge, with rising CVD prevalence and incidence [6].

Beyond its human toll, CVD also imposes a substantial economic burden. Globally, CVD consumes nearly 10% of total health expenditures, with projected costs exceeding $1 trillion by 2030 [7]. In Brazil, between 2010 and 2015, health expenditures averaged 9.5% of gross domestic product (GDP), and CVD alone accounted for an average of 0.7% of GDP [8], with MI representing the most expensive form of CVD [9]. The Unified Health System (SUS), which serves approximately 72% of the Brazilian population [10], faces mounting financial pressure as MI incidence rises. This burden is further exacerbated by regional disparities in healthcare infrastructure, which are reflected in differences in MI survival rates between weekdays and weekends, suggesting shortages of healthcare professionals [11]. In regions without cardiologists, MI care frequently depends on primary care providers and allied health professionals [6].

Together, these epidemiological and economic pressures underscore the need for comprehensive, up-to-date data on hospitalizations, costs, and mortality to guide effective public health policies and interventions. Although previous studies have focused on specific regions [12], a national, equity-oriented assessment is essential to capture demographic and regional heterogeneity and to inform targeted resource allocation.

Against this backdrop, we analyzed the burden of MI within Brazil's public healthcare system by examining long-term trends in emergency hospitalizations, in-hospital mortality, and reimbursement-based cost indicators, with particular emphasis on regional disparities and the COVID-19 period. By integrating descriptive trend analyses with supply-side panel models, this study seeks not only to quantify how this burden evolved from 2008 to 2023, but also to clarify how regional health-system structure may have shaped these patterns.

Methods

Study design and population

This descriptive, retrospective observational study analyzed trends and the distribution of emergency hospitalizations for MI in Brazil across a 16-year period. Secondary data were obtained from the SUS Hospital Information System (SIH/SUS), covering emergency hospitalizations from January 2008 to December 2023 across Brazil’s five regions. The dataset included claims for individuals aged from under 1 year to 80 years and older, covering both biological sexes, and all racial categories as defined by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE; White, Black, Asian, Indigenous, and Brown). Data on associated costs and in-hospital mortality were analyzed to identify trends across demographics and regions. Cost information was obtained from the SIH/SUS administrative reimbursement database and represents amounts paid by the SUS for each hospitalization (i.e., claims-based expenditures rather than micro-costed hospital accounting). SIH/SUS reports multiple cost fields (e.g., total reimbursed amount, hospital service component, professional/workforce component, and mean cost per admission). These values should be understood as claims-based SUS reimbursement amounts rather than direct hospital accounting costs, and all subsequent cost analyses were designed to interpret them accordingly. In this study, we used the total reimbursed cost for each hospitalization, then derived standardized metrics such as inflation- and purchasing-power-adjusted cost per length of stay (LOS). As this study utilized only historical data, no interventions or experimental conditions were implemented. This design enables the identification of long-term trends and regional inequities in MI burden within SUS, while remaining subject to the inherent limitations of administrative data sources.

To complement the descriptive analyses and examine whether temporal changes in health-system supply were associated with MI hospitalization, mortality, and cost indicators, we also assembled region-year measures of SUS service supply and capacity using public administrative data. All supply indicators were obtained from the TABNET/DATASUS platform, drawing on the Cadastro Nacional de Estabelecimentos de Saúde (CNES) modules for establishments, physical resources, and human resources.

Emergency hospitalization and mortality rates

Emergency hospitalization and mortality rates were calculated as age- and sex-standardized rates per 100,000 population, using population projections from IBGE and the direct standardization method. The Brazilian population from the 2022 census served as the standard population for this analysis. Annual population estimates were utilized for intercensal years, given the decennial nature of the Brazilian census.

Standardization proceeded as follows. The number of emergency hospitalizations and in-hospital deaths due to MI recorded in the SIH/SUS database was first divided by the corresponding population estimates for each age and sex group to obtain crude rates. These crude rates were then adjusted using the direct standardization method: the age- and sex-specific crude rates were multiplied by the proportion of the standard population in each corresponding age and sex group, and the weighted rates for all groups were summed to produce the age- and sex-standardized rate per 100,000 population. This process ensured that the resulting rates accounted for differences in population composition over time, allowing consistent comparisons across years and between regions.

Five-year averages were computed for three intervals (2008–2012, 2013–2017, and 2018–2022) to smooth annual fluctuations and highlight long-term trends, whereas annual trend analyses covered the full 2008–2023 study period. This approach enhanced interpretability and supported robust regional comparisons by reducing sensitivity to single-year shocks.

Inflation- and purchasing-power-adjusted standardized cost

The age- and sex-standardized cost of emergency hospitalizations for MI relative to the LOS was analyzed, expressed in Brazilian Real (BRL) per day. Standardization was achieved using the direct method, adjusting costs based on age and sex distributions in the Brazilian standard population. The cost per emergency hospitalization was calculated by dividing the total expenditure associated with MI emergency hospitalizations, as recorded in the SIH/SUS database, by the total number of hospital days. These standardized costs were calculated for the entire study period and further stratified by region. Because SIH/SUS records reimbursement values in nominal BRL, we first standardized these amounts over time using national inflation and then applied a regional price-level proxy to improve cross-region comparability.

All costs were converted to constant 2023 BRL using the official Extended National Consumer Price Index (IPCA). To address regional differences in consumer price dynamics, we then applied a secondary adjustment using the IBGE table of accumulated IPCA variation by region, based on grouped reference cities across Brazil, as a proxy for regional cost-of-living differences. Monthly IPCA index values were obtained from IBGE’s system for automated data retrieval, Sistema IBGE de Recuperação Automática (SIDRA), Table 1737 [13]. For each calendar year (y), the annual IPCA index was computed as the arithmetic mean of the 12 monthly index values, and an inflation adjustment factor was constructed as: Adjustment factor (y) = Annual IPCA index (2023)/Annual IPCA index (y). Nominal costs for year (y) were then multiplied by this factor to express them in 2023 BRL.

To improve comparability of the inflation-adjusted cost-per-day metric across regions with different local price levels, we additionally applied a regional price-level adjustment using IBGE regional IPCA variation as a proxy for differences in cost of living across regions [14]. Accordingly, the resulting estimates should be interpreted as regionally price-level-adjusted reimbursement/resource intensity per inpatient day. For each region (r) and year (y), we rescaled the IPCA-deflated cost per LOS using the regional accumulated IPCA variation relative to the national reference, yielding values expressed as regionally adjusted BRL/day. This adjustment was intended to improve cross-region comparability of claims-based reimbursement values, but it does not represent a direct measure of hospital production costs. Because many high-cost cardiac inputs, such as stents, pacemakers, and other device-intensive technologies, are nationally priced and/or import-dependent, the regional adjustment is likely more informative for locally varying components such as labor and general service conditions than for tradable technology costs.

Building on these inflation- and purchasing-power-adjusted costs, simple arithmetic means were calculated for the periods 2008–2012, 2013–2017, and 2018–2022, separately for each region and for Brazil overall, and percent changes between adjacent periods were computed to assess trends in resource utilization and expenditure. As with hospitalization and mortality rates, five-year averages of inflation- and purchasing-power-adjusted standardized costs per day were computed to highlight long-term trends. Even after these adjustments, the resulting metric should be interpreted as a standardized measure of reimbursement/resource intensity per inpatient day rather than as a direct estimate of hospital production cost or technical efficiency. In line with the interpretation of SIH/SUS payment data, inflation- and purchasing-power-adjusted cost per LOS was treated as a proxy for SUS reimbursement/resource intensity per inpatient day, rather than as a direct measure of efficiency, quality of care, or total economic burden. Accordingly, regional differences in this metric are interpreted as variation in per-day reimbursement/resource intensity, after adjustment for time inflation (IPCA) and cross-regional purchasing power.

Health-system supply indicators and composite indices

To characterize SUS service supply expansion over time, we compiled annual region-level indicators from CNES via TABNET/DATASUS (establishments, physical resources, and human resources) for 2008–2023. Indicators were harmonized to the five Brazilian regions and converted to population-normalized (per-capita) rates to support comparability across regions and years.

Supply measures were organized into three conceptual domains: (i) primary care capillarity, (ii) inpatient capacity, and (iii) workforce. Primary care capillarity was captured using primary care team availability indicators (e.g., Family Health Strategy and primary care teams). Inpatient capacity was represented by hospital physical-resource indicators reflecting inpatient service availability (e.g., hospital and intensive care unit beds) and used as proxies for acute-care capacity relevant to MI admission and inpatient management. Workforce capacity was captured using human-resource indicators (e.g., cardiologists – both clinicians and interventionists – and nursing professionals) and used as proxies for clinical and procedural staffing relevant to emergency and inpatient cardiovascular care.

Within each domain, we constructed standardized composite indices by transforming each underlying indicator into a pooled z-score across all region-year observations (i.e., subtracting the overall mean and dividing by the overall standard deviation). Domain-specific indices were computed as the unweighted mean of the z-scored indicators within that domain, and an overall supply index was then computed as the unweighted mean of the three domain indices, yielding a single summary measure of SUS supply/capacity expansion. For association analyses, we used one-year lagged versions of the indices (t-1) to reflect that changes in supply may precede changes in utilization, outcomes, and reimbursement intensity.

Ethics

This study analyzed publicly available, de-identified administrative records from the SUS. As the dataset contains no identifiable private information and includes no direct interaction with individuals, the study did not constitute human subjects research as defined; therefore, ethics approval and informed consent were not required.

Statistical analyses

Emergency hospitalization rates, costs, mortality rates, and population data were aggregated by region (North, Northeast, Southeast, South, Central-West) from 2008 to 2023. Age- and sex-standardized emergency hospitalization rates, in-hospital mortality rates, and costs per LOS for MI were calculated using the direct standardization method as previously described, enabling consistent comparisons across regions and time periods. These standardized outputs were plotted to analyze trends at both the national and regional levels.

To assess long-term trends, data were analyzed across three 5-year intervals: 2008–2012, 2013–2017, and 2018–2022. Analyses included 95% confidence intervals (CI) calculated using the normal approximation method. Standard errors for the age- and sex-standardized hospitalization rates, in-hospital mortality rates, and costs per LOS were derived from the respective ratios (e.g., standardized cost per LOS to the square root of the total LOS, or similar calculations for rates). All results were rounded to two decimal places.

Several visualizations were developed to depict national and regional trends in age- and sex-standardized emergency hospitalization rates, in-hospital mortality rates, and costs per LOS across Brazil over the study period. The geographical analysis was conducted in R using specialized packages (sf, ggplot2, and rnaturalearth). State-level geographic data from the Natural Earth database were aggregated into Brazil’s five administrative regions, and five-year averages for each outcome were calculated for 2008–2012, 2013–2017, and 2018–2022 intervals. Regional differences were displayed using comparative plots and choropleth maps with a graduated blue color scale, applying consistent scaling and cartographic parameters across periods to support valid comparisons. Maps were arranged in a three-panel layout using the patchwork package to facilitate visualization of temporal patterns and regional disparities.

To evaluate statistical significance, Z-tests were performed to compare age- and sex-standardized emergency hospitalization rates, in-hospital mortality rates, and costs per LOS between intervals (2008–2012 vs. 2013–2017, 2013–2017 vs. 2018–2022, and 2008–2012 vs. 2018–2022). P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant and were reported to three decimal places. Observations with missing values for variables required in each analysis were excluded (complete-case analysis), and no imputation was performed.

To further evaluate associations between supply expansion and MI outcomes, we estimated region-year panel regressions with two-way fixed effects (region and year) and heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors. Models related each outcome – (i) age- and sex-standardized MI hospitalization rate per 100,000 population, (ii) in-hospital MI mortality rate per 100,000 population, and (iii) log inflation- and purchasing-power-adjusted cost per LOS – to the lagged overall supply index and, in separate specifications, to the lagged domain indices (primary care, inpatient capacity, workforce) entered individually and jointly. Because the number of regions is small (N = 5), regression findings were interpreted as ecological associations describing within-region changes over time rather than causal effects. To visualize the estimated linear within-region associations, we also report linear two-way fixed effects trend panels for each outcome, plotting region- and year-residualized (within-transformed) outcomes against the region- and year-residualized lagged indices with an overlaid model-implied fitted line.

All analyses were performed in R 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) within a Jupyter Notebook environment, ensuring computational consistency and reproducibility. The complete analytical pipeline, including all data processing, statistical analyses, and visualization code, is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Results

Trends in age- and sex-standardized emergency hospitalization rates for MI in Brazil

Trends in age- and sex-standardized emergency hospitalization rates for MI per 100,000 population across Brazil from 2008 to 2023 are presented in Figs. 1 and 2. Across Brazil, hospitalization rates rose significantly over time. During the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022), MI emergency hospitalization rates accelerated in all regions, reversing the declines observed from 2019 to 2020. Regional trends were further analyzed using five-year averages (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table S2). Between 2008–2012 and 2013–2017, a national increase of 31.53% was observed (from 35.33 to 46.47 per 100,000 population). Although the South and Southeast remained highest in absolute rates, the largest proportional increases in this interval occurred in historically lower-rate regions, including the North (+ 47.34%, from 13.92 to 20.51 per 100,000 population) and the Northeast (+ 47.66%, from 21.55 to 31.82 per 100,000 population). Rates in the South increased by 32.12% (from 51.53 to 68.08 per 100,000 population), followed by the Central-West (+ 29.58%, from 28.94 to 37.50 per 100,000 population). The Southeast recorded the smallest proportional increase in rates (+ 25.53%, from 44.19 to 55.47 per 100,000 population).

In the subsequent interval, between 2013–2017 and 2018–2022 five-year averages continued to rise, with a national increase of 30.54% (from 46.47 to 60.66 per 100,000 population). The most striking change was observed in the Central-West, which increased by 76.51% (from 37.50 to 66.19 per 100,000 population), representing the largest inter-period change among all regions. Increases of 41.59% and 33.41% were observed in the North (from 20.51 to 29.04 per 100,000 population) and Northeast (from 31.82 to 42.45 per 100,000 population), respectively. More modest increases were noted in the Southeast (+ 27.01%, from 55.47 to 70.45 per 100,000 population) and the South (+ 20.70%, from 68.08 to 82.17 per 100,000 population). Despite smaller proportional changes in these high-burden regions, their absolute rates remained the highest nationwide (Fig. 2).

Together, these patterns indicate both persistently high MI hospitalization burden in the South and Southeast and rapid relative growth in the North, Northeast, and Central-West. All inter-period comparisons between five-year averages were statistically significant (p < 0.05, Table S2), underscoring the substantial upward trends in emergency hospitalization rates for MI across Brazil. To examine whether these descriptive trends were accompanied by changes in regional health-system structure, we next evaluated associations between SUS supply expansion and MI hospitalization rates over time.

Association between SUS supply expansion and MI hospitalization rates

To contextualize these trends within the broader health-system landscape, we examined associations between SUS supply expansion and MI hospitalization rates using two-way fixed-effects panel regressions (region and year) with one-year lagged supply indices (t-1). Higher values of the overall SUS supply index were positively associated with standardized MI hospitalization rates (β = 10.03; robust SE = 4.64; p < 0.05; Supplementary Table S3 and Supplementary Figure S1). In domain-specific models, inpatient capacity was inversely associated with MI hospitalization rates (β = −9.94; SE = 2.91; p < 0.01), whereas workforce capacity was positively associated with MI hospitalization rates (β = 11.14; SE = 3.89; p < 0.01); the primary care capillarity domain showed no statistically significant association (β = 4.80; SE = 3.65; p > 0.05). When the three domains were entered jointly, the inverse association for inpatient capacity (β = −7.54; SE = 2.50; p < 0.01) and the positive association for workforce (β = 10.16; SE = 2.89; p < 0.001) remained significant, while the primary care index remained non-significant (β = 3.53; SE = 3.39; p > 0.05). Overall, these models indicate that within-region temporal variation in MI hospitalization rates tracked more consistently with inpatient-capacity and workforce domains than with the primary care capillarity domain in this regional panel. We then turned from utilization to spending, examining whether the growing hospitalization burden was accompanied by parallel changes in standardized per-day reimbursement intensity.

Age- and sex-standardized, inflation- and purchasing-power-adjusted cost per emergency hospitalization length of stay for MI

Alongside rising hospitalization rates, we examined trends in standardized costs. The age- and sex-standardized, inflation- and purchasing-power-adjusted cost per emergency MI hospitalization relative to LOS (BRL/day) declined overall at the national level, while remaining consistently highest in the South (Fig. 3). Nationally, costs decreased from 715.64 BRL/day in 2008 to 662.81 BRL/day in 2023 (cumulative −7.38%), with a period of relative stability through 2012 followed by declines through 2019. Overall, Figs. 3 and 4 show that standardized inflation- and purchasing-power-adjusted costs per LOS generally trended downward nationwide, but with persistent regional stratification – highest in the South – and a divergent upward shift in the Central-West in the most recent period.

Association between SUS supply expansion and cost per length-of-stay

Complementing the descriptive cost trends, two-way fixed-effects panel regressions (region and year fixed effects) using one-year lagged supply indices (t-1) were used to examine supply-side associations with cost per LOS. The overall SUS supply index was not statistically associated with log inflation- and purchasing-power-adjusted cost per hospital day (β = 37.70; robust SE = 41.77; p > 0.05; Supplementary Table S6 and Supplementary Figure S2). In domain-specific models, inpatient capacity showed a consistent inverse association with log cost per hospital day (β = -60.67; SE = 24.99; p < 0.05), whereas primary care capillarity (β = 33.13; SE = 33.63; p > 0.05) and workforce capacity (β = 40.50; SE = 50.21; p > 0.05) were not statistically associated with costs in isolation. When the three domains were entered jointly, the inverse association between inpatient capacity and cost per LOS remained significant (β = -50.43; SE = 24.98; p < 0.01), while primary care (β = 26.47; SE = 34.45; p > 0.05) and workforce (β = 33.79; SE = 44.34) remained non-significant.

Interpreted on the log scale, the inpatient-capacity coefficient in the joint model corresponds to approximately 11% lower cost per hospital day for a one-standard deviation increase in inpatient capacity in the prior year, holding other domains constant. Taken together, these findings suggest that within-region changes in inpatient capacity were more consistently related to variation in inflation- and purchasing-power-adjusted cost per LOS than changes in primary care or workforce supply in this macro-region panel. Finally, we assessed in-hospital mortality to determine whether worsening utilization patterns and changing reimbursement intensity were accompanied by deterioration in hospital outcomes.

In the subsequent period, from 2013–2017 to 2018–2022, mortality continued to rise across all regions, with a national increase of 27.86% (from 8.29 to 10.60 deaths per 100,000 population). The South again recorded the highest mortality rate, reaching 13.85 deaths per 100,000 population, but showed the smallest proportional increase (+ 24.77%). Association between SUS supply expansion and in-hospital MI mortality rates

Finally, we examined whether supply expansion was associated with in-hospital mortality trends. In two-way fixed-effects panel regressions (region and year fixed effects) using one-year lagged supply indices (t-1), the overall SUS supply index was not statistically associated with standardized in-hospital MI mortality rates (β = 0.029; robust SE = 0.426; p > 0.05; Supplementary Table S9 and Supplementary Figure S3). In one-domain models, primary care capillarity (β = 0.124; SE = 0.201; p > 0.05) and workforce capacity (β = 0.232; SE = 0.249; p > 0.05) were not statistically associated with mortality rates. Inpatient capacity showed a marginal inverse association with mortality in the one-domain model (β = −0.531; SE = 0.265; p < 0.10). When the three domains were entered jointly, the inpatient-capacity coefficient was negative (β = −0.488; SE = 0.278; p > 0.05) but did not reach statistical significance, while the primary care (β = 0.068; SE = 0.207; p > 0.05) and workforce (β = 0.177; SE = 0.264; p > 0.05) coefficients remained non-significant. Overall, these findings provide limited evidence of a consistent linear association between supply indices and in-hospital MI mortality rates in this macro-region panel, although inpatient-capacity variation showed a weak inverse signal in the bivariate specification – a pattern consistent with the cost findings and warranting further investigation with finer-grained data.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is a pioneering study on the epidemiology of MI emergency hospitalizations in Brazil from 2008 to 2023, offering critical insights into recent trends and regional disparities. Our findings highlight significant increases in hospitalization rates, in-hospital mortality, and resource needs during this period. Despite the establishment of the SUS as the world’s largest community-based public health system [15], serving approximately 72% of Brazil’s population in 2020, healthcare infrastructure and resources may not have expanded sufficiently to meet the growing demand for MI treatment and prevention [16, 17]. Over this period, emergency hospitalizations for MI increased by 145.91%, and in-hospital mortality rates increased by 166.67% nationally. Importantly, when costs were expressed as age- and sex-standardized spending per hospital day and adjusted for both inflation and regional purchasing power, they did not rise in parallel with the increasing clinical burden. Instead, Brazil’s purchasing-power-adjusted five-year mean cost per hospital day declined from 768.69 BRL/day in 2008–2012 to 712.19 BRL/day in 2013–2017 and 663.98 BRL/day in 2018–2022, with a statistically significant long-interval reduction (2008–2012 vs. 2018–2022; p < 0.001), and statistically significant adjacent-period declines (2008–2012 vs. 2013–2017: p = 0.009; 2013–2017 vs. 2018–2022: p = 0.012). Taken together, these patterns suggest that measured MI burden increased substantially over time, while real inflation- and purchasing-power-adjusted per-day inpatient spending recorded in SIH/SUS did not expand commensurately.

These national trends, however, mask important regional heterogeneity that is central to understanding the equity implications of our findings. We would expect large regional disparities in MI outcomes based on the current healthcare infrastructure. Hospital beds are concentrated in medium and large cities, with the Southeast having more hospital beds than the combined total in other regions [18]. Limited access to medical care in more rural regions is associated with treatment delays, which have been linked to higher mortality rates for patients with ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) (each hour of delay increases the risk of death by 6.2% in Brazil) and greater cost through preventable complications and prolonged care pathways (each 3-h delay adds Int$497) [19]. These challenges are compounded by prolonged waiting times for specialized care, as highlighted by a study from Salvador, Bahia, which revealed that delays reflect deficiencies in healthcare infrastructure and limited recognition of MI symptoms [20]. Together, these factors may contribute to increased risk of death for MI patients in under-resourced regions of Brazil. The results of our study are consistent with this expectation and provide further insight into regional nuances. Notably, the marked rise in MI emergency hospitalizations in the North and Northeast is a central finding of this study. Even after age- and sex-standardization, these historically under-resourced regions showed substantial growth in recorded MI hospital utilization, consistent with an epidemiological transition and/or improved access and recognition. From a health-system perspective, this shift is particularly consequential because it indicates that the MI burden is increasingly concentrated in settings where timely diagnosis, reperfusion capability, and referral networks may be less available – thereby amplifying equity concerns within SUS. At the same time, because SIH/SUS data are derived from Hospital Admission Authorizations completed by frontline professionals for administrative/reimbursement purposes, our findings are inherently dependent on the completeness and accuracy of AIH form filling and coding. This dependence is a well-recognized challenge of using Brazilian secondary administrative data and may vary across services and regions [21], potentially contributing to differential misclassification and/or temporal changes in recorded MI hospitalizations. Having described where the burden rose most sharply, we next examined whether these regional trends tracked changes in SUS service supply over time.

To directly address whether changes in health-system supply tracked MI outcomes over time, we incorporated CNES-derived measures of SUS service supply (primary care capillarity, inpatient capacity, and workforce) and evaluated their associations with MI hospitalization rates, in-hospital mortality rates, and cost per LOS using region-year panel models with region and year fixed effects. These analyses are ecological and should be interpreted as associations describing within-region changes over time, not causal effects. Within this macro-region panel, inpatient-capacity expansion showed the most consistent signal: higher inpatient capacity was associated with lower inflation- and purchasing-power-adjusted cost per LOS in both one-domain and joint-domain models (β ≈ −0.12 to −0.15 on the log scale), and with lower MI hospitalization rates in both one-domain and joint-domain models (β ≈ −7.5 to −9.9 per 100,000 population). In contrast, workforce capacity was positively associated with MI hospitalization rates (β ≈ 10–11 per 100,000 population), potentially reflecting increased access and diagnostic/admission capacity in settings with expanding staffing, rather than changes in underlying MI incidence. For mortality, the overall supply index and domain indices showed limited evidence of a consistent linear association; inpatient capacity exhibited only a weak inverse signal in bivariate specification (p < 0.10), which attenuated when domains were modeled jointly. Overall, these results suggest that temporal variation in measured MI utilization and reimbursement intensity per day may be more closely aligned with changes in inpatient capacity and workforce supply than with primary care capillarity at the macro-region level. These supply-side findings are especially relevant for interpreting the cost results, because they suggest that temporal changes in measured reimbursement intensity may partly reflect changes in service capacity and case flow rather than simple changes in efficiency. We therefore interpret the regional cost findings not as direct measures of hospital production costs, but as patterns in standardized SIH/SUS reimbursement intensity that require careful contextual reading.

Turning to regional cost patterns, the South, despite having the highest hospitalization and mortality rates, also maintained the highest standardized costs per LOS across all five-year intervals, but period-to-period differences were not statistically significant. Specifically, the South’s inflation- and purchasing-power-adjusted mean cost per day was 1152.11 BRL/day in 2008–2012, 1130.27 BRL/day in 2013–2017, and 1102.69 BRL/day in 2018–2022 (all pairwise p > 0.05). This pattern is consistent with greater availability of specialized services and higher-cost treatment pathways in this region, including pPCI, where more resource-intensive acute MI care is both accessible and routinely delivered within SUS facilities [22]. However, rising mortality despite persistently higher per-day costs suggests that higher spending intensity alone may be insufficient to offset the effects of increasing case volume, delays in presentation, and health-system strain. Such strain may be compounded by patient migration from resource-limited regions seeking better care. Similarly, the South and Southeast, where private health insurance coverage is higher (34.10 and 23.89 insured per 100 population, respectively, compared to 9.69 per 100 population in the North region), may experience different demand dynamics within public facilities because a larger share of residents can access private-sector services [23]. The South and Southeast, with better-equipped hospitals and access to specialized treatments, may also attract patients from under-resourced areas such as the North and Northeast, as suggested by analogous patterns documented for other high-complexity services [24, 25]. A similar pattern may occur in the Central-West, home to referral centers in cities such as Brasília and Goiânia.

At the end of the cost spectrum, the North showed the steepest and most consistently statistically supported decline in standardized costs per LOS, decreasing from 684.38 BRL/day (2008–2012) to 539.13 BRL/day (2013–2017) (p = 0.002) and to 465.70 BRL/day (2018–2022) (p < 0.001 for 2008–2012 vs. 2018–2022), whereas the 2013–2017 vs. 2018–2022 comparison was not statistically significant (p = 0.068). The Northeast and Southeast also declined over the long interval (Northeast: 573.52 to 496.21 BRL/day, p = 0.003; Southeast: 732.48 to 627.06 BRL/day, p < 0.001), although adjacent-period comparisons were weaker (Northeast 2008–2012 vs. 2013–2017 p = 0.460; 2013–2017 vs. 2018–2022 p = 0.006; Southeast 2008–2012 vs. 2013–2017 p = 0.021; 2013–2017 vs. 2018–2022 p = 0.084). These reductions should not be interpreted as reduced MI burden – hospitalizations and mortality rose substantially – but rather as a signal that real per-day inpatient spending did not expand in step with demand. Several non-mutually exclusive mechanisms could explain this pattern, including constrained availability of high-acuity services, shifts in SUS reimbursement relative to inflation, differences in case mix captured within SIH/SUS, or changes in coding and billing practices. Such case-mix differences may include unobserved changes in the relative proportion of STEMI and non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) admissions over time, which could affect average per-day reimbursement and mortality patterns because these syndromes often follow different diagnostic and treatment pathways. Because our cost metric is standardized, expressed per day, and adjusted for inflation and purchasing power, it is best interpreted as a proxy for SUS reimbursement/resource intensity per inpatient day (in constant, Brazil-average inflation- and purchasing-power BRL), rather than a measure of efficiency, quality of care, or total economic burden. Moreover, the regional adjustment was based on accumulated IPCA variation by region as a proxy for differences in consumer price dynamics and cost of living, rather than on a healthcare-specific PPP; accordingly, it may not fully capture the behavior of nationally priced or import-dependent cardiac technologies such as stents and pacemakers. In the Brazilian public system, another plausible explanation for the downward trend in inflation-adjusted cost per hospital day is the behavior of administratively defined reimbursement schedules. SUS hospital reimbursement values are governed by SIGTAP [26], and these values may remain unchanged for prolonged periods rather than being automatically rebalanced to the IPCA. Therefore, part of the apparent “real” decline in per-day cost may reflect a widening gap between fixed reimbursement values and general inflation, rather than a true reduction in clinical resource use or greater technical efficiency in MI care. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted primarily as trends in real reimbursement intensity recorded in SIH/SUS, not as direct evidence that hospitals consumed fewer resources per inpatient day. Consistent with this interpretation, we observed an inverse association between inpatient capacity and cost per LOS, suggesting that periods of higher capacity were associated with lower per-day reimbursement intensity, potentially reflecting changes in case mix, LOS dynamics, or reimbursement mechanisms rather than “efficiency gains” per se.

The Central-West presented a distinct trajectory. Unlike the national monotonic decline, this region showed a non-monotonic pattern across periods (618.84 BRL/day in 2008–2012; 600.84 BRL/day in 2013–2017; 651.33 BRL/day in 2018–2022), with a significant increase from 2013–2017 to 2018–2022 (p = 0.012), while the 2008–2012 vs. 2013–2017 (p = 0.373) and 2008–2012 vs. 2018–2022 (p = 0.131) comparisons were not statistically significant. Accordingly, the apparent increase in 2018–2022 should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect changing service delivery, evolving procedural capacity, referral patterns, or reimbursement/case-mix changes that cannot be disentangled with administrative aggregates. Notably, this shift coincided with the largest growth in MI hospitalizations observed in our analysis and may reflect changing service delivery, expanding procedural capacity, or escalating system pressure in this region – hypotheses that require patient-level clinical data to confirm.

These system-level pressures are further corroborated by our companion national SUS analysis of emergency MI treated with pPCI (2008–2023). pPCI use increased markedly (+ 190.94%), yet regional inequities persisted (South 26.22 vs. North 1.49 per 100,000 population in 2023). After inflation adjustment, pPCI costs fell (−36.34%/day; −47.12%/admission), while population-adjusted mortality after pPCI rose (+ 121.74%) [22]. Taken together with our MI-burden findings (rising hospitalizations and mortality alongside stable-to-declining real cost per LOS), this pattern suggests that growing treatment demand and cost compression have not translated into better outcomes. This may point to persistent gaps in timeliness, transfer networks, and consistency of high-quality acute MI care, particularly in underserved regions.

A critical contextual factor shaping all these trends is the COVID-19 pandemic, which represents one of the key findings of this study. The pandemic had a devastating effect on the country, positioning Brazil among those hit hardest globally in infections and fatalities. Although Brazil comprises just 3% of the world’s population, it accounted for 10% of global COVID-19 cases and deaths. By 2021, the country had recorded over 600,000 COVID-19-related deaths, ranking second worldwide in total fatalities [27]. The rapid surge in COVID-19 patients overwhelmed the healthcare system, particularly affecting the public sector and the most vulnerable regions, leading to reallocation of medical resources, delays in routine care, and overcrowded hospitals [28]. In our data, the pandemic period was also marked by abrupt changes in standardized costs per LOS, with a clear spike in 2020 at the national level and in multiple regions, consistent with disrupted care pathways and resource scarcity. These shifts occurred alongside sharp increases in MI in-hospital mortality, underscoring the dual effect of increased cardiovascular risk and reduced access to timely emergency care during periods of system saturation [29, 30]. However, the observed rise in in-hospital MI mortality during 2020–2022 should not be interpreted solely as evidence of a generalized decline in the quality of cardiovascular care. During periods of peak system stress, coding priorities and diagnostic attribution may have shifted, such that some patients with concurrent acute MI and severe respiratory syndromes were preferentially coded under COVID-19, acute respiratory distress syndrome, or severe acute respiratory syndrome categories rather than MI. In parallel, restricted bed availability and delayed care-seeking may have produced a severity bias in the hospitalized MI population, whereby only the most critical patients reached hospital admission during the pandemic shock. Under this scenario, higher in-hospital mortality would partly reflect a sicker and more selectively admitted case mix, rather than only worsening in-hospital cardiovascular care performance.

The regional impact of the pandemic was not uniform. The Central-West region experienced a significant increase in MI mortality during the pandemic, suggesting that local infrastructure may not have been equipped to manage COVID-19 and acute cardiovascular emergencies simultaneously. In regions with lower hospital capacity, peak pandemic demand likely reduced the ability to deliver timely, evidence-based MI care, contributing to poorer outcomes. In addition, emerging evidence suggests that COVID-19 infection may directly elevate the risk of cardiovascular complications, including MI, through inflammation and endothelial dysfunction [31, 32]. At the same time, pandemic-era admission thresholds may have become more restrictive in overwhelmed settings, which would further enrich the hospitalized MI population for severe presentations and complicate interpretation of rising facility-based mortality rates. Excess cardiovascular deaths were observed in Brazil, particularly at home, while in-hospital deaths decreased in some settings as fewer people sought medical care due to overcrowding and limited bed availability [33, 34]. These findings reinforce COVID-19’s dual impact: increased cardiovascular risk and reduced access to timely care.

Globally, Brazil had one of the highest excess mortality rates during the pandemic, with large fluctuations from 10 to 83% from 2020 to 2022 [35]. This excess mortality reflects both the direct impact of COVID-19 and indirect effects, including disruptions to essential healthcare services. Cardiovascular diseases, including MI and heart failure, contributed substantially to this additional mortality [36–38]. Given that this cardiovascular excess burden fell disproportionally on already vulnerable population groups and structurally disadvantaged regions, targeted interventions to mitigate the increase in cardiovascular mortality are urgently needed [39, 40].

Compounding these challenges, regional differences in mortality may also reflect underreporting, particularly in under-resourced regions with limited diagnostic capability. Underreporting may have been amplified during the pandemic; for example, respiratory failure secondary to acute pulmonary edema in cardiogenic shock may have been attributed to COVID-19. In addition, limited bed availability may have contributed to a “virtual reduction” in hospitalizations, further complicating interpretation of facility-based indicators. These pandemic-era findings suggest that the observed worsening in hospital-based MI outcomes likely reflected a combination of increased cardiovascular risk, disrupted access, selective admission of more severe cases, and possible diagnostic/coding shifts during periods of peak system stress.

Taken together, these findings point to the urgent need for a regionally tailored approach to public policy development to alleviate the burden that MI places on the Brazilian public healthcare system. The unequal distribution of healthcare resources reflects long-standing structural challenges, including the concentration of hospitals, specialists, and high-acuity capacity in the South and Southeast compared with under-resourced regions such as the North and Northeast [41]. The COVID-19 crisis further exposed these fragilities [42, 43]. Considering the pronounced increases in MI hospitalizations observed in the North and Northeast, equity-oriented planning is increasingly urgent: these regions are experiencing rapidly rising demand while simultaneously facing structural constraints in emergency transport, diagnostic capacity, and access to reperfusion and intensive care. In this context, the combination of rising hospitalizations and mortality with declining inflation- and purchasing-power-adjusted cost per LOS in several regions suggests that demand is increasing in settings where the capacity to deliver resource-intensive, time-sensitive MI care may not be expanding commensurately. Strengthening MI networks, improving pre-hospital triage and inter-facility transfer, expanding access to timely reperfusion when feasible, and improving quality of acute MI care could reduce delays and prevent avoidable complications, yielding both clinical and economic benefits. These policy implications should be interpreted in light of important measurement constraints inherent to national administrative data. Because SIH/SUS case selection in TABNET is based on the morbidity category “Acute myocardial infarction” rather than a user-selectable list of ICD-10 subcodes, we analyzed MI admissions as a single combined outcome and could not reliably stratify results by MI subtypes. This limitation may mask clinically meaningful heterogeneity in severity, treatment pathways, and outcomes. It also raises the possibility of temporal case-mix shifts between STEMI and NSTEMI within the aggregated MI category. Because these subtypes differ in acuity, reperfusion use, expected mortality, and treatment-related costs, changes over time in the relative proportion of STEMI versus NSTEMI admissions could influence the observed aggregate trends in in-hospital mortality and cost per day independently of underlying system performance. For example, if expanded diagnostic access and troponin-based recognition increased the capture of NSTEMI admissions over time, the pooled SIH/SUS category could reflect changing subtype composition rather than only changes in care delivery, reimbursement intensity, or disease burden. In addition, temporal changes in SIH/SUS reimbursement-based cost metrics may reflect multiple mechanisms that cannot be distinguished with the available administrative data, including technological incorporation (e.g., diffusion of higher-cost devices and therapies) and/or increasing clinical complexity and comorbidity burden among patients treated within SUS. Accordingly, trends and regional differences in inflation- and purchasing-power-adjusted cost per LOS should be interpreted as variation in reimbursement/resource intensity per inpatient day rather than direct evidence of changes in efficiency, quality of care, or the total economic burden of MI. In addition, because no official annual regional healthcare PPP series is available from IBGE, our regional adjustment relied on accumulated regional IPCA variation as an approximate proxy for cross-regional price differences and therefore may not fully reflect the cost structure of high-complexity inpatient cardiovascular care.

This study’s geographical scope enables an initial exploration of regional disparities in MI outcomes; however, several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, our analyses rely on secondary administrative hospital data (SIH/SUS) and are therefore dependent on the quality of Hospital Admission Authorizations completed by frontline professionals. Variability in documentation completeness, diagnostic/procedural coding accuracy, and local auditing/billing routines may introduce measurement error and potential differential misclassification across regions and over time. Second, underreporting and coding changes may have occurred over the study period, and may have been accentuated during the COVID-19 pandemic due to system overload, shifts in care-seeking behaviors, and competing diagnostic priorities. More specifically, during COVID-19 peaks, some admissions involving both AMI and severe respiratory illness may have been preferentially coded under respiratory or COVID-19-related categories, while hospital access constraints may have selectively concentrated the admitted MI population toward more severe cases; together, these processes could inflate observed in-hospital mortality among recorded MI admissions independent of any generalized decline in care quality. Thus, part of the observed temporal variation – particularly around 2020–2021 – may reflect changes in ascertainment and recording rather than true changes in MI burden alone. Third, SIH/SUS captures in-hospital events and does not reliably capture out-of-hospital MI deaths. Consequently, our estimates likely understate the total MI burden, especially in remote and underserved areas with limited emergency access and higher likelihood of pre-hospital fatal events. Potential biases in reporting may also affect mortality and hospitalization estimates. Fourth, the absence of patient-level clinical data (e.g., MI subtype, comorbidities, reperfusion procedure, ICU admission, complications) limits causal inference and preclude adjustment for clinical severity and case mix, restricting interpretation of cost trends as “care intensity” versus administrative reimbursement effects. We could not assess whether the subtype composition of admitted MI cases changed over time; a shift toward a greater proportion of NSTEMI admissions could alter pooled mortality and reimbursement patterns, including cost per hospital day, without necessarily indicating improved or worsened system performance. In addition, because SIH/SUS payment values are tied to SIGTAP reimbursement sheets that are not automatically updated for inflation [26], the observed decline in inflation-adjusted cost per LOS may partly capture reimbursement lag rather than changes in underlying clinical resource consumption. Fifth, the supply indices were derived from CNES/TABNET and aggregated to the macro-region level; this coarse geographic resolution (N = 5) limits statistical power and may mask within-region heterogeneity in access to specialized MI services. More specifically, macro-regional aggregation can over-smooth meaningful subregional disparities and thus may obscure localized gaps in access to timely MI care. We considered finer geographic stratification (e.g., state-level analyses), but several outcomes (particularly in-hospital deaths) and selected supply indicators are sparse when disaggregated, which can yield unstable estimates and limited interpretability. Finally, our dataset is limited to patients treated within SUS. While SUS covers most Brazilians, a more comprehensive assessment would compare hospitalization, mortality, and standardized costs between the public and private systems. Such analyses could clarify whether the observed decline in real cost per hospital day reflects changes in reimbursement and coding, shifts in case mix, or true changes in service intensity. Relatedly, because we restrict to SUS-treated hospitalizations, our results should be interpreted as trends in the public system’s treated burden and may not generalize to the full Brazilian population in settings with higher private-sector utilization. Continuous monitoring post-pandemic remains essential, particularly given Brazil’s aging population and the expectation of increasing cardiovascular burden in coming years [40]. This necessitates robust prevention strategies, including education and risk-factor control, improved early screening, and equitable access to cardiovascular care [44].

Conclusions

In conclusion, emergency MI hospitalizations and in-hospital mortality increased substantially in Brazil from 2008 to 2023, with pronounced disruption during the COVID-19 pandemic and persistent regional disparities. Meanwhile, age- and sex-standardized cost per LOS – adjusted for inflation and regional purchasing power – declined for Brazil overall from 2008–2012 to 2018–2022, remained consistently highest in the South, and showed a non-monotonic pattern in the Central-West. Formal supply-side analyses further revealed that inpatient-capacity expansion was the domain most consistently associated with lower per-day reimbursement intensity and lower hospitalization rates within regions over time, while supply indices showed limited association with in-hospital mortality at the macro-region level. These findings suggest that MI burden rose more rapidly than per-day inpatient reimbursement/resource intensity recorded in SIH/SUS, highlighting the need to strengthen acute MI care networks and reduce regional inequities.
