Disseminated Histoplasmosis: The Great Mimicker in Patients With Advanced HIV Disease
Authors: Leslie Atenas Arteaga Bruno, Josue Luis Medina Estrada, Maritza de los Angeles Astudillo Melgar, Moises De la Cruz Cuevas, Marina Romero Navarrete
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Histoplasmosis is a systemic mycosis. In patients infected with HIV, the disseminated form represents one of the most severe presentations of the disease. While the infection primarily manifests in the pulmonary system, it can also present cutaneously or intestinally, posing a significant diagnostic challenge. We report the case of a 28-year-old male patient with a diagnosis of HIV and disseminated histoplasmosis (involving cutaneous, pulmonary, and intestinal systems). He presented with disseminated dermatosis, hepatosplenomegaly, pancytopenia, dysphagia, and subsequent gastrointestinal bleeding; skin and intestinal biopsies confirmed the diagnosis. The patient was treated with amphotericin B (sodium deoxycholate), resulting in a favorable clinical outcome. He was discharged with a subsequent regimen of oral antifungal therapy and antiretrovirals. Disseminated histoplasmosis remains a frequent opportunistic infection in patients living with HIV. Cutaneous manifestations are often nonspecific. In patients with severe immunosuppression, histoplasmosis should always be considered among the differential diagnoses.

Introduction

Histoplasmosis is a leading opportunistic infection and a major cause of mortality in individuals with advanced HIV disease [1]. It is considered an emerging fungal disease worldwide, with the majority of cases reported in the Americas [2].

Histoplasma is a dimorphic fungus that exists in either mycelial or yeast form. Disseminated histoplasmosis is the predominant clinical presentation in people with advanced HIV infection and may involve multiple organ systems beyond the lungs. Cutaneous involvement is typically associated with disseminated disease and is characterised by a broad spectrum of lesions, including papules, nodules, ulcers, and molluscum contagiosum-like lesions. This marked clinical polymorphism requires differentiation from other opportunistic infections commonly encountered in people living with HIV, particularly tuberculosis, cryptococcosis, disseminated herpes simplex infection, and molluscum contagiosum [3]. Histoplasmosis occurs upon the inhalation of Histoplasma spores naturally present in soil contaminated by bird and bat droppings, or as a result of occupational or recreational activities [4].

It is more prevalent in men than in women, with a ratio of 6:1. Initial clinical manifestations can be nonspecific; however, the most severe forms of the disease occur in children, the elderly, and patients with some form of immunodeficiency, particularly individuals infected with HIV [5]. Histoplasma capsulatum includes two human-pathogenic varieties: var. capsulatum (globally distributed) and var. duboisii (restricted to Africa) [6]. Histoplasmosis is a frequent opportunistic fungal infection in people living with HIV, associated with 5% to 15% of annual deaths within this population [7].

Case presentation

We present the case of a 28-year-old male patient from a tropical region of Mexico. He had a history of pulmonary tuberculosis, previously treated with a complete regimen consisting of rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol. He had a recent diagnosis of HIV infection and was receiving an initial antiretroviral regimen with Triumeq (dolutegravir, abacavir, and lamivudine), with a viral load of 78,303 copies/mL and a CD4 count of 37 cells/µL.

The patient was referred to Dermatology with a suspected diagnosis of drug eruption. Upon physical examination, he presented with a disseminated dermatosis affecting the face, anterior chest, and upper extremities. Facial examination revealed papulonodular, umbilicated neoformations with an erythematous base, accompanied by edema, nasal swelling, and seropurulent discharge from the right nostril (Figure 1a).

Examination of the oral cavity showed cotton-like plaques on the dorsum of the tongue and petechiae on the soft palate.

Abdominal examination revealed hepatosplenomegaly. Subsequently, the patient developed mixed dysphagia and a fever of 38.3°C, which prompted hospital admission. Laboratory workup upon admission revealed pancytopenia, with leukopenia (white blood cell count 2.70 ×10³/µL; reference range: 4-10 ×10³/µL), normocytic normochromic anemia (hemoglobin 8.5 g/dL; reference range: 11-16 g/dL), and thrombocytopenia (platelet count 106 ×10³/µL; reference range: 130-400 ×10³/µL). A chest X-ray (Figure 2) documented a diffuse micronodular interstitial pattern.

An incisional punch biopsy of a facial lesion on the left chin was performed and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (Figure 3), which reported superficial granulomatous dermatitis with small yeast cells consistent with histoplasmosis.

Histology of a skin biopsy from the chin showing a dense inflammatory infiltrate composed predominantly of lymphocytes and histiocytes. Black arrows indicate inflammatory infiltrates and small vacuoles within histiocytes containing yeast forms, consistent with Histoplasma spp. (H&E stain, 10× magnification).

Treatment was initiated with amphotericin B deoxycholate, as liposomal amphotericin B was not available in our setting. Amphotericin B deoxycholate was administered at an initial dose of 0.5 mg/kg/day, with renal function and electrolyte monitoring every 72 hours. During hospitalization, the dose was adjusted according to the patient’s clinical evolution, reaching 1 mg/kg/day. The treatment resulted in marked improvement of the cutaneous lesions, with no infusion-related reactions or significant drug-related adverse effects documented. One week later, the patient developed gastrointestinal bleeding and an acute abdomen, necessitating an exploratory laparotomy. Intraoperative findings revealed a jejunal perforation, and segmental resection of the affected small bowel was performed. Histopathological examination demonstrated acute-on-chronic enteritis with transmural perforation associated with Histoplasma capsulatum, confirming gastrointestinal histoplasmosis (Figures 4, 5).

During hospitalization, the patient showed progressive clinical improvement, with resolution of fever, adequate oral intake tolerance, gastrointestinal symptom control, and marked improvement of the cutaneous lesions, evidenced by decreased erythema and regression of the papulonodular lesions two weeks after treatment initiation (Figure 1b). Most lesions progressed to a crusting/healing phase. He continued outpatient follow-up after discharge, maintaining clinical stability without evidence of recurrence during the six-month follow-up period.

Discussion

Disseminated histoplasmosis is considered the most frequent clinical presentation of this disease in patients living with HIV. The primary form causes pulmonary infection but can spread to other organs, especially those of the reticuloendothelial system. The clinical presentation remains a challenge due to the lack of specificity of symptoms that overlap with other infectious diseases and the limited access to specific diagnostic assays and treatments. The differential diagnosis should include tuberculosis and cryptococcosis, among others [5,7]. Initially, the patient was referred with a suspected diagnosis of a drug eruption; however, the diagnosis was concluded based on skin histopathology and the morphology of the dermatological lesions.

A CD4 T-lymphocyte (CD4) cell count <150 cells/µL is associated with a higher risk of symptomatic disease in people with HIV [8]. In this patient's case, the cell count was 37 cells/µL, leaving him exposed to multiple coinfections and complications.

Mucocutaneous manifestations are usually the result of disseminated disease, occurring in 10% to 25% of cases. The morphological patterns of disseminated dermatosis present as polymorphic papules, plaques with or without crusts, pustules, nodules, mucosal ulcers, erosions, punched-out ulcers, molluscum contagiosum-like lesions, acneiform eruptions, erythematous papules and keratotic plaques, purpuric lesions, and localized and generalized vegetating forms [9].

Common symptoms of pulmonary histoplasmosis include cough, dyspnea, chest pain, fever, and chills. Non-pulmonary manifestations such as pericarditis, arthritis, and dermatological involvement arise concomitantly with the pulmonary syndrome in a small number of patients [3].

Gastrointestinal involvement is common, occurring in approximately 70-90% of cases with disseminated fungal infection, but only 3% to 12% of these patients are symptomatic. Atypical but more concerning signs include submucosal nodules, hematochezia, melena, splenic infarction, obstructive masses, and intestinal perforation [10]. The patient presented with one of the unusual signs, intestinal perforation, emphasizing the complexity of management in these patients and the importance of multidisciplinary support from other specialties. Chest radiography frequently shows patchy bilateral opacities, sometimes with hilar or mediastinal lymphadenopathy [3].

The isolation of Histoplasma capsulatum in culture or the identification of yeast forms in histopathology are the gold standards for diagnosis. Patients with disseminated histoplasmosis have a higher rate of positive cultures (74%) than patients with acute pulmonary histoplasmosis (42%). Mycelial phase growth occurs within two to four weeks but can be delayed up to eight weeks, limiting timely diagnosis and treatment. Antigen detection in urine has generally proven to be slightly more sensitive than in serum across all manifestations of histoplasmosis but it is not available in all centers [11].

Histopathology is another valuable diagnostic tool; necrotizing or non-necrotizing granulomas are often observed. The characteristic pattern of yeast forms can be identified, predominantly within macrophages, but also extracellularly. Yeast forms are best visualized using a Gomori-Grocott methenamine silver (GMS) stain. Alternatively, a periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) stain can be used. Differentiating histoplasmosis from other pathogens can be difficult as it is limited by sample quality and operator training [3,12].

The treatment of severe disseminated disease comprises an induction phase of at least two weeks, or until clinical improvement is achieved. Liposomal amphotericin B (3 mg/kg/day intravenously) is the preferred induction therapy, with amphotericin B lipid complex (5 mg/kg/day intravenously) as an alternative. This is followed by maintenance therapy with oral itraconazole for at least 12 months, administered at 200 mg three times daily for the first three days and then 200 mg twice daily. For patients who are unable to tolerate itraconazole, alternative azoles, including posaconazole, voriconazole, and fluconazole, may be considered [8]. Our patient achieved excellent control during the six-month follow-up.

Conclusions

Disseminated histoplasmosis remains a frequent opportunistic infection in patients living with HIV and must always be considered among the differential diagnoses in cases of severe immunosuppression. Although cutaneous manifestations may be nonspecific, they provide a clinically accessible diagnostic clue. Furthermore, when antigen tests or cultures are unavailable, histopathology is fundamental to establish the diagnosis. The initial management of these conditions poses a true clinical challenge; therefore, establishing an accurate diagnosis and rapidly initiating treatment is crucial. Improving timely HIV diagnosis and promoting treatment adherence remain important for prevention.
