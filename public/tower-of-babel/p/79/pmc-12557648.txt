The impact of the toll-like receptor 1 (TLR1) variation rs5743557 on tuberculosis susceptibility in the Thai population
Authors: Chanyapak Saengchart, Nuttida Nimitsurachat, Weeraya Phaisal, Kamon Kawkitinarong, Pajaree Chariyavilaskul, Monpat Chamnanphon, Sasiwimol Ubolyam, Yamaratee Jaisin, Papavee Samatiwat, Punyabhorn Rattanacheeworn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major public health concern in Thailand and worldwide. Genetic factors, including single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), play a part in influencing susceptibility to TB. The aim of this study was to investigate the allele and genotype frequencies of the rs5743557 (G>A) polymorphism and its potential impact on TB susceptibility in the Thai population.MethodsA total of 149 leftover peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) samples were collected and divided into two groups: patients with TB (TB group, n=92) and patients without TB (non-TB group, n=57). TaqMan real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed for genotyping. Logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the association between rs5743557 (G>A) and susceptibility to TB.ResultsThe analysis revealed that the A allele of the rs5743557 variant was relatively prevalent in the study population. Although a trend indicated that individuals carrying the A allele may have a reduced probability of developing TB, the association did not reach statistical significance.ConclusionsOur findings reveal a trend of reduced TB susceptibility in rs5743557 allele A, although it did not reach statistical significance. There is clinical relevance, marking a promising starting point for further research within the Thai population. In addition, this perspective opens avenues for future research efforts in the field.Clinical Trial Registrationhttps://www.thaiclinicaltrials.org/ (TCTR20220506002, registered on May 6, 2022).

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study identified a potential association between the minor A allele of the toll-like receptor 1 (TLR1) gene variant rs5743557 and decreased susceptibility to tuberculosis (TB) in a Thai population.

• Although the association did not reach statistical significance, individuals carrying the GA or AA genotypes exhibited a lower risk of TB infection compared to those with the GG genotype.

What is known and what is new?

• TLR1 is a critical component of the innate immune response to Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Previous studies in Asian populations have suggested a link between rs5743557 and TB susceptibility.

• The present study contributes new data, indicating that the A allele is relatively common in the Thai population and may act as a protective factor against TB infection.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Despite the lack of statistical significance, the observed trend supports the potential role of rs5743557 as a genetic marker for TB risk assessment.

• These findings underscore the importance of further investigations with larger sample sizes and expanded genetic analyses to better elucidate the role of host genetic factors in TB susceptibility and to inform future preventive strategies.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is one of the most challenging problems in the global healthcare system (1). According to the World Health Organization (WHO) Global Tuberculosis Report 2022, based on data collected from 202 countries and territories, in 2021, about 10.6 million people developed the disease, and 1.6 million people also died of TB in the same year (2). Additionally, the incidence rate of TB was 3.6% higher in 2021 compared to 2020 (2). Countries with a high burden of TB are usually located in the regions of Africa or South East Asia (1). According to the national surveillance system, there were 143,997 reported cases of TB in Thailand between 2011 and 2020 (3). TB is an airborne infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) (4,5). Individuals who are infected with MTB are at risk of developing an active form of the disease (6). Risk factors for developing active TB include (I) being a person who has recently had an MTB infection, and (II) being an immunocompromised person, who is someone at a higher risk of developing TB compared to the general population (7-9), especially a person with a condition such as Human immunodeficiency viruses (HIV) infection, diabetes mellitus, organ transplant, etc. (10). Among those infected, certain patients may exhibit a positive test for TB infection without any evidence of disease, referred to as latent TB infection (LTBI) (11-13). Furthermore, it is important to note that genetic variations, in particular, immune genes, including toll-like receptors (TLRs), interferon-gamma (INF-ɣ), interleukin-17 (IL-17), killer immunoglobulin-like receptors (KIRs), and tumor necrosis factor (TNF), have been reported as one of the risk factors for developing TB disease (14).

TLR1 is a component of the pattern recognition receptor (PRR) family which has a crucial function in the initial protection against TB infection in that it triggers the innate immune response and subsequent stimulation of the adaptive immune system through recognition of the triacylated lipoproteins of MTB (15-17). TLR1 along with TLR2 form a heterodimer known as the TLR1/TLR2 complex. The TLR1/TLR2 complex triggers signaling pathways that lead to the activation of innate immune responses (16,18-20). These include the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and type I interferon (21). The rs5743557 polymorphism is located 595 bp upstream of the TLR1 gene (22). Previous studies suggested that the AA genotype of rs5743557 was associated with a reduced risk of TB in adult Tibetan and western Chinese populations, but not in the Han Chinese population (22-24).

However, a knowledge gap persists regarding some inconsistency in the association of TLR1 rs5743557 with susceptibility to TB and the impact of this variant in the Thai population, which warrants investigation of its potential implications. This pioneering study aims to clarify the relationship between the TLR1 rs5743557 variant and TB susceptibility in the Thai population, shedding light on the allele and genotype frequency of this variant. These findings hold great promise in unraveling preventive measures for TB infection and potentially reducing the mortality rate, not only among the Thai population but also globally. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-147/rc).

Methods

Participants

A total of 241 leftover peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) samples from Thai TB patients were initially assessed for eligibility in this study. Of these, 92 samples were excluded as they did not meet the inclusion or exclusion criteria. The remaining 149 PBMC samples were included (Figure 1). The participants in this study were divided into two groups: patients diagnosed with TB infection for the target group and non-TB patients for the control group. All participants included in the study were adults aged 18 years and older. To ensure a homogeneous study population, individuals with coexisting infectious diseases, such as HIV, and those with a history of recurrent TB infection were excluded. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and the Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Before the start of the study, written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This cross-sectional study has obtained approval from both the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University, and the Faculty of Medicine, Srinakharinwirot University. The study was prospectively registered with the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR) on May 6, 2022, under the registration number TCTR20220506002 (https://www.thaiclinicaltrials.org/). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study protocol was approved and reviewed by the committee in strict adherence to the ethical guidelines established by the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Medicine, Srinakharinwirot University, Bangkok, Thailand (SWUEC-M-046/2565E) and Chulalongkorn University, Bangkok, Thailand (IRB No.086/54). Before their enrollment, all participants underwent a detailed process, through which written informed consent was obtained, guaranteeing their voluntary participation and informed decision-making. In addition, strict measures were implemented to maintain the confidentiality and anonymity of participants’ personal information throughout the entire research process.

Sample collection

A total of 149 leftover PBMC samples from Thai TB patients were collected from the HIV Netherlands Australia Thailand Research Collaboration (HIV-NAT), affiliated with the Faculty of Medicine at Chulalongkorn University. Specifically, the samples were sourced from participants enrolled in the research project titled “Optimizing Treatment Outcomes for Tuberculosis Infection in Tertiary Care Hospitals (Chula TB NRU)”, IRB No.086/54.

Data collection

Clinical data and relevant information were collected from the participants through a case record form. Detailed demographic data, including age, sex, and other relevant factors, as well as the diagnosis of TB and genotyping data for the rs5743557 G>A variant of the TLR1 gene, were accurately documented.

Genotyping analysis

All PBMC samples belonging to the Chula TB NRU project were previously stored in vapor-phase liquid nitrogen. After sample collection, each sample underwent extraction for genomic deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) using the QIAGEN DNA blood mini kit and was stored at −80 ℃ until use. TLR1 (rs5743557 G>A, C_32158727_10) genotyping was carried out by QuantStudio 3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test for the normality of continuous data. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic characteristics presented as median [interquartile range (IQR)] and frequency (%) for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. A Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare continuous variables between groups. For categorical variables, the chi-square test was applied. The allele and genotype frequencies of TLR1 rs5743557 were determined using descriptive statistics. To evaluate the associations between the TLR1 variant and susceptibility to TB, a logistic regression analysis was performed, and a multivariate model was used to adjust for potential confounders, including age, gender, weight, and underlying disease, calculated as an adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with a P value <0.05 considered to be significant.

Results

General characteristics

The general characteristics of the patients in our study were distributed, except for the significant differences observed in age and weight (Table 1). The median age was significantly different between the TB group and the non-TB group (P<0.001). The underlying diseases of the two groups consisted of diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia (DLP), hypertension (HTN), and cancer (CA), respectively.

Allele and genotype frequency

The frequency of rs5743557 minor allele A was 27.2%. Regarding genotype distribution, the GA genotype was present in 36.9%, the AA genotype was present in 8.7%, and the overall frequency of the GA + AA genotypes was 45.6% (Table 2).

Association of rs5743557 with risk of TB

In the logistic regression analysis, no statistically significant association was observed between rs5743557 and TB susceptibility (P=0.28); however, there was a notable trend indicating a possible reduction in the risk of TB within the A allele group [odd ratio (OR) = 0.75, 95% confidence interval (95% CI): 0.45, 1.26]. A trend of decreasing TB risk was shown in the dominant model in the AA genotype (OR =0.8, 95% CI: 0.24, 2.68), and the dominant model in the combination of GA + AA genotypes (OR =0.63, 95% CI: 0.33, 1.23) (Table 3).

Discussion

We are the first to report the allele frequency and association of rs5743557 G>A with TB infection in the Thai population. Our findings reveal that the frequency of the A allele variant is high in the Thai population, aligning closely with those of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Allele Frequency Aggregator (ALFA) project, which reported an A allele frequency of 34% in the East Asian population (25).

The distribution of allele and genotype frequencies among populations in Southeast Asia exhibits consistency between five distinct nationalities: Thai, Han Chinese, Tibetan Chinese, western Chinese, and Sudanese (Figure 2). In Figure 2A,2C, our study found frequencies of 27.2% for the A allele and 45.6% for the GA + AA genotype, which are consistent with the trends observed in the Tibetan population (32.2% for the A allele and 53.2% for the GA + AA genotype) (24). This consistency supports the credibility of our findings and suggests a shared genetic pattern within these Asian populations. In contrast, both the western Chinese population (43.7% for the A allele and 68.9% for the GA + AA genotype) and the Sudanese population (43.6% for the A allele and 62.1% for GA + AA genotype) exhibited notably higher A allele frequencies, as shown in Figure 2A (22,27), indicating a different trend. These differences highlight the potential influence of ethnic background on genetic variation.

Allelic frequency of rs5743557 in Thai and other populations. (A-C) The data are based on Thai (current study), Han Chinese (23,26), Tibetan Chinese (24), western Chinese (22), and Sudanese (27) populations.

Our results demonstrated a specific focus on the association between rs5743557 and TB susceptibility. Importantly, the association remained consistent after adjusting for potential confounders, including sex, in the multivariate analysis, indicating that the observed genetic association is independent of sex-related effects. The frequency of the A allele notably showed more importance in reducing the risk of TB than the presence of the G allele. Individuals carrying the A allele expressed a lower susceptibility to TB, highlighting its potential as a protective genetic variant. Focusing on genotype frequencies, the presence of the A allele was also associated with a reduction in the proportion of the population in the TB group, indicating a 28% decrease in the risk of TB disease associated with GA + AA genotypes. This finding emphasized the impact of the A allele on decreasing the prevalence of TB within the studied cohort. However, our findings did not reach statistical significance and were still inconclusive regarding whether rs5743557 was associated with the risk of TB disease in the Thai population.

As summarized in Table 5, the review of other pertinent studies demonstrates that the results of our investigation generally align with similar trends observed in various populations. Even among the Chinese population, there are still genetic variations between different ethnicities in China. Genetic variations in Han Chinese populations do not tend to affect susceptibility to TB, as corroborated by findings from two previously published studies (23,26), while the minor allele A of rs5743557 in Tibetan Chinese populations has been found to reduce the risk of TB by 31% (OR =0.69, 95% CI: 0.58, 0.81; P<0.001) (24). Another study has shown that the rs5743557 minor allele A is associated with a 27% reduced risk of pulmonary TB through the dominant model in western Chinese populations (OR =0.73, 95% CI: 0.54, 0.98; P=0.04) (22). However, a meta-analysis carried out using the aggregated data from previous studies, encompassing both Han Chinese children and adults as well as Tibetan individuals (26), indicated that the rs5743557 minor allele A was associated with a decreased susceptibility to TB in the adult Chinese population (23). Another recently conducted meta-analysis also showed that rs5743557 (G>A) significantly reduced the risk of TB in the Chinese population (OR =0.81, 95% CI: 0.71, 0.92; P=0.001) (28). A recent study found that the rs5743557 (G>A) polymorphism was more frequent in the TB group compared to the healthy control group among Sudanese patients (27). Therefore, our study, which is the first study of TB susceptibility in the Thai population, exhibits clinical potential as it has demonstrated a nearly 30% reduction in TB risk although the findings have not reached statistical significance.

Therefore, we proceeded to analyze the limitations identified in this study and found that the PBMC samples had been selected from a previous study project with a slightly limited number of participants. Another constraint was the imbalance in sample sizes between groups. The number of patients in the TB group was almost twice that of the non-TB group, which could have affected statistical power because a larger sample size would generally provide more accurate results. However, a substantial imbalance between the groups’ sample sizes may have led to difficulty drawing conclusions in the smaller group, which was the non-TB group, as the focus was mainly on the TB group’s impact rather than an overall analysis. The third barrier was that this study was a retrospective study, which imposed restrictions on some of the inclusion and exclusion criteria for the sample study. The fourth condition was that only one single nucleotide variation (SNV) was studied. If more SNVs were studied, the study could also show the result of haplotypes in the Thai population.

To address these few limitations in our study, we recommend that a prospective study design be employed in further research, which could clearly establish inclusion/exclusion criteria to ensure independence among the participant groups in addition to matching with healthy volunteers. Furthermore, including LTBI individuals could provide valuable information in our field. Implementing these suggested research strategies is expected to provide interesting and more conclusive results in the future.

Conclusions

This current study is the first to reveal an observable trend that indicates a possible association of the A allele and the AA genotype of rs5743557 in the TLR1 gene with a lower risk of TB infection among the Thai population. Although statistical significance was not achieved, the observed clinical relevance provides a promising basis for future research. Specifically, these findings could inform the design of a prospective studies by identifying key variables, patient subgroups or populations that warrant further investigation. In practice, the insights gained could guide preliminary clinical decisions or hypothesis generation in similar clinical scenarios, supporting more targeted and efficient study designs moving forward.
