Augmented reality-assisted cholangioscopy for biliary interventions: initial clinical experience in four cases
Authors: Saad Abu Zahra, Ahsun Riaz, Ashima Kundu, Robert J. Lewandowski, Andrew C. Gordon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo evaluate the feasibility, safety, and workflow integration of augmented reality (AR)-assisted visualization using the Apple Vision Pro headset during live biliary interventions incorporating cholangioscopy.Materials and methodsIn this single-center, IRB-approved pilot study, four biliary interventions were performed by a single attending interventional radiologist wearing an AR headset. Cholangioscopic video output from the SpyGlass™ DS system was transmitted via HDMI/SDI encoders over a local area network and rendered as virtual displays within the headset. The AR interface functioned as the primary visualization modality for guidance during each procedure.ResultsFour complex biliary cases were performed—all procedures were technically successful (100%) without complications (0%). AR-assisted visualization enabled continuous intraprocedural integration of cholangioscopy without technical failure, latency-related interruption, or breach of sterility. Qualitative operator feedback suggested improved procedural continuity, enhanced visualization during balloon dilation and stent deployment, and reduced need for repetitive head movement between monitors. The system was successfully incorporated into the standard sterile procedural workflows.ConclusionAR-assisted cholangioscopy during live biliary interventions is technically feasible and safe in this initial clinical experience. Spatial computing platforms may provide workflow consolidation and ergonomic advantages during complex procedures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00464-026-13023-w.

Biliary interventions represent an important aspect of interventional radiology practice, covering a wide range of therapeutic and diagnostic procedures for biliary obstruction, strictures, and stone disease [1]. Cholangioscopy has emerged as an invaluable adjunct to these procedures, providing direct visualization of the biliary tree and enabling targeted therapy under direct vision [2]. However, the technical success of these procedures relies on simultaneously monitoring multiple video sources, including fluoroscopy, cholangioscopy, and patient monitoring systems, which are typically displayed on separate monitors positioned throughout the interventional suite. This fragmented visual workflow requires operators to divide their attention between the procedural field and multiple external monitors, often necessitating suboptimal head and neck positioning [3]. Such ergonomic constraints can increase cognitive load, compromise procedural efficiency, and contribute to work-related musculoskeletal disorders that affect a significant proportion of interventional radiologists [3].

Augmented reality (AR) and virtual reality (VR) technologies represent an emerging paradigm in medical visualization, with the potential to address many of these limitations. By superimposing digital information onto the operator’s field of view, AR headsets can consolidate multiple video sources into a unified visual experience while eliminating the need to divert attention to distant monitors [4]. Recent advances in spatial computing have enabled the development of consumer-grade AR/VR devices with sufficient resolution, minimal latency, and improved ergonomics to potentially support clinical applications. While AR/VR devices have demonstrated utility in surgical specialties, their application in interventional radiology remains largely unexplored [4]. To our knowledge, there have been no published reports of AR/VR headset use during endoscopic interventional radiology procedures.

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the feasibility of using the Apple Vision Pro (AVP) (Apple, Cupertino, CA) headset to display the cholangioscopic video source as a customizable virtual monitor during biliary interventions. Objectives were to 1) establish a technical workflow for transmitting real-time cholangioscopy to the headset as virtual displays viewable during active procedures, 2) assess operator-perceived impact on procedural workflow and ergonomics, and 3) evaluate the safety of this approach in a preliminary case series.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This was an IRB-approved, HIPAA compliant pilot study conducted at a single academic interventional radiology practice from January 2026 to February 2026. Informed consent was obtained from patients undergoing biliary interventions. Four consecutive biliary interventions were performed by a single attending interventional radiologist while wearing the AVP (Apple, Cupertino, CA). The headset was used as the primary visualization method throughout each procedure to display cholangioscopic images.

The configuration used to transmit video sources from the interventional suite to the AVP headset (Apple, Cupertino, CA) is shown in Fig. 1. Video outputs from the cholangioscopy system (SpyGlass™ DS Direct Visualization System; Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA) were diverted to commercially available video encoders via HDMI/SDI connections. The encoders transmitted video streams via ethernet to a high-bandwidth wireless router capable of propagating 4 K video via local area network (LAN). Setup of the mobile cart-based infrastructure (wireless router, video encoder) occurred before the patient arrived into the procedure room during room turnover. Since these tasks can occur in parallel with standard room preparation activities (room turnover, patient positioning, timeout, sterile prep) they did not noticeably delay or increase procedural start times in any case.Fig. 1System configuration for Apple Vision Pro integration in interventional radiology suite. After connecting the AVP headset (Apple, Cupertino, CA) to the LAN, video sources were displayed using open-source application software capable of detecting the encoded video streams and rendering them as individual virtual displays within the headset’s visual field. The virtual display served as the primary procedural monitor and was fully customizable within the operator’s spatial environment. Display size, distance, and three-dimensional position within the angiography suite were adjustable in real time, allowing optimization of ergonomics and line-of-sight alignment with the sterile field. This spatial configurability enabled the operator to maintain forward gaze toward the procedural field while simultaneously visualizing high-resolution intraprocedural imaging streams. Standard conventional interventional suite monitors remained present to enable technologists, nurses, and other team members to view the procedures.

Procedures

Prior to each procedure, the operator donned the AR headset and performed eye tracking and hand calibration using the headset’s internal calibration functionality. The attending interventional radiologist had undergone prior training and familiarization with the headset, including the required hand gestures, gaze control, display manipulation, and spatial positioning functions before clinical implementation (Fig. 2). Preprocedural cross-sectional imaging was available for review on VisageEase VP within the headset environment prior to each procedure. While simultaneous display of cross-sectional imaging alongside real-time cholangioscopy was technically feasible, this functionality was not required during active procedures in this pilot series. The operator then performed standard sterile preparation and draping while wearing the headset. The headset’s video see-through capability provided an unobstructed view of the external procedural field, allowing the operator to visualize the patient, access site, instruments, and the surrounding interventional suite environment. This capability enabled the operator to perform all aspects of the procedure, including patient positioning, access, wire and catheter manipulation, and cholangioscope insertion and navigation, while continuously viewing virtual displays overlaid on the physical environment. The video stream was transmitted in real time for display only and not cached or written to persistent memory. No video data were recorded or stored on the headset, encoders, or network devices unless specifically recorded by the operator. The AVP is institution-owned and password-protected with recorded videos accessible to team members on our study IRB. Statistical analyses

Given the small sample size (n = 4 cases) and absence of comparator group, descriptive statistics are provided. Continuous variables are reported as median ± interquartile range (IQR). All analyses were conducted using Stata version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Results

Case characteristics

Across the four cases, technical success was achieved in 100% (4/4) of procedures. The median patient age was 67 years (IQR, 55.0–68.0 years). All interventions were completed as planned, with no intraprocedural or periprocedural complications observed. The median total procedural time was 105.0 min (IQR, 87.5–121.0 min). The median fluoroscopy time was 9.40 min (IQR, 6.10–13.35 min), with a median radiation dose of 54.5 mGy (IQR, 19.5–95.0). The median contrast volume administered was 40.0 mL (IQR, 19–60 mL). Case presentations

Case–1: Post-liver transplant multifocal biliary strictures

A 69-year-old man with hepatitis C cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma status post-orthotopic liver transplantation developed complex post-transplant biliary complications, including multifocal strictures involving the right hepatic duct and common bile duct. Prior endoscopic management was unable to access the right intrahepatic system after multiple attempts, necessitating placement of a right-sided internal–external biliary drain. Percutaneous transhepatic cholangiography confirmed multifocal stricturing with delayed antegrade flow. AR-guided cholangioscopy demonstrated no focal mass (Video 1). Balloon cholangioplasty was performed at multiple stations, followed by placement of two 8.5-French × 12 cm plastic Cotton–Leung stents (Cook Medical) spanning the right hepatic duct and common bile duct. Completion cholangiography demonstrated brisk flow into the bowel, and the external drain was removed after confirming tract maturity. A concurrent core liver biopsy was obtained. The AVP headset (Apple, Cupertino, CA) was used throughout to integrate cholangioscopic video within the operator’s visual field during sequential balloon dilation and stent deployment, allowing continuous procedural visualization without shifting attention between monitors. The intervention was technically successful without complications, and the patient was discharged with planned follow-up for stent exchanges with interventional GI and pending core liver biopsy results.

Case 2–Ampullary adenocarcinoma with malignant biliary obstruction

A 67-year-old man with multiple cardiovascular comorbidities presented with severe anemia and obstructive jaundice and was diagnosed with invasive intestinal-type ampullary adenocarcinoma. ERCP was unable to achieve papillary cannulation due to a large obstructing ampullary mass, and an external biliary drain was placed for decompression. PET/CT imaging demonstrated activity at the ampullary mass and biliary confluence, raising concern for possible proximal involvement. Given symptoms from the external drainage catheter and ongoing palliative chemotherapy, the goal was to transition from external biliary drainage to internal stenting to improve quality of life. AR-guided cholangioscopy was performed to directly evaluate the extent of malignant biliary involvement to select covered metal stent placement and to assess proximal ductal patency. Cholangiography demonstrated distal common bile duct obstruction with patent proximal ducts. Direct cholangioscopy confirmed a malignant distal CBD stricture without clear proximal extension. A 10 mm × 40 mm covered metal stent (Viabil; W. L. Gore & Associates, Flagstaff, AZ) was deployed below the cystic duct, spanning the malignant segment and restoring brisk flow. The external drain was removed. Post-stenting, the patient experienced improved symptoms and normalization of liver function tests. He continued palliative FOLFOX chemotherapy with planned reassessment imaging and multidisciplinary review.

Case 3–Metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma with biliary stricture

A 67-year-old woman with metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma underwent a Whipple procedure. Her oncologic course was marked by widespread progression requiring multiple chemotherapy regimens. She developed pulmonary metastases requiring surgical resection in December 2024. Postoperatively, she developed a high-grade biliary stricture at the choledochojejunostomy anastomosis, necessitating placement of internal–external biliary drain. Subsequent MRI demonstrated a persistent 3-cm biliary stricture extending to the choledochojejunostomy with associated soft tissue thickening. Given concern for malignant progression at the anastomotic site, AR-guided cholangioscopy with biopsy was planned to directly assess the stricture and obtain tissue for diagnosis. Conventional cholangiography confirmed a long-segment distal common bile duct stricture extending to the anastomosis (Fig. 3A). AR-guided cholangioscopy revealed a friable intraductal lesion, and multiple targeted biopsies were obtained under direct visualization with SpyBite forceps (Boston Scientific) (Fig. 3B–C). A 10 mm × 40 mm covered metal stent (Viabil; W. L. Gore & Associates, Flagstaff, AZ) was then deployed across the stricture, restoring luminal patency, and the drain was downsized to an 8-French capped safety drain (Fig. 3D). After stent deployment, endoscopic visualization of stent expansion and lesion coverage, combined with fluoroscopic confirmation of position, enhanced operator confidence in stent placement (Fig. 3E). The system eliminated interruptions caused by shifting attention between separate monitors. The procedure was technically successful, with adequate tissue sampling and successful covered metal stent placement across the stricture. Histopathologic evaluation of the biopsy specimen demonstrated a benign/reactive process with no evidence of malignancy. The patient was discharged with a capped 8-French safety drain that was subsequently removed on follow-up. Case 4–Post-liver transplant ischemic cholangiopathy

A 43-year-old woman with hepatic adenomatosis underwent orthotopic liver transplantation (OLT), complicated by graft cirrhosis requiring repeat OLT. Her postoperative course was notable for intraoperative hemorrhage, stress cardiomyopathy (recovered), duodenal perforation, and prolonged hospitalization. Subsequently, she developed ischemic cholangiopathy characterized by recurrent biliary strictures and episodes of biliary sepsis requiring placement of a percutaneous transhepatic internal–external biliary drain. She presented for drain exchange in the setting of persistent biliary ductal irregularity and progressive intraductal debris accumulation. Cholangiography demonstrated extrahepatic ductal irregularity with focal narrowing at the right duct–central duct transition. AR-guided cholangioscopy revealed significant biliary debris and small stones within the common bile duct, with mucosal changes consistent with ischemic injury (Fig. 4). An endoscopic stone retrieval basket (Wittich Stone Retrieval Basket; Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN, USA) was used to extract debris and stones, followed by multiple antegrade balloon sweeps using a Fogarty balloon catheter. A focal stricture in the proximal common duct was treated with 10-mm balloon cholangioplasty. Completion cholangiography demonstrated improved luminal caliber and brisk antegrade flow into the small bowel. A new 14-French internal–external drain was placed. During the procedure, an AVP (Apple, Cupertino, CA) headset was used to display cholangioscopic images, allowing integrated visualization of endoscopic findings and device positioning on fluoroscopy with successful stone extraction (Video 2). The procedure was technically successful without complications. Discussion

This pilot clinical experience demonstrates the initial feasibility and safety of using an augmented reality (AR) headset to display real-time cholangioscopic video during biliary interventions. Across four cases, AR-assisted visualization enabled continuous intraprocedural image integration. The headset functioned as a primary visualization interface, consolidating video inputs into a unified visual field while preserving direct visualization of the sterile field and surrounding procedural environment. This study extends prior work evaluating AR/VR for preprocedural planning in 50 complex transarterial interventional oncology cases. In that cohort, AR/VR review required a median of five minutes and was perceived to alter procedural approach in 46% of cases, primarily through improved three-dimensional spatial understanding. The present study represents conceptual progression from immersive preprocedural planning to active intraprocedural integration.

Biliary interventions involving cholangioscopy require simultaneous attention to fluoroscopy, endoscopic visualization, hemodynamic monitors, and the sterile procedural field. These visual inputs are typically distributed across multiple monitors positioned throughout the angiography suite, necessitating repeated head and neck rotation. This fragmented visual environment adversely contributes to the substantial musculoskeletal burden experienced by interventional radiologists: 88% report musculoskeletal symptoms, with 61% experiencing lower back pain, 56% neck pain, and 46% shoulder complaints [5]. Importantly, cervical pain is specifically associated with ceiling-mounted monitors and the repetitive head and neck movements required to view these monitors during procedures [6]. The virtual monitor is fully customizable, allowing cholangioscopic visualization directly within the operator’s line of sight. AR consolidation may reduce repetitive head movement and streamline visual attention. This hypothesis is supported by recent objective data: a motion analysis study of AR-assisted musculoskeletal interventions demonstrated significantly reduced head turn frequency and magnitude during needle placement procedures compared to conventional monitor use [7].

Beyond ergonomics, fragmentation of visual inputs may increase cognitive load during technically demanding procedures. Surgical studies using validated NASA-TLX assessments have demonstrated that AR visualization reduces mental workload, with lower mental demand, effort, and frustration scores compared to conventional monitors [8]. Although cognitive workload was not formally measured in this pilot, qualitative operator feedback included improved procedural continuity during interventions where cholangioscopic views remained continuously accessible within the primary visual field.

AR and VR technologies in interventional radiology have historically been applied in education and simulation rather than direct clinical care. Mohammed et al. described VR-based endovascular planning in illustrative clinical cases [9], and Park et al. demonstrated the potential of AR in a translational phantom model, showing 54% reduction in needle passes (7.4 to 3.4), 41% reduction in radiation dose (28.7 to 16.9 mGy), and 50% reduction in procedure time (8.93 to 4.42 min) during CT-guided lesion targeting [10]. However, published reports describing the use of AR headsets during live interventional radiology procedures remain sparse. The current work differs from prior studies in that it evaluates real-time intraprocedural video streaming rather than static 3D overlays, and continuous headset wear during active procedures rather than brief planning sessions. This shift from immersive planning to workflow augmentation represents an important technical evolution toward spatial computing-enabled procedural environments.

Limitations

This pilot has several important limitations that should be categorized into study design, hardware, and workflow integration considerations. The small sample size (n = 4) and single-operator design limit generalizability, but this is an initial feasibility study. Ergonomic benefits were assessed through qualitative operator feedback rather than validated ergonomic instruments. While the operator reported subjective improvement in procedural continuity and reduced neck movement, formal assessment using standardized tools was not performed. Future comparative studies should incorporate objective ergonomic measurements to validate these preliminary observations. Headset weight and comfort remain a constraint. While short-duration use during targeted procedural segments appears highly feasible, prolonged wear may result in discomfort. Hardware refinement, including improved weight distribution, battery optimization, and sterile-compatible configurations, will be essential for broader clinical adoption. Operator training time and learning curve were not formally assessed but represent important implementation considerations.

Conclusion

This pilot study demonstrates that augmented reality-assisted cholangioscopy can be safely integrated into biliary interventions. Real-time consolidation of video feeds within the operator’s line of sight was feasible and did not disrupt procedural performance or sterile workflows. Although limited by a small sample size, these findings suggest that AR-based visualization may enhance workflow continuity and reduce ergonomic strain. Larger comparative studies with objective ergonomic and procedural metrics are needed to define clinical impact and scalability in interventional radiology.
