Maternal intake of inadequate dietary folate, and low serum folate levels during pregnancy are associated with increased risk of preterm birth in rural Ethiopia: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Kaleb Mayisso, Tafese Bosha, Dessalegn Tamiru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Inadequate intake of dietary folate is associated with an increased risk of preterm birth, but evidence is limited in low-income settings, including the study area. This study examines the association between maternal intake of inadequate folate and low serum folate levels during pregnancy and the risk of preterm birth in rural Ethiopia. A prospective cohort study examined the association of maternal intake of inadequate dietary folate and low serum folate levels during pregnancy and an increased risk of preterm birth. An assessment of dietary folate intake was conducted using 24-h dietary method at enrollment and each trimester to determine nutrient adequacy throughout the study. A blood sample was collected at their first prenatal visit and at term for serum folate analysis. Logistic regression analyses were fitted to identify the predictors of preterm birth among pregnant women. In this study, the average gestational age at birth was 37.50 weeks, and the preterm birth rate was 23.58%. Inadequate dietary diversity (< 5), dietary folate consumption below recommended levels, and malnutrition were associated with increased odds of preterm birth, with adjusted odds ratios (AOR) of 0.52 (95% CI: 0.301, 0.890), 0.84 (95% CI: 0.74, 0.93), and 1.105 (95% CI: 0.98, 1.24), respectively. Pregnant women with serum folate levels < 4.4 µg/l had 0.760 times higher odds of preterm birth compared to those with levels > 4.4 µg/l(AOR = 0.760, 95% CI: 0.552–0.933) adjusting for other variables. Preterm birth is a significant problem. Maternal intake of inadequate dietary folate during pregnancy indicated by low serum folate is strongly associated with preterm birth. Strengthening public health interventions, including providing additional food for pregnant mothers, ensuring iron and folic acid supplementations, food fortification with folate, dietary diversity and monitoring anthropometric indicators earlier during antenatal care, is crucial.

Introduction

Preterm birth is defined as a live birth that occurred before 37 complete weeks of gestation1,2. Despite a significant global health issue, a significant reduction in preterm birth rates has not been observed worldwide3,4.preterm birth affects 4.84 million infants globally each year, with the highest rates in Southeast Asia and sub-Saharan Africa5–8. The prevalence of preterm birth is a significant public health concern in Ethiopia, with regional estimates varying from 2.8% to 11.41% and an overall pooled prevalence of 10.48%9,10.

Adequate intake of maternal folate is crucial for healthy pregnancies and for the synthesis of DNA building blocks11,12. Higher maternal folate levels correlate with a 28% reduction in risk of preterm birth; approximately a 10% decrease with dietary folate and folic acid supplementation4. On the other hand, inadequate intake of dietary folate is linked to a higher risk of adverse birth outcomes13–15. Women of childbearing age require 400 μg/day of folate, increasing to 600 mcg DFE/day during the peri-conceptional period16. This higher intake supports rapid cell growth, prevents neural tube defects like spina bifida, and aids fetal development. This amount is needed to maintain an appropriate cutoff, even though serum/plasma folate concentrations decrease during pregnancy and recover after delivery11,12,17. Recommended serum folate concentrations (4 µg/l), the most widely used indicator for assessing nutritional status across all populations, should be used to determine adequate folate level18,19.

Folate deficiency prevalence exhibits socioeconomic disparities: lower-income countries have a prevalence exceeding 20%, while higher-income countries reported less than 5%2. Within Africa, prevalence of folate deficiencies among pregnant women varies considerably, it ranges from 0.8% to 57.7% in Kenya, Niger, Senegal, and Sudan20–23, and is 46% in Ethiopia24. Maternal anthropometric deficits is also linked to a higher risk of preterm birth25–27. Maternal short stature (≤ 145 cm) is associated with small for gestational age (SGA) and PTB, especially in resource-poor settings25. A MUAC ≤ 23.5–25.0 cm can indicate underweight and combined with low gestational weight gain (GWG), predicts PTB and LBW25–27. Weight gain recommendations during the second and third trimesters depend on pre-pregnancy BMI, as advised by the Institute of Medicine28.

Assessing maternal intake of dietary folate early in pregnancy can help determine the nutritional status of the women20–24. There are limited studies identified the association of inadequate intake of dietary folate during pregnancy and the low serum folate levels and the risk of preterm birth in Ethiopia. The existing studies had methodological drawbacks and yielded conflicting findings on folate deficiency in pregnant women29–31. In addition to the findings could introduce potential bias and none of the studies established objective evidences and they are characterized by significant variations in dietary consumption practices. On the other hand, no longitudinal study with repeated measurements of dietary folate intake, serum folate levels, and MUAC for clinical undernutrition assessment has provided stronger evidence than existing cross-sectional data linking inadequate dietary folate intake to low serum folate levels and PTB risk. Therefore, this study examines the association between maternal intake of inadequate maternal dietary folate and low serum folate levels during pregnancy and the risk of preterm birth in rural Ethiopia, following women from their first trimester (8 to < 14 weeks) to term.

Methods and materials

Study setting and period

The study was conducted in two randomly selected hospitals in two rural districts that were representative of the different agro ecologic zones of the Northern Sidama zone, Sidama Region, Ethiopia. The districts were selected using cluster sampling method. Of the 36 kebeles (the smallest admiration unit) identified, 12 were selected as study clusters, with six from each district representing both urban and rural areas using purposive sampling. The districts are located at 300 and 337 km from Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia, respectively. The Northern Sidama zone is one of the surplus producing agricultural areas of Ethiopia, with major production of corn, pulses like haricot beans and false banana. The study conducted from March to December 2024.

Study design, study population and eligibility criteria

This cohort study examined the association between inadequate maternal dietary folate intake and low serum folate levels with the risk of preterm birth by comparing pregnant women with inadequate(exposed group) and adequate folate intake (unexposed group). The source population for the study included all pregnant women residing in the Northern Zone of the Sidama Region. Participants were included if they were willing to participate, had a confirmed pregnancy within gestational age of < 12 weeks, and were residing in the study area. Gestational age was based on the last menstrual period during the first antenatal care visits and later on confirmed by fundal height palpation and ultrasound during follow-up visits until term. Women with multiple pregnancy, fetal anomalies, severe mental disorders; history of major complications interfering with preterm birth; advanced maternal age; high BMI at delivery; prior preterm birth; women planned to move out of the area; and inability to communicate in the study language were excluded.

Sample size, sampling technique and data collection methods

Sample size was calculated using open Epi Info statistical software available at http://www.openepi.com/SampleSize/SSCohort.htm with the following parameters and assumptions including a 95% significance level (2-sided), 80% power, and a prevalence of folate deficiency of 46% (≤ 4 ng/mL) among exposed pregnant women, compared to 21.2% in the unexposed group24. This yielded a total of 114 subjects /arm and to allow for ≤ 20% attrition and a design effect of 1.6 by the end of the study, a sample size of 438 was required. Data were collected using a pretested questionnaire. Baseline data were retrieved at 12 weeks of gestation. If a woman missed her scheduled interview, the next eligible pregnant woman was contacted, and the originally scheduled woman was contacted the following day. End line data was collected from participants who successfully completed the follow up using questionnaires pretested in a similar setting.

Allocation to study arms and follow‑up

We purposively sampled and followed up pregnant women delivering at designated hospitals. The final analysis included 424 pregnant women, equally divided into exposed (women with intake of inadequate dietary folate (< 600 mcg DFE/day) and unexposed groups (women with intake of adequate dietary folate (≥ 600 mcg DFE/day)) (n = 212 each). Subgroup analysis of serum folate levels was conducted on blood samples from 158 exposed and 160 unexposed volunteer participants (74.53% and 75.47% respectively), a sample size deemed adequate for these analyses. A study enrolled pregnant women at their first antenatal care (ANC) visit, using births compiled by rural health extension workers, revealed that only 28% initiated ANC in the first trimester, while 32% began their first visit in the fourth or fifth month of pregnancy32.

Dietary folate intake was assessed at enrollment during the first trimester (8 to < 14 weeks) using a 24-h dietary recall method following FAO guidelines33. Pregnant women were categorized by baseline dietary folate intake as either having inadequate (exposed group, n = 219) or adequate (unexposed group, n = 219) intake, based on study results34. Both groups were recruited from similar populations in selected districts (Fig. 1). The cohort was followed up during the second trimester (20 to < 24 weeks), third trimester (32 to < 34 weeks), and at delivery. Participants’ expected delivery dates (EDD) were recorded. Births within the cohort were promptly identified and reported by local community health promoters (“Women Developmental Army (WDA)”) and health extension workers (HEW).Fig. 1Study participant flow of the prospective cohort of pregnant mothers in northern zone of Sidama Region, Ethiopia. Assessment of socio-demographic characteristics

Data on socio-economic and demographic characteristics were collected using a questionnaire adapted from the Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey and the FAO32,33. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to generate a household’s wealth index35. Household wealth quintiles are compiled by assigning the household score.

Assessment of anthropometric characteristics and gestational weight

This longitudinal study used anthropometric indices: BMI and MUAC to assess maternal anthropometric deficits. Data on maternal weight and height were collected at first trimester (8 to < 14 weeks’) gestation to calculate pre-pregnancy BMI. Their height was measured to the nearest millimeter with a portable device equipped with calibrated and standardized height gauges (SECA 206 body meter). The women’s MUAC was assessed subsequently every trimester until delivery, at least eight times as recommended by WHO36 as follows: in the first ante natal contact (12 weeks) of gestation, two contacts in the second trimester (20 and 26 weeks) of gestation, and five contacts in the third trimester (30, 34, 36, 38, and 40 weeks). The last measurement before delivery treated in the final model analysis. The MUAC of the left arm was measured to the nearest millimeter with a non-stretch measuring tape. The data were collected by 4 well-trained and experienced midwives who work permanently in the antenatal care service provision units of health centers in the community.

To track gestational weight gain, the weight was taken in the early first trimester (8 to < 14 weeks) of gestation. Subsequently, the women were weighed at each visit from enrollment to delivery following the standardized procedures recommended by WHO, to the nearest 100 g using electronic scales with a weighing capacity of 10–140 kg37. The following schedule was used: second appointment 8 weeks later; the third, 6 weeks later; fourth and fifth 4 weeks apart; and then the rest every 2 weeks was used for assessment of gestational weight gain. Gestational weight gain in relation to gestational age at birth was taken at labor, and total gestational weight gain was estimated by subtracting the early first trimester weight from the last measured weight before delivery16.

Assessing dietary folate intake, nutrient adequacy and serum levels of folate

Dietary folate intake was assessed using 24-h recall questionnaires throughout the three trimesters, from enrollment to delivery, during both pre-harvest (March–May) and post-harvest (June–September) seasons to estimate nutrient adequacy. The cut-off value for intake of dietary folate was indicated by the Institute of Medicine as 600 mcg DFE/day (RDA), the recommended daily allowance for pregnant women16.

Serum folate levels were determined from blood samples collected during the first and third trimesters at the initial and last prenatal visits, respectively. Professional laboratory technologists collected 5 mL venous blood samples aseptically from the antecubital vein into anticoagulant-free test tubes. Serum samples were separated within 6 h and stored at − 80℃ until analysis. The blood samples were centrifuged and the serum was separated before being frozen at − 80 °C and transported to Addis Ababa clinical laboratory for analysis. Serum folate was measured by a fully automated immunoassay analyzer (Cobas e601 (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany)) using a commercial kit (Elecsys Folate III (Roche Diagnostics)) according to the manufacturer’s instruction at Addis Ababa clinical laboratory. The cut-off value for serum folate were indicated based on international standard of the nutrients for pregnant women based on metabolic indicators with the reference of WOH cut-offs of 10 nmol/L (4 ng/mL)38.

Outcome measurement

Preterm birth, defined as birth before 37 weeks of gestation, was the primary outcome variable. Maternal intake of inadequate dietary folate and serum folate levels were the independent variables. Trained midwives estimated gestational age based on the last menstrual period during the first antenatal care visits. Physicians later confirmed gestational age using fundal height palpation and ultrasound during follow-up visits until term. Additionally, birth weight, small for gestational age, delivery methods, and pregnancy induced hypertension, neonatal mortality, and stillbirth were recorded by the same professionals at birth.

Quality control

All data collectors were trained physicians, laboratory technologists and midwives with at least a diploma in nursing. They also received 5-d training on participants’ enrollment, follow-up, anthropometric measurements. The training was conducted just before the study and was followed by practical tests to ensure the skills were transferred. In each of the health posts and hospitals selected, one supervisor (usually the head) was assigned to oversee data collection. In addition, the investigator made a weekly visit to check the completeness and quality of the data collected. Anthropometric measurements were taken only by trained personnel, and all equipment was calibrated with standard weights and length rods on a daily basis. Neither the pregnant women nor the data collectors were aware of the allocation into groups.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered using Epidata statistical software (Version 20). The data were double-entered, cleaned, and then exported to SPPS (version 27.0) for statistical analyses. All data on singleton pregnancies were analyzed according to their categorization by intake of dietary folate. All continuous variables were checked for normality by use of the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Variables not normally distributed were log-transformed. Means and SEMs were used to describe continuous variables. An independent Student’s t test was used to compare means in the groups that consumed or did not consume dietary folate. A x2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used to test for independence in the distribution of categorical variables between the two study groups. In all comparisons, differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Logistic regression was performed to determine how intakes of inadequate dietary folate, low serum folate, and other confounding variables like dietary diversity and mid-upper arm circumference during pregnancy increase the risk of preterm birth. Associations between preterm births and the predictors were investigated, adjusting for covariates. All variables with P < 0.25 in the binary analyses were included in the multivariable analysis after checking for multicollinearity using variance inflation factors (VIF). The direction and strength of statistical association were measured using the adjusted odds ratio along with their corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). An AOR with a 95% CI was reported to show an association at a P-value < 0.05.

Model fit was assessed using the Omnibus tests of model coefficients and Hosmer–Lemeshow statistic. Model summary like Nagelkerke R Square, which tells us the percentage of the variation in the response variable that can be explained by the predictor variables, was performed. Covariate interactions were also evaluated using a likelihood ratio test. To estimate the economic level of the families, a wealth index was employed. The wealth dispersion was generated by applying PCA. The index was calculated based on 41 household variables, including the ownership of a latrine, agricultural land and its size, selected household assets, the quantity of livestock, and the source of drinking water.

Ethical clearance

This study obtained favorable ethical approval from the college of medicine and health Sciences Hawassa University Institutional Review Board (Protocol #: IRB 098/2016). The health offices provided an authorization letter. All methods were carried out in according with the relevant tenets of Helsinki Declaration and good clinical practice39. All research participants provided informed verbal consent. Written informed consent was also secured from all participants, who provided blood samples for biochemical analysis of serum folate.

Anthropometric characteristics and gestational weight of the participants

This shows the average of the women fell under the normal body weight category. Women with adequate dietary folate intake had higher BMI and were heavier than those with inadequate dietary folate intake (P < 0.05). By the end of the second trimester, the women’s mean weight was 56 kg, representing an average weight gain of 2.55 kg, with a weekly weight gain of 0.40 kg. During the third trimester, the women averaged 57.75 kg, having gained a mean of 4.33 kg since enrollment and 0.55 kg per week.

Pregnant women in the intake of adequate dietary folate had significantly higher weight, higher weight gain in both the second and third trimesters and at the term of gestation (P < 0.05). In the third trimester, the women’s mean MUAC was 25.31 cm, a 3.33 cm increase since enrollment. Dietary folate intake, nutrient adequacy and serum levels of folate

Of 424 eligible participants enrolled in the serum folate level investigation, 318 (75%) were included in the final analysis (158 with inadequate and 160 with adequate dietary folate intake). The overall dropout rate was 25%, balanced across groups. Reasons for dropout included refusal to provide a blood sample (n = 58), inadequate blood sample collected (n = 26), absence during sample collection (n = 10), and spoiled blood sample (n = 12). In the first trimester, 73.3% of women had serum folate levels within the normal range (≥ 4 ng/ml), while 25.8% had elevated levels. By the third trimester, 89% of women had levels within the normal range, and 11% had elevated levels. Across both trimesters, women with adequate dietary folate intake had significantly higher serum folate levels than those with inadequate intake (P < 0.05).

The mean NAR of folate increased from 33.33 at enrollment to 51.12 at the end of the follow-up. Initially, there was no significant difference in mean NAR between women with adequate and inadequate dietary folate intake (P > 0.05). A significant difference was observed in mean NAR between women with adequate dietary folate intake than those with inadequate intake (P < 0.05) by the end of the follow-up. The proportion of pregnant women taking IFA tablets was very high (97%). Discussions

A prospective cohort study of pregnant women found that inadequate dietary folate intake was significantly associated with increased risk of low serum folate levels and preterm birth, a significant public health concern contributing to child mortality6. While preterm birth incidence and mortality are declining overall, incidence has increased in low Socio-demographic Index (SDI) regions and decreased in high SDI regions. Sub-Saharan Africa experiences the highest annual mortality40, including Ethiopia, which is one of the countries that accounts for the highest burden of preterm birth41. In the present study, approximately 24% of the neonates were born preterm, which aligned with the findings identified in the systematic analysis of the burden of preterm birth40 and the observational studies conducted in Ethiopia42.

This study found a higher proportion of preterm female neonates compared to males, which contradicts a systematic analysis40 that reported a higher incidence of preterm male births. This discrepancy may be due to differences in study settings and design. Preterm birth occurred in 23.58% of births (95% CI: 19.8–27.6), a rate higher than the 12.3% and 11.5% reported in the Global Action Report for Sub-Saharan African countries and a 2016 study in Brazil, respectively43,44.

Preterm birth rates varied across public health facilities in Dodola (13%), Axum (13.3%), and Gondar (4.4%)45–47, likely due to differing study settings. The present study, conducted in a higher-risk rural area with health-related gaps, found a higher preterm birth prevalence than a study in northern Ethiopia (16.1%)41. This discrepancy may stem from methodological differences related to the rural setting of the current study, where preterm birth risk factors are more prevalent compared to urban areas. This study’s preterm birth rate was lower than rates reported in Ethiopia and Cameroon (25.5% and 26.5%, respectively) and at Jimma University Specialized Hospital (25.9%)48,49. These differences may be due to our study’s exclusion of multiple gestations and women with a history of abortion. Additionally, the Jimma study reported high rates of alcohol and substance use during pregnancy, factors not observed in our study.

The preterm birth rate in this study was higher than rates reported in Ethiopian and Kenyan public hospitals (13.2%, 10.48%, and 20.2%, respectively)50–52. These differences may stem from variations in study settings. For example, surgical uterine evacuation, which can increase the risk of subsequent preterm birth, was performed in one study. Similarly, our finding contrasts with studies in Ethiopian and Malaysian public hospitals, which reported preterm birth rates of 16.15% and 16.9%, respectively53,54. These inconsistencies could be attributed to the rural setting of our study, where participants were more likely to have risk factors for preterm birth and consume a less diverse diet, particularly one low in folate, compared to participants in the Addis Ababa and Malaysia studies.

Lower maternal folate levels in early pregnancy are associated with a higher risk of preterm birth, consistent with a previous meta-analysis showing a 28% reduction in preterm birth risk with higher folate levels4. While this study found a significant positive association between dietary folate intake and preterm birth risk, contrasting with a similar study that reported a significant negative association, folic acid supplementation did not show a significant relationship with decreased preterm birth risk. This contrasts with prior research indicating a 10% lower risk of preterm birth with higher folic acid supplementation4. Our results indicate that greater dietary diversity, leading to increased nutrient intake and a wider variety of foods, is associated with a lower risk of preterm birth. This finding aligns with a prior systematic review in Ethiopia, but contrasts with another meta-analysis from Ethiopia55, hat found no significant association. This discrepancy may be due to differences in study design, as the previous study was a meta-analysis while ours was a longitudinal study.

Maternal undernutrition is common in resource-poor settings, ranging from 10 to 19% but exceeding 20% in some regions, such as sub-Saharan Africa. In this area, approximately 28.7% of the estimated 134.8 million annual births are preterm8. The study area’s preterm birth prevalence was 23.58%, lower than South Africa’s average gestational age at birth of 37.62 ± 1.74 weeks. Consistent with other data, approximately 15% of preterm births occurred before 32 weeks of gestation, requiring increased neonatal care (< 28 weeks: 4.2%, 28–32 weeks: 10.4%)8. Low gestational weight gain, mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) < 24 cm, and low birth weight were significantly associated with preterm birth. Specifically, a maternal MUAC < 24 cm was associated with a 0.048 times increased risk of preterm birth compared to a MUAC ≥ 24 cm. This finding aligns with studies in northwest Ethiopia and Bangladesh, as well as those in India and Switzerland56–59, which identified MUAC as strongly associated with preterm birth. Prior research consistently demonstrates an association between low maternal MUAC and increased preterm birth risk26.

This study found that maternal height < 145 cm was not a statistically significant determinant of preterm birth, consistent with reports from Addis Ababa hospitals60. However, this contrasts with a California study of singleton live births from 2007–201061, potentially due to socioeconomic and methodological differences. Our study’s rural setting, with factors like food insecurity, may contribute to the discrepancy. Conversely, matched analyses indicated that women with a height ≤ 157.5 cm or a pre-pregnancy BMI < 19.8 kg/m2 had an increased risk of idiopathic preterm labor62. While we found no linear or adjusted associations between BMI and preterm births, a systematic review of UK, USA, South Australian, Norwegian, and Danish datasets showed an elevated adjusted risk of any preterm birth at low BMIs, with the risk remaining low or slightly increasing at higher BMIs63.

While low pre-pregnancy body mass index is recognized as a predictor of adverse pregnancy outcomes such as preterm birth, particularly in low and middle-income countries, this study found no association between a pre-pregnancy BMI under 18.5 kg/m2 and increased preterm birth risk26,64. Maternal height, pre-pregnancy weight, and gestational weight gain measurements exhibited high test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.97, 0.99, and 0.91, respectively)62.

Gestational weight gain from pre-pregnancy to nine months was associated with lower odds of preterm birth, consistent with prior research62. Women with high gestational weight gain had a decreased risk of preterm birth overall (< 37 weeks), as well as for births between 32–36 weeks and < 32 weeks, aligning with previous systematic review65. Weight gain during pregnancy significantly predicted preterm birth; a rate less than 0.27 kg/week increased the risk of idiopathic preterm labor, corroborating matched-based analysis62. Pre-pregnancy BMI and maternal height were poor predictors of preterm birth at < 36 weeks’ gestation, a finding supported by a systematic review66.

Conclusion and recommendations

The magnitude of preterm birth in this study is substantial. Low dietary diversity score, low intake of dietary folate, serum folate below recommended level, low birth weight, a total weight gain of less than 11.5–16 kg, had mid-upper arm circumferences of 23 cm were among the significant determinants of low birth weight. Therefore, it is essential to strengthen public health interventions by considering identified factors. This includes taking into account providing additional food for pregnant mothers, and ensuring iron and folic acid supplements are available. Raising awareness about the benefits of tracking pregnancy as early as possible and monitoring anthropometric indicators during antenatal follow-up is also crucial.

Limitations of the study

This community-based study reflects the actual experiences of women during the study period. However, the 24-h dietary recall method relies on self-reporting, making it prone to recall bias. To minimize errors from memory lapses and distortions, the study employed a carefully designed 24-h recall protocol, standardized multiple-pass interviewing techniques, and memory aids like photographs and probing. The study did not account for potential confounders of preterm births, such as infectious diseases like malaria.
