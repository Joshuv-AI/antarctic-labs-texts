Elastocaloric evidence for a multicomponent superconductor stabilized within the nematic state in Ba(Fe1−xCox)2As2
Authors: Sayak Ghosh, Matthias S. Ikeda, Anzumaan R. Chakraborty, Thanapat Worasaran, Florian Theuss, Luciano B. Peralta, P. M. Lozano, Jong-Woo Kim, P. J. Thompson, Philip J. Ryan, Linda Ye, Aharon Kapitulnik, Steven A. Kivelson, B. J. Ramshaw, Rafael M. Fernandes, Ian R. Fisher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceMaterial realizations of multicomponent superconductors (SCs), where the superconducting order parameter has two or more components, are quite rare. Using the AC elastocaloric effect—a thermodynamic probe of phase transitions under uniaxial strain—we have found evidence of additional symmetry-breaking within the superconducting state in the iron-based SC Co-doped BaFe2As2, which indicates a multicomponent SC. Remarkably, this multicomponent state condenses only within the nematic phase and extremely close to optimal doping, underscoring the significant role of nematic order in determining superconducting pairing. Our work not only opens this exotic and previously hidden state to investigations through other techniques but also demonstrates the effectiveness of uniaxial strain as a tuning parameter in well-studied quantum materials, paving the way for future discoveries.

Phases of quantum materials are described by order parameters (OPs) that capture the broken symmetries characteristic of each phase. In a superconductor (SC), the OP is given by the Cooper pair wavefunction, or the superconducting gap. While most SCs exhibit a single-component order parameter—such as s-wave in BCS SCs like lead and aluminum, or d-wave in the high-Tc cuprates—less common are multicomponent OPs. The degeneracy of the multiple components can be enforced by symmetry or might be accidental. A classic example of a symmetry-enforced degeneracy is the px+ipy state in a tetragonal lattice (1). This was believed to be realized in Sr2RuO4 for a long time, although recent measurements have ruled it out (2). In cases of accidental degeneracies, two superconducting instabilities often exist in close energy proximity, as was initially suggested in the recently discovered heavy-fermion SC UTe2 (3). In such scenarios, the dominant pairing mechanism is expected to determine Tc. However, the subdominant instability may produce a second transition if it is sufficiently close in energy or if stabilized by other phases. Understanding how multiple superconducting instabilities may emerge and interact with each other, as well as with other non-SC phases in a material, is central to uncovering the pairing mechanism(s) that exist in a multicomponent SC (4, 5). In addition, due to their extra degrees of freedom, multicomponent SCs may give rise to emergent effects unattainable in single-component SCs (6, 7).

The Fe-based SCs are generally accepted to have an s± order parameter with opposite sign of the gap on the electron and the hole pockets (8–10). The dominant pairing mechanism is believed to be spin fluctuations which promotes both s± and d-wave (specifically, dxy in the 2-Fe unit cell description) pairing, with s± usually prevailing (11, 12). However, the multiband nature of the materials may stabilize the d-wave, or even more exotic OPs—such as s+is, s+d, or s+id—under suitable conditions (13, 14). In K-doped BaFe2As2, for example, the superconducting gap evolves from nodeless s± at low doping to a nodal SC at high doping, passing through a fully gapped phase with broken time-reversal symmetry at intermediate doping, which has been interpreted as evidence for s+is or s+id state (15–18). The question of whether a d-wave, or any subdominant, instability exists is not simply a matter of detail—it provides crucial insights into how the nematic phase, which is ubiquitous among various Fe-based SCs, and its fluctuations interact with superconductivity (19, 20). In this work, we study the archetypal Fe-based SC: Co-doped BaFe2As2. Near optimal doping, Raman scattering (21, 22) and thermal conductivity (23) measurements indeed show evidence for a subdominant instability. However, no experimental signature of a second SC transition has been observed in thermodynamic measurements like specific heat. Further, how the subdominant instability evolves across optimal doping also remains an open question.

While Co-doped BaFe2As2 has been studied extensively utilizing chemical doping and through transport measurements under uniaxial strain, thermodynamic measurements under uniaxial strain have been lacking. Previous work by Meingast et al. (24) indicates that uniaxial tensile strain along a [100] direction should tune the structural and superconducting phase transitions in a similar fashion as does increasing Co substitution. We employ the AC elastocaloric effect (ECE)—related to the strain derivative of a material’s entropy—to probe the phase diagram near optimal doping under uniaxial stress conditions. The ECE is a highly sensitive, thermodynamic probe of phase transitions, which has been recently employed to study the nontrivial strain response of various quantum materials (25–28). Uniaxial strain offers two primary advantages compared to doping-dependent studies to assess the evolution of multiple competing phases in a material. First, strain eliminates the need for multiple samples grown at slightly different dopings, which invariably host different levels of substitution-induced disorder (29). Second, since the applied stress can be controlled precisely and also reversibly, it enables us to probe the phase diagram at a level of detail not achievable with chemical doping. Further, features beneath the superconducting dome which are invisible to resistivity measurements are accessible in ECE. Here, by comparing the ECE response between overdoped and slightly underdoped samples, we have uncovered thermodynamic evidence for a phase transition (at T∗) below Tc on the underdoped side, which has remained undetected in previous studies. We find that our observations are most straightforwardly explained through the intertwining between the s and d-wave SC instabilities, enabled by their near-degeneracy and by the presence of nematic order. While in the nematic phase, there is no symmetry distinction between s and dxy order, it is physically illuminating to label the order parameter components according to their transformation properties in the nearby tetragonal phase, which is the notation we adopt here. Thus, the scenario best describing our data is an s+d SC state condenses at Tc, while at a slightly lower temperature, T∗, time-reversal symmetry is broken in a state characterized by an s+eiϕd OP, with the phase ϕ moving toward π/2 with decreasing temperature.

Experiment

The ECE measures the temperature changes δT induced by an applied strain δϵ under adiabatic conditions. This is the strain analog of the traditional magnetocaloric effect, which measures the temperature changes induced by an applied magnetic field. The ECE directly probes the strain dependence of the entropy landscape (∂S/∂ϵ) through the relation (32)[1]δTδϵ|S=−TCϵ·∂S∂ϵ|T,

where Cϵ is the specific heat under constant strain. Experimentally, we obtain the ECE response by applying a small AC stress to the sample and measuring the induced temperature oscillations δT at the center of the sample through standard lock-in techniques—see ref. 32 and SI Appendix for more experimental details. For all the experiments described here, uniaxial stress is applied along the crystalline [100] direction, that is, along the Fe-As bond direction. Note that this is different from the direction associated with nematic order, which is [110], and thus a phase transition to a nematic state (relevant for underdoped compositions) is still allowed. The applied stress induces a longitudinal strain ϵxx, along with ϵyy=−νxyϵxx and ϵzz=−νxzϵxx, where νxy and νxz are the in-plane and out-of-plane Poisson’s ratios, respectively (33). The experiment is performed using a Razorbill CS100 strain cell (34), which induces a given longitudinal strain based on the voltage applied to the driving piezoelectric stacks, and the strain ϵxx is measured by a capacitive sensor. In terms of irreducible strains,* the sample experiences a combination of symmetric (A1g) and antisymmetric (B1g) strains, given by ϵA1g,1=ϵxx+ϵyy, ϵA1g,2=ϵzz and ϵB1g=ϵxx−ϵyy. The measured ECE response δT is thus the total response of the material to this combination of strains. The AC stress can be superimposed on top of a fixed DC stress to measure the ECE response at different applied uniaxial stresses.

Results

Overdoped.

We start with ECE measurements on the overdoped side of the phase diagram (x=7.3%), where superconductivity condenses within the tetragonal phase in an s-wave state. The entropy landscape arising from the critical degrees of freedom near a superconducting transition, as shown in the Inset of Fig. 1A, leads to a large ∂S/∂ϵ at Tc. This leads to a sharp, discontinuous feature in the ECE response (δT) as the sample becomes superconducting. As the external stress is varied from negative (compressive) to positive (tensile), the discontinuity moves to lower temperatures (Fig. 1A). To extract the strain dependence of Tc, we plot the derivative of δT with respect to temperature T, which leads to the temperature-strain phase diagram plotted in Fig. 1B—the bright band shows the superconducting phase boundary. We find that Tc decreases monotonically with increasing ϵxx, consistent with the fact that uniaxial strain ϵxx tunes the material in a similar fashion as Co doping (24).

Within a Landau theory, the linear dependence of Tc on strain requires a coupling term of the form |Δ|2ϵμ, where Δ is the (complex) superconducting gap and ϵμ is an irreducible strain. Any single-component superconducting gap can only couple to symmetric (A1g) strains as |Δ|2ϵA1g. Experimentally, we apply strain along the [100] direction (ϵxx), which is a combination of symmetric (A1g) and antisymmetric (B1g) strains. Hence, this coupling is allowed and leads to the linear dependence of Tc on ϵxx. The linear dependence has a direct implication for the behavior of elastic moduli of the material through Tc (35, 36). An allowed coupling of the form |Δ|2ϵμ leads to a “jump” discontinuity in the corresponding elastic modulus cμ at Tc. This elastic modulus discontinuity Δcμ is related to the specific heat discontinuity ΔCpTc and dTcdϵ through the Ehrenfest relation (37)[2]Δcμ=−ΔCpTc(dTcdϵμ)2,

where the negative sign denotes the opposite signs of the elastic modulus and specific heat discontinuities. Thus, a nonzero dTc/dϵμ implies a jump in the corresponding elastic modulus cμ at Tc.

To confirm this, we performed resonant ultrasound spectroscopy (RUS) experiments on an overdoped sample, at a doping level close to the ECE sample. RUS measures the mechanical resonances of a solid, which are directly related to the elastic moduli of the solid—details of the experimental setup can be found in ref. 30. The discontinuity in one of the resonances (1,486 kHz) at Tc is shown in Fig. 1C. After converting this frequency jump to an equivalent elastic moduli jump (2Δf/f=Δc/c; see ref. 30), we can relate it to dTc/dϵ measured in ECE through Eq. 2.† Our experimentally measured modulus jump Δc=−(0.028±0.008) GPa compares well with the Δc=−(0.0271±0.0004) GPa calculated from dTcdϵ=−(244±2) K and ΔCpTc=28 mJ/mol/K2 [reported by Budko et al. (38) for a 7.4% doped sample]. This shows quantitative consistency between different thermodynamic probes of the superconducting transition, namely, specific heat, elastic moduli, and ECE, on the overdoped side of the phase diagram.

Slightly Underdoped.

With a detailed understanding of the ECE measurements on the overdoped side, we can now investigate what happens on the underdoped side, where superconductivity condenses within the nematic/orthorhombic phase. We consider a composition that is only just underdoped, such that the antiferromagnetic phase boundary is well separated from the nematic boundary (39). For these compositions, the only phase transitions are for the nematic and superconducting phases. The ECE data for such a slightly underdoped (x=6.2%) sample is shown in Fig. 2. The sample first undergoes the nematic transition (marked as TS), where the tetragonal unit cell becomes orthorhombic. Since the unit cell distorts along the [110] direction, this remains a sharp transition even in the presence of the applied [100] stress. Around the superconducting transition, in stark contrast to the overdoped ECE data, multiple discontinuous features are seen in the ECE, marked as Tc and T∗. This implies two adjacent thermodynamic phase transitions—this second transition below Tc is our central finding.

We now focus on how strain along [100], ϵxx, tunes these transitions. Under the largest compressive strain (Fig. 2A), three clear transitions can be identified, marked by TS, Tc and T∗. The onset of superconductivity at Tc is also known through the observation of Meissner screening in simultaneous mutual inductance measurements on this sample (see SI Appendix for details). On tuning toward positive (tensile) strains, these transitions move closer together, such that only a single superconducting transition remains above a certain tensile strain (ϵxx≈0.07%). The ECE discontinuity at large tensile strains (Fig. 2C) looks similar to the data from the overdoped sample (Fig. 1A), consistent with the fact that uniaxial tension along [100] tunes the material toward the overdoped side. It is important to note that in ECE, we probe a small region at the center of the sample where strain is homogeneous (32), which eliminates the possibility of any strain-induced phase separation. The presence of three distinct transitions (bright and dark bands) is clearly seen when we take the derivative of the ECE data (Fig. 2D). The experimentally determined temperature–strain phase diagram (Fig. 2D) looks similar to the well-known temperature-doping phase diagram across optimal doping, with the additional T∗ transition line that we have uncovered and which was not observed in previous doping-dependent studies. Since no additional features are observed in the ECE measurements (which were taken for fixed offset strains while varying the temperature), the implication is that the orthorhombic and tetragonal phases are separated by a vertical line of first-order phase transitions. We will elaborate this further in Discussion. Compared to Tc, whose variation with strain is weak and mostly similar between underdoped and overdoped samples, T∗ is seen to vary strongly with strain. We will return to this point later to explain why the T∗ transition was not observed before.

To further resolve the behavior of the Tc and T∗ phase boundaries, we measured the ECE response from a second underdoped sample (x=5.6%) over a strain range where both Tc and T∗ are observable (Fig. 3A). We find that the T∗(ϵ) line stays separate from Tc(ϵ) until the highest applied strains. Fits to the data for the two phase transitions indicate that they do not intersect for any value of the induced strain (Fig. 3B), implying that the T∗ transition always occurs below Tc, that is, within the superconducting state. The expected behavior of the three transitions near optimal doping, TS, Tc and T∗, as indicated by their evolution under uniaxial strain ϵxx, is schematically shown in the temperature-doping phase diagram in Fig. 4.

We further use the data in Fig. 3A to perform an important consistency check that relates the magnitude of ECE discontinuities to the strain derivative of the transition temperatures, dTc/dϵ and dT∗/dϵ. This arises due to the Ehrenfest relation (see ref. 27 for a derivation and SI Appendix for details of how the various quantities are obtained from experimental data),[3]ΔCpdTcritdϵ=ΔCpδTδϵ⇒dTcritdϵ=ΔCpδTδϵΔCp,

where Cp denotes specific heat and Tcrit is the transition temperature. The right-hand side of Eq. 3 can be calculated from the measured ECE data if the specific heat is known. Since this quantity gives dTcrit/dϵ, Tcrit(ϵ) can be obtained by integrating the right-hand side. The fits to Tc(ϵ) and T∗(ϵ), shown as solid red and purple lines in Fig. 3B, are obtained through this procedure—further details can be found in SI Appendix. The goodness of the fits confirms the self-consistency of the data for both transitions. It also establishes that the second transition we have found at T∗ indeed behaves like a continuous thermodynamic transition.

From the above Ehrenfest relation, we estimate the expected specific heat discontinuity at the T∗ transition. This is particularly important since specific heat measurements only show a single discontinuity (ΔCp) at Tc, with no signatures at T∗. Our measured specific heat on a similar doping sample also shows a single discontinuity (shown in SI Appendix), consistent with existing reports and ruling out any sample-specific issues. From the fit to T∗ϵ in Fig. 3B (purple line), we extract the expected specific heat discontinuity at T∗, ΔCp|T∗, which is a fitting parameter for the fit. ΔCp|T∗ is found to be approximately 3% of the magnitude of ΔCp|Tc. Intuitively, this follows from Eq. 3 and the fact that dT∗/dϵ is about a factor of 11 larger than dTc/dϵ, while the ECE discontinuities at Tc and T∗ are comparable in magnitude (Fig. 3A). The smallness of ΔCp|T∗, combined with a considerable phonon heat capacity at T∗≈19 K (shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S1), explains why, experimentally, a specific heat discontinuity is not resolved at T∗. In fact, the large value of dT∗/dϵ, which implies a strong sensitivity to strain, makes ECE the ideal probe to detect this transition, which has remained hidden to previous investigations. For the case of Sr2RuO4 mentioned earlier, evidence has been advanced for a time-reversal symmetry breaking transition under strain (40) but no second transition has been seen in specific heat or ECE measurements (26, 41, 42), highlighting the importance of the present data for Co-doped BaFe2As2.

In certain families of underdoped pnictides, an orthorhombic-to-tetragonal reentrant transition has been observed in the superconducting phase (43, 44). In electron-doped 122 compounds, this was attributed to the backbending of the nematic transition line due to the competition between nematic and superconducting phases. In hole-doped 122 compounds, this was attributed to the so-called C4 magnetic phase (45). No magnetic phase is present in the electron-doped (co-doped) compounds at the dopings we have measured (39). To check for reentrant tetragonality, we performed X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements on a slightly underdoped sample where zero-strain TS, Tc and T∗ are known from resistivity and ECE measurements (details of the XRD setup can be found in SI Appendix). The X-ray data show the onset of orthorhombicity at TS, with the sample remaining orthorhombic down to the lowest measured temperature (∼14 K), which is about 5 K lower than T∗ (Fig. 3C). This rules out reentrant tetragonal phase or a competing magnetic phase as the origin of the phase transition at T∗. Furthermore, in ultrasound experiments on an underdoped sample, the sound waves are completely attenuated below TS due to the formation of orthorhombic domains (46). No sharp resonances are resolved even at 10 K, which indicates that the sample remains orthorhombic down to this temperature. Both the XRD and ultrasound experiments are performed on unstrained samples, eliminating the possibility of any strain-induced domain formation.

Discussion

We now discuss the most probable scenario for the origin of the T∗ transition. Several theoretical microscopic models have predicted a dxy-wave (in the 2-Fe crystallographic unit cell) superconducting instability to be nearly degenerate with the leading s±-wave superconductivity near optimal doping (see refs. 8 and 47 for a comprehensive review). Such a near-degeneracy arises from the multiorbital nature of the material combined with the different interactions that couple electronic states from distinct bands. Within the nematic phase, an s+d order parameter must arise since the nematic order (B2g symmetry) couples the s (A1g) and d (B2g) superconducting OPs. Provided that these SC instabilities are close by in energy, the admixture between the s-wave and d-wave components in the nematic phase is not only large, but a second transition may also occur toward a time-reversal symmetry-breaking (TRSB) s+eiϕd state, with ϕ≠0,π (20). Such a state can be understood as a mixture between the s+d and s+id states that can arise in a tetragonal system with closely competing s and d-wave instabilities. Therefore, near but below optimal doping, where superconductivity condenses within the nematic state, we argue that an s+d superconducting state first condenses at Tc, followed by a TRSB transition at T∗ (Fig. 4). This scenario is consistent with the observation of a subleading d-wave Bardassis-Schrieffer collective mode in Raman spectroscopy (21, 22) and the anisotropic gap deduced from thermal conductivity measurements (23). Note that, inside the nematic phase, the s-wave and d-wave gaps defined in the tetragonal phase have the same symmetry properties. As a result, both behave as s-wave gaps in the orthorhombic phase, such that the TRSB transition is formally an s to s+is transition with respect to the symmetries of the orthorhombic lattice. In the remainder of the paper, however, we refer to this transition as one between an s+d state and an s+eiϕd state, since the microscopic mechanism responsible for the TRSB is more intuitively understood using the tetragonal notation.

To formalize this scenario, we consider a phenomenological mean-field Landau theory of competing s and d-wave superconducting OPs, Δs and Δd, with nearby transition temperatures (see SI Appendix for details). In the absence of nematic order, if both OPs condense, their relative phase ϕ is determined by the biquadratic term u|Δs|2|Δd|2cos2ϕ, which, from weak-coupling calculations, favors a fully gapped s+id state, i.e. ϕ=±π/2 (u>0) (13, 14). The presence of nematic order, however, generates a new bilinear coupling gΔsΔdcosϕ, which favors instead a relative phase ϕ=0,π (19). Due to the existence of these two competing terms, this model can display two sequential superconducting transitions, as discussed in ref. 20. At Tc, the bilinear term wins and superconductivity condenses with Δs,d≠0 and ϕ=0,π, which is the time-reversal invariant s+d state. Note that, because both s and d OPs transform in the same way under the symmetry operations of the orthorhombic phase, this SC state does not break any additional symmetries. As the temperature is lowered and the gaps increase, the biquadratic term becomes more important relative to the bilinear term. As a result, for a range of parameters of the model, the relative phase ϕ unlocks from its Tc value at T∗<Tc, leading to a time-reversal symmetry breaking (TRSB) transition into an s+eiϕd state (with ϕ≠0,π) (20).

To directly relate this theory to our experiments, we calculate the ECE discontinuities predicted by this model (details in the SI) and show that the ECE signatures at both Tc and T∗ can be qualitatively reproduced. This is particularly nontrivial since dTc/dϵ and dT∗/dϵ are both positive (Fig. 3), but the signs of the ECE discontinuities, which, for completely independent order parameters, are expected to follow the signs of dTcrit/dϵ, are opposite at Tc and T∗. We find that, because both the s and d-wave OPs are already nonzero at T∗, a TRSB transition from the s+d to the s+eiϕd state naturally allows for the ECE discontinuity at T∗ to not follow the sign of dT∗/dϵ. Importantly, this is not allowed for a transition from a single-component (s or d) to a multicomponent SC state (s+d) at T∗. The opposite signs of the ECE discontinuities therefore strongly indicate that the lower transition is due to TRSB. Moreover, our analysis reveals wide ranges of parameters where additional features of the thermodynamics are reproduced, including the relative magnitude of the jump in heat capacity at the two transitions, and the fact (Fig. 3) that the jump in ECE is smaller for larger values of dT∗/dϵ. We emphasize that none of these features is easily understood unless the phase transition involves two superconducting order parameters that have already condensed. This conclusion motivates further work to directly probe the existence of broken time reversal symmetry. Muon spin rotation (μSR) measurements under uniaxial strain may provide direct experimental evidence of time-reversal symmetry breaking at T∗ (18). It should also be possible to train the s+eiϕd state using a combination of uniaxial strain ϵxx and out-of-plane magnetic field to observe a nonzero polar Kerr signal. Preliminary Kerr effect measurements under a combination of uniaxial strain and magnetic field (∼30 mT) do not show clear evidence for TRSB at T∗, though the absence of a signature does not preclude the existence of such a state and further experiments are ongoing.

In summary, our work establishes the presence of a symmetry-breaking transition within the superconducting state in slightly underdoped Ba(Fe1−xCox)2As2. The observation of this highly strain-sensitive transition was facilitated by using the AC ECE, which measures the strain derivative of the entropy (∂S/∂ϵ) and consequently detects a large signal at this transition. The presence of this transition modifies the zero-strain doping phase diagram (shown in Fig. 4) and emphasizes the need for reexamining the superconducting pairing state in this extensively studied material. Further XRD measurements under external strain should reveal the exact shape of the possibly first-order phase boundary shown in Fig. 4. The modified phase diagram also indicates two crucial roles of the nematic order in affecting superconductivity. First, the nematic phase appears to be a key determinant for the SC pairing symmetry, rendering the SC order parameter different in the orthorhombic phase compared to the tetragonal phase. This suggests a possible scenario where the nematic transition ends in a line of first-order transitions below the SC dome (Fig. 4). The present AC ECE measurements would not be sensitive to a strongly first-order transition with a large coercive strain, necessitating further XRD or stress–strain measurements to confirm or refute its existence. Second, Tc appears to be maximized when the nematic transition line meets the superconducting dome, which defines the optimal doping point in the phase diagram. Given the prevalence of a nematic phase among several families of Fe-based SCs (48–50), whether the existence of a subdominant pairing channel is ubiquitous among them remains an intriguing question for future studies.

Materials and Methods

Co-doped BaFe2As2 single crystals were grown by a self-flux technique described by Chu et al. (39). Heat capacity and 4-point resistance measurements were performed to find the structural and superconducting transition temperatures. These measurements were performed in a commercial PPMS.

ECE measurements at cryogenic temperatures were performed using a Razorbill CS-100 strain cell. We follow the methods elaborated previously in ECE studies reported from our group (27, 32). The sample is attached to Ti mounting plates with DEVCON 2-ton Epoxy and cured at room temperature. The samples used for ECE measurements are shaped into long thin bars with typical dimensions 1.80×0.35×0.03 mm, with strain being applied along the long direction. Care is taken to have at least 300 μm of the sample held by the plates on either side, this ensures good strain transmission into the sample and reduces chances of the sample slipping during cool-down. To measure the ECE temperature oscillations, we use a chromel-Au (0.07% Fe) thermocouple, which is attached to the center of the sample using a thin layer of AngstromBond AB9110LV.

XRD measurements were performed at the BM28 beamline of the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF). The incident X-rays were tuned to 6.977 keV and the diffraction measurements were performed using a Pilatus3 300 K area detector. The sample was mounted on the cold finger of an ARS closed-cycle He cryostat. A temperature dependence of θ to 2θ scans around the (2 2 6) reflection was performed, which is reported in Fig. 3.

For RUS measurements, a custom-made low temperature setup was utilized (details in ref. 30). The sample is placed in weak mechanical contact with two piezoelectric transducers. An AC excitation is applied to one of the transducers to generate an ultrasonic wave within the sample. The voltage sensed on the second transducer at the same frequency shows a peak when the excitation frequency matches a mechanical resonance of the sample. The samples used were single crystals cut into thin rectangular plate-like shapes. The dimensions of the overdoped sample (x=7.3%) is 1.40×1.14×0.19 mm, with 0.19 mm along the tetragonal c-axis. The dimensions of the underdoped sample (x=5.6%) is 1.69×1.51×0.23 mm, with 0.23 mm along the tetragonal c-axis.
