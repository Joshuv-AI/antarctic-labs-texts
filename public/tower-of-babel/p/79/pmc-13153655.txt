Association Between Severe Aortic Stenosis and Amyloid Deposition in the Myocardium of Patients With Wild-Type Transthyretin Cardiac Amyloidosis
Authors: Kentaro Furukawa, Hiroaki Kawano, Ryohei Akashi, Chisa Eguchi, Tomohiro Honda, Tadatomo Fukushima, Yusuke Takenaka, Masamichi Eguchi, Tsuyoshi Yoshimuta, Takashi Kudo, Masahiro Nakashima, Mitsuharu Ueda, Satoshi Ikeda, Koji Maemura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough wild-type transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis (ATTRwt-CA) is frequently observed in older patients with aortic stenosis (AS), its relationship with AS is unclear. Therefore, this exploratory study aimed to investigate the relationship between myocardial amyloid deposition and AS in patients with ATTRwt-CA.Methods and ResultsThis study included 39 patients (mean age 76 years) with biopsy-confirmed ATTRwt-CA. Myocardial amyloid deposition was quantitatively analyzed using histological evaluation of the biopsied myocardium and the heart-to-contralateral (H/CL) ratio was evaluated using bone scintigraphy. These parameters were then compared between 5 patients with severe AS and 34 patients without severe AS, and predictors of severe AS were evaluated. Myocardial amyloid deposition was greater in ATTRwt-CA patients with severe AS than in those without severe AS (mean [±SD] 32.6±12.7% vs. 19.9±11.7%; P=0.0311) but there was no difference in the H/CL ratio between the 2 groups. Multivariate analysis identified only greater myocardial amyloid deposition (per 10%) as an independent determinant of severe AS in patients with ATTRwt-CA (odds ratio 2.91; 95% confidence interval 1.23–8.67; P=0.0254).ConclusionsThis exploratory study suggests that severe AS may be associated with myocardial amyloid deposition in patients with ATTRwt-CA, although larger prospective research is needed to validate our results.

Central Figure

Transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis (ATTR-CA) is a progressive and fatal disease characterized by the deposition of transthyretin (TTR) amyloid fibrils in the myocardium.1–8 ATTR-CA is classified as hereditary (ATTRv-CA), with a pathogenic TTR variant, or wild-type (ATTRwt-CA), which exists without a mutation in the ATTR gene. In ATTRwt-CA, amyloid fibril formation results from an aging-related process.9

Aortic stenosis (AS) is the most common valvular heart disease that primarily affects older adults and can be caused by age-related degeneration, rheumatic fever, or congenital malformations.10,11 The prevalence of both ATTR-CA and AS increases with age, and several studies have reported ATTR-CA in patients with severe AS.10 However, there have been no reports on the relationship between AS and myocardial amyloid deposition in patients with ATTRwt-CA.

Thus, the aim of the present exploratory study was to investigate the relationship between severe AS and myocardial amyloid deposition in patients with ATTRwt-CA based on histopathological evaluation of the biopsied myocardium and cardiac scintigraphy, which is used for the evaluation of amyloid deposition in ATTRwt-CA.

Methods

Study Population

This retrospective study evaluated 56 consecutive patients with ATTRwt-CM, diagnosed on the basis of endomyocardial biopsy and no ATTR gene variations, who presented to Nagasaki University Hospital between December 2019 and November 2025. Of the 56 patients, 27 were excluded due to a lack of bone scintigraphy results. Thus, 39 patients were included in the study (Figure 1). Figure 1.Study flowchart. ATTRwt-CA, wild-type transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis; 99 mTc-PYP, technetium-99 m pyrophosphate.

Study flowchart. ATTRwt-CA, wild-type transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis; 99 mTc-PYP, technetium-99 m pyrophosphate.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nagasaki University Hospital (23022016-3) and was conducted in accordance with the 2024 Declaration of Helsinki.

Standard Echocardiographic Assessment

Echocardiography was performed using the GE Vivid E95 system (GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL, USA) and images were acquired from standard views. Cardiac function and morphology were assessed using transthoracic echocardiography. All standard echocardiographic measurements were performed in accordance with the American Society of Echocardiography and European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging guidelines.12

Speckle Tracking Left Ventricle Analysis

Speckle tracking analysis was performed offline using the EchoPAC software package (GE HealthCare) to evaluate left ventricular global longitudinal strain (GLS).

For GLS deformation analysis, longitudinal strain was calculated using an automated contouring detection algorithm. The averaged peak longitudinal strain of 17 segments from apical 4-, 2-, and 3-chamber views was generated as a bull’s-eye map. In patients with atrial fibrillation, it is sometimes difficult to construct a bull’s-eye map due to heart rate fluctuations. In such cases, patients with relatively stable RR intervals were included to enable evaluation of GLS. If the evaluation of GLS was still challenging, the average strain value for each segment was calculated and then evaluated.

Cardiac Amyloid Radionuclide Imaging

Patients were injected intravenously with 700–900 MBq technetium-99 m pyrophosphate (99 mTc-PYP) and planar images of the anterior and lateral projections were obtained 1 and 3 h later. Single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) was performed 3 h after the acquisition of planar images. Data from SPECT images were not used in the present study, except for the Perugini grading of patients.

Planar imaging and SPECT/computed tomography (CT) were performed using a Symbia T SPECT/CT camera (Siemens, Munich, Germany). For planar images, data were acquired using a 128×128 matrix, a low-energy high-resolution collimator, and a 15% energy window. For SPECT/CT, the same energy window with a 64×64 matrix and 360° rotation was used with CT attenuation corrections.

Image Interpretation

Images were analyzed using 2 methods. Semiquantitative visual scoring of cardiac retention using the Perugini score13 was performed using planar and SPECT/CT images. Grades 2 and 3 were considered positive based on the American Heart Association statement. In the quantitative analysis,14 a circular region of interest (ROI) was drawn over the heart on anterior images in a standard manner and copied and mirrored over the contralateral chest. The fraction of the mean counts in the heart ROI-to-contralateral chest ROI was calculated as the heart-to-contralateral (H/CL) ratio. This calculation was performed on images acquired at 1 and 3 h. To assess bone accumulation, an equal volume with an irregular ROI was drawn over the sternum on anterior planar images. OsiriX MD 11.0 software (Pixmeo, Geneva, Switzerland) was used for image interpretation and analysis.

Quantitative Evaluation of Amyloid Deposition in Biopsied Myocardium

Amyloid deposition in the biopsied myocardium obtained from the right ventricular septum during cardiac catheterization (38 patients) or the right atrium during surgery for aortic valve replacement (1 patient) was quantitatively evaluated using Fiji (Fiji Is Just ImageJ), version 1.54p (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The area of amyloid deposition (Congo red staining) and the total myocardial area were evaluated by adjusting the color threshold. Amyloid deposition was assessed by two authors (K.F., H.K.) who were blinded to the patients’ clinical data.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics are expressed as the mean±SD or median with interquartile range for continuous variables and as counts and percentages for categorical variables. Baseline parameters were compared between groups using a paired t-test or Wilcoxon’s signed-rank test, as appropriate. First, clinical background was compared between patients with and without severe AS. Second, univariate logistic regression analysis was used to investigate predictors of severe AS among general characteristics that differed significantly (P<0.05) between groups and known predictors. Variables identified as significant (P<0.10) in the univariate analysis were further evaluated using multivariate logistic regression. Multivariate analysis of age and indicators of myocardial amyloid deposition and myocardial damage were associated with amyloidosis.

All hypothesis tests were 2-sided, and statistical significance was set at P<0.05. All analyses were performed using the JMP statistical software (JMP Student Edition 18; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Intraclass correlation coefficient analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 23.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

The baseline characteristics of the study participants are summarized in Table 1. The mean (±SD) age of the included patients was 76±6 years and 35 (90%) were male. There were 23 (59%) patients with New York Heart Association (NYHA) Class II and 16 (41%) with NYHA Class III. Five (13%) patients were treated using surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR; n=2 patients) or transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR; n=3 patients), and were defined as having severe AS. Patients were diagnosed and treated with either SAVR or TAVR in accordance with Japanese Circulation Society (JCS) guidelines.15 Patients with severe AS who did not undergo valve replacement were excluded from the analysis. ATTRwt-CA was diagnosed at the time of diagnosis of severe AS or within 1 year after SAVR or TAVR. Hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and coronary artery disease were identified in 56%, 20%, 36%, and 32% of patients, respectively. Myocardial amyloid deposition was greater in patients with than without severe AS (32.6±12.7% vs. 19.9±11.7, respectively; P=0.0311; Table 1). No significant differences were found between the 2 groups in other clinical or laboratory characteristics (Table 1). Discussion

This study demonstrated that only increased myocardial amyloid deposition, based on histological evaluation, and no other factors, including 99 mTc-PYP cardiac amyloid radionuclide imaging data and age, was significantly associated with severe AS in patients with ATTRwt-CA. In a multiple regression analysis of 398 patients with ATTRwt-CA, Annabi et al. showed that the presence of any grade (mild to severe) AS was associated with older age and coronary artery disease (CAD), but that previous study did not mention the technique used to determine myocardial amyloid deposition.16 In the present study, age and CAD were not associated with severe AS in patients with ATTRwt-CA.

Four previous studies included histological examinations of the myocardium in patients with AS and ATTRwt-CA.17–20 In 46 AS patients who underwent SAVR, Singal et al. reported that none of the interventricular septum biopsy specimens had amyloid deposits, compared with 33 (71.7%) native aortic valves that showed amyloid deposits (including 19 [57.6%] with transthyretin deposition), which was suggestive of isolated valvular amyloidosis.17 The patients in that study were younger (mean age 70 years) and had better left ventricular function (median GLS 18.4%) than the patients included in the present study (mean age 76 years; median GLS 10%). These factors may explain the differences observed between the 2 studies. In patients undergoing TAVR, Beuthner et al. reported that CA was identified on hematoxylin-eosin, Elastica van Gieson, and Congo red-stained endomyocardial biopsies in 8 of 162 patients (4.9%);18 however, immunostaining to diagnose the type of CA and further precise histological examination were not performed. In 171 patients undergoing TAVR for severe AS, Holt et al. performed myocardial biopsy for verification in patients with Grade 1 or ambiguous uptake after screening for ATTR-CA using bone scintigraphy (99 mTc-3,3-diphosphono-1,2-propanodicarboxylic acid or 99 mTc-hydroxymethylene diphosphonate), with ATTR-CM defined as cardiac tracer uptake Perugini Grade 2 or greater in the absence of abnormal monoclonal protein or abnormal free light chain ratio.19 In that study, 6 (3.5%) patients had cardiac bone tracer uptake Perugini Grade 2 or greater in the absence of abnormal monoclonal protein/free light chains. Endomyocardial biopsies confirmed the diagnosis of 2 (1.2%) additional patients, whereas 5 patients with low-grade uptake did not have ATTR-CM.19 In a retrospective histological analysis descriptive study of 46 patients who underwent SAVR due to severe AS with amyloid deposits, Brette et al.20 found that 2 (4%) patients had positive bone scintigraphy (99 mTc-HMDP) and that of the 5 myocardial biopsies performed during surgery, 4 (80%) were positive for ATTR deposits.20 This indicated that myocardial biopsy was more sensitive for the detection of mild amyloid deposits than bone scintigraphy.20 Although these studies evaluated myocardial amyloid deposition in some patients with severe AS, data on the relationship between myocardial amyloid deposition and severe AS is limited. The present study examined myocardial amyloid deposition in all patients with ATTRwt-CA with and without severe AS.

Bone scintigraphy parameters (H/CL ratio and Perugini grade) did not differ between groups in the present study. Because the H/CL ratio uses the contralateral lung field as the denominator, if there is lung field or soft tissue accumulation, the denominator becomes high, and it is difficult to clearly evaluate upper and lower myocardial accumulation. It has also been reported that the more extracardiac accumulation there is, the worse the prognosis.21 Moreover, Milani et al. reported that pulmonary amyloid is detected in approximately 14% of autopsy cases of ATTR amyloidosis.22 For these reasons, it can be said that PYP assessment (especially planar) is suitable for determining whether a patient is positive or negative, but is not suitable for assessing the degree of deposition. To overcome this, quantitative analysis using SPECT is necessary. Regarding the Perugini grade, there are only 4 grades, with positive results are usually concentrated in Grade 3; therefore, the problem is that there is not enough variation between groups to allow for correlation analysis.

The precise mechanisms underlying the association between AS and ATTRwt-CA remain unclear, although several potential mechanisms have been proposed, including age-related features, the contribution of TTR amyloidosis to both ATTRwt-CA and AS, oxidative stress-induced amyloid formation and aortic valve calcification, and high shear stress.23 Among these mechanisms, the results of the present study suggest that TTR amyloidosis deposition itself may contribute to both ATTRwt-CA and severe AS.

Study Limitations

The present study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective study with a small sample size, particularly in the severe AS group. This may not be sufficient to detect reliable statistical significance, especially in the multivariate analysis, because of events per variable. Second, myocardial specimens may not accurately evaluate myocardial amyloid deposition in the entire heart because of the small size used and patchy TTR amyloid deposition. However, the patients in this study had relatively advanced or severe ATTR-CA, as suggested by the laboratory and echocardiographic data. This suggests that TTR amyloid deposition may be diffuse, reducing the impact of small specimens on the evaluation of amyloid deposition.

Further large studies are required to evaluate these factors.

Conclusions

The present histological quantitative study on amyloid deposition in biopsied myocardium suggests that severe AS may be associated with myocardial amyloid deposition in patients with ATTRwt-CA, and larger prospective studies are needed to validate our results.
