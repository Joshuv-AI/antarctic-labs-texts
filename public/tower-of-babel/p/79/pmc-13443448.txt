Comprehensive post-transcriptional modification profiles in individual Staphylococcus aureus tRNA species
Authors: Jose R Jaramillo-Ponce, Philippe Wolff, Virginie Marchand, Yuri Motorin, Maximilian Kohl, Béatrice Chane-Woon-Ming, Hiroki Kanazawa, Aila Ruiz-Paterson, Caroline Paulus, Johana Chicher, Marine Lenon, Martina Krämer, Anne-Sophie Gribling-Burrer, Constantinos Stathopoulos, Redmond Smyth, Mark Helm, Pascale Romby, Stefano Marzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPost-transcriptional modifications modulate transfer RNA (tRNA) structure, stability, and codon decoding properties, contributing to translation regulation and adaptation across diverse organisms, including bacterial pathogens. We provide a comprehensive analysis of tRNA modifications in Staphylococcus aureus using extensive oligonucleotide mass spectrometry and deep-sequencing methods, generating a high-confidence modification map for each individual tRNA species, including non-proteogenic tRNAGly. While the overall tRNA modification landscape is conserved among Gram-positive bacteria, our data uncovered unexpected S. aureus-specific features. These include the absence of m2A37 in tRNAs despite the presence of the methyltransferase RlmN, a single multi-site DusB2 enzyme catalyzing all tRNA dihydrouridylation, and evidence suggesting a dedicated pseudouridine synthase responsible for Ψ32. Besides, heterogeneous modification patterns were observed in tRNALeu(UAA) and tRNALys(UUU), highlighting a complex interplay in anticodon hypermodification. Time-course proteomics revealed dynamic expression of tRNA modifying enzymes during growth. Integration of ribosome profiling and Nanopore tRNA sequencing offered a global view of S. aureus decoding properties, revealing efficient four-way wobble recognition, slower translation of rare codons by low abundant tRNAs, and distinctive decoding dynamics of Gly codons potentially influenced by the unusual modification status of tRNAGly(UCC). This work establishes a framework to dissect the role of tRNA modifications in S. aureus physiology and pathogenesis.

Introduction

Transfer RNA (tRNAs) are key components of the translation apparatus, ensuring accurate decoding of messenger RNA (mRNA) codons and delivery of the correct amino acids to the ribosome [1]. The balance between the abundance of different tRNA species and the codons they decode influences translation speed and fidelity [2, 3], thereby affecting protein synthesis rates, co-translational folding, and protein localization [4]. Among the factors modulating this balance, the modified nucleotides present in tRNA molecules play a crucial role by shaping their structural properties and decoding capacities [5]. These post-transcriptional modifications can be regulated in response to environmental signals [6], reprogramming translation through mechanisms such as codon-biased decoding [7], changes in tRNA aminoacylation efficiency [8], and effects in tRNA stability and maturation [9, 10], ultimately promoting adaptation. Although still underexplored, this layer of regulation is likely central to bacterial physiology, particularly in pathogenic species, enabling a rapid reconfiguration of gene expression in response to fluctuating environments and host-imposed stresses [11].

Around 60 distinct natural modifications have been identified in bacterial tRNAs [12], which contain on average eight modified nucleotides [13]. These modifications are predominantly located in the anticodon loop and in the tRNA core region formed by the interaction of the D- and TΨC loops [14]. Within the anticodon loop, positions 34 and 37 are the most frequently modified, exhibiting remarkable chemical diversity among different tRNA species [15]. Modifications at position 34 influence wobble base pairing, which enables a single tRNA to recognize multiple synonymous codons [16], while those at position 37, immediately adjacent to the anticodon, modulate the stability of the codon–anticodon interaction and contribute to reading frame maintenance [17]. In contrast, modifications in the tRNA core are less diverse and primarily affect structure dynamics and stability of tRNAs [18, 19], with pseudouridine (Ψ), dihydrouridine (D), and 5-methyluridine (m5U) being the most common and widespread across tRNA species.

tRNA modifications are introduced by a diverse array of enzymes with distinct substrate specificities and catalytic mechanisms [20]. Many modifications, especially those in the tRNA core region, are installed by stand-alone enzymes, whereas modifications in the anticodon loop often arise through the sequential action of multiple enzymes [21]. Most enzymes modify specific positions across multiple tRNA species by recognizing structural or sequence features conserved within their substrates [22]. The tRNA–dihydrouridine synthase (Dus) families exemplify the diversity of substrate recognition, as distinct species-specific enzymes install the same modification at different tRNA positions [23]. In Escherichia coli, DusA introduces D20 and D20a, while DusB and DusC modify specifically positions 17 and 16, respectively [24]. In contrast, Bacillus subtilis possesses two types of DusB enzymes with overlapping but distinct preferential activities: DusB1 is responsible for all D17, D20, D20a, and D47 modifications, while DusB2 is more specific to positions 20 and 20a [25]. Notably, some enzymes exhibit modification activities beyond tRNA, such as E. coli RlmN, which introduces 2-methyladenosine (m2A) both in tRNA and 23S rRNA [26].

Despite their recognized importance in translation, comprehensive information on tRNA modifications and their corresponding enzymes is available for a limited number of bacteria, including the model species E. coli [21] and B. subtilis [27], as well as several pathogenic species such as Vibrio cholerae [28, 29], Mycobacterium tuberculosis [30], Streptomyces albidoflavus [31], Bartonella henselae/quintana [32], Pseudomonas aeruginosa [33], and Streptococci [34]. While these studies showed that many tRNA modifications and their biosynthetic enzymes are broadly conserved across bacteria, they revealed species-specific modification patterns, underscoring the need for more systematic investigations in additional bacterial species across phylogeny. However, these studies remained rather global, combining approaches such as nucleoside mass spectrometry, deep-sequencing, genetic validations, and bioinformatic predictions. While informative, they do not provide high-confidence modification maps for every individual tRNA, leaving the full diversity and heterogeneity of modification profiles uncompleted. To date, Mycoplasma capricolum remains the only organism in which the complete set of native tRNA sequences has been systematically determined in a single study [35].

We previously reported a partial map of tRNA modifications in Staphylococcus aureus, a major human pathogen responsible for a wide range of diseases [36]. The genome of this Gram-positive bacterium encodes 30 distinct tRNA species decoding all 61 sense codons, with the number of gene copies for each isoacceptor varying between strains [37]. In addition, S. aureus harbors three non-proteogenic tRNAGly species [38, 39] that are not used for protein synthesis [40]. Instead, they serve as substrates for the aminoacyl-tRNA transferases FemX, FemA, and FemB, which catalyze the formation of pentaglycine bridges in the peptidoglycan cell wall [41]. In this work, we combined oligonucleotide mass spectrometry with multiple deep-sequencing methods to generate comprehensive, high-confidence maps of post-transcriptional modifications in each individual S. aureus tRNA species, including the non-proteogenic tRNAGly. This enabled assignment of the corresponding tRNA-modifying enzymes by gene orthology, the analysis of their expression profiles, and the identification of several unexpected modification patterns in S. aureus. By integrating ribosome profiling and Nanopore tRNA abundance measurements, we also provide a global picture of the decoding properties of S. aureus tRNAs and dynamics of codon-decoding.

Materials and methods

Definition of tRNA reference sequences

tRNA sequences used as references in this study were retrieved from the annotated S. aureus HG001 genome (NCBI RefSeq assembly GCF_001900185.1; submitted GenBank assembly GCA_001900185.1) [42]. The number and nucleotide sequences of tRNA genes are identical to those of the parental S. aureus NCTC8325 strain and match the tRNAscan-SE predictions reported in GtRNAdb [37]. Reference sequences correspond to mature tRNAs, with the 3′-CCA added in silico when not genomically encoded. Nucleotide positions are numbered according to the standard tRNA numbering system described in [43]. tRNAs are designated by amino acid and anticodon (i.e. isoacceptor species). When multiple loci encode identical mature sequences, they were grouped as single tRNA species. The S. aureus HG001 genome contains 61 tRNA genes corresponding to 37 unique tRNA sequences, including 34 proteogenic and 3 non-proteogenic tRNAGly(UCC). For tRNAHis(GUG) (2 genes), tRNALeu(UAA) (2 genes), tRNALys(UUU) (3 genes), and tRNASer(UGA) (3 genes), the corresponding genes encode two distinct sequence variants (isodecoders) differing by only few nucleotides. These tRNAs cannot be unambiguously resolved by our analytical workflow and were therefore analyzed as single species. Consequently, 30 proteogenic tRNA species were analyzed. The full correspondence between loci, mature sequences and grouping is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

S. aureus was manipulated in a BioSafety Level 2 Laboratory for class II pathogens. Strain HG001, a derivative of the RN1 (NCTC8325) strain with restored rsbU (SigB activator), was used for tRNA modification analysis by UPLC-MS/MS and deep-sequencing. Strain USA300 JE2 was employed to verify the presence of ms2t6A37, as the mtaB gene responsible for this modification is truncated in HG001. Mutant and complemented ΔdusB2 strains in RN4220 and HG001 backgrounds were used to assess tRNA dihydrouridylation. Glycerol stocks were streaked onto blood agar plates (HG001 and USA300 JE2) or BHI agar plates containing 10 μg/ml chloramphenicol (ΔdusB2 strains) and incubated overnight at 37°C. The composition of the BHI medium is 7.7 g/l calf brain (infusion from 200 g), 9.8 g/l beef heart (infusion from 250 g), 10 g/l proteose peptone, 5 g/l NaCl, 2 g/l dextrose, and 2.5 g/l disodium phosphate (BD Bacto™, 237 500). The following day, single colonies were inoculated into BHI, supplemented with antibiotic when needed, and incubated overnight at 37°C with agitation at 180 rpm. Overnight cultures were then diluted to OD600 nm 0.05 in fresh BHI and grown under the same conditions for approximately 4 h to reach late exponential phase (OD600 nm: 2.0–3.0). Bacteria were harvested by centrifugation at 2100 × g for 10 min at 4°C and stored at −20°C until use. The culture volume was adjusted according to RNA requirements, with 1 L cultures in 5-L flasks used for large-scale RNA preparations and 25 ml cultures in 100-ml flasks for smaller preparations.

Generation of ΔdusB2 mutants and complementation

The dusB2 gene in S. aureus RN4220 (WKU35_RS00190) and HG001 (BSR30_RS00205) was deleted using the pCasSA CRISPR–Cas9 system as described in [44]. Guide RNA (gRNA) sequences specific to the target gene were designed with the CCTop-CRISPR/Cas9 target online predictor [45]. Primers used for polymerase chain reaction (PCR), cloning, screening, and DNA sequencing are listed in Supplementary Table S2. Construction of the pCasSA-UPDN-gRNA plasmid, carrying both the homologous recombination template (UPDN) and the gRNA, was performed in two steps. First, the 1-kb upstream (UP) and downstream (DN) flanking regions of dusB2 in HG001 were fused by overlap PCR using primers Dus_5UP_F, Dus_5UP_R, Dus_3DN_F and Dus_3DN_R, and the resulting 2-kb UPDN fragment was ligated into the pCasSA vector via XhoI and XbaI restriction sites. Second, primers gRNA_DusB2_F and gRNA_DusB2_R specific to the dusB2 locus were phosphorylated, annealed, and inserted into pCasSA-UPDN by Golden Gate assembly using the two BsaI restriction sites. Cloning was carried out in E. coli TOP10 strain, with selection on LB agar plates containing 50 μg/ml kanamycin at 30°C for 24–36 h. The resulting plasmid pCasSA-UPDN-gRNA (1–5 μg) was first electroporated (2.5 kV, 5 ms) into S. aureus RN4220 and subsequently into HG001 to generate the ΔdusB2 mutant strains. Transformants were selected on TSB agar plates supplemented with 10 μg/ml chloramphenicol at 30°C for 24–36 h. Several colonies were inoculated into 3 ml TSB containing 10 μg/ml chloramphenicol and incubated overnight at 30°C under agitation. The following day, 3 μl of each culture were diluted into 3 ml BHI without antibiotic, incubated at 42°C for 7 h, and streaked onto TSB agar plates with or without 5 μg/ml chloramphenicol to confirm plasmid curing. Genomic DNA was then extracted, and clones carrying the desired gene deletion were identified by PCR using primers Dus_up and Dus_down and further confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Complementation of ΔdusB2 mutants was achieved using plasmid pCN38-DusB2, which carries the dusB2 gene along with 388 nucleotides upstream and 100 nucleotides downstream the coding sequence. Primers SphI_P_DusB2_F and KpnI_TT_DusB2_R were used to amplify the gene and add restriction sites for cloning. The empty plasmid pCN38, conferring chloramphenicol resistance, was also transformed in the WT strains and ΔdusB2 mutants.

Total RNA extraction

RNA extraction was performed from bacterial pellets obtained from either 25- or 500-ml cultures. Parameters indicated in parentheses correspond to large-scale RNA preparations. Bacteria were resuspended in 0.5 ml (6 ml) phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and transferred into a 2-ml (50-ml) tube containing 0.6 g (6 g) of Lysis Matrix B (MP Biomedicals) and 0.5 ml (6 ml) of acidic phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol 25:24:1 (PCI). Cells were mechanically disrupted for 40 s at 6 m/s using the FastPrep-24 system (MP Biomedicals), and the tubes were centrifuged at 16 000 × g (8370 × g) for 10 min at 4°C to remove beads and cell debris. The aqueous phase was transferred to a new 1.5-ml (50-ml) tube and re-extracted with an equal volume of acidic PCI by vortexing for 10 s. After centrifugation under the same conditions, the upper aqueous phase was collected and extracted sequentially with an equal volume of cold chloroform and then chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (19:1). RNA was precipitated by adding 1/10 volume of 3 M sodium acetate (pH 5.2) and 3 volumes cold absolute ethanol, followed by incubation overnight at −20°C. RNA was pelleted by centrifugation at 16 000 × g (8370 × g) for 30 min (45 min) at 4°C, washed with 0.5 ml (5 ml) cold 80% ethanol, air-dried for 20 min (1 h) at 20°C, and resuspended in 50 μl (500 μl) milli-Q water.

Purification of bulk tRNA

Anion exchange chromatography was used to enrich tRNA, removing most of the ribosomal RNA and DNA. Total RNA (up to 6 mg) was applied to a 1-ml Fast Flow DEAE column (Cytiva) equilibrated in 100 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.4). The column was washed with 10 ml of equilibration buffer, followed by 10 ml of the same buffer supplemented with 100 mM NaCl, and bound RNA was eluted using a linear NaCl gradient from 100 to 1000 mM. Fractions enriched in tRNA, identified by agarose gel electrophoresis, were pooled, ethanol-precipitated, and resuspended in 400 μl of milli-Q water. Alternatively, size-exclusion chromatography was used to prepare purer tRNA from small-scale RNA preparations. Total RNA was first treated with DNase I to remove residual DNA. The reaction was carried out in a final volume of 100 μl containing 50 U of RNase-free DNase I (Roche) in the appropriate buffer and incubated for 2 h at 20°C. DNA-free RNA was subsequently extracted with an equal volume of acidic PCI, followed by chloroform, ethanol-precipitated, and resuspended in 400 μl of milli-Q water. The RNA sample (up to 1.5 mg) was applied onto a Superose 6 10/300 column (Cytiva) equilibrated and eluted at 0.4 ml/min with 300 mM ammonium acetate (pH 6.8). Fractions containing pure tRNA were pooled, ethanol-precipitated, and resuspended in 20–50 μl of milli-Q water.

Chromatographic fractionation of tRNA species

RNA was extracted from five bacterial pellets obtained from 500-ml cultures harvested at late exponential phase. DNA and ribosomal RNA were first removed by DEAE anion exchange chromatography, and total tRNA was subsequently purified by size-exclusion chromatography under the conditions described above. All tRNA preparations were pooled (3 mg total) and applied to a DeltaPak C4 column (300 Å, 15 μm, 7.8 mm × 300 mm, Waters) equilibrated with buffer A (10 mM sodium phosphate pH 7.0, 1 M sodium formate, and 8 mM MgCl2). The column was washed with 7 ml of equilibration buffer and bound tRNA was eluted using a linear gradient of buffer B (10 mM sodium phosphate pH 7.0, 20% methanol) from 0 to 100%. Fractions of 1 ml were collected, and 20 μl of each were dried under vacuum for 30 min using a SpeedVac centrifuge (Savant), resuspended in 8 μl of denaturing loading buffer (95% formamide, 0.025% xylene cyanol, and 0.025% bromophenol blue), and analyzed by electrophoresis on a 12% denaturing urea–polyacrylamide gel in 1× TBE buffer. The remaining portions of each fraction were precipitated by adding 100 μl of 5 M ammonium acetate (pH 5.3) and 1 ml of isopropanol, followed by incubation overnight at −20°C. RNA was recovered by centrifugation at 16 000 × g for 45 min at 4°C, washed twice with cold 80% ethanol, and air-dried for 20 min at 20°C. Fractions with similar electrophoretic profiles were pooled by sequentially resuspending the corresponding pellets in 15 μl of milli-Q water.

Cyanoethylation of tRNA

Acrylonitrile treatment was performed based on the optimal conditions described in [46]. The reaction mixture contained 100 μl of 41% ethanol/1.1 M triethylammoniumacetate (pH 8.6), 5–13 μl of tRNA (∼100 μg), and 12 μl of acrylonitrile (Sigma–Aldrich). The mixture was incubated at 70°C for 2 h, and the reaction was stopped by adding 12 μl of 5 M ammonium acetate (pH 5.3) and 300 μl of cold absolute ethanol. After incubation overnight at −20°C, the precipitated tRNA was collected by centrifugation at 16 000 × g for 30 min at 4°C, washed with 80% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in 20 μl of milli-Q water.

Two-dimensional urea–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis

The first dimension was performed on a denaturing 15% polyacrylamide gel in 1× TBE buffer containing 8.3 M urea, cast between two 30 × 40 cm glass plates with 1-mm spacers and a 15-well comb forming wells 1.5 mm deep and 0.75 mm wide. The gel was pre-run for 30 min at 900 V using a PowerPac 3000 power supply (Bio-Rad). Samples were prepared in a total volume of 8 μl containing 15–20 μg of tRNA in the presence of 47.5% formamide, 0.0125% xylene cyanol, and 0.0125% bromophenol, and heated for 2 min at 95°C prior to loading. Electrophoresis was carried out in 1× TBE running buffer at 13 W for 24 h at 20°C using an S2 sequencing gel electrophoresis apparatus (BR Life Technologies). The gel was stained with ethidium bromide, visualized under UV light using a BenchTop UVP transilluminator (Fisher scientific), and the lane containing tRNA was excised and soaked in water. The second dimension was performed using a semi-denaturing 20% polyacrylamide gel in 1× TBE buffer containing 4.15 M urea, cast between two 16 × 20 cm glass plates compatible with the Protean II xi Cell electrophoresis apparatus (Bio-Rad). The excised 1D lane was placed horizontally at the top and between the two plates before pouring the gel solution to embed it after polymerization. Electrophoresis was then performed in 1× TBE running buffer at 6W/gel for 24 h at 20°C. The gel was visualized under UV light, photographed using a Gel Doc EZ imager (BioRad), and the individual spots were excised, transferred into 1.5 ml tubes, dried for 30 min at 20°C in SpeedVac centrifuge (Savant), and stored at −20°C.

Purification of individual tRNAs

Individual S. aureus tRNAs were purified using specific biotinylated probes (Supplementary Table S2). tRNALys(UUU) and tRNAThr(UGU) were each purified from 750 μg of bulk tRNA isolated from the USA300 JE2 strain. Proteogenic tRNAGly(UCC) P1, as well as non-proteogenic tRNAGly(UCC) NP1 + NP2 mixture and NP3, were purified from 2000 μg of bulk tRNA isolated from an RN4220 strain lacking one gene copy of proteogenic tRNAGly(GCC) P1 [40]. Bulk tRNA was prepared by DEAE anion-exchange chromatography and dissolved in buffer 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 0.9 M tetraethylammonium chloride, and 0.1 mM EDTA. For preparation of the affinity column, 200 μl of streptavidin sepharose resin (Cytiva) were washed with water (10 × 200 μl), equilibrated in 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5) and 150 mM NaCl (10 × 200 μl), and incubated with 100 μl of 100 μM biotinylated probe for 30 min at 37°C under agitation. Following washing with 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5) (10 × 200 μl), the tRNA sample was applied to the resin and incubated at 37°C for 1 h under agitation for tRNALys(UUU) and tRNAThr(UGU), or overnight with continuous recirculation using a peristaltic pump for all tRNAGly(UCC) P1, NP1 + NP2, and NP3. The resin was washed with 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5) (4 × 500 μl), and tRNAs were eluted four times with 200 μl of the same buffer preheated to 65°C, incubating for 5 min each time. Fractions were pooled, ethanol-precipitated and resuspended in water.

LC-MS/MS oligonucleotide mass spectrometry

Gel spots were digested following different protocols depending on the RNase used. For RNase T1 digestions, 50 μl of 100 mM ammonium acetate (pH 6.8) containing 1U/μl RNase T1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to each spot and incubated at 50°C for 3 h, then at 37°C under agitation (300 rpm) for 9 h. For RNase A digestions, the same conditions were used, except that 0.01 μg/μl RNase A (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used. For RNase U2 digestions, 45 μl of 250 mM ammonium acetate (pH 4.7) was added to each spot and incubated at 50°C for 1 h, after which 5 μl of RNase U2 (0.01 mg/ml in the same buffer) was added, and incubation was continued for 30 min. Digestion in solution was performed in a final volume of 20 μl containing 100–500 ng of tRNA under the conditions described above.

Samples were incubated at 95°C for 2 min, and on ice for 3 min prior to desalting using ZipTip C18 pipette tips (0.2 μl resin bed, Merck Millipore). The resin was activated with 50% acetonitrile (5 × 10 μl), washed with milli-Q water (5 × 10 μl), and equilibrated in 200 mM ammonium acetate (pH 6.8). The digestion reaction was passed 30 times through the ZipTip, the resin was sequentially washed with 200 mM ammonium acetate (10 × 10 μl) and milli-Q water (10 × 10 μl), and the bound RNase-derived oligonucleotides were finally eluted with 50% acetonitrile (2 × 10 μl) into an HPLC vial insert (Fisher Scientific). The vial was placed into a 1.5 ml tube, and the solution was dried for 30 min at 20°C in SpeedVac centrifuge (Savant). The pellet was resuspended in 3 μl of milli-Q water and injected onto an Acquity UPLC peptide BEH C18 column (130 Å, 1.7 μm, 75 μm × 200 mm) equilibrated with buffer containing 7.5 mM triethylammonium acetate, 7 mM triethylamine, and 200 mM hexafluoroisopropanol using a nanoAcquity UPLC system (Waters). Oligonucleotides were eluted at 300 nl/min, first with a 15%–35% methanol gradient over 2 min, followed by a 35%–50% methanol gradient over 20 min. Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) was performed using a SYNAPT G2-S instrument (Waters) equipped with a NanoLockSpray-ESI source operated in negative mode, with a capillary voltage set to 2.6 kV and a sample cone of 30 V. The source temperature was set to 130°C. MS analysis was performed over an m/z range from 500 to 1500, followed by fast data-dependent acquisition (FastDDA) of MS/MS scans using collision-induced dissociation (CID) for fragmentation. MS/MS data were processed with MassLynx 4.1 (Waters). Multi-charged CID spectra were deconvoluted into single-charged spectra using the MaxEnt3 tool and manually sequenced by following the typical fragmentation ion series [47]. Correct spectra interpretation was verified using the MongoOligo online calculator (https://mstoolbox.github.io/).

LC-MS/MS nucleoside mass spectrometry

Digestion to nucleosides was performed for 2 h at 37°C in 20-μl reactions containing 0.6 U nuclease P1 from Penicillium citrinum (Sigma–Aldrich), 0.2 U snake venom phosphodiesterase from Crotalus adamanteus (Worthington), 10 U benzonase (Sigma–Aldrich), 0.2 U bovine intestinal phosphatase (Sigma–Aldrich), and 200 ng pentostatin (Sigma–Aldrich) in 5 mM Tris–HCl pH 8 and 1 mM MgCl2. LC-MS/MS analysis was carried out on an Agilent 1260 LC series chromatography system coupled to an Agilent 6460 Triple Quadrupole mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ion source (ESI). Nucleosides were separated at 35°C on a Synergi Fusion RP18 column (80 Å, 4 μm, 2.0 mm × 250 mm; Phenomenex) equilibrated in buffer A (5 mM ammonium acetate pH 5.3) and eluted with buffer B (acetonitrile) at 0.35 ml/min. For analysis of total tRNA, 6 μg were digested and 1 μg of the hydrolysate was spiked with 50 ng of E. coli 13C-labeled RNA prior to chromatographic separation using a linear gradient of buffer B from 0% to 10% over 20 min, 10% to 25% over 10 min, 25 to 80% over 10 min, 80 to 0% over 3 min, and 100% buffer A for 11 min. For analysis of purified tRNAGly, 450 ng were digested, and 400 ng of each hydrolysate were spiked with 50 ng of yeast 13C-labeled RNA prior to separation with a linear gradient of buffer B from 0% to 8% over 10 min, from 8% to 40% over 10 min, 40% to 0% over 3 min, and 100% buffer A for 7 min. The UV signal at 254 nm was recorded using a diode array detector. The ESI parameters were the following: gas temperature 350°C, gas flow 8 l/min, nebulizer pressure 50 psi, sheath gas temperature 350°C, sheath gas flow 12 l/min, capillary voltage 3000 V, and nozzle voltage 0 V. The mass spectrometer was run in positive mode, and dynamic multiple reaction monitoring (dMRM) was performed using Agilent MassHunter software. Absolute quantification was carried out using external calibrations with synthetic standards and internal calibration with 13C-labeled RNA spiked into the samples as described in [48]. Raw data were converted to open mzML format using ProteoWizard MSconverter and the resulting files were processed using custom Python scripts employing Pyteomics. The area under the curve (AUC) for each 12C and 13C nucleoside was computed and the ratio AUC(12C)/AUC(13C) was used to generate calibration curves. The tRNA amount was estimated from the UV signal of adenosine, assuming an average of 16 adenosine residues per tRNA for analysis of total tRNA. Relevant parameters for dMRM acquisition, sample lists, AUC values, and quantification results are provided in Supplementary Data S4 for analysis of total tRNA, and in Supplementary Data S5 for analysis of purified tRNAGly species.

Analysis of tRNA modifications by next-generation sequencing

RiboMethSeq

Detection of 2′-O-ribomethylations (Nm) by RiboMethSeq is based on the resistance of the phosphodiester bond following the Nm residue to alkaline hydrolysis. The analysis was performed essentially as previously described [49]. Briefly, total tRNA (150 ng) was subjected to alkaline hydrolysis (pH 9.4) followed by ethanol precipitation. Fragments were end-repaired and ligated to adapters using the NEBNext Small RNA kit for Illumina. Sequencing was performed on a NextSeq2000 instrument. After removal of adapter sequences by trimmomatic v0.39, reads were mapped to S. aureus HG001 tRNA sequences using bowtie2 in end-to-end mode. Reads’ 5′- and 3′-ends were counted and the different RiboMethSeq scores were calculated [50]. Only MethScore values associated with ScoreMEAN > 0.92 and ScoreA > 0.5 were considered as relevant.

AlkAnilineSeq

Detection of m7G and D by AlkAnilineSeq relies on aniline-mediated scission at the abasic site that arises from nucleobase loss following cleavage of the fragile N-glycosidic under alkaline, high-temperature conditions. The analysis was performed as previously described [51]. Briefly, tRNA (100 ng) was subjected to fragmentation under mild alkaline conditions (5 min, 96°C) followed by ethanol precipitation. Fragments were end-repaired and then subjected to aniline treatment, resulting in deprotection of a 5′-phosphate at the N + 1 nucleotide, which serves for selective ligation of sequencing adapters. RNA fragments were converted to libraries and sequenced as described above. Trimmed reads were aligned to tRNA sequences and the reads’ 5′-ends were counted after applying a −1 shift. Two scores were used for data analysis: normalized cleavage score, which represents the 1000× proportion of reads starting at a given position to the total number of reads mapped to the corresponding tRNA sequence, and stop ratio, which is calculated as the ratio of reads starting at a given position to the total number of reads covering it.

HydraPsiSeq

Detection of Ψ and 5-substituted U by HydraPsiSeq is based on their resistance to hydrazine cleavage, while unmodified U are efficiently cleaved. The analysis was performed as previously described [52]. In brief, tRNA (200 ng) was subjected to hydrazine cleavage, followed by aniline cleavage of the phosphodiester bond at all unmodified U. Fragments were converted to libraries and sequenced as described above. After trimming, reads were mapped on the tRNA sequences and HydraPsiSeq scores were calculated from the normalized U-profile. Only Ψ-Score values associated with ScoreMEAN > 0.92 and ScoreA > 0.5 were considered as relevant.

BIDseq

Detection of Ψ by BIDseq relies on the deletion signature induced by a bisulfite-Ψ adduct. This analysis was performed following an adapted version of the original BIDseq protocol [53]. Briefly, tRNA (100 ng) was subjected to fragmentation followed by bisulfite treatment and desulfonation as previously described [54]. Treated and untreated RNAs were converted to libraries and sequenced as described above. After trimming, reads were mapped on the tRNA sequences allowing to retain 1–3 nt gapped reads. Further analysis was performed using samtools’s mpileup utility to count the deletion score at every position in the reference.

GLORIseq

Detection of N6-substituted A is based on selective deamination of unmodified A, which is performed on an RNA sample treated with glyoxal to protect G from nitrite-induced deamination. This is followed by deprotection and reverse transcription to convert RNA into complementary DNA. The conditions used were as described in [55]. In brief, tRNA (100 ng) was subjected to glyoxal treatment (30 min, 50°C) followed by boric acid treatment (30 min, 50°C). The protected RNA was then subjected to nitrite-mediated deamination, deprotected in 500 mM TEAA/formamide buffer, and ethanol precipitated. Fragments were end-repaired, converted to libraries and sequenced as described above. Trimmed reads were mapped to the A → G converted tRNA sequences allowing 1 nt mismatch. Further analysis was done with samtools’s mpileup utility to count the mismatch profile at every position in the reference. G-to-A mismatches correspond to deamination-resistant modified residues (e.g. m6A, t6A, and i6A). The score value corresponds to the molar ratio of the modified A.

Dihydrouridine quantification

Dihydrouridine was quantified using a colorimetric assay based on the detection of the ureido group generated by ring-opening upon alkali treatment [56]. The assay was performed following the protocol described in [57]. Bulk tRNA was prepared by DEAE anion-exchange chromatography as described above. A total of 100 μg of tRNA in 50 μl of milli-Q water were combined with 5 μl of 1 N KOH and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. The solution was then acidified with 25 μl of concentrated H2SO4, followed by the addition of 50 μl of a freshly prepared 1:1 mixture of 2 mg/ml N-phenyl-p-phenylendiamine and 3% (m/v) 2,3-butanedione monoxime as described in [58]. Samples were incubated at 95°C for 10 min, then at 50°C for 10 min, after which 50 μl of 1 mM FeCl3 in concentrated H2SO4 were immediately added. The reactions were transferred into a 96-well plate, and the absorbances at 550 and 465 nm were measured using a Multiskan SkyHigh microplate reader (Thermo Fisher). The difference between these two readings was correlated with dihydrouridine concentration using a standard curve prepared from multiple concentrations (up to 250 μM) of dihydrouracil (Thermo Fisher).

tRNA dihydrouridylation assay

Synthetic tRNAGly(UCC) NP1 and NP2 were produced as described in [38]. Purification of recombinant S. aureus DusB2 is described in Supplementary Methods. In vitro dihydrouridylation was performed under aerobic conditions in a 50-μl reaction containing 8 μM tRNA and 2 μM DusB2 in 50 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), and 15% glycerol. The reaction was initiated by adding 2 μl of NADPH 50 mM and incubated for 1 h at 37°C. Quenching was performed by sequential extraction with acidic PCI and chloroform, followed by ethanol precipitation. The recovered sample was separated on a denaturing 10% polyacrylamide gel, and the excised tRNA bands were digested with RNase U2 and analyzed by UPLC-MS/MS as previously described.

Protein mass spectrometry

Time course-proteomics analysis was performed in four biological replicates of S. aureus grown in BHI rich medium. Overnight cultures were diluted to an OD600 of 0.05 in 50 ml of BHI (250-ml flasks) and incubated at 37°C with shaking at 180 rpm. Culture volumes corresponding to 10 OD600 units were collected at 2 h (condition A), 3 h (condition B), 4 h (condition C), and 4.5 h (condition D) by centrifugation at 2100 × g for 10 min at 4°C (Supplementary Data S3A). Bacterial pellets were resuspended in 400 μl of buffer UTCT (7 M urea, 2 M thiourea, 4% CHAPS, and 20 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0), transferred into 2 ml tubes containing 0.6 g Lysis Matrix B (MP Biomedicals) and lysed mechanically for 40 s at 6 m/s using a FastPrep-24 system (MP Biomedicals). Tubes were centrifuged at 16 000 × g for 10 min at 4°C to remove beads, and the supernatant was recovered and stored at −20°C.

For mass spectrometry analysis, 10 μg of S. aureus proteins were precipitated with 0.1 M ammonium acetate in 100% methanol (5 volumes, −20°C) to get rid of detergents and other contaminants. After reduction (5 mM DTT, 95°C, 10 min) and alkylation (10 mM iodoacetamide, 20°C, 20 min), proteins were digested overnight with 1:50 (v:v) of sequencing-grade trypsin (Promega). Peptide mixtures were analyzed by nanoLC-MS/MS on a nanoElute2 coupled to a TIMS-TOF Pro 2 mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonik Gmbh). Peptides were first separated on an Aurora Ultimate column (IonOpticks, 25 cm × 75 μM, 1.7 μm particle size, and 120 Å pore size) with a 55 min gradient and analyzed on the mass spectrometer using a data-dependent acquisition (DDA).

Proteins were identified via comparison to a homemade S. aureus protein database (2623 sequences) and analyzed using Mascot algorithm (version 2.8, Matrix Science) with a decoy strategy and 20 and 30 ppm MS and MS/MS tolerances. Oxidation (M), carbamidomethylation (C), acetylation (protein Nter), and phosphorylation (S, T, and Y) were set up as variable modifications. In Proline v2.0 software [59], identified proteins were validated on a Mascot pretty rank equal to 1, a Mascot score threshold of 25, and 1% FDR on both PSM and protein levels; and MS1 eXtracted Ion Chromatograms (XIC) were used to quantify each protein. An exhaustive map alignment followed by a median ratio normalization of the intensities was followed by a 60 s cross assignment within groups only. Prostar (v1.32.3) was used for statistical analyses of intensities. Partially observed values (POV) and values missing in the entire condition (MEC) were imputed with det quantile 1%. A LIMMA statistical test with a log fold change (FC) threshold of 1 and a Benjamini–Hochberg correction were used to generate log2(FC) and adjusted P-values for the following pairwise comparisons: 3 h versus 2 h (B versus A), 4 h versus 2 h (C versus A), 4.5 h versus 2 h (D versus A), 4 h versus 3 h (C versus B), and 4.5 h versus 4 h (D versus C) (Supplementary Data S3B). For comparative analysis of temporal trends and change amplitudes, only log2(FC) values relative to the first condition (2 h) were considered.

Nanopore tRNA sequencing

The relative abundance of the S. aureus tRNA species was estimated by Nanopore tRNA sequencing of bulk tRNA samples purified as described above from cultures at late exponential phase (OD600 2.5–3.0) in three biological replicates. First, 3 μg of tRNA were polyadenylated in a 20 μl reaction containing 0.25 U of E. coli Poly(A) polymerase (NEB), 1 mM ATP, 20 U RNase inhibitor (Promega), 50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.1, 250 mM NaCl, and 10 mM MgCl2, for 30 min at 37°C. The reaction was purified with 1.6 volumes of SPRI beads (Mag-Bind TotalPure NGS, Omega), washed twice with 200 μl of fresh 80% EtOH, and eluted in 10 μl of RNase-free H2O. Polyadenylated tRNAs were then barcoded following the WarpDemux strategy [60]. Custom RTA adapters (10 μM forward, 10 μM reverse) were annealed in 30 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.5), 100 mM K-Acetate by incubation for 1 min at 95°C followed by cooling to 25°C at a rate of 0.5°C/min. The annealed RTAs were then diluted to 0.7 μM and stored at −20°C until use. For each sample, 100 ng of polyA-selected RNA was ligated to 1 μl of 0.7 μM annealed RTA in the presence of 2 μl of 5 × NEBNext Quick Ligation Buffer (NEB), 0.5 μl of T4 DNA ligase high concentration (NEB), and 0.2 μl of RNAsin (Promega) in a final volume of 10 μl by incubation at 22°C for 15 min. To stop the ligation, 2 μl of 0.5 M EDTA was added and samples were pooled and purified with 0.5 volumes of SPRI beads (Mag-Bind TotalPure NGS, Omega Bio-tek), washed twice with 200 μl of fresh 80% EtOH, and eluted in 10 μl of RNase-free H2O. Reverse transcription was performed by adding 8 μl of 5 × Induro buffer, 2 μl of 10 mM dNTP, 2 μl of 10 μM random hexamer oligonucleotides, 2 μl of Induro RT (NEB), 0.5 μl of RNasin in a final volume of 40 μl and incubation 2 min at 22°C (annealing), 15 min at 60°C (reverse transcription) and 10 min at 70°C (heat-inactivation). The reaction was purified with 1.4 volumes of SPRI beads, washed twice with 200 μl of fresh 80% EtOH, and eluted in 10 μl of RNase-free H2O. The sequencing adapter ligation was realized using the SQK-RNA004 library kit (ONT) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 10 μl of purified library were mixed with 6 μl of 5 × NEBNext Quick Ligation Buffer, 5 μl of RLA sequencing adapter (ONT SQK-RNA004), and 3 μl of T4 DNA Ligase high concentration in a final volume of 30 μl, and incubated 15 min at 22°C. The reaction was purified with 0.5 volumes of SPRI beads, washed twice with 100 μl of RNA wash buffer (WSB, ONT SQK-RNA004). The library was then eluted in 32 μl of RNA elution buffer (REB, ONT SQK-RNA004). The library was loaded onto a Promethion (FLO-PRO004RA) flow cell for sequencing. Data acquisition was performed using MinKNOW version 24.11. Basecalling was performed with dorado v0.9.1 using the model parameters rna004_130bps_sup@v5.1.0. Demultiplexing was performed using the demux command of warpdemux v0.4.4. Following basecalling, reads were processed to remove polyA using cutadapt v5.0 with the parameters cutadapt -a A{10} –times 1 -e 0.1 -m 20 –overlap 10, then aligned to tRNA references using bwa (v0.7.18) with the parameters bwa mem -W 13 -k 6 -x ont2d -T 20. Reads corresponding to isodecoders were merged. For each tRNA in each sample, read counts were normalized by the total number of reads to obtain relative abundance.

Ribosome profiling

Ribosome profiling was performed in two biological replicates of S. aureus HG001 grown in rich medium to late exponential phase. Overnight cultures were diluted to an OD600 of 0.05 in 50 ml of BHI (500-mL flasks) and incubated at 37°C with shaking at 180 rpm for 3.6 h, reaching a final OD600 of ≈ 2.6. Cultures were rapidly cooled by swirling in an ice bath for 3 min, divided into 40- and 10-ml fractions, and centrifuged at 2100 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The 10-ml pellets were frozen in liquid nitrogen and used for total RNA sequencing, as previously described [61]. The 40-ml pellets were resuspended in 550 μl of cold lysis buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 50 mM MgCl2, 100 mM NH4Cl, 0.4% Triton X-100, 0.1% Nonidet P-40, 0.1 U/μl DNase I, and 1 mM GMPPNP), transferred to 2-ml tubes containing Lysis Matrix B (MP Biomedicals), and mechanically lysed in FastPrep-24 system (MP Biomedicals) for 40 s at 6 m/s. Following centrifugation at 16 000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, the supernatants containing polysomes were recovered, and their absorbance at 260 nm (10-mm path) was measured using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Ribosome-protected mRNA fragments (RPFs) were generated by treating 40 000 AU of polysomes with 250 U of RNase If (NEB) for 20 min at 37°C under agitation at 800 rpm. The reaction was stopped by adding 100 U of SUPERase-In (Thermo Fisher Scientific) prior to separation on an 11-ml 5%–50% sucrose gradient in buffer G (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM MgCl2, 100 mM NH4Cl, and 2 mM DTT) at 39 000 × g for 2 h and 46 min at 4°C. Monosomes were isolated using a piston gradient fractionator (Biocomp) and the RPFs were extracted using hot phenol–chloroform and size-selected on a denaturing 15%-polyacrylamide/8M-urea gel as previously described [61].

Total RNA and RPF samples were RNA-depleted using a Ribo-Seq ribo-POOL kit adapted to S. aureus (siTOOLs Biotech). RPF samples were dephosphorylated with Antarctic phosphatase (NEB) and rephosphorylated with T4 polynucleotide kinase (NEB) prior to library preparation using the NEBNext Small RNA Library Prep Set for Illumina (NEB). For total RNA samples, libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina after RNA fragmentation in 50 mM bicarbonate buffer at 96°C for 8 min. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 1000 instrument and the demultiplexed FASTQ sequencing files were processed as previously described [61], using published and publicly available workflows [62]. Read lengths of 35–51 nucleotides and 15–45 nucleotides were considered for analysis of abundances at the transcriptome and translatome level, respectively. Transcript levels were first provided as RPKM (reads per kilobase of transcript per million read mapped) and then converted to TPM (transcript per million) to enable comparison of relative gene abundance within each dataset [63]. For pausing analysis, only 28-nucleotides length reads were considered, using -13, -16, and -19 shifts for codons in the A-, P-, and E-sites respectively.

Quantification and statistical analyses

Unless otherwise stated, all experiments were performed using independent biological replicates, and the exact number of replicates (n) is indicated in the corresponding Methods sections and figure legends. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (error bar) for modification-specific RNA sequencing, colorimetric quantification of dihydrouridine, and Nanopore tRNA relative abundance (Supplementary Fig. S13) (n = 3). For LC-MS/MS nucleoside quantification of total tRNA, data represent the mean of two independent biological replicates, with individual replicate values shown. LC-MS/MS nucleoside analyses of purified tRNAGly species were performed on individual purified samples, with each tRNA species analyzed by a single LC-MS/MS injection. For ribosome profiling, mRNA abundances in the transcriptome and translatome, translation efficiencies, and codon pause scores at the A-, P-, and E-sites were calculated independently from two biological replicates, and the reported values correspond to the average of these replicates. These analyses were used for descriptive comparisons within a single experimental condition; therefore, no statistical hypothesis testing was performed. Differential protein abundance in the time-course proteomics experiment was assessed using empirical Bayes moderated LIMMA (Prostar v1.32.3).

Results

tRNA modification profiling by integrating mass spectrometry and deep-sequencing approaches

We previously developed a method for identifying tRNA modifications within their sequence context using ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) [64]. In this strategy, bulk tRNAs are fractionated by two-dimensional polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (2D-PAGE), and the excised spots are digested with a specific RNase to generate oligonucleotides for UPLC-MS/MS analysis. Application of this method to S. aureus, analyzing RNase T1 (G-specific) and RNase A (C/U-specific) digests, enabled partial mapping of modifications in 24 of the 30 proteogenic tRNA species, with an average sequence coverage of 60% [36]. However, this strategy did not allow detection of Ψ as this abundant modification is isobaric with U. To overcome these limitations and achieve a comprehensive map of modifications in all S. aureus tRNA species, we refined the method by incorporating additional steps (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S1). Bulk tRNAs were first separated by reversed-phase chromatography to enrich low-abundance species, and the resulting fractions were treated with acrylonitrile prior to 2D-PAGE to generate stable cyanoethylated (ce) Ψ sites [46, 65]. Interestingly, 4-thiouridine (s4U) was also labeled, but only when the reaction was performed before electrophoresis (Fig. 1B). These data suggesting that s4U had been artificially lost in our previous study, most likely due to gel exposure to UV light [66]. Other modifications were also cyanoethylated, including lysidine (k2C) (Fig. 1E), inosine (I), 5-methoxyuridine (mo5U), queuosine (Q), 5-methylaminomethyluridine (mnm5U), and the 2-thiolated mnm5s2U (Supplementary Table S3). Near full-sequence coverage was achieved by including digestion with RNase U2 (purine specific) [67], which mainly cleaves after A, leaving 2′, 3′ cyclic phosphate termini (>p) and yielding a mixture of shorter and longer fragments due to frequent missed cleavages. This facilitated the analysis of G- and U-rich tRNA regions poorly resolved by RNase T1 and RNase A, such as the 5′ termini (Fig. 1B), the D loop (Fig. 1C), and the TΨC stem (Fig. 1G). UPLC-MS/MS results are compiled in Supplementary Data S1, which provides, for each tRNA, a representative set of deconvoluted MS/MS spectra showing sequence coverage and detected modifications.

Our UPLC-MS/MS modification mapping was complemented with several deep-sequencing approaches (Fig. 1A), enabling detection of specific modifications across all tRNA sequences (Supplementary Data S2). This was particularly important for methylations, as oligonucleotide mass spectrometry is unable to determine the exact location of methyl groups within the nucleotide. RiboMethSeq (RMS) [49] confirmed 2′-O-methylations (Nm) (Fig. 1D) and the extracted reverse transcriptase (RT) misincorporation signatures corroborated s4U sites (Fig. 1B). AlkAnilineSeq (AAS) [51, 68] readily detected 7-methylguanosine (m7G) and supported UPLC-MS/MS assignments, which displayed a characteristic 165 Da neutral loss (Fig. 1F). It also responded to several D positions (Fig. 1C) and k2C, the latter likewise associated with a typical neutral loss in UPLC-MS/MS (Fig. 1E). GLORIseq [55] detected several N6-modified adenosines, such as N6-methyladenosine (m6A) (Fig. 1E), N6-isopentenyladenosine (i6A), and N6-threonylcarbamoyladenosine (t6A). It also detected 1-methyladenosine (m1A), with a signal likely arising from sub-stoichiometric Dimroth rearrangement into m6A [69], as evidenced by systematic lower scores (Fig. 1C). For assignment of Ψ sites, distinct sequencing protocols were combined, as cyanoethylation in our conditions produced several false positives (Supplementary Table S3). HydraPsiSeq (HPS) [52] detects Ψ and 5-modified uridines, such as m5U, mnm5U, and mo5U, based on their resistance to the hydrazine cleavage, quantified by a Ψ-score [50] (Fig. 1D and G). BIDseq [53] uses bisulfite to generate an adduct producing a deletion signature at Ψ sites (Fig. 1D and G). Furthermore, Nanopore-direct RNA sequencing, primarily used here to quantify tRNA abundance, also provided additional information about modified positions, including Ψ sites (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Repertoire of modifications and enzymes in proteogenic tRNAs

Our integrative analysis provided a high-confidence map of tRNA modifications in S. aureus under rich medium conditions, covering all 30 proteogenic tRNA isoaceptors (Fig. 2, Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. S3). Here, tRNAHis(GUG), tRNALeu(UAA), tRNALys(UUU), and tRNASer(UGA), each existing as two sequence variants differing by only a few nucleotides, were treated as single tRNA species. As these isodecoders could not be resolved by our experimental approach, their modification profiles were determined at the pooled species level and are shown identically (Fig. 2 ). With the repertoire of modifications established, the corresponding enzymes were assigned based on literature data and FoldSeek-based ortholog predictions [70] (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table S4). The genomic context of the identified enzymes in the S. aureus HG001 strain is shown in Supplementary Fig. S4.

Within the tRNA core region six modified nucleotides were identified: s4U, D, m5U, and Ψ present in most tRNAs, while m1A and m7G appeared only in distinct specific subsets (Fig. 3A). All proteogenic tRNAs contained the two highly conserved m5U54 and Ψ55 in the TΨC-loop [71], likely installed by the predicted TrmFO [72] and TruB [73] orthologs, respectively. In the D-loop of nearly all tRNAs, D was found at positions 17, 20, and 20a, except for tRNAGly(UCC) and tRNALeu(GAG) lacking U at these sites. In the junction between the acceptor- and D-stems, s4U8, likely introduced by the putative ThiI ortholog, was detected in 21 tRNAs, while the remaining 9 contained an unmodified U. In the D-arm, m1A22 was found in 12 tRNAs spanning the seven isoacceptor families reported in previous studies [74], all of which carry a purine at position 13, a strict requirement for the activity of the corresponding enzyme TrmK [75]. Consistent with this, tRNAArg(CCG), tRNAAsp(GUC), and tRNAGly(UCC) containing an U13:A22 base pair lacked this modification. In the variable region, 14 tRNAs carried m7G46, potentially installed by the predicted TrmB ortholog, within a 5-nucleotide loop in which only G46 and U47 were strictly conserved. All of these species contain a C13:G22 base pair known to interact with m7G46, stabilizing the tRNA structure [76]. In contrast, all other non-substrates of TrmB either lacked G46 or exhibited shorter or longer variable regions.

A greater diversity of modifications was identified in the anticodon loop, specifically at positions 34 and 37 (Fig. 3A), with only initiator tRNAfMet(CAU) and tRNASer(GGA) lacking any modification in this region. Modifications at position 34 were found in 20 tRNAs and could occur on any nucleoside. The A34- and C34-modifications were restricted to specific tRNAs, each catalyzed by well-conserved stand-alone enzymes: I34 in tRNAArg(ACG) (TadA) [77], k2C34 in tRNAIle(CAU) (predicted TilS) [78], Cm34 in tRNALeu(CAA) (predicted TrmL) [79], and N4-acetylcytidine 34 (ac4C) in elongator tRNAMet(CAU) (predicted TmcAL) [80]. No other tRNA contained A34 and C34 was only unmodified in tRNAArg(CCG) and tRNATrp(CCA). G34 was exclusively modified to queuosine (Q) in tRNAAsn(GUU), tRNAAsp(GUC), tRNAHis(GUG), and tRNATyr(GUA), while remaining unmodified in all other G34-containing tRNAs. In contrast, U34 was always modified, displaying either mo5U or xm5U derivatives. In addition to the six species reported to contain mo5U34 in B. subtilis [81], tRNAGly(UCC) in S. aureus also harbored this modification instead of the usual mnm5U [71]. Among the xm5U modifications, we detected mnm5U in tRNAArg(UCU); mnm5s2U in tRNAGlu(UUC), tRNAGln(UUG), and tRNALys(UUU); cmnm5s2U also in tRNALys(UUU); and cmnm5Um in tRNALeu(UAA), where the putative TrmL likely introduces the 2′-O-ribomethylation. Notably, we did not detect standalone s2U34 in any tRNA species, consistent with previous observations in E. coli and B. subtilis suggesting rapid conversion of s2U-modified tRNAs into xm5U34 derivatives [82, 83]. The synthesis of Q34, mo5U34, and xm5U34 modifications require multiple enzymes (Fig. 3B). The order of the distinct enzymatic reactions within these complex pathways are shown in Supplementary Fig. S5.

Modifications at position 37 are all purine-derived. All seven tRNAs with G37 carried 1-methylguanosine (m1G) at this position, and most of them also contained G36, which is required for efficient m1G synthesis by TrmD [84]. In contrast, A37 could remain unmodified or be modified to m6A, t6A, or i6A (Fig. 2). The m6A37 modification was restricted to tRNAIle(CAU), tRNAMet(CAU), and tRNAVal(UAC), and a putative TrmM ortholog [85] is present in S. aureus. In contrast, t6A and i6A were more broadly distributed, each present in six different tRNAs. In addition to the conserved TsaCBDE enzymes responsible for t6A37 synthesis, we identified a potential TcdA ortholog known to catalyze formation of the cyclic hydantoin form of t6A (ct6A) [86] (Supplementary Fig. S5). This modification was only validated in tRNAThr(UGU) (Supplementary Data S1), but its presence cannot be excluded in other tRNAs as this modification is labile [87]. Also, the methylthiolated t6A form, 2-methylthio-N6-threonylcarbamoyladenosine (ms2t6A), was detected in tRNALys(UUU) when purified from the S. aureus USA300 JE2 strain. Among the tRNAs containing i6A37, likely installed by the predicted MiaA ortholog, tRNALeu(UAA), tRNALeu(CAA), and tRNATyr(GUA) also displayed 2-methylthio-N6-isopentenyladenosine (ms2i6A), which is expected to be introduced by the putative MiaB enzyme. A few low-intensity MS/MS spectra suggested the presence of ms2io6A37 in tRNALeu(UAA) and tRNATyr(GUA) (Supplementary Data S1), but this might be an artifact as no MiaE ortholog could be identified in S. aureus. For the remaining eight tRNAs, A37 was unmodified.

Finally, two Ψ sites were identified in the anticodon stem-loop: Ψ32 in a few tRNAs, and Ψ39 in all tRNAs carrying U39, except for tRNAGln(UUG). However, detection of Ψ at these positions was often challenging (Supplementary Data S2). In tRNAGly(GCC), U32 showed significant HPS Ψ-score (ScoreMean > 0.92 and Score A2 > 0.5) but was not detected by BIDseq, whereas in tRNAPro(UGG), U32 produced a clear BID signal, but its Ψ-score did not meet the detection thresholds. Both HPS and BID signals for U32 in tRNAPhe(GAA) were only moderately convincing. The gene yjbO, responsible for Ψ31 and Ψ32 in B. subtilis [27], is also present in S. aureus (Fig. 3B), but only tRNAPro(UGG) is a common substrate, supporting the presence of Ψ32 for this tRNA. In the case of Ψ39 in tRNAArg(CCG), assignment was based on cyanoethylation and Nanopore C-to-U mismatch (Supplementary Fig. S2), as reliable HPS or BIDseq data could not be obtained, possibly due to the low abundance of this tRNA. Intriguingly, cyanoethylation in tRNALeu(UAA) occurred only at U39, but higher HPS and BID signals were unexpectedly observed at U42 (Fig. 1D), a position not known to be modified by TruA in other bacteria [88].

Distinct expression profiles of tRNA-modifying enzymes

The expression of tRNA modification enzymes in the S. aureus HG001 strain was monitored during exponential growth in rich medium by time-course label-free protein LC-MS/MS (Supplementary Data S3A–C). Protein abundances were expressed as log2 fold-changes relative to the earliest time point (2 h) to facilitate visualization of temporal expression trends and amplitude of changes (Fig. 3B, left heatmap). Most enzymes displayed only modest variations in abundance through the time course (|log2 FC| < 1), although a few exhibited more pronounced changes. Additionally, the relative abundance of these enzymes in the transcriptome and translatome was estimated using TPM-normalized read counts from total RNA sequencing and ribosome profiling at late exponential growth phase (Supplementary Fig. S6). TPM values corresponding to tRNA-modifying enzymes were scaled between 0 and 1 to facilitate comparison of their abundance within this enzyme set (Fig. 3B, right heatmap). The ratio between RPF and RNA sequencing RPKM reads was calculated to estimate translation efficiency (TE) of each transcript. Despite the limitations of label-free proteomics for quantitative comparisons between distinct proteins within a sample, a positive correlation was observed between protein abundance estimates and TPM-normalized ribosome occupancies at the proteomics condition closest to the ribosome profiling experiment (Supplementary Fig. S7).

Except for MtaB and a truncated form of DusB2 (DusB2-C), all tRNA-modifying enzymes were detected in the time-course proteomics experiment in the HG001 strain, revealing distinct expression profiles. Many enzymes showed highest levels during early exponential growth, and then declined over time. This was the case for ThiI and NifZ, both likely required for s4U8 synthesis [89], consistent with findings in E. coli showing that s4U levels decrease with growth rate [90]. In contrast, other enzymes accumulated later, with their levels either remaining stable or gradually increasing over time. Among these, TmcAL and TrmM, both potentially targeting the anticodon loop of the elongator tRNAMet(CAU), appeared to progressive accumulate over time. Likewise, the putative ms2i6A37 synthesis enzymes MiaA and MiaB showed similar expression trends, together with TrmL, in line with the requirement of ms2i6A37 for TrmL-dependent methylation of position 34 [79, 91].

Distinct expression patterns were also observed among the enzymes involved in complex modification pathways (Supplementary Fig. S5). Synthesis of mo5U34 occurs through a two-step process, involving hydroxylation of U34 to 5-hydroxyuridine (ho5U) followed by subsequent methylation. The first step is catalyzed independently by two types of hydroxylases, TrhP1/P2 and the O2-dependent TrhO, which displayed distinct expression profiles. While the accumulation of the putative TrhP1 and TrhP2 proteins showed only modest variations throughout growth, the levels of the predicted TrhO enzyme decreased markedly over time (Fig. 3B), likely reflecting reduced O2 availability at higher cell density. In contrast, TrmR, the enzyme expected to catalyze the final methylation step, appeared to progressively accumulate during growth. Similarly, among the enzymes involved in Q34 synthesis, the two putative reductases QueG (cobalamin-dependent) and QueH (Fe-dependent) [92], which redundantly catalyze the final step of the pathway in S. aureus, displayed distinct expression profiles, possibly reflecting differences in the availability of their distinct cofactors, with QueH being more translated than QueG at late exponential phase.

To gain insight into the mechanistic basis of the expression patterns, we extended our proteomics and ribosome profiling analyses to include neighboring genes within operons encoding tRNA-modifying enzymes (Supplementary Fig. S8). Despite their genomic organization, genes within the same operon often displayed distinct temporal protein level trends, indicating gene-specific regulation. Only the trmD operon, which includes two ribosomal proteins (rpsP and rplS) and one ribosome maturation factor (rimM), showed coordinated expression trends, potentially reflecting shared transcriptional control. However, examination of mRNA abundances and ribosome occupancy revealed differences across these genes, suggesting additional post-transcriptional mechanisms that modulate final protein levels. Furthermore, we observed substantial heterogeneity in the distribution of translation efficiency (TE) values of tRNA-modifying enzymes (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S6), indicating that they are not uniformly translated. Notably, several gene pairs are arranged in close proximity, with very short intergenic regions or even overlapping stop and start codons. Although such organization is typically associated with translational coupling, our data indicate that these gene pairs can exhibit distinct TE. For example, in the nifZ-thiI and queG-trmL loci, the downstream gene is more efficiently translated, whereas the opposite trend is observed for mnmL-mnmM (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S4).

Taken together, these data strongly suggest that the expression of the tRNA-modifying enzymes is adjusted according to the availability of their cofactors and is sensitive to the metabolism adaptation during growth, pointing to the involvement of multiple regulatory layers.

Divergence of dual-specific RNA modification activities

In E. coli, three rRNA-modifying enzymes also act on tRNAs (Fig. 4A). RluF is responsible for Ψ2604 in 23S rRNA and Ψ35 in tRNATyr(GUA) [93], RluA introduces Ψ746 in 23S rRNA and Ψ32 in four tRNA species [94], and RlmN synthesizes m2A2503 in 23S rRNA and m2A37 in six tRNAs [26]. However, such dual-specific activities do not appear conserved in S. aureus. We did not detect any convincing Ψ35 in tRNAs, which is consistent with the absence of an RluF ortholog in S. aureus. Although Ψ32 was identified in tRNAPro(UGG), the potential S. aureus enzyme YjbO, experimentally linked to this modification in B. subtilis [27], does not seem an RluA ortholog, as it contains an N-terminal S4-like domain absent in E. coli RluA [95] (Fig. 4B, left panel). Moreover, none of the potential YjbO substrates in B. subtilis or S. aureus contained the ΨUXXAAA motif recognized by E. coli RluA [95], and the 23S rRNA position corresponding to Ψ746 in B. subtilis (U793) is not modified despite the presence of the motif [96, 97]. Whether YjbO exhibits dual activities specific to S. aureus remains to be determined.

S. aureus harbors a clear RlmN ortholog and m2A at position 2530 of 23S rRNA (equivalent to 2503 in E. coli) has been experimentally linked to this enzyme [98]. However, our tRNA oligonucleotide UPLC-MS/MS analysis revealed no methylated A37 (Supplementary Fig. S9), except for m6A37 in three tRNA species (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Data S1). Further LC-MS/MS nucleoside quantification confirmed that m2A is essentially absent from tRNA, whereas common modifications such as m5U, Ψ, D, and m7G were detected at levels consistent with their distribution across tRNA species (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Data S4). Since proteomics and ribosome profiling data indicated that RlmN is expressed (Fig. 3B), we asked whether the absence of m2A could be explained by the lack of tRNA features recognized by RlmN (Fig. 4D). Among the S. aureus tRNA species carrying unmodified A37, tRNAAla(UGC), tRNAGly(GCC), tRNAGly(UCC), tRNAfMet(CAU), and tRNASer(GGA) are not RlmN substrates in E. coli, and all except tRNAAla(UGC) possess A38, which is known to abolish the activity of the enzyme in vitro [99]. In contrast, tRNAGln(UUG), tRNAGlu(UUC), and tRNAAsp(GUC) have anticodon stem-loop sequences nearly identical to their E. coli counterparts carrying m2A37 yet remain unmodified by S. aureus RlmN (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. S3). Hence, our data suggest that m2A synthesis in S. aureus may be restricted to 23S rRNA. We then compared the S. aureus RlmN enzyme with the E. coli dual-specific RlmN ortholog and P. aeruginosa TrmV, which introduces m2A specifically in tRNAs [33]. While S. aureus RlmN retains the conserved R204 essential for tRNA recognition (Fig. 4D), it lacked several residues mediating non-sequence-specific tRNA contacts as observed in the crystal structure of E. coli RlmN–tRNAGlu(UCC) complex [100] (Supplementary Fig. S10). Furthermore, analysis of electrostatic surface potential revealed that tRNA-binding regions are strongly positive in both E. coli RlmN and P. aeruginosa TrmV but significantly weaker in S. aureus RlmN, potentially limiting its ability to bind tightly tRNAs (Fig. 4E). However, B. subtilis and Enterococcus faecalis RlmN also exhibit weaker positive binding surfaces (Supplementary Fig. S10), yet m2A has been found in tRNAs from these species [27, 101], indicating that reduced surface positivity alone does not preclude tRNA methylation. The apparent loss of m2A37 in S. aureus tRNA illustrates a more complex evolution of RlmN activity across bacteria, which most probably reflects an adaptation of S. aureus to its ecological niche.

Strain- and tRNA-specific A37 methylthiolation patterns

Among the six S. aureus tRNAs containing the MiaA-dependent i6A37 modification, only tRNALeu(UAA), tRNALeu(CAA), and tRNATyr(GUA) appeared to be MiaB substrates and carried also ms2i6A37 (Fig. 3A). This aligns with the presence of an A31:Ψ39 base pair only in these tRNAs (Fig. 2), which was proposed to be required for i6A37 methylthiolation in B. subtilis tRNAPhe(GAA) and tRNATyr(GUA) [102]. However, UPLC-MS/MS analysis indicated that ms2i6A37 was most prominent in tRNALeu(UAA), as this modification was detected only rarely and at low intensities in tRNALeu(CAA) and tRNATyr(GUA), with most fragments carrying i6A37 (Supplementary Data S1). Comparison of the GLORIseq profiles for these three tRNAs revealed that tRNALeu(CAA) and tRNATyr(GUA) were likely fully modified at position 37, whereas tRNALeu(UAA) displayed markedly lower scores, suggesting sub-stoichiometric i6A37 levels (Fig. 5A). Interestingly, UPLC-MS/MS clearly detected ms2A37 within the anticodon loop of tRNALeu(UAA) (Fig. 5B), implying that S. aureus MiaB can introduce the methylthio group directly onto unmodified A37. Yet, fragments containing only i6A37 were also observed (Supplementary Data S1), supporting the canonical MiaA-to-MiaB pathway for ms2i6A37 formation (Supplementary Fig. S5).

Detection of ms2t6A37 in tRNALys(UUU) was only possible when tRNA was isolated from the S. aureus USA300 JE2 strain, as the corresponding putative mtaB gene in HG001 strain was disrupted by a naturally occurring internal stop codon (Fig. 5C). The same mutation was also identified in the HG003 strain, which, like HG001, was generated through genetic manipulation of the NCTC8325 parental strain carrying an intact mtaB gene [103]. Although protein LC-MS/MS detected expression of the N-terminus of MtaB (Fig. 3B), this truncated protein is very likely inactive, as it lacks most of the tRNA-binding surface as well as one of the two [4Fe–4S] clusters involved in catalysis [104, 105]. Since MtaB was proposed to recognize its substrates through similar determinants as MiaB [102], the presence of ms2t6A37 was assessed in purified S. aureus USA300 JE2 tRNALys(UUU) and tRNAThr(UGU), which are the only species containing t6A37 and A31:Ψ39 base pairs. Like in B. subtilis, only tRNALys(UUU) carried ms2t6A37 (Fig. 5D), while this modification could not be found in tRNAThr(UGU) (Supplementary Data S1). Notably, methylthiolation was detected in tRNALys(UUU) only modified with cmnm5s2U34, but not with mnm5s2U34, suggesting a possible interdependence between modifications at positions 34 and 37 in this tRNA.

A single DusB2 enzyme is responsible for tRNA dihydrouridylation

Among the three Dus families, only DusB is present in Gram-positive bacteria, where it occurs as one or more members of the DusB1, DusB2, or DusB3 subgroups [106]. In S. aureus, two genes are annotated as DusB2, but only one encodes a full-length enzyme (DusB2) containing the catalytic site, whereas the second (DusB2-C) encodes only the C-terminal tRNA binding domain [107, 108] (Fig. 6A). The absence of the catalytic site in the truncated protein, together with its lack of detection in the proteomics analysis (Fig. 3B), suggested that DusB2 is the sole enzyme responsible for D synthesis, prompting us to generate a ΔdusB2 mutant to test this hypothesis.

Deletion of dusB2 in RN4220 and HG001 strains caused a strong reduction of D levels in bulk tRNA, as quantified by a colorimetric assay [57], which were restored to near wild-type levels upon complementation with a plasmid carrying the dusB2 gene (Fig. 6B). To determine which specific D sites were affected, individual tRNAs were isolated by 2D-PAGE, digested with RNase U2, and analyzed by UPLC-MS/MS. In the absence of DusB2, tRNALys(UUU), which normally carries D17 and D20, yielded only unmodified D-loop fragments (Fig. 6C), and tRNAGlu(UUC) was likewise devoid of D at positions 20 and 20a (Fig. 6D). D17 was also found absent in tRNALeu(UAA) and tRNAVal(UAC), D20 in tRNAAsn(GUU) and tRNAGly(GCC), and D20a in tRNATyr(GUA) (Supplementary Fig. S11). Together, these results indicate that D synthesis in S. aureus tRNAs is mediated by a single multi-site DusB2 able to modify positions 17, 20, and 20a.

Furthermore, our comprehensive mapping of D sites across all S. aureus tRNAs revealed distinct dihydrouridylation patterns, indicating that sequence features within the D-loop determine the sites targeted by DusB2 (Fig. 2). The tRNAs carrying only D17 did not contain any U at positions 20 or 20a, whereas those containing only D20 and/or D20a lacked position 17 or displayed another nucleotide. Notably, tRNAfMet(CAU) and tRNAPro(UGG) exhibited unmodified U17 together with an additional pyrimidine at position 17a, suggesting that this insertion may be a negative determinant for DusB2 modification. In contrast, all other tRNAs with U17, U20, and/or U20a were always modified.

Non-proteogenic tRNAs are sub-stoichiometrically hypomodified

In addition to the two proteogenic tRNAGly(GCC) “P1” and tRNAGly(UCC) “P2”, S. aureus expresses three additional tRNAGly(UCC) species, designated NP1, NP2, and NP3 (Fig. 7A), which are involved in the synthesis of pentaglycine bridges in the peptidoglycan cell wall. These non-proteogenic tRNAs are excluded from translation due to the absence of strong EF-Tu determinants [109] and display several unusual sequence features. Notably, they lack the highly conserved G18-G19 and U55 residues in the D and TΨC loops, respectively, and contain an extra C31-G38 base pair that shortens the anticodon loop [110]. Moreover, the NP3 species is characterized by a shortened three-base-pair D-stem due to unpaired U10 and U25 [38].

UPLC-MS/MS analysis of S. aureus HG001 non-proteogenic tRNAs revealed the presence of only two modifications, s4U8 and D20, which were not uniformly distributed. NP1 and NP2, differing by a single nucleotide, could not be separated by 2D-PAGE, and the resulting gel spot (IV-09, Supplementary Fig. S1) contained a mixture of both species. In contrast, tRNAGly(UCC) NP3, carrying more sequence differences, was resolved as a separate spot (IV-13, Supplementary Fig. S1). While s4U8 was identified in both samples, D20 was only detected in the NP1 + NP2 mixture, with fragmentation spectra indicating coexistence of modified and unmodified fragments (Fig. 7B). This pattern suggests that either one of the two species, NP1 or NP2, is modified by DusB2, or that both are partially modified. To resolve this ambiguity, we performed an in vitro assay using recombinant DusB2 with synthetic NP1 or NP2 substrate, and subsequent UPLC-MS/MS analysis indicated that only NP2 was dihydrouridylated (Fig. 7C).

As HPS and BIDseq were inefficient in detecting Ψ in non-proteogenic tRNAGly(UCC) species (Supplementary Data S2), we purified the NP1 + NP2 mixture and NP3 using specific biotinylated probes and assessed the presence of this modification by LC-MS/MS nucleoside quantification (Supplementary Data S5). As a control, the same analysis was performed on the proteogenic tRNAGly(UCC) P2. These tRNAs were isolated from an edited S. aureus RN4220 strain lacking one gene copy of tRNAGly(GCC) P1, which expresses higher levels of the non-proteogenic species [40] and reduces potential contamination from P1. This analysis confirmed that Ψ was present at stoichiometric levels only in P2, but not in the NP1 + NP2 mixture (Fig. 7D). Although higher levels of Ψ were detected in NP3, this likely reflects contamination with proteogenic tRNAs, as indicated by the concurrent detection of similar levels of m5U. Consistent with our mapping data in the HG001 strain, relevant levels of D were detected in the NP1 + NP2 sample, but not in P2. Unexpectedly, substantial amounts of D were also observed in NP3 from the edited RN4220 strain, contrasting with our data in HG001 indicating unmodified D-loop fragments in tRNAGly(UCC) NP3 (Supplementary Fig. S12A and B). Further oligonucleotide UPLC-MS/MS analysis of the purified NP3 confirmed the presence of D20, albeit at sub-stoichiometric levels, as CID-MS/MS spectra indicated a mixture of modified and unmodified fragments (Fig. 7E). Interestingly, nucleoside quantification also revealed partial modification with mo5U34 in tRNAGly(UCC) P2, and with s4U8 in all samples, especially for NP3, which also contrast with our UPLC-MS/MS mapping in the HG001 strain.

Altogether, these results indicate that non-proteogenic tRNAGly(UCC) species exhibit minimal and heterogenous modification profiles, with s4U8 present in all species and D20 potentially restricted to specific tRNAs. This is supported by comparative analysis of non-proteogenic tRNAGly(UCC) sequences from other Staphylococci, indicating that U20 is not strictly conserved (Supplementary Fig. S12C). Finally, tRNA quantification by Nanopore sequencing revealed that non-proteogenic tRNAGly(UUC) species were among the less abundant tRNA species in S. aureus HG001, with NP3 being the rarest of the three tRNAs (Supplementary Fig. S13).

Codon decoding profiles and tRNA abundance

Inspection of the decoding capacities of the S .aureus tRNA set indicates that multiple codon boxes are decoded by single tRNA species (Fig. 8A), highlighting an important role of tRNA modifications in expanding their decoding capacities. Only the 4-fold degenerate Gly-GGN, Leu-CUN, Arg-CGN, and Ser-UCN boxes, as well as the split Leu-UUN box, are decoded each by two distinct isoacceptors. A special case is the Ile/Met box, which requires two tRNA species to decode the three Ile codons. Notably, superwobbling [111] appears to occur for Ala, Pro, Thr, and Val, since a single tRNA carrying mo5U34 is enough to decode the four synonymous codons for these amino acids. To assess whether these predicted expanded decoding capacities are reflected in vivo, we analyzed the relationship between tRNA decoding rates, tRNA abundance, and codon usage in S. aureus under optimal rich medium conditions.

The S. aureus tRNA decoding rates were investigated using codon-resolution ribosome profiling [61]. In this approach, translating polysomes are digested with an RNase, and the resulting ribosome-protected mRNA fragments (RPFs) are sequenced and mapped to the coding sequences, revealing ribosome occupancy of codons at the A-, P-, and E-sites. Higher occupancies are generally associated with slower translation or ribosome pausing at these codons. This experiment was performed in the absence of procedures inducing pausing artifacts, such as antibiotic treatment and cell harvesting by filtration, thereby ensuring capture of biologically relevant translation dynamics [112]. Consistently, we did not observe the strongest A- and E-site pauses at Gly and Ser codons, which are typically associated with such technical biases (Supplementary Fig. S13). Here, we focused specifically on A-site pausing, which generally reflects tRNA decoding rates (Fig. 8A).

In rich medium, Ser codons, recognized by three distinct tRNA species, showed the fastest decoding rates. Conversely, the slowest decoding tRNAs were tRNAArg(CCG) and tRNATrp(CCA), both reading single codons. For several Leu codons, strong A-site pauses were also observed, but only for specific codons recognized by the same tRNA. For instance, tRNALeu(GAG) decoded faster CUU than CUC, whereas tRNALeu(UAA) read UUA more efficiently than UUG, despite the presence of tRNALeu(CAA), also recognizing UUG. Although such differences were less evident for most of the other synonymous codons, which were decoded at more similar rates, the fastest codon–anticodon interactions tended to involve either canonical U34-A3 or wobble G34-U3 base pairings. Only tRNAGly(UCC) showed the opposite behavior, decoding GGG faster than GGA through an mo5U34:G3 wobble base pair. These decoding dynamics showed correlation with mRNA usage of codons recognized by the same tRNA, as the most frequent tended to be decoded faster (Fig. 8B). Interestingly, all codons within each of the 4-fold degenerate boxes read by a single tRNA (Ala, Pro, Thr, Val) were decoded at comparable rates, with C-ending codons consistently being the fastest, indicating that superwobble recognition in vivo is very efficient.

Since codon decoding rates are strongly influenced by tRNA availability, S. aureus tRNA species were quantified by Nanopore direct RNA sequencing (Fig. 8C) to relate their abundance to the decoding patterns observed by ribosome profiling. Although no general correlation emerged between tRNA abundance and A-site pausing (Supplementary Fig. S14), the most abundant tRNASer(UGA) corresponded to the fastest-decoded codons, whereas tRNAArg(CCG) was the less abundant, consistent with its markedly slower decoding rate. In contrast, tRNASer(GGA) was detected at very low levels, yet the UCC and UCU codons displayed very high decoding rates in ribosome profiling, suggesting that tRNASer(UGA) may also participate in their decoding through superwobble interaction. Interestingly, this tRNA carries mo5U34 as found for tRNAAla(UGC), tRNAPro(UGG), tRNAThr(UGU), and tRNAVal(UAC), all forming superwobble interactions. Similar discrepancies were observed for Leu-CUN and Gly-GGN boxes, where codons read by the lower-abundant tRNA were decoded as fast as those recognized by the most abundant species. While potential tRNALeu(UAG) superwobbling could account for the differences among Leu-CUN codons, this explanation seems unlikely for Gly-GGN codons, as tRNAGly(GCC) is unable to compensate for tRNAGly(UCC) in vivo [113].

Discussion

The role of tRNA modifications as key regulators of bacterial physiology and pathogenicity, particularly under stress, has only been recognized in recent years [11, 114]. Their dynamic nature in response to environmental cues enabled integration into regulatory networks modulating translation through effects on tRNA stability, aminoacylation, recognition, and codon decoding [115–118]. Yet, our understanding of epitranscriptomic regulation remains limited, as the full repertoire of tRNA modifications and their enzymes is known for only a few bacterial species [119, 120]. In this context, several studies have characterized the global tRNA modification landscapes across diverse bacteria [27–29, 31–34, 121–124], revealing both conserved activities and numerous species-specific features, some with potential therapeutic relevance [75, 125–127]. Motivated by these findings, we performed a detailed analysis in Staphylococcus aureus, using oligonucleotide mass spectrometry to confidently map modifications in each individual tRNA species of this clinically important pathogen.

Although oligonucleotide mass spectrometry provides high-confidence information about the nature and position of modified nucleotides, it has some important limitations [128]. First, it requires isolation of individual tRNA species, which is labor-intensive and can limit detection of rare tRNAs. Second, it cannot distinguish between isobaric modifications such as different methylated nucleotides or U and Ψ. Even with improvements made to address these challenges, comprehensive and reliable mapping of tRNA modifications in S. aureus would not have been possible without integrating deep-sequencing approaches designed to detect specific types of modifications. The complementarity of the two methods is exemplified by the low abundance tRNAIle(CAU), which became accessible through our improved purification method (Fig. 1E). Alone, mass spectrometry detected a methylated adenosine at position 37, but the assignment of m6A37 can only be inferred from data reported in other organisms [71]. In contrast, GLORIseq, which specifically detects N6-modified adenosines, produced a signal at the same position that could be misinterpreted as t6A, which is more commonly occurring in tRNAIle(CAU). Only by combining the two approaches, we managed to unambiguously assign m6A37, underscoring the importance of integrating orthogonal approaches for accurate modification mapping. This is even more critical for Ψ detection, as illustrated by the challenging assignment of Ψ32 sites in S. aureus, mapped confidently only in tRNAPro(UGG) but remaining tentative for tRNAGly(GCC) and tRNAPhe(GAA). In such cases, the use of mutants of modification enzymes becomes crucial to validate modified positions by deep-sequencing [129, 130]. Furthermore, the integration of the extensive mass spectrometry and deep-sequencing datasets generated here allowed to confidently establish a modification map for tRNALeu(GAG), despite the limited sequence coverage by mass spectrometry.

Hence, our analysis provides a comprehensive map of S. aureus tRNA modifications, which in turn enabled inference of the corresponding modifying enzymes (Supplementary Table S4). Compared to other bacteria, particularly Gram-negative species, S. aureus tRNAs are less extensively modified. Viewed globally, the modified positions are well conserved, with only a few modifications, such as m1A22, mo5U34, and ms2t6A37, characteristic of Gram-positive bacteria [27]. However, our detailed analysis at the level of individual tRNAs, revealed several distinctive features that would otherwise have remained unnoticed. One major difference concerns the modifications introduced by enzymes targeting both tRNA and rRNA in other bacteria. The pseudouridine synthase YjbO, likely responsible for Ψ32 in S. aureus tRNAPro(UGG) and genetically linked to ψ31/ ψ32 in several B. subtilis tRNAs [27, 131], does not appear to be an ortholog of the E. coli dual-specific RluA enzyme, which introduces Ψ32 in tRNAs and Ψ746 in 23S rRNA through recognition of a ψUXXAAA motif. Although this motif is conserved at the equivalent 23S rRNA region in B. subtilis and S. aureus, no ψ modification has been reported at this position, suggesting that YjbO does not recognize the RluA motif [96, 97, 132]. Conversely, the tRNAs reported to contain ψ31/ ψ32 in S. aureus and B. subtilis do not carry the RluA recognition motif, suggesting that YjbO relies on distinct determinants. Structurally, YjbO also differs from RluA by the presence of an N-terminal S4-like domain characteristic of other bacterial pseudouridine synthases targeting rRNA, including RsuA, RluB, and RluC [133]. Together, these observations suggest that YjbO may represent a novel pseudouridine synthase, functioning either specifically on tRNA or as a dual-specific enzyme acting on other rRNA positions or even mRNA [134]. A more comprehensive characterization of S. aureus YjbO, including mutant construction and systematic ψ-profiling, will be necessary to unambiguously determine its substrate specificity. Such approaches may reveal additional tRNA substrates that were not identified in our study due to limitations in ψ detection, as well as the possibility of condition-dependent or partial modification. In this context, yjbO did not display particularly low abundance in our translatome dataset (Supplementary Fig. S6B), suggesting that poor detection of ψ32 in our study is more likely linked to technical aspects.

A more intriguing aspect of tRNA modification in S. aureus involved the methyltransferase RlmN. Although the enzyme is conserved and modifies the same rRNA site as its dual-specific E. coli counterpart [98], it does not appear to install m2A37 in tRNAs. This was initially puzzling as sequence and structural comparisons with dual-specific orthologs suggested that S. aureus RlmN and its potential tRNA substrates possess all features required for tRNA methylation. However, closer examination of the enzyme revealed differences in the conservation of residues mediating non-sequence specific RNA contacts, which may impair tRNA recognition. Nevertheless, similar patterns were observed in the RNA binding surface of RlmN in B. subtilis and E. faecalis, two Gram-positive bacteria in which m2A has been detected in tRNA [27, 101], pointing to additional structural or regulatory features that may compensate for the weaker RNA-binding surface in these organisms. This suggests that S. aureus RlmN may still be able to modify tRNAs, but only under specific conditions, potentially involving increased expression to compensate for its apparently lower tRNA affinity. The unexpected absence of m2A37 in S. aureus tRNAs provides a unique opportunity to investigate the specific role of 23S rRNA m2A2530 in translation, without confounding effects resulting from differential tRNA modification status. Interestingly, a mutation in rlmN gene identified in a clinical S. aureus isolate abolished the m2A2530 modification, had little effect on bacterial growth but increased resistance to linezolid [98]. These findings underscore the importance of understanding the evolutionary dynamics of bacterial RNA-modifying enzymes, as alterations in modification profiles may promote antibiotic resistance with minimal fitness cost, facilitating their rapid dissemination among bacterial populations.

Detailed inspection of individual tRNAs also evidenced heterogenous modification profiles, particularly in tRNALeu(UAA) and tRNALys(UUU). Unlike E. coli, A37 methylthiolation in S. aureus appears to be largely restricted to these two tRNA species, which are likely modified by the predicted MiaB and MtaB orthologs, respectively. Previous studies in Gram-negative bacteria established that ms2i6A37 synthesis by MiaB requires prior i6A37 modification by MiaA [104, 135]; however, here we detected ms2A37 in tRNALeu(UAA), suggesting that MiaB in S. aureus can act on unmodified A37. This notion is supported by the accumulation of ms2A observed in Streptomyces albidoflavus ΔmiaA upon MiaB overexpression [31], which correlates with MiaB being the most abundant tRNA-modifying enzyme in the S. aureus translatome (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, GLORIseq indicated that tRNALeu(UAA) was the only tRNA partially modified by the putative MiaA enzyme, likely reflecting competition between MiaA and MiaB and the potential inability of MiaA to act on ms2A37. Intriguingly, Um34 in tRNALeu(UAA), detected by RiboMethSeq at apparently stoichiometric levels (Fig. 1D), co-occurred with ms2A37 (Fig. 5B), contrasting with the reported requirement of i6A37 for TrmL modification [79, 91]. This dependency seems to hold true for S. aureus, as the only predicted TrmL substrates, tRNALeu(UAA) and tRNALeu(CAA), also contain i6A37. By contrast, tRNAPhe(GAA), which in B. subtilis carries ms2i6A37 and Gm34 [91], remains unmodified by TrmL in S. aureus, likely due to the presence of m1G37 instead of i6A37. Interestingly, whereas E. coli TrmL modifies only C34- or U34-containing tRNAs, B. subtilis CspR, which is more closely related in sequence to S. aureus TrmL, can additionally target G34 substrates [91]. Although further experiments will be required to clarify the substrate specificity of S. aureus MiaB and TrmL, including the preference of MiaB for tRNALeu(UAA) over tRNALeu(CAA) and tRNATyr(GUA), these observations suggest a more complex interdependency between A37 hypermodification and the nucleotide at position 34.

Notably, within the xm5U synthesis pathway (Supplementary Fig. S5), tRNALeu(UAA) and tRNALys(UUU), both carrying methylthiolated A37, are the only species in which modification at position 34 appears to halt more at the cmnm5(s2)U intermediate. While E. coli tRNALys(UUU), which is not methylthiolated at t6A37, is modified up to the final mnm5s2U34 product [82], the same tRNA in B. subtilis carries ms2t6A37 [136] and predominantly retains cmnm5s2U34 [83]. In this context, the presence of mnm5s2U34 in tRNALys(UUU) isolated from the S. aureus HG001 strain may have been enhanced by the inactivation of the putative MtaB, while this tRNA presents cmnm5s2U34 when purified from USA300 JE2 strain with active MtaB. Similar interdependencies might also account for the lack of xm5U-pathway activity on tRNAGly(UCC) in S. aureus. Importantly, these mechanisms are unlikely to operate in a strict binary manner, as we often detected multiple intermediates of the xm5U pathway within the same tRNA species (Supplementary Data S1), pointing to a more nuanced, tRNA-specific progression pathway. Remarkably, we detected non-thiolated mnm5U in tRNAs that are MnmA substrates, indicating that xm5U modification can occur independently of thiolation in S. aureus. While this observation is consistent with structural studies suggesting that prior xm5U modification may impair MnmA-dependent thiolation [137], it does not support a strictly ordered pathway in which s2U precedes xm5U synthesis. For tRNAArg(UCU) and tRNALeu(UAA), the absence of s2U34 is consistent with the lack of U35, an important determinant for MnmA tRNA binding [137].

The repertoire of tRNA-modifying enzymes for the existing modifications in S. aureus is overall conserved, with several distinctive features of Gram-positive bacteria [72, 80, 102, 138]. Particularly notable among these are the putative dedicated cysteine desulfurases NifZ and YrvO, which support s4U8 (ThiI) and s2U34 (MnmA) synthesis, respectively [139, 140], along with two independent MnmL and MnmM predicted enzymes enabling conversion of cmnm5U into mnm5U within the xm5U pathway (Supplementary Fig. S5) [141, 142]. A feature more specific to S. aureus is the presence of the two putative enzymes QueG and QueH, using distinct cofactors to catalyze redundantly the last step of Q34 synthesis [92]. Our time-course proteomics experiment revealed distinct expression patterns among the S. aureus tRNA-modifying enzymes (Supplementary Data S3B), with some more expressed earlier (e.g. TruB, TrmK, and ThiI) than others (e.g. TmcAL and TsaC) and related enzymes showing coordinated expression (e.g. MiaA, MiaB, and TrmL). Despite the modest amplitude of these changes (often below ± 1 log2 FC), the observed trends were consistent across replicates, suggesting controlled regulation rather than stochastic variation. In line with this, QueG and QueH, as well as TrhP1/P2 and TrhO—two pairs of enzymes expected to catalyze the same reactions but relying on distinct cofactors—displayed divergent temporal expression patterns, indicating that S. aureus dynamically adjusts modification activities depending on nutrient availability. The expression of the putative TrhO declined with growth rate, possibly reflecting the progressive depletion of oxygen required for its activity. Likewise the predicted QueG, which depends on cobalamin [143], was expressed at lower levels than the iron-dependent QueH [144], pointing to differential cofactor availability. The dependency of tRNA-modifying enzymes on specific cofactors is likely exploited as a regulatory mechanism, integrating signals from metabolic states to fine-tune tRNA modification and translation.

The genomic distribution of tRNA-modifying enzymes within operons comprising genes with diverse functions (Supplementary Fig. S4) suggests that their expression is linked to broad regulatory programs linked to cellular physiology. Our expression analyses of these operons point to a complex regulatory landscape involving multiple layers of control. A representative example is the trmD operon, which groups genes functionally linked to translation. In E. coli, this operon has the same composition and organization and is transcribed as single polycistronic mRNA in a growth-rate-dependent manner, with uniform mRNA stability across genes [145]. Nevertheless, the corresponding proteins levels differ, indicating a mechanism of translational control [146]. In S. aureus, the coordinated protein expression trends during growth may reflect also transcription control at the operon level, while differences in ribosome occupancy support differential protein production. However, the distinct mRNA levels of these genes suggest an additional layer of post-transcriptional regulation, likely involving mRNA stability, not observed in E. coli. Another example is provided by miaA, whose expression in E. coli is reduced by the RNA-chaperone Hfq, which affect the stability of the miaA-hfq co-transcript [147]. Although the operon organization is not conserved in S. aureus, the genomic association between miaA and hfq is maintained, and we observe similar opposing protein expression trends (Supplementary Fig. S8). Interestingly, gene pairs expected to undergo translational coupling based on their genomic organization (e.g. nifZ-thiI) also displayed distinct translation efficiencies, indicating the contribution of mechanisms acting the translational level, potentially driven by differences in the strength and accessibility of Shine–Dalgarno sequences during translation initiation [148]. Additional regulation may occur at the post-translational level, as some enzymes depend on cofactor incorporation (e.g. Fe-S cluster) for proper folding and stability [149]. However, our data do not allow to directly assess such mechanisms.

The connection between the expression of tRNA-modifying enzymes and the physiological state of S. aureus also suggests that tRNA modifications can be dynamically remodeled. In this context, the modification landscape likely emerges from the balance between tRNA stability and the expression of modifying enzymes (writers). Consistent with this view, only a limited number of demodifying enzymes (erasers) have been described in bacteria [150, 151], and none have been reported in S. aureus, although such activities may remain to be identified. Given the remarkable stability of tRNAs in bacteria [152], the modification patterns are expected to reflect the cumulative history of modification events over the course of growth rather than instantaneous enzymatic activity. This is supported by the observation that several enzymes displayed decreased expression at late exponential phase in our proteomics data, while the corresponding modifications remained readily detectable, with no indication of partial occupancy. Accordingly, sampling at this growth stage provided a robust representation of the modification landscape under conditions of active growth, while minimizing confounding effects associated with entry into stationary phase. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that this approach may preferentially capture a core set of tRNA modifications, and that additional, growth- or condition-dependent modifications may not be detected. For instance, s4U8 was not detected in several tRNA species potentially because its incorporation may vary across growth phase, as reported in E. coli [153]. Similarly, ms2i6A37 was predominantly observed in a single tRNA, but its presence in other tRNAs may depend on specific physiological conditions, as observed in B. subtilis [154]. Furthermore, additional condition-dependent activities are likely to be discovered in S. aureus, as illustrated by recently characterized dual tRNA/rRNA activity of the methyltransferase RsmF promoted by its upregulation in oxidative stress in E. coli [155].

Perhaps the most distinctive S. aureus tRNA modification enzyme is DusB2, responsible alone for D synthesis at all positions 17, 20, and 20a. Our comprehensive mass spectrometry mapping of D sites suggested that sequence features within the D-loop determine the sites targeted by DusB2, with position 17a potentially acting as a negative determinant. Interestingly, non-proteogenic tRNAGly(UCC) species, which lack canonical D- and TΨC-interactions, were only partially modified by DusB2, which may indicate subtle structural differences in these unconventional tRNAs preventing efficient dihydrouridylation. Remarkably, recombinant S. aureus DusB2 modified quite efficiently synthetic non-proteogenic tRNAGly(UUC) NP2 (Fig. 7C), in contrast to other Dus enzymes acting preferentially on modified tRNAs [108, 156]. Another notable S. aureus feature is the presence of a second inactive DusB2 consisting only in the C-terminal tRNA binding domain, which could potentially interfere with the activity of the functional DusB2 enzyme. Interestingly, this truncated protein is located within a genomic region associated with sulfide stress response genes [157, 158] (Supplementary Fig. S4), raising the possibility that it may contribute to the modulation of D synthesis under these conditions. The absence of significant expression of DusB2-C in our datasets is consistent with repression of the locus by the transcriptional regulator CstR, which is strongly expressed under our experimental conditions [157].

S. aureus possesses three non-proteogenic tRNAGly(UCC) species—NP1, NP2, and NP3—which are aminoacylated by the glycyl-tRNA synthetase and subsequently delivered to the aminoacyl-tRNA transferases FemX, FemA, and FemB for the synthesis of the pentaglycine bridges that crosslink the peptidoglycan subunits in the cell wall. Consistent with previous findings in Staphylococcus epidermidis [159, 160], the non-proteogenic tRNAGly(UCC) species in S. aureus are markedly hypomodified compared to their proteogenic counterparts, showing only partial s4U8 and D20 levels. This reduced modification state might contribute to their selective recognition by the Fem transferases, ensuring that only the three non-proteogenic tRNAGly(UCC) species are used for cell-wall synthesis rather than entering translation. Moreover, their lower cellular abundance relative to proteogenic tRNAGly species likely prevents competition with the translational machinery and reinforces their specialized role in pentaglycine bridge formation. Although the preferences of Fem proteins for each non-proteogenic tRNAGly(UCC) species have not been yet investigated, our analysis reveals subtle structural differences and variations in the hypomodification patterns of NP1, NP2, and NP3 that may contribute to potential differential recognition by FemX, FemA, and FemB.

Interestingly, D20 was detected in NP3 only in the edited S. aureus RN4220 strain, in which deletion of one gene copy of tRNAGly(GCC) P1 results in increased levels of the three non-proteogenic tRNAGly, suggesting that DusB2 activity on these substrates may be influenced by their abundance. In this context, we assessed the relationship between tRNA abundance and modification status of individual tRNA species (Supplementary Fig. S15) and observed no strong correlation between tRNA levels and the overall extent of tRNA modifications. Nevertheless, a positive correlation emerged between tRNA abundance and the number of predicted targeting enzymes, suggesting a link between tRNA abundance and the complexity of modification pathways. Together, these observations indicate that substrate availability is not the primary determinant of tRNA modification levels, although it may influence modification efficiency in specific cases.

S. aureus encodes fewer tRNA species than the 32 theoretically required to decode all 61 sense codons under the classical wobble rules [161], highlighting the importance of tRNA modification status for efficient codon decoding. In this context, integration of ribosome profiling, Nanopore tRNA quantification, codon usage analysis, and the tRNA modification landscape in S. aureus under optimal growth conditions allowed direct assessment of how efficiently codons predicted to require modification-dependent decoding capacities are translated in vivo. Our analysis indicates that codon decoding speed cannot be explained solely by tRNA abundance, suggesting contributions from additional determinants such as codon–anticodon interaction properties, tRNA modification status, and potentially amino acid identity (Supplementary Fig. S14). Our ribosome profiling A-site pausing analysis suggested that most synonymous codons recognized by the same tRNA are read at comparable rates, with a slight preference for A- and U-rich codons—more frequently used due to the genome’s low GC content. Notably, tRNAAla(UGC), tRNAPro(UGG), tRNAThr(UGU), and tRNAVal(UAC), all carrying mo5U34, efficiently decoded all four synonymous codons for their respective amino acids, including C-ending triplets, which are considered to form less favorable superwobble interactions [162, 163]. This observation is particularly remarkable because equivalent superwobbling properties are generally mediated by unmodified U34 in other organisms [35, 164, 165]. The overall efficient decoding observed across the four synonymous codons for these amino acids, together with previous studies on xo5U34 in other organisms [119, 162, 163, 166, 167], suggest that absence of mo5U34 in S. aureus would impact efficient decoding of both canonical (G-ending) and non-canonical (U- and C-ending) wobble interactions. In parallel, Nanopore direct tRNA sequencing correlated the pronounced ribosome pausing at CGG codons with the low abundance of tRNAArg(CCG), in line with the notion that rare codons are translated more slowly [7]. It also revealed discrepancies between tRNA abundance and the decoding speed of Ser-UCN codons, suggesting that tRNASer(UGA), carrying mo5U34, substantially contribute to reading of UCC and UCU codons despite the presence of tRNASer(GGA), which is better adapted to these codons but is present at much lower levels. This observation further supports the idea that mo5U34 expands the decoding capacities of these tRNAs in vivo.

Another outstanding feature in S. aureus is the modification at position 34 in tRNAGly(UCC), which carries mo5U rather than the mnm5U observed in most bacteria, including B. subtilis [71]. Since the anticodon stem-loop sequence is conserved between S. aureus and B. subtilis, this difference may instead relate to other tRNA determinants, such as the absence of D20 in S. aureus tRNAGly(UCC), or to intrinsic differences in the enzymes involved in the corresponding modification pathways. While mnm5U34 is known to promote efficient decoding of G-ending codons within split boxes [168], the presence of mo5U34 likely extends the decoding capacities of tRNAGly(UCC) to support recognition of GGC and GGU codons by tRNAGly(GCC). Consistent with this, deletion of one of the two gene copies of tRNAGly(GCC) in S. aureus RN4220 did not cause translation defects likely due to compensation by tRNAGly(UCC) [40]. Furthermore, our data evidenced atypical decoding properties for tRNAGly(UCC), as it recognized the least frequently used GGG codon more efficiently than GGA (Fig. 8). Unlike the mo5U-containing tRNALeu(UAG) and tRNASer(UGA), which were more abundant than their corresponding G34-containing isoacceptors, tRNAGly(UCC) was present at lower levels than the tRNAGly(GCC) isoacceptor. Despite its lower abundance, tRNAGly(UCC) decoded GGG at speed comparable to that of codons read by the more abundant tRNAGly(GCC), whereas GGA remained translated more slowly. This discrepancy points to a potential role of mo5U34 in modulating the recognition of specific Gly codons, a hypothesis that will, of course, require more detailed experimental validation using appropriate modification mutants.

In summary, our integrative analysis provides a comprehensive view of the full set of tRNA modifications and their corresponding enzymes in S. aureus, delivering high-confidence modification maps for each tRNA species, including non-proteogenic tRNAGly. While the overall modification repertoire is conserved in Gram-positive bacteria, examination of individual tRNA modification profiles uncovered significant S. aureus-specific features, including enzymes with distinct substrate specificities, intrinsic tRNA determinants influencing enzyme recognition, coordinated and interdependent modification pathways, and a complex interplay between tRNA abundance, modification status, and codon decoding dynamics. Importantly, this study demonstrates the value of integrating complementary mass spectrometry and deep-sequencing analyses to achieve accurate and comprehensive tRNA modification mapping. Our work establishes a foundation for further research aimed at dissecting the roles of specific tRNA modifications in S. aureus physiology, stress adaptation, and pathogenesis.
