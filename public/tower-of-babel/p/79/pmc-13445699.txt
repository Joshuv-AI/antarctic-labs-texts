Preoperative volume of mildly delayed time predicts 24-hour complete reperfusion after successful mechanical thrombectomy
Authors: Hui Zhang, Yimeng Liu, Hongchen Zhao, Yifeng Ling, Qiang Dong, Wenjie Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSuccessful macrovascular recanalization after mechanical thrombectomy (MT) does not always ensure tissue-level microvascular reperfusion, highlighting the need for reliable preoperative imaging biomarkers to predict microvascular success.AimsTo investigate whether the preoperative volume of mildly delay time (DT 2–4 s), as quantified by MIStar CT perfusion (CTP), can predict 24-hour complete reperfusion (CR) in patients achieving successful MT.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed patients with acute anterior circulation large vessel occlusion treated between January 2015 and December 2022 who achieved successful recanalization (eTICI 2b-3). All participants received baseline and follow-up CTP at 24 h processed by MIStar. CR was defined as 100% reperfusion on follow-up imaging. The relationship between preoperative DT 2–4 s volume and CR was evaluated using multivariable logistic regression and Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.ResultsSixty patients (mean age 65 years, 58.3% male) were included, with 36 (60%) achieving CR. Preoperative DT 2–4 s volume was significantly smaller in the CR group (64.8 ± 27.2 ml) compared to the incomplete group (113.5 ± 52.3 ml; p < 0.001). After adjusting for confounders, DT 2–4 s remained independently associated with CR (adjusted OR 0.94; 95% CI 0.9 to 0.98). ROC analysis yielded an AUC of 0.81. A threshold of DT 2–4 s < 95 ml predicted CR with a sensitivity of 0.84, a specificity of 0.81.ConclusionPreoperative DT 2–4 s volume is a novel and sensitive biomarker for predicting 24-hour complete tissue-level reperfusion. It identifies patients at risk of microvascular failure despite successful large-vessel recanalization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-05146-3.

Introduction

Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) caused by large vessel occlusion (LVO) remains a leading cause of disability and mortality worldwide [1, 2]. The advent of mechanical thrombectomy (MT) has revolutionized the treatment landscape, achieving high rates of successful recanalization, typically defined as an expanded Thrombolysis in Cerebral Ischemia (eTICI) score of 2b to 3 [3, 4]. However, a significant clinical paradox remains: despite successful opening of the occluded large vessels, approximately 50% of patients do not achieve favorable functional outcomes—a phenomenon often attributed to incomplete microvascular reperfusion or the “no-reflow” phenomenon [5–7].

Successful arterial recanalization does not always guarantee that oxygenated blood will effectively reach the brain parenchyma at the capillary level. Factors such as distal microembolization, pericyte constriction, and endothelial edema can impair the microcirculation even after the primary thrombus has been removed [8]. Therefore, identifying preoperative imaging biomarkers that can predict the likelihood of achieving complete reperfusion (CR) of tissue-level is critical for refining patient selection and managing clinical expectations [9, 10].

CT perfusion (CTP) has become a standard tool in the acute stroke, primarily used to estimate core volume and penumbra [11]. Emerging evidence suggests that the delay time (DT) profile may provide deeper insights into the hemodynamic state of the brain. While severely delayed tissue (e.g., DT > 6s) is often associated with the ischemic core, DT >3s represents a hypoperfused state 12, 13]. We hypothesized that the preoperative volume of DT 2–4 s might reflect the baseline integrity of the microvascular bed and its potential for CR post-MT.

Materials and methods

Patients

We identified AIS patients who underwent MT in our institution between 01/2015 and 12/2022 within 24 h of stroke symptom onset and received multimodal CT imaging prior and post to mechanical thrombectomy. Patients with middle cerebral artery (MCA) M1 or M2 segment occlusion and/or internal carotid artery (ICA) occlusion were recruited for inclusion in our study (Fig. 1). Demographic, clinical, and radiologic data were recorded and included age, sex, onset-to-recanalization time (ORT). Risk factors, such as smoking history, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, history of stroke or transient ischemic attack, and atrial fibrillation, were also recorded. The study was performed with the informed consent of the patients or their next of kin and with ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of our institution.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

CT scanning and image processing

Whole-brain perfusion and angiography were performed at the time of emergency before therapy using a 64-slice CT scanner (Discovery CT750 HD; GE Medical Systems, Waukesha, Wisconsin). A 40-ml bolus of nonionic contrast media was injected at a rate of 5 ml/s. CT scanning was initiated 7 s after a contrast agent bolus, as follows: shuttle mode, 80 kVp/220 mAs; 26 cycles for 34 s; 312 slices.

Imaging analysis

(CTA) images were reviewed first to identify patients with complete occlusion of the MCA +/- ICA. For each patient, the final eTICI score was retrospectively assessed by a neurologist (Y.L.) and a neurointerventionalist (H.Z.) blinded to both the first reader’s scoring and the clinical outcome. A consensus was reached with a senior neurointerventionalist (W.C.) for cases with discrepant judgments.

Ischemic volumes of infarct core and hypoperfused lesion on CTP were calculated by commercial software MIStar (Apollo Medical Imaging Technology) based on CTP maps. Volumes of delayed time (DT) >3s, DT >2s and < = 4s (DT 2–4 s), DT >4s and < = 6s (DT 4–6 s) and DT >6s were also calculated by MISar. Complete reperfusion was defined that there were no any hypoperfused lesion on DT map of 24 h CTP (Fig. 2).

Statistics analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS, Version 23 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA). P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and means and compared using Chi-squared and Fisher’s exact tests where appropriate. Ordinal or continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the T test. Factors predictive of CR in the univariate analysis (P < 0.15) were considered for inclusion in the multivariate binary logistic regression to identify independent factors of CR. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the Youden index was used to determine optimal threshold. Positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity at the optimal threshold were calculated.

Results

Patient baseline characteristics and univariate analysis of CR are detailed in Table 1. Between January 2015 to December 2022, two hundred seventy-one patients with acute ischemic stroke presented to our institution within 24 h of stroke-symptom onset received MT. Of those, 60 patients with acute anterior circulation large vessel occlusion who achieved successful recanalization [2b: 48.3% (29/60); 2c: 25% (15/60) and 3: 26.7% (16/60)] received baseline and follow-up CTP at 24 h. The mean patient age was 65.2 ± 13.3 years with 35/60 (58.3%) being men. Hypertension was found in 58.3% (35/60); diabetes mellitus, in 26.7% (16/60); atrial fibrillation (AF), in 31.7% (19/60); current smoking, in 36.7% (22/60) and previous stroke 20% (12/60) of the patients. Mean onset-to-recanalization time (ORT) was 573.3 ± 362.2 min. There were 21 patients (35%) treated with intravenous recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (IV-tPA), 9 patients (15%) received angioplasty and 22 patients (36.7%) received stenting during MT.

Baseline CTP showed the mean volume of infarct core was 26.1 ± 31.6 ml; DT > 3s, 110.1 ± 64.3 ml; DT 2–4 s, 84.3 ± 45.7 ml; DT 4–6 s, 41.9 ± 27.3 ml and DT >6s, 33.3 ± 30.3 ml. On 24 h CTP, 60% (36/60) patients achieved CR.

In the univariate analysis, factors significantly associated with an CR were: AF, (p = 0.002); lower volumes of DT >3s (p = 0.012), DT 2–4 s (p < 0.001) and DT 4–6 s (p = 0.03) (Table 1). After multicollinearity testing in Model 1, age, gender, AF, ORT, TICI 2b–3, DT > 3 s, DT 2–4 s, and DT 4–6 s were included in a multivariate logistic regression analysis. AF (OR 21.6, 95% CI 2.1–220.7; p = 0.009) and DT 2–4 s (OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.90–0.98; p = 0.005) were identified as independent predictors of CR. In Model 2, after excluding DT > 3 s, AF (OR 19.89, 95% CI 2.12–186.52; p = 0.009) and DT 2–4 s (OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.90–0.98; p = 0.005) remained significantly associated with CR (Table 2).

In ROC analysis, DT 2 ~ 4s had the highest accuracy to predict CR with area under curve (AUC) 0.81 (95% CI, 0.69–0.93; P < 0.001), followed by DT >3s (AUC: 0.71, 95%CI 0.57 to 0.84; P = 0.006) and DT 4–6 s (AUC: 0.67, 95%CI 0.53 to 0.81; P = 0.021) (Fig. 3). DT 2–4 s < 95 ml, determined by the Youden index, predicted CR with a sensitivity of 84% (95% CI 68%-93%), a specificity of 81% (95% CI, 59%–94%) and a positive predictive value of 88% (95% CI, 73%–96%).

Discussion

The major finding of this study is that in stroke patients achieving successful arterial recanalization (eTICI 2b-3), the preoperative volume of mildly delayed tissue (DT 2–4 s) is a potent predictor of 24-hour complete tissue-level reperfusion. Specifically, a DT 2–4 s volume of less than 95 ml demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity (> 0.80) in identifying patients who would achieve optimal microvascular restoration.

In the MIStar dSVD algorithm, Delay Time (DT) isolates the temporal lag caused by collateral pathways from the intrinsic tissue transit time. While DT > 3s is widely accepted as the threshold for the total ischemic penumbra, the sub-segment of DT 2–4 s represents tissue with “mild” hypoperfusion, often sustained by robust leptomeningeal collaterals [12, 14].

We hypothesize that a smaller volume of DT 2–4 s tissue (< 95 ml) reflects a more organized and efficient collateral recruitment. When the DT 2–4 s volume is limited, it suggests that the ischemic insult is either highly localized or that the collateral flow is sufficiently brisk to prevent a widespread “stagnant” flow state. Conversely, a large volume of mildly delayed tissue may indicate a fragile hemodynamic balance over a vast territory, where the microvasculature is structurally intact but functionally exhausted [15, 16].

The “no-reflow” phenomenon occurs when capillaries remain non-functional despite the removal of the proximal occlusion, often due to pericyte constriction, endothelial edema, or microthrombosis [6]. Our results offer new insights into the discrepancy between macrovascular recanalization (eTICI) and microvascular reperfusion. Patients with a preoperative DT 2–4 s volume > 95 ml were significantly less likely to achieve complete 24-hour reperfusion. This suggests that a high burden of mildly delayed tissue might serve as a surrogate marker for a “pre-exhausted” microvascular bed. In these cases, the prolonged state of low-pressure perfusion over a large area may trigger inflammatory cascades and capillary collapse that cannot be reversed immediately by mechanical thrombectomy alone.

Current triage for thrombectomy relies heavily on the “mismatch” between the necrotic core and the penumbra [17]. However, our findings suggest that the quality of the penumbra, specifically the volume of the DT 2–4 s fraction, is equally critical for predicting technical success at the tissue level. A threshold of 95 ml could potentially guide post-procedural management; patients exceeding this volume may require adjunctive pharmacological strategies (such as microvascular vasodilators or anti-inflammatory agents) to combat the no-reflow phenomenon even after a successful eTICI 3 recanalization.

Interestingly, atrial fibrillation (AF) also emerged as an independent predictor in our multivariate model. Previous studies have also found a significant association between atrial fibrillation and delayed reperfusion after mechanical thrombectomy [18, 19]. This may be explained by the different clot compositions associated with cardioembolic sources, which are often more fibrin-rich and may behave differently during fragmentation and distal migration compared to atherosclerotic thrombi [20, 21]. Furthermore, patients with AF often have different collateral flow patterns, which could influence the DT maps at baseline [22] .

The proportion of patients undergoing angioplasty or stenting in our cohort is relatively high. This is largely attributable to the underlying stroke etiology in our study population. Specifically, a substantial proportion of patients were diagnosed with intracranial atherosclerotic disease–related large vessel occlusion (ICAS-LVO), which is more prevalent in Asian populations compared to Western cohorts. In such cases, rescue angioplasty and/or stenting are frequently required to achieve and maintain successful reperfusion [23].

Our study has limitations, including its retrospective design and relatively small sample size from a single center. Our cohort may represent a relatively higher prevalence of ICAS-LVO and consequently a higher rate of adjunctive endovascular therapies. Additionally, while MIStar provides reliable automated maps, different perfusion software packages may yield varying DT thresholds [13]. Future prospective studies are needed to validate the 95 ml cutoff and to determine if specific intra-procedural interventions can convert incomplete reperfusion into complete success in patients with unfavorable preoperative DT 2–4 s profiles. Finally, the present study was designed as a purely imaging-based analysis. Therefore, clinical outcome measures such as NIHSS and mRS were not systematically collected. Future studies integrating both imaging findings and clinical outcomes are warranted to further validate the clinical relevance of our results.
