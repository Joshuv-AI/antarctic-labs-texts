Impact of carbon and nitrogen source variation on B-vitamin accumulation in Yarrowia lipolytica: Toward microbial fortification of food ingredients
Authors: Panwana Khunnonkwao, Sitanan Thitiprasert, Atsushi Minamino, Tatsuya Matsuno, Katsushige Yamada, Shang-Tian Yang, Nuttha Thongchul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractYarrowia lipolytica is a food-compatible yeast with strong potential for sustainable B-vitamin enrichment of functional food ingredients. This study evaluated the effects of glucose, xylose, mixed, and lignocellulosic sugars, as well as diverse nitrogen sources, on yeast growth and B-vitamin yields in controlled fermentations. Glucose with yeast extract led to the highest biomass and intracellular accumulation of thiamine (B1), niacin (B3), and pyridoxine (B6), while mixed sugars supported maximal intracellular folate (B9) content. Intracellular B-vitamin levels were consistently higher with yeast extract than with defined nitrogen sources. Cobalamin (B12) was detected in biomass under all substrate and nitrogen conditions; based on current knowledge of Y. lipolytica metabolism and the presence of trace B12 in yeast extract, these observations are consistent with uptake and retention from the medium rather than de novo biosynthesis. Results indicate that nutrient formulation, particularly the use of renewable sugars and organic nitrogen, can be strategically tuned to generate Y. lipolytica biomass with distinct B-vitamin profiles suited to different food fortification scenarios. The demonstrated use of commercial lignocellulosic sugar streams underscores the compatibility of this process with food-grade industrial substrates and circular-economy concepts, providing a practical basis for deploying B-vitamin-enriched yeast as a functional ingredient in plant-based beverages, cereal products and other fortified foods.Keywords: Yarrowia lipolytica, B-vitamins, Fermentation, Functional food, Sustainable production

Introduction

B-vitamins are essential water-soluble micronutrients that serve as critical cofactors in cellular metabolism and support the health of humans and animals. These compounds—including thiamine (B1), riboflavin (B2), niacin (B3), pantothenic acid (B5), pyridoxine (B6), biotin (B7), folate (B9), and cobalamin (B12)—play diverse roles in metabolic pathways and have extensive applications in food fortification, animal feed, nutraceuticals, and supplements. The demand for natural and functional vitamin-enriched food ingredients is rapidly increasing as populations shift toward plant-based and specialized diets, driving global health initiatives [[1], [2], [3]].

Microbial fermentation has emerged as a sustainable approach for vitamin B production, leveraging environmentally friendly feedstocks, mild process conditions, and reduced environmental impact compared to traditional chemical synthesis [4,5]. Fermentation using bacteria or yeasts offers the only scalable, clean-label pathway for complex vitamins such as cobalamin (B12). For example, Propionibacterium freudenreichii and related strains specialize in cobalamin biosynthesis, whereas Bacillus subtilis is a model organism for commercial riboflavin production [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Notably, robust yeast hosts offer superior food safety, broad substrate utilization, and the capacity to produce multiple B-vitamins simultaneously [4,10].

Among yeast species, Yarrowia lipolytica is particularly promising, given its metabolic versatility, ability to grow on lignocellulosic and food waste-derived substrates, and GRAS status for food applications. Recent studies have reported measurable intracellular contents of thiamine, riboflavin, pyridoxine, biotin, folate and cobalamin in Y. lipolytica biomass, with vitamin levels varying with strain background and cultivation conditions [3,11,12]; in the present work, concentrations up to 262.28 mg/L (B1), 321.36 mg/L (B3), 137.12 mg/L (B6), 4.46 mg/L (B9) and 9.25 mg/L (B12) were achieved under optimized fermentations (Section 3.2−3.3). Beyond strain and pathway optimization, a critical challenge is translating microbial vitamin production into ingredients that function reliably within real food systems. Vitamin-enriched biomass must be generated on food-compatible, preferably renewable, substrates and must withstand processing operations such as pasteurization, baking and extrusion without unacceptable nutrient losses or quality defects. With its GRAS status, capacity to utilize commercial lignocellulosic sugar syrups, and ability to accumulate multiple B-vitamins simultaneously, Y. lipolytica represents a promising clean-label fortification platform for plant-based beverages, cereal-based products and savory snacks. In this context, the present work not only maps how carbon and nitrogen sources affect B-vitamin biosynthesis but also derives bioprocess design principles that can guide the production of vitamin-enriched yeast ingredients for food applications. Advances in metabolic engineering—such as CRISPR-based technologies—have accelerated targeted improvements in vitamin biosynthesis pathways; however, native regulatory mechanisms and nutrient-responsive metabolism remain essential for developing food-grade, non-GMO vitamin sources [13,14].

This study investigates intracellular B-vitamin accumulation in Y. lipolytica, focusing on the effects of diverse carbon sources (glucose, xylose, mixed sugars, lignocellulosic hydrolysates) and nitrogen regimes (organic vs defined) on yeast growth and micronutrient accumulation under food-compatible fermentation conditions. Simultaneous multi-B-vitamin accumulation under diverse carbon−nitrogen regimes is retained. The objectives are to: (i) characterize yeast growth and vitamin yields from renewable, food-relevant substrates; (ii) map accumulation patterns for B1, B3, B6, B9, and B12 under different nutrient formulations; (iii) identify optimal carbon/nitrogen combinations for maximal vitamin output; (iv) elucidate metabolic pathways underlying nutrient-responsive vitamin biosynthesis; and (v) provide actionable principles for optimizing yeast fermentation for functional food ingredient development. Through these efforts, this work advances sustainable, natural vitamin enrichment strategies for modern food biotechnology.

Despite growing interest in Y. lipolytica as a vitamin-enriched food ingredient, no prior study has systematically evaluated simultaneous multi-B-vitamin accumulation under diverse carbon–nitrogen regimes using a wild-type, non-GMO strain. Furthermore, the influence of mixed sugars—including lignocellulosic hydrolysates—on folate (B9) production via pentose phosphate pathway activation in this GRAS yeast remains unexplored. This work addresses these gaps by providing the first comprehensive quantitative mapping of B1, B3, B6, B9, and B12 accumulation in Y. lipolytica cultivated on glucose, xylose, and mixed/lignocellulosic sugars, establishing actionable bioprocess principles for natural, food-grade vitamin fortification.

Materials and methods

Microorganisms

Yarrowia lipolytica TISTR5212 (Thailand Institute of Scientific and Technological Research, TISTR) was used in all experiments. This strain was selected because it is a wild-type, non-genetically modified isolate from a recognized national culture collection and is therefore suitable for evaluating food-compatible B-vitamin enrichment without the confounding effects of metabolic engineering. In addition, the strain had shown stable growth and reproducible fermentation performance in our preliminary laboratory studies. Working stocks were maintained in 25% (v/v) glycerol at −80 °C, a preservation method routinely used in our laboratory for long-term storage of yeast cultures while maintaining viability and phenotypic stability.

Fermentation experiments

Vitamin B fermentations were conducted in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 50 mL working volume of fermentation medium. The primary seed culture was grown in yeast extract peptone dextrose (YPD) medium (10 g/L yeast extract, 20 g/L peptone, and 20 g/L glucose) at 30 °C and 200 rpm for 16 h. This incubation time was selected on the basis of preliminary growth experiments showing that the culture had entered active exponential growth and provided a reproducible inoculum for subsequent fermentation. A secondary seed was then prepared by transferring 3% (v/v) of the primary seed into fresh YPD medium and incubating for an additional 6 h under identical conditions to obtain metabolically active cells prior to inoculation.

The fermentation was initiated by inoculating the secondary seed at 10% (v/v), corresponding to an initial biomass concentration of approximately 0.3 g CDW/L based on the OD600–CDW correlation described in Section 2.3. Carbon and nitrogen sources were varied systematically as shown in Table 1. The study followed a defined factorial comparison of carbon source type, carbon concentration, and nitrogen source, rather than a response-surface or Central Composite Design.

In this study, ‘mixed sugars’ refers to a defined synthetic mixture of glucose and xylose prepared from analytical-grade sugars at an 8:2 mass ratio, whereas ‘C-sugars’ refers to a commercial lignocellulosic sugar syrup supplied by Cellulosic Biomass Technology Co., Ltd. and composed predominantly of glucose and xylose together with minor impurities derived from biomass processing. Fermentations were performed at 30 °C, 200 rpm, and initial pH 5.5 for up to 120 h. Samples were withdrawn every 12 h for determination of biomass, residual sugars, extracellular metabolites, and intracellular vitamin B content. All fermentation conditions were conducted in biological duplicate, and each analytical measurement was performed in technical duplicate.

Dry cell biomass estimation

For biomass determination, 1.0 mL of well-mixed fermentation broth was withdrawn at each sampling point and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min. The cell pellet was resuspended in 1.0 mL deionized water and homogenized by vortexing prior to spectrophotometric measurement at 600 nm. When the absorbance exceeded the linear range of the spectrophotometer, the suspension was diluted appropriately with deionized water and the measured OD600 value was multiplied by the corresponding dilution factor.

Biomass was monitored by OD600 rather than by direct gravimetric drying at every time point because the study involved a large number of samples collected across multiple fermentation conditions and time intervals. A standard calibration curve established for Y. lipolytica TISTR5212 was used to convert OD600 values to cell dry weight (CDW), whereby an OD600 of 3.0 corresponded to 1.0 g/L CDW. Biomass-specific vitamin contents were subsequently calculated and expressed as mg vitamin per g CDW.

Cell harvest and autolysis

Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C and washed twice with ice-cold 0.85% NaCl solution. Intracellular vitamins were extracted by an autolysis procedure adapted from Demirgül et al. Briefly, 1 g wet cell pellet was suspended in 5.6 mL sterile deionized water adjusted to pH 6.0 and incubated at 50 °C with shaking at 120 rpm for 36 h. The lysate was subsequently heated at 80 °C for 30 min, centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for vitamin analysis [15].

Preliminary internal checks indicated no appreciable loss of detectable B-vitamin signal under the applied extraction conditions within the experimental variability of the method. The heat treatment was therefore retained to improve extract clarification and reproducibility.

Vitamin B quantification

Vitamin B concentrations were determined by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Shimadzu). Separation was performed with a reverse-phase C18 column (Phenomenex Synergi™ 4 μm Hydro-RP 80A, 250 × 4.6 mm) using a binary gradient of Buffer A(10 mM Na2PO4, pH 2.6, 0.8 mM octane sulfonate) and Buffer B (acetronitrile). Elution gradient: 0—3 min, 10% A; 3—7 min, ramp to 70% A; 7—10 min, 70% A; 10—11 min, ramp to 90% A; 11—13 min, 90% A; 13—14 min, ramp to 10%A; 14—20 min, 10% A. Detection was performed at 210 nm. The system was operated at flowrate of 1.4 mL/min; column temperature of 30 °C; and injection volume of 5 μL. Stock standards for B-vitamins (B1, B3, B6, and B12) were prepared in HPLC-grade water (100 mg/L), while B9 standard was prepared separately in 20 mM NaOH. In all analyses, vitamin B9 was quantified using folic acid as the calibration standard, and peak identification was based on retention time matching to this monoglutamyl reference. The method does not include an enzymatic deconjugation step or chromatographic separation of polyglutamylated forms; therefore, the reported B9 values represent total folate expressed as folic-acid equivalents and do not distinguish between mono- and polyglutamylated species. The standards were stored at −20 °C. Linear calibration for working standard solutions ranged 12.5−75.0 mg/L. Intracellular vitamin concentrations were expressed as mg/L fermentation broth and calculated using the cell extract dilution factor (DF).

Vitamin concentrations were primarily determined from cell extracts obtained after autolysis and expressed as mg/L fermentation broth and mg vitamin per g CDW. In addition, for selected conditions (20 g/L glucose or 20 g/L xylose combined with either yeast extract or YNB), cell-free culture supernatants were collected at multiple time points during cultivation and analyzed by the same HPLC method to quantify time-course profiles of residual vitamin B in the fermentation broth. These supernatant measurements were used to assess extracellular vitamin levels relative to intracellular titers but were not sufficient to close full vitamin mass balances or to independently quantify net de novo synthesis versus uptake for each vitamin.

Sugar and metabolite analysis

Residual sugars and extracellular metabolites were analyzed using a method adapted from Thitiprasert et al. (2017) [16]. The principal modifications were the use of a 15 μL injection volume and calibration with standard mixtures in the range of 0.5–2.0 g/L under the chromatographic conditions described below.

Cell-free supernatants were diluted, filtered, and injected into an Aminex HPX-87H organic acid column (Bio-Rad, 300 × 7.8 mm) at 45 °C with 0.005 M H2SO4 as mobile phase (0.6 mL/min). Detection employed a refractive index detector, and standard curves were constructed with reference mixtures (0.5−2.0 g/L).

Results

Growth characteristics of Y. lipolytica on various carbon and nitrogen sources

The growth behavior and substrate utilization of Y. lipolytica were systematically evaluated in media supplemented with different carbon (glucose, xylose, and a glucose-xylose mixture) and nitrogen (yeast extract, YNB) sources to elucidate their individual and combined impacts on microbial proliferation and metabolite synthesis.

All reported OD600 values represent dilution-corrected measurements obtained within the linear range of the spectrophotometer, as described in Section 2.3. Therefore, values greater than 1.0 reflect calculated biomass concentrations after appropriate sample dilution rather than direct undiluted absorbance readings. Mannitol and xylitol were also monitored as minor extracellular metabolites; however, their concentrations remained low under all tested conditions and did not materially affect the interpretation of growth or vitamin accumulation trends.

When glucose was used as the sole carbon source, Y. lipolytica demonstrated robust growth and efficient glucose assimilation regardless of nitrogen source or initial sugar concentration (Fig. 1). Notably, cultures supplemented with yeast extract showed an accelerated rate of glucose consumption compared to those containing YNB (Fig. 1A and B). At 20 g/L glucose, yeast extract supplementation enabled complete substrate depletion by 36 h, reaching a maximum OD600 of 52.67 (Fig. 1A). In contrast, the equivalent medium containing YNB supported slower glucose utilization, with complete depletion at 48 h and a lower peak OD600 of 33.13 (Fig. 1B). Elevating the initial glucose concentration to 40 g/L further increased the maximum OD600 achieved up to 72.40 with yeast extract and 37.87 with YNB, highlighting the positive effect of yeast extract in supporting higher biomass yield under increased substrate loads (Fig. 1C and D). However, only a modest increase in OD600 was observed in the YNB group at higher glucose concentrations, suggesting nitrogen limitation at elevated carbon availability.

When xylose served as the sole carbon source, substrate consumption and cell growth were markedly slower compared with glucose (Fig. 2). Complete assimilation of 20 g/L xylose was observed only in media supplemented with yeast extract, with a maximal OD600 of 37.47 at 96 h (Fig. 2A). The growth parameters obtained with xylose consistently lagged behind those achieved with glucose, indicating a clear carbon source preference in Y. lipolytica for glucose over xylose. In YNB-based media, xylose utilization remained incomplete even after extended incubation, concomitant with reduced cellular biomass (Fig. 2B–D). Notably, increasing xylose concentration to 40 g/L led to a pronounced lag phase (∼12 h) and apparent substrate inhibition, with a maximum OD600 of only 22.57 achieved after 120 h (Fig. 2C).

A carbon substrate mixture (glucose and xylose, 8:2 w/w) was next evaluated to simulate more complex sugar streams (Fig. 3). The presence of glucose improved overall xylose uptake and enabled complete co-consumption during batch fermentation. Correspondingly, higher cell biomass was obtained with sugar mixtures compared to xylose alone, but slightly lower than with glucose as the sole carbon source. With 20 g/L total sugars and yeast extract as the nitrogen source, a peak OD600 of 50.20 was observed at 36 h (Fig. 3A). Substitution of yeast extract with YNB consistently resulted in reduced growth, exemplified by a maximum OD600 of 32.67 at 36 h (Fig. 3B). At elevated total monosugar concentrations (40 g/L), increasing cell biomass was observed with yeast extract (maximum OD600 64.80 at 60 h; Fig. 3C), whereas similar increases were marginal in the YNB group (OD600 24.47, Fig. 3D), further confirming nitrogen limitation in minimal media. In all cases, diauxic growth patterns were evident, with glucose being fully consumed prior to onset of xylose utilization (Fig. 3, Table 2).

Experiments employing commercial cellulosic sugars (C-sugars, containing predominantly glucose and xylose) mirrored the growth and substrate utilization kinetics observed in defined mixed-sugar fermentations. Complete glucose depletion preceded the onset of xylose metabolism, and maximum cell densities correlated with the abundance of yeast extract in the medium. Approximately 25 g/L initial total sugars supported a maximal OD600 of 52.60 at 48 h in the presence of yeast extract (Fig. 3E), whereas only 33.27 was attained at 36 h when YNB was provided as the nitrogen source (Fig. 3F).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that both the choice of carbon and nitrogen sources significantly influence the growth dynamics of Y. lipolytica. Glucose was the most favorable carbon substrate for rapid biomass accumulation, followed by glucose-xylose mixtures, while xylose alone resulted in the lowest yields and substrate utilization efficiency. Throughout the study, yeast extract outperformed YNB in supporting fast growth and high cell densities, particularly at higher sugar concentrations, due to its richer nutrient composition (Table 2). In the context of industrial fermentations leveraging complex or cellulosic sugar substrates, optimization of both carbon and nitrogen sources is thus essential for maximizing Y. lipolytica growth and productivity.

Vitamin B accumulation in Y. lipolytica cultivated with various carbon and nitrogen substrates

Intracellular B-vitamins (B1, B3, B6, and B12) were detected in Y. lipolytica cell biomass under all tested fermentation conditions. Notably, folate (B9, quantified as folic-acid equivalents) was exclusively identified in cultures grown on mixed sugars and commercial cellulosic (C-) sugars (Figs. 4−9, Table 3). Media supplemented with glucose and yeast extract consistently supported the highest accumulation of vitamins B1, B3, and B6. Specifically, peak concentrations of B1 (235.74 mg/L), B3 (272.91 mg/L), B6 (132.27 mg/L), and B12 (9.25 mg/L) occurred at 72 h, 60 h, 24 h, and 36 h, respectively, in cultures with 20 g/L glucose and yeast extract (Fig. 4A and 5A). When YNB replaced yeast extract, significantly lower vitamin yields were obtained, with maxima for B1 (163.82 mg/L), B3 (149.74 mg/L), B6 (62.36 mg/L), and B12 (7.44 mg/L) (Fig. 4B and 5B), underscoring the importance of nitrogen source quality. Using the OD600−CDW correlation described in Section 2.3, we also calculated biomass-specific vitamin contents (mg/g CDW) for each condition (Table 3). Under 40 g/L glucose with yeast extract, Y. lipolytica reached high volumetric titers and simultaneously exhibited elevated biomass-normalized contents of B1, B3 and B6, indicating that these conditions enhanced both cell density and vitamin content per gram of biomass rather than merely increasing titers through higher CDW. In contrast, some high-biomass conditions with minimal nitrogen showed comparatively lower mg/g CDW values, suggesting that their higher volumetric titers largely reflected increased cell mass rather than improved intracellular vitamin accumulation.

Increasing the initial glucose concentration from 20 to 40 g/L substantially enhanced the accumulation of several B-vitamins, particularly under yeast extract supplementation, with clear increases in both volumetric titers and biomass-specific vitamin contents. Maximum yields reached 262.28 mg/L (B1), 321.36 mg/L (B3), 137.12 mg/L (B6), and 6.91 mg/L (B12) at 72 h, 96 h, 36 h, and 96 h, respectively (Fig. 4C and 5C). In contrast, the use of YNB at high glucose loads resulted in reduced vitamin B concentrations, with maxima of 123.88 mg/L (B1), 136.11 mg/L (B3), 55.53 mg/L (B6) and 3.10 mg/L (B12) (Fig. 4D and 5D).

Substitution of glucose with xylose as the sole carbon source severely diminished vitamin B production (Fig. 6, Fig. 7). In media containing 20 g/L xylose and yeast extract, maximal B1, B3, B6, and B12 levels were 145.65, 57.47, 46.78, and 4.08 mg/L, respectively (Fig. 6A and 7A), with further reductions observed when YNB was the nitrogen source (53.29, 48.77, 20.79, and 3.91 mg/L; Fig. 6B and 7B). Doubling the initial xylose concentration did not enhance vitamin production, and in some cases, further limited B12 synthesis (Fig. 6C–D, 7C-D).

Importantly, increasing the total sugar concentration in mixed sugar fermentations (to 40 g/L) increased overall vitamin output regardless of the nitrogen source, although yeast extract remained superior. Maximum vitamin concentrations achieved with yeast extract were 210.99 mg/L (B1), 102.26 mg/L (B3), 7.18 mg/L (B6), 3.89 mg/L (B9), and 2.15 mg/L (B12) (Fig. 8C and 9C). When YNB was provided, maxima were substantially lower (80.90, 15.51, 2.23, 0.92, and 0.92 mg/L, respectively; Fig. 8D and 9D).

Vitamin profiles for C-sugar fermentations mirrored those found with mixed sugars. At 20 g/L, yeast extract supplementation yielded up to 116.03 mg/L (B1), 166.49 mg/L (B3), 2.11 mg/L (B6), 3.19 mg/L (B9), and 1.44 mg/L (B12) (Fig. 8E and 9E), whereas YNB resulted in lower accumulations (73.81, 66.89, 3.21, 3.23, and 1.93 mg/L; Fig. 8F and 9F). All reported values represent the mean of biological duplicates.

Collectively, these results show that both vitamin type and accumulation kinetics are strongly dependent on the choice and concentration of carbon and nitrogen sources. Yeast extract consistently promoted higher B-vitamin yields, especially in glucose-rich and mixed-sugar fermentations. Mixed sugars and lignocellulosic C-sugars uniquely enabled biosynthesis of B9, suggesting enhanced metabolic routing and pentose phosphate pathway activation (Fig. 10). These findings highlight the potential for food-compatible fermentation formulations to optimize B-vitamin enrichment in Y. lipolytica for functional food applications.

Discussion

Substrate-dependent biomass production and implications for functional food ingredient development

This study demonstrated that Y. lipolytica, a GRAS-status yeast, can serve as a sustainable platform for producing B-vitamin-enriched biomass suitable for functional food applications. Table 3 summarizes cell biomass and B-vitamin production under various fermentation conditions, revealing critical relationships between substrate selection, nitrogen source, and micronutrient accumulation. Understanding these relationships is essential for developing economically viable and nutritionally optimized fermentation processes for food ingredient fortification.

In this work we focused on Y. lipolytica TISTR5212, a wild-type strain obtained from the Thailand Institute of Scientific and Technological Research (TISTR). The elevated intracellular B-vitamin contents reported here therefore reflect the combined effect of this specific genetic background and the optimized fermentation conditions, rather than representing a universal property of all Y. lipolytica strains. Comparison with literature data for other Y. lipolytica isolates (e.g., strain A-101 grown on biofuel waste) in Table 4 highlights that substantial strain-to-strain variability in vitamin content can occur, underscoring the need for broader strain screening to identify genotypes best suited for multivitamin production.

Y. lipolytica exhibited superior growth on glucose, achieving maximum OD600 values of 52.67 and 72.40 with 20 and 40 g/L glucose supplemented with yeast extract, respectively (Fig. 1A and C, Table 3). Substituting yeast extract with YNB reduced biomass yields by 37-48% under equivalent glucose concentrations (Fig. 1B and D), highlighting the critical importance of nitrogen source quality for maximizing biomass production. This finding has important implications for industrial fermentation economics, as higher biomass yields can increase the volumetric output of vitamin-enriched yeast biomass, although the nutritional value of the final ingredient also depends on vitamin content per unit biomass.

Xylose utilization was markedly slower than glucose consumption, consistent with the preferential glucose metabolism characteristics of Y. lipolytica [30,31]. Complete xylose consumption occurred only with 20 g/L xylose and yeast extract after 96 h, yielding an OD600 of 37.47 (Fig. 2A). The native Y. lipolytica strain exhibited limited growth when xylose served as the sole carbon source [31,32], though genetic engineering approaches targeting xylose metabolism genes (XYR, XDH, XKS) have demonstrated improved xylose utilization in engineered strains [33]. Nevertheless, the capacity of the wild-type strain to utilize xylose, albeit slowly, represents an important attribute for processing lignocellulosic hydrolysate—a renewable, low-cost feedstock for sustainable food ingredient production. Mixed sugar fermentation exhibited sequential substrate utilization driven by carbon catabolite repression (CCR), with glucose depletion preceding xylose assimilation [30,32]. This sequential consumption pattern resulted in intermediate biomass production (OD600 of 50.20-64.80) (Fig. 3A–D, Table 3). From a food processing perspective, the ability of Y. lipolytica to co-utilize mixed sugars derived from lignocellulosic biomass or food-processing side streams positions this strain as a versatile host for circular-economy ingredient production [30,34]. Commercial cellulosic sugars (C-sugars) yielded biomass levels comparable to those obtained with synthetic mixed sugars, demonstrating that industrial cellulosic syrups can be used directly as fermentation feedstocks without compromising growth or vitamin accumulation. In practical terms, glucose-rich media are well suited for generating yeast biomass enriched in thiamine, niacin and pyridoxine, which can subsequently be dried and blended into doughs, batters or beverage bases where these vitamins are limiting. Mixed-sugar or lignocellulosic formulations, by contrast, are attractive when folate-enriched biomass is desired, such as for breakfast cereals, bakery products or plant-based dairy alternatives targeting enhanced folate intake (Fig. 3E and F). These application-oriented interpretations connect laboratory fermentation outcomes with realistic product formats in which Y. lipolytica-derived ingredients could be implemented.

Nitrogen source complexity drives biomass yield and metabolic capacity

The stark contrast between yeast extract and YNB performance reveals fundamental nutritional requirements for optimizing Y. lipolytica fermentations for food applications. Yeast extract, a complex nitrogen source containing amino acids, peptides, nucleotides, and B-vitamins (Table 2), consistently outperformed YNB across all carbon substrate conditions [35,36]. This advantage was particularly pronounced in mixed sugar fermentations, where yeast extract-supplemented cultures (20 g/L mixed sugars) reached stationary phase within 36 h at OD600 exceeding 50.20 (Fig. 3A), contrasting with 45% lower biomass in YNB media despite identical initial C/N ratios (Fig. 3B).

The growth advantage conferred by yeast extract stems from its supplemental micronutrients, including thiamine (B1), niacin (B3), and pyridoxine (B6), which are absent in YNB (Table 2). B6 can be imported from the medium by the high-affinity proton symporter Tpn1 or synthesized de novo. The imported forms (pyridoxine, pyridoxamine, and pyridoxal) are phosphorylated by the pyridoxine kinase Bud16 and subsequently oxidized to pyridoxal-5-phosphate (PLP) by the pyridoxine oxidase Pdx31. Additionally, d-glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate, l-glutamine, and keto-d-ribose 5-phosphate can be transformed into PLP through the action of proteins encoded by the SNO1,2,3 and SNZ1,2,3 genes [37]. These cofactors facilitate enzymatic reactions in central carbon metabolism, particularly within the pentose phosphate pathway required for xylose utilization [37,38]. Yeast extract also significantly reduced lag phases when Y. lipolytica was cultivated with high carbon concentrations, enabling high-cell-density cultivation through simplified batch processes [39]. Worland et al. [12] reported 2-fold higher β-carotene concentrations in yeast extract-supplemented media compared to YNB-based formulations, further demonstrating the stimulatory effects of complex nitrogen sources on secondary metabolite biosynthesis.

Conversely, YNB's minimal composition (0.67 g/L) created nutrient bottlenecks despite adequate carbon availability. At 20 g/L mixed sugars, biomass production plateaued prematurely at OD600 of 22.47 at 36 h (Fig. 3B), indicating nitrogen exhaustion. Increasing carbon load to 40 g/L exacerbated this limitation, with cultures exhibiting slow growth and reduced maximum biomass (OD600 of 25.20 at 60 h) compared to yeast extract counterparts (Fig. 3D). This C/N imbalance likely triggered stress responses that diverted cellular resources from growth to maintenance metabolism. While YNB enables precise control of nitrogen levels for mechanistic studies [40,41], yeast extract remains the superior choice for industrial-scale production of vitamin-enriched biomass, balancing nutritional support with cost-effectiveness for food-grade fermentation processes. These findings also inform the design of food-oriented fermentation strategies. Complex nitrogen sources such as yeast extract not only stimulate vitamin biosynthesis but also facilitate robust high-cell-density cultures in simple batch mode, which is advantageous for the economical production of dried yeast ingredients for use in foods. In contrast, minimal media based on YNB are more appropriate for mechanistic investigations where precise control of nutrient supply is required, but are less attractive for large-scale manufacturing of vitamin-enriched biomass intended for incorporation into real food matrices. Selecting nitrogen sources with these considerations in mind can help align fermentation performance with downstream application needs.

Carbon source specificity in B-vitamin biosynthesis: mechanistic insights and food fortification strategies

In this work, intracellular vitamin levels were quantified under different fermentation conditions, whereas the pathway discussion is intended to provide biochemical context for the observed patterns rather than direct proof of pathway-specific flux or enzyme activity. Unlike biomass production, B-vitamin accumulation patterns varied dramatically across fermentation conditions, revealing complex metabolic regulation responsive to carbon and nitrogen sources (Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7, Fig. 8, Fig. 9, Table 3). Understanding these carbon source-specific vitamin profiles is critical for developing targeted fermentation strategies to produce customized nutrient-enriched food ingredients. By normalizing vitamin titers to CDW, we could distinguish conditions in which higher B-vitamin titers primarily result from increased biomass formation from those in which cells also contain more vitamin per gram biomass. Glucose with yeast extract increased both CDW and mg/g CDW values for B1, B3 and B6, whereas some mixed-sugar and minimal-nitrogen conditions exhibited moderate titers but relatively low biomass-specific contents, indicating that volumetric performance in these cases is driven largely by biomass.

The supernatant time-course data further clarify how these intracellular patterns should be interpreted. Under representative glucose- and xylose-based conditions with either yeast extract or YNB, extracellular B-vitamin concentrations remained low throughout cultivation compared with intracellular levels, and no major accumulation of vitamins in the broth was observed (Fig. 11). This behavior is consistent with Y. lipolytica acting primarily as a sink that retains vitamins inside the cells, whether those vitamins originate from de novo biosynthesis or from uptake and salvage, while net secretion into the medium remains limited under the tested batch conditions.

Glucose fermentation yielded the highest concentrations of vitamin B1 (262.28 mg/L), B3 (321.36 mg/L), and B6 (137.12 mg/L) in the 40 g/L glucose with yeast extract condition (Fig. 4C and 5C, and Table 3). These impressive titers, which exceed previously reported levels from Y. lipolytica and other microorganisms (Table 4), reflect the strong metabolic flux through glycolysis and the TCA cycle when glucose serves as the carbon source [42,43]. Niacin (B) accumulation was particularly robust due to cellular demand for NAD+ as an electron acceptor in central carbon metabolism [43]. The biosynthetic connection between vitamins B3 and B1—where NAD+ participates in thiazole ring synthesis during thiamine biosynthesis [26,44,45]—explains the coordinated high-level accumulation of these vitamins under glucose-rich conditions. Thiamine biosynthesis in yeasts proceeds through well-characterized pyrimidine and thiazole branch pathways that converge to form thiamine diphosphate and is tightly integrated with central carbon and nitrogen metabolism. In our study, we do not experimentally resolve individual enzymatic steps or transport processes; instead, we use this established biochemical framework to interpret how changes in carbon source and nitrogen complexity translate into altered intracellular B1, B3 and B6 contents. The strong co-accumulation of B1 and B3 under glucose-rich, yeast-extract conditions is consistent with the high demand for NAD in central metabolism and its known involvement in thiazole formation, but confirming the contribution of specific enzymes or transporters will require targeted genetic and flux analyses beyond the scope of this work [11,26,44,45].

Xylose fermentation produced substantially reduced vitamin yields compared to glucose, with vitamin B12 decreasing to only 0.14 mg/L in 40 g/L xylose with YNB (Fig. 6D and 7D, Table 3). The slower xylose metabolism and lower biomass yield inherently limited vitamin production capacity. However, the ability to produce any B-vitamins from xylose remains significant from a sustainability perspective, as xylose-rich streams from lignocellulosic processing represent abundant, underutilized resources for food ingredient production. Reporting both volumetric titers (mg/L) and biomass-normalized contents (mg/g CDW) clarifies whether nutrient manipulations primarily affect vitamin production capacity per cell, total biomass formation, or both. For example, xylose-based cultures displayed lower titers and lower mg/g CDW for most vitamins relative to glucose-based cultures, indicating that both reduced cell yield and reduced intracellular vitamin content contribute to the diminished performance under these conditions.

The most striking carbon source-dependent effects emerged during mixed sugar fermentation, which uniquely enabled vitamin B9 (folate) accumulation while dramatically reducing vitamin B6 synthesis (Fig. 8, Fig. 9, Table 3). In 20 g/L mixed sugars with yeast extract, folate reached 4.46 mg/L (Fig. 9A), while vitamin B6 decreased by 95% to only 2.98 mg/L compared to glucose fermentation (Fig. 8A). This metabolic shift reflects the complex interplay between carbon metabolism and vitamin biosynthesis pathways, with relevance to folate's biosynthesis requirements.

Folate biosynthesis in mixed sugar fermentation: pentose phosphate pathway activation for functional food application

The emergence of intracellular folate (B9) quantified here as folic-acid equivalents, specifically during mixed-sugar and C-sugar fermentations represents a novel and significant finding with important implications for developing folate-enriched functional food ingredients. Folate biosynthesis requires precursors from both purine metabolism (GTP) and the shikimate pathway via erythrose-4-phosphate, whose availability depends on carbon flow through the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). Mixed-sugar conditions, particularly those containing xylose, are known to enhance PPP activity, which increases erythrose-4-phosphate and thereby supports 4-aminobenzoic acid formation, a key folate precursor. Our observation that folate (B9, expressed as folic-acid equivalents) is detected only under mixed-sugar and C-sugar conditions in Y. lipolytica aligns with reports in other yeasts that mixed glucose−xylose substrates stimulate folate production via PPP activation [38,46]. The synergistic effects of xylose-activated PPP (providing pABA precursors) and glucose-driven purine synthesis (providing GTP derivatives) create optimal conditions for folate accumulation in mixed sugar environments.

Conversely, glucose-only fermentation supports robust growth and metabolism but provides limited PPP flux due to preferential glycolytic routing and CCR effects [30,32]. This metabolic bias explains the absence of detectable folate in glucose-only cultures despite high biomass yields. The dramatic reduction in vitamin B6 during mixed sugar fermentation (Fig. 8, Fig. 9) suggests metabolic competition between B6 and B9 pathways. Since pyridoxal-5-phosphate (PLP, the active form of B6) serves as a precursor for thiamine biosynthesis via HMP-PP synthesis [11,43], the cellular demand for B1 under all conditions may divert B6 toward thiamine production rather than accumulation as free B6.

Because our HPLC method did not resolve individual folate vitamers or polyglutamylation states, further work using deconjugation and LC−MS or specialized HPLC protocols will be needed to define the detailed folate profile and to assess the nutritional bioavailability of B9 in Y. lipolytica biomass.

Cobalamin accumulation: uptake and retention mechanisms in food-grade yeast biomass

The detection of vitamin B12 (cobalamin) in Y. lipolytica biomass across all substrate and nitrogen conditions (Fig. 5, Fig. 7, Fig. 9, Table 3) represents a significant finding for food biotechnology applications, given the scarcity of B12 in plant-based diets. However, Y. lipolytica is not known to possess a complete cobalamin biosynthesis pathway, and our data therefore support uptake and intracellular retention of B12 from the medium rather than de novo synthesis [3,42]. This interpretation is supported by the supernatant vitamin time-courses, which show that extracellular B12 levels remain low throughout cultivation under yeast extract supplementation, while B12 is consistently detected in the biomass (Fig. 11). Together with the known presence of trace B12 in yeast extract (Table 2), these data strongly support uptake and intracellular retention of medium-derived B12 rather than de novo cobalamin biosynthesis in Y. lipolytica.

The observed B12 in biomass is most plausibly explained by uptake and intracellular retention of trace cobalamin present in the culture medium rather than by bacterial contamination or de novo synthesis by Y. lipolytica. No bacterial contamination was detected during fermentation based on routine aseptic handling, microscopic inspection, and culture purity checks. Because extracellular B12 was not monitored over time, a complete mass balance could not be established; accordingly, the present interpretation is based on the known metabolic capacity of Y. lipolytica, the composition of the medium, and the consistent detection of B12 in harvested biomass. The metabolic fate of B12 in Y. lipolytica involves its participation in methionine biosynthesis, where methylcobalamin (a B12 derivative) facilitates homocysteine-to-methionine conversion in collaboration with tetrahydrofolate (the active form of B9) [17,47]. The reversible conversion between methylcobalamin and cobalamin enables B12 retention within cellular pools, explaining its accumulation in biomass.

The rare detection of free folate (B9) in biomass despite active biosynthesis in mixed sugar conditions reflects this metabolic channeling—folate accumulates predominantly as tetrahydrofolate bound in methylation reactions rather than free folic acid [17,26]. From a food ingredient perspective, the capacity to concentrate B12 from fermentation media into yeast biomass offers a practical route for biofortification, particularly if fermentation designs intentionally supplement B12-containing media components to enhance final biomass B12 content for food applications.

Food science implications: toward sustainable vitamin-enriched ingredients

The quantitative vitamin profiles achieved in this study represent substantial advances over previously reported microbial production systems (Table 4). Our results demonstrate that Y. lipolytica TISTR5212 cultivated in optimized conditions (40 g/L glucose with yeast extract) produces thiamine (262.28 mg/L), niacin (321.36 mg/L), and pyridoxine (137.12 mg/L)—significantly exceeding yields from other yeast and bacterial species. Moreover, the novel detection of folate (4.46 mg/L) in mixed sugar fermentation and cobalamin retention across all conditions position Y. lipolytica as a versatile multivitamin source for functional food fortification. Because these comparisons are made across different Y. lipolytica strains and culture conditions, the high vitamin levels observed for TISTR5212 should be viewed as evidence of the potential achievable within the species, rather than as a general specification for all Y. lipolytica strains.

Several factors contributed to these improvements: (i) use of defined media with optimized C/N ratios compared to complex waste substrates employed in prior studies, enabling precise nutrient control; (ii) systematic fermentation optimization targeting specific vitamin biosynthesis pathways; (iii) strategic nitrogen management through yeast extract supplementation, providing essential cofactors; and (iv) mixed sugar utilization, which uniquely activates the pentose phosphate pathway to enable folate production. These mechanistic insights provide actionable design principles for industrial fermentation processes.

From a food processing perspective, the ability to tailor vitamin profiles through substrate selection offers considerable flexibility for designing yeast-derived fortification ingredients with distinct nutritional targets. Glucose-based fermentations maximize B1, B3, and B6 content for applications requiring these specific vitamins, while mixed sugar fermentations produce folate-enriched biomass—particularly valuable given folate's importance in preventing neural tube defects and its limited bioavailability in many diets. The utilization of lignocellulosic sugars and C-sugars for vitamin production aligns with circular economy principles, converting agricultural and food processing residues into high-value nutritional ingredients [34].

The GRAS status of Y. lipolytica and its established use in food applications (e.g., citric acid production, lipid-based products) facilitate regulatory acceptance of vitamin-enriched biomass as a food ingredient [3,42]. Potential applications include direct incorporation into bakery products, beverages, nutritional supplements, and plant-based meat alternatives, addressing the growing consumer demand for natural, sustainable vitamin sources. The production of multiple B-vitamins simultaneously within a single fermentation process offers economic advantages over chemical synthesis or multi-strain fermentation systems requiring separate optimization for each vitamin.

Limitations and future directions

While this study establishes foundational principles for B-vitamin production in Y. lipolytica, several aspects must be addressed before full implementation in commercial food systems. First, targeted media optimization and, where appropriate, genetic interventions that preserve GRAS status could be used to further enhance folate and cobalamin contents, thereby broadening the nutritional impact of the yeast biomass. Second, scale-up studies are required to validate the fermentation strategy at pilot and production scales, including assessment of process economics, reproducibility of vitamin profiles and compliance with food-grade quality and safety standards.

An important limitation of the present study is that we did not directly distinguish between de novo synthesis, salvage, and uptake of individual B-vitamins, particularly in the case of thiamine (B1), niacin (B3), pyridoxine (B6), and folate (B9). Although our pathway analysis and media composition (in particular the presence of vitamins in yeast extract) provide strong mechanistic cues—most notably supporting medium-derived cobalamin uptake—definitive resolution of synthesis versus uptake would require vitamin-free or vitamin-defined media, isotopic tracer studies, and time-resolved measurement of vitamin concentrations in both biomass and culture supernatants (Fig. 10). While this study includes time-resolved measurements of vitamin B concentrations in the culture supernatant under representative conditions (Fig. 11), these data are not sufficient to close complete vitamin mass balance or to unambiguously separate de novo biosynthesis from uptake and salvage for each vitamin. We explicitly acknowledge that even a minimal confirmatory experiment in vitamin-free medium would have provided stronger evidence for de novo biosynthesis of B1, B3, B6, and B9 in Y. lipolytica, and we therefore consider implementation of such vitamin-free fermentations, coupled with parallel measurements of intracellular and extracellular vitamin levels, as an immediate next step in our research program rather than a distinct, exploratory future direction. Subsequent studies will build on these initial vitamin-free experiments by incorporating more comprehensive vitamin-defined media, expanded time-course sampling, and where feasible, isotope-tracer analyses to quantitatively distinguish net intracellular biosynthesis from uptake-driven accumulation for individual B-vitamins.

Another important avenue for future research is systematic evaluation of strain diversity within Y. lipolytica. The present study demonstrates that TISTR5212 can accumulate multiple B-vitamins at nutritionally relevant levels, but it does not establish whether these profiles are typical for the species. High-throughput screening and genomic characterization of additional wild-type and industrial strains, under comparable fermentation conditions, will be required to disentangle species-level traits from strain-specific properties and to select or breed strains optimized for multivitamin enrichment.

Equally important is understanding how vitamin-enriched Y. lipolytica biomass behaves during downstream food processing. Future work should quantify the stability and bioavailability of B-vitamins when the biomass is incorporated into model products and subjected to thermal treatments typical of pasteurized beverages, baked goods and extruded snacks, as well as during subsequent storage. Such studies will determine the extent to which microbial vitamin content is retained in finished products and will inform realistic fortification levels and label claims. Comparative in vitro digestion or in vivo feeding studies contrasting vitamin uptake from yeast-based ingredients versus synthetic supplements would provide critical validation of functional benefits.

Finally, expanding substrate diversity beyond the sugar streams examined here—for example to dairy whey, fruit-pomace hydrolysates and other food-processing side-streams—could further enhance the sustainability profile of the process while revealing additional metabolic responses relevant to vitamin biosynthesis. Collectively, these investigations will complete the bridge from laboratory-scale fermentation optimization to robust development of Y. lipolytica-derived ingredients in real food systems.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that Y. lipolytica TISTR5212 is a robust, food-grade platform for the production of B-vitamin-enriched biomass through strategic manipulation of carbon and nitrogen sources. Glucose-based fermentations maximize thiamine (B1), niacin (B3) and pyridoxine (B6) accumulation, whereas mixed sugar fermentations uniquely promote folate (B9) biosynthesis via activation of the pentose phosphate pathway. Yeast extract markedly enhances both biomass formation and vitamin production across substrate conditions, underscoring the importance of nitrogen source complexity for high-cell-density, vitamin-rich cultures. The successful use of lignocellulosic commercial C-sugars further supports sustainable, circular-economy approaches that valorize renewable or side-stream carbon sources for value-added ingredient production.

By interpreting these fermentation outcomes in the context of prospective food applications, the present work provides actionable bioprocess design principles for tailoring vitamin profiles in Y. lipolytica biomass to specific nutritional targets. The results indicate that vitamin-enriched yeast could be deployed as a clean-label fortification ingredient in plant-based beverages, cereal-based products and other functional foods, provided that future studies confirm vitamin stability and bioavailability under industrial processing conditions. Overall, this study advances microbial production platforms for food fortification and respond to the growing demand for natural, plant-compatible sources for B-vitamins in modern diets.
