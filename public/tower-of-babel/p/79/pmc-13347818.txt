Epidemiology of Treponema pallidum, Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Trichomonas vaginalis among forcibly displaced populations: a global systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ghina R Mumtaz, Iman Fakih, Eman Sharara, Rayane El-Khoury, Cyrine Naja, Laith J Abu-Raddad, Sasha A Fahme
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurable sexually transmitted infections (STIs), including Treponema pallidum (syphilis), Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Trichomonas vaginalis, remain a major global health concern; yet their epidemiology among forcibly displaced populations is poorly understood. Limited access to healthcare, high-risk exposures and structural vulnerabilities may contribute to a heightened burden of STIs in these populations.MethodsA systematic review conducted up to 12 July 2024 assessed the prevalence of syphilis, C. trachomatis, N. gonorrhoeae and T. vaginalis infections among refugees, asylum seekers and internally displaced populations globally. Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines were followed. Pooled mean prevalence of current and/or lifetime infection for each STI was estimated through random-effects meta-analyses. Random-effects meta-regression analysis was feasible for syphilis and investigated sources of heterogeneity, associated factors and temporal trends in prevalence.ResultsThe review identified 82 reports contributing 232 prevalence measures based on over 1.1 million test results, with syphilis being the most frequently studied STI. The estimated pooled mean prevalence of current infection was 0.94% (95% CI 0.53% to 1.45%) for syphilis, 0.87% (95% CI 0.33% to 1.61%) for C. trachomatis, 0.14% (95% CI 0.06% to 0.32%) for N. gonorrhoeae and 17.07% (95% CI 0.07% to 51.37%) for T. vaginalis. The prevalence of lifetime syphilis was 2.78% (95% CI 1.54% to 4.31%). Meta-regression analyses explained 64% of the variation in syphilis prevalence. Populations displaced from the African region had a 2.90-fold higher prevalence of syphilis compared with those from the Eastern Mediterranean region (95% CI 1.58 to 5.33), and those hosted in low-income countries had a 5.20-fold higher prevalence than those in high-income countries (95% CI 1.47 to 18.38). Syphilis prevalence declined over time at a relative annual rate of 6%.ConclusionThis review highlights a substantial burden of syphilis among forcibly displaced populations, suggesting active transmission. Evidence for the other curable STIs is more limited, hindering a comprehensive understanding of their epidemiology in humanitarian settings. Systematic screening campaigns and effective STI surveillance are urgently needed, particularly in low-resource settings.

Introduction

Curable sexually transmitted infections (STIs), including syphilis, chlamydia, gonorrhoea and trichomoniasis, are prevalent worldwide, with over 1 million new infections estimated to occur daily.13 The epidemiology of these infections in forced displacement settings is poorly understood. As armed conflicts, political crises and human rights violations escalate, the number of forcibly displaced people worldwide has nearly doubled in the past decade,4 with projections suggesting that over one billion people could be displaced by 2050.5 6

Refugees, asylum seekers and internally displaced populations (IDPs) have typically limited access to STI screening while displaced. On average, refugees remain displaced for 20 years,7 8 predominantly in neighbouring low- and middle-income countries (LMICs),4 which often have limited capacity to integrate them into national health systems.9 10 In these settings, insufficient communicable disease surveillance infrastructure, limited sexual health services capacity and competing health priorities hinder our understanding of the burden of curable STIs among forcibly displaced populations.1113 Meanwhile, some high-income countries have instituted STI screening programmes for incoming refugees and asylum seekers.14 15 However, these initiatives predominantly focus on HIV and syphilis, are often based on individual risk assessments and exclude undocumented individuals.14 15 Substantial country-wide and subnational variations in the scope and implementation of such screening campaigns further contribute to the knowledge gap.15

Structural vulnerabilities and sexual-risk behaviour within dynamic sexual networks may increase the risk of STI transmission during forced displacement.1618 Factors such as sexual violence (both in origin and host countries),19 20 early marriage,21 22 transactional sex for essential goods and services23 24 and substance use25 have been documented in various displacement settings, where they are compounded by limited access to STI prevention and treatment.26 Certain displaced subpopulations, including women, adolescents and sexual and gender minorities, may be particularly vulnerable to STIs.11 22 27 28

While sexual health determinants have been well-documented among diverse forcibly displaced populations globally,29 their explicit association with curable STIs remains insufficiently characterised. One recent narrative review on sexual health challenges among displaced populations reported higher STI prevalence compared with host populations.30 However, this review only included publications from 2022 to 2023 and did not distinguish refugees, asylum seekers and IDPs from migrant populations. Yet, these two groups differ significantly in their reasons and conditions of displacement, legal status, healthcare access and social integration, leading to distinct vulnerabilities to sexual and reproductive health outcomes.26 Nonetheless, the review underscored a substantial gap in knowledge of the epidemiology of STIs among displaced populations.30

To address these gaps, this systematic review and meta-analysis aims to characterise the global epidemiology of Treponema pallidum (syphilis), Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Trichomonas vaginalis infections among persons forcibly displaced by armed and political conflicts, natural disasters and famine. The specific objectives are to (1) systematically review and synthesise available evidence on the prevalence of these infections; (2) estimate the pooled mean prevalence of each STI and (3) identify sources of between-study heterogeneity, factors associated with infections’ prevalence and temporal trends of STIs among these populations.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

We conducted a systematic review informed by Cochrane’s collaboration guidelines.31 Findings were reported based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.32 33

We searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and Scopus up to 12 July 2024, without language or date restrictions. Our search strategy was broad and incorporated both free-text and indexing terms, which were exploded to include all relevant subheadings. Additionally, we reviewed abstracts from international conferences, including the International AIDS Conference,34 the International AIDS Society Conference on HIV Pathogenesis, Treatment and Prevention35 and the International Society for Sexually Transmitted Disease Research.36 The detailed search strategy is available in online supplemental table 1.

Search results were imported into EndNote (Thomson Reuters, USA), where duplicates were identified and removed. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by three authors (CN, ES and IF). Discrepancies were resolved by GRM. Full texts of potentially relevant citations were then retrieved and assessed for eligibility. Reports containing primary data on the prevalence of syphilis, C. trachomatis, N. gonorrhoeae and T. vaginalis among forcibly displaced populations, regardless of country, region or setting, were eligible for inclusion.

Forcibly displaced populations were defined as refugees, asylum seekers or IDPs displaced due to conflict, natural disasters or famine. Reports on voluntary migrants or displacement due to development projects were excluded. Only studies reporting laboratory-confirmed prevalence estimates were included. Measures based on self-reported or syndromic-only STI diagnoses were excluded. Additionally, qualitative studies, editorials, letters to the editor, commentaries and reviews were excluded, although citations of relevant reviews were screened to ensure no eligible studies were missed. The bibliographies of all included reports were similarly hand-searched to identify additional relevant studies. Prevalence measures extracted from conference abstracts were cross-checked against records identified in the scientific databases to avoid duplication.

Data extraction and synthesis

Data were independently double-extracted by ES and IF using a predesigned, computerised data extraction sheet. Discrepancies were settled by GRM and group consensus. Data from five non-English-language articles (two in German, two in Turkish and one in Danish) were extracted from the English abstract. All other articles were in English. Both overall and stratified prevalence measures were extracted when applicable.

Prevalence measures for each of the four pathogens of interest were stratified by infection type based on the diagnostic test used, as in similar studies.3742 Syphilis measures were classified into four categories: (1) probable current syphilis infection (positive both treponemal and non-treponemal tests); (2) possible current infection, unspecified (positive non-treponemal test, irrespective of treponemal test result if done); (3) lifetime syphilis infection (positive treponemal test, irrespective of non-treponemal test result if done) and (4) unclear (the diagnostic test used was not reported or not adequately described).

While ideally an active syphilis diagnosis would entail non-treponemal antibody titres to distinguish individuals with a past, adequately treated infection, this information was rarely available in the studies identified. We therefore followed WHO guidelines, which rely on a positive result using both non-treponemal and treponemal tests without titres for the diagnosis of a probable current syphilis infection (category 1). More details about the classification, interpretation and limitations in the interpretation of syphilis serological test results can be found in online supplemental table 2.

The remaining infections, C. trachomatis, N. gonorrhoeae and T. vaginalis, were classified as follows: (1) current infection (positive culture, nucleic acid amplification test or microscopy) and (2) unclear diagnostic method (the diagnostic test used was not reported or not adequately described).

Bias and precision assessment

Each prevalence measure was evaluated for precision and risk of bias. Precision was categorised as low or high based on sample size (<500 for low vs ≥500 for high). Risk of bias was assessed using the Appraisal tool for Cross-Sectional Studies (AXIS),43 following methods in similar studies.44 45 Assessment and scoring were conducted independently by two authors (IF and REK), with discrepancies resolved through consensus. After scoring individual quality domains, total scores were calculated, and each STI prevalence measure was classified as having a high (score 0–10) or low (score 11–20) risk of bias.

Publication bias in meta-analyses was assessed, stratified by infection type, using Doi plots along with the Luis Furuya-Kanamori (LFK) index whenever the number of pooled measures exceeded three.46 An asymmetrical Doi plot indicated potential publication bias—the spread of the prevalence measures may not be due to chance alone.46 An LFK index value exceeding ±1 was considered indicative of the presence of publication bias.46

Statistical analysis

For each of the four STIs, the pooled mean prevalence and 95% CIs were estimated using meta-analyses. Estimates for each infection type (current, lifetime and unclear) were pooled using Dersimonian-Laird random-effects models, with inverse-variance weighting to account for sampling variation and to capture true heterogeneity.47 48 Variances were stabilised using the Freeman-Tukey type arcsine square-root transformation.47 49 In cases where back-transformation from the Freeman-Tukey method was inappropriate, generalised linear mixed models with a logit transformation were employed.50 These models account for the binomial distribution of the data and are recommended when traditional transformations are inadequate.50

When available, stratified prevalence measures, rather than the overall measure, were used in the meta-analyses. When more than one stratification was reported in a single report, we used only one stratification based on a predefined hierarchy that prioritises test modality, followed by country of origin, population type, year of data collection, sex, collection site and then age. One outlier report published in 1951 was excluded from the meta-analysis; all other studies were published after 1977. Meta-analyses were conducted when at least three prevalence measures were available. 13 prevalence measures were reported without documentation of the sample size or the number of positive cases. For these measures, the sample size was imputed using the median sample size of all other prevalence measures included in the systematic review. The I2 heterogeneity metric was computed to estimate variation across studies that is due to real differences in effect size rather than chance.51 52

Random-effects meta-regression analysis was conducted to investigate sources of between-study heterogeneity and identify factors associated with syphilis prevalence. There was an insufficient number of data points to conduct meta-regression analyses for the other STIs. Population-related and study methodology-related independent variables were considered a priori. Population characteristics included type of forcibly displaced population, region of the country of origin, region of the host country, sex at birth and mid-year of data collection. Missing mid-year data collection was imputed by subtracting 4 years from the year of publication—the mean difference between the year of publication and mid-year data collection for all reports with data on both dates. The region of origin was categorised based on the WHO regions,53 while the host region was categorised based on income as per World Bank classification.54 Study methodology characteristics included infection type (as classified above), participant recruitment site (camp, shelter, reception centre, health facility and others), sampling methodology (convenience vs probability-based), precision (low vs high) and risk of bias (low vs high).

Univariable and multivariable meta-regression models were conducted using log-transformed prevalence measures. Crude and adjusted prevalence ratios (APR) and corresponding 95% CIs were reported. Factors associated with prevalence at p≤0.20 in the univariable analyses were included in the multivariable analysis. Factors with p≤0.05 in the multivariable model were considered significant predictors of prevalence.

All analyses were conducted in R V.4.3.2.55 The meta package was used for the conduct of meta-analyses and the creation of forest plots.56 The metafor package was used for the conduct of the meta-regressions.57

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not directly involved in this review; we used publicly available data for the analysis.

Role of the funding source

The funders of the study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation or writing of the article. The corresponding author had full access to all the data in the study and had the final responsibility for the decision to submit for publication.

Results

Search results and scope of evidence

As depicted in the PRISMA flowchart (figure 1), the search identified 8672 citations: 1899 from PubMed, 3318 from Embase, 1893 from Web of Science and 1562 from Scopus. After removing duplicates and screening titles and abstracts, 259 unique reports were retrieved for full-text screening. Of these, 188 reports were deemed ineligible and excluded. Additional eligible reports were identified through searches of international conference abstracts (n=2) and hand-searching bibliographies of relevant reports and reviews (n=9), resulting in a total of 82 reports included in the systematic review. These reports contributed 232 overall prevalence measures (henceforth referred to as ‘studies’), detailed individually in online supplemental tables 3-6, which yielded 244 stratified prevalence measures. After excluding one outlier measure published in 1951,58 243 stratified prevalence measures were included in the meta-analysis and meta-regression analysis. These comprised 159 measures for syphilis, 51 for N. gonorrhoeae, 29 for C. trachomatis and 4 for T. vaginalis.

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) flowchart depicting the study selection process for the systematic review.

There were over 1.1 million test results reported for all infections combined. Online supplemental table 7 presents the distribution of all prevalence studies by key characteristics. The studies span a wide timeframe, with the earliest conducted in 194758 and the most recent in 2023.59 Only 18.1% of studies were conducted before 2000, while around half were conducted from 2010 onwards. For all pathogen types, current infection was the most commonly assessed type: 42.4% (n=64) of syphilis prevalence measures, 69.2% (n=18) of C. trachomatis measures, 86.3% (n=44) of N. gonorrhoeae measures and 75.0% (n=3) of T. vaginalis measures.

Refugees were the primary population studied (77.6%), while asylum seekers and IDPs were represented in 15.5% and 1.3% of the studies, respectively. The WHO African region was the most common region of origin for displaced populations (24.6%), followed by the Eastern Mediterranean region (13.8%) and the Western Pacific region (12.5%). Notably, 30.6% of studies reported on populations of mixed origin without stratifying results by specific countries of origin. Most studies were conducted in high-income host countries (78.4%), with only 8.8% conducted in low- or lower-middle-income host countries. The majority of studies (69.4%) included a mixed population of males and females, while 19.4% focused exclusively on females and 3.9% on males (online supplemental table 7).

The majority of studies (79.3%) used convenience sampling for participant recruitment. Recruitment sites were most frequently catered specifically to forcibly displaced populations, such as immigration, asylum-seeking and reception centres (55.2%), followed by health facilities (37.5%). Only a small number of studies (n=16, 6.9%) were conducted in other settings, such as refugee camps or dwellings within host communities (online supplemental table 7). Slightly less than half of the studies (42.7%) had high precision based on the sample size tested, and the majority of studies (83.2%) were deemed at low risk of bias based on their AXIS score (online supplemental table 8).

Publication bias assessment is summarised in online supplemental table 9. With only two exceptions, the meta-analyses for all infection types displayed asymmetrical Doi plots and LFK index values exceeding ±1, indicating the presence of publication bias.

All variables were associated with higher odds of syphilis infection with p<0.20 in the univariable meta-regression analyses and were therefore included in the multivariable model. The year of data collection and the precision of individual estimates each explained 25% of the variation in syphilis prevalence. These were followed by region of origin, host region, type of syphilis infection (as defined above) and recruitment site, each accounting for 17%–19% of the variation. The remaining variables contributed only marginally to the variation in syphilis prevalence.

Notably, refugees from the WHO African region had a 2.90-fold (95% CI 1.58 to 5.33) higher prevalence of syphilis compared with those from the Eastern Mediterranean region. Refugees hosted in low-income countries had a 5.20-fold (95% CI 1.47 to 18.38) higher syphilis prevalence compared with those in high-income countries. Male refugees exhibited more than twice the prevalence of syphilis compared with females (APR 2.59, 95% CI 0.94 to 7.13). Syphilis prevalence decreased by 0.94-fold per calendar year (95% CI 0.92 to 0.98), indicating a 6% relative annual decline in prevalence (online supplemental figure 19). The prevalence of lifetime syphilis infection and that of a possible unspecified current infection were 2.35 (95% CI 1.41 to 3.91) and 2.45 (95% CI 1.47 to 4.10) times higher, respectively, than the prevalence of a probable current syphilis infection. Finally, studies with larger sample sizes reported approximately 50% lower prevalence than those with smaller samples (APR 0.51, 95% CI 0.32 to 0.80) (table 2).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis presents, to our knowledge, the first comprehensive assessment of the epidemiology of four STIs—syphilis, C. trachomatis, N. gonorrhoeae and T. vaginalis—among forcibly displaced populations worldwide. Syphilis was the most frequently studied infection, with findings revealing a considerable burden and marked regional variation. Data on the other infections were more limited, underscoring persistent gaps in surveillance and research.

The pooled mean prevalence of probable current syphilis infection was 0.94%, comparable to a global meta-analysis estimate of 1.11% (95% CI 0.99 to 1.22) in the general population,60 but higher than the WHO 2020 global estimate of the population at large at 0.6% (95% CI 0.5% to 0.7%).2 Methodological differences may explain some variation between these estimates; however, the pooled prevalence suggests active transmission within forcibly displaced populations. This may reflect high-risk sexual exposures in the context of displacement-related social, legal and economic vulnerabilities but could also be driven by the ongoing spread of untreated, often asymptomatic infections due to limited access to STI screening and treatment.26 30 Global shortages in benzathine penicillin may have further exacerbated this issue in recent years.61

Notably, syphilis prevalence was found to be declining over time at an annual rate of 6%. This decline may reflect global improvements in antenatal syphilis screening and treatment,62 as well as the routine inclusion of syphilis testing—alongside HIV screening—in health programmes for refugees and other displaced populations in several high-income host countries, from which the majority of data are derived.14 15 Still, the lack of universal STI screening practices, particularly within resource-limited settings where most displaced populations are resettled, constrains the interpretation of these observed trends.

Calendar time, country of origin and host country collectively explained 60% of the variation in syphilis prevalence. Displaced individuals from the WHO African region had a significantly higher prevalence, likely reflecting the region’s background syphilis prevalence, which is the highest globally at 1.7% in 2020.2 This may also be partly due to cross-reactivity with antibodies against non-venereal treponematoses—such as yaws, bejel and pinta—endemic in several African countries.63 Higher syphilis prevalence has similarly been reported among economic migrants from the African region.30 In parallel, we found that refugees settled in low-income countries had significantly higher syphilis prevalence than those in high-income countries, reflecting heightened vulnerability to poor sexual health in these settings. As forcibly displaced individuals resettle, their risk of syphilis infection becomes shaped by various host-country factors, particularly in protracted displacement situations. These include local syphilis prevalence, sexual network dynamics, structural vulnerabilities and limited access to sexual health services.64

The pooled prevalence of C. trachomatis (0.87%) and N. gonorrhoeae (0.14%) was, on the other hand, substantially lower than the WHO 2020 global estimates of 3.2% (95% CI 2.7 to 3.9%) and 0.7% (95% CI 0.5 to 1.1%), respectively.2 Interpreting these lower prevalence estimates within the context of high vulnerability and displacement is challenging. Similar patterns have been observed for HIV, in which a lower HIV prevalence was paradoxically found in conflict-affected populations in some Sub-Saharan African countries, despite the well-documented increase in the social and structural determinants of STI transmission and severity in humanitarian settings.65 This may be due to social isolation and a decline in mobility and urbanisation during periods of war.6466 However, such conditions may not be applicable to all settings, such as in the Eastern Mediterranean region, where millions of displaced people have resettled in urban settings within neighbouring countries over the years.9 67 68

Several other factors may contribute to the observed low prevalence of C. trachomatis and N. gonorrhoeae. Most studies reporting on these infections were conducted in high-income host countries, where sexual health services are more accessible. In LMICs where antimicrobial stewardship is often inadequate, the widespread overuse of oral antibiotics may have contributed to the lower infection burden observed.4 69 Additionally, C. trachomatis and N. gonorrhoeae prevalence tend to be highest among younger individuals aged 15–24 years,7072 whereas the subset of studies that reported participants’ age indicated a slightly older population (average of 26 years, data not shown).

Despite the relatively large number of identified studies reporting over 1.1 million test results, significant knowledge gaps remain regarding the burden of curable STIs among forcibly displaced populations. Most data were on syphilis, allowing for more robust prevalence estimates, while evidence for N. gonorrhoeae, C. trachomatis and T. vaginalis was limited. For C. trachomatis and N. gonorrhoeae, the lack of widely available rapid tests and reliance on syndromic management in many low-resource and humanitarian settings likely contributed to the scarcity of prevalence data. Although some point-of-care options exist for T. vaginalis, they are not widely deployed in humanitarian programmes, and the infection is often overlooked. Only three studies reported on T. vaginalis prevalence, precluding a reliable pooled estimate. As such, while our conclusions are most reliable for syphilis, the limited data on the other infections suggest a variable and possibly underestimated burden, pointing to the urgent need to strengthen diagnostic capacity and generate more representative data for these infections.

Most data were collected over the past 15 years, aligning with the rapid increase in forcibly displaced populations in recent decades. However, the regional distribution of available prevalence measures does not reflect global displacement patterns, and as a result, our findings may not be representative of broader forcibly displaced populations. For instance, only 21.6% of prevalence measures were determined in LMICs, despite these countries hosting 75% of the world’s displaced population.4 Refugees from the Eastern Mediterranean region were also under-represented, although three out of the six countries from which the majority of refugees originate—Afghanistan, Syria and Sudan—are situated in this region.4 Notably, only seven studies reported on Syrian refugees and three on Afghani refugees, the two largest refugee populations globally.4 Syrian refugees in particular have been living in protracted displacement for over a decade. Although the number of studies identified in the systematic review increased over time, this population was not captured in very recent literature, underscoring a critical gap in available data.

Despite comprising the vast majority of forcibly displaced populations globally,4 IDPs are also severely under-represented in available data, accounting for less than 2% of study populations in this review. Women and adolescents are disproportionally impacted by internal displacement,4 often residing in complex humanitarian settings where ongoing conflict and heightened risk of sexual violence73 74 may drive STI transmission, while simultaneously limiting access to healthcare.75 In contrast, most studies included in this review were conducted in high-income European settings where female refugees constitute a minority of the displaced population,76 despite women comprising approximately half of refugees and asylum seekers globally.4 As a result, the female refugees examined in these studies may not be representative of the broader female refugee population.

While women in forced displacement settings face increased STI-related vulnerabilities,77 they are also more likely than men to seek healthcare, particularly antenatal care,78 which presents opportunities for STI detection and treatment. This is especially relevant in high-income host countries with potentially better access to sexual and reproductive health services. Still, our findings underscore the need to further study sex- and gender-based differences in STI transmission and risk factors among forcibly displaced populations, as these patterns may vary across sociocultural contexts and economic regions of resettlement. Additionally, as the health needs of IDPs remain largely understudied and poorly characterised,79 more research is urgently needed among conflict-affected IDPs to better estimate the prevalence and determinants of curable STIs in these settings.

Finally, our review did not identify any studies reporting STI prevalence among forcibly displaced individuals who are men who have sex with men, sex workers or other key populations. Given the disproportionate burden of STIs among these groups, this represents a significant gap in existing research and overlooks the intersecting vulnerabilities of forced displacement and key population status. Future studies should prioritise the inclusion of these populations to provide a more comprehensive understanding of STI epidemiology in humanitarian settings.

Reliable estimation of STI burden among forcibly displaced populations is hindered by a lack of standardised surveillance systems and comprehensive, population-based data collection in both LMICs and high-income host countries. Routine programmatic data contain significant gaps due to the transient and often undocumented status of these populations, making continuous monitoring and follow-up difficult. Exclusionary policies in host countries, including restrictions on healthcare access for undocumented individuals, further limit the availability, reliability and representativeness of surveillance data. In LMICs, where 75% of the global refugee population and the overwhelming majority of IDPs are settled,4 syndromic management is often the standard of care due to resource constraints. Syndromic management, a systematic, symptoms-based approach to diagnosing and treating suspected STIs,80 fails to capture asymptomatic cases, which represent the majority of curable STIs.81 This approach can lead to both the undertreatment of infected individuals and the overtreatment of persons with symptoms due to alternate conditions, the latter of which has implications for antimicrobial resistance.82

In high-income countries, where diagnostic and surveillance infrastructures are comparatively robust,83 refugees and asylum seekers also face barriers to accessing sexual health services. These include prohibitive costs, perceived discrimination, language and communication challenges with providers, limited awareness of services, poor health literacy and stigma.8486 Left untreated, curable STIs can result in significant morbidity, including pelvic inflammatory disease, infertility, perinatal complications and increased risk of HIV transmission.8789 Forcibly displaced persons may be at risk of these sequelae, though data are sparse.

In addition to gaps in the availability and scope of data, this study has several limitations. First, substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies in terms of population characteristics, sampling methods, diagnostic modalities, and study settings. While the meta-regression analysis accounted for 64% of the variability in syphilis prevalence, a considerable proportion of the heterogeneity remains unexplained. The relatively small number of studies that reported on C. trachomatis, N. gonorrhoeae, and especially T. vaginalis limited our ability to explore sources of heterogeneity for these infections. Second, the data span over four decades, making it challenging to distinguish true epidemiological changes from shifts in diagnostic technologies, STI treatment policies or surveillance systems. Older data may also not accurately reflect current STI risk patterns among forcibly displaced populations, given evolving migration pathways and geopolitical contexts.

Another limitation is the wide array of diagnostics used for STI ascertainment, which vary in sensitivity and specificity. While we stratified our analysis into broad testing diagnostic categories reflecting current versus lifetime infections, we could not adjust for potential biases associated with individual tests within each category. Also, for syphilis specifically, the absence of non-treponemal antibody titres in most studies may have led to misclassification of past adequately treated infections as probable current infections.90 The potential biases in syphilis diagnostic interpretation are summarised in online supplemental table 2 and discussed in detail in a prior publication.37

A strong small-study effect was observed and confirmed by the analysis that demonstrated the presence of publication bias, with studies testing at least 500 participants reporting a 49% lower syphilis prevalence. However, over half of the studies included in the analysis had large sample sizes (online supplemental table 8), enhancing the precision of the estimates. Finally, most studies relied on convenience sampling from health facility attendees or immigration reception sites, potentially under-representing individuals without healthcare access or undocumented populations. In some settings, the latter often constitute a large proportion of forcibly displaced populations. For example, in Lebanon, while 755 426 Syrian refugees are registered with the United Nations, government estimates suggest a total of approximately 1.5 million.91 92

This study has several strengths. It provides the first comprehensive assessment of curable STI epidemiology among forcibly displaced populations worldwide, addressing a major gap in the existing literature. By synthesising data from over 1.1 million test results, it generates robust pooled prevalence estimates, particularly for syphilis. The use of meta-regression analysis further strengthens the analysis by identifying key sources of heterogeneity and offering insights into regional, temporal and socioeconomic influences on STI prevalence in humanitarian settings. Despite limitations in data availability and scope, this study enhances epidemiological understanding of STIs in this population and establishes a foundation for future research and targeted public health interventions in displacement contexts.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the impact of forced displacement on STI epidemiology is complex, infection-specific and context-dependent, shaped by the interplay of factors related to both origin and host countries. Our findings indicate a substantial burden of syphilis, while also revealing critical gaps in the evidence base for C. trachomatis, N. gonorrhoeae and T. vaginalis infections. This imbalance in available data constrains a full understanding of STI epidemiology among displaced populations. It also reinforces the importance of investing in reliable point-of-care testing for these overlooked infections. Despite these limitations, the syphilis data yield critical insights. The association between higher syphilis prevalence and refugees hosted in low-income countries, as well as those originating from the WHO African region, highlights the intersection of structural vulnerabilities, healthcare access limitations and STI transmission dynamics in displacement settings. Although syphilis prevalence was found to be declining over time, the rate of decline at 6% per year is insufficient to achieve the targets of the WHO’s Global Health Sector Strategy on STIs.93

Addressing structural and sociocultural barriers to sexual healthcare (eg, costs, legal concerns and stigma) in displacement settings, as well as expanding STI screening through the integration of point-of-care testing into existing primary or antenatal care programmes, are essential for mitigating STI-related morbidity and mortality. Studies among representative populations—particularly in LMICs—are urgently needed to produce more robust STI estimates and identify subpopulations at greatest risk. This should include key populations, who face compounded vulnerabilities to poor sexual health in humanitarian settings yet remain largely absent from the available literature. Addressing these knowledge gaps will require international cooperation, country-level policy shifts toward inclusive healthcare, and targeted research investments that prioritise the sexual health of forcibly displaced individuals. Advancing these efforts is essential to promote health equity and safeguard the well-being of these growing mobile populations globally.
