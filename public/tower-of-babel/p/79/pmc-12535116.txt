Enhancing parental awareness on multiple chemical sensitivities: lessons from repeated cross-sectional questionnaire surveys on children
Authors: Akihiro Kawase, Masato Nagayoshi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultiple chemical sensitivity (MCS) is not yet familiar to the public. Patients suffer from a lack of understanding of their rare condition by those around them. This study aimed to (1) investigate the public awareness rate of MCS and (2) whether respondents who repeatedly responded to a survey about MCS have significantly increased their awareness of MCS compared to those who responded only once.MethodsWe distributed a self-reported questionnaire to parents of all elementary and junior high school students in Joets City, Niigata, in 2010 and 2017. The questionnaire was completed by 13,444 parents in 2010 and 7039 parents in 2017 (N = 20483). Using the same questionnaire multiple times in our surveys, we utilized the nature of a natural experiment, which is the presence of identical respondents in the dataset. We performed multivariate regression analyses.ResultsThe parental awareness of MCS was 64.2% on average from our surveys. Multivariate regression analyses revealed that the awareness of MCS was 6% points higher among parents who responded to the survey repeatedly than among parents who responded only once.ConclusionsRepeated conducting questionnaire surveys had a positive impact on MCS awareness of parents. Conducting surveys has the potential to work as a public awareness campaign to reduce the prevalence of MCS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24833-3.Keywords: Multiple chemical sensitivity, Awareness, Parents, Children, Japan

Background

Multiple Chemical Sensitivity (MCS) is a new syndrome that is still little known worldwide. Cullen [1] defined MCS as “an acquired disorder characterized by recurrent symptoms, referable to multiple organ systems, occurring in response to demonstrable exposure to many chemically unrelated compounds at doses far below those established in the general population to cause harmful effects.” MCS has been criticized because it suggests that chemical exposure is the actual cause has not been proven. Therefore, many other terms are also used, such as ‘environmental illness’ or the most recent term (recommended by WHO) is idiopathic environmental intolerance (IEI) [2]. However, some authors also concluded that IEI is not a separate entity but a variant of somatoform disorder [3]. Nowadays, the prevalence of MCS in the general population has been reported as 0.5–12.8% in medical diagnoses and 5.5–33% in self-reports in different countries [4]. In Japan, the number of people diagnosed by a physician is 1.02%, and the number of people suspected of having MCS is estimated to be 5.6–8.1% of the population [5, 6].

The prevalence of MCS in teenagers is much lower than in adults [7]. A previous study suggested that the prevalence of MCS was 4.5% in mothers and 0.25% in their three-year-old children, which indicated that MCS is an acquired condition [8]. In addition, a study suggested that exposure to chemicals during childhood was significantly associated with a possible increased risk of MCS [9]. Children depend on their parents to prevent and address health problems, and parental health literacy is crucial to children’s health outcomes. A cross-sectional study reported that high parental health literacy was associated with positive health behaviors in children [10].

Having MCS changes their lives drastically and limits social and occupational functioning [11]. The primary symptoms of MCS are such as headache, nausea, fatigue, and depression. Since these symptoms are non-specific and primarily subjective, patients feel isolated and alienated because their surroundings cannot understand them, and it creates a situation that can be called social exclusion [12]. According to qualitative studies that analyzed the contents from interviews or free-form text of a questionnaire of MCS patients, their primary challenge is the lack of understanding from other people [13, 14]. The only helpful treatments most highly rated by MCS patients were creating a chemical-free living space and avoiding chemicals [15].

In recent years in Japan, a primary source of indoor air pollution has shifted from sick building origin to fragranced consumer products, such as perfume, fabric softener, and personal care products [16]. Indirect or involuntary exposure to fragranced products in public, other than through intentional use by individuals, is an example of a negative externality. Steinemann [17] named this phenomenon “secondhand scents” in an analogy to secondhand smoke. Since elementary and junior high school students learn in the same classroom every day, i.e., in the same indoor air environment, the chemical use of surrounding children also affects the child’s chemical intolerance. If a child is sensitive to chemicals, preventing or addressing the problems would be difficult without understanding their parents and school.

In Joetsu City, Niigata Prefecture, in 2004, four children at the same elementary school became sick with the smell of paint and ink, developing urticaria and sometimes fainting, and were unable to attend school. As a result, they were diagnosed as having MCS. In response to these cases, Joetsu City conducted a questionnaire survey in July 2005 targeting approximately 12,000 children in all elementary schools in Joetsu City to (1) identify children suspected of having MCS and (2) raise awareness of MCS [18]. Then, Nagayoshi et al. conducted a second survey on approximately 17,000 students in July 2010 [19] and a third survey on approximately 11,000 students in July 2017 [20] in all elementary and junior high schools in Joetsu City. The results indicate that children with MCS-like symptoms were 9.5% in 2005, 12.4% in 2010, and 12.1% in 2017 [21]. Thus, while the first aim of the survey has been clarified in previous studies, the second one has yet to be clarified.

All three surveys in Joetsu City were conducted in July; the 2010 survey was conducted five years after the 2005 survey, and the 2017 survey was conducted seven years after the 2010 survey. Thus, fortunately, some grades were asked to respond to the survey only once, while others were asked to respond twice. In this study, we treated this as a natural experiment and have taken advantage of it in our analysis. The three surveys used almost the same questionnaire, asking about the child’s grade and symptoms and the parent’s awareness of MCS. In addition to the survey form, a request letter to parents regarding their cooperation was enclosed, which included a description of the current situation regarding MCS. How would we react if we were asked the same question repeatedly in every survey? Some may obtain information from the request letter, ask someone who knows more about it, search on books or Google, or even read academic journal articles.

To our knowledge, however, academic studies on the awareness rate of MCS in the general public have yet to be conducted. Therefore, this is the first study to investigate the awareness rate of MCS among the public. However, a study on experts’ familiarity with the term ‘MCS’ does exist. Gibson and Lindberg [22] conducted a survey on 90 physicians in the state of Virginia in the United States. The study noted that 48% of the respondents were “somewhat familiar,” and 8% were “very familiar” with MCS. Even physicians with expertise in the field are only slightly more than half aware of MCS.

This study aimed to (1) understand the rate of parental awareness of MCS and (2) investigate whether repeated surveys contributed to increased awareness of MCS among responding parents using questionnaire surveys conducted in 2010 and 2017 in all elementary and junior high schools in Joetsu City, where individual-level data were available for us. MCS is still a little-known syndrome, and the awareness of MCS by many people could improve the situation of patients and reduce the prevalence of MCS.

Methods

Data collection

The cross-sectional surveys were conducted in 2005, 2010, and 2017 in Joetsu City. Joetsu is a city located southwest of Niigata Prefecture and one of the municipalities of Japan. In 2017, the city had an estimated population of 194,000 people, and the total area was 974 km2. The city’s population density is about 199 persons per square kilometer. Joetsu City is the third largest population in Niigata Prefecture after Niigata City and Nagaoka City.

In the 2005 survey, questionnaires were distributed to 12,045 students in all 54 municipal elementary schools in July 2005, and responses were received from 10,348 students (response rate: 85.9%). In the 2010 survey, questionnaires were distributed to 16,700 students in all 76 municipal elementary and junior high schools in July 2010, and responses were received from 14,024 students (response rate: 84.0%). In the 2017 survey, questionnaires were distributed to 11,271 students in 62 of the 72 municipal elementary and junior high schools in July 2017, of which the consent of the school principals was obtained, for a total of 7224 responses (response rate: 64.1%). We only have access to individual-level data for the 2010 and 2017 surveys.

Research tools

The self-reported questionnaires were distributed through the school to parents, who were asked to respond to questions about their child’s grade, symptoms (14 items) observed by the parents, and parent’s awareness of MCS. The survey was conducted anonymously, as well as the name of schools, are not identifiable. In addition, due to the limited paper width of the questionnaires (one sheet printed on both sides of A4), items related to the sociodemographic characteristics of the students and respondents were not included. The questionnaires are provided in Supplementary Materials 1 and 2.

However, if a child has siblings close in age, the identical parent may have responded to more than one survey form. Unfortunately, the same respondents were not identifiable because the survey was administered to students and did not ask for any answers about the parents who responded.

Study design

All three waves of the survey were conducted in July, meaning the 2010 survey was conducted five years after the 2005 survey, and the 2017 survey was conducted seven years after the 2010 survey. Thus, the 2010 sixth- through ninth-graders were first- through fourth-graders as of 2005, making it probable that their parents were surveyed twice, once in 2005 and once in 2010. In addition, the 2017 eighth- to ninth-graders were first- to second-graders as of 2010, making it probable that their parents were surveyed twice, once in 2010 and once in 2017. Figure 1 shows the identical participants in the 2005 and 2010 surveys in blue and the 2010 and 2017 surveys in red. In this study, we utilized this feature as a natural experiment in the analysis.

Survey participants for each year

However, it is possible that some of the respondents moved into Joetsu City between the previous and relevant surveys. In this regard, the Niigata Prefecture Population Census shows that among the available age groups, those aged 0–14, closest to the elementary and junior high school students covered in the report, had an excess of out-migration during the relevant period [23]. In addition, it is considered that a small number, though not all, of students from national elementary schools go on to municipal junior high schools. Therefore, when considered in the aggregate, we could disregard the presence of transferees into Joetsu City.

Parental awareness of MCS

The question about the parents’ awareness of MCS asked, “Do you know the term ‘multiple chemical sensitivity’?” The respondents were asked to answer on a four-point scale of “know a lot (1),” “know (2),” “know a little (3),” and “don’t know (4)”. Since the interest of this study is whether respondents know about the MCS or not, we categorized this four-level scale into a binary dependent variable (“know” and “don’t know”) in the analysis.

Criteria for identifying MCS in the child

In this paper, we determined the presence or absence of MCS symptoms in the child based on the parent’s observations and responses to questions. Since these symptoms differ from those of MCS diagnosed by a physician, we refer to them as “MCS-like symptoms.” Japanese MCS diagnostic criteria were devised under the auspices of the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare in 1997. According to the diagnostic criteria for MCS in Japan, we used 14 items (five major items and nine minor items) to determine MCS-like symptoms. Table 1 shows these items. MCS-like symptoms were required when positive for at least two major items and four minor items or one major item and six minor items [24].

Discussion

This study utilized individual-level data from self-reported questionnaire surveys related to MCS to parents of all elementary and junior high school students in Joetsu City, Niigata, in 2010 and 2017. Due to the nature of the survey, our dataset contains a mix of parents who responded only once and those who responded twice. Taking advantage of the characteristics of the dataset, we examined whether parents who responded to a repeated cross-sectional survey twice had significantly higher awareness of MCS than parents who responded for the first time. Multivariate regression analysis after controlling for child’s MCS-like symptoms revealed that implementing repeated cross-sectional surveys increased respondents’ awareness of MCS by 6% points. The results suggest that repeated responses to a similar survey increased respondents’ awareness of MCS. While the Japanese government has been running information campaigns on MCS since 2021, repeatedly conducting questionnaire surveys could also work as a public awareness campaign to reduce the prevalence of MCS.

The awareness rates obtained from our survey were higher than those obtained from previous study [22]. Of course, even with the same “know” response, a simple comparison would not be possible, as there were likely differences between physicians who responded that they “know” the term MCS from a professional standpoint and the general public who responded that they “know” the term MCS. The results of this study, however, showed that the term MCS has become widespread among the general public in Japan. The challenge for the future is how to translate the penetration of the term into empathy and understanding for others, especially for MCS patients, and behaviors such as not using fragranced products.

This paper is the first study showing that the identical respondents have increased their awareness of the issue by repeatedly taking the survey. The decline in MCS awareness from 2010 to 2017 by over 10% points might be because MCS was first brought to the attention of elementary schools in Joetsu City in 2004 [18], and the public’s attention has declined since then. Despite these overall trends, it is worth noting that the MCS awareness rate among parents who have responded to previous surveys is higher than among parents responding for the first time.

Various studies have shown that repetition has a positive effect [27, 28]. However, repetition may also have adverse effects if it exceeds a certain threshold, resulting in an inverted U-shaped relationship [29, 30]. Repetition might enhance awareness, but too much exposure might diminish its effectiveness. In the case of this study, the surveys are spaced 5 and 7 years apart, respectively, and it is not yet known how often they should be conducted. The results of this study, however, indicate that it is essential to conduct periodic surveys to understand the actual situation and raise awareness of the issue.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, parents’ awareness of MCS is naturally influenced by their characteristics, such as age, gender, education, occupation, and income. Unfortunately, however, there was no question item asking about the sociodemographic characteristics of the respondents in our questionnaire, which we could not control for in this analysis.

Second, since the survey was administered per student, the identical parent might have responded more than once if there were siblings close in age. Furthermore, respondents might have moved into Joetsu City from other areas between surveys. These points should be kept in mind when interpreting the results of this study.

Third, the survey was conducted in Joetsu City, a limited area of Japan, so the results of this study cannot be immediately generalized. In the future, a nationwide survey should be conducted to verify its general validity.

Fourth, all three waves of surveys used the same diagnostic items to determine MCS-like symptoms. This is because we used the same items as in the 2005 survey to capture trends in the actual conditions of children. However, the use of the Quick Environmental Exposure and Sensitivity Inventory (QEESI) is now the gold standard for assessing chemical sensitivity [31], and the possibility of using its simplified version, the Brief Environmental Exposure and Sensitivity Inventory (BREESI), is being explored [32]. When conducting large-scale surveys in the future, using the QEESI or BREESI adapted to allow parents to respond would be desirable.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that implementing the survey contributes to understanding the actual situation and educating the respondents. Although MCS is still low awareness, its prevalence is likely to increase because of secondhand scents and other factors. Our results indicate that repeatedly conducting the survey is expected to educate the public and increase their awareness to save our lives.
