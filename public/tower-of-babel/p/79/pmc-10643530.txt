Development of thermoultrasound assisted blanching to improve enzyme inactivation efficiency, drying characteristics, energy consumption, and physiochemical properties of sweet potatoes
Authors: Huihuang Xu, Yaru Guan, Chun Shan, Wanru Xiao, Min Wu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Highlight•Thermoultrasound was more effective in inactivating enzymes than hot water blanching.•Drying time and energy consumption were significantly reduced by thermoultrasound.•Thermoultrasound at low temperature (65 °C) better preserved microstructure and color.•Thermoultrasound led to higher levels of phytochemicals and antioxidant capacity.Keywords: Ultrasound, Enzyme activity, Drying characteristics, Energy consumption, Physicochemical properties, Sweet potatoes

Introduction

Sweet potato, as the world’s sixth largest food crop, is favored by consumers due to its richness in nutrients, such as carbohydrates, amino acids, and phytochemicals [1]. According to FAO, the global production of sweet potatoes was 88.87 million tons in 2021, with a harvested area of 7.41 million hectares [2]. However, the annual loss of sweet potatoes during storage, transport, and processing is estimated to be 65 % due to their high moisture content (approximately 80 % (w.b.)) and perishability [3]. Therefore, the valorization of sweet potato products commonly requires drying to reduce moisture content and inhibit enzyme activity that results in the quality deterioration of products. However, endogenous enzymes (e.g., polyphenol oxidase and peroxidase) distributed in the tissues and organs of sweet potatoes are released during drying and catalyze various deleterious biochemical reactions, especially in aerobic environments where these reactions are more rapid [4]. Hot water blanching (HWB) is conventionally performed as an indispensable procedure before drying to inactivate the quality-related enzyme. Generally, peroxidase (POD) activity is used as an important indicator to determine the blanching condition because it has higher heat resistance to HWB pretreatment than polyphenol oxidase (PPO), pectin methyl esterase (PME), etc. [5]. Despite its proven effectiveness in deactivating peroxidase, HWB has low processing efficiency. More importantly, remarkable deterioration of food quality might occur during HWB, particularly with the development of off-flavor, color changes, and loss of soluble nutrients [6]. In addition, the large amounts of wastewater generated by HWB pose a considerable environmental challenge, as its direct disposal can lead to pollution. These drawbacks are incompatible with the principles of sustainable development, such as the production of healthier food, reduced energy consumption, and environmental protection [7]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore alternative processing technologies that can help to the achievement of sustainable development goals.

Thermoultrasound (USB), namely ultrasound combined with moderate heating (50–80 °C), as an emerging and promising technology is developed. This technique has been shown to be more effective in inhibiting the activity of endogenous enzymes in plant tissues than HWB, with lower temperatures and shorter blanching times [8]. Previous research has demonstrated the effectiveness of USB in reducing drying time and costs by enhancing moisture diffusion within food products [9]. This advantage is particularly attractive to the energy-intensive drying industry. In addition, the USB technique has shown considerable potential in the production and enhancement of dried products, such as improving nutritional profile and sensory attributes [10]. Given these advantages, the USB technique is more closely aligned with sustainability goals than the conventional HWB technique. To date, numerous scientific and industrial researchers have evaluated its effects as blanching pretreatment on the physicochemical properties of agri-food products. For example, Gamboa-Santos et al., [11] found that USB (20 kHz, 60 °C, 10 min) effectively inactivated the PME of carrots and better preserved their microstructure compared to HWB (60 °C, 40 min). Sulaiman et al., [12] studied the influence of different ultrasonic processes on PPO deactivation in fruits and concluded that USB (24 kHz, 460 W power, 32 °C) allowed the production of high-quality strawberries with higher retention of bioactive compounds at lower temperatures compared to thermal treatment alone (60 °C). Jiang et al., [13] applied USB in the drying of Agaricus bisporus and found that 40–45 % of the moisture was dehydrated from the samples after USB (25 kHz, 60–75 °C, 3–20 min) compared to the untreated ones, thus enhancing the subsequent drying process. The effect of USB on agri-food products in terms of enzyme inactivation, availability of bioactive compounds, drying behavior, and microstructure could be ascribed to the “additive effect” of heat and ultrasound-induced cavitation and mechanical forces [14]. In detail, the cavitation effect caused by ultrasonic waves is responsible for the formation of cavitation bubbles. Such microbubbles present in liquid media will undergo a series of kinetic processes of oscillation, expansion, contraction, and even collapse [15]. The instantaneous implosion of the microbubbles generates local high temperature (5000 K) and high pressure (1000 MPa) and triggers a strong shear force, which is a key mechanism leading to enzyme inactivation [16]. Meanwhile, water molecules are broken down by the cavitation effect to generate O2–, H+, and OH–, which will attack specific sites of enzymes or oxidize amino acid residues related to the catalytic activity and structural stability of enzymes, thus affecting the activity of specific enzymes [17]. Notably, to ensure the effectiveness of USB, the treatment parameters, such as temperature, time, and ultrasound power must be reasonably selected, especially the temperature used, which is crucial for the kinetics process of microbubble formation. The increase in temperature could reduce the energy threshold for cavitation bubbles generation, which facilitates the formation of cavitation bubbles [18]. The explosion of more cavitation bubbles can induce a more uniform and intense shear force, which will enhance the effect of USB. However, as the temperature is further increased, the vapor pressure inside the cavitation bubble will gradually increase, which acts as a buffer for the explosion of the microbubble and leads to a weakening of the ultrasonic cavitation intensity [19]. Considering these issues, there is a strong need for process optimization studies of this technology to fully develop its potential as a promising food processing technology.

Therefore, the objectives of this study were (1) to investigate the effect of USB under different conditions on POD and PPO activities of raw sweet potatoes compared to HWB alone; (2) to explore the mechanism of the influence of HWB and USB on drying behavior of blanched sweet potato by analyzing the evolution of its texture and microstructure; (3) to evaluate color, water holding capacity, total phenolic content, total carotenoid content, antioxidant capacity (ABTS and DPPH), amino acid composition, and non-enzymatic browning index of dried sweet potatoes treated with USB and HWB. These results are expected to provide data reference for the development and process optimization of ultrasound-assisted blanching technology for sweet potatoes, as well as guidance for energy conservation and emission reduction in the sweet potato processing industry.

Material and method

Materials

Fresh sweet potatoes (Dioscorea esculenta (Lour.) Burkill) were purchased from a supermarket in Beijing, China. Sweet potatoes of uniform size and maturity, free from mechanical damage and mold, were manually selected as experimental material. All sweet potatoes were stored in a refrigerator (4 °C) and used within 3 days to prevent frost damage. Before the experiments, sweet potatoes were cut into slices with a thickness of 2.40 ± 0.30 mm and a diameter of 48.00 ± 0.50 mm using a vegetable slicer. The materials used were prepared on the day of the experiment. The moisture content of fresh samples measured using the AOAC method (No. 984.25) was 80.83 ± 0.98 % (wet basis) [20].

Thermoultrasound blanching (USB)

The USB experiments were performed by mixing sweet potato slices (300 g) with distilled water (2400 mL) and sonicating them in an ultrasonic reactor (JP-040, Skymen Cleaning Equipment Shenzhen Co., ltd., Shenzhen, China, internal dimensions: 300 × 240 × 150 mm) with an ultrasonic power density of 60 W/L, a frequency of 40 kHz, and temperatures of 50, 65 and 80 °C for 15 and 30 min, respectively. These blanched samples were collectively referred to as USB50 oC-15/30, USB65 oC-15/30, and USB80 oC-15/30, respectively. After USB, they were taken out and the water was removed from their surfaces with absorbent paper.

To ensure the consistency of experimental conditions, hot water blanching (HWB) of sweet potatoes as control was performed in the ultrasonic cavity described above without ultrasound. The blanching temperature and time of HWB were the same as in the USB experiment, and the blanched samples were collectively referred to as HWB50 oC-15/30, HWB65 oC-15/30, and HWB80 oC-15/30, respectively.

POD and PPO activity

The enzyme extraction and the determination of enzyme activity in fresh and blanched sweet potatoes were conducted using the methodology of Zhang et al., [21]. 5 g of sample was placed in a pre-cooled mortar and then homogenized with 15 mL of sodium phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 6.0). Afterward, the homogenate was centrifuged (6000 rpm, 15 min, 4 °C) and then its supernatant was collected as an extract of the enzyme and stored in a refrigerator before use.

For PPO assay, 0.7 mL of supernatant, 2.6 mL of phosphate buffer, and 0.7 mL of catechol were mixed. Subsequently, the absorbance value was recorded per 30 s at 420 nm for 10 min using a UV spectrophotometer (TU-1810, Beijing Puekinje General Instrument Co., Ltd, Beijing, China). For POD assay, 0.02 mL of supernatant was blended with 3.2 mL of 25 mmol/L guaiacols, and then 0.2 mL of 0.5 mol/L H2O2 was added. Texture analysis

Texture properties of fresh and blanched sweet potatoes were assessed in terms of hardness using a texture analyzer (TA-XT plus, Stable Micro System, UK) with a P/36 probe in TPA mode with the following operating parameter settings: pre-test, test, and post-test speed of 1 mm/s, trigger force of 5 g and compression ratio of 45 %. The hardness of the sample was expressed as the maximum peak value at the first compression.

Microstructure observation

The microstructure observation of fresh and blanched sweet potato slices was performed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (SU3500, Hitachi Co., Tokyo, Japan). The samples were fixed on an SEM stub and subjected to gold spraying for 45 s. The micrographs of sample cross-sections were acquired at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV.

Drying characteristic

Drying of fresh and blanched sweet potato slices was carried out in a microwave-assisted hot-air dryer. Based on preliminary experiments, the optimized drying parameters were set as follows: hot air temperature of 60 °C, microwave power of 1 W/g, and hot-air speed of 0.5 m/s [22]. The mass loss and energy consumption of the sample during drying were recorded using an electronic balance and an electric meter, respectively.

Determination of bioactive compound and functionality

Total phenolic compound (TPC)

The extraction and determination of phenolic compounds in dried sweet potatoes were conducted based on the method introduced in our previous study [25]. The dried sample powder (0.1 g) was mixed with 10 mL of ethanol (70 %, v/v). The mixture was then extracted by ultrasonication at 70 °C for 30 min and centrifuged (12000 g, 4 °C, 15 min). The supernatant was collected and then its TPC was determined using a colorimetric method. Folin-Ciocalteu reagent (1 mL) and Na2CO3 (15 %, w/v, 1 mL) were added sequentially to the extract (1 mL). After incubation at 25 °C for 30 min protected from light, the absorbance of the reaction solution at 765 nm was determined. All results were expressed as gallic acid equivalents (mg GAE/100 g).

Total carotenoid content (TCC)

TCC of dried samples was examined from the method outlined by Tayyab Rashid et al., [26] with minor modifications. Sample powder (0.5 g) was mixed with a solution of ethanolic butylated hydroxyl toluene (1 %, w/v, 5 mL) and kept in an 85 °C water bath for 5 min. Potassium hydroxide (64 %, w/v, 1 mL) was then added to the heated mixture for saponification. A 5 s vortex was performed to thoroughly mix this mixture before returning it to the water bath (85 °C, 10 min). Afterward, the mixture was removed from an ice bath, and cold distilled water (3 mL) was added to it. 3 mL of hexane was added to the cooled mixture and centrifuged (4 °C, 8000 rpm, 5 min) to separate it into two layers. The yellow solution located in the upper layer was transferred to a new tube and collected. The above procedure was repeated until the upper layer was colorless. Antioxidant capacity

The scavenging capacity of 2, 2′-azinobis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) and 1,1-Diphenyl-2-picryl-hydrazyl (DPPH) radicals of the extract was determined to evaluate the antioxidant capacity of dried samples. For ABTS test, 50 μL of the extract was added to the 3.95 mL of ABTS+ solution (absorbance value of 0.70 ± 0.02) and reacted for 15 min at 25 °C in the darkness. Its absorbance at 734 nm was then determined. For DPPH test, the reaction solution was obtained by mixing the extract (0.5 mL) and 40 mg/L DPPH solution (3 mL). Subsequently, this solution was stored in the darkness (25 °C) for 30 min and then its absorbance at 517 nm was determined.

Amino acid profile analysis

The amino acid composition of dried sweet potatoes was measured by an amino acid analyzer (L-8900, Hitachi High Tech, Japan) following the method of Wang et al., [27]. 100 mg of the sample powder was blended with 10 mL of HCl (6 M) in a 20 mL ampoule. The ampoule was sealed and placed in an oven at 110 °C for 24 h of hydrolysis, then taken out and cooled to room temperature. Immediately afterward, the hydrolysate was transferred to a 50 mL volumetric flask and its volume was fixed on the calibration line with distilled water. The above solution (1 mL) was put in a concentrator and then vaporized to dryness at 60 °C. The resulting dry sample was solubilized in 1 mL of sodium citrate buffer (pH 2.2), shaken thoroughly, and filtered through a 0.22-µm microporous membrane to prepare the solution to be measured.

Non-enzymatic browning index

The non-enzymatic browning index (BI) is used as an indicator to quantify the level of Maillard reaction products in dried sweet potatoes. The BI of dried samples treated with different blanching conditions was determined following the method of Krishnan et al., [28] with slight modifications. 1 g of dried sample was dissolved in 15 mL of 80 % ethanol followed by extraction for 60 min at room temperature. The mixture was then centrifuged at 10000 rpm for 15 min (4 °C) to obtain the supernatant. The absorbance of the supernatant was then measured using a UV spectrophotometer set at 420 nm, a wavelength specifically indicative of the final product of the Maillard reaction [29].

Statistical analysis

The effect of blanching methods on the quality attributes of sweet potatoes was statistically analyzed by SPSS 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA) and Origin 2021 (Origin Lab, USA). All tests were performed in triplicates. The mean values of quality parameters under different blanching conditions were compared using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) by Duncan test at the 5 % significance level. All results were presented as the mean ± standard deviation of triplicate data.

Results and discussion

Effect of blanching on PPO and POD inactivation of fresh sweet potatoes

The relative enzyme activities of sweet potatoes treated with different blanching conditions were shown in Fig. 1 A&B. Data showed that PPO and POD activities of blanched samples (p < 0.05) were significantly lower than the control (fresh sample). Specifically, the relative PPO activity of blanched samples at a temperature of 50 °C and time of 15–30 min ranged from 22.19 to 23.07 % for HWB and from 15.54 to 19.37 % for USB (Fig. 1A). The increase in temperature from 50 to 80 °C led to a substantial reduction in PPO activity to reach values of 13.33–20.07 % for HWB and 3.36–12.81 % for USB. This result was supported by Nicolas et al., [30] who reported that PPO exhibited significant thermal inactivation when the temperature exceeded 40 °C. Zhu and Pan [31] also noted that most PPO activity could be inactivated in the temperatures range of 60 to 80 °C. There was indeed a significant negative correlation (p < 0.001, r = -0.90) between blanching temperature and PPO activity. In a similar manner, blanching pretreatment significantly decreased the POD activity, with a decrease of up to 72.96 % compared to fresh samples (Fig. 1B). As expected, blanching for 30 min at 80 °C resulted in the lowest activity values of PPO (3.36 % for USB) and POD (27.04 % for USB). Notably, POD still had about 30 % activity, although PPO activity was already below 10 % at this point. This result might be because the heat resistance of POD was much higher than that of PPO. Zymogram analysis showed that multiple POD isoenzymes were commonly present in plants with significant differences in their thermal sensitivity [32]. The study of Agüero et al., [33] also confirmed that heat-resistant POD isoenzyme required blanching temperatures above 70 °C to be inactivated.

The inactivation of PPO and POD might be associated with the overall conformation alteration of the enzyme structure. The intermolecular forces such as hydrogen and ionic bonds that maintained the active center and advanced structure of the enzyme could be broken by high temperatures generated during blanching, which altered the spatial structure of the enzymes and thus led to a loss of their functionality [34]. A comparison of the inactivation effect of blanching methods revealed that USB significantly (p < 0.05) increased the inactivation efficiency by 16.04–74.80 % for PPO and 7.36–30.41 % for POD under the same blanching conditions, compared to conventional HWB. This result could be attributed to the synergistic action of heat and ultrasound. During USB, the environment in which the enzyme was exposed was disturbed by the formation, evolution, and collision of cavitation bubbles (e.g., changes in temperature, pressure, and stress). Meanwhile, the intense shear generated by bubble implosion could break secondary bonds in the polypeptide chain, leading to alternations in the secondary and tertiary structural conformations of the enzyme. Moreover, an increase in temperature lowered the cavitation threshold, which further promoted the formation of cavitation bubbles and enhanced the synergistic effect of thermal and mechanical damage [35]. Similarly, Anaya-Esparza et al., [36] found that USB could reduce PPO activity to less than 10 % in a much shorter time compared to heat and ultrasonic treatment alone. These findings suggested that USB held promise for replacing the traditional HWB in terms of improving the efficiency of enzyme inactivation.

Effect of blanching on hardness of fresh sweet potatoes

The hardness of fresh and blanched sweet potatoes is shown in Fig. 2 and results indicated that temperature, time, and their interactions have a significant effect on hardness. Compared to fresh samples, blanched samples exhibited a significantly lower hardness of up to a 57.32 % decrease for HWB and a 59.38 % decrease for USB. Moreover, the hardness values presented a gradual decrease with increasing temperature and time, regardless of the blanching method used. The substantial hardness reduction especially found for higher temperatures could be related to several factors, including changes in cell turgor pressure, degree of starch gelatinization, and content of cell wall polysaccharides [37]. The disruption of cell membrane structure might alter membrane permeability, which would cause changes in cell turgor pressure and thus reduce hardness. The degree of starch gelatinization in sweet potatoes gradually increased as the temperature and time increased, which also promoted the softening of tissues. Moreover, the main components of the cell wall, such as pectin and hemicellulose, were degraded during blanching, which could cause a weakening of the mechanical strength of the cell wall and intercellular adhesion [38]. Notably, USB-treated samples presented lower hardness values than HWB-treated ones under the same blanching conditions, implying an intensifying effect of ultrasound on softening the texture of sweet potatoes. This phenomenon was because the cavitation, shock waves, and microstreaming of ultrasound could generate strong vortices, eddies, and other turbulent fields, allowing samples to absorb heat more uniformly and thus accelerate their softening process [39]. Similar results on the softening effect of blanching process on the texture of agri-foods have been reported for steam blanching of ginger [40] and thermoultrasound blanching of carrots [41].

Effect of blanching on microstructure of fresh sweet potatoes

The spatial structure and surface morphology of fresh and blanched sweet potatoes was observed by SEM. As shown in Fig. 3, a typical honeycomb-like structure was observed in the fresh sample tissues, i.e., tightly arranged cell tissues, regular shape, and clear contours. The blanching process has a significant influence on the microstructure of the sample, mainly reflected in the contraction and deformation of the cell structure. Moreover, the size of irregular pores gradually increased and the boundary between cells gradually disappeared with increasing blanching intensity. Particularly, micrographs of the samples blanched at 80 °C showed that some cells appeared to fracture and formed huge pores. Notably, white particles appeared in the blanched sample tissues, while they were not observed in the fresh samples. This phenomenon might be associated with the formation of lipid droplets [42]. A comparison of the impact of blanching methods on the microstructure showed that samples treated with USB below 65 °C still preserved the typical honeycomb-like structure relatively well. Moreover, they exhibited an increase in porosity and pore size and formation of microporous channels compared to the HWB-treated samples. Similar structures were also found in ultrasonically treated ginger by Ren et al., [43]. The change in microstructure might be related to the continuous contraction and expansion of the sample under the mechanical action of ultrasound, which expanded the intercellular space and thus promoted the formation of microporous channels and sponge-like structures [44]. A recent study reported by Shi et al., [45] confirmed that these changes contributed to enhanced heat and mass transfer, thus accelerating the drying process. In addition, the thermal and mechanical effects of USB also might induce the release of soluble substances from the cytoplasmic matrix by disrupting the cell structure [46].

Effect of blanching on drying characteristic and energy consumption

Drying time

The effect of HWB and USB on the drying characteristics of sweet potatoes are presented in Fig. 4A&B&C. The moisture content of all samples gradually decreased over drying time. For HWB, drying time data showed that for a blanching time of 15–30 min, increasing blanching temperature from 50 to 80 °C resulted in reductions in drying time by 5.26–15.79 %, compared to the control (unblanched samples). The significant (p < 0.05) reduction in drying time was probably because the cellular microstructure in the sample was disrupted during HWB, which promoted moisture diffusion during the subsequent drying. This coincided with the study of Oshima et al., [47], who reported a 45–373 min reduction in the required drying time of carrots after HWB. However, when blanching at a temperature of 80 °C for 30 min, a slight increase in drying time was noted (Fig. 4C). This phenomenon might be due to the collapse of the cell wall structure and the loss of tissue integrity caused by excessive blanching, which increased the resistance to moisture migration [48]. Similar to HWB, successive increases in the blanching temperature of USB also caused reductions in drying time. For a temperature range of 50–80 °C, the drying time values were in the range of 65–75 for 15-min blanching and 60–75 for 30-min blanching, respectively. In comparison to the control, USB significantly reduced the drying time required for samples, and the shortest drying time was obtained after 30 min of USB at 65 °C. This might be due to the increased permeability of the cell membrane induced by the synergistic effect of ultrasound and heat, which intensified the mass transfer during drying [49]. Moreover, the degradation of the polysaccharide network structure of the cell wall resulting from the mechanical and cavitation effects of ultrasound could promote the release of bound and immobilized water, which would increase the evaporation of water during drying. Sledz et al., [50] previously reported a 15 % reduction in drying time for parsley leaves after ultrasonic pretreatment. Notably, USB was more effective than HWB alone in reducing drying time. This might be attributed to the fact that the liquid jets caused by the implosion of cavitation bubbles destroyed the tissue and generated many micro channels, which made it easier to remove water during drying. This has been confirmed during the drying process of many agricultural products, such as mushrooms, brussels sprouts, and cauliflower [51].

Effective moisture diffusivity

The results of the calculated effective moisture diffusivity (Deff) for blanched sweet potatoes under different conditions are shown in Table 1, with Deff values ranging from 1.93 × 10-8 to 3.14 × 10-8 m2/s. These values are within the typical range reported for food products, ranging from 10-11 to 10-6 m2/s [52]. This study showed that both HWB and USB pretreatments resulted in increases in Deff values, up to 31.61 % and 62.69 %, respectively, compared to the unblanched samples. Similar results were reported in the blanching of green peas [53]. Blanching pretreatment has been shown to help remove air from the intercellular spaces and soften the texture, thus reducing the resistance to water migration and improving water diffusion [54]. Notably, a decrease in Deff values was observed with an increase in blanching temperature from 65 to 80 °C and an increase in blanching time from 15 to 30 min. The lowest Deff value was obtained for samples treated with HWB at 80 °C for 30 min (1.80 × 10-8 m2/s), which could be attributed to the increased resistance to water migration resulting from starch gelatinization [55]. Similarly, Sun et al., [56] reported a 22.32 % decrease in the Deff value of broccoli with increasing blanching time. When comparing the effects of the two blanching methods on Deff values, it was found that the USB-treated samples generally had higher Deff values than the HWB-treated samples under the same conditions. The highest Deff value was obtained for samples treated with USB at 65 °C for 30 min (3.14 × 10-8 m2/s). This result was in agreement with the findings of Shi et al., [45], who observed a 40.72 % increase in the Deff value of ultrasound-treated bananas compared to those not treated with ultrasound. They attributed this to the disruption of the diffusive boundary layer caused by the cavitation effect of ultrasound, facilitating water diffusion. Furthermore, Pearson’s correlation analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between Deff values and drying time (p < 0.001, r = -0.98), suggesting that increased moisture diffusion contributed to reduced drying times.

Specific energy consumption

Fig. 4D presents the SEC values of dried samples subjected to blanching with different conditions. The result showed that blanching process led to a significant reduction in energy consumption of 7.36–19.53 % for HWB and 19.79–28.54 % for USB, respectively, compared to the control (13.39 kW·h/kg). Correlation analysis showed a significant positive correlation (p < 0.001, r = -0.95) between SEC values and Deff values, which implied that an increase in effective moisture diffusivity could significantly reduce energy consumption. This was in agreement with Singh et al., [57] in their study on the effect of the blanching process on the energy consumption of mangoes. Regarding the effect of different blanching methods on SEC, the SEC of USB-treated samples (10.77–14.51 kW·h/kg) was reduced by 6.59–33.43 % compared to HWB-treated samples (9.57–10.74 kW·h/kg). This suggested that USB has an advantage over HWB in terms of energy saving.

Effect of blanching on physical properties of dried sweet potatoes

Color properties

Table 2 shows the color parameters of dried sweet potatoes with different blanching conditions. The L*, a*, and b* for the control (dried samples without blanching) were 78.09, 8.4, and 38.55, respectively, while after blanching, these values showed a significant (p < 0.05) increase. Specifically, the L*, a*, and b* values were increased to 78.79–82.81, 10.13–12.91, and 39.29–44.35 for HWB and 79.74–83.3, 10.88–13.8, and 45.06–46.96 for USB, respectively. The total color difference (ΔE) value was calculated to visually assess the color deviation of blanch-treated dried samples from the control, which ranged from 2.01 to 8.73 for HWB and 6.20 to 10.85 for USB, respectively.

The increase in L* was probably linked to the inactivation of polyphenol oxidase. Managa et al., [58] pointed out that during the drying process, polyphenol oxidase presented in fruits and vegetables catalyzed the oxidation of endogenous phenolics to quinones that subsequently underwent further oxidation and polymerization to form brown or black compounds. The blanching pretreatment could inhibit the formation of these compounds by inactivating polyphenol oxidase, thus improving the brightness of the dried sample. Notably, the L* values did not always maintain an increasing trend with increasing blanching temperature or time, especially in the case of HWB treated at 80 °C for 30 min, where the lowest L* value was obtained (77.36 ± 0.09). This phenomenon was in line with the observations made by Savas [59] during the drying process of sweet potatoes, who attributed it to the prolonged exposure of the samples to the drying environment, which exacerbated the thermal degradation of the pigments and the chemical reactions associated with the color change. Indeed, L* values were found to be negatively correlated with drying time (r = -0.72, p < 0.05). Generally, dried sweet potatoes with relatively high a* and b* values were more attractive to consumers. Compared to HWB-treated ones, dried samples treated with USB had higher a* and b* values. In particular, the highest values were obtained for samples blanched with USB at 65 °C for 30 min (13.8 ± 0.18 and 46.96 ± 1.43). Kutlu et al., [60] reported that ultrasound could induce the isomerization of carotenoids in fruits and vegetables, leading to the production of reddish-brown substances that increased the a* and b* of the samples. This result was confirmed by correlation analysis, which showed significant correlations between both a* (r = 0.85, p < 0.001) and b* (r = 0.60, p < 0.05) values and total carotenoid content. However, carotenoids were susceptible to degradation and loss under high-temperature conditions and aerobic environments. The a* and b* values showed a decrease when the temperature was increased from 65 to 80 °C and the time was prolonged to 30 min. Furthermore, the increase in L*, a*, and b* values jointly determined the ΔE values. Comparatively, USB-treated dried samples exhibited higher ΔE values, which indicated that their color deviated more from that of the unblanched dried samples. This result might be linked to the effect of USB on enzymatic reactions [61]. Compared to HWB, it was more effective in inactivating PPO and POD involved in color degradation, thus inhibiting color deterioration during the subsequent drying. These findings suggested that suitable USB conditions are more favorable for improving the color properties of dried samples compared to HWB.

Water holding capacity (WHC)

WHC is a measure of the ability of dried products to retain moisture during rehydration, which determines the sensory experience of the consumer as well as the economy and sustainability of the process [62]. The influence of blanching conditions on WHC of dried sweet potato is shown in Table 2 and data indicated that appropriate blanching conditions resulted in a significant increase in WHC of dried samples compared to the control. Specifically, at a blanching time of 15–30 min and temperature range of 50–80 °C, WHC values increased to 2.79–3.19 g/g for HWB and 2.76–4.67 g/g for USB, respectively. Comparatively, USB exhibited significantly higher WHC except at 80 °C. In particular, the highest WHC value (4.67 ± 0.05 g/g) was obtained for dried samples treated at 65 °C for 15 min. This might be ascribed to the cavitation effect of ultrasound. During cavitation, intense mechanical forces were generated due to the collapse of bubbles in the liquid, which disrupted the cell membrane, allowing for the formation of pores [63]. The presence of pores allowed more moisture to diffuse into the sample, thus resulting in an increase in WHC. However, WHC showed a significant decrease of up to 46.67 % when increasing the temperature from 65 to 80 °C, regardless of the blanching method used. This suggested excessive blanching has a detrimental effect on the water holding capacity of the dried sample. This reduction in WHC might be due to the irreversible collapse of the tissue structure caused by high temperature, which resulted in the loss of structural integrity and weakened the ability of the sample to retain water. These findings were consistent with a previous study that reported a decrease in WHC of pleurotus eryngii due to disruption of the supporting structures of the tissues as a result of excessive blanching [64].

Effect of blanching on phytochemicals and antioxidant capacity of dried sweet potatoes

Total phenolic content (TPC)

The influence of blanching process on the TPC of dried sweet potatoes is shown in Table 2. The TPC values of dried samples after blanching under different blanching conditions were in the range of 14.07–22.92 mg GAE/100 g for HWB and 15.54–24.24 mg GAE/100 g for USB. Compared to the control (24.89 ± 0.43 mg GAE/100 g), these values showed considerable reductions. The reduction in TPC might be due to the blanching-induced cell damage promoting the release of some soluble phenolics. However, the loss of these substances would occur in the presence of water during blanching. A similar phenomenon was observed by Li et al., [65], who attributed the decrease of TPC in dried kidney beans after ultrasound to the loss of their leachability. Moreover, the relatively high PPO activity was considered an important factor contributing to the reduction of TPC. Goodrich et al., [66] noted that PPO and phenolic compounds are located in the cytoplasm and vesicles of plant cells, respectively. The damage caused by heat treatment to plant tissues resulted in the rupture of vesicles, which was conducive to the reaction of PPO in contact with phenolic compounds. As mentioned above, a significant increase in TPC was indeed observed with decreasing PPO activity from 23.07 % to 3.36 %. This confirmed that blanching process could improve the retention level of phenolic compounds by inhibiting the enzymatic browning reaction.

Compared to HWB, suitable USB conditions significantly increased TPC of the dried samples. Specifically, the highest value of TPC (24.24 ± 0.16 mg GAE/100 g) was obtained with USB at 65 °C for 30 min. The mechanism of USB to preserve phenolic compounds was related to ultrasound action. Research has shown that exposure of plants to ultrasound could trigger endogenous signals, such as the secretion of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which acted as a signaling molecule causing enhanced activity of various enzymes involved in the synthesis of phenolics, thereby inducing the biosynthesis of phenolics [67]. In addition, the increase in TPC might be also associated with its enhanced extractability. During USB, plant cells were subjected to periodic compression and expansion forces, leading to the loosening of the cell wall pectin network and the breakage of covalent bonds. This facilitated the release of bound phenolics located in the cell wall matrix, thus causing an increase in TPC [68]. The positive impact of ultrasound treatment on the retention level of phenolics was also verified in the processing of peaches [69]. However, a significant decrease in TPC was observed at higher temperatures (80 °C). Particularly the lowest value (15.54 ± 0.05 mg GAE/100 g) of TPC was obtained under 30 min of USB. This phenomenon might be due to the degradation of heat-insensitive phenolics induced by high thermal stress. This was in agreement with the study of Nowacka et al., [70] on the blanching of cranberry fruits.

Total carotenoid content (TCC)

The influence of blanching process on the TCC of dried sweet potatoes is shown in Table 2. TCC values of dried samples blanched under different conditions exhibited a considerable increase, ranging from 1.81 to 2.88 μg/mL, compared to the control (1.77 ± 0.05 μg/mL). A similar trend was reported by Tang et al., [71]. The increased TCC of the dried samples may be because blanching accelerated the drying process and thus shortened the time the sample was exposed to thermal processing conditions. Previous studies have shown that carotenoids in plants primarily are present in their all-trans structure [72]. However, the breakdown of the conjugated double bonds during processing could lead to the isomerization of carotenoids to their cis isomers. Typically, the cis-isomer was thermodynamically unstable, and therefore prolonged exposure to light or heat could result in increased degradation [73]. Moreover, the increase in TCC was also associated with the inactivation of oxidase enzymes (e.g., lipoxygenase) related to carotenoid degradation caused by blanching [74]. However, as the blanching temperature was increased from 65 to 80 °C, a significant decrease in TCC was observed, especially in the case of blanching for 30 min. This implied that excessive blanching intensity was detrimental to the preservation of carotenoids. A similar phenomenon was observed by Marx [75] during the blanching of carrots. This phenomenon might be attributed to the disruption of the cell wall structure caused by excessive blanching, which facilitated the dissolution of carotenoid crystals by cell lipids, thus reducing the availability of carotenoids. Notably, USB resulted in a significant increase in TCC values of up to 23.76 % compared to HWB. Similar findings were reported by Wu et al., [76], who ascribed it to the enhanced release of carotenoids from the food matrix by mechanical disruption of the cell wall by ultrasound. Carotenoids were found in chromosomes rich in polar lipids. The mechanical and thermal effects of USB damaged the cell structure and promoted the diffusion of pectin, cellulose, and proteins, which could effectively protect carotenoids from dissolving in lipids.

Antioxidant capacity

As shown in Table 2, compared to the control, blanching process led to a reduction in the antioxidant capacity of dried sweet potatoes. Data showed that at blanching times of 15 and 30 min and temperature ranges of 50–80 °C, the ABTS values decreased by 32.29–51.07 % for HWB and 28.10–38.22 % for USB, while the DPPH values decreased by 1.49–13.81 % for HWB and 10.08–15.69 % for USB. This phenomenon might be related to the loss of substances with antioxidant capacity during blanching. The thermal and mechanical effects generated during blanching could disrupt cellular and subcellular compartments, which led to increased exposure of phenolic acids, flavonoid compounds, etc. to oxidative reactions [10]. A similar result was found by Li et al., [77] during the processing of Chinese yam. This was also confirmed by the Pearson correlation analysis, where a positive correlation was shown between TPC and ABTS (p < 0.05, r = 0.71) and DPPH (p < 0.05, r = 0.77) (Fig. 6). Notably, DPPH and ABTS values increased to a maximum value and then decreased with increasing blanching temperature. Specifically, the maximum values of both ABTS and DPPH were obtained at a blanching temperature of 65 °C, regardless of the blanching method. A similar trend was observed during the blanching of fruit peelings [78]. Furthermore, do Nascimento et al., [79] also demonstrated that the sonication of passion peel at low temperatures was more effective than at high temperatures in increasing the retention levels of antioxidant substances. The increase in the antioxidant capacity might be because blanching destroyed the cell microstructure and inactivated oxidative enzymes (PPO and POD), thus enhancing the availability of antioxidants. As for the weakening of antioxidant capacity caused by high temperatures (80 °C), it may be related to the thermal degradation of antioxidant substances [80]. Regarding the effect of blanching methods on antioxidant capacity, the dried samples treated with USB under suitable conditions (65 °C) exhibited higher antioxidant capacity compared to those treated with HWB, with the highest values of ABTS (10.86 ± 0.36 μmol Trolox/g) and DPPH (48.85 ± 0.83 μmol Trolox/100 g). This result well confirmed the superiority of USB in preserving antioxidants.

Effect of blanching on amino acid composition of dried sweet potatoes

The profile of amino acids (AA) in dried sweet potatoes blanched under different conditions was quantitatively analyzed and results were summarized in Table 3. Fifteen amino acids, including seven essential (EAA) and eight non-essential amino acids (NEAA), were detected in all samples, among which aspartic acid (75.1–164.7 mg/g), glutamic acid (42.57–65.88 mg/g), valine (21.00–45.00 mg/g), and leucine (22.25–42.55 mg/g) were considered to be the major constitutes of amino acids due to their relatively high levels. This result was in agreement with that reported previously by Mu et al., [81]. For the effect of the blanching process on AA, a significant decrease in AA was found after blanching, which implied that the AA profile of dried samples was affected by different blanching temperatures and times. Similarly, Trigo et al., [82] observed a general decrease in the content of all amino acids after blanching except glycine, histidine, proline, and arginine. Deng et al., [83] identified that the evolution of AA profile was the result of a combination of factors, including material properties, AA type, and processing technique. Surprisingly, dried samples showed higher AA values of up to 61.64 % when the blanching temperature of HWB was increased from 50 to 80 °C. This might be because the increase in temperature led to the enhanced breakage of the peptide bond in proteins, which promoted the hydrolysis of proteins and thus increased the content of free amino acids [84]. In contrast, increasing the blanching temperature of USB did not have a positive effect on the preservation of AA. In particular, AA exhibited a significant decrease when the temperature exceeded 65 °C or the blanching time was extended to 30 min, although USB significantly increased the AA content compared to HWB when the temperature was below 65 °C. A similar phenomenon was found in sausages pretreated with ultrasound that supported prolonged exposure of the samples to the ultrasound bath had no desirable effect on their amino acid levels [85]. In addition, the Millard reaction was also recognized as a critical cause for the reduction in AA. During USB, the high-intensity ultrasound produced strong cavitation, which led to the generation of high temperatures in the local area. Meanwhile, under the vibration of ultrasonic waves, the water molecules in the blanching medium underwent strong high-frequency oscillations and generated heat through mutual friction, which led to an increase in the overall temperature of the blanching medium [86]. All these factors mentioned above together facilitated the Millard reaction, thus reducing the AA content. Hu et al., [87] also stated that the reduction of amino acid content in dried mushrooms was mainly due to their loss in the Millard reaction.

To investigate the relationship between amino acid content and the Maillard reaction, the level of Maillard reaction products in dried sweet potatoes was assessed by measuring the absorbance of the melanin at 420 nm. As shown in Table 2, dried samples without blanching showed a BI value of 0.29 ± 0.02. The blanching pretreatment resulted in a significant (p < 0.05) increase in the BI value of the dried sample, with BI values ranging from 0.30 to 0.59 for HWB-treated dried samples and from 0.34 to 0.57 for USB-treated dried samples. This increase in the BI indicated that blanching pretreatment had an enhanced effect on the Maillard reaction. Wang et al., [88] reported similar results in their study on dried chili peppers, where BI values were higher in blanched samples than in non-blanched samples. Yu et al., [89] found that the high temperatures generated during thermal processing contributed to reducing the activation energy of the Maillard reaction, thereby accelerating the Maillard reaction. Furthermore, a correlation analysis between BI values and total amino acid content showed a significant negative correlation (p < 0.001, r = -0.92). This result was consistent with the findings of Tang et al., [90] in their study on the drying of Lycium barbarum L., who attributed higher levels of the Maillard reaction products in dried samples to a significant (p < 0.05) reduction in amino acid and reducing sugar content.

Chemometric analyses of amnio acid profile

To clearly distinguish different dried sweet potatoes produced different blanching conditions by AA composition, HCA and OPLS-DA were performed in this study. As shown in Fig. 5A, the HCA dendrogram was used to reveal the intrinsic relationships among dried samples blanched under different conditions according to the profile of 15 amino acids. Furthermore, a heat map was plotted to visualize the distribution of AA composition in dried samples blanched with different conditions. The dried samples were divided into four clusters at a Euclidean distance of 25. The control was clustered with HWB80 oC-15, USB50 oC-15, and USB50 oC-30 (Cluster I), which implied that the effect of these blanching conditions on the AA profile of dried samples were similar. HWB80 oC-30 was separately classified as Cluster II due to its relatively high AA level. Correspondingly, HWB50 oC-15 with relatively low AA content was separately classified as Cluster IV. The remaining dried samples were all classified as Cluster III. Notably, the color blocks of USB-treated dried samples in Cluster III were darker than those of the HWB-treated dried samples, indicating that USB possessed an advantage over HWB in preserving AA. Overall, with the help of HCA, we could gain more insight into the differences among dried samples blanched under different conditions based on the distribution and dynamics of AA composition, which contributed to assessing the AA profile of dried samples more comprehensively.

As presented in Fig. 5B, the OPLS-DA score plot showed that the first, second, and third components accounted for 87.6 %, 4.66 %, and 5.54 % (R2X (cum) = 97.8 %), respectively. Furthermore, the interpretation (R2Y (cum)) and prediction rate (Q2 (cum)) of the OPLS-DA model were 1 and 0.813, implying a strong fit and good predictability of OPLS-DA model. A 200-response ranking test was carried out to check for the accuracy and overfitting of OPLS-DA model. As shown in Fig. 5C, the original value (R2 and Q2) on the right side was always higher than its corresponding value after the left side replacement, which proved the validity of OPLS-DA model. The intersection of the Q2 regression line with the vertical axis was less than 0, suggesting that the OPLS-DA model was not overfitted. To assess the overall contribution of each variable to this model, variable importance in the projection (VIP) values were calculated and the result indicated that the VIP of 2 amino acid components greater than 1, that was aspartic acid and lysine (Fig. 5D). This indicated that these two components contribute significantly to the discrimination of dried samples.

Conclusion

In this study, we compared the effect of thermoultrasound (USB) and conventional hot water (HWB) blanching on enzyme inactivation efficiency, drying characteristics, energy consumption, color, phytochemicals, and structural properties. Results showed that USB significantly enhanced the inactivation efficiency of POD and PPO compared to HWB, especially the USB at 80 °C for 30 min increased the inactivation efficiency of PPO by 74.8 % and that of POD by 30.41 %, respectively. The blanching process resulted in softening of texture, enhancement of moisture diffusion, disruption of cell structure, and formation of microporous channels, and consequently affected the drying process and phytochemical content. These microstructural alterations promoted moisture diffusion to reduce drying time and energy consumption, particularly for USB. Compared to HWB, USB was more favorable to improve the physical properties and levels of bioactive composition, except for WHC and ABTS, which were better preserved by USB at 65 °C for 15 min, while USB at 65 °C for 30 min showed better performance in improving color, TPC, TCC, and DPPH. Furthermore, USB under appropriate conditions presented effective improvements in amino acid composition, despite the detrimental effects of blanching process on them. In conclusion, these findings provided important information to gain insight into the effects of USB on the inactivation of enzymes and quality attributes of sweet potatoes and demonstrated USB as a promising blanching technology for sweet potatoes. In the future study, we will adopt an optimized USB process to investigate in depth its mechanisms affecting the physicochemical properties of sweet potatoes to further promote the application of USB technology for the industrial production of dried sweet potatoes with high-added value.
