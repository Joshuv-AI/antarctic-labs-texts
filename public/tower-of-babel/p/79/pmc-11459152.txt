Automated determination of transport and depositional environments in sand and sandstones
Authors: Michael Hasson, M. Colin Marvin, Mathieu G. A. Lapôtre
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe surfaces of quartz sand grains—among the most ubiquitous materials on Earth—hold information about how sand was transported from one place to another. Accurately reading these textures provides vital details about geology, archaeology, and forensics when few other clues are present. However, no objective and quantitative method to do so exists. Here, a convolutional neural network is developed that uses electron microscope images of sand grains to determine the environment through which they were transported. The model provides unique insight into the history of both modern and lithified sediments, the oldest of which predate fossils of complex multicellular life. This workflow quantitatively transforms the study of quartz microtextures, enabling new research frontiers across geological and other historical sciences.

As sand moves across Earth’s landscapes, the shapes of individual grains evolve and accumulate microscopic textures (“microtextures”) on their surfaces (Fig. 1). Because different transport processes form different microtextures, shape and microtexture analyses can be used to infer the transport histories of modern quartz sand and ancient, quartz sand-bearing deposits (1–12).

Microtextural analysis is particularly useful in instances where geologic features and context alone are insufficient to determine transport history. Recent applications of this technique to sediment cores, which often lack geologic context, include understanding past glacial dynamics (e.g., refs. 13–15) and geoarchaeological insights into the environments that paleolithic people once occupied (16). Similar techniques have been used to trace sand supply chains in construction (17) and as a forensic tool (18), indicating the wide applicability of microtextural studies.

The power of this technique was recognized shortly after the commercial availability of scanning electron microscopes (SEM) in the early 1960s (1–3), which allowed users to observe and document previously invisible micron-scale features. However, two main drawbacks continue to hinder broad adoption:

(1)Microtextural description is based on subjective analysis such that even individuals describing similar grains from similar environments often fail to agree on the presence of a predetermined list of descriptors (5, 19). This variability alone may not preclude valid and statistically robust conclusions when aggregating large data volumes using robust statistical techniques (e.g., refs. 12, and 19). However, when combined with small sample sizes (often, only 20 to 50 grains are used to determine paleotransport environment; e.g., refs. 8, 20, and 21, and nonstandard lists of microtextures, quantitative statistical analyses may not be broadly reproducible between authors. Other efforts to quantify microtextural analyses using atomic force microscopy (22) have not been broadly adopted, likely due to the technique’s time- and resource-intensive nature.(2)The terminology and number of features used to characterize the surface textures of different grains are not standardized, precluding robust, quantitative comparison between studies: (6) document 23; (8) 34; (21) 32; (23) 30; (10) 17; and (12) document 20.

We address the aforementioned limitations by developing a deep neural network (DNN) model, SandAI, that automatically classifies SEM individual images of sand grains by transport environment. SandAI is hosted online and freely available for third-party use with detailed instructions and no coding required (24). Williams et al. (25) demonstrated the promise of early neural networks to achieve this task, and modern iterations have been demonstrated to perform it well in site-specific soil classification contexts (26). Other image processing techniques that do not rely on neural networks have yielded promising results for forensic (27) and provenance studies (11), but none have solved the problem of classifying sand grains with world and geologic time-wide generalizability. Modern convolutional neural networks (and specifically ResNets like ResNet-50, which forms the backbone of the model used herein) provide a state-of-the-art tool for identifying images based on shapes and textures (28). They are thus ideal for more generalized analyses of transport environments from SEM images of sand grains than have been performed to date.

The model was trained on quartz grains sampled from a variety of the most common modern transport environments (eolian, fluvial, glacial, and beach swash zone; Fig. 2) and imaged using a variety of SEM instruments to ensure broad utility (Materials and Methods). Our model enables users to quickly and automatically decipher the transport and depositional histories recorded in individual grains of quartz sand. In this workflow, the classes of individual SEM images are predicted by the DNN. Users can then use those results to classify the bulk sample (by taking the mode of the predicted classes) as well as explore the in-sample variability.

This technique transforms microtextural analysis by 1) accounting for the full grain morphology (shape and texture), 2) removing subjective human description from quantitative analysis, and 3) enabling users to predict the transport environments of individual grains (rather than the overall transport history of a bulk sample), which provides insight into the variance within bulk samples. Automation also lets users quickly obtain results without tedious, time-consuming visual identification of surface textures.

We demonstrate the broad and reliable utility of our model to aid interpretation of modern sediments and ancient rocks through three examples. First, we show that the model achieves 88.43% overall accuracy on unseen individual sand grains from a test dataset of 270 images from 23 modern sampling locations, and 100% accuracy on bulk samples of over nine grains. We also demonstrate that, based on the environment (class) with the worst test performance, only five grains are necessary for the modal prediction to be correct with 95% confidence.

Second, we apply the model to images of sand grains taken from modern environments and ancient sandstones that were not included in any stage of model development (“external validation”; e.g., refs. 29, and 30) to evaluate the model’s utility when examining unknown modern and lithified grains. External validation demonstrates that model predictions on modern samples not used in model development match the samples’ true transport environments. Similarly, model predictions for ancient sediments agree with previous paleoenvironmental interpretations of well-studied eolian, fluvial, and beach-deposited Early Jurassic (ca. 200 Ma) to Pliocene (ca. 5 Ma) rocks from the United States. Third, we illustrate the power of the model to help understand the ambiguous Cryogenian (ca. 665 Ma) Bråvika Member of Svalbard and its unique insights relative to existing approaches and interpretations.

Finally, we explore the limits of the DNN by using it to predict the classes of sand grains from environments not included in the model training. The platform used to host the model for third-party use guides the end user through a set of checks to avoid applying the model to samples that are unlikely to produce valid results. However, testing on samples that are unsuitable for model input reveals that, if a user were still to use such samples, low retention (compared to suitable samples) and predictions across all classes are signs that the sample was inappropriate for model input.

Altogether, our model predicts the transport environment of modern grains with high accuracy, correctly classifies samples of quartz grains from ancient siliciclastic rocks, and can be used to gain insight into the depositional histories of rocks of contested origin. Furthermore, these insights can be gained with far smaller sample sizes than have been necessary to date. The model is published and hosted online such that users can preprocess and make predictions using the model without coding or downloading any software ((24); Data, Materials, and Software Availability).

Results and Discussion

Model Validation and Testing.

Using standard machine learning validation practices (Materials and Methods), our model achieves an accuracy of 89.77% and 88.43% on validation and test datasets of 215 and 270 images respectively (Table 1). Images were classified using the highest probability with no threshold to understand the model’s base performance without removing low-confidence predictions. In addition to accuracy, we measured class-wise accuracy and macro averaged precision, recall, and F1 score across classes (Table 1).

Whereas individual images are classified based on transport environment (using features determined by the DNN during training; Explaining Model Classification), bulk samples (i.e., dominant depositional environments) are classified based on the most common class (mode) of the predicted population. By assessing the minimum number of grains required to identify the correct mode with 95% confidence (Materials and Methods), we found that just five grains were necessary.

The overall model test accuracy of 88.43% is similar to the outcomes of similar image classification problems using the ResNet model architecture [e.g., refs. 26, 31, and 32]. The accuracy of our model is somewhat lower than that of (26) who achieved ~98% accuracy on a similar—but less complex—sand classification task, in which the goal was to separate images based on sample location. The goal of the present study was to develop a tool that encompasses the diversity of samples in each environment, across the globe, to be as broadly useful as possible. Broad classes were chosen because more specific environments (e.g., alluvial fan vs. fluvial) are unlikely to have different microtextural signatures given the similarity in transport processes. The dataset used herein thus includes a multitude of sites from each environment, which expands the diversity of each class and drastically increases the difficulty of fitting a well-performing model. Achieving 88.43% accuracy on the test dataset of individual sand grains (and 100% on bulk samples) is evaluated to be a strong result given the complexity of the task.

The class-wise test accuracy suggests that some classes of grains are more differentiable than others (Table 1). For instance, eolian grains were correctly predicted with 95.4% accuracy in the test dataset whereas the fluvial class had a lower test accuracy of 82.53%. This is likely due to differences and similarities between transport processes that form microtextures and shape grains in different environments: eolian saltation yields grains with distinctive round, bulbous edges, whereas the similarity in subaqueous transport processes (and resulting textures) between fluvial and coastal grains may make them less distinct.

Importantly, the training dataset was assembled to represent the fullest possible global sample diversity of each transport environment. Not only do samples come from all continents, but they represent a range of transport distances within their respective environments. For example, the model was trained on eolian grains from the relatively small, wet coastal dune fields of Jericoacoara, Brazil, and the extensive and dry Rub’al Khali, Saudi Arabia; similarly, the fluvial class included the mountain-front Estero Rapilermo, Chile, and the distal Po River, Italy. Attaining 88.43% accuracy on such a diverse set of examples suggests that the model is capable of discriminating between transport environments despite significant variation in transport distance and regional setting.

Explaining Model Classification.

DNN models are often treated as “black boxes” in which the reasoning behind image classification is unknown. However, understanding the features that these models use to make decisions is critical for making them trustworthy and ensuring that they are making unbiased decisions based on appropriate factors. Creating the methods to robustly explain DNNs has been the subject of significant recent research (33).

To determine which features are viewed as diagnostic of transport environment by the model, we used a selection of common class activation maps (CAMs; 34), which highlight the regions of an image that the DNN is most reliant on for classification. We quantified the quality of the CAMs using the Remove and Debias (ROAD) method (35, 36) implemented as the ROADcombined metric (35; see Materials and Methods for details). When the CAM is working well (i.e., displaying the relevant region of an image for model decision-making), removal of irrelevant pixels elicits no or small positive confidence changes as noise is removed. In contrast, removal of relevant pixels leads to a significant confidence decrease. Therefore, the greater the ROADcombined score, the higher the quality of the CAM heatmap output.

We visualized the CAMs for each of the main convolution blocks in the ResNet-50 architecture (28) (referenced as layers 1 to 4 here, with layer 4 being the last convolution block) for one example grain from each class. Finally, we qualitatively compared CAM identifications with manually outlined microtextures that were previously shown, through statistical analyses, to be most diagnostic of transport environment (12).

CAM heatmaps demonstrate that different model layers identify different regions of the images. Both edges and surface textures appear to be important (Fig. 3). Pixels that make up image backgrounds are seldom activated, and never in the final layer (layer 4). Initial layers show activation of pixels that generally highlight edges (Fig. 3, row 2) whereas subsequent layers highlight broader regions of importance (Fig. 3, rows 4 to 5), as is expected from deeply layered DNN architectures (e.g., ref. 37).

Before analyzing heatmaps, it is essential to understand their quality. The mean ROADcombined metric score is 0.208 ± 0.0979 (SD). The ROADcombined metric has a theoretical minimum of −1 that could be achieved if confidence significantly decreased when irrelevant pixels were removed and remained constant when the most relevant pixels were removed. The theoretical maximum is 0.5, which would indicate that there was no confidence change when the least relevant pixels were removed, and a major decrease when the most relevant pixels were removed. These bounds contextualize the mean ROADcombined metric of 0.208 ± 0.0979 and indicate that the CAMs encompass the vast majority of the features that the model relies on to classify images, and do not (erroneously) highlight regions of little relevance, since that would lead to negative ROADcombined scores.

It is critical to emphasize that the DNN was not trained to identify features that a human would identify, and quantitative comparisons between CAMs and human annotations (e.g., an intersection-over-union metric) are therefore inappropriate. Rather, the DNN has learned which features differentiate classes during the training process (e.g., ref. 37). As such, comparison with manual annotations is purely qualitative and done to assess possible similarities between the regions that the DNN and a human would use to categorize grains. To make such comparisons, four common textures that drive much of the class separation in principal component space were manually labeled on the example grains (Fig. 3, row 6; 12).

Doing so indicates that, to a first order, regions that drive decision-making for the model are similar to those that drive separation in manual, principal component analysis (PCA)-based microtexture study techniques. For example, based on PCA loadings (i.e., which variables explain most of the separation in principal component space), (12) demonstrate that eolian grains are best distinguished by edge rounding and precipitation features. The CAM for the eolian grain shows activation of the rounded grain boundaries in layer 1 (Fig. 3E) and to a lesser extent layer 2 (Fig. 3I), which directly corresponds with manually labeled edge rounding. Whereas other classes show some edge outlining in early layers, the eolian class displays the greatest activation. Furthermore, comparison of the internal regions highlighted by the CAM in layer 3 (Fig. 3M) with manually documented precipitation features also shows considerable overlap. The same pattern follows for grains of the other classes, in which small surface textures are identified in layers 1 and 2 (Fig. 3, rows 2 to 3).

In addition to microtextures, grain relief drives much of the class separation in principal component space (12). This variable is unlikely to be associated with specific regions of a grain because it constitutes a summary assessment of the roughness of the surface, consistent with the identification of larger regions of surface textures in layers 3 and 4 (Fig. 3, rows 4 and 5). Importantly, the final layer (Fig. 3, row, 5) is always centered on the interior of the grain, indicating that the background (and by extension, sample preparation technique) is not an important factor in decision-making. Rough, angular patches of the glacial grain (Fig. 3, column 2) appear to be emphasized by the model in layers 2 and 3 and likely indicate that the DNN is, to some degree, reliant on the overall roughness of the surface of the grain. CAMs of the beach grain (Fig. 3, column 3), show that rounded edges are highlighted in layer 3, which correspond with areas marked for edge rounding in manual annotation. Finally, the fluvial grain appears to highlight smooth areas associated with conchoidal fracturing, which also drives much of the separation of fluvial grains when analyzed with a PCA (12).

In summary, CAM heatmaps demonstrate that different model layers identify different regions of the images, and that both grain edges and surface textures are important features used by the model to classify grains by transport environment.

External validation demonstrates the model’s utility when applied to both modern and ancient samples. The model correctly classifies modern grains not included in the training process, indicating that it has generalized well and yields trustworthy results. A confidence threshold of 75% was selected because of the four-class classification scheme. If the model predicts a 75% chance that the image belongs to a given class, and equal probability is assumed, the probability of the image being classified as any other class would be less than 10%, which is deemed to be low enough for most tasks. However, this parameter is tunable depending on context: for instance, if the model is being applied with minimal expert oversight and large sample sizes, it would likely be advantageous to set a very high confidence threshold. In such a case, the loss of some predictions (because they did not meet the threshold) would likely be a reasonable tradeoff for fewer, high-confidence predictions. Alternatively, in a case with few available grains, significant expert oversight, or in which the model predictions are being treated solely as accessory data, a low (or no) confidence threshold may be appropriate. We have configured the inference software such that 75% is the default threshold, but it can easily be modified by the user.

As in the test dataset, eolian grains appear to be easily recognized. In contrast, despite all samples from the beach class yielding the correct mode, there is greater intrasample variability. Samples selected for the training dataset were clear transport environment end-members: i.e., beach samples used for training were chosen because they lacked nearby fluvial input. This is not the case for, e.g., Cannon Beach, OR (predicted as dominantly beach and secondary fluvial), where many streams deposit sediment onto the beach. Variability likely represents a true mixed transport signal in quartz grains.

The two samples that are incorrectly predicted, from the distal Mississippi River and Aletsch Glacier, illustrate important model limitations. The fluvial sample from the distal Mississippi—predicted as dominantly eolian with secondary fluvial influence—demonstrates the impact of overprinting. The Mississippi River catchment hosts extensive eolian sand and loess deposits, which contribute to the river’s sediment load along most of its length (38), likely explaining the dominant eolian signal. After some transport distance, grains may begin to be overprinted with fluvial textures, or the eolian signal may be diluted by tributary input. Whereas model predictions do not correspond with the actual sample location (a point bar), this sample exemplifies the need to understand inherited sand characteristics—a noted and long-discussed limitation of surface texture studies (7, 8, 23, 39–41).

In turn, one Aletsch Glacier sample (sample A2) demonstrates the need to prepare samples in such a way that surface textures remain visible in SEM images. Many images in this sample appear to have smoothed surfaces due to thick coatings, rendering them unsuitable for classification using our DNN (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). In such a case, microtextural information is lost and predictions will lose fidelity.

For rocks composed of nonrecrystallized grains, the mode of the predicted classes was correct in all cases (Fig. 4 and Table 2). Furthermore, intrasample variability provides insight into secondary or nearby transport processes. For example, the sample from the wet eolian Ojo Caliente Formation is interpreted to be composed of 82.6% eolian grains and 17.3% fluvial grains (Table 2), consistent with stratigraphic observations of dune and fluvial interdune strata in the sampled section (42–46).

External validation also emphasizes the importance of manually determining whether samples are recrystallized. Older rocks are more likely to have experienced higher pressure and temperature burial conditions that may lead to recrystallization and loss of primary surface textures (e.g., ref. 47). Images of these grains are not suitable for model input and will not yield accurate results. Grains from younger rocks are therefore more likely to be suitable. Despite this, our model uses both shape and texture to identify transport environments such that grains with slight overgrowths (e.g., those from the Navajo Sandstone; 48) can still be accurately predicted if the original grain shape is preserved.

Application: Cryogenian Bråvika Member, Svalbard.

The Bråvika Member (unassigned to any formation) of Svalbard is a Cryogenian unit that has been interpreted as 1) a glacial outwash plain (49), 2) a glacio-eolian environment (50), 3) a tropical eolian environment (51), and 4) a tropical fluvial system (52). A thorough description and full review of previous analyses is provided by ref. 12, who imaged the grains discussed herein. The samples were collected from an interval with 1 to 3 m thick cross-strata, adhesion ripples, and pinstripe lamination, which (12) interpret as strong indicators of an eolian origin. The Bråvika Member is transitionally overlain by diamictite of the Wilsonbreen Formation (associated with the Cryogenian Marinoan panglaciation, or “Snowball Earth”; 50, 51, 53).

Of 86 grains, 83.7% were predicted to be eolian, and 16.3% were predicted to be glacial (Fig. 4 and Table 4). For comparison, model predictions for 132 modern periglacial eolian sand grains from Antarctica are presented for comparison (also imaged by ref. 12): 70.5% were predicted to be eolian and 29.5% were predicted to be glacial (Fig. 4 and Table 4).

Despite their ~645 Ma age, grains that compose the Bråvika Member are relatively unaltered and retain original surface textures. The predicted microtextural signature is dominantly eolian with accessory glacial influence (Fig. 4 and Table 4). This interpretation is consistent with that of (12) who analyzed manually documented microtextures using a principal component analysis (PCA). Our results thus align with physical stratigraphy and manual microtexture analysis, indicating that the distribution of environments predicted by our model yields similar, if not deeper, insight than is achieved when solely considering stratigraphic context. Furthermore, Bråvika Member grains demonstrate that the model can accurately predict the classes of even Precambrian sand grains provided that they retain their original morphology.

Based on model predictions, eolian sand collected from lakes in the McMurdo Dry Valleys of Antarctica may provide a suitable modern analog to the Bråvika Member grains. That Antarctic eolian sand shows a primary eolian signal in a continent so dominated by glaciers indicates that grains are rapidly reshaped by eolian transport. Eolian textures are known to develop rapidly due to the high energy of collisions (e.g., ref. 7), and the similarity between Antarctic and Bråvika Member results supports the notion that the predicted class distribution represents a true periglacial eolian signal. The Bråvika Member is also predicted to have a greater ratio of eolian to glacial grains, which likely reflects more significant eolian activity (recorded by dune cross-stratification in stratigraphy) than is present in Antarctica (where eolian grains were collected from lake cores; 12).

Altogether, externally validating the model on modern and ancient quartz grains demonstrates three critical points:

(1)Sample classification using our model corresponds very closely with (a) the variability expressed in physical stratigraphy and (b) classification using manual identification of microtextures and PCA ordinations.(2)Our model, though trained on modern grains, is equally capable of classifying unaltered rock grains of at least Late Precambrian age.(3)Classification of individual grains permits analysis of the range of transport environments reflected in a given sample rather than simply the most dominant environment. Whereas our model allows a user to take the mode to understand the dominant transport environment of a sample, its variance can also be used to understand whether the sample was influenced by other accessory transport processes.

Model Limitations: Samples Not Included in Training Dataset.

The repository in which the model and inference software is hosted provides detailed instructions for how users can achieve reliable results when predicting the classes of their samples. However, to explore the boundaries of the model (and to simulate the results of a user inputting an inappropriate sample to the model) we tested the tool on samples from three categories: 1) transport processes that characterize environments that the model was not trained to identify (debris flows, pyroclastic flows, and turbidity currents); 2) grains overgrown by silica cement; and 3) grains with coatings (related to preparation for SEM imaging) that obscure the textures present on grains (Table 5).

Of 18 samples that met the criteria for model input, the mean fraction of the sample that met the confidence threshold of 75% was 71.24 ± 16.9% (SD). In comparison, of 11 samples that did not meet the criteria (for any of the three reasons listed above), the mean fraction that met the confidence threshold was 55.43 ± 14.68% (SD). A two-sample homoscedastic (assuming equal variance due to similarity in SD) t test results in a P-value of 0.016. The fraction of a sample that meets the confidence threshold is thus significantly lower in samples that do not meet the criteria for model input.

Furthermore, bulk samples characterized by debris flows and pyroclastic flows are predicted to contain grains from all environments included in the training dataset. No samples that meet the criteria for model input show this signature. In contrast, the turbidity current sample is dominantly fluvial (77.8%) and secondarily beach (22.2%).

The DNN developed for this study was designed to be used within specific boundaries, namely: images of individual quartz sand grains from the four terrestrial environments in the training dataset that have not been obscured by silica overgrowth or sample preparation. Because of these limitations, we emphasize that samples must be manually examined before the model is used or results may not be reliable. A visual checklist is thus included in the software that third-party users will use to make predictions. We emphasize that this tool is in no way meant to take the place of geologists. Rather, it can be used to provide additional information that can complement human analyses rather than replace them.

Despite this, the results of model prediction on grains that do not meet those criteria provide information that can be used to indicate that a sample is unsuitable. Whereas we do not define a cutoff fraction of the sample that should meet the confidence threshold, unsuitable samples have significantly fewer grains that meet the threshold. Furthermore, samples from environments that were not included in the training dataset were the only samples to have grains predicted in every category. While possible, an environment that truly contains a signal of eolian, glacial, beach, and fluvial transport is unlikely and, without external, supporting evidence, should be taken as a signal that the model predictions are unreliable.

The model was not trained to identify grains transported in turbidity currents, and as a result, a relatively small fraction of the grains (~35%) from the Butano Formation turbidite sample met the confidence threshold; those that did were classified as either fluvial or beach. However, the Butano Formation is known to have been deposited proximal to the continent (54–58). Given possible nearby fluvial sources, proximity to the shore zone, and similarities between subaerial and submarine channelized flows (i.e., transport in suspended load), it is plausible that the model detected true fluvial and beach signatures on the grains that met the confidence threshold.

Microtextural Overprinting.

As sand grains move through multiple environments, they may develop multiple generations of microtextures. Generally, glaciers and rivers erode mountains, and rivers flush the produced sediment toward a depocenter. Depending on climate and local geography, wind can transport the grains away from rivers, or rivers may route sediment to an ocean. Once sediment has reached a shoreline, it can be retained along coasts (as beach sediment) or transported by submarine processes. As such, some degree of microtextural overprinting can be expected, which may complicate recognition of the dominant or most recent transport environment (7, 23, 39–41).

Overprinting complicates the field of microtexture studies as a whole, and assessment of overprinting rates informs the degree to which overprinting may be problematic. Most studies have focused on downstream trends in microtexture development in periglacial fluvial systems (23, 40, 41). Although these studies focused on the presence or absence of classes of microtextures (rather than suites of microtextures as in, e.g., principal component analyses), they demonstrated downstream increases in the ratio of fluvial to glacial microtextures. After 15 to 20 km of fluvial transport, the ratio was approximately 1:1, and after 25 km (Salmon River) to ~200 km (Chitina River) the ratio was approximately 10:1 (40, 41). In wind tunnel experiments, eolian microtextures were apparent after just 6 m of transport (7).

The rate of overprinting varies with the energy associated with grain-surface modification (e.g., grain-to-grain collisions during eolian saltation or sustained grinding under a glacier) and is thus not identical across environments. However, these studies demonstrate that overprinting of a bulk sample is not a binary phenomenon (i.e., bulk samples will rarely be completely overprinted or not-overprinted); rather, samples will progressively contain a greater proportion of grains that reflect the most recent or proximal transport environment. The classification of individual grains, as opposed to bulk samples, offers unique insights into how many have been overprinted. Furthermore, mixed transport is reflected in higher prediction uncertainty and greater in-sample variance. However, because the DNN classifies images based on the dominant texture (which may not be the most recent), the model is unable to distinguish which class has overprinted another. This is a limitation of the model when compared to an expert human examiner, who may be able to distinguish the most recent class based on the superposition of patterns. Despite this limitation, the results of our model’s individual-grain classification scheme provide more detailed information than any other classification technique currently established and the DNN will thus be a valuable tool for further quantifying overprinting rates.

Conclusions

The use of state-of-the-art deep learning techniques to classify quartz sand grains based on surface textures produced by sediment transport provides a standardized, automatic technique for identifying the transport and depositional history of quartz sand and quartz-bearing sandstones from individual sand grains. Microtextural analyses have long been known to provide important evidence of ancient depositional environments but have relied on time-consuming manual measurements of a nonstandard set of features, which has hindered wide adoption of such techniques.

We demonstrate that our model is capable of reliably identifying the transport environments associated with both modern and lithified individual sand grains, and that we can do so with smaller sample sizes than other techniques require. This approach provides a pathway for systematic analyses of overprinting rates in different environments that are necessary to provide quantitative constraints on transport distances from mixed paleotransport environment signals. Altogether, our model enables quantitative investigations of transport environments and sediment provenance in the geo-, archeological, and forensic sciences.

Materials and Methods

Data Curation.

Data for the training, validation, and test datasets consist of images compiled from literature sources (12) and images collected by the authors or colleagues. All samples originate from 23 globally distributed sites where one transport process unequivocally dominates (SI Appendix, Table S3). A total of 1,438 images were assembled (Fig. 2). Images not captured by the present authors were manually checked to ensure that they contained only one grain per image and did not contain any distortion (e.g., from electrical charging during the SEM imaging process), text, or scale bars.

Model Training and Validation.

The compiled dataset was divided into training, validation, and test sets using a 64/16/20% split, respectively. The model was trained using transfer learning of a pretrained ResNet-50 model (28) using Pytorch (59). The ResNet-50 model was pretrained on the ImageNet-1 k dataset (using the V2 weights, which are the most current as of the time of writing), which contains 1,281,167 images belonging to 1,000 classes. Therefore, we simplified the original model architecture to predict four image classes (eolian, fluvial, glacial, and beach). These classes were selected because more specific environments (e.g., fluvial vs. alluvial fan) are unlikely to be reliably distinguishable given the similarity of transport processes. During training, grains from three fluvial sites and one glacial site were oversampled by duplicating the images to improve model performance (due to their relatively smaller sample sizes) (SI Appendix, Table S2). Only the training dataset was manipulated in this way.

To meet the necessary image configurations for ResNet architecture image input, all single-band (grayscale) images were converted to three-band RGB images. Images were preprocessed by resizing images to 224 × 224 pixels (the standard ResNet input image size) and equalizing the image histograms using the native Pytorch equalization transform. Images were augmented using random rotations and sharpness adjustments.

The pretrained ResNet model was trained for 100 epochs using an early stopping procedure (to prevent overfitting) such that only the model iteration with the lowest validation loss was saved. The model reached its lowest validation loss on epoch 54. We used a batch size of 64, an Adam optimizer, a categorical cross-entropy loss function, and a learning rate reducer to decrease the learning rate by a factor of 10 if the validation loss plateaued for 10 epochs. The initial learning rate was set to 0.0001. The model was then used to predict the classes of images in the test dataset. The hyperparameter sweep involved testing batch sizes from 32 to 128 and initial learning rates from 0.01 to 0.00001, but the final hyperparameters listed above yielded the lowest final validation loss. In order to account for the role of randomization when splitting the dataset into training/test/validation sets, we repeated this process five times. The resulting model with the lowest validation loss was ultimately the one used for the study.

We assessed the minimum number of grains (N) required for the correct mode to be predicted with 95% confidence using the results of the least accurate class in the test dataset (fluvial; 82.53%). The least accurate class was selected because any other class would require fewer samples to obtain the correct mode. No confidence threshold was used to remain conservative. We did so by resampling with replacement (“bootstrapping”) 100,000 subsamples for subsamples (with N of 2 to 10) from the list of predicted classes. We then analyzed the percentage of the 100,000 subsamples that correctly corresponded with the true class (fluvial). Pooling the predicted class of every fluvial sand grain in our dataset (regardless of whether it was correct) allowed us to establish the minimum number of grains required to correctly infer the class of the bulk sample over 95% of the 100,000 trials. Doing so revealed that just five grains are necessary to produce the correct mode 95% of the time. The minimum number of grains would be fewer for any other, more accurate class.

To assess uncertainty associated with the DNN being used to classify grains that do not meet the requirements for prediction, we tested the DNN on grains that were a) sampled from deposits characterized by processes not included in the training dataset (debris flows, pyroclastic flows, and turbidity currents); b) contaminated by silica precipitation on the grain surface, c) prepared in such a way that surface textures were obscured. We analyzed the most common classes to be predicted and the fraction of the bulk sample that met the confidence threshold of 75%.

Explaining Model Classification.

To explain what the DNN uses to classify images, we employed several common CAM methods: GradCAM and GradCAM++ (which assess the importance of feature maps from each convolutional layer using gradients in class scores associated with different feature maps; (60, 61), and AblationCAM (which does it by removing features; 62) (Fig. 3). We evaluated the quality of the CAMs using the ROAD method (35, 36) implemented as the ROADcombined metric:[1]ROADcombined=(LeRF-MoRF)/2,

using the “PyTorch library for CAM methods” (35). This metric quantifies the quality of the CAM using two implementations of the Area Over the Perturbation Curve metric: least relevant first, or LeRF, and most relevant first, or MoRF (63). Both are calculated by taking the average confidence change associated with repredicting after replacing set percentiles (termed perturbations; here, the 20th, 40th, 60th, and 80th percentiles were used) of the least (LeRF) or most (MoRF) relevant pixels with noisy linear imputations (35, 36).

Sample Collection and Preparation.

We sampled 14 units of known depositional environment to evaluate the utility of our model on unseen lithified grains (SI Appendix, Table S1). Two quartz sand-rich samples of approximately ~0.5 kg were collected from each unit. Rock samples were disaggregated and cleaned of iron oxide and carbonate cements by soaking in 1 M HCl for 10 min at 100 °C. Grains were then cleaned of fine particles/organics by soaking in 30% H2O2 for 10 min at ambient temperature. They were then sieved to retain grains between 65 and 2,000 μm, the approximate established size range of grains that retain microtextures (2, 8, 20). The majority of grains fall in the 65 to 500 µm range. Disaggregated, formerly lithified grains and grains collected from modern environments were prepared using the same process. Samples were then separated into low and high-density mineral components using heavy liquid separation techniques (lithium metatungstate) and magnetic separation.

sLow-density grains (dominantly quartz and feldspar) were mounted on aluminum stubs using double-sided carbon tape and coated with 20 nm of carbon using a Leica EM ACE600 carbon sputter coater. To ensure that only quartz grains were included in the analysis, mineralogy was assessed using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy. Secondary electron images were then captured using a JEOL JSM-IT500h environmental SEM at 15 kV and exported as 980 × 1,260 pixel.png files.

Critically, previously published SEM photographs were acquired using different SEM instruments (JEOL JSM 5200 LV and JEOL JSM-5910 LV, (11) Zeiss FESEM Supra55VP, (12) and mounting techniques (e.g., sputter coating with 5 nm of platinum/palladium alloy; 12) such that the model is not restricted to the exact conditions or preparation techniques used for our measurements. However, whatever preparation technique is used must not coat samples too thickly or microtexture information will be lost (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).
