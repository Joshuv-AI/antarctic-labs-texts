The Molecular Neurobiology of NG2-Expressing Glial Cells in Cerebral Small Vessel Disease Pathogenesis
Authors: Zaw Myo Hein, Arwa Ossama Zakareia Sayed Ahmed Abdelrazek, Sheikh Bilal Ahmed Shahbaz, Che Mohd Nasril Che Mohd Nassir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) is a major contributor to stroke and vascular cognitive impairment, yet effective disease-modifying treatments remain lacking. Emerging evidence suggests that neuro-glial–vascular interactions play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of CSVD. Among glial populations, neuron-glia antigen 2 (NG2)-expressing glial cells, also known as polydendrocytes or oligodendrocyte precursor cells, are increasingly recognized as dynamic regulators of neurovascular integrity, immune signaling, and white matter repair. Traditionally viewed as progenitors for myelinating oligodendrocytes, NG2 glia also form synaptic contacts with neurons, associate with blood vessels, and engage in bidirectional crosstalk with astrocytes, microglia, endothelial cells, and pericytes. These properties enable them to influence blood–brain barrier (BBB) stability, angiogenesis, inflammatory polarization, and remyelination, processes directly implicated in the development and progression of CSVD. This narrative review synthesizes current knowledge on NG2 glial cells neurobiology with a focus on molecular mechanisms relevant to CSVD, including hypoxia-driven signaling, extracellular matrix remodeling, and context-dependent differentiation. This review also highlights translational opportunities, such as targeting inhibitory pathways, modulating trophic interactions with microglia, and leveraging NG2-mediated vascular support to promote repair. By reframing CSVD through the lens of NG2 glial function, this review provides a mechanistic framework that positions NG2 glia as one of the central orchestrators of neurovascular health and identifies them as promising targets for therapeutic intervention in a disease of high societal and clinical burden.Keywords: NG2 glial cells, Cerebral small vessel disease, Neurovascular, Neuroinflammation, Therapy

Introduction

Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) comprises a spectrum of pathological changes affecting the brain’s microvasculature, including small penetrating arteries, arterioles, capillaries, and venules [1]. Often clinically silent, CSVD is detected incidentally through neuroimaging; a recent cross-sectional study found that nearly 30% of asymptomatic adults aged 25–60 already exhibit radiological signs of CSVD [2–4]. When symptomatic, CSVD manifests as ischemic stroke, cognitive decline, or vascular dementia, accounting for roughly one-quarter of ischemic strokes [5] and up to 45% of dementia cases worldwide [6]. Radiological markers such as white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) and enlarged perivascular spaces (ePVS) serve as their hallmarks [7]. While traditionally viewed as an age-related disorder, recent data reveal that CSVD-related lesions also accelerate ischemic events in younger adults, suggesting a bidirectional interplay between vascular injury and neurodegeneration [7, 8]. Overall, by age 60, as many as 60% of individuals harbor some degree of CSVD-associated brain changes [1], underscoring the urgent need for mechanistically informed therapies.

The pathogenesis of CSVD involves multiple tightly interconnected processes, including neuroinflammation, endothelial dysfunction, blood–brain barrier (BBB) breakdown, and impaired interstitial waste clearance [9–11]. Moreover, glymphatic dysfunction, a cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)-driven clearance pathway dependent on aquaporin-4 (AQP4), has recently been implicated in CSVD-related white matter injury, linking sleep-dependent metabolic clearance failure to neurotoxic protein accumulation, chronic inflammation, and cognitive decline [12–15]. These vascular and immune perturbations occur within a highly regulated neurogliovascular network, in which glial cells actively shape vascular tone, barrier integrity, and inflammatory cascades.

Among glial populations, astrocytes and microglia are the best characterized. Astrocytes maintain BBB integrity, regulate cerebral blood flow (cBF), recycle neurotransmitters, and modulate extracellular ion homeostasis through endfoot interactions with cerebral vasculature [16]. Microglia, the central nervous system (CNS) resident macrophages, continuously monitor the parenchyma, responding rapidly to injury by proliferating, migrating, and releasing inflammatory mediators [17, 18]. Together, astrocytes and microglia coordinate neuroinflammatory responses by transitioning across a spectrum of context-dependent activation states shaped by vascular injury, metabolic stress, and chronic inflammation, with distinct molecular programs that can differentially influence neuronal survival, white matter integrity, and tissue repair [19]. Dysregulation of this crosstalk contributes to the chronic low-grade inflammation characteristic of CSVD.

By contrast, neuron-glia antigen 2 (NG2)-expressing glial cells (also known as polydendrocytes or oligodendrocyte precursor cells, OPCs) remain comparatively underexplored despite representing 5–8% of all CNS glia [20]. Historically classified primarily as progenitors of myelinating oligodendrocytes, NG2 cells exhibit far broader functional versatility [21]. They express the NG2 chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan and platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha (PDGFRα) [22], and, in the adult mammalian CNS, predominantly differentiate into oligodendrocytes. Reports of astrocytic or neuronal differentiation have been described in specific developmental or experimental contexts, but remain limited and not yet systematically validated across species or disease models [19, 23]. NG2 glia also form bona fide synaptic-like contacts with excitatory and inhibitory neurons, reflecting a specialized mode of neuron–glial communication rather than evidence of broad multipotent neuronal lineage potential [24, 25]. Beyond their developmental roles, NG2 cells participate in CNS repair, vascular interactions, and immune modulation, suggesting they may occupy a critical position at the intersection of vascular injury, neuroinflammation, and white matter repair.

Recently, the contribution of glial cells to CSVD pathogenesis has gained momentum, yet NG2 cells remain a missing piece in this puzzle. While neuron–NG2 synaptic signaling is an important aspect of activity-dependent myelination, in the context of CSVD, the vascular and perivascular functions of NG2 glia are likely to be more pathophysiologically relevant. Understanding how these dynamic progenitors influence BBB stability, angiogenesis, inflammatory polarization, and remyelination may unlock novel therapeutic avenues. Therefore, this narrative review synthesizes emerging evidence on NG2 glia, highlights their underappreciated roles in CSVD, and explores how targeting NG2-mediated cellular crosstalk may offer new strategies for preserving neurovascular health and promoting repair in small vessel disease.

NG2 Cells in the Adult Brain: Biology and Functional Versatility

Developmental Origins and Fate Plasticity

During embryogenesis, NG2 cells arise in several waves from discrete germinal zones, including the ventral subventricular zone of the forebrain and hindbrain [23]. These progenitors migrate extensively to populate both gray and white matter, establishing a ubiquitous reservoir of oligodendrocyte precursors (Fig. 1). The timing and region of origin confer subtle heterogeneity in proliferation rates, lineage potential, and responsiveness to environmental cues. In adulthood, the astrocytic stem cell population within the subventricular zone continues to generate NG2-positive progenitors [24]. These cells are mobilized in response to demyelinating injury, where they differentiate into myelinating oligodendrocytes to restore conduction velocity and network synchrony. This capacity for lifelong oligodendrogenesis underscores their critical role in maintaining white matter integrity across the lifespan.

Beyond their canonical oligodendrocyte lineage, NG2 glia exhibit fate plasticity, whereby under specific conditions, including hypoxia, inflammatory cytokine exposure, or growth factor modulation, they can differentiate into astrocytes [19] (Fig. 1). Although evidence for neuronal differentiation remains controversial, reports of neuron-like phenotypes in select spinal and cerebellar regions suggest that lineage flexibility may be region-specific and context-dependent [25]. This adaptive potential becomes particularly relevant in disorders like CSVD, where combined vascular and inflammatory insults demand coordinated repair responses. Moreover, NG2 glia, interchangeably referred to as OPCs or polydendrocytes, are distributed throughout the CNS in both gray and white matter [20]. They are identified by the coexpression of neuron–glial antigen 2 (NG2; chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan 4, CSPG4) and platelet-derived growth factor receptor-α (PDGFRα) [21]. This dual marker profile is widely used in both experimental and human neuropathology to distinguish them from astrocytes, mature oligodendrocytes, and microglia. In terms of abundance, NG2 glia represent approximately 5–8% of total glial cells in the adult CNS, a population size comparable to that of microglia [20]. Their relative uniformity across brain regions suggests a homeostatic role beyond transient developmental functions.

Importantly, NG2 glia remain mitotically active throughout adulthood, a property uncommon among differentiated CNS cell types, implying an enduring capacity to respond to physiological demands and pathological insults [22]. Besides, the spatial distribution of NG2 cells is nonrandom; they preferentially occupy perivascular niches in close apposition to capillaries, arterioles, and venules, as well as neuronal soma and axons [26]. While this strategic positioning is unlikely to directly initiate cerebrovascular pathology, it enables NG2 cells to act as context-sensitive responders and modulators of neurovascular dysfunction. Their proximity to both vascular and neuronal compartments positions them to integrate neurovascular signals and influence lesion progression and repair dynamics in conditions such as CSVD, in which neurovascular coupling and white matter integrity are chronically compromised.

Molecular Architecture and Functional Interfaces with Neural and Vascular Networks

NG2 is a type I transmembrane proteoglycan with a large extracellular domain that binds growth factors, integrins, and extracellular matrix components [22]. These interactions regulate OPC adhesion, migration, and proliferation by transmitting mechanical and chemical cues from the microenvironment. The intracellular cytoplasmic tail associates with scaffolding proteins that link external stimuli to intracellular Ca2+ dynamics and cytoskeletal remodeling [23]. Moreover, PDGFRα functions as a receptor tyrosine kinase, whereby upon binding PDGFR, it activates canonical pathways, including phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt) and mitogen-activated protein kinase/extracellular signal-regulated kinases (MAPK/ERK) cascades that promote cell cycle progression and survival [22] (Fig. 2). Beyond its canonical mitogenic role, PDGFRα signaling in NG2 glia is increasingly recognized as a context-sensitive survival and repair pathway within the neurovascular unit. In the healthy brain, pericyte-derived PDGF-B provides a key trophic signal to PDGFRα-expressing NG2 cells, sustaining PI3K/Akt- and MAPK/ERK-dependent survival, proliferation, and lineage progression toward myelinating oligodendrocytes [26]. In CSVD, however, chronic microvascular degeneration, pericyte dropout, and capillary rarefaction progressively reduce local PDGF-B bioavailability [27]. This vascular attrition functionally silences PDGFRα-dependent survival cascades in perivascular NG2 cells, rendering them vulnerable to hypoxia, inflammatory cytokines, and metabolic stress.

Importantly, this hemodynamic–biochemical coupling provides a mechanistic link between small vessel pathology and remyelination failure in white matter lesions. Reduced PDGF-B/PDGFRα signaling not only limits OPC survival and expansion but also disrupts activity-dependent myelin repair, contributing to the chronic persistence of WMHs [28]. Thus, impairment of the PDGF-B–PDGFRα axis represents a convergence point at which vascular injury is transduced into glial dysfunction, positioning this pathway as a potential therapeutic target to restore oligodendroglial resilience and white matter integrity in CSVD. Furthermore, coexpression of NG2 and PDGFRα not only serves as a reliable histological signature but also reflects a functional coupling of extracellular sensing and mitogenic signaling. Importantly, the NG2 proteoglycan can be cleaved by metalloproteinases, releasing a soluble ectodomain that can modulate the surrounding microenvironment [29]. This “shedding” phenomenon is implicated in modifying axonal growth, immune cell trafficking, and extracellular matrix composition during injury (Fig. 2). Such molecular versatility extends the influence of NG2 cells beyond their immediate membrane boundaries, hinting at paracrine roles in tissue repair and immune regulation. Moreover, NG2 glia form synaptic-like contacts with both excitatory and inhibitory neurons, enabling activity-dependent regulation of oligodendrocyte lineage dynamics. However, unlike astrocytes, which broadly ensheath virtually all synapses, the neurovascular relevance of NG2 glia in CSVD is more likely mediated through their perivascular positioning and signaling interactions with endothelial cells and pericytes, rather than through direct synaptic modulation [24]. While astrocytes broadly envelop and modulate synaptic transmission, NG2 glia engage in direct neuron–glial synaptic signaling, suggesting distinct but complementary modes of neuronal communication. These synapses enable NG2 cells to detect action potential-dependent neurotransmitter release, thereby linking neuronal circuit activity to the proliferation and differentiation of OPCs (Fig. 2). Such activity-dependent regulation of myelination has been proposed as a mechanism for fine-tuning conduction velocity and optimizing network plasticity [25]. Moreover, the vascular interface of NG2 glia is equally significant. Positioned adjacent to endothelial cells and pericytes, NG2 cells influence angiogenesis, vessel stabilization, and BBB maintenance [30].

Experimental studies have shown that depletion of NG2 cells or disruption of their signaling pathways impairs vessel maturation, compromises barrier function, and alters perfusion dynamics [31, 32]. These effects mirror pathological features observed in CSVD. Furthermore, NG2 glia engage in reciprocal signaling with astrocytes and microglia, whereby they can secrete growth factors and cytokines that modulate astrocytic reactivity and microglial polarization, thereby integrating immune and vascular responses [30]. In the context of small vessel pathology, these interactions could determine whether the local environment favors repair or degeneration. Hence, understanding these multimodal interfaces is central to reframing CSVD not merely as a vascular disorder but as a neurogliovascular syndrome.

Immune Competence and Inflammatory Plasticity of NG2 Glia

Cytokine Responsiveness

NG2 glia are not passive bystanders in the immune landscape of the CNS. They express a broad repertoire of receptors for cytokines and chemokines, enabling them to sense changes in their microenvironment and adapt their proliferative and differentiative programs accordingly [33, 34]. This responsiveness allows NG2 cells to participate in both injury-associated inflammatory responses and baseline homeostatic regulation. Unlike mature astrocytes, which primarily shape the immune milieu through reactive gliosis, NG2 cells actively integrate cytokine signaling into lineage decisions. Moreover, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) have been shown to inhibit oligodendrogenesis, diverting NG2 progenitors toward astrocytic fates or inducing growth arrest [35] (Fig. 3). This shift has important consequences in diseases like CSVD, where impaired remyelination may compound white matter damage. Additionally, experimental models demonstrate that even transient exposure to pro-inflammatory cytokines can create long-lasting deficits in myelin repair capacity, suggesting that NG2 fate decisions are sensitive to inflammatory “memory” encoded within the local environment [35].

NG2 glia as active modulators of CNS immune responses. NG2 glia actively participate in CNS immunity by integrating various signals from their microenvironment. Their cytokine responsiveness allows them to shift between injury-exacerbating and repair-promoting states, with pro-inflammatory cytokines inhibiting oligodendrogenesis and anti-inflammatory cytokines supporting it. TGF-β signaling from NG2 glia to microglia maintains microglial quiescence, while bidirectional crosstalk influences NG2 proliferation and fate. NG2 glia also exhibit endocytic and phagocytic potential, contributing to debris clearance and influencing lineage decisions. Furthermore, they demonstrate inflammatory polarization, adopting pro-inflammatory or reparative phenotypes based on environmental cues. This integrated role highlights NG2 glia as crucial players in chronic low-grade inflammation conditions like CSVD, offering potential therapeutic targets

Conversely, anti-inflammatory cytokines and trophic factors, including IL-10, insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), can support NG2 proliferation and differentiation into oligodendrocytes [36]. These cues stabilize BBB function, promote extracellular matrix remodeling conducive to axonal regrowth, and attenuate oxidative stress (Fig. 3). In this way, the cytokine milieu acts as a molecular rheostat, shifting NG2 glia between injury-exacerbating and repair-promoting states, a balance that may be particularly crucial in CSVD, where chronic, low-grade inflammation subtly shapes long-term outcomes.

TGF-β Signaling and Microglial Crosstalk

Among the signaling pathways governing NG2-immune interactions, TGF-β signaling has emerged as a central regulator, whereby NG2-derived TGF-β2 maintains microglia in a quiescent state under physiological conditions [37]. When TGF-β signaling is genetically disrupted in NG2 cells, microglia exhibit heightened activation, producing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and pro-inflammatory cytokines that accelerate neuronal and vascular injury [37] (Fig. 3). These findings highlight that NG2 cells are not merely downstream targets of immune responses but active modulators of microglial tone.

Interestingly, this crosstalk is bidirectional, whereby activated microglia release cytokines and growth factors that influence the proliferation and fate specification of NG2. In models of neuroinflammation induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) or ischemia, microglia-derived mediators alter NG2 proliferation rates, induce proteoglycan shedding from the NG2 surface, and influence whether OPCs differentiate or remain in a progenitor state [38, 39]. This reciprocal relationship suggests a feedback loop in which NG2 cells and microglia collectively determine the trajectory of tissue response to injury. The relevance of this interplay extends beyond classical inflammatory insults. In CSVD, subtle endothelial dysfunction and chronic hypoperfusion provide a continuous low-grade stimulus for immune activation. The capacity of NG2 glia to dampen or amplify microglial reactivity via TGF-β and other signaling axes may, therefore, directly influence the balance between repair and degeneration. Targeting this interface pharmacologically, either by stabilizing TGF-β signaling or modulating receptor sensitivity, represents an emerging therapeutic strategy worth further exploration.

Endocytic and Phagocytic Potential

Although traditionally overshadowed by microglia in debris clearance, NG2 glia display limited but functionally relevant endocytic and phagocytic capabilities. They can internalize amyloid-β (Aβ42) via macropinocytosis and degrade it through autophagic pathways, albeit at lower efficiency than microglia [40] (Fig. 3). This process may represent a supplementary clearance mechanism in areas where microglial activity is compromised or saturated. In demyelinating conditions, NG2 cells have been observed to interact with and internalize myelin debris, potentially preparing the local ECM for remyelination [41]. Whether similar activity occurs in the context of CSVD, where fibrinogen, oxidized lipids, and plasma proteins accumulate due to BBB breakdown, remains an open question. Early evidence suggests that proteoglycan shedding may facilitate or hinder the recruitment of other immune cells, adding another layer of complexity [41]. Importantly, phagocytic and endocytic functions may also intersect with lineage fate decisions. Uptake of specific substrates can trigger intracellular signaling cascades that bias differentiation or maintain progenitor states. In vascular cognitive impairment, where both neurodegeneration and repair must be balanced over long time frames, the nuanced capacity of NG2 cells to manage extracellular debris may represent a subtle but therapeutically exploitable property.

Inflammatory Polarization

Emerging data indicate that NG2 glia, similar to microglia and astrocytes, transition across a continuum of activation states shaped by the local inflammatory and vascular microenvironment, rather than adopting discrete polarization phenotypes. In pro-inflammatory microenvironments, NG2 cells adopt phenotypes that secrete cytokines, inhibit oligodendrogenesis, and exacerbate tissue damage [42]. Conversely, under reparative conditions, they express factors that support angiogenesis, promote oligodendrocyte maturation, and suppress excessive immune activation [43]. This duality implies a context-dependent switch mechanism embedded within NG2 biology. This capacity for polarization carries translational significance, whereby modulating the extracellular cues that govern NG2 phenotype, it may be possible to tilt the balance toward regenerative outcomes in disorders such as CSVD. For example, enhancing anti-inflammatory signaling pathways or inhibiting key mediators of the pro-inflammatory shift could improve remyelination and vascular stability in vulnerable white matter tracts. Finally, the recognition of NG2 cells as active participants in inflammatory networks broadens the conceptual framework of CNS immunity. Rather than a unidirectional cascade from immune insult to glial damage, NG2 glia emerge as integrators capable of influencing vascular tone, immune cell recruitment, and repair processes simultaneously. In the setting of small vessel disease, characterized by chronic low-grade inflammation, microvascular leakage, and progressive white matter rarefaction, this integrated role offers a new lens through which to interpret pathogenesis and design targeted interventions [9, 11, 30].

NG2 Cells in CSVD Pathogenesis

Pathophysiological Landscape of CSVD

CSVD comprises a spectrum of conditions affecting the brain’s small-caliber vessels, including arterioles, venules, and capillaries, which range in diameter from 50 to 400 μm [44]. The primary lesions occur in the subcortical gray matter and deep white matter, regions particularly vulnerable to hypoperfusion and barrier dysfunction. Clinically, CSVD presents heterogeneously, with manifestations spanning ischemic strokes, cognitive decline, and vascular dementia, while neuroimaging often reveals white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), cerebral microbleeds, lacunes, and enlarged perivascular spaces (ePVS) [5]. Increasingly, the field recognizes that CSVD is not a singular pathology but rather a dynamic interplay of vascular injury, inflammation, and glial dysfunction.

One widely accepted model attributes white matter lesions in CSVD to a triad of BBB leakage, chronic hypoperfusion, and impaired cerebrovascular reactivity [1]. Hypertension remains a dominant risk factor whereby sustained elevation in blood pressure accelerates arteriosclerosis, lipo-hyalinosis, and fibro-hyalinosis, progressively narrowing small vessel lumina and reducing compliance [45]. These changes compromise autoregulatory capacity, leading to episodes of ischemia and subsequent white matter injury. Besides, microthrombi formation exacerbated by endothelial dysfunction further impedes perfusion and potentiates local inflammatory cascades (Fig. 4).

Additionally, endothelial cells under stress release von Willebrand factor (vWF) in response to inflammatory signaling, which enhances platelet adhesion and activation. Platelets subsequently produce thromboxane A2 and adenosine diphosphate (ADP), triggering vasoconstriction and further platelet aggregation, both hallmarks of microvascular sclerosis [46]. Beyond hypertension and arteriosclerosis, other contributors include venous collagenosis, arteriolar tortuosity, demyelination, and glial cell loss, each of which is worsened by low-grade inflammation associated with aging (“inflammaging”) [47]. This chronic inflammatory milieu reflects a convergence of DNA repair deficits, stem cell senescence, and sustained cytokine release, collectively damaging oligodendrocytes, impairing myelination, and producing the imaging and clinical features characteristic of CSVD.

BBB Breakdown: Mechanisms and NG2 Glial Contributions

The BBB is maintained by the neurogliovascular unit (NGVU), composed of endothelial cells, basement membrane, pericytes, and astrocytic endfeet [48]. This highly selective interface regulates molecular exchange and supports the metabolic needs of neurons and glia. Disruption of any NGVU component destabilizes the BBB and alters cBF, impairing normal brain homeostasis [49]. Increasing evidence indicates that BBB breakdown is an early driver of CSVD pathogenesis rather than a late consequence. When the BBB is compromised, blood-derived proteins, cytokines, and immune cells infiltrate the parenchyma, amplifying neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and white matter injury [50]. This vascular leakage represents a core pathological mechanism underlying CSVD-related white matter degeneration. BBB disruption also triggers reactive responses in microglia, astrocytes, and NG2 glia, which further modulate perivascular inflammation and lesion evolution [25]. Importantly, this neuroimmune activation does not simply reflect a bystander effect but actively shapes disease progression.

While pathways such as Wnt, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), fibroblast growth factor (FGF), and TNF broadly regulate angiogenesis and neuroinflammatory responses, they are not BBB-defining signaling programs per se. BBB identity and barrier function are instead governed by endothelial-specific molecular regulators, including major facilitator superfamily domain-containing 2 A (MFSD2A), which suppresses vesicular transcytosis [51]; sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor 1 (S1P1), which stabilizes endothelial junctional architecture [52]; glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), which supports metabolic barrier function; and tight junction proteins such as claudin-5 that directly control paracellular permeability [53]. In CSVD, pericyte degeneration, hypoperfusion, and chronic inflammatory stress are associated with reduced MFSD2A expression, increased transcytosis, junctional destabilization, and metabolic transporter dysfunction, providing a mechanistically precise link between microvascular injury and BBB failure.

Furthermore, NG2 glia are closely associated with vascular architecture, whereby during embryogenesis, NG2 cells align with nascent vasculature, guiding angiogenic sprouting and stabilizing developing networks [54]. This vascular partnership persists postnatally within the adult neurovascular niche. NG2-derived hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) promotes endothelial proliferation and vasculogenesis in white matter tracts such as corpus callosum [55], while NG2-derived Wnt7 ligands activate β-catenin signaling supporting vascular stabilization rather than BBB specification per se [56]. Beyond angiogenic modulation, NG2 cells influence BBB integrity directly through secretion of TGF-β, which modulates tight junction protein expression; selective deletion of TGF-β from NG2 cells leads to barrier breakdown in neonatal models [57]. Conversely, endothelial-derived TGF-β regulates NG2 cell development, highlighting a bidirectional signaling network critical for BBB maintenance [58, 59]. Moreover, NG2 interactions with astrocytes and pericytes further reinforce barrier stability; Wnt signaling disruptions in NG2 cells impair astrocytic end-foot coverage, increase BBB permeability, and perturb pericyte proliferation [60–62]. These observations suggest that the most CSVD-relevant functions of NG2 glia are exerted at the level of the neurovascular unit, through interactions with endothelial cells, pericytes, basement membrane components, and astrocytic end-feet, rather than through neuron-centered synaptic mechanisms. Collectively, these findings suggest that NG2 dysfunction, whether through signaling defects, inflammatory reprogramming, impaired trophic support, or disrupted vascular crosstalk, likely contributes to BBB vulnerability and lesion progression in CSVD.

White Matter Injury, Perivascular Inflammation, and NG2 Glial Response

White matter injury is a defining feature of CSVD and is detectable through neuroimaging markers such as WMHs, cerebral microbleeds, lacunes, and atrophy [63]. WMHs, characterized by increased water content in white matter bundles, appear as hyperintensities on FLAIR MRI and reflect ischemic damage, myelin disruption, and gliovascular dysfunction [64]. Their development is driven by ischemia, demyelination, vessel wall degeneration, reactive gliosis, microhemorrhages, and disturbances in BBB integrity and CSF dynamics. Statistical evidence confirms a strong association between BBB permeability and WMH burden [65], reinforcing the idea that barrier failure and white matter pathology are mechanistically intertwined.

The perivascular space (PVS) is increasingly implicated in CSVD pathophysiology. Widened PVS correlates with impaired waste clearance, BBB dysfunction, perivascular inflammation, and reduced cerebrovascular reactivity [66, 67]. As part of the glymphatic system, PVS channels facilitate CSF-mediated clearance of metabolic waste. Increased arteriolar stiffness, commonly observed in hypertension, reduces pulsatility-driven CSF movement, leading to metabolite accumulation. This buildup disrupts neurovascular homeostasis, amplifies glial reactivity, and promotes white matter degeneration [68]. Thus, the interplay between vascular rigidity, impaired clearance, and glial activation represents a self-reinforcing cycle that drives disease progression.

Within the perivascular inflammatory niche of CSVD lesions, NG2 cells exhibit both protective and maladaptive responses. Normally, NG2 cells proliferate following oligodendrocyte loss, differentiating into myelinating oligodendrocytes to restore conduction. However, the hostile microenvironment of CSVD lesions, including hypoxia, immune infiltration, myelin debris, plasma protein leakage, ECM degradation, and persistent astrocytic and microglial activation, impairs NG2 differentiation, stalling remyelination [25]. Inhibitory cues such as degraded myelin-associated glycoproteins interact with Nogo receptors on NG2 cells, diverting them toward astrocytic rather than oligodendrocytic fates. Experimental blockade of Nogo receptor 1 (NgR1) restores oligodendrogenesis, reduces maladaptive astrocytic transformation, and improves white matter repair even when treatment is delayed [69]. Similarly, extracellular matrix-derived hyaluronic acid fragments suppress myelin gene expression via the Toll-like receptors (TLRs) that lead to the activation of the AKT pathway, which in turn regulates the activity of the Forkhead box O3 (FoxO3) transcription factor (TLR/AKT/FoxO3) pathway, further obstructing lineage progression [70]. These findings highlight NG2 cells as crucial yet vulnerable nodes in CSVD-associated white matter injury.

Additional regulatory layers involve angiogenic and trophic signaling. In this case, NG2 migration is guided by Wnt-chemokine receptor 4 pathways, enabling vessel-associated navigation during injury responses [71]. Dysregulated Wnt signaling disrupts perivascular anchoring and tight junction integrity, increasing BBB permeability [61]. NG2-derived Wnt7a and Wnt7b promote angiogenesis and endothelial proliferation both in vitro and in vivo, while HIF signaling supports white matter angiogenesis [55, 72]. Furthermore, VEGF isoforms differentially regulate NG2 proliferation and migration (VEGF-C and VEGF-A, respectively) [73–75], while FGF2 and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) further enhance NG2 expansion [76]. This bidirectional “oligo-vascular niche,” wherein endothelial cells support NG2 proliferation and NG2 cells stabilize vasculature, is a potentially powerful therapeutic target [77].

Importantly, NG2 expression is not exclusive to NG2 glia; pericytes and mural cells of the NVU also express NG2 proteoglycan under normal conditions [78]. Pericytes regulate capillary blood flow via contractile mechanisms, influence BBB integrity, and participate in angiogenesis [79]. Their dysfunction is now implicated in CSVD, linking vascular stiffness and permeability to glial dysregulation. Recent positron emission tomography (PET) imaging studies using 11C-PK11195 demonstrated that increased neuroinflammation in regions with enlarged PVS but no direct correlation with BBB permeability [80]. This dissociation suggests that inflammatory reprogramming, potentially mediated by perivascular NG2 cells, may amplify or mitigate damage depending on the activation state. Indeed, NG2 cells secrete cytokines such as TNF-α and chemokines like CCL2 in response to injury, positioning them as active immune modulators rather than passive structural elements [81, 82].

Finally, hormonal and metabolic cues shape NG2 lineage outcomes; for example, triiodothyronine (T3) promotes NG2 differentiation into mature oligodendrocytes, a process often disrupted by inflammatory cytokines. In vitro, both pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-4, IL-10, IFN-γ) impaired T3-driven maturation, while specific combinations (e.g., TNF-α with IFN-γ) partially restored differentiation [83]. Similarly, antigen presentation via MHC-II is dynamically regulated by cytokine interactions, further emphasizing that NG2 cell fate and immune properties are highly context-dependent. These findings collectively underscore NG2 glia as central players in the pathophysiological web of CSVD, integrating vascular integrity, immune signaling, and myelin repair.

NG2 Cell Crosstalk with Microglia and Astrocytes

Microglia–NG2 Interactions in Myelination and White Matter Repair

Although microglia–NG2 interactions are often studied in the context of neuronal circuits and developmental myelination, in CSVD, these interactions are most relevant insofar as they shape white matter integrity, perivascular inflammation, and repair capacity in chronically hypoperfused tissue. Microglia are the primary immune cells of the CNS and serve as first responders to injury and disease. Arising from primitive myeloid progenitors before embryonic day 8, microglia maintain brain immune surveillance throughout life [17]. While most studies have investigated NG2 cells or microglia in isolation, emerging evidence suggests that the dynamic interactions between these two cell types critically influence both neurodevelopment and the pathogenesis of disorders such as CSVD. NG2 cells, as OPCs, are indispensable for myelination, while microglia contribute both to myelin sculpting and clearance, thus indirectly influencing oligodendrogenesis. Understanding the nature of this crosstalk could uncover novel therapeutic avenues.

Experimental models provide strong evidence that microglia actively modulate myelination. In zebrafish, microglia phagocytose excessive or aberrant myelin sheaths during developmental refinement; when microglia are depleted, ectopic myelin persists, demonstrating their role in sculpting functional connectivity [84]. Neuronal activity further shapes microglial behavior, regulating both their contact with neuronal cell bodies and their ability to phagocytose myelin in an activity-dependent manner [85]. Ultrastructural analyses show that microglia recognize degenerating myelin via exposed phosphatidylserine, an “eat-me” signal, and clear it during early postnatal maturation [86]. More recent work further demonstrates that microglial state transitions and lipid-handling programs are required for efficient myelin remodeling and adaptive remyelination, and that disruption of these programs impairs oligodendrocyte lineage progression and white matter integrity [87, 88]. As NG2 cells give rise to oligodendrocytes, the source of newly formed myelin, these findings support a coordinated glial circuit in which microglia serve as the primary mediators of myelin clearance, while NG2 lineage cells contribute to myelin replacement and remodeling through lineage progression and trophic signaling, rather than through direct phagocytic activity.

Beyond debris clearance, microglia provide trophic support to NG2 cells. Microglia-conditioned media (MCDM) protect NG2 glia from apoptosis during growth factor deprivation by inhibiting Bax translocation to mitochondria, thereby preventing intrinsic caspase-dependent apoptosis. MCDM promotes morphological differentiation, increases APC+ mature oligodendrocytes, and decreases O4+ immature NG2 cells, reflecting enhanced lineage progression [89, 90]. Insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) appears central to this effect, being 6.3-fold more abundant in MCDM compared to astrocyte-conditioned media (ACDM). The CREB pathway, though only weakly activated, may contribute to this trophic influence. Importantly, MCDM also contains unique cytokine profiles, including elevated E-selectin, fractalkine, neuropilin-2, IL-2, IL-5, and VEGF, that distinguish it from astrocytic support and hint at specialized microglial programming [90]. Collectively, these findings highlight the ability of microglia to modulate NG2 fate and promote myelin repair.

IGF family members have further been implicated in sustaining oligodendrocytic health. CD11c+ microglia are a transient, developmentally enriched subset that populate primary myelinating regions, serving as a major source of IGF-1. Loss of IGF-1 or its receptor disrupts myelination and can result in microcephaly, while IGF-1 overexpression enhances oligodendrocyte numbers and brain size. Partial Igf1 depletion in CD11c+ microglia reduces myelin gene expression (Plp, Mbp, Mag, Mog), decreases brain weight, and increases poorly myelinated fibers in the corpus callosum [91]. Similarly, IGF-2 from both resting and IFNγ-activated microglia protects oligodendrocytes from apoptosis and mitigates TNFα-induced cytotoxicity via blockade of JNK activation [92]. Together, these studies suggest that microglia-derived IGF signaling orchestrates NG2 differentiation and survival, providing a mechanistic basis for targeted interventions in CSVD aimed at preserving white matter integrity.

Microglial signals also regulate NG2 proliferation and remyelination through additional molecular pathways. Transglutaminase-2 (TG2) released by microglia, in concert with laminin, activates the adhesion GPCR ADGRG1 (GPR56) on NG2 cells, stimulating OPC proliferation and enhancing remyelination in demyelination models [93]. Neuropilin-1 (Nrp1), a transmembrane receptor expressed on amoeboid and activated microglia, facilitates NG2 proliferation by trans-activating PDGFRα signaling, especially in suboptimal PDGF-AA environments [94]. These mechanisms suggest a highly nuanced, context-dependent crosstalk that could be pharmacologically leveraged to enhance repair processes in CSVD, where remyelination failure contributes to progressive cognitive and motor deficits.

Importantly, the activation state of microglia determines their impact on NG2 cell responses. Toll-like receptor (TLR) pathways exemplify this dichotomy: TLR4 activation via lipopolysaccharide (LPS) promotes NG2 proliferation and differentiation through ciliary neurotrophic factor (CNTF) upregulation, whereas TLR2 activation via zymosan induces oligodendrocyte loss and secondary demyelination without stimulating NG2 proliferation [95]. Thus, microglial phenotypes oscillating between pro-regenerative and cytotoxic states directly influence the capacity of NG2 cells to restore white matter integrity. In the context of CSVD, where chronic low-grade inflammation predominates, clarifying these activation dynamics may inform therapeutic strategies aimed at biasing microglia toward regenerative phenotypes.

Genetic ablation studies further underscore the complexity of this axis. Cell-type-specific deletion of NG2 in either OPCs or myeloid lineage cells revealed distinct yet intersecting consequences. Myeloid NG2 deletion reduced macrophage recruitment and lesion size following demyelination but paradoxically impaired remyelination, likely due to insufficient cytokine support and suboptimal debris clearance [96]. In contrast, OPC-specific NG2 deletion compromised intrinsic proliferation and delayed oligodendrocyte generation, underscoring cell-autonomous contributions. Together, these findings reveal a bidirectional, NG2-dependent feedback loop between microglia/macrophages and OPCs that orchestrates white matter repair and suggests multiple intervention points for modulating remyelination efficiency.

Astrocyte–NG2 Interplay in Neuroinflammation and Vascular Homeostasis

Astrocytes are the most abundant glial population in the CNS, comprising 20–50% of brain cells depending on the region [97]. Traditionally viewed as passive support cells, astrocytes are now recognized as active regulators of neuroinflammation, synaptic function, and cerebrovascular dynamics. Broadly classified as fibrous (white matter) and protoplasmic (gray matter) astrocytes, these cells maintain CNS homeostasis by regulating ion balance, neurotransmitter recycling, and cerebral blood flow. Under pathological conditions, astrocytes undergo astrogliosis, marked by hypertrophy, increased GFAP expression, and proliferation, leading to the formation of reactive astrocytes that influence both repair and disease progression [98, 99]. Understanding their interplay with NG2 cells is essential for unraveling the complex glial networks underlying CSVD.

NG2 cells and astrocytes frequently converge at sites of injury. Following stab wounds in adult rat brains, NG2 cells proliferate and participate in glial scar formation alongside reactive astrocytes, indicating coordinated roles in structural repair [100]. While native astrocytes are the primary drivers of astrogliosis, NG2 cells can also differentiate into astrocytic phenotypes under certain inflammatory conditions. This complementary division of labor highlights a flexible glial response, where astrocytes dominate scar architecture while NG2 cells contribute progenitor versatility. Astrocytes further regulate cerebral perfusion by coupling neuronal activity to vascular responses via endfoot-mediated signaling, thereby influencing metabolic supply and interstitial fluid dynamics [19]. These vascular interactions intersect with NG2-mediated vascular support, suggesting a collaborative regulation of neurovascular integrity relevant to CSVD pathophysiology.

Astrocytes also supply trophic and mitogenic signals that govern oligodendrocyte lineage dynamics. OPCs require continuous growth factor support to avoid apoptosis; astrocyte-conditioned media (ACDM) enhances OPC survival and proliferation, likely via factors including PDGF-AA, bFGF, IGF-1, CNTF, growth hormone, TIMP-1, and thrombospondin [90]. Comparative studies reveal that ACDM strongly promotes proliferation, while MCDM more effectively drives maturation, suggesting that astrocytes and microglia provide temporally distinct cues for OPC expansion and differentiation [90]. These signaling ecosystems are highly relevant to CSVD, where white matter degeneration reflects both impaired progenitor support and disrupted differentiation trajectories.

Finally, astrocytes engage in complex metabolic and signaling crosstalk with neurons and other glia. Na+-dependent glutamate uptake in astrocytes induces intracellular sodium signaling, which propagates through gap junction–connected astrocytic networks [101, 102]. This functional syncytium coordinates ionic homeostasis, neurotransmitter clearance, and possibly intercellular metabolic support, indirectly influencing NG2 function by shaping the extracellular environment. In the context of CSVD, where BBB leakage, hypoperfusion, and chronic inflammation converge, deciphering how astrocytic signaling influences NG2 cell fate could unlock new approaches to stabilize white matter and mitigate disease progression.

Integrated Glial Dynamics in CSVD Progression

The interplay between microglia, astrocytes, and NG2 glia represents a coordinated yet context-dependent network that governs neurovascular health and white matter integrity. In CSVD, this network becomes imbalanced: microglia may skew toward maladaptive inflammatory phenotypes, astrocytes toward chronic reactivity, and NG2 cells toward stalled differentiation or maladaptive remodeling. These disturbances are not merely additive but synergistic. Reactive astrocytes can amplify microglial activation, releasing cytokines that further suppress OPC maturation. Conversely, microglia-derived trophic factors may partially rescue NG2 differentiation if delivered in an appropriately timed reparative window. Moreover, glial–vascular interactions through pericytes, endothelial cells, and basement membrane components feed back into this loop, either restoring BBB integrity or perpetuating its breakdown. Understanding these dynamic cellular interdependencies is critical for identifying therapeutic windows and combinatorial strategies. Future interventions may require simultaneous modulation of multiple glial populations to recalibrate the neurovascular unit, promote remyelination, and preserve cognitive function in patients with CSVD.

Therapeutic Implications, Limitations, and Future Directions

The accumulating evidence presented in this review highlights NG2-expressing glial cells as both contributors to and potential modulators of CSVD pathology. However, translating these cellular insights into effective therapies remains challenging. Three key dimensions require critical attention: targeting NG2 cell differentiation and remyelination, modulating glial–vascular crosstalk, and addressing the chronic inflammatory milieu that shapes disease progression.

Strategies to promote NG2 cell differentiation into mature oligodendrocytes represent a promising, yet underexplored, approach. In CSVD, demyelination and failed remyelination underlie WMHs and cognitive decline. Interfering with inhibitory signals such as NgR1 (a key suppressor of axonal sprouting and OPC differentiation) or hyaluronic acid fragment–mediated FoxO3 activation could relieve differentiation blocks, as demonstrated in stroke and demyelination models [69, 70]. Monoclonal antibodies and peptide antagonists targeting the Nogo–NgR1 axis (e.g., anti-Nogo-A antibodies and NgR1 decoy peptides) have progressed to early-phase clinical evaluation in spinal cord injury and stroke [103]. In the context of CSVD, where chronic white matter injury and remyelination failure dominate, targeted NgR1 inhibition could be repurposed to relieve intrinsic differentiation blocks in perivascular OPCs. Importantly, CSVD-specific adaptation would require careful titration and lesion-stage targeting to avoid aberrant axonal sprouting or maladaptive plasticity in chronically hypoperfused tissue. However, OPCs in the CSVD environment are exposed to hypoperfusion, BBB leakage, and sustained inflammation, factors that differ substantially from classical demyelinating diseases such as multiple sclerosis. Importantly, while rodent studies demonstrate robust NG2 cell recruitment and differentiation under permissive conditions, direct functional evidence for efficient remyelination by NG2 cells in human CSVD lesions remains limited.

Human clinico-pathological studies have reported accumulations of PDGFRA+ OPC-like cells at the margins of white matter degeneration, suggesting recruitment occurs [104], yet whether these cells complete lineage progression into myelinating oligodendrocytes in the chronically hypoperfused human white matter remains unclear. Additional small-molecule and biologic candidates with translational relevance include clemastine fumarate, an antihistamine shown to promote OPC differentiation and remyelination in multiple sclerosis clinical trials; benztropine, a muscarinic receptor antagonist that accelerates oligodendrocyte maturation in preclinical demyelination models; and metformin, which rejuvenates aged OPCs and enhances remyelination capacity in animal studies [105, 106]. Although these agents have not been tested in CSVD, their established CNS bioavailability and safety profiles position them as attractive repositioning candidates for proof-of-concept trials targeting white matter repair in vascular cognitive impairment. Thus, whether targeted modulation of intrinsic inhibitors is sufficient in this unique vascular-inflammatory context remains uncertain. Rigorous in vivo modeling, incorporating vascular dysfunction and low-grade inflammation, alongside validation in human tissue and transcriptomic datasets, will be essential to evaluate feasibility and safety.

Furthermore, NG2 cells operate within a glial–vascular niche, interacting with endothelial cells, astrocytes, and pericytes to maintain BBB integrity and vascular stability. This reciprocal communication offers therapeutic leverage points. While direct PDGFRα agonists are not clinically available, pharmacological strategies that preserve pericyte survival or enhance endothelial PDGF-B expression, such as angiopoietin/Tie2 pathway modulators or endothelial stabilizers [107], may indirectly restore trophic support to NG2 glia in CSVD. Similarly, small-molecule modulators of Wnt signaling (e.g., tankyrase inhibitors or porcupine inhibitors) offer experimental tools to probe Wnt-driven OPC recruitment, although their systemic effects on angiogenesis and metabolism necessitate careful CNS-targeted delivery strategies before translational consideration. NG2-derived TGF-β supports tight-junction integrity, while endothelial Wnt and TGF-β signaling reciprocally promotes NG2 proliferation and maturation [57–59]. Interventions that preserve or restore this bidirectional signaling may simultaneously stabilize the BBB and enhance reparative myelination. However, much of the mechanistic evidence for Wnt-guided NG2 migration and vascular patterning derives from zebrafish and rodent systems, and its conservation in human CSVD remains largely inferred from transcriptional enrichment rather than functional validation [108]. Emerging human snRNA-seq datasets of cerebrovascular disease and white matter pathology identify PDGFRA+ glial populations and enrichment of Wnt/TGF-β pathway components, supporting partial conservation of these signaling modules [109], but spatially resolved confirmation of Wnt-driven NG2 migration in human lesions is still lacking. Likewise, targeting neuropilin-1 (Nrp1)-mediated PDGFRα activation or TG2–ADGRG1 pathways could fine-tune OPC proliferation in regions of vascular insufficiency [93, 94], yet these pathways intersect with angiogenesis and immune regulation in humans, raising concerns regarding context-dependent off-target effects. The timing and regional specificity of such interventions are therefore likely critical, as excessive proliferation without coordinated differentiation risks maladaptive remodeling or glial scarring.

CSVD also exists within a background of chronic, low-grade neuroinflammation, characterized by maladaptive microglial activation and astrocytic reactivity. Biasing microglia toward regenerative phenotypes (e.g., IGF-1/IGF-2-enriched, LPS/TLR4-like activation) while avoiding detrimental inflammatory cascades (e.g., sustained TLR2/zymosan-like responses) could enhance endogenous NG2-driven repair [90, 92, 95]. Notably, subsets of inflammation-associated NG2 cells, including CD11c+ populations described in rodent demyelination and stroke models, have been implicated in lesion remodeling and immune–glial crosstalk [110]. However, whether analogous NG2 subpopulations exist in human CSVD lesions remains unresolved. Current human transcriptomic datasets support the presence of reactive OPC states with immune-interactive signatures [111] but do not yet definitively map CD11c+ NG2 subsets or their functional role in human pathology. This translational gap underscores the need for spatial transcriptomics and lesion-stage-resolved profiling of NG2 heterogeneity in human CSVD tissue. Together, these examples illustrate that translational progress in CSVD will likely derive less from de novo drug discovery and more from rational repositioning of remyelination-promoting and neurovascular-stabilizing agents developed for demyelinating and cerebrovascular disorders (Table 2). Future studies should prioritize pharmacodynamic biomarkers (e.g., OPC differentiation signatures, myelin-sensitive imaging) and lesion-stage stratification to guide precision deployment of these candidates in CSVD.

Beyond modulating endogenous NG2 glial responses through anti-inflammatory or trophic mechanisms, emerging regenerative strategies aim to supplement or replenish the NG2 lineage in disease contexts characterized by progenitor exhaustion or maladaptive fate restriction. Transplantation of OPCs or NG2+ progenitors has demonstrated robust engraftment, migration along vascular scaffolds, and functional remyelination in models of demyelination and white matter injury, including ischemic and hypoperfusion paradigms [56]. Although not yet evaluated in chronic CSVD models, these studies provide proof-of-principle that exogenous NG2+ cells can integrate into gliovascular niches and contribute to structural repair. In parallel, in situ lineage reprogramming approaches offer a non-transplantation-based alternative to expand the reparative NG2 pool. Forced expression or pharmacological modulation of lineage-specifying transcription factors (e.g., Olig2, Sox10, Ascl1) has enabled the conversion of astrocytes and NG2-negative glial populations into oligodendrocyte-lineage cells in vivo in demyelination and stroke models [115–117]. Such strategies may be particularly relevant to CSVD, where chronic inflammation, metabolic stress, and vascular insufficiency constrain endogenous NG2 differentiation. Importantly, any NG2 supplementation strategy in CSVD must account for the hostile microenvironment of chronically hypoperfused white matter. Successful cell-based interventions will likely require combinatorial approaches that couple progenitor replacement or reprogramming with restoration of the neurovascular niche, including normalization of BBB integrity, pericyte support, and metabolic substrate delivery. Thus, NG2 cell supplementation should be conceptualized not as a standalone therapy but as part of an integrated regenerative strategy targeting the oligo-vascular unit.

Despite these therapeutic opportunities, several limitations temper optimism. NG2 glial biology in humans remains incompletely understood, and much of the mechanistic evidence derives from rodent models that only partially recapitulate the chronic, heterogeneous, and age-associated nature of human CSVD. There are currently no validated biomarkers of NG2 activity or lineage progression in vivo, restricting the ability to monitor therapeutic response or stratify patients for targeted interventions. Furthermore, pathways implicated in NG2 proliferation, migration, and differentiation often overlap with essential processes in angiogenesis, synaptic remodeling, and immune surveillance; their manipulation may therefore carry unintended off-target effects. The temporal dynamics of NG2 function, beneficial during early lesion repair yet potentially maladaptive during chronic gliosis, add another layer of complexity to translational design. A critical unmet need is the development of integrated experimental models that accurately reflect the multifactorial nature of CSVD. Most current models emphasize either vascular injury or demyelination but fail to reproduce the combined effects of age, hypertension, BBB dysfunction, and white matter degeneration. Multidimensional platforms, including organotypic cultures, microfluidic NVU models, and inducible multi-hit animal paradigms, will be essential to test NG2-targeted interventions in a biologically faithful context. Moreover, leveraging advanced imaging techniques (e.g., DTI-ALPS for glymphatic flow, PET-based neuroinflammation markers, or in vivo OPC lineage tracing) could accelerate translation by linking mechanistic changes to clinically relevant biomarkers.

In summary, NG2-expressing glial cells occupy a pivotal position at the intersection of vascular dysfunction, inflammation, and white matter repair in CSVD. While preclinical evidence identifies multiple potential intervention points, from enhancing OPC differentiation to stabilizing glial–vascular interactions, the complexity of CSVD demands integrated, systems-level therapeutic strategies. Future research should prioritize combinatorial approaches, rigorous temporal mapping of cellular responses, and biomarker-guided clinical translation. By addressing these challenges, the field may move closer to disease-modifying interventions capable of preserving cognitive and functional independence in individuals at risk of CSVD.

Conclusion

CSVD is a leading cause of stroke and vascular cognitive impairment, yet its cellular drivers have remained incompletely understood. This review consolidates emerging evidence positioning NG2-expressing glial cells as pivotal regulators of neurovascular integrity, inflammatory responses, and white matter repair. Once regarded primarily as oligodendrocyte progenitors, NG2 glia now stand out as dynamic cellular hubs that sense, integrate, and respond to vascular, immune, and metabolic signals within the neurovascular unit. Their interactions with microglia and astrocytes, as well as with endothelial cells and pericytes, shape the balance between injury propagation and tissue recovery. The translational promise of NG2 glia lies in their dual capacity: they are both victims of the hostile CSVD milieu and potential agents of repair if their intrinsic and extrinsic signaling pathways can be appropriately modulated. Targeted strategies to promote NG2 differentiation, stabilize glial–vascular crosstalk, and recalibrate neuroinflammation may, for the first time, offer disease-modifying approaches in a condition long considered irreversible and purely degenerative. However, the complexity of CSVD demands integrated solutions. Successful translation will depend on combinatorial therapies informed by rigorous temporal and spatial mapping of glial responses, validated in experimental models that faithfully capture human CSVD’s multifactorial nature. By bridging cellular neuroscience with vascular biology, the study of NG2 glia opens a new frontier, one that may transform our capacity to preserve cognitive and functional independence in aging populations at risk for this silent yet devastating disease.
