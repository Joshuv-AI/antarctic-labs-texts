Cerebellar liponeurocytoma (cLNC), classified as a WHO grade 2 neoplasm, is a rare neuronal tumor that predominantly occurs in the posterior fossa of adults.1 Although typically slow-growing, cLNC carries a documented risk of local recurrence, particularly after subtotal resection.2,3 Because of its rarity, its evolutionary history and optimal postoperative management remain incompletely defined. Gross-total resection is generally recommended; however, complete removal is not always feasible because of anatomical constraints.2,3 Adjuvant radiotherapy has been considered in selected cases, especially when residual tumor or elevated proliferative indices are present; however, no consensus guidelines exist.3 The status and role of stereotactic radiosurgery, including GKRS, have not been fully established, and available reports remain limited.4,5 We describe a case of postoperative progression of cLNC successfully treated with GKRS and discuss management considerations that integrate anatomical and molecular factors.

Illustrative Case

A woman in her 50s initially presented with a headache. MRI revealed a 21-mm homogeneously enhancing tumor in the fourth ventricle, involving the cerebellar vermis (Fig. 1). The lesion was well demarcated, iso- to hypointense on T1-weighted imaging with focal marginal hyperintensity, and mildly hyperintense on T2-weighted imaging. Contrast-enhanced sequences showed relatively homogeneous enhancement with a small cystic component. Three-dimensional CT angiography revealed a hypovascular tumor (Fig. 2). Methionine PET showed increased uptake, which was consistent with metabolically active tumor tissue (Fig. 2).

The patient underwent a microsurgical resection at another institution. Histopathological examination revealed uniform small round cells arranged in sheets, with interspersed vacuolated, lipidized cells resembling adipocytes (Fig. 3). The Ki-67 labeling index was approximately 5%. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated diffuse positivity for synaptophysin and focal positivity for neuron-specific enolase, while the cells were negative for GFAP, OLIG2, and EMA. Beta-catenin immunostaining showed membranous and cytoplasmic expression without nuclear accumulation. These findings supported the diagnosis of cLNC.

Comprehensive genomic profiling using a 324-gene panel (FoundationOne, Foundation Medicine) identified no APC, KDR, or TP53 missense mutations or ATRX alterations.

Postoperatively, dizziness, and truncal ataxia persisted for a prolonged period, necessitating transfer to a rehabilitation hospital. Although rehabilitation led to gradual improvement of the condition, residual dizziness and right upper and lower limb ataxia persisted. The patient was then discharged.

Intraoperatively, a small residual lesion was left adjacent to the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) loop because of vascular adherence (Fig. 4). Follow-up MRI demonstrated slower progression of the residual tumor over 1 year 8 months (Fig. 4), prompting a referral to our institution. Given the small tumor volume, focal progression, and intimate relationship with the PICA loop, repeat microsurgical resection was expected to be technically challenging and potentially associated with increased morbidity. Therefore, salvage GKRS was selected.

A marginal dose of 16 Gy (maximum dose 24.6 Gy) was administered into the tumor margin. Treatment strategy emphasized conformal coverage while minimizing radiation exposure to the adjacent brainstem and vascular structures (Fig. 4). Follow-up MRI confirmed local tumor control. At 2 years 3 months after GKRS, no further progression was observed, and the patient remained neurologically stable without treatment-related morbidity (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Observations

This case demonstrated long-term local control of the postoperative progression of small residual cLNC with GKRS. The lesion was small, well circumscribed, and located adjacent to the PICA loop, making radiosurgery a rational, minimally invasive treatment option. A marginal dose of 16 Gy achieved sustained tumor control without any radiation-induced complications.

Cerebellar LNC and Role of Stereotactic Radiosurgery

Cerebellar LNC was first described by Bechtel et al.6 in 1978 as a mixed mesenchymal and neuroectodermal cerebellar tumor. Most reported patients are in middle adulthood showing symptoms related to posterior fossa mass effects, such as headache, dizziness, or gait disturbance.7,8 Histopathologically, the tumor is characterized by neuronal differentiation with diffuse synaptophysin expression and the presence of lipidized or adipocyte-like cells within the neoplasm.1,8 Although it is generally considered a low-grade tumor with relatively indolent behavior, it has been reported to recur in approximately 30% of cases, particularly after subtotal resection. Consequently, maximal safe resection remains the primary treatment strategy, and gross-total resection is associated with improved long-term tumor control.2,3,7 Elevated Ki-67 labeling indices have also been associated with an increased recurrence risk.3,9,10

Adjuvant conventional radiotherapy has been proposed for residual or recurrent disease, although the available evidence for its efficacy remains limited.3 In particular, considering the potential risk of radiation-related neurotoxicity, adjuvant radiotherapy has been proposed as a reserved option rather than a routine recommendation in most patients.7 In a systematic review of 73 reported cases, Gembruch et al. reported that postoperative adjuvant fractionated radiotherapy, most commonly with doses of approximately 54 Gy, was associated with a lower recurrence rate compared with cases without adjuvant irradiation.2 These documented radiation treatment cases are summarized in Table 1. Conventional fractionated radiotherapy in this dose range corresponds to a single-fraction equivalent dose of approximately 15–17 Gy when estimated using a linear-quadratic model, providing a useful radiobiological reference for stereotactic radiosurgery.2 Nevertheless, contrary to the report of Gembruch et al., more recent reviews have emphasized that the role of routine adjuvant irradiation remains uncertain.

Since cLNC shares histological and biological features with central neurocytoma, the treatment strategies documented for central neurocytoma may provide a useful reference for stereotactic radiosurgery for cLNC. Furthermore, radiosurgery in central neurocytoma, with marginal doses typically ranging from 12 to 22 Gy (mean of approximately 16 Gy), has demonstrated favorable local control rates and may facilitate effective control in selected patients (Table 1).4,5 However, data specific to cLNC remain sparse. In this context, stereotactic radiosurgery may represent a useful minimally invasive treatment strategy for selected patients with focal postoperative progression or localized recurrence. In the present case, a marginal dose of 16 Gy was selected based on prior radiosurgical experience with neuronal tumors and a published series. Sustained tumor control beyond 2 years without toxicity reinforces the safety and efficacy of this treatment approach in selected patients.

Molecular and Anatomical Considerations

Emerging data suggest biological heterogeneity within cLNC. TP53 missense mutations have been reported in a subset of cases, and additional alterations involving APC, KDR, or ATRX have been reported in individual tumors, including cases with recurrence or spinal dissemination.11–14 These findings suggest that molecular heterogeneity may underlie adverse biological behavior in some patients, even when the histological appearance is similar. High proliferative indices and incomplete resection are potential adverse prognostic factors.9,10 In the present case, targeted molecular analysis revealed no APC, KDR, or TP53 missense mutations or ATRX alterations. The absence of currently reported candidate adverse molecular events and a moderate Ki-67 labeling index (5%) may support a localized treatment strategy rather than broader adjuvant radiotherapy.11

Anatomical factors were central to the treatment selection. The close relationship between the residual tumor and the PICA loop increased the potential risks of repeat microsurgical manipulation, including vascular injury and cerebellar or brainstem morbidity. Radiosurgery enabled conformal dose delivery without mechanical disruption of critical neurovascular structures.

Collectively, this case illustrates that postoperative tumor progression of cLNC does not necessitate reoperation. Management should be individualized, integrating tumor volume, anatomical constraints, proliferative activity, and the molecular profile.

Lessons

GKRS can provide durable local control for small-volume postoperative progression of cLNC. Treatment selection should integrate three key factors: extent of prior resection and tumor volume, anatomical relationship with critical neurovascular structures, and molecular profile.

Stereotactic radiosurgery is a safe and rational salvage strategy for tumors with focal progression in an anatomically constrained region and no identifiable candidate high-risk molecular abnormalities.
