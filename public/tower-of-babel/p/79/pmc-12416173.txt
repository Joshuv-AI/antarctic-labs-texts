Divergent on-target off-tumor effects by CAR T and CAR NK cells suggest different efficacy and safety of cell therapies
Authors: Katharina Schindler-Wnek, Anika Stahringer, Nadine Heimer, Ulrike Koehl, Stephan Fricke, Dominik Schmiedel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCAR-based cell therapies have shown clinical success in treating various cancers, with CAR T cell therapies entering the clinical route and CAR NK cell therapies being evaluated in early-stage clinical trials. A key challenge is the presence of tumor-associated antigens on healthy cells, risking on-target off-tumor toxicities. Our comparative analysis of CAR T and CAR NK cells targeting the multiple myeloma-associated antigens BCMA, SLAMF7, and CD38 revealed that antigen density on target cells significantly modulates CAR NK cell activation and cytotoxicity. The cytotoxic potential of CAR NK cells was comparable to that of CAR T cells when targeting BCMA and CD38, but notable differences were observed in SLAMF7-directed CAR cells. While CAR sensitivity was similar in both cell types, CAR NK cell activity was balanced by inhibitory receptors like KIRs and NKG2A. This balance allows effective tumor control while potentially reducing on-target off-tumor effects on healthy cells with low antigen expression. Consequently, CAR NK cells offer greater flexibility in target antigen selection, potentially expanding the range of targetable antigens for cancer immunotherapy.KEYWORDS: CAR T cells, CAR NK cells, on-target off-tumor cytotoxicity, immunotherapy

Introduction

In recent years, cell-based immunotherapies, particularly chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) therapies, have revolutionized cancer treatments. CAR T cell therapy has demonstrated remarkable clinical efficacy in various hematological malignancies, including multiple myeloma (MM), B cell leukemias, and lymphomas.1–3 This treatment involves the genetic modification of autologous T cells to express a CAR targeting a specific tumor-associated antigen, thereby enabling selective recognition and elimination of malignant cells.

Despite its success, CAR T cell therapy still faces several limitations. These include severe adverse events, such as cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and neurotoxicity, as well as the potential for antigen loss on tumor cells, which can compromise treatment efficacy.4 One major challenge of CAR therapy development is the identification of suitable tumor-specific antigens.5 The phenomenon of “on-target off-tumor” toxicity occurs when CAR-engineered cells recognize and attack nonmalignant tissues that express the target antigen. The consequent immune-mediated damage to normal tissues can manifest as organ dysfunction or failure, potentially compromising the therapeutic index of CAR-based immunotherapies.6,7 Furthermore, when the target antigen is expressed on immune cells, it can lead to fratricide (killing of bystander immune cells by CAR-positive cells). Considering these toxicities, the selection of target antigens is a critical factor in expanding the applicability of CAR-based therapies. However, identifying such antigens has proven challenging, as most tumoral antigens are shared with normal tissues.

This issue is also reflected in targets used in the treatment of MM, including B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA), signaling lymphocytic activation molecule family member 7 (SLAMF7), and CD38. These antigens are currently targeted by approved monoclonal antibodies, antibody-drug conjugates, and CAR T cell therapies in various stages of clinical development.8–13 While all three antigens are highly expressed in MM cells,14 BCMA is present on healthy plasma cells, whereas SLAMF7 and CD38 exhibit varying degrees of expression on normal immune cells.15 This differential expression pattern presents potential challenges for targeted therapies due to the risk of on-target off-tumor effects, evidenced by studies observing a remarked reduction in lymphocyte counts after treatment.16,17 This lymphodepletion, while generally tolerated in current therapeutic settings, raises concerns about potential severe immunosuppression in broader applications or combination strategies.

To develop safer and widely applicable therapies, researchers have expanded their focus to alternative effector cell types, notably natural killer (NK) cells. NK cells offer several potential advantages over T cells, including a more favorable cytokine profile that may reduce the risk of CRS.18 Furthermore, their non-major histocompatibility complex (MHC) restricted cytotoxicity allows for allogeneic “off-the-shelf” products, circumventing the need for patient-specific manufacturing. Numerous clinical studies investigated the potential of NK cells19,20 and one recent clinical study of CAR-engineered NK cells displayed encouraging safety and efficacy data.21

NK cells possess intrinsic anti-tumor properties, enabling them to lyse target cells without prior sensitization.22 Their activation is regulated by a complex interplay of inhibitory and activating receptors on the cell surface. Activating receptors, such as NKG2D or NKp46, promote NK cell activation, leading to cytokine production and cytotoxic activity against target cells.23 Conversely, inhibitory receptors like NKG2A and certain Killer-cell immunoglobulin-like (KIR) receptors are crucial for maintaining self-tolerance. This balance of activating and inhibitory receptors acts as a sensory system, allowing NK cells to distinguish between target cells and healthy cells.24 By introducing a CAR into NK cells, an artificial activating receptor is added to this equation, potentially altering the delicate equilibrium of activation and tolerance.

In this study, we evaluated the sensitivity and activity of CAR T and CAR NK cells against various target cells using target antigens with different expression patterns (BCMA, SLAMF7, CD38). Furthermore, we investigated how the equilibrium between activating and inhibitory signals modulates and balances the CAR-mediated activity of NK cells and explored how this delicate balance can be strategically fine-tuned to optimize CAR NK cell function, with the ultimate goal of enhancing both the specificity and efficacy of NK cell-based cancer immunotherapies.

We postulated that CAR NK cells, owing to their intrinsic tolerance mechanisms and advantageous characteristics, could mitigate off-tumor effects, making them suitable for therapeutic applications where CAR T cells are contraindicated, thereby potentially expanding the range of targetable antigens in CAR therapy.

Material and methods

Construction of y-retroviral vectors

All retroviral CAR constructs in this study are second-generation designs targeting either BCMA, SLAMF7, or CD38. The anti-BCMA scFv (C12A3.2) is derived from sequence 4 of a patent,25 while the anti-SLAMF7 scFv (huLuc63) is derived from the humanized antibody elotuzumab26 and the anti-CD38 scFv is derived from the human monoclonal antibody daratumumab.10 The CAR constructs follow a standardized 5‘to 3’ structure: CD8a signal peptide, scFv (VH-VL), human IgG1 hinge, CD8a transmembrane domain, and CD28/CD3zeta signaling domains. Identical framework regions facilitated cloning with a single primer set. All constructs include GFP as a reporter, with BCMA and CD38 CARs using a P2A site, while SLAMF7 CAR is directly fused to GFP. These CARs were inserted into the gamma-retroviral pBullet vector, provided by Reno Debets (Erasmus University Medical Center, RRID:Addgene_53065).27

Retrovirus production and transient transfection of HEK293T cells

HEK293T cells were used to generate gamma-retroviral particles (DSMZ Cat# ACC-635, RRID:CVCL_0063). The process started by seeding 37,500 cells in each well of a 12-well plate 48 hours before transfection. On the transfection day, retroviral transfer plasmids containing CAR sequences were co-transfected with viral packaging plasmids pHIT60 and Baboon Envelope (BaEV) using TransIT transfection reagent (Mirus Bio, Madison, WI, USA). The transfection mixture used a 1:1.5 ratio, consisting of 0.154 µg pHIT60 and 0.230 µg BaEV. After a 48-hour incubation period, the supernatant containing viral particles was collected and immediately used for transducing primary T cells and NK cells.

Generation of primary human CAR T and CAR NK cells

T and NK cells were isolated from the peripheral blood of healthy donors using RosetteSep™ Human T Cell and NK Cell Enrichment Cocktails (Stemcell Technologies, Vancouver, Canada). All donors provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committees of the University Clinic Leipzig (ethics vote number 327/22-ek).

Cells were cultured in specific media: T cells in TheraPEAK® T-VIVO® medium (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) with IL-2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Lenexa, KS, USA) and human T Cell TransAct™ (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) and NK cells in NK MACS medium (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) with human serum (Sigma Aldrich, Laramie, WY, USA), IL-2, and IL-15 (both from Thermo Fisher Scientific, Lenexa, KS, USA). After 7 days, cells were transduced with CAR constructs using Vectofusin®-1 (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) and viral supernatant. For NK cell tolerance interference, anti-NKG2A (CD159a, clone Z199, Cat# IM2750, RRID:AB_131495, Beckman Coulter GmbH, Krefeld, Germany) and anti-pan-HLA-ABC antibodies (HLA-ABC Monoclonal Antibody, clone W6/32, Cat# 14–9983–82, RRID:AB_468661, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Lenexa, KS, USA) were added post-transduction. Experiments were conducted 3–10 days post-transduction using flow cytometry. T and NK cells were isolated from the same donor(s) for direct comparison, minimizing variability.

Flow cytometry-based detection of different CAR constructs

CAR expression was assessed on days 3, 6, and 10 post-transduction using biotinylated primary antibodies. For the construct with the Whitlow linker (BCMA), an anti-Whitlow monoclonal antibody (mAb) (Cat# 32523, RRID:AB_3626309, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) was used, while constructs with the G4S linker (SLAMF7, CD38) were detected using an anti-G4S mAb (Cat# 17621, RRID:AB_3626304, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). Cells were incubated with the primary antibody for 30 minutes at 4°C, washed once, and then incubated for an additional 10 minutes at 4°C with APC-conjugated streptavidin (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) as a secondary reagent. After two final washing steps, the cells were analyzed using a MacsQuant10 Analyzer (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany).

Flow cytometry-based analysis of cell counts, cell death and receptor expression on CAR-engineered cells

The MacsQuant Analyzer was used to assess the cell numbers of all CAR-transduced cells on days 3, 6, and 10 post-transduction. Propidium iodide (PI, Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) was added immediately before each measurement to assess cell death. The viability of non-transduced cells in the cell suspension was determined by analyzing PI staining in the GFP-negative population. The mAbs LAG-3 (CD223 Antibody, anti-human, REAfinity™, Cat# 130–127–931, RRID:AB_2904843), PD-1 (CD279 Antibody, anti-human, REAfinity™, Cat# 130–120–389, RRID:AB_2752075) and SLAMF7 (CD319 (CRACC) Antibody, anti-human, REAfinity™, Cat# 130–126–031, RRID:AB_2889550) (all purchased from Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) were used for receptor expression on CAR cells on days 3, 6 and 10 post-transduction. Briefly, cells were incubated with the respective antibodies for 10 minutes at 4°C, washed once, and analyzed using a MacsQuant10 analyzer (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany).

Target-cell independent CAR activation assay

To investigate SLAMF7 CAR activation, a dilution series of recombinant SLAMF7 protein (Biotinylated Human SLAMF7, Avitag,His Tag (SL7-H82E0), Avantor, Germany) was generated. 700,000 SLAMF7 CAR T and CAR NK cells were resuspended in a culture medium (RPMI 1640 without phenol red, 10% FCS). In a 96-well plate, each well received 50 µL of cell suspension (100,000 cells). After SLAMF7 protein staining, CAR cells were co-incubated with anti-CD107a-PE-Vio770 and with/without anti-Biotin MACSiBead Particles (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) for 4 hours. For NK cell inhibition, anti-NKG2A antibody (CD159a, clone Z199, Cat# IM2750, RRID:AB_131495, Beckman Coulter GmbH, Krefeld, Germany) and a-mouse F(ab’)2 antibody (Biotin-SP (long spacer) AffiniPure™ F(ab’)₂ Fragment Goat Anti-Mouse IgG, Cat# 115–606–072, RRID:AB_2338928, Jackson ImmunoResearch Europe LTD) were added. After incubation, wells were washed and analyzed with the MacsQuant10 Analyzer (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany).

Degranulation assay CD107a

CD107a expression on both CAR T and CAR NK cells was assessed 3, 6, and 10 days after transduction. Briefly, 100 µL of each cell suspension was transferred to a 96-well plate and the mAbs anti-human CD107a (LAMP-1, PE-Vio® 770, REAfinity™, Miltenyi, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) and anti-human CD56 (APC-Vio770 REAfinity™, Miltenyi, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) were added. After an incubation period of 4 hours, the cells were washed once and analyzed using a MacsQuant10 analyzer (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). For CAR T cells, CD107a expression was quantified in the CD56-negative, GFP-positive population. Conversely, CAR NK cell CD107a expression was assessed in the CD56-positive, GFP-positive population. This gating strategy allowed for the specific analysis of transduced cells within each population, as indicated by GFP expression while distinguishing between T cells (CD56-negative) and NK cells (CD56-positive).

Flow cytometry-based cytotoxicity assay of CAR T and CAR NK cells

To assess CAR T and CAR NK cell functionality, MM.1S (RRID:CVCL_8792) and XG-1 (RRID:CVCL_4797) cells (kindly provided by Prof. Dr. Maximilian Merz, University Clinic Leipzig) were labeled with CellTrace™ Violet (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Lenexa, KS, USA) to distinguish them from CAR T and CAR NK cells. The labeling process involved incubating MM.1S and XG-1 cells with 0.5 µM CellTrace™ Violet for 20 minutes at 37°C, followed by the addition of five volumes of serum-containing culture medium (RPMI 1640 without phenol red, 10% FCS) to halt the reaction. Labeled tumor cells were then co-cultured with either non-transduced or CAR-engineered T or NK cells at effector-to-target (E:T) ratios of 0.125:1 (MM.1S) or 0.5:1 (XG-1) for 24 hours at 37°C and 5% CO2. Flow cytometry analysis was performed using a MACSQuant10 (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). Immediately before each measurement, PI (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) was added to assess cell death. Tumor cell viability was determined by analyzing PI staining in the CellTrace™ Violet-positive population.

On-target off-tumor cytotoxicity assay

To investigate whether CAR T cells and CAR NK cells (effector cells) recognize healthy, antigen-positive cells, they were co-cultured with non-transduced T cells and NK cells (target cells) from the same donor (autologous co-culture) or different donors (allogeneic co-culture). To differentiate between effector and target cells, the target cells were stained with CellTrace™ Violet (same labeling procedure as above). After labeling, target and effector cells were co-cultured in a 96-well plate at an E:T ratio of 1:1. To assess degranulation, the cells were stained with mAbs anti-human CD107a (LAMP-1, PE-Vio® 770, REAfinity™, Miltenyi, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). After an incubation period of 4 hours, the cells were washed once and analyzed using a MacsQuant10 analyzer (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). To assess target cell death, the co-culture was incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 24 hours. The next day, PI (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) was added immediately before measurement to assess cell death. Target cell viability was determined by analyzing PI staining in the CellTrace™ Violet positive population.

Thymidine incorporation assay

To observe changes in cell proliferation, a thymidine incorporation assay was performed 3 days post-transduction. Briefly, 100,000 cells/100 µL medium were seeded in a 96 well plate and 1 µCi of [3 H]-thymidine (Perkin Elmer, Boston, MA, USA) was added per well. After 16 hours of incubation, cells were harvested onto glass fiber filter mats using a cell harvester (Perkin Elmer, Boston, MA, USA). [3 H]-thymidine incorporation was analyzed using a beta counter (Perkin Elmer, Boston, MA, USA). The measured radioactivity is expressed as counts per minute (cpm).

CelltraceTM violet cell proliferation assay

NK cell proliferation was assessed using flow cytometry-based CellTraceTM Violet cell proliferation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Lenexa, KS, USA, Catalog Nr. C34571, USA). NK cells were incubated with 1 µM CellTrace Violet in PBS for 20 minutes at 37°C. The reaction was halted by adding five-fold volume of serum-containing culture medium and incubating for 5 minutes. The stained cells were resuspended in NK MACS medium (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) containing 5% human serum (Sigma Aldrich, Laramie, WY, USA), 500 U/mL IL-2, and 140 U/mL IL-15 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Lenexa, KS, USA) and plated into culture plates. After incubating the cells for 4 days at 37°C and 5% CO2, the fluorescence signal was analyzed by flow cytometry.

Ki-67-based proliferation assay

CAR NK cells were fixed and permeabilized 3 days post transduction by incubation in 80% ice-cold ethanol at −20°C overnight. Following fixation, cells were washed twice with staining buffer to remove residual ethanol. Intracellular staining was performed using BD Pharmingen™ PerCP-Cy™5.5 Mouse anti-Ki-67 antibody (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA, Cat. No. 561284), with cells incubated for 30 minutes at room temperature in the dark to ensure optimal antibody binding. After a final wash to remove unbound antibody, samples were analyzed by flow cytometry (MacsQuant10 analyzer, Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) to assess Ki-67 expression.

Flow cytometry-based intracellular staining of SLAMF7

To investigate SLAMF7 expression more in detail, an intracellular staining was performed. To this end, BCMA CAR NK cells and SLAMF7 CAR NK cells were stained 3 days post transduction using True-NuclearTM Transcription Factor Buffer set (BioLegend®, San Diego, CA, USA). Briefly, cells were washed and resuspended in True-NuclearTM 1x Fix Concentrate and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 45–60 minutes. After several washing steps with True-Nuclear™ 1X Perm Buffer, the cells were incubated with the monoclonal antibody SLAMF7 (CD319 (CRACC) Antibody, anti-human, REAfinity™, Cat# 130–126–031, RRID:AB_2889550, Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) for 30 minutes. Following several washing steps, the cells were resuspended in cell staining buffer and analyzed using a MacsQuant10 analyzer (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany).

Detection of human IFNγ using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Human interferon-gamma (IFNγ) concentrations in cell culture supernatants were quantified using a DuoSet® ELISA Kit (human IFNγ, R&D Systems GmbH, Wiesbaden, Germany). The assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, a 96-well microplate was coated with capture antibody and incubated overnight. Following aspiration, the plate was washed with wash buffer (1× PBS + 0.05% Tween20) and blocked with reagent diluent (1% BSA in 1× PBS) for 1 hour at room temperature. Subsequently, 100 µL of diluted samples were added in technical duplicate. T cell supernatants were diluted 1:30, while NK cell supernatants were diluted 1:3 in reagent diluent. The plate was then incubated for 2 hours at room temperature. After washing, detection antibody was added and incubated for 2 hours at room temperature. Streptavidin-HRP was then applied and incubated for 20 minutes in the dark. Substrate solution was added for 20 minutes, followed by the addition of stop solution. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm and 560 nm using a spectrophotometer (Tecan Group AG, Männedorf, Zürich, Switzerland). Optical density was calculated by subtracting the absorbance at 560 nm from the absorbance at 450 nm to correct for background signal prior to statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 6.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, RRID:SCR_002798, San Diego, CA, USA). If not stated otherwise, results are expressed as mean values or mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test was employed. When analyzing three or more groups with one independent variable, one-way ANOVA was performed. Two-way ANOVA was applied when analyzing three or more groups with two independent variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Differential expression patterns of BCMA, SLAMF7 and CD38 allow for the comparative analysis of tolerance and CAR-induced cytotoxicity by NK and T cells

To evaluate the comparative efficacy of CAR T cells and CAR NK cells, we aimed to engineer T and NK cells to express various CAR constructs targeting antigens with differential expression levels. Specifically, we selected antigens suited to model absent, low, and high expression in healthy tissues (Figure 1A). To this end, we generated CARs targeting BCMA, SLAMF7, and CD38, all of which were highly expressed on malignant MM cell lines MM.1S and XG-1 (Figure 1B). However, these antigens exhibited distinct expression patterns on healthy immune cells: BCMA showed negligible expression on T and NK cells, SLAMF7 demonstrated low expression, and CD38 was highly expressed on both T and NK cells (Figure 1B and Supplementary Figure S1). This experimental design allowed for the assessment of CAR-mediated effector functions with varying levels of target antigen expression on both effector and target cells and thus to study induction of CAR-dependent cytotoxicity and tolerance toward healthy cells.

CAR T and CAR NK cells demonstrate distinct phenotypic profiles when exposed to self-antigens

Transduction efficiencies ranging from 40% to 90% were achieved for all CAR constructs at days 3, 6, and 10 post transduction (Figure 2A). Anti-BCMA (BCMA) CAR expression remained stable throughout the observation period in both T and NK cells. However, anti-SLAMF7 (SLAMF7) CAR expression in T cells increased significantly from day 3 onwards, suggesting substantial depletion of CAR-negative cells in this population (Figure 2A). This phenomenon was not observed in SLAMF7 CAR NK cells. Notably, exceptionally high transduction efficiencies of up to 90% from day 3 onwards were observed in all anti-CD38 (CD38) CAR-engineered cells, indicating a marked reduction of CAR-negative cells in both populations (Figure 2A).

All CAR-engineered cells were expanded using cytokine stimulation alone, initiating with 250,000 cells post transduction. Both BCMA CAR T and CAR NK cell populations exhibited significant proliferation over time, as evidenced by increasing log-fold expansion values (Figure 2B). Notably, CAR T cells demonstrated a stronger overall expansion compared to CAR NK cells. However, SLAMF7 CAR T cells exhibited a transient reduction in cell numbers at days 3 and 6 post transduction, likely due to fratricide, before recovering to demonstrate similar growth rates by day 10 post transduction. Notably, no significant reduction was observed in SLAMF7 CAR NK cells, suggesting a low or absent fratricide in this cell suspension.

To investigate, how CAR T cell counts recover between day 6 and day 10, a thymidine incorporation assay was executed and revealed elevated cpm values in SLAMF7 CAR T cells compared to BCMA CAR T cells, indicating enhanced cellular proliferation. In contrast, no significant differences in proliferation patterns were observed between SLAMF7 CAR NK cells and BCMA CAR NK cells (Supplement Figure S2A-C). Furthermore, no significant variations were detected in the proliferation profile between the CAR-negative and CAR-positive populations of CAR NK cells (Supplement Figure S2B).

The proliferation pattern of CD38 CAR cells was uniformly lower compared to BCMA CAR constructs in both T and NK cell populations, suggesting substantial fratricide in both cell types (Figure 2B), likely explained by the high CD38 expression on both NK and T cells.

Surface expression of SLAMF7 and CD38 was quantitatively assessed via flow cytometry at 3, 6, and 10 days post transduction. Both SLAMF7 CAR T and SLAMF7 CAR NK cells exhibited a significant reduction in SLAMF7 surface expression compared to the BCMA CAR group, indicating selective killing of SLAMF7-positive cells (Figure 2C). Subsequent intracellular flow cytometric analysis revealed the presence of SLAMF7 within the cytoplasm, suggesting that while SLAMF7 protein is still synthesized, its trafficking to the cell surface is impaired (Supplement Figure S2D). An analogous phenomenon was observed in CD38 CAR cells, where surface CD38 expression was similarly diminished (Figure 2D). These findings indicate that CAR expression may interfere with the surface localization of its cognate antigen, potentially through a mechanism of antigen sequestration or altered protein trafficking.

Observing similar behavior of BCMA and CD38 targeting CAR T cells and CAR NK cells sparked our interest in understanding the differences observed in SLAMF7 CAR T and CAR NK cells.

Differential responses of CAR T cells and CAR NK cells upon exposure to self-antigens

To investigate the CAR activity of T cells and NK cells in greater detail, we assessed cell death, degranulation, and the expression of exhaustion markers. The SLAMF7 and CD38 CAR T cells exhibited significantly enhanced cytotoxicity against CAR-negative populations (Figure 3A), while there was no enhanced killing in the CAR-positive population (Figure 3B), likely explained by the reduced SLAMF7 and CD38 surface levels of CAR-positive cells mentioned above. Furthermore, we found increased degranulation in SLAMF7 and CD38 CAR T cells compared to BCMA CAR T cells as evidenced by a marked upregulation of CD107a surface expression (Figure 3C). Additionally, we noted a significant increase in the expression levels of lymphocyte-activation gene 3 (LAG-3) and programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) from day 3 onwards compared to BCMA CAR T cells, indicating a pronounced cellular activation of SLAMF7 and CD38 CAR-positive T cells (Figure 3D,E). This highly activated phenotype was further confirmed with increased IFN-γ levels in the supernatant of SLAMF7 CAR T cells compared to BCMA CAR T cells (Supplemental Figure S3A). Human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class I (HLA-A, B, C) expression of BCMA CAR T cells and SLAMF7 CAR T cells remained high at all time points (Supplemental Figure S3B).

In contrast, among the CAR-engineered NK cells, only those expressing the CD38 CAR demonstrated enhanced cytotoxicity against CAR-negative cells in the cell suspension (Figure 3A), while the CAR-positive population was spared (Figure 3B). CD38 CAR NK cells exhibited significant upregulation of CD107a surface expression, reflecting increased degranulation and activation (Figure 3C). No significant differences were observed in the expression levels of LAG-3 and PD-1 across the various CAR constructs, suggesting that the activation of NK cells did not influence the regulation of these markers (Figure 3D,E). Furthermore, there were no differences in the IFN-γ levels in the supernatant of BCMA and SLAMF7 CAR NK cells, indicating no enhanced activation of SLAMF7 CAR NK cells (Supplemental Figure S3).

CAR T and CAR NK cells show potent anti-myeloma function in vitro

To evaluate the effector function of various CAR T and CAR NK cells, we utilized the MM cell lines MM.1S and XG-1 as target cells. All CAR-engineered T and NK cells demonstrated significantly enhanced cytotoxicity against MM.1S cells compared to their non-transduced counterparts (Figure 4A). Non-transduced NK cells exhibited superior killing capacity relative to non-transduced T cells, which can be attributed to the innate cytotoxic properties of NK cells (Figure 4A). BCMA and CD38 CAR T and CAR NK cells displayed comparable cytotoxic efficacy, while SLAMF7 CAR T cells demonstrated marginally higher potency compared to their CAR NK counterparts, restricted to the MM.1S cell line (Figure 4A).

Statistical significance was determined using ordinary one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test (*p ≤ 0.05).

In the case of XG-1 target cells, all CAR constructs, regardless of the effector cell type, exhibited significantly enhanced cytotoxicity compared to non-transduced controls (Figure 4B). However, no significant differences in killing efficiency were observed between CAR T and CAR NK cells across the various CAR constructs. Interestingly, non-transduced NK cells showed minimal cytotoxicity against XG-1 cells, and no significant difference between non-transduced T and NK cells was observed (Figure 4B), suggesting inherent resistance mechanisms that protect XG-1 cells from natural cytotoxicity.

SLAMF7 CAR NK cells cause less on-target off-tumor cytotoxicity against healthy immune cells

To evaluate the specificity and potential off-target effects of CAR-engineered cells, cytotoxicity and degranulation assays were conducted using healthy cells in both autologous and allogeneic settings. As anticipated, BCMA-engineered cells did not exhibit enhanced cytotoxicity or degranulation against healthy cells in either setting (Figure 4C, Supplement Figure S4A).

Co-culture experiments demonstrated that SLAMF7 CAR T cells mediated robust cytolytic activity and markedly increased degranulation against SLAMF7-positive T cells and NK cells under both autologous and allogeneic conditions (Figure 4D and Supplement Figure S4A). Interestingly, this fratricide effect was not observed with SLAMF7 CAR NK cells, even when allogeneic immune cells from HLA-unmatched donors were used, suggesting a potential selective sparing of healthy cells by these effectors.

CD38, which is highly expressed on various immune cell populations, was also investigated. Co-culture of CD38 CAR T and CAR NK cells with healthy immune cells resulted in significant cytotoxicity (Figure 4E), indicating that antigen density may play a crucial role in determining CAR activity, particularly for NK cells.

Additionally, we assessed SLAMF7 expression on unmodified T and NK cells after co-culture. SLAMF7 expression on NK cells remained unchanged following co-culture with SLAMF7 CAR NK cells. In contrast, the fraction of SLAMF7-positive T cells was significantly reduced after co-culture with SLAMF7 CAR T cells, demonstrating a selective loss of SLAMF7 positive T cells (Supplement Figure S4B).

These findings indicate that T and NK cells may exhibit distinct on-target off-tumor toxicity profiles, suggesting that the choice of effector cell type could significantly impact the safety of engineered cellular therapies.

CAR NK-mediated cytotoxicity is modulated by inhibitory receptors and target antigen density

In a series of experiments, we investigated the intrinsic tolerance mechanisms of NK cells, which are regulated by a complex interplay between activating and inhibitory receptors (Figure 5A). We sought to elucidate the behavior of SLAMF7 CAR NK cells when perturbing this tolerance mechanism through selective blockade of the inhibitory receptor NKG2A, HLA-ABC molecules on NK cells, or both interactions simultaneously (Figure 5A).

Modulation of CAR NK-mediated cytotoxicity by inhibitory receptors and target antigen density. (A) schematic representation of NK cell interaction outcomes with target cells. Left box: tolerance mechanism: equilibrium between stimulatory and inhibitory receptor signaling results in target cell preservation. Middlebox: missing-self mechanism (NKG2A blockade): NK cell activation and subsequent target cell lysis due to inhibition of NKG2A signaling. Right box: missing-self mechanism (HLA-E blockade): NK cell activation and target cell lysis resulting from interference with inhibitory KIR receptor signaling. (B) SLAMF7 CAR NK cells were co-cultured immediately post-transduction with or without monoclonal antibodies targeting CD159a alone, HLA-ABC alone, or in combination. Flow cytometric analysis was performed at 3, 6, and 10 days post-transduction to assess cell counts, CAR expression, and cell death of the CAR-negative and CAR-positive population via PI staining. Data of cell counts and CAR expression are presented as mean ± SEM log2 fold change to mitigate donor variation; n = 5. Data of cell death are presented as mean ± SD; n = 5. Statistical significance was determined using two-way ANOVA followed by Sidak’s multiple comparison test (*p ≤ 0.05). (C) evaluation of CD107a surface expression on SLAMF7 CAR T or SLAMF7 CAR NK cells at 3 days post-transduction in response to varying SLAMF7 protein concentrations, with or without bead stimulation. Additionally, one experimental condition included co-incubation with CD159a monoclonal antibody and anti-mouse FAB antibody to induce cross-linking of the SLAMF7 protein staining. Blocking NKG2A/HLA-E-interactions with an anti-NKG2A monoclonal antibody alone resulted in a non-significant trend toward reduced SLAMF7 CAR NK cell counts and a slight increase in CAR expression (Figure 5B). Notably, a trend toward an increase in cell death within the CAR-negative population was observed at days 3 and 6 post transduction, suggesting enhanced fratricide of NK cells upon NKG2A blockade.

Interference with KIR/HLA-ABC interactions yielded more pronounced effects, including a significant reduction in cell count and a significant increase in CAR expression from day 6 onwards (Figure 5B). Furthermore, there was a trend of increased cell death compared to the non-treated group (Figure 5B). These data indicate that modulation of the KIR pathway exerts a stronger influence on CAR NK-mediated cytotoxicity.

Simultaneous blockade of both NKG2A receptors and HLA-ABC molecules demonstrated the most substantial impact on CAR NK-mediated cytotoxicity. This was evidenced by a significant reduction in cell count from day 6 onwards, increased CAR expression, and enhanced cell death in the CAR-negative population at day 10 post transduction (Figure 5B). These findings collectively suggest that CAR-mediated activity is modulated by inhibitory receptors on NK cells and that both KIR/HLA-ABC and NKG2A/HLA-E interactions contribute to the induction of tolerance and balance CAR-dependent activation of NK cells.

Furthermore, no significant increase in cell death was detected within the GFP-positive CAR NK cell population, even under conditions of dual pathway blockade. This finding suggests that cytotoxicity is selectively directed toward the SLAMF7-positive (CAR-negative) NK cell subset, rather than affecting the broader CAR NK cell population (Figure 5B).

Additionally, we conducted an analogous experimental series utilizing BCMA CAR-modified NK cells. The observed effects were less pronounced and primarily attributed to the innate cytotoxic mechanisms of NK cells rather than antigen-specific interactions (Supplemental Figure S5).

To further characterize CAR activity, we assessed CD107a expression levels in response to varying stimulation concentrations. Both SLAMF7 CAR T cells and SLAMF7 CAR NK cells exhibited significant increases in CD107a surface expression up to a concentration of 4.8 µg/mL, with no further increase observed at 3.2 µg/mL, indicating comparable CAR sensitivity between the two cell types (Figure 5C).

Additionally, we performed cross-linking of the SLAMF7 protein by incubating the cell suspension with an anti-NKG2A monoclonal antibody and anti-mouse F(ab’)2 antibody, thereby activating the inhibitory receptor signaling. This cross-linking had no discernible effect on SLAMF7 CAR T cell activity but significantly impaired SLAMF7 CAR NK cell activity (Figure 5C). These data underscore that activation of inhibitory receptors can fully ablate CAR-mediated activation.

In conclusion, our experiments demonstrated that CAR NK activity is finely balanced and can be modulated through manipulation of inhibitory receptor signaling pathways, giving the opportunity to tune CAR NK cell activation to maximize on-target on-tumor and minimize on-target off-tumor effects.

Discussion

The rapid evolution of cell therapies, with enhanced gene-editing technologies, advanced recombinant protein design, and additional cell sources, as well as in-depth patient characterization, promises the development of disease- or even patient-individual treatment strategies for cancer.

CAR NK cells are currently under clinical investigation, and the first published results are promising and showcase that allogeneic CAR NK cells may show similar efficacy compared to autologous CAR T cells when targeting CD19.21,28 However, these studies follow in the footsteps of CD19 CAR T cell trials, and cell-type specific differences may become more relevant in cancer types which, so far, showed little clinical benefit by CAR T cell therapies for example due to antigen loss, CAR T cell-associated toxicities or due to tumor heterogeneity.4 One major hurdle is the identification of suitable tumor-specific antigens as tumor-specific antigens are rare. Tumor-associated antigens, shared between healthy tissues and cancer cells, pose a critical risk for treatment-restricting on-target, off-tumor toxicities.29 These toxicities remain a significant challenge in the field of cell-based therapies. To address these challenges, a variety of innovative strategies are currently being explored to precisely regulate CAR activity and thereby minimize adverse effects. These approaches include optimizing CAR construct design, for example, through the use of logic-gated CARs or by modulating antigen-binding affinity. Additionally, control mechanisms for CAR cells are being implemented, such as the incorporation of suicide switches or the localized, site-specific administration of the cell product.30

In this study, we sought to elucidate the efficacy and applicability of CAR T and CAR NK cells across diverse therapeutic contexts, comparing them when encountering antigens with different expression patterns. Using peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) as a model to assess on-target, off-tumor cytotoxicity against nonmalignant cells, we focused on three highly expressed, therapeutically exploited antigens in MM, some of which are also expressed on healthy T and NK cells: BCMA, which is confined to malignant cells; SLAMF7, demonstrating with low expression on healthy immune cells; and CD38, exhibiting elevated expression on both T and NK cells. Using this setup, we aimed to gain a better understanding of the differences between CAR T and CAR NK cells in terms of (i) execution of fratricide during cell therapy manufacturing, (ii) sensitivity and activation toward an antigen expressed on healthy cells, (iii) expression of checkpoint receptors and potential mechanisms to tune cellular activities.

In our comparative analysis of BCMA CAR-engineered T cells and NK cells, we observed that CAR-modified T cells and NK cells exhibited comparable phenotypes and proliferation kinetics. Both cell types demonstrated efficient cytotoxicity against MM cells in vitro. As expected, non-transduced NK cells displayed superior killing capacity against MM.1S cells compared to non-transduced T cells, attributed to the intrinsic cytotoxicity of NK cells, which allows them to effectively target tumor cells without prior sensitization.31 However, this effect was not observed with XG-1 cells, which exhibit increased resistance to NK cell-mediated recognition, as previously documented in various tumor cell lines.32,33

For CD38 CAR T and CAR NK cells, we unsurprisingly observed substantial fratricide during the manufacturing process, characterized by low cell yields, an increasing CAR-positive population, and elevated cellular degranulation. Our experimental data further demonstrate the capacity of CAR T and CAR NK cells for high-specific lysis of MM cell lines in vitro as well as autologous cytotoxicity, as evidenced by the effective elimination of CD38-positive immune cell populations.

The observed data corroborate findings from multiple preclinical and clinical investigations employing CAR T cells targeting antigens expressed on both malignant and nonmalignant cells in solid and hematological malignancies, resulting in on-target, off-tumor toxicities of varying severity.7,34–36 A particularly striking illustration of this phenomenon was demonstrated in the study conducted by Thistlethwaite et al., which evaluated anti-CEACAM5 CAR T cells in patients with advanced-stage CEACAM5-positive solid tumors.37 This trial resulted in unanticipated, clinically significant adverse events, including tachypnea and respiratory distress, attributed to the expression of CEACAM5 in nonmalignant lung tissue samples. Consequently, these severe on-target, off-tumor effects necessitated the premature termination of the trial.37

The activation-induced checkpoint receptors LAG-3 and PD-1, recognized as significant markers of T cell exhaustion, were exclusively induced on SLAMF7 and CD38 CAR T, but not CAR NK cells. Their decrease throughout the time course of the experiment may either be explained by the decreasing rates of fratricide in the culture, which primarily occurs shortly after CAR expression, or by the outcompetition of exhausted CAR T cells by a more proliferative population. Interestingly, despite the cytotoxicity inflicted in this co-culture, CAR NK cells did not upregulate these receptors, arguing, that additional factors imposed by the microenvironment may be critical to drive the expression of these receptors. Anyhow, whereas exhaustion is well-studied in CAR T cells and linked to a transcriptional profile, a recent study by Pouxvielh et al. claims that NK cell dysfunction is uncoupled from immune checkpoint protein expression in their tumor models, indicating that immune checkpoint act more like additional inhibitory receptors on NK cells rather than indicating an alternative functional state.38

Our data demonstrate that SLAMF7 CAR T cells display a pronounced exhausted phenotype, characterized by elevated expression of checkpoint molecules, increased IFNγ production, and heightened proliferative activity, indicative of terminal differentiation. These observations are in line with the findings of Cadinanos-Garai et al.,39 showing that this state of exhaustion is characterized by diminished regulatory capacity and persistent activation, which can impair the ability of CAR T cells to discriminate effectively between malignant and healthy cells. As a result, there is an increased risk of off-tumor toxicity and bystander killing, a phenomenon that has been highlighted in recent studies.40,41 This risk is particularly pronounced when checkpoint molecules are upregulated and memory potential is lost, as terminally differentiated CAR T cells are less capable of restraining their cytotoxic activity. In contrast, CAR NK cells do not acquire this exhausted phenotype and maintain a more balanced functional profile, thereby avoiding excessive or indiscriminate cytotoxic responses. This fundamental difference may contribute to the improved safety profile observed with CAR NK cell therapies.

Immune cell fratricide is a well-established phenomenon understood to play a role in maintaining homeostasis.42 However, in therapeutic settings, particularly in the field of CAR-based immunotherapies, fratricide can lead to killing of bystander immune cells during the manufacturing process as well as killing of nonmalignant cells that are equipped with the same target antigen.43 Indeed, Gogishvili et al. observed a significant loss of SLAMF7-positive normal lymphocytes after co-culture with SLAMF7-directed CAR T cells.44 This is also in line with our findings, where equipping T cells with SLAMF7 CAR resulted on the one hand in superior anti-myeloma efficacy and the other hand in enhanced killing of SLAMF7-positive lymphocyte population in vitro. Furthermore, we observed fratricide within the cell suspension of SLAMF7 CAR T cells, evidenced by reduced cell counts, an increasing CAR-positive population, upregulated exhaustion and degranulation markers, and SLAMF7 loss on the cell surface, which is also in line with the findings of Gogishvili and colleagues.44 Notably, we could show that SLAMF7 CAR T cells exhibited enhanced proliferation profiles, enabling expansion to therapeutic doses. This phenomenon may be attributed to the negative impact of SLAMF7 on T cell proliferation, as SLAMF7 CAR T cells express low levels of SLAMF7, promoting increased proliferation compared to BCMA CAR T cells with surface SLAMF7 expression.45 Alternatively, also the increased cellular activation of CAR T cells due to the fratricide in the culture may further boost expansion. Consequently, various research approaches have been developed to mitigate fratricide and overcome these limitations in CAR-based cellular therapies, e.g., by genetic ablation of the self-antigen,46–48 however, this approach will not limit off-tumor toxicities in the treated patient.

Interestingly, SLAMF7 CAR NK cells show also potent anti-myeloma efficacy but the fratricide in the generation process was low to absent. Additionally, our study is the first to demonstrate that SLAMF7 CAR NK cells do not elicit cytotoxicity against healthy SLAMF7-positive populations. By performing co-cultures with HLA-matched (autologous) immune cells as well as HLA-unmatched immune cells (allogeneic), we showed that this tolerance toward healthy PBMC is induced independent of HLA status.

This tolerance can be attributed to the presence of inhibitory receptors on NK cells that recognize classical and non-classical MHC class I molecules in healthy cells.49 Both NKG2A and inhibitory KIR receptors are critical to maintain this tolerance and in particular combined blocking of both pathways induces NK cell activation, a phenomenon called the “missing-self-recognition.” This was shown as treatment of SLAMF7 CAR NK cells with either anti-HLA-ABC antibody or anti-NKG2A antibody resulted in a more aggressive phenotype of CAR NK cells as shown by a diminished cell count, enhanced CAR-positive population as well as higher cell death of CAR-negative cells in cell suspensions likely due to fratricide of CAR NK cells, which was absent in non-treated SLAMF7 CAR NK cells. Furthermore, we observed no increase in overall cell death within the CAR-positive population under either condition, indicating that the observed cytotoxicity is specific to the SLAMF7-positive subsets as a result of fratricide, rather than a consequence of generalized apoptosis due to hyperactivation.

Accordingly, our data demonstrate that SLAMF7 CAR recognition in NK cells is finely balanced by inhibitory receptors, such as NKG2A and KIRs, allowing for context-dependent regulation of cytotoxicity. Interestingly, by using a cell-free, plate-bound SLAMF7 degranulation assay, we could show that CAR T and CAR NK cells display comparable sensitivity to CAR-mediated antigen stimulation. However, triggering of inhibitory signal could effectively balance CAR-mediated activation. Importantly, these observations indicate that NK cells are not inherently less responsive than T cells to CAR-mediated activation. Instead, their activity is actively suppressed by inhibitory mechanisms when they encounter healthy, CAR-antigen-positive cells – an effect that is dependent on antigen density. These findings underscore the crucial role of physiological inhibitory pathways in safeguarding against off-tumor effects mediated by CAR NK cells and in preserving self-tolerance.

Unlike classical checkpoint receptors, both NKG2A and KIRs are not activation-induced but expressed in resting conditions to mediate self-tolerance under homeostatic conditions.23 Several strategies have been developed to overcome this inhibitory signaling and enhance NK cell anti-tumor activity. These approaches include blocking KIRs using monoclonal antibodies,50,51 utilizing NKG2C-positive,52 NKG2A-negative NK cells, and developing CARs with concurrent knockout of inhibitory receptors such as NKG2A.53,54 While these approaches have shown promise in augmenting NK cell efficacy against tumor cells, our findings suggest that caution is warranted when targeting self-antigens. Manipulation of these inhibitory pathways may inadvertently enhance on-target, off-tumor effects, which is undesirable if the target antigen is expressed on healthy tissues or immune cells, and off-tumor toxicities are of imminent concern. This potential for collateral damage underscores the need for careful consideration of the potential consequences of disrupting inhibitory signaling in NK cell-based immunotherapies.

Our research elucidates the differential behavior of CAR-engineered T and NK cells, particularly in the context of fratricide and cytotoxicity against targets with low antigen expression. We demonstrate that (i) CAR T and CAR NK cells exhibit distinct fratricide patterns when targeting antigens with low expression on healthy cells, (ii) CAR sensitivity is comparable between T and NK cells, but CAR NK cell activity is modulated by inhibitory receptors, such as KIR and NKG2A, (iii) abrogation of the missing-self recognition mechanism in CAR NK cells results in CAR-induced cytotoxicity patterns comparable to that observed in CAR T cells. Additionally, we identified an antigen-expression threshold required for CAR activation. Altogether, these observations suggest that CAR NK cells may have a broader therapeutic window compared to CAR T cells, potentially allowing for their application in settings where the target antigen is expressed at low levels in healthy tissues. Consequently, as antigen dependency is greater for NK cell activation, a downmodulation of the antigen may result in easier immune escape from CAR-NK cells and compromise therapeutic efficacy. However, our results indicate that CAR NK cell therapy can be modulated to meet specific therapeutic requirements. For instance, disruption of inhibitory signaling pathways can confer more aggressive properties to CAR NK cells. However, this approach should be carefully considered in contexts where safety concerns are predominant, highlighting the need for a balanced approach in optimizing CAR NK cell therapies for different clinical scenarios.

In conclusion, these results provide insights into the complex interplay between CAR-mediated and missing-self-induced cytotoxicity in CAR-engineered NK cells, offering a foundation for the development of more refined and tailored cellular immunotherapies.
