Inhibition of miR-4284 could reduce apoptosis and neuroinflammation by targeting APBA1/JAK1/STAT3 signaling in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Jiyun Choi, Jinsu Hwang, Dohee Kim, Eunjae Jang, Geupil Jang, Hyong-Ho Cho, Byeong C. Kim, Han-Seong Jeong, Sujeong Jang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundmicroRNA-4284 is associated with various diseases, but its role in Alzheimer’s disease remains unclear. This study explores the therapeutic potential of miR-4284 inhibition by targeting the APBA1 and the JAK/STAT3 pathways in AD models.ResultsmiR-4284 expression was analyzed in Aβ-treated SH-SY5Y cells and 5xFAD mice. Luciferase assays identified APBA1 as a direct target of miR-4284. Apoptosis, inflammation, and neuronal survival were assessed using qPCR, western blotting analysis, FACS, and immunohistochemistry. The Morris water maze test evaluated cognitive function, while western blotting analysis examined the JAK/STAT3 pathway. miR-4284 was upregulated in AD models. Its inhibition increased APBA1 expression, reduced Aβ accumulation, suppressed apoptosis and inflammation, and enhanced neuronal survival and cognitive function, correlating with JAK/STAT3 activation.ConclusionsmiR-4284 inhibition confers neuroprotection by modulating APBA1 and JAK/STAT3 signaling, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target for AD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-025-01498-4.

Background

Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the most common type of dementia, accounts for 60–80% of cases and is a severe neurodegenerative disorder marked by cognitive and behavioral impairments, including difficulty with planning or problem-solving, memory loss, and social withdrawal [1–3]. AD arises from various pathogenic processes, including the amyloid cascade, tau toxicity, synapse loss, and synaptic dysfunction [4, 5]. Risk factors include aging, genetics, family history, vascular diseases, head trauma, environmental influences, and infections [6, 7]. Amyloid-β (Aβ) fibril formation, a hallmark of AD, results from altered cleavage of amyloid precursor protein (APP) by β- and γ-secretases in the amyloidogenic pathway [8, 9]. These Aβ fibrils induce tau hyperphosphorylation and form intracellular tau neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) [10–12]. This cascade activates microglial inflammation, contributing to neurotoxicity [13]. As populations age and life expectancy increases, AD incidence is projected to rise globally, significantly straining healthcare systems, particularly in developing countries [14]. Thus, halting AD progression through Aβ clearance or inhibition is crucial for effective therapeutic development [15]. Recently, microRNAs (miRNAs) have emerged as promising tools for elucidating AD pathogenesis and as potential therapeutic agents [16–18].

miRNAs are small non-coding RNAs that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally by binding to the 3ʹ-untranslated region (3ʹ-UTR) of target mRNAs [19–21]. Nuclear miRNA genes, transcribed by RNA polymerase II, are processed by the RNase Drosha into precursor (pre)-miRNA, which is then exported to the cytoplasm via exportin-5 [22, 23]. In the cytoplasm, Dicer cleaves pre-miRNAs to generate mature double-stranded miRNAs. [24, 25] The RNA-induced silencing complex then incorporates one miRNA strand with Argonaute (Ago) proteins to bind target sequences within the mRNA 3ʹ-UTR [26]. miRNAs regulate various essential biological functions, including cell proliferation, growth, apoptosis, development, and tissue differentiation, by modulating multiple target genes [27, 28]. Previous studies have also suggested links between miRNAs and the JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway in AD models (Table S1).

miR-4284 is upregulated in various cancers, including non-small cell lung cancer, gastric cancer, and ovarian carcinoma [29–32]. It targets CXCL5 and is associated with pediatric ulcerative colitis and ankylosing spondylitis [33, 34]. Additionally, miR-4284 inhibitors exhibit anti-apoptotic effects by reducing Bax and increasing Bcl-2 levels in arteriosclerosis obliterans [35]. However, its role in AD remains unexplored.

APBA1, a neuronal adaptor protein, binds to APP and regulates its processing, reducing Aβ production [36, 37]. It also supports synaptic function by interacting with CASK to facilitate vesicle exocytosis and neurotransmitter release [38, 39]. Dysregulated APBA1 expression promotes Aβ production and exacerbates synaptic and neuronal dysfunction, making APBA1 preservation a potential AD therapy [40]. It has been reported that miR-502-3p can modulate APBA1 expression [41]; However, it has not been studied which miRNAs specifically target APBA1 as a direct target gene.

In this study, the effect of miR-4284 was found to be upregulated in the hippocampus of 4-month-old 5xFAD mice compared to wild-type mice. Additionally, miR-4284 targets APBA1, which inhibits Aβ accumulation in in vivo AD model. Therefore, we hypothesized that miR-4284 inhibition could elevate APBA1 levels and exert a therapeutic effect in AD models. This study aims to examine the anti-apoptotic, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties of miR-4284 inhibitors in an AD model, specifically focusing on JAK/STAT3 signaling.

Methods

Cell culture and reagents

The human neuroblastoma cell line SH-SY5Y (RRID: CVCL_0019l, ATCC® CRL-2266) and HEK293T cells (ATCC® CRL-11268) were purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Cat. No. SH30243.01, Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Cat. No. SV3020702, Cytiva), 0.2% amphotericin B (Cat. No. 15290–018, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Cat. No. LS202-02, WELGENE, Gyeongsan-si, Republic of Korea). Cultures were maintained at 37 ℃ with 5% CO₂ in a humidified atmosphere and used between passages 5 to 8. We confirmed that the cell lines used in our study were commercially purchased from ATCC, which provides authenticated cell lines for research use.

Aβ1-42 peptide (Cat. No. AS-20276, ANASPEC, Fremont, CA, USA) was dissolved in 1% NH4OH and diluted to a 1 mM stock solution. For in vitro AD modeling, Aβ1-42 was applied at 500 nM as a final concentration. The miR-4284 inhibitor (miR-4284-I; UGGGGUGUGUGGCCC, Bioneer, Daejeon, Korea) was dissolved in ultra-pure distilled water (Invitrogen Co., Carlsbad, CA, USA). After a 1-h incubation with Aβ1-42, the inhibitor was added at concentrations of 0.1, 0.5, and 1 pM. Treated cells were cultured for 24 h before subsequent analyses. miR-4284 mimic (miR-4284-M; UACCCCACUACACUCGGG, Bioneer), negative control inhibitor (NC-I; Bioneer), and negative control mimic (NC-M; Bioneer) were also dissolved in ultra-pure distilled water to investigate target gene expression using qPCR and luciferase assays.

Biological analysis and antibody array

To assess differences in protein expression between Aβ-treated and miR-4284-I-treated SH-SY5Y cells following Aβ exposure, a cell signaling phospho-array analysis was performed using the Full Moon BioSystems antibody array. SH-SY5Y cells were collected after treatment with Aβ and miR-4284-I, and the antibody array analysis was conducted by E-BIOGEN Inc. (Seoul, Republic of Korea). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway and Gene Ontology (GO) analyses were performed using ExDEGA and DAVID (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) [42, 43]. Potential miR-4284 target genes were predicted using TargetScan and miRDB.

Luciferase assay

To confirm miR-4284 binding to APBA1 3ʹ-UTR, the pmirGLO dual-luciferase miRNA target expression vector (Cat. No. E1330, Promega, Madison, WI, USA) was used. The 3ʹ-UTR containing the predicted miR-4284 target site was cloned into the vector (Fig. S1). A mutant version of this site was generated using the EZchange™ site-directed mutagenesis kit (Cat. No. EZ004S, Enzynomics, Daejeon, Republic of Korea). HEK293T cells were co-transfected with either wild-type or mutant pmirGLO-APBA1 plasmids and miR-4284-M or NC using Lipofectamine 3000 (Cat. No. 2412049, Invitrogen). After 48 h, luciferase activity was measured using the dual-luciferase reporter assay system (Cat. No. E2940, Promega) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Firefly and Renilla luminescence were sequentially measured, and firefly-to-Renilla ratios were calculated for normalization.

RNA isolation and quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA from treated SH-SY5Y cells or mice brains was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Cat. No. 9109, Takara Bio Co., Shimogyo-ku, Japan), isopropanol (Cat No. 67–63-0, EMD Millipore Corporation, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA), and ethanol (Cat No. 64–17-5, Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA) [44]. Subsequently, RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a PCR instrument (Takara Bio Co.). qPCR was performed using SYBR Green Premix Ex Taq (Takara Bio Co.) on a LightCycler 480 II system (Roche, Switzerland) at an annealing temperature of 60 ℃. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)

Apoptosis in SH-SY5Y cells was assessed using FACS after Aβ or miR-4284-I treatment. Cells were washed with cold phosphate-buffered saline, resuspended in 1X binding buffer, and then incubated with FITC Annexin V and PI (Cat. No. 556547, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, NJ, USA) for 15 min in the dark. Flow cytometry was conducted using a FACSCanto II (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed using the BD FACSDiva software (BD Biosciences).

Animals

Four-month-old 5xFAD transgenic mice (B6SJL; Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA) were used as an AD model. These mice overexpress APP with three familial AD mutations (Swedish: K670N, M671L; London: V717I; Florida: I716V) and presenilin-1 mutations (L286V and M146L). Hemizygous 5xFAD mice were bred with B6SJL F1/J mice for maintenance. Mice were divided into four groups; C57BL/6 wild-type (WT; n = 14), 5xFAD transgenic mice (5xFAD; n = 12), 5xFAD injected with NC-I (5xFAD + NC; n = 12), and 5xFAD injected with miR-4284-I (5xFAD + miR-4284-I; n = 12). For immunostaining and western blotting analysis, sample sizes (4–6 mice per group) were determined with reference to prior studies using similar experimental designs and were consistent with established protocols in the field [45–47]. All procedures adhered to the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines (CNU IACUC-H-2024–39) at Chonnam National University Medical School [48]. Animals were housed under standard conditions (22 ± 2 ℃, 50 ± 10% humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle) with up to five per cage, and they had ad libitum access to food and water. Both male and female mice were included in the study to avoid sex bias. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups, and data collection and analysis were conducted by investigators blinded to group allocation.

miRNA inhibitor transfection in 5xFAD mouse brain

Mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg; JW Pharmaceuticals, Seoul, Republic of Korea) and secured in a stereotaxic apparatus [48]. A Hamilton syringe (Cat. No. 788130, Hamilton Company, Reno, NV, USA) was used to inject 50 μM of either miR-4284-I or NC-I into the hippocampus at a rate of 0.5 μL/min (coordinates: A/P -2 mm, M/L ± 1.5 mm, D/V -1.5 mm). After 3 days of recovery, behavioral tests were conducted, followed by sacrifice for histological and biochemical analyses. Brain tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Cat. No. SM-P01-100, GeneAll, Seoul, Republic of Korea) or stored at − 80 °C.

Morris water maze (MWM) test

The MWM test was conducted to evaluate spatial learning and memory. A circular pool divided into four quadrants contained a hidden platform in one quadrant. Mice were trained to locate the platform from the opposite quadrant, with escape latency recorded (max: 60 s per trial). If a mouse reached the platform and stayed for 15 s, the next mouse was trained. If unsuccessful within 60 s, escape latency was set at 60 s. Training included four trials per day over 4 days. A probe test without the platform assessed memory retention. Data on platform crossings, swimming speed, and time spent per quadrant were analyzed using EthoVision XT 17 software (SCITECH KOREA INC., Seoul, Republic of Korea).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Mouse brains were perfused with 4% PFA, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, and embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (Cat No. 3801480, Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, Germany). The tissues were sectioned into 10-μm slices using a DE/CM1860 cryostat (Leica Biosystems).

Diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining

For DAB staining, tissue sections were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Cat No. X100, Sigma Aldrich) and blocked using 10% normal goat serum (NGS; Cat No. 005–000-121, Jackson ImmunoResearch Labs, West Grove, PA, USA). Sections were then incubated with primary antibodies against Aβ (Cat No. sc28365, 1:1000), Bax (Cat No. sc493, 1:1000), Bcl-2 (Cat No. sc492, 1:1000), and NeuN (Cat No. 24307, 1:1000). Biotinylated secondary antibodies and Vector Elite ABC kit (Cat No. PK-6100, 1:100, Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA) were used for signal enhancement. Signals were visualized using 0.03% DAB (Cat No. ENZ-KIT159-0150, Enzo life sciences, Farmingdale, NY, USA). Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin (Cat No. S3309, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and analyzed under a ZEISS Axio Vert.A1 light microscope (Carl Zeiss, Gottingen, Germany).

Immunofluorescence staining

Primary antibodies against NFH (Cat. No. 2836, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:200) and MAP2 (Cat. No. 8707, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:200) were used for immunofluorescence staining. Secondary antibodies included Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse (Invitrogen; 1:200) and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (Invitrogen; 1:200). Nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (1 μg/mL, Life Technologies Corporation, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for 30 min. Fluorescent signals were visualized using a ZEISS Axio Vert.A1 fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.5 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. In most experiments, all groups satisfied the assumption of normality (p > 0.05). In some experiments, one group showed a slight deviation from normality, but given the small sample size (n = 4–6) and the robustness of parametric tests to minor violations of normality, parametric analyses were applied. Group differences were analyzed using a one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s post-hoc tests or Student’s t-tests for pairwise comparisons. The significance levels were denoted as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001 compared to the control or WT group; #p < 0.05, ##p < 0.01, and ###p < 0.001 compared to the Aβ-treated group or 5xFAD + NC group.

Results

miR-4284 is upregulated in in vitro and in vivo AD models

To evaluate miR-4284 expression in AD models, we performed microRNA qPCR on Aβ-treated SH-SY5Y cells and the hippocampus of 4-month-old 5xFAD mice. miR-4284 expression was significantly upregulated in Aβ-treated SH-SY5Y cells compared to untreated cells (Fig. 1A). Similarly, its expression markedly increased in the hippocampus of 5xFAD mice compared to that in WT mice (Fig. 1B). Although direct evidence of altered miR-4284 expression in human AD brain tissue or cerebrospinal fluid has not yet been reported, data from the miRNATissueAtlas and previous studies indicate that miR-4284 is expressed in the brain, stem cells, nerves, and blood, suggesting potential relevance to AD pathophysiology (Fig. S2A) [50]. We next examined whether miR-4284 expression could be modulated. In SH-SY5Y cells, miR-4284-I treatment significantly suppressed miR-4284 expression across all concentrations (1 pM to 25 nM) compared to that in the NC group, (Fig. 1C), while miR-4284-M treatment markedly increased miR-4284 expression at 100 pM and 25 nM compared to that in the NC (Fig. S2). To explore the biological pathways affected by miR-4284 inhibition, we conducted an antibody array analysis on Aβ-treated SH-SY-5Y cells with or without miR-4284-I (1 pM). KEGG pathway analysis suggested a potential association between the JAK-STAT signaling pathway and miR-4284-I treatment effects (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, miR-4284 inhibition was associated with reduced apoptosis in the in vitro AD model (Fig. 1E–G). Similarly, miRWalk analysis associated miR-4284 with the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, intrinsic apoptotic signaling, and neuron projection development (Fig. S3). These findings suggest that miR-4284 inhibition may modulate JAK-STAT signaling and suppress apoptosis in AD models.

APBA1 is a direct target of miR-4284

To identify potential target genes of miR-4284, we utilized TargetScan and miRDB prediction tools. Among the predicted targets, APBA1 was selected because of its role in APP stabilization and Aβ inhibition (Fig. 2A). qPCR analysis in SH-SY5Y cells showed that miR-4284 inhibition significantly increased APBA1 mRNA levels, suggesting miR-4284 promotes mRNA degradation (Fig. 2B). A dual-luciferase reporter assay further confirmed this interaction. WT and mutant 3ʹ-UTR sequences of APBA1 were cloned into the pmirGLO vector (Fig. 2C). Co-transfection of miR-4284-M with pmirGLO-APBA1 in HEK293T cells significantly decreased luciferase activity compared to cells transfected with NC-M and pmirGLO-APBA1 (Fig. 2D), confirming direct binding of miR-4284 to APBA1 3ʹ-UTR. Furthermore, western blotting analysis of 5xFAD mouse brains revealed significantly increased APBA1 protein expression after miR-4284-I injection (Fig. 2E–F). Inhibition of miR-4284 rescues cognitive impairment in 5xFAD mice

To evaluate cognitive function, 4-month-old WT and 5xFAD mice injected with NC or miR-4284-I underwent the MWM test for 5 days (Fig. 5A). Discussion

AD, a progressive neurodegenerative disorder and the leading cause of dementia, is characterized by cognitive decline, behavioral abnormalities, and accumulation of Aβ plaques and tau NFTs. Despite extensive clinical trials focusing on Aβ clearance, effective treatment options remain limited. This study explored the therapeutic potential of miR-4284 inhibition in AD. We identified APBA1, a key modulator of Aβ accumulation and synaptic integrity, as a direct target of miR-4284, confirmed through qPCR, luciferase assays, and western blotting analysis. miR-4284 inhibition reduced neuronal apoptosis and protected against neurodegeneration in both in vitro and in vivo AD models. Additionally, miR-4284-I treatment significantly lowered Aβ deposition in the brains of 5xFAD mice and improved cognitive performance during the MWM test. Further analysis revealed that miR-4284-I activates the JAK/STAT3 pathway, enhancing neuroprotection by attenuating apoptosis and inflammation (Fig. 9). miRNAs play a crucial role in understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying AD pathogenesis. For instance, miR-22-3p attenuates apoptosis and reduces Aβ accumulation via the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway [51], while miR-331-3p and miR-9-5p promote autophagy decreasing amyloid plaque deposition in AD models [45]. miR-146a and miR-155 modulate neuroinflammation during AD progression, while miR-132 enhances neurogenesis and mitigates memory deficits [16, 52–55]. Furthermore, miR-532-5p overexpression and miR-29a inhibition improve cognitive function in 5xFAD mice [56, 57]. Beyond these examples, recent studies have emphasized several mechanistic themes of miRNA-mediated regulation in AD (Table S2). For instance, miR-128 represses GSK3β and APPBP2/mTOR, thereby lowering tau phosphorylation and amyloid levels [58], while miR-137 suppresses CACNA1C to prevent tau hyperphosphorylation [59]. In terms of immune regulation, miR-155 deletion in AD mice enhances anti-inflammatory mediators such as c-Maf, SHIP1, and SOCS1, leading to reduced Aβ burden [60], whereas upregulation of miR-146a-5p activates the p38 MAPK/ROS pathway and increases Aβ accumulation [61]. Similarly, synaptic and metabolic regulation has also been reported, as miR-455-3p overexpression decreases APP levels and promotes mitochondrial and synaptic gene expression to improve cognition [62], while loss of miR-124 de-represses RyR3, resulting in Ca2⁺ overload and memory impairment [63]. Compared with these previously reported mechanisms, our findings highlight a novel miR-4284–APBA1–JAK/STAT3 signaling axis that exerts neuroprotection predominantly through anti-inflammatory regulation. Specifically, miR-4284 inhibition reduced Aβ plaque accumulation, suppressed apoptosis and neuroinflammation, and improved cognitive function in 4-month-old 5xFAD mice, supporting its potential as a therapeutic strategy in AD.

Previous studies show that miR-4284 plays diverse roles across various disease models, particularly in cancer and cardiovascular conditions. In renal cancer cells, miR-4284 overexpression suppresses proliferation, induces apoptosis, and inhibits tumorigenicity by targeting GAD1 [64]. In ovarian carcinoma, it contributes to chemoresistance by reducing sensitivity to paclitaxel by targeting DMC1 [30]. In non-small cell lung cancer, miR-4284 acts as an oncogene, promoting proliferation, migration, and invasion, making it a potential prognostic biomarker and a therapeutic target [31, 32]. A similar tumor-promoting effect occurs in gastric cancer, where miR-4284 enhances tumor cell growth, migration, and invasion by directly targeting TET1 [29]. Beyond its role in cancer, miR-4284 is associated with osteoclastogenesis. In mesenchymal stem cells, its downregulation increased CXCL5 levels, significantly inhibiting osteoclast differentiation [34]. Additionally, miR-4284 has been implicated in vascular diseases, as its downregulation in human arterial smooth muscle cells inhibited apoptosis in arteriosclerosis obliterans, suggesting a protective role in vascular pathologies [35]. Unlike previous studies, our study is the first to investigate the role of miR-4284 inhibition in an AD model. We demonstrated that miR-4284 inhibition reduced apoptosis and inflammation, decreased Aβ formation, and provided neuroprotection by targeting APBA1 in both in vitro and in vivo AD models. These findings suggest a novel function of miR-4284 in neurodegeneration, extending its known biological significance beyond cancer and cardiovascular diseases.

APBA1 is a key adaptor protein involved in protein trafficking, APP processing, and synaptic organization [39]. It regulates APP metabolism by inhibiting the transcriptional activity of APP-derived fragments, stabilizing APP levels, and influencing its intracellular transport and processing [65]. Additionally, APBA1 forms a heterotrimeric complex with mLin-2 and mLin-7, essential for neuronal function and protein localization [39]. APBA1 modulates its processing and reduces Aβ production by binding to APP, suggesting its neuroprotective role [39]. Furthermore, APBA1 interacts with γ-secretase to inhibit Aβ generation and is associated with serotonin receptor expression, suggesting a link between cognition and mood regulation [66]. While APBA1 has been proposed as a potential therapeutic target for neurodegenerative diseases, its functional role in in vitro and in vivo AD models remains unclear. This study demonstrated that APBA1 upregulation significantly reduces Aβ plaque formation, apoptosis, and neuroinflammation, while restoring neuronal integrity and cognitive function in 5xFAD mice. These findings highlight the critical role of APBA1 in AD pathology and its potential as a therapeutic target.

Although both 0.1 pM and 1 pM miR-4284 inhibitor produced beneficial effects, the responses were pathway-dependent. Specifically, apoptosis-related markers were more effectively suppressed at 1 pM, whereas neuronal differentiation markers responded more strongly to 0.1 pM. Such non-linear or pathway-specific responses are not uncommon for miRNA inhibitors, reflecting optimal concentration windows rather than simple dose-dependent increases [67–70]. They may be explained by partial saturation of miR-4284 inhibition or concentration-dependent off-target effects, which have been reported in other studies of miRNA modulators [71]. This suggests that there may be an optimal concentration window where neuronal protective markers are maximally enhanced, while higher concentrations favor effects on cell survival pathways.

Several studies have reported the benefits of p-STAT3 activation in AD models [72]. For instance, STAT3 activation downregulates miR-204 expression while enhancing TRPML1 levels, thereby reducing reactive oxygen species production and promoting mitochondrial autophagy, ultimately inhibiting AD progression [47]. Similarly, nanodiamonds enhanced hippocampal p-STAT3 and the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 while suppressing NF-κB and caspase-3, improving cognition and reducing histopathological alterations in AD rat models [73]. Additionally, research shows that STAT3 activation ameliorates cognitive deficits by regulating NMDAR expression, while Tau accumulation impairs synaptic plasticity by inactivating STAT3 through STAT1 acetylation [74]. In the present study, we observed that p-STAT3 was predominantly localized in the nuclei of hippocampal and cortical neurons under physiological conditions, consistent with its canonical role as a transcription factor. In AD mice, nuclear p-STAT3 signals were markedly reduced, indicating impaired STAT3 signaling activity. Interestingly, treatment with the miR-4284-I partially restored p-STAT3 expression, but with a distribution pattern distinct from that in WT mice. Specifically, p-STAT3 colocalized with APBA1 in periplaque regions and accumulated within cytoplasmic or neuritic structures rather than in the nucleus. Although no direct JAK/STAT3 pathway components have been identified as targets of miR-4284, our findings suggest that APBA1 may provide an indirect mechanistic link. Given APBA1’s established role in APP processing and synaptic organization, the observed p-STAT3–APBA1 colocalization in plaque-associated neurites raises the possibility that STAT3 reactivation after miR-4284 inhibition is mediated through APBA1-dependent regulation of APP-related signaling. This indirect mechanism may underlie the observed reduction in apoptosis and neuroinflammation and the improvement of cognitive function following miR-4284 inhibition.

The present study had several limitations other than those aforementioned. A key limitation of this study is the unclear mechanism by which miR-4284 inhibition activates the JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway. Although KEGG analysis and miRwalk data suggest an association between JAK/STAT signaling and miR-4284 inhibition in Aβ-treated cells, specific miR-4284 target genes regulating this pathway remain unidentified, directly. In addition, although our study utilized mixed-sex 5xFAD mice to minimize sex bias, we did not perform sex-stratified analyses. Given that sex differences are increasingly recognized as an important factor in AD pathogenesis and treatment responses, future investigations should specifically examine whether miR-4284 inhibition exerts sex-dependent effects. Another limitation is that the systemic safety profile of miR-4284 inhibition remains unexplored. While hippocampal delivery demonstrated local neuroprotective effects, potential off-target and toxic effects in peripheral organs were not evaluated. Finally, our conclusions are based on pharmacological inhibition of miR-4284, and genetic approaches such as CRISPR/Cas9-mediated miR-4284 knockout or AAV-driven APBA1 overexpression would provide more definitive evidence for the causal role of the miR-4284–APBA1–JAK/STAT3 axis.

In conclusion, we identified miR-4284 as a novel AD regulator and demonstrated the therapeutic potential of its inhibition. miR-4284 directly targets APBA1, a protein that interacts with APP to reduce Aβ accumulation. Additionally, miR-4284 inhibition attenuated apoptosis and neuroinflammation while promoting neuronal survival and cognitive function in both in vitro and in vivo AD models, primarily through JAK/STAT3 activation. These findings highlight miR-4284 inhibition as a promising therapeutic strategy for AD and provide a foundation for developing miRNA-based treatments for neurodegenerative diseases.
