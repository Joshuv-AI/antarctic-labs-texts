Insulin resistance surrogates predict major adverse cardiovascular events in patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction and chronic kidney disease: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Feng Wang, Wendi Huang, Zhuoquan Wang, Di Zhang, Hongyang Qiao, Nianshao Chen, Xiaoqiu Ni, Jinguo Cheng, Weicheng Ni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) frequently coexist and portend a poor prognosis. Insulin resistance (IR) is implicated in cardiorenal pathophysiology, but practical surrogate IR indices for predicting risk in this high-risk and understudied population are lacking. This study evaluated and compared the prognostic value of four IR indices for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in patients with HFpEF and CKD, and assessed their incremental value over the established Meta-Analysis Global Group in Chronic Heart Failure (MAGGIC) risk score.MethodsThis retrospective analysis included 2412 patients admitted with HFpEF and CKD from 2012 to 2023. Baseline metabolic parameters computed four IR indices: triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, TyG-body mass index (TyG-BMI), atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), and Metabolic Score for Insulin Resistance (METS-IR). The composite primary endpoint was MACE, defined as all-cause mortality or first heart failure hospitalization. Associations were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier analysis, multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression, and restricted cubic splines. Predictive capability was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), continuous net reclassification improvement (cNRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI).ResultsOver a median follow-up of 1.58 years, 1122 (46.52%) MACE events occurred. Unadjusted analyses showed that all four IR indices were significantly associated with MACE risk. The TyG index demonstrated the strongest association (Q4 vs. Q1: adjusted hazard ratio [HR] = 2.60, 95% confidence interval [CI] 2.05–3.29, P < 0.001) and the best discrimination (AUC = 0.676). After adjustment, AIP (adjusted HR = 2.07) and METS-IR (adjusted HR = 1.42) remained significant predictors, whereas TyG-BMI did not (P = 0.09). TyG significantly outperformed the other indices in AUC, cNRI, and IDI (all P < 0.001). All IR indices added significant incremental prognostic value to the MAGGIC score (AUC 0.560), with TyG providing the largest improvement (AUC = 0.679, IDI = 0.052, cNRI = 0.189; all P < 0.001). These associations were consistent across prespecified subgroups.ConclusionIn patients with coexisting HFpEF and CKD, surrogate IR indices independently predicted MACE risk. The TyG index exhibited the strongest association and best predictive performance, offering significant incremental utility beyond the MAGGIC score. Integrating the TyG index into clinical practice could enhance risk stratification and management, directly improving patient outcomes by identifying those at highest risk for MACE.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02764-7.

Introduction

Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) frequently coexists with chronic kidney disease (CKD), affecting approximately half of patients with CKD stages 3–5 [1]. Studies report an extremely high 5-year major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) rate among patients with HFpEF and CKD, with all-cause mortality exceeding 50% and heart failure (HF) re-hospitalization rates exceeding 20% [2, 3]. Compared to patients with either condition alone, these individuals experience substantially higher MACE rates, including 1-year mortality exceeding 30% and HF re-hospitalization rates approaching 50% [2, 3]. Renal dysfunction exacerbates myocardial fibrosis and endothelial inflammation; concurrently, cardiac impairment accelerates renal hemodynamic stress. This interaction creates a vicious cycle that drives MACE [4, 5]. However, pivotal HFpEF trials have systematically excluded patients with severe CKD, leading to inadequate representation of this vulnerable subpopulation in evidence-based guidelines [6, 7]. Consequently, early identification of modifiable risk factors in patients with HFpEF and CKD is of heightened clinical significance. Conventional risk stratification methods have proven insufficient for addressing the unique pathophysiology of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome [4]. This complex clinical entity—defined by the triad of metabolic dysregulation, renal impairment, and cardiovascular (CV) pathology—drives progressive multisystem damage [4].

Emerging evidence identifies insulin resistance (IR) as a pivotal mediator of cardiorenal crosstalk in HFpEF [4, 5]. Beyond impairing myocardial energetics and promoting diastolic dysfunction [8–10], IR induces glomerular hyperfiltration, podocyte injury, and renal fibrosis via hyperinsulinemia-driven oxidative stress and chronic inflammation [11, 12]. The efficacy of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) in HFpEF underscores the therapeutic relevance of metabolic pathways. In addition to natriuresis, these agents improve insulin sensitivity and reduce ectopic fat deposition—mechanisms potentially underlying their cardiorenal benefits [5, 13]. These advances position IR not only as a therapeutic target but also as a predictor of residual risk in patients with HFpEF and CKD.

Although the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp remains the gold standard for IR assessment, its clinical utility is limited by invasiveness and cost, precluding routine use [14]. This constraint has generated interest in surrogate IR indices derived from routine metabolic parameters. The triglyceride-glucose index (TyG), TyG-body mass index (TyG-BMI), atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), and metabolic score for IR (METS-IR) demonstrate strong correlations with clamp-measured IR and prognostic value in diabetes and CV diseases [15–18]. CKD may exacerbate IR severity through uremic toxin accumulation, chronic inflammation, and altered adipokine signaling, potentially modifying the predictive utility of IR indices in this high-risk subgroup [4, 19, 20]. Despite established links between IR and adverse outcomes in CKM syndrome, evidence directly comparing IR indices for MACE prediction in patients with HFpEF and CKD remains scarce. Prior studies validate surrogate IR indices for risk stratification, though performance varies by population. In coronary artery disease (CAD), METS-IR and TyG-BMI demonstrated superior performance for predicting multivessel disease severity, with METS-IR showing the highest discriminative capacity [21]. Conversely, in type 2 diabetes, the triglyceride-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (TG/HDL-C) was the optimal CAD predictor, while TyG exhibited a U-shaped risk association [22].

This study aims to evaluate and compare the prognostic value of four IR indices (TyG, TyG-BMI, AIP, METS-IR) for MACE in patients with HFpEF and CKD, a population that has been understudied. This study further systematically assess their incremental predictive value when incorporated into the validated Meta-Analysis Global Group in Chronic Heart Failure (MAGGIC) risk score [23].

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University. It focused on patients diagnosed with both HFpEF and CKD, who were admitted to the hospital from January 2012 to December 2023. The exclusion criteria included: (1) missing essential variables for IR index calculation; (2) age < 18 or > 90 years; and (3) incomplete follow-up data.

The Institutional Review Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University granted ethical approval for this study. All personally identifiable information was removed, and data were fully anonymized prior to analysis to ensure patient confidentiality. The retrospective design exempted the requirement for participant consent.

Baseline data

Comprehensive baseline medical histories, demographic information, laboratory parameters, echocardiographic measurements, and medication regimens—were retrospectively collected from the institutional medical record system using standardized protocols.

All clinical measurements were obtained during the index hospitalization following standardized protocols. Blood pressure was measured using calibrated automated sphygmomanometers. Weight and height were recorded using a calibrated digital scale (RGZ-120, Wuxi Weigher Factory, China; precision ± 0.1 kg) and a wall-mounted stadiometer (Seca 213, Hamburg, Germany; precision ± 0.1 cm), respectively. Fasting peripheral venous blood samples taken within 24 h of admission, after an overnight fast (≥ 8 h). Fasting blood glucose (FBG), TG, HDL-C, and B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) were analyzed using standardized enzymatic methods on a Cobas 8000 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). All assays adhered to institutional quality control protocols.

Comprehensive transthoracic echocardiography was performed on all enrolled patients. Studies were conducted by certified cardiac sonographers using commercially available ultrasound systems. Echocardiographic assessments were performed following standardized protocols in accordance with the current guidelines [24]. All examinations included standardized views along with Doppler assessments. Echocardiograms were interpreted offline by two independent readers blinded to clinical data, with experience more than five years, and the reports were ultimately determined by consensus. While formal inter-observer variability analysis was not routinely performed due to the retrospective design of this large cohort research, all readers underwent periodic quality assurance audits to maintain consistency, and the institution utilizes standardized reporting templates aligned with guidelines to minimize measurement variability.

Definitions

The diagnosis of HFpEF was made following established guidelines [25], requiring the presence of both an left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≥ 50% and concurrent signs and/or symptoms of heart failure, along with evidence of LV diastolic dysfunction or elevated filling pressures. This diagnosis was supported by objective cardiac structural and functional abnormalities, such as elevated BNP levels.

IR indices, derived from fasting laboratory data, included the TyG index, calculated as ln[(TG, mg/dL) × (FBG, mg/dL)/2], the TyG-BMI, derived by multiplying the TyG index by BMI, the AIP, expressed as log(TG/HDL-C), and METS-IR, computed using the formula (ln[2×FBG (mg/dL) + TG (mg/dL)] × BMI)/(ln[HDL-C (mg/dL)]) [15–18]. CKD was defined according to the current guidelines ([26]– [27]).

The MAGGIC risk score was calculated for each patient using the 13 variables as per the published model [23]. Data are presented as median (IQR) for the MAGGIC risk score (a unitless continuous variable). Specific calculation criteria as in Supplementary Table 1.

Comorbidities and risk factors were identified based on standardized criteria derived from medical record reviews. Current smoking was defined as self-reported active smoking within the 6 months prior to hospitalization. Hyperlipidemia was classified as either a clinical diagnosis, under lipid-lowering therapy, or fasting serum low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) ≥ 3.36 mmol/L or total cholesterol (TC) ≥ 5.17 mmol/L. Diabetes mellitus was defined by a clinical diagnosis, under the glucose-lowering medications, FBG ≥ 126 mg/dL, or glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%. Atrial fibrillation (AF) was confirmed through a documented history or evidence from a standard 12-lead electrocardiogram during hospitalization. CAD was defined by a history of myocardial infarction, prior coronary revascularization, or angiographically confirmed stenosis ≥ 50%, as determined from prior cardiac catheterization reports or specialist diagnoses in the electronic health record system.

Study endpoint and follow up

The primary endpoint was adjudicated as MACE, encompassing all-cause death and first HF hospitalization. The secondary outcomes were defined as all-cause death and first HF hospitalization. From integrated hospital records and routine telephone assessments conducted as part of standard clinical care, the follow-up data were obtained. The observation period began at index hospitalization and continued until death confirmation. For participants without MACE, censoring occurred at the last documented clinical encounter—defined as either in-person consultation or telephone assessment. First hospitalization due to HF was adjudicated based on review of medical records, requiring typical signs and symptoms and objective evidence of HF treatment intensification.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate the normality of the data, the Shapiro-Wilk test was conducted along with visual inspection of P-P plots and histograms. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR), while categorical variables were shown as frequency counts (percentages). For comparative analysis, Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed continuous data, the Mann-Whitney U test for skewed continuous data, and Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables.

Event-free survival was visualized using Kaplan-Meier (K-M) curve, with between-group comparisons evaluated via log-rank testing. To assess the correlations between IR indexes and MACE, his study performed multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression after verifying proportionality assumptions through Schoenfeld residual analysis. A Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) greater than 10 is generally considered indicative of high multicollinearity. The covariates incorporated into the multivariable model were predetermined based on clinical relevance, including age, gender, diabetes, CAD, AF, eGFR, ACEI/ARB/ARNI, beta-blocker, loop diuretics, BNP, LVEF and MAGGIC score. Missing data (less than 10% of covariates) were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations [28]. Potential nonlinear relationships were explored with restricted cubic splines (RCS) with four knots.

Subgroup analyses were conducted across predefined clinically relevant groups: age (≤ 75 vs. >75 years), sex, BMI (< 25 vs. ≥25 kg/m²), LVEF (< 65% vs. ≥65%), and comorbidity status (CAD, hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus). Interaction effects were tested by including multiplicative interaction terms in adjusted Cox proportional hazards models.

The IR indices were assessed both individually and in combination with the validated MAGGIC risk score to determine their incremental prognostic value, using three complementary metrics: (1) receiver operating characteristic (ROC), (2) continuous net reclassification improvement (cNRI), and (3) integrated discrimination improvement (IDI).

All statistical tests employed two-tailed hypotheses, with p < 0.05 defining statistical significance. Analyses were executed using SPSS Statistics 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) for primary data management and R 4.4.3.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The initial cohort comprised 2,721 patients diagnosed with HFpEF and CKD between 2012 and 2023. After excluding 309 patients due to incomplete IR indices data, age < 18 or > 90 years, or lost follow-up, 2,412 patients were enrolled (Fig. 1). During a median follow-up of 1.58 years (IQR 0.55–3.69), 1,122 patients (46.5%) experienced MACE. The MACE group exhibited significantly higher prevalences of diabetes (57.6% vs. 52.9%), CAD (62.0% vs. 55.0%), and AF (38.7% vs. 30.0%), alongside reduced use of guideline-directed therapies including ACEI/ARB/ARNI (40.2% vs. 54.8%) and beta-blockers (57.8% vs. 63.2%). Biomarker analysis revealed elevated BNP (median 813 vs. 667 pg/mL), TG (1.87 ± 1.46 vs. 1.52 ± 0.93 mmol/L), and FBG (8.66 ± 4.86 vs. 6.20 ± 3.24 mmol/L) in the MACE group, with concomitant reductions in LVEF (62.3 ± 9.3% vs. 64.4 ± 7.4%), eGFR (median 41.3 vs. 49.2 mL/min/1.73 m²), and HDL-C levels (0.93 ± 0.27 vs. 1.07 ± 0.29 mmol/L). The VIF of covariates is shown in Supplementary Table 2, indicating that there is no high degree of collinearity. In multivariable Cox regression, patients in the highest TyG quartile (Q4) had a 2.6-fold increased risk, with each unit increment conferring 47% excess risk. For AIP, Q4 patients exhibited 107% higher risk (adjusted HR 2.07, 95% CI 1.66–2.58) versus Q1, while METS-IR Q4 was associated with 42% increased risk (adjusted HR 1.42, 95% CI 1.15–1.75). TyG-BMI showed weaker associations, with only 0.3% risk elevation per unit increase (adjusted HR 1.003, 95% CI 1.001–1.004) and no significant quartile-based risk gradient after adjustment (P = 0.09, Table 2).

Comparative analysis of the IR indices for predicting MACE

Discriminatory performance, assessed by the AUC, revealed significant differences among the IR indices for predicting MACE (Table 3). The TyG index demonstrated the highest discriminative ability (AUC = 0.676), followed by the AIP (AUC = 0.633), METS-IR (AUC = 0.614), and TyG-BMI (AUC = 0.577). Pairwise comparisons confirmed TyG’s significant superiority: its AUC was significantly higher than TyG-BMI (difference = 0.099, P < 0.001), AIP (difference = 0.043, P < 0.001), and METS-IR (difference = 0.062, P < 0.001). TyG-BMI exhibited the lowest discriminative power, evidenced by its significantly lower AUC compared to both AIP (difference = −0.056, P < 0.001) and METS-IR (difference = −0.037, P < 0.001). No significant AUC difference was observed between AIP and METS-IR (difference = 0.019, P = 0.069).

Sensitivity analysis

First, recognizing that conditions like active malignancy, acute infection, hepatic failure, systemic inflammatory diseases, and specific medications (insulin, corticosteroids, SGLT2i, statins) may influence IR markers, this study excluded 1,430 patients with these comorbidities or treatments. In this refined cohort (n = 982), the TyG index continued to demonstrate the most substantial incremental prognostic value when added to the MAGGIC score, increasing the AUC from 0.600 (95% CI: 0.562–0.637) to 0.687 (IDI = 0.055, cNRI = 0.248; all P < 0.001). While the performance hierarchy among IR indices remained consistent with primary findings, the AIP (AUC = 0.654) showed strong discrimination in this subgroup (Supplementary Table 5). The relatively stronger discriminative performance of AIP in this medication-free subgroup may be due to the removal of the confounding effects of lipid-lowering therapies, which potently lower triglyceride levels and could attenuate the natural variability and predictive power of AIP in the overall cohort.

Second, to mitigate potential reverse causality from early events, this study excluded 638 patients who experienced MACE within the first two years (remaining n = 1,774). This analysis confirmed the persistent prognostic significance of all IR indices beyond the initial high-risk period (Supplementary Table 6). Notably, the TyG index maintained robust predictive capacity (MAGGIC + TyG AUC = 0.691, IDI = 0.085, cNRI = 0.240; all P < 0.001), and the AIP emerged with the highest discriminative performance in this model (MAGGIC + AIP AUC = 0.705).

Discussion

This study established that the TyG index demonstrated superior prognostic performance relative to TyG-BMI, AIP, and METS-IR, exhibiting both the highest adjusted hazard ratio (Q4 vs. Q1: HR 2.60) and discrimination capacity (AUC 0.676). Crucially, the TyG index provided significant incremental prognostic value when added to the MAGGIC risk score, enhancing risk stratification in this understudied population with high risk. In contrast, TyG-BMI lost independent predictive significance following multivariable adjustment, while AIP and METS-IR showed modest associations.

IR is prevalent among population with HFpEF and contributes to the disease progression [12]. The gold standard for IR assessment is not feasible due to procedural complexity and cost [14]. This constraint has driven the development of surrogate IR markers [15–18], among which the TyG index has undergone substantial validation. The TyG index correlates significantly with 1-year mortality in patients with CAD and CKD [29] and independently predicts prognosis in patients with stroke or HFpEF [30, 31]. However, evidence specifically evaluating the IR indices in patients with coexisting HFpEF and CKD remains limited. Furthermore, direct head-to-head comparisons of predictive utility among different IR indices in this population have been lacking.

The predictive ability of TyG index in this cohort may originate from its unique integration of lipid and glucose metabolism pathways [15]. In CKD, impaired lipoprotein lipase activity [32] combined with hepatic IR-driven gluconeogenesis [33] synergistically elevates both TG and FBG levels. The results of this study align with prior CAD studies evaluating surrogate IR indices. Yadegar et al. identified the TyG index as a robust predictor of CAD severity [22], while Zhang et al. demonstrated that combining multiple IR indices with machine learning effectively predicts CAD in patients with diabetes [21]. CAD research similarly recognizes TyG as a stronger MACE predictor than other IR indices [34]. This study extends existing evidence by specifically evaluating patients with HFpEF and CKD, demonstrating that the TyG outperformed TyG-BMI, AIP, and METS-IR. The diminished predictive utility of TyG-BMI may reflect CKD-specific confounders: fluid overload potentially overestimates BMI measurements, while concomitant uremic sarcopenia may impair muscle IR independent of adiposity [35]. This mechanistic interplay likely attenuates TyG-BMI’s prognostic value, consistent with observational studies showing weakened TyG-BMI-mortality associations in CKD cohorts [36, 37].

Current evidence indicates AIP and METS-IR demonstrate predictive value for CV and metabolic disorders as surrogate IR markers. However, their utility in populations with coexisting HFpEF and CKD remains underexplored. In both general populations and individuals with abnormal glucose metabolism, AIP has been validated as a robust prognostic biomarker [38, 39]—an association potentially mediated through lipoprotein remnant-induced endothelial inflammation and oxidative stress [17]. In CKD populations, HDL functional abnormalities may attenuate AIP’s predictive value for MACE. Studies demonstrate that HDL particles undergo qualitative alterations in CKD—including apolipoprotein A1 glycation and reduced antioxidant activity—shifting from anti-inflammatory to pro-inflammatory phenotypes [40]. This phenomenon manifests paradoxically in patients undergoing hemodialysis, where elevated TG/HDL-C ratios predict fewer CV and mortality events versus the general population [41]. This suggests traditional HDL-C metrics may not accurately reflect MACE risk in CKD. Consistent with this biological rationale, this study observed that while AIP maintained significant MACE associations, its predictive performance (AUC = 0.633) was inferior to the TyG index. This likely results from HDL dysfunction interfering with the AIP formula.

METS-IR, a multi-parameter index integrating FBG, TG, HDL-C, and BMI, demonstrates strong IR predictive capability in populations with diabetes and obesity [18]. The Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (n = 8,243) found METS-IR predictive of new-onset CKD risk [42], whereas another study reported loss of significant association between METS-IR and CKD risk after multivariable adjustment [43]. In patients with HFpEF, metabolic syndrome (centered on IR) associates with increased ventricular stiffness and renal hyperfiltration [44]. In this cohort, METS-IR’s association with MACE (Q4 HR = 1.42) may reflect its comprehensive capture of systemic metabolic dysregulation. However, its predictive performance (AUC = 0.614) remained inferior to the TyG index. This limitation potentially stems from BMI measurement inaccuracies and HDL dysfunction in CKD [40, 41]. Collectively, while AIP and METS-IR convey partial metabolic risk profiles in patients with HFpEF and CKD, their dependence on HDL-C and BMI components likely limits prognostic utility.

The evaluation of the AIP is situated within the broader context of lipid-based indices of metabolic dysfunction. In this regard, the TG/HDL-C warrants particular emphasis as a widely accessible and validated surrogate marker of IR and atherogenic dyslipidemia. Its prognostic value is well-documented, including recent evidence demonstrating its association with atherosclerotic injury in familial hypercholesterolemia and with arterial stiffness, a key measure of vascular aging [45, 46]. While the TG/HDL-C ratio provides immense clinical utility, this study selected its logarithmic transformation, the AIP, for the primary analysis. This decision was based on the normalized distribution of AIP, which is more suitable for the regression models employed in this study, and was made to avoid statistical redundancy. Therefore, by utilizing AIP, this findings directly reflect the well-established pathophysiological information captured by the TG/HDL-C ratio, while offering enhanced methodological rigor for comparative analyses.

While this study establishes IR indices as independent prognostic markers in patients with HFpEF and CKD, the precise mechanisms underlying this association remains unknown. Dysfunctional adipose tissue secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines and pro-oxidative mediators that directly promote IR, myocardial fibrosis, endothelial dysfunction, and glomerular injury. This creates a shared pathophysiological substrate for cardiorenal damage [47–49]. IR further exacerbates hyperinsulinemia, which stimulates renal sodium reabsorption while amplifying systemic inflammation and oxidative stress [50, 51]. These processes collectively accelerate atherosclerosis and impair myocardial relaxation—a hallmark pathomechanism in patients with HFpEF [49]. Concurrently, CKD amplifies CV risk through uremia-induced inflammation, anemia, and dysregulated mineral metabolism, promoting vascular calcification and left ventricular hypertrophy [52, 53]. Crucially, CKD-induced alterations in insulin signaling impair cardiac mitochondrial function, reducing energy availability for diastolic relaxation [12, 54]. This establishes bidirectional cardiorenal crosstalk and further exacerbates cardiac remodeling [53]. Within this integrated CKM framework, IR thus functions as both catalyst and amplifier, driving multiorgan dysfunction that culminates in MACE.

The MAGGIC risk score has served as a widely adopted prognostic tool for decades, extensively used for mortality prediction in patients with HFpEF [23]. However, given its incorporation of serum creatinine and ACEI/ARB use, this score may introduce bias with declining renal function. Consistent with this concern, MAGGIC demonstrates limited discrimination in CKD populations [55]. Although the TyG index alone achieved substantial discrimination (AUC 0.676), it significantly enhanced both discriminatory and reclassification performance when added to MAGGIC. As a cost-effective biomarker requiring only routine metabolic parameters, TyG can be readily integrated into clinical workflows [26, 56]. These findings support incorporating the TyG index into CV risk assessment frameworks to refine prognostic stratification and guide tailored secondary prevention in this high-risk cohort.

In the future, investigating whether serial measurements of the TyG index provide incremental prognostic information beyond a single baseline assessment would be valuable for monitoring disease trajectory and therapeutic response. Besides, predictive models integrating cardiorenal and metabolic markers are warranted to further optimize risk stratification. Furthermore, prospective studies are needed to validate optimal TyG index thresholds for risk stratification and to integrate them into clinical decision-making pathways for this high-risk population.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several key strengths. First, while the associations between IR and CV outcomes are established, patients with HFpEF and CKD remain frequently excluded from clinical trials ([6]– [7]). CKD-related confounders, such as fluid overload distorting BMI measurements, dysfunctional HDL, and dysregulated glucose-lipid metabolism, challenge the applicability of existing IR surrogates in this population. By providing the first comprehensive evaluation of IR indices in patients with HFpEF and CKD, this study addresses a critical gap and delivers subgroup-specific evidence that directly informs risk stratification in CKM syndrome. Second, it features a large sample size, adequately powered follow-up duration, and comprehensive evaluation of IR indices using both Cox regression models and reclassification statistics (AUC/cNRI/IDI).

The clinical significance resides in TyG’s ability to substantially improve MAGGIC score performance (AUC increase from 0.56 to 0.68). This provides the first validated IR risk assessment tool for this clinically excluded population, enabling timely risk stratification and guiding metabolically targeted therapies.

However, limitations warrant consideration. First, the retrospective single-center design and the exclusion of screened patients may introduce selection bias, and unmeasured confounders could influence results. Second, IR indices were calculated from baseline measurements; dynamic changes in metabolic profiles during follow-up were not assessed. Third, the absence of tissue-level data limits the mechanistic insights. Fourth, waist circumference was not routinely collected in this retrospective cohort. Finally, the absence of data on albuminuria prevented the application of the full, standard CKD diagnostic criteria that incorporate both reduced eGFR and markers of kidney damage.

Conclusions

The study confirms that key IR indices independently predict MACE in the HFpEF and CKD cohort. Crucially, their addition to the MAGGIC score significantly improves risk discrimination, suggesting a clear clinical application. Adopting the TyG index in practice would enhance the identification of patients at the highest risk, facilitating targeted management strategies that are expected to directly improve patient outcomes.
