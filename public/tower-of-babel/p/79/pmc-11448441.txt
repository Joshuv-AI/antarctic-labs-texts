Identification of a Novel Frameshift variant of the ATRX gene: a Case Report and Review of the genotype–phenotype relationship
Authors: Yishan Wang, Qizhou Ma, Jing Chen, Shaoxin Li, Feifei Zheng, Lei Shi, Xiaoshun Li, Sinan Li, Guanglei Tong, Hong Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundX-linked intellectual disability-hypotonic facies syndrome-1 (MRXHF1) and Alpha-thalassemia X-linked intellectual disability (ATR-X) syndrome are caused by pathogenic variant in the ATRX gene, a member of the switch/sucrose non-fermentable (SWI-SNF) protein family that exhibits chromatin remodeling activity. These syndromes show a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations, such as distinctive dysmorphic features, mild-to-profound intellectual disability, motor development delay, seizures, urogenital abnormalities, and gastrointestinal disorders.Case presentation and literature reviewA 3-year-old boy from a Chinese non-consanguineous family was diagnosed with MRXHF1 by whole-exome sequencing. Comprehensive family history information was obtained. The Medline database was searched until 1st Aug 2023 for articles related to ATRX pathogenic variant. Data on gene/protein mutations and clinical symptoms were extracted. The proband showed intellectual disability, motor development delay, typical facial abnormalities, urogenital defect, behavior problems, and optical nerve dysplasia. A novel frameshift mutation c.399_400dup, (p.Leu134Cysfs*2) in the ATRX gene was the primary cause, which occurs right before the ATRXDNMT3-DNMT3L (ADD) domain of ATRX protein. Missense mutation is the most common variation type. The ADD and helicase-like domains are the most frequently affected domains. Epilepsy, congenital heart disease, urogenital defect, acoustic defect, and optical defect are more prevalent in patients with frameshift mutations compared to those with missense mutations. There are more urogenital defects with C-terminal frameshift mutations than with N-terminal frameshift mutations.ConclusionWe described a novel frameshift mutation in the ATRX gene in a patient with MRXHF1 syndrome and summarized the genotype–phenotype relationship of ATRX pathogenic variant by variation type and affected protein domain. The regulatory mechanism underlying ATRX variant requires comprehensive analysis in future studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-024-05088-0.Keywords: ATRX gene, X-linked intellectual disability-hypotonic facies syndrome-1, Whole exome sequencing, Alpha-thalassemia X-linked intellectual disability (ATR-X) syndrome

Background

ATRX pathogenic variant was first identified in patients with alpha-thalassemia X-linked intellectual disability (ATR-X) syndrome (OMIM # 301040), after which the ATRX gene was named. ATR-X syndrome is a rare genetic condition with an estimated prevalence of 1/30,000–1/40,000 in male newborns [1–5]. The gene encoding ATRX maps to Xq21.1, and it encodes at least two alternatively spliced transcripts that give rise to slightly different proteins of 265 kDa and 280 kDa [6]. The protein product is a member of the SNF2 family of chromatin remodeling factors and has two main domains. At the N-terminus is the ADD domain, a plant homeodomain (PHD)-like zinc finger with an additional C2-C2 motif. In the C-terminal half of the protein lies the helicase/adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase) domain comprising several highly conserved colinear helicase motifs [7, 8]. ATRX germline mutations mainly cause ATR-X syndrome, while somatic ATRX mutations can cause alpha-thalassemia myelodysplastic syndrome (ATDMs), a neoplastic disorder of the myeloid lineage of hematopoiesis, and various tumors [4].

Multiple clinical syndromes other than ATR-X syndrome are attributed to ATRX germline mutations, for example, X-linked intellectual disability-hypotonic facies syndrome-1 (MRXHF1, OMIM #309580) and syndromes previously reported as sporadic cases that were later demonstrated to result from ATRX mutations, such as Carpenter-Waziri, Chudley-Lowry-Hoar, Juberg-Marsidi, Renier-Gabreels-Jasper, Smith-Fineman-Myers, and Holmes-Gang syndromes [9].

Syndromes caused by ATRX germline mutation share numerous symptoms, such as global development delay (GDD), intellectual disability (ID), typical facial features, epilepsy, congenital heart abnormality, and urogenital defects; more rarely, autism spectrum disorder, acoustic defects, and optical defects occur. However, few studies have been conducted on the intrinsic connection between genotypes and clinical phenotypes [8, 10–13].

In this article, we present a case of a 3-year-old boy diagnosed with MRXHF1 resulting from a novel frameshift mutation in ATRX (c.399_400dup, p. Leu134Cysfs*2, NM_000489.6) that has not been reported previously. We also screened ATRX case reports. We sorted the cases by pathogenic variant type and associated the clinical symptoms with variation types and affected protein domains. We found that frameshift and nonsense mutations had a higher prevalence of specific clinical symptoms, such as epilepsy and urogenital, acoustic, and optical defects, than missense mutations. The ADD (mostly GATA-like domain and PHD-like domain) and helicase-like domains are the most affected domains in view of clinical symptoms. Other domains, such as the EZH2 binding domain, RNA binding domain, and MECP2 interacting domain, also participate in ATR-X syndrome.

Subjects and methods

Subject

A 3-year-old boy from a Chinese non-consanguineous family diagnosed with MRXHF1 was analyzed. His two brothers were normally developed, and his mother was confirmed to be heterozygous. Comprehensive family history information was obtained during genetic counseling. Informed written consent was obtained from the child’s parents as guardians according to the ethics committees and review boards of Anhui Provincial Children’s Hospital.

Whole-exome sequencing

We used xGen Exome Research Panel version 2.0 (IDT, IA, USA) to extract DNA from peripheral blood leukocytes. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was performed by DNBSEQ-T7 (MGI, China) with no less than 99% regions covered at least 10×. Raw sequence readouts were processed by fastp. Paired-end sequence reads were aligned to the GRCh37/hg19 genome with Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA). GATK was used for initial mapping and variant site identification. Annotations were retrieved from the online system by Chigene (Beijing, China), including allele frequency in the Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (dbSNP), 1000 Genomes Project, Exome Aggregation Consortium (ExAC), Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD), ESP, and Chigene in-house MAF databases; positions and associated clinical phenotypes were determined using the UCSC, RefGene, GENCODE, ENSEMBL, LOVD, SWISS, Clinvitae, HGMD, OMIM, and ClinVar databases. Amino-acid changes were predicted using software packages (Provean, SIFT, PolyPhen2, MutationTaster, M-CAP, REVEL, and CADD; MaxEntScan, dbscSNV, and GTAG; GERP, phyloP, and phastCons). Variant filtering was conducted with minor allele frequency (MAF) < 1. Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were evaluated in accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) practice guidelines.

Literature search and data extraction

We searched the Medline database for articles until 1st Aug 2023. The search criteria included “ATRX,” “XNP,” “ATMDS,” “MRXHF1,” “SFMS,” “Chudley-lowry syndrome,” “Holmes-Gang syndrome,” “Carpenter-Waziri syndrome,” “Juberg Marsidi syndrome,” and all their synonyms. The search strategy details are presented in Supplementary material 1. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) case reports, series, or any type of articles related to diseases caused by ATRX gene pathogenic variant; (2) only patients with confirmed ATRX pathogenic variant in affected pedigrees were included; (3) clinical phenotypes integrated for cases caused by the same pathogenic variant. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) articles not related to ATRX pathogenic variant; (2) multiple gene mutations including ATRX; (3) chromosomal mutation spans several genes including ATRX; (4) cases without gene confirmation; (5) female patients; (6) articles not in English or Chinese; (7) incomplete clinical data; (8) full text not available; and (9) ATMD cases.

The following data were extracted from the included articles: gene mutation locus, predicted protein mutation locus, variation type, affected protein domain, clinical symptoms include α-thalassemia/HbH inclusion, motor development delay, intellectual disability, epilepsy, autism/behavioral problems (for example, poor eye contact, communicative disorders, stereotyped behavior, irritability, impulsion, and hyperactivity), congenital heart disease, urological defects, genital defects, gastrointestinal symptom, skeletal/muscular abnormalities (for example, abnormal muscle strength and tone, scoliosis, and skeleton deformity), acoustic defects, and optical defects.

Result

Case presentation

The proband was born by normal spontaneous vaginal delivery at 41 weeks of gestation and weighed 3150 g. He was the third child of his mother, and the development process of the two elder brothers (13 and 10 years old at the time of writing) was normal. No family history of neuromuscular disease was reported. The proband was admitted to the hospital for feeding difficulties and failure to thrive in the neonatal period. Echocardiography showed an atrial septal defect (ASD) of 0.16 cm, which was diminished at age 3. Whole-exome sequencing showed a frameshift mutation in the ATRX gene due to c.399_c.400dup(exon6) causing p.Leu134Cysfs*2 (NM_000489.6); the pathogenic variant was considered likely pathogenic according to the ACMG guideline PVS1 + PM2-Supporting and was related to MRXHF1(OMIM:309580), no other likely pathogenic/pathogenic variant was identified that could potentially be contributing to the patient’s phenotype. Sanger sequencing further revealed that the mother was heterozygote, and the father was not affected (Fig. 1). Verification of pedigree was refused. The diagnosis was made MRXHF1 for him based on the patient’s clinical symptoms and WES result later on. The patient experienced recurrent severe respiratory infections before 3 years of age. His global development was profoundly delayed, he could not walk until 2 years of age, he spoke no meaningful words, and could barely comprehend language. His behavioral abnormalities included unprovoked screaming, hyperactivity, and somnipathy. He was admitted to our hospital for severe intellectual disability at 3 years of age. He was 90 cm (− 2 SD) in height and weighed 16 kg (+ 1 SD) on admission. His occipitalfrontal circumference (OCF) was 48 cm (− 2 SD). The clinical examination showed hyper-folded ears, a flat nose bridge, an open mouth with drooling, a triangular upper lip shaped like a tent, and widely spaced upper incisors (Fig. 2). Mild hypotonia was seen in both lower extremities. Urogenital ultrasound showed unilateral cryptorchism and a separated renal pelvis in both kidneys. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a thickened frontal cranium and poor pneumatization of mastoid. His scores in the Binet-Simon Intelligence Scale and Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test were 0; the Activities of Daily Living (ADL) test showed severe defect. Fundus examination revealed optic nerve dysplasia. Brainstem auditory evoked potential (BAEP) showed an elevated response threshold on both sides (left 70 dB, right 65 dB). His blood test results, thyroid function, and blood biochemistry were all normal. Hemoglobin electrophoresis revealed normal HbA, HbF, and HbA2 levels. Brilliant cresyl blue staining showed no HbH inclusion, and the peripheral blood smear result was also normal. The patient underwent speech training and occupational therapy, and drooling was reduced, but no obvious cognitive progress was observed.

Literature review

A flowchart demonstrating the article inclusion and exclusion process is shown in Fig. 3. Briefly, 2881 articles were identified in the initial search, and 43 articles covering 63 patients and 50 pathogenic variants were ultimately included [9–11, 14–53].

Flowchart demonstrating the article inclusion and exclusion process

In articles in which the pathogenic variant type could be identified, the most frequent pathogenic variant type was missense mutation. There were 35 different missense mutations (43 patients), 7 frameshift mutations (7 patients) including the patient we reported here, 4 nonsense mutations (9 patients), and 5 splicing mutations (5 patients). Missense mutation c.736 C > T p.(R246C) and nonsense mutation p.Arg37* are the two most frequent variant types, c.736 C > T p.(R246C) presents in 7 patients across 6 articles, while p.Arg37* presents in 7 patients of 4 articles. The pathogenic variants and clinical phenotypes sorted by variation type are presented in Supplementary material 2.

Due to the wide difference in protein structure changes according to pathogenic variant type, the genotype–phenotype relationship of four major mutation types (missense, frameshift, nonsense, and splicing mutations) was described separately (Table 1). All patients presented with intellectual disability regardless of the mutation type. Motor development delay was more common in patients with missense mutations (74.42%; 32/43) than in those with other mutation types. Epilepsy frequently occurred in those with frameshift mutation (57.14%; 4/7) and nonsense mutation (55.56%; 5/9). Urogenital defects were more frequent in those with frameshift (urological defect, 42.86%, 3/7; genital defect, 57.14%, 4/7) and nonsense (genital defect 55.56%, 5/9) mutation, while congenital heart disease, acoustic defect, and optical abnormalities only occurred in patients with missense or frameshift mutations.

Among the patients with missense mutations, 32 out of 43 cases showed motor development delay, it was associated with a wide range of protein domains; the ADD domain (50.00%, 16/32), helicase-like domain (28.13%, 9/32), and MECP2 interacting region (18.75%, 6/32) are the most frequently affected domains. The enhancer of zeste (EZH2) binding domain is also affected (3.13%, 1/32). All 43 cases presented with intellectual disability, it was associated with the helicase-like domain (41.86%, 18/43) and ADD domain (39.53%, 17/43); less commonly affected domains, such as the EZH2 binding domain and RNA binding region (RBR), were also involved. The helicase-like domain (40.00%, 4/10) is more likely to be involved in patients with epilepsy (10 out of 43 cases developed epilepsy); involvement of this domain among patients with this presentation was more than that of the ADD domain (30.00%, 3/10) and MECP2 interacting region (20.00%, 2/10). More than half of the patients with autism/behavioral problem had ADD domain involvement (63.16%, 12/19). Regarding genital defects, 65.00% of the cases with genital defect affected the ADD domain, equally distributed into GATA and PHD like domain. More than half of the patients with digestive abnormalities had ADD domain mutations (70.59%, 12/17). Patients with musculoskeletal abnormality (in total 29 cases) resulted from protein mutations located in the ADD domain (51.72%, 15/29), RBR (3.45%, 1/29), helicase-like domain (34.48%, 10/29), and MECP2 domain (10.34%, 3/29). Two patients had acoustic defects and five had optical defects. Acoustic defect was related to the ATP binding domain and MECP2 domain, while optical defect was associated with the PHD-like domain, ATP binding domain, and MECP2 interacting region. Seven patients with frameshift mutation were included. The novel frameshift mutation we report in this manuscript and the pathogenic variant reported by Vivante et al. [38] are close to the N-terminus of the ATRX protein, while the others are close to the C-terminal. Motor development delay, urogenital abnormalities, and severe ID were present in both patients with N-terminal mutations.

Nine patients had nonsense mutations; α-thalassemia/HbH inclusion, motor development delay, epilepsy, musculoskeletal abnormalities, and autistic-like behavior were only reported in patients with p.R37*, which is nearest to the N terminus ever since. Only one patient with C-terminal mutation (p.Gln2398*) showed gastrointestinal symptoms. None of the patients with nonsense mutations had acoustic or optical defects.

Five articles described splicing mutations [9, 16, 50, 51, 54]. All patients with splicing mutations demonstrated ID, however, no epilepsy, congenital heart disease, acoustic abnormalities, or visual loss was recorded.

Discussion and conclusions

In this case report and literature review, we described the case of a patient with a novel ATRX frameshift mutation (c.399_400dup, p.Leu134Cysfs*2, NM_000489.6) causing severe ID, facial abnormalities, and multi-organ defects. We then reviewed the literature on ATRX pathogenic variant, summarized genotype–phenotype correlations, and sorted clinical symptoms by variation type and affected protein domain.

ATRX has emerged as a key player in gene expression, DNA repair, replication, and recombination. It acts on tandem repeat (TR) sequences and influences the deposition of a histone variant, prevention of replication fork stalling, and the suppression of a homologous recombination-based pathway of telomere maintenance [55].

In accordance with Baden et al.’s findings, ADD domain (mostly influence the PHD-like domain) and the helicase domain are hotspots of ATRX pathogenic variants and accounts for most of the clinical symptoms of ATRX pathogenic variant [10], however, with growing knowledge of ATRX protein domain and by incorporating more patients with ATRX mutation, we proposed that, GATA-like domain is also a high frequency pathogenic variant spot.

Unusually, a broad phenotypic spectrum is associated not only with ATRX mutation type but also in those with the same mutation. Multiple mechanisms underlying ATRX mutations could explain this phenomenon.

Alpha-thalassemia in ATR-X syndrome is attributed to reduced expression of the α-globin genes located on chromosome 16 [55]. Downregulation of α-globin expression leads to an excess of β-globin chains, which tend to form beta tetramers (HbH) [8]. Although cells with HbH inclusions are not seen until α-globin expression is downregulated by 50%, the proportion of cells with HbH inclusions can be used as a rough indicator of alpha globin gene expression. Law et al. demonstrated that the most prominent feature of the ATRX targets in both human and mouse is the presence of variable number tandem repeats (VNTRs), which in many cases are G and C rich and contain a high proportion of CpG dinucleotides. In ATR-X syndrome, when ATRX function is compromised, the degree of perturbation in gene expression is related to the size of the tandem repeat sequence, which has the potential to form G-quadruplex (G4) DNA structures. Law et al. showed that ATRX binds to G4 DNA in vitro, and a significant correlation was seen between the level of HbH inclusions and the size of the TR. These findings explain the variable percentage of HbH inclusion in patients with identical ATRX pathogenic variants [12].

ID is an important clinical symptom of ATR-X syndrome, and its degree ranges from mild/moderate to severe. Gibbons et al. [8] and Badens et al. [56] confirmed that mutations in the ADD domain are associated with more severe psychomotor retardation than those in the helicase region. Indeed, the three missense mutations and one frameshift mutation associated with mild to moderate ID discussed here were all located in the C-terminal helicase-like domain (in total, 32 cases provided with the degree of ID which was marked in supplementary material 2). Shoida et al. reported that Atrx mutation induces aberrant upregulation of Xlr3b expression in the mouse brain. ATRX normally binds to G-quadruplexes in CpG islands of the imprinted Xlr3b gene, regulating its expression by recruiting DNA methyltransferases. Overexpression of Xlr3b inhibits the dendritic transport of the mRNA encoding CaMKII-α, promoting synaptic dysfunction. 5-ALA is a G-quadruplex ligand that inhibits Xlr3b expression. Administering 5-ALA represses Xlr3b transcription and rescues decreased synaptic plasticity and cognitive deficits in ATR-X model mice [57]. Thus, ATRX perturbs cognition-related genes through binding to G-quadruplexes in CpG islands.

One nonsense mutation c.324 C > T (Arg37*) was found to be related with mild ID [15]. Although nonsense mutations have been identified in the ATRX gene, in ATR-X cases, some full-length proteins derived from the mutant allele have been identified in protein extracts derived from cell lines carrying these mutations. Similar to observations with R37X, intraexonic splicing/alternative splicing can lead to the presence of two isoforms, a full-length version carrying the mutation and a shorter form from which an 87-bp segment of mRNA (encoding 29 amino acids) containing the mutations has been excised, leading to mutation skipping and some degree of phenotypic rescue [8].

Urogenital abnormality is not rare in ATR-X syndrome. It has been established that the C-terminus of the ATRX protein may play a specific role in urogenital development, and mutation in the C-terminus is frequently associated with severe genital abnormalities, including micropenis and genital ambiguities. Gibbons et al. reported that 5 out of 7 mutations associated with severe urogenital abnormalities are truncating mutations that result in the loss of the C-terminal domain [13]. Indeed, the urogenital defects we described and reviewed here are more frequent in those with frameshift and nonsense mutations, which have the tendency to cause a truncated protein product; moreover, 5 out of 7 frameshift mutations discussed here caused the loss of the C-terminus of the protein, specifically the MECP2 interacting region. The other two frameshift mutations occurred in the N-terminus and resulted in a more severely truncated protein product.

Acoustic and optical defects are rare, but by no means absent. These two conditions are associated with missense and frameshift mutations. The case we reported here showed optic nerve dysplasia and an elevated response threshold on both sides of the ears by BAEP. Acoustic defects reported in the literature included hearing loss [17] and hearing defect for high pitched sounds [58], while optical defects included strabismus, hypermetropia [17], and optic atrophy [51]. Acoustic and optical abnormalities are less frequently seen in those with missense mutations than in those with frameshift mutations. Almost all cases have missense mutations located in the C-terminus. No case of acoustic and optical abnormalities was seen in those with nonsense mutations, and the underlying mechanism for this is unclear.

However, there are some limitations of this article. Reporting bias could not be neglected, due to authors could only report partial clinical sign and other crucial symptoms could be missed, besides, clinical phenotype such as epilepsy, congenital heart disease and autism could be age dependent, which would influence the accuracy of this report.

In this article, we reported a novel frameshift mutation in ATRX in a patient with typical MRXHF1 syndrome and described the ATRX pathogenic variant genotype–phenotype relationship. It is hoped that the genotype–phenotype relationship will be more accurately illustrated in the future with larger sample-size studies and that the regulatory mechanism underlying ATRX mutation will be comprehensively understood.
