ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONIntegrating real‐world data (RWD) with clinical research datasets creates more complete, context‐rich resources to drive discovery and enable precision medicine in Alzheimer's disease and related dementias. The National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center conducted a proof‐of‐concept (POC) project to link its longitudinal Uniform Data Set (UDS) with electronic health records (EHRs), Medicare claims, and genomic data.METHODSIn partnership with three Alzheimer's Disease Research Centers (ADRCs), we developed scalable data pipelines and a governance framework to collect, harmonize, and securely share linked data through the National Institute on Aging's LINKAGE Program.RESULTSWe linked UDS and EHR data for 2206 participants and added Medicare claims for 1522. Nearly 500 participants have 5 or more years of linked data, with genomic profiles available for 93%.DISCUSSIONThis POC demonstrates the feasibility of integrating clinical, genomic, and RWD across ADRCs, offering a scalable model for broader implementation.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD) and related dementias (AD/ADRD) represent one of the most urgent biomedical and public health challenges of our time. More than seven million Americans are currently affected, with annual care costs exceeding $384 billion, and these figures are projected to rise dramatically in coming decades. 1 Despite recent advances, including disease‐modifying therapeutics such as lecanemab and donanemab, 2 , 3 realizing the full potential of precision medicine for AD/ADRD will require transformative changes in how we detect, characterize, and treat disease.

AD/ADRD pathology accumulates silently over decades, making early and personalized detection strategies essential. 4 , 5 At the same time, the biological heterogeneity of the disease complicates diagnosis, treatment selection, and clinical trial design. 6 , 7 Precision medicine offers a path forward by enabling researchers to account for diverse etiologies, risk factors, and treatment responses across individuals and populations. Achieving this vision requires integrating complementary sources of data, including structured clinical research data and real‐world data (RWD), to generate individualized, data‐driven insights at scale.

The National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC), established in 1999 as the data and coordination hub for the National Institute on Aging (NIA)‐funded Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (ADRC) Program, is uniquely positioned to advance these goals. In partnership with over 40 ADRCs, NACC has built one of the world's most comprehensive standardized datasets for AD/ADRD research. The Uniform Data Set (UDS), collected since 2005, provides rich, longitudinal, standardized multidomain clinical and cognitive data from participants across the disease progression spectrum, ranging from unimpaired aging to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to dementia, with a wide range of pure and mixed dementia etiologies represented. 8 , 9 NACC has collected standardized neuropathology data from ADRCs since 2002, and nearly 60% of ADRC participants are registered as brain donors.

In recent years, NACC has expanded its data platform to integrate additional modalities, including standardized neuroimaging, fluid biomarkers, and genomic data, through partnerships with other national repositories and initiatives. 10 , 11 , 12 The modernization of NACC's cyberinfrastructure, including a cloud‐based multimodal data integration and harmonization platform and the launch of the NACC Data Front Door (a centralized access point for ADRC participant data), has enabled scalable, secure, and interoperable data sharing. These efforts have laid a strong and scalable foundation for a precision medicine framework that supports early detection, patient stratification, and targeted interventions.

However, clinical research data, while deeply phenotyped and rigorously collected, are inherently episodic and limited in scope. 9 RWD, including electronic health records (EHRs) and Medicare claims data from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS), provide a complementary lens by capturing routine clinical care trajectories, medication exposures, comorbidities, and health outcomes. Seminal initiatives, such as IDEAS and New IDEAS, 13 , 14 demonstrate the power of RWD to inform clinical decision‐making and reshape care delivery. Together, clinical research data and RWD offer synergistic value. The former provides structured, protocol‐driven longitudinal assessments with deep phenotyping, while the latter captures continuous, real‐world health trajectories across varied populations and healthcare settings. Integrating these modalities is essential for generating real‐world evidence, improving generalizability, and accelerating discoveries that translate to better outcomes. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18

Despite this promise, integrating RWD into AD/ADRD research poses substantial challenges, including incomplete data and variation in clinical coding and documentation practices. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Legal, ethical, and regulatory considerations, such as Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA) compliance, participant consent, and complex data governance, pose significant obstacles for access and sharing.

To address these challenges and demonstrate a scalable model for RWD integration, NACC launched a proof‐of‐concept (POC) initiative in collaboration with three ADRCs – Indiana University, Columbia University, and Washington University – to link real‐world and genomic data with gold‐standard clinical phenotypic data. This platform securely integrates EHR, Medicare claims, and genomic data at the participant level within a NIA LINKAGE Secure Data Enclave 23 managed by NACC. The resulting infrastructure enables harmonized, participant‐level linkage across diverse and sensitive data types over time under a unified governance framework created specifically for this pilot.

While there have been other efforts focused on linking or mapping UDS data to RWD (EHR and/or claims), 24 , 25 our POC is distinct in that we combined these data types across multiple ADRCs, linked them to genomic data, and established a secure enclave and governance framework that is a first step toward developing a globally accessible RWD research platform.

In this article, we describe the development, implementation, and early outcomes of this POC. We outline the technical architecture, governance strategies, data linkage workflows, and quality assurance procedures. We also share lessons learned and offer recommendations for others pursuing similar efforts. As AD/ADRD research advances toward personalized prevention and treatment, scalable platforms that integrate clinical research and RWD will be critical to realizing the full promise of precision medicine. This combined dataset will be a valuable resource for advancing artificial intelligence (AI)‐driven discovery in AD/ADRD, enabling researchers to gain new insights into early detection and predicting progression and treatment responses.

METHODS

We used a multistakeholder co‐development and implementation approach to develop the infrastructure and governance for EHR and claims data sharing and linkage. The project followed these parallel tracks: (1) building partnerships and workgroups, (2) defining system requirements (governance), and (3) determining technical architecture (pipelines, quality, harmonization).

Track 1 – Building partnerships and workgroups

NACC

In partnership with over 40 past and present ADRCs across the United States, NACC has built one of the largest and most comprehensive standardized datasets for AD/ADRD, encompassing more than 54,500 participants, over 20,000 of whom are currently followed longitudinally via annual visits. Participants have up to 20 years of follow‐up visits. Among over 200,000 total data collection visits, 177,000 include a neuropsychological assessment. In addition, over 8000 autopsies conducted using standardized neuropathological assessments are available for deceased participants. Participants span the cognitive spectrum from unimpaired aging to dementia, with robust representation of both pure and mixed neuropathological etiologies.

NACC is actively integrating and providing access to additional data modalities from ADRC participants to enable new insights into the complexity of AD/ADRD. These include clinical, neuropathological, neuroimaging (magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]/positron emission tomography [PET]), genetic/genomic data, and fluid biomarkers through expanded interoperability with national repositories such as NIA Genetics of Alzheimer's Disease Data Storage Site (NIAGADS) and the National Centralized Repository for Alzheimer's Disease and Related Dementias (NCRAD). NACC is also working to incorporate multi‐omics and digital neuropathology data. Recent efforts have also focused on modernizing the NACC cyberinfrastructure, including deploying an electronic data capture system via REDCap; a scalable, cloud‐based, multimodal data integration and harmonization platform; and the Data Front Door, which serves as a centralized data search, visualization, and access portal for all ADRC participant data housed at NACC and partner repositories. All NACC data is made freely available to approved researchers around the world through the NACC Data Front Door.

Partner ADRCs

NACC selected three ADRC sites that were geographically diverse and had strong institutional informatics research support structures with expertise in EHR data extraction and sharing. Sites had varying approaches to their data‐sharing governance and varying levels of experience in working with EHR data.

Columbia University ADRC and Data Science Institute. Columbia's ADRC supports extensive Alzheimer's disease and aging research, enrolling over 6000 participants with approximately 500 under active follow‐up. It provides access to comprehensive clinical, neuropsychological, imaging, genetic, and phenotypic data. Coordination across cores enables collection of plasma, cerebrospinal fluid, and DNA biosamples. Its Neuropathology Core maintains a brain bank with tissue from over 1000 cases.

Indiana ADRC (IADRC), Indiana University School of Medicine (IUSM), and Regenstrief Institute. The IADRC is organized around the scientific theme of precision medicine, with resources focused on clinical and neuropsychological assessment, multimodal neuroimaging, genetics and multi‐omics biomarkers, and data sciences, including informatics and biostatistics. The IADRC collaborates broadly across IUSM departments and affiliated institutions and has over 500 participants under active follow‐up. The IU‐affiliated Regenstrief Institute integrates health services research into patient care and supports this work with advanced expertise in medical informatics, including EHR data.

Knight ADRC and Institute for Informatics, Data Science, and Biostatistics (I2DB), Washington University. The Knight ADRC focuses on early‐stage AD, maintaining an active cohort of approximately 900 participants ranging from cognitively unimpaired to having mild dementia. The I2DB supports scalable, data‐driven dementia research by integrating complex clinical and research data through an advanced informatics infrastructure.

Workgroup collaboration

NACC assessed existing data governance structures and EHR data extraction capabilities at each site. A governance workgroup, including legal and site representatives, developed cross‐site data protocols. An EHR informatics workgroup was established with university informatics leaders and technical experts to define EHR data extraction methods and specifications and lead quality review efforts. The workgroup engaged stakeholders, including individuals from understudied groups, to assess ethical considerations of RWD consenting for data sharing. We also consulted university legal and information security teams to ensure the appropriate transfer of EHR data and personally identifiable information (PII) for linkage.

NACC identified site champions at each participating ADRC to coordinate activities and participate in monthly meetings. Collaboration across working groups was maintained from December 2022 through May 2025 through regular meetings, documentation, and milestone tracking.

NIA data platform and repositories

NACC collaborated with the three partner ADRCs, NIAGADS, and the NIA Data LINKAGE Program (LINKAGE) to establish governance agreements and procedures to enable data flow from the partner ADRCs, NACC, and NIAGADS to a secure enclave managed by NACC within the LINKAGE cloud environment. The NIA LINKAGE Program is a secure, cloud‐based data environment designed to integrate NIA‐funded study data with external datasets, including claims data from CMS. The platform enables linkage, management, and analysis of sensitive data within a controlled‐access cloud workspace that operates under a “data visiting” model, allowing researchers to conduct analyses without downloading or locally storing data. 23

NIAGADS. NIAGADS is a NIA repository for AD/ADRD genetic and genomic data that supports research with datasets including genome‐wide association studies (GWASs), whole‐exome sequencing (WES), whole‐genome sequencing (WGS), RNA sequencing, and variant annotations.

NIA Data LINKAGE Program. The LINKAGE program connects NIA‐funded study data with claims data through a secure, standardized data platform. 23 This integration enables remote, privacy‐compliant analysis of linked clinical and claims data in a cloud‐based data statistical analysis environment to support aging and dementia research. All linked data generated through the POC – including UDS, EHR, claims, and genomic data – were securely integrated and made available within the NACC‐hosted LINKAGE enclave. This environment supports controlled access for approved researchers from the POC sites and NACC, enabling collaborative analysis of sensitive multimodal data while preserving participant privacy. The enclave currently supports R, Python, SAS, and Stata and provides central processing unit (CPU)‐based computing resources suitable for longitudinal and multimodal analyses.

Track 2 – Data governance framework

To ensure the secure and standardized collection of diverse data types across sites, a robust data governance framework was developed through collaborative efforts among NACC, NIAGADS, ADRC site champions, and legal representatives.

Institutional Review Board (IRB) approvals

Successful implementation of the multisource data integration and linkage framework required coordinated IRB oversight and compliance across all participating institutions. Given the variability in institutional policies, regulatory interpretations, and data governance structures, extensive efforts were necessary to align stakeholders and establish a compliant framework for data sharing, linkage, and use. The governance workgroup played a central role in guiding these efforts, particularly among ADRCs, where institution‐specific IRB approvals were required to enable data collection and linkage activities. The workgroup developed standardized IRB modification language that was shared with each site, and consent language was also collected from each site.

NACC IRB modification: IRB modifications were required at NACC to enable EHR and CMS data collection and sharing for the project. Clauses were added to the NACC IRB to specify these data types and how they would be shared. Language was also included to clarify that any directly identifiable data or notes sections from these data would not be transmitted to NACC.

ADRC IRB approvals: IRB approvals followed institution‐specific processes, reflecting differences in local oversight requirements. The governance workgroup worked with the three ADRCs to obtain IRB approvals for three key areas: (1) collecting data using the NACC COVID survey, (2) sharing de‐identified EHR data, and (3) collecting PII for CMS data linkage.

NACC COVID survey: All three ADRCs submitted IRB modifications to include the new COVID survey. Each site integrated the survey into existing workflows to ensure consistent data collection.

De‐identified EHR data: IRB modifications were required at each of the three participating ADRCs to enable EHR data collection and sharing for the project. Two sites submitted IRB modifications to specify EHR data sharing with NACC, while the third submitted a new IRB application because EHR data collection and sharing were not originally included.

PII for CMS linkage: Each ADRC determined its own approach to collecting and sharing PII needed for CMS linkage. NACC did not collect any PII; all PII from ADRCs was shared directly with LINKAGE, who then shared this with CMS. CMS transferred the claims data to the LINKAGE enclave so that it could be linked there with other data types at the participant level longitudinally. Strategies included making PII collection optional, adding a consent addendum, or submitting new IRB applications. In each case, institutional information security reviews were conducted to ensure proper data protection.

IRB Standard Operating Procedure (SOP): The diverse IRB strategies employed by the three participating ADRCs informed the development of successful case examples in support of a standardized set of IRB guidelines that could support future ADRCs’ governance efforts to integrate and link real‐world, genomic, and clinical research data. They provide a practical roadmap for navigating IRB modifications and consent requirements for permission sharing for consented data across complex, multisite initiatives.

Data‐sharing agreements

Several new and existing data‐sharing agreements were required to enable secure data flow, storage, and sharing for this pilot. New data use agreements were established between NACC, NIAGADS, LINKAGE, and the ADRCs, ensuring compliance with regulatory standards and facilitating responsible data sharing. These are described in more detail in Section 3.1.

Data pipelines and quality testing

Data pipelines were established to collect and harmonize data across data sources linked by NACCID, a unique identifier assigned to UDS participants in NACC's database (Figure 1). Each dataset underwent processes that included data quality testing and ensuring that governance compliance was met.

UDS data. ADRC sites collect participants’ UDS data at annual study visits, and all clinical core participants administered the UDS are assigned a unique NACCID at the time of enrollment and consent, enabling longitudinal linkage of all data modalities for ADRC participants across repositories. The UDS has been updated four times (versions 1, 2, 3, and 4) in order to accommodate changes in clinical practice and scientific developments in the field. 8 , 9 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 This has caused some individual items to be discontinued at the end of a version and new ones added at the next. These are accounted for in the NACC Researchers Data Dictionary (RDD). Further, the proprietary neuropsychological battery used in versions 1 and 2 was replaced in version 3 and beyond with a like‐domain, non‐proprietary test battery. Crosswalk studies were accomplished to ensure data continuity. 29 , 31 Under the Alzheimer's Disease Sequencing Project (ADSP) Phenotype Harmonization effort, these UDS versions were additionally “harmonized” with other studies through application of Item Response Theory (IRT). 32 The RDD also contains a number of pre‐calculated “NACC‐derived variables.” This provides inherent harmonization/standardization for variables across versions and commonly used conversions. The 18 UDS forms cover topics including demographics, neurological exam findings, medical history, medication use, neuropsychological assessments, and diagnosis. The recently updated version of UDS, UDS version 4, includes over 1500 standardized variables for research use. 9 For this pilot, participating ADRCs submitted UDS data available at the time of linkage (versions 1 to 3) to NACC through established processes, as UDS version 4 was not yet available for incorporation into the LINKAGE platform. Extensive data quality checks were run to ensure the data were clean and standardized. After passing data quality checks, all UDS data for all participants at each site were securely uploaded to the LINKAGE enclave.

Genomic data. NIAGADS and the Genome Center for Alzheimer's Disease (GCAD) jointly support the ADSP genetic and genomic data harmonization through a coordinated, single data‐flow path. ADRC samples selected for single‐nucleotide polymorphism array genotyping or sequencing are plated and sent by the NCRAD to the genotyping and sequencing centers. NIAGADS receives all incoming GWAS, WES, and WGS data, manages cohort registration and research‐use limitations, and harmonizes GWAS datasets through Alzheimer's Disease Genetics Consortium (ADGC) workflows 33 that include quality control, allele and metadata harmonization, and imputation. Raw WGS and WES reads are routed by NIAGADS to GCAD, which processes them using the ADSP Variant Calling Pipeline (VCPA) 34 to perform read mapping, sample‐level gVCF generation, and large‐scale joint genotype calling, followed by variant‐ and sample‐level quality control using the ADSP quality control (QC) pipeline 35 to ensure consistency across cohorts and sequencing centers. GCAD returns harmonized, QC‐filtered CRAM, gVCF, and project‐level joint‐call VCFs to NIAGADS for integration and release. Provided to this project were ADRC participant data extracted from the ADC1‐15 GWAS datasets, 33 the ADSP R2 QCed WES dataset, 36 and the ADSP R4 QCed WGS dataset, 37 all available for request through the NIAGADS Data Sharing Service (DSS). GWAS data, WES, and WGS data from ADRC site participants were securely transferred to the enclave via Secure File Transfer Protocol, a network protocol that transfers files over an encrypted Secure Shell connection.

COVID data. NACC's COVID‐19 impact surveys were created in collaboration with ADRC site leaders and the ADRC Clinical Task Force to capture impacts of COVID‐19 on cognitive and behavioral symptoms via standardized assessments. COVID impact form data were collected from the three partner ADRC sites via file upload to a dedicated REDCap project, downloaded to the NACC secure computing platform, and interrogated via a data quality checklist. Once data were determined to be clean and standardized, they were securely uploaded to the LINKAGE enclave. 23 Harmonization of variables across the two versions of the COVID‐19 impact surveys is underway, with both forms having been released to the ADRC Program. The initial COVID form, released in June 2020, captures basic COVID diagnosis information. The updated form, released in July 2022, captures additional data on vaccinations and long‐COVID symptoms. 38 , 39

EHR data. All EHR data for this project were harmonized to the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) Common Data Model (CDM) under the Observational Health Data Sciences and Informatics (OHDSI) framework, which supports harmonization of data across heterogeneous EHR platforms. 40 This standardized approach enabled the integration of multisite EHR data into a consistent structure suitable for scalable, privacy‐preserving research. For the POC, we harmonized EHR data across the Epic (Columbia University and Washington University) and Cerner (Indiana University) EHR platforms. To guide this harmonization, an EHR Informatics Workgroup composed of partner site champions, site‐based informatics leads, and NACC technical faculty and staff adopted the National COVID Cohort Collaborative (N3C) data specification. 41 The N3C initiative provides a standardized Extract, Transform, Load (ETL) framework and shared codebase to help contributing sites transform their local EHR data into the OMOP CDM format. The N3C data specification primarily encompasses structured EHR data elements, including demographics, diagnoses, procedures, medications, laboratory results, and encounters.

To identify EHR data elements relevant to AD/ADRD, a clinical domain expert workgroup was convened to compile a comprehensive list of additional variables of interest. While many of these elements were recognized as valuable for future inclusion (e.g., cognitive and functional measures, neuroimaging metadata), they were excluded from the scope of this POC due to feasibility and bandwidth constraints at the participating sites. The final specification was therefore limited to data elements already available through existing institutional informatics pipelines that aligned with the N3C OMOP‐based framework.

All ADRC sites leveraged local informatics expertise and used OMOP‐compatible code and pipelines previously developed through their institutional participation in the N3C initiative. This enabled rapid ETL of partner site EHR data. The OMOP CDM incorporates standardized clinical vocabularies (e.g., SNOMED, RxNorm, LOINC), enabling consistent phenotyping and making it well suited for large‐scale, multisite research using limited datasets.

To operationalize this infrastructure, NACC implemented a cloud‐based OMOP data pipeline using Flywheel software within NACC's cloud‐based data platform. A centralized project environment was established to host separate ingest and staging areas for each ADRC, as well as a harmonized production workspace. Site‐based informatics teams extracted EHR data in accordance with the N3C EHR data specification and submitted OMOP‐formatted files to Flywheel using a secure upload process supported by custom scripting and role‐based access controls.

Upon upload, validation checks confirmed basic file completeness before triggering a sequence of ingestion routines. These included parsing, ETL transformation, referential integrity testing, and standardized concept mapping using a centralized terminology server and value set library. Each site's data were stored in a dedicated OMOP schema to allow for site‐specific quality assurance (QA) prior to aggregation.

NACC harmonized the submitted datasets using a two‐step process that combined local site‐level mapping with centralized review and standardization, conducted comprehensive data quality testing in collaboration with the site‐based informatics teams, and uploaded cleaned and standardized data to the NACC LINKAGE enclave for approved research use. Participating ADRC sites first applied OMOP‐compatible ETL pipelines to map local EHR data to the target data specification. NACC then performed centralized harmonization to review and resolve cross‐site inconsistencies in coding, structure, and representation prior to quality assessment. Data quality testing was performed by NACC using a collaborative, iterative review process that combined structured quality checks with expert review by informatics and clinical research staff. Each site's data quality report was reviewed both numerically and visually by informatics and clinical research experts. This involved the following steps: (1) verifying dataset completeness and structural integrity, including the presence of required tables, row counts, and expected date ranges; (2) visualizing data trends by month and year to detect periods of missingness or low volume; (3) validating the existence and relationships of primary and foreign keys across tables to ensure internal consistency; (4) reviewing demographic distributions (age, race, ethnicity, gender) to identify implausible values or unexpected patterns; and (5) assessing the plausibility and completeness of clinical content (e.g., diagnoses, procedures, observations) across domains for internal consistency. Findings from these reviews were shared with site‐based informatics teams, and datasets were revised and resubmitted as needed until quality criteria were met. Harmonized datasets that successfully passed quality review were promoted from staging to the production environment, and structured extracts were generated for secure integration into the LINKAGE platform.

PII data. ADRC sites provided LINKAGE with a Finder File containing PII for their consented participants (2937 participants across the three sites). LINKAGE offered a list of PII variables that could be used to match participants to their corresponding claims data using various combinations to maximize chances of successful matching (e.g., First name, Last name, Date of birth, Five‐digit zip code, Sex, NACCID, Date of death, State, City, Middle initial/name, Zip four‐digit extension, Medicare Beneficiary Identifier, Medicaid ID, and Social Security number). The project sites provided varying combinations of PII variables based on their available data and consent frameworks. LINKAGE generated match rate reports for each site. Once these match rate reports were reviewed and approved by each site, LINKAGE extracted the CMS data for the matched participants and transferred them to the enclave.

CMS data. Medicare claims data obtained from CMS comprise administrative billing records that capture healthcare services delivered across a wide range of clinical settings and institutions. These data include beneficiary enrollment information, demographics, diagnostic and procedure codes, prescription drug events, healthcare utilization, and associated costs. Claims span multiple care settings, including ambulatory care, inpatient and outpatient hospital services, skilled nursing facilities, hospice, and home healthcare, enabling longitudinal assessment of healthcare utilization, diagnoses, procedures, and medication exposure across the continuum of care.

Data availability

Data availability across data sources was assessed at the participant level by mapping all site‐specific participant identifiers to unique NACCIDs, enabling linkage across data types. All participants enrolled in an ADRC clinical core are assigned a static, persistent NACCID at enrollment, which remains constant over time and serves as the foundation for participant‐level longitudinal data integration across modalities. To determine data availability, a master participant‐level data availability table was created using the complete roster of participants at each of the three participating ADRCs. For each participant, binary availability flags (available/not available) were assigned for each data modality based on the presence of the corresponding NACCID in a given data source. Longitudinal data types, including UDS, EHR, Medicare claims, and COVID‐related data, as well as start and end years of data availability, were recorded based on the calendar year of clinic visits or claims activity. All available versions of UDS data (versions 1 to 3), all genomic data types (GWAS, WES, and WGS), and all Medicare claim file types (inpatient, outpatient, physician, home health, skilled nursing facility, durable medical equipment, hospice, and drug) were included in the assessment of data availability. Data availability summaries were stratified by cognitive status at the participant's most recent UDS visit (cognitively unimpaired, MCI, dementia, or impaired but not MCI). Overlap of longitudinal data across sources was quantified as the number of calendar years during which data from two or more modalities were concurrently available for a given participant.

RESULTS

Agreements solution

To enable data sharing for this POC, a complex patchwork of governance agreements had to be woven together across institutions. These included both longstanding agreements (such as the NACC/ADRC Data Transfer Use Agreement) and newly developed agreements tailored to the initiative, including data access and use agreements with NIAGADS, LINKAGE, and among the three participating ADRCs. Each agreement addressed specific data types used for claims linkage (ranging from UDS and sequencing data to PII) and required coordination across multiple entities. CMS match rate

LINKAGE generated site‐specific match rate reports for each ADRC (Columbia ADRC: 93%; Indiana ADRC: 85%; Knight ADRC: 91%), yielding an overall match rate of 86% (2532 participants matched). “Matched” participants were defined as individuals enrolled in Medicare and/or Medicaid, regardless of whether claims data were available at the time of linkage. Among matched participants, 2374 (81%) across the three ADRCs were confirmed to have claims data available. Of these, 1622 participants also had UDS data available. Medicare claims and UDS data were successfully linked for these participants and are now accessible through the NACC LINKAGE enclave.

DISCUSSION

A successful POC platform

Our POC platform demonstrates the feasibility of securely linking gold‐standard clinical research data (UDS), RWD (EHR and claims), and genomic profiles to build holistic, longitudinal participant profiles for over 2,000 consented individuals across three ADRCs. This multimodal integration, which is accessible through a secure research enclave, established a novel resource for the AD/ADRD research community. The sociotechnical solution addressed key governance and technical barriers by aligning data governance structures, operational workflows, and secure infrastructure with mechanisms to support trust and collaboration across institutions, laying the groundwork for broader scaling across the full ADRC Program and its nearly 55,000 participants. If expanded, this effort could unlock unprecedented insights into disease progression, risk, and treatment response at both individual and population levels. Studies using RWD demonstrate its utility in AD/ADRD care, including for supporting early detection, disease monitoring, strengthening the evidence for the long‐term safety and effectiveness of therapies, and improving personalized treatment strategies and health system planning. 18 , 43

The resulting dataset integrates standardized UDS assessments, EHR data, Medicare claims, and genomic profiles to provide a comprehensive view of clinical status, comorbidities, healthcare exposure, and biological risk across time and care settings. UDS contributes over 1000 structured elements per participant through annual visits, including demographics, diagnoses, functional measures, and multidomain neurocognitive testing. Structured EHR data offer detailed clinical information (diagnoses, medications, procedures, labs, vitals, and encounters) harmonized to the OMOP Common Data Model for consistent cross‐site analysis. Medicare claims data complements EHR data by capturing healthcare utilization, durable equipment, and prescriptions beyond ADRC‐affiliated systems. Genomic data (particularly GWAS, WES, and WGS) enable investigations into gene–environment interactions, disease mechanisms, and precision‐tailored risk stratification.

By combining these complementary data streams, the platform supports granular, longitudinal analyses of cognitive trajectories in the context of comorbidities, clinical interventions, and real‐world outcomes – laying a scalable foundation for AI‐driven discovery and precision medicine in AD/ADRD.

Progress toward a scalable governance solution

Governance emerged as one of the most complex aspects of the POC, driven by institutional differences in IRB policies and consent practices for sensitive data types like PII, EHR, claims, and genomics. All three ADRCs required IRB modifications; some operated under broad, pre‐existing consent language while others needed revisions to support new linkage protocols. Although all sites secured IRB approval and signed shared agreements, their timelines and institutional workflows varied.

To enable data sharing, a patchwork of pre‐existing and new agreements had to be coordinated across institutions. These included the long‐standing NACC/ADRC Data Transfer Use Agreement and new agreements with NIAGADS, LINKAGE, and the participating ADRCs. Each agreement covered distinct data types and required coordination among multiple entities. While workable for a POC, this fragmented structure is not sustainable at scale for the full ADRC Program, highlighting the need for a more centralized governance approach at NACC.

Nevertheless, the POC yielded critical insights into workflows, protections, and approvals needed for multimodal data sharing. Governance components developed during this effort will inform a future centralized framework. In this way, the POC exposed governance challenges while accelerating progress toward a more standardized and scalable model.

Importantly, the linkage of multiple sensitive data sources introduces potential risks associated with re‐identification and expanded secondary use that do not arise when data types are analyzed in isolation. To mitigate these risks, the POC adopted a conservative governance approach that emphasizes data minimization, secure enclave–based analysis, role‐based access controls, and explicit constraints on approved uses, reflecting an intentional balance between analytic utility and participant privacy and trust. In addition, the NIA LINKAGE platform provides a federally compliant, secure research enclave in which analyses are conducted in situ, with restrictions on raw data export, audit logging, and oversight of analytic outputs, further reducing risks associated with re‐identification and unauthorized secondary use.

At present, access to the LINKAGE platform is restricted to NACC staff and approved investigators from the three participating ADRCs, reflecting its intentional scoping as a POC designed to enable close oversight while informing the design of a future, larger‐scale data‐sharing platform. As governance evolves, sustained engagement of participants and patient representatives will remain essential. While individual ADRCs maintain local participant advisory boards, a national‐level participant advisory board could complement these efforts by coordinating input across sites and informing decision‐making related to informed consent, data sharing, secondary use, and emerging ethical considerations, helping to ensure scalability without compromising participant trust or ethical rigor.

To support this transition, NACC plans to develop a centralized IRB and eConsent process for the ADRC network. This model aims to streamline regulatory review, reduce institutional burden, and support inclusion of emerging data modalities. Site‐level stakeholders and community partners will help shape the framework to ensure local context, autonomy, and ethics remain central. Investing in governance infrastructure will better position the ADRC Program to evolve with the field and maximize its translational impact.

This POC was intentionally scoped to a limited set of sites and investigators to inform governance and technical design, rather than to enable broad data sharing. However, the infrastructure, workflows, and governance components established through this effort position NACC to make linked ADRC participant data broadly available to the global research community in the future under appropriate safeguards.

Progress toward automated EHR integration

This POC demonstrated the feasibility of harmonizing multisite EHR data to the OMOP Common Data Model using a standardized approach based on the N3C specification. Leveraging institutional expertise developed through N3C participation accelerated data extraction and transformation across the three POC ADRCs by reducing technical burden and providing a shared foundation for efficient cross‐site collaboration. At the time of this effort, nearly all major academic medical centers were contributing data to N3C and had established processes for submitting data in N3C‐compatible formats, substantially lowering barriers to future expansion across additional ADRCs. Since then, N3C has evolved from the National COVID Cohort Collaborative to the National Clinical Cohort Collaborative, with many original contributors electing to continue participation, further strengthening the sustainability and generalizability of this harmonization framework beyond COVID‐19‐specific use cases.

The project also strengthened connections between ADRCs and their institutional informatics and clinical infrastructures. These partnerships are critical for sustainable EHR integration, particularly given the heterogeneity of EHR platforms, data models, and local workflows across the ADRC network. This POC emphasized the importance of engaging informatics partners early to align priorities and workflows.

While the POC focused on elements already available through institutional pipelines, the effort also surfaced opportunities for deeper disease‐specific integration. Over 50 AD/ADRD‐specific EHR variables – such as cognitive scores, functional status, and imaging metadata – were identified through cross‐site stakeholder review as high‐priority targets for future integration.

Although harmonization and QA were done manually during the POC, the underlying workflow is well suited for automation. Implementing a modular, automated ETL and QA pipeline will be essential for scaling multimodal integration across the ADRC network. Such automation would support routine refreshes, reduce site‐level burden, and improve consistency across data submissions, enabling broader research–EHR integration, streamlined data workflows, and powerful multisite analyses to drive precision research in AD/ADRD.

Establishing the LINKAGE enclave as a core component of the NACC Data Front Door

All linked data generated through the POC have been securely integrated within the NACC‐hosted NIA LINKAGE enclave. LINKAGE is envisioned as a core component of the NACC Data Front Door, providing a centralized, secure platform through which researchers can access and analyze sensitive ADRC data. Secure enclaves offer a powerful model for enabling research on high‐value datasets, such as claims, EHR, and genomics, while preserving privacy and compliance. Because Medicare claims data cannot be exported, additional modalities are brought into the enclave and analyses are conducted in situ, with only approved aggregate results permitted to leave the environment.

As NACC's multimodal data sources grow in volume and complexity, advanced analytical methods, including AI, will play an increasingly important role in unlocking their full value. These tools have the potential to detect early disease signatures, predict trajectories, and forecast treatment response, enabling precision interventions. In addition, AI‐enabled approaches may support clinical trial design, cohort discovery, and the identification of health disparities, promoting more equitable care and prevention strategies.

To fully support AI‐driven discovery, future enhancements to the LINKAGE enclave may include graphics‐processing unit‐based computing resources, interactive notebook environments, and preloaded machine learning libraries, such as TensorFlow, PyTorch, and scikit‐learn. In parallel, NACC plans to modernize governance processes to broaden access for qualified investigators while maintaining rigorous privacy, security, and oversight safeguards.

These enhancements have the potential to substantially strengthen NACC's enclave as a platform for multimodal, AI‐enabled discovery. 44 , 45 They will create new onramps for researchers to access FAIR‐aligned, harmonized datasets – including longitudinal UDS, RWD, genomics, and other expanding modalities – all linked at the participant level and securely integrated. Together, these developments position the NACC Data Front Door as an increasingly valuable and scalable resource for advancing AD/ADRD research.

Future directions

Scaling this POC platform across the full ADRC Program presents a pivotal opportunity to advance real‐world evidence generation, precision medicine, and AI‐enabled discovery in AD/ADRD. By linking RWD with deeply phenotyped clinical and genomic data, researchers can generate new insights into disease risk, early detection, progression, treatment response, and care trajectories.

A near‐term priority is enabling longitudinal monitoring of participants receiving US Food and Drug Administration‐approved disease‐modifying therapies (DMTs), such as lecanemab and donanemab. The ADRC Program/NACC infrastructure is uniquely equipped for this role. UDS version 4 now captures standardized DMT use via RxNorm, 46 and linked RWD will provide visibility into prescribing patterns, comorbidities, and outcomes.

An April 2025 ADRC‐wide survey confirmed that over 750 clinical core participants at the time were already being treated with DMTs or enrolled in associated clinical trials. Crucially, the ADRC network will continue collecting multimodal data before, during, and after DMT exposure, enabling robust analyses of treatment durability, subgroup effects, and responder versus non‐responder profiles. These capabilities exceed the reach of traditional clinical trials and are essential for evaluating the long‐term, real‐world effectiveness and safety of emerging therapies.

The platform's expansion also strengthens interoperability with national initiatives. This includes the Alzheimer's Network for Treatment and Diagnostics (ALZ‐NET), which aims to capture RWD on AD/ADRD diagnosis and treatment. Like NACC, ALZ‐NET has adopted the OMOP CDM. Harmonizing ADRC‐linked RWD with UDS data will support development of AD/ADRD‐specific common data elements (CDEs) that bridge research and care, thereby enabling cross‐platform comparison and the generation of robust disease signatures. 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51

Bridging the NACC data platform with broader RWD efforts also supports a bidirectional validation pipeline, establishing NACC as a high‐value testbed for predictive modeling and machine learning. Researchers can train models within the NACC enclave (e.g., forecasting transitions from cognitively unimpaired to MCI) and test their generalizability using broader RWD datasets like ALZ‐NET. Conversely, models developed on large‐scale RWD can be validated against the gold‐standard cognitive assessments and diagnoses from the ADRC network.

In addition, researchers can leverage the linked RWD, UDS, imaging, fluid biomarker, genomic, and other data modalities available through NACC to conduct deeper multimodal analyses that support causal inference and mechanistic insight. This two‐way bridge enhances reproducibility, real‐world applicability, and the clinical translation of AI tools.

Beyond individual analytic use cases, the scale of the linked dataset itself represents a transformative opportunity. As this platform expands across the full ADRC Program – ultimately encompassing tens of thousands of deeply characterized participants with longitudinal clinical, utilization, and genomic data – it will provide a uniquely powerful substrate for modern AI and machine learning approaches that require large, heterogeneous, and well‐annotated datasets.

Importantly, linkage to RWD substantially enhances characterization of the existing ADRC cohort beyond what is captured in research visits alone. Claims and EHR data provide longitudinal information on healthcare utilization, comorbidities, medication exposure, and cost, as well as proxies for socioeconomic and structural factors such as insurance type, care settings, and access patterns. This enriched phenotyping enables more nuanced modeling of disease trajectories, treatment response, and real‐world outcomes.

Finally, this platform provides a critical vehicle for improving the representativeness and generalizability of AD/ADRD research. By integrating OMOP‐harmonized data, NACC can more readily build analytic bridges with external studies and health system datasets, supporting analyses across a broader range of populations and care settings. This interoperability enables examination of AD/ADRD risk, progression, and treatment effects across varied sociodemographic contexts, strengthening the robustness and external validity of research findings.

Together, these future directions position NACC and the ADRC Program as a critical infrastructure for the next era of AD/ADRD research, where RWD, precision medicine, and AI converge to accelerate progress in discovery, translation, detection, and personalized therapy.
