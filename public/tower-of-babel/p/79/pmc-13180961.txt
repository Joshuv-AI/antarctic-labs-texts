Density clustering based fast and stable satellite selection for LEO navigation
Authors: Zi Lu, Shangyue Zhang, Yuming Wang, Junting Xiong, Rui Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid deployment of mega low Earth orbit constellations provides unprecedented opportunities for global navigation, but the proliferation of visible satellites creates a critical bottleneck for real-time satellite selection. Existing methods struggle to balance computational complexity with geometric performance: exhaustive search fails to meet real-time requirements, while heuristic algorithms lack theoretical guarantees and temporal stability. This study proposes a density-clustered fast stable selection algorithm. The algorithm identifies high-density regions in celestial sphere distributions, employs hybrid geometric optimization to approximate optimal solutions within constrained search spaces, and reduces frequent handovers through the Forward Stable Strategy. Eight experiments demonstrate that the algorithm achieves near-optimal geometric precision within millisecond-level computation time. Continuous tracking experiments reveal significantly reduced satellite handover frequency, while the algorithm maintains robust performance across diverse scenarios including temporal stability, different latitudes, environmental occlusion, and parameter variations. Ablation studies confirm that the synergy between density clustering initialization and geometric optimization is critical to algorithm effectiveness. This study provides a solution that balances computational efficiency with geometric performance for real-time satellite selection in LEO navigation systems.Keywords: Satellite selection, Low Earth orbit, Geometric dilution of precision, Density clustering, Real-time navigationSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

High-precision, reliable positioning, navigation, and timing (PNT) services have become critical infrastructure for modern society, supporting a wide range of applications from autonomous driving to precision agriculture and emergency response. Traditional Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS) have served as the primary PNT provider for decades. However, GNSS signals are susceptible to occlusion and multipath effects in challenging environments such as urban canyons, indoor-adjacent areas, and high-latitude polar regions, leading to significant degradation in positioning accuracy1. This vulnerability has motivated researchers to explore robust alternative or complementary navigation solutions.

Low Earth orbit (LEO) mega-constellations, due to their lower orbital altitudes, provide stronger signal power, lower latency, and faster geometric change rates compared to GNSS. In recent years, LEO constellations represented by Starlink and OneWeb have been rapidly deployed2, primarily for global broadband communications and internet access services. Their high relative velocities generate significant Doppler shift effects, making signals of opportunity-based navigation (LEO-SoOP) an effective complementary solution to GNSS. Recent studies have achieved meter-level positioning accuracy using Starlink signals3 and validated the applicability and robustness of this technology across constellations including Orbcomm4, Iridium5, and OneWeb6, as well as in multi-constellation fusion scenarios7,8. Although LEO-SoOP positioning is not limited to Doppler observations, and related research has applied differential carrier phase4, direction of arrival9, and multi-observation fusion including pseudorange10, GDOP was selected as the evaluation metric because it decouples from specific measurement models, has high engineering maturity, and enables consistent geometric characteristic comparison and evaluation across different observation modes.

However, the rapid geometric changes that enhance positioning performance also raise a critical question: how to select the optimal subset from numerous visible LEO satellites at any given moment for navigation computation. In practical navigation systems, satellite selection must comprehensively consider multiple factors including signal quality, tracking stability, and geometric diversity. Consequently, under fixed elevation constraints, the number of visible LEO satellites at any given moment can reach tens to hundreds. However, limited by the number of hardware correlators, navigation receivers can typically track only a limited number of satellites simultaneously. Selecting a k-element subset from N candidate satellites to minimize the geometric dilution of precision (GDOP) is essentially a combinatorial optimization problem with a search space of size . As the number of visible satellites increases, the search space grows exponentially, making exhaustive search impractical for real-time LEO-SoOP applications. Meanwhile, the rapid orbital motion of LEO constellations causes continuous changes in the visible satellite set and geometric configuration11, imposing higher requirements on the real-time performance and stability of satellite selection algorithms. Therefore, developing satellite selection algorithms that achieve high precision, real-time performance, and stability within large-scale LEO constellations represents a core technical challenge.

Satellite selection research has spanned decades, with numerous methods aimed at minimizing GDOP. Early approaches exploited the maximum tetrahedron volume criterion12, although its effectiveness diminished as the number of selected satellites increased13. Shi et al.14 combined Sherman-Morrison updates with volume-based strategies to accelerate selection in 11-satellite scenarios. Quasi-optimal methods recursively eliminated redundant satellites to maintain favorable geometric configurations15. Clustering techniques have also been introduced to reduce complexity. Tang et al.16 proposed a K-means-based selection method, but its random initialization led to non-repeatable results and degraded performance under non-uniform distributions. K-means++-based improvements have also been proposed. Xu et al.17 improved the clustering strategy by prioritizing high-elevation satellites, but focusing solely on elevation while neglecting azimuth diversity failed to guarantee geometric dispersion across the celestial sphere. Wang et al.18 further proposed the FCSDp algorithm based on a DGDOP lower bound model, introducing the Hungarian algorithm for combinatorial optimization on top of K-means++ clustering, but the non-deterministic selection of the Hungarian algorithm when multiple optimal solutions exist may lead to result instability. More importantly, none of these clustering methods considered temporal stability, resulting in frequent satellite handovers.

Heuristic optimizers have also been extensively explored, including genetic algorithms19, particle swarm optimization20, and multi-strategy fusion schemes such as MSFGWO21. Among these, Lv et al. introduced the entropy weight method in MSFGWO to consider temporal continuity, alleviating the handover frequency problem to some extent. However, heuristic methods typically require extensive hyperparameter settings and are sensitive to tuning, and their stochastic search mechanisms produce non-repeatable results, which is undesirable for safety-critical navigation systems requiring determinism.

In the specific scenario of LEO-SoOP navigation, research has primarily adopted relatively ad hoc satellite selection strategies. Khalife et al.22,23 in their SOP positioning studies mainly screened satellites based on signal strength, lacking a systematic GDOP optimization framework. However, the inherent frequent satellite handover characteristics of LEO constellations24,25 increase the computational burden and energy consumption of the system, which is particularly prominent in resource-constrained navigation receivers. Therefore, in the context of large-scale LEO constellations, designing satellite selection algorithms that simultaneously achieve geometric optimality, computational real-time performance, and temporal stability has become a key challenge in advancing the engineering application of LEO-SoOP technology.

Addressing the above challenges, this paper proposes the Density-Clustered Fast Stable Selection (DC-FSS) algorithm, which systematically addresses the LEO satellite selection problem through a three-stage framework combining density clustering, geometric optimization, and temporal stability control. Unlike machine learning methods requiring training data or metaheuristic methods sensitive to parameters, DC-FSS provides a deterministic, interpretable solution with real-time performance. Standing assumptions

To establish formal guarantees for DC-FSS, three assumptions are stated that reflect standard real-time navigation practice. First, within each decision epoch, the visible-satellite set and all algorithm inputs (satellite states , receiver state , and parameters ) are fixed. This snapshot view matches how selection is executed in real time. Second, DC-FSS resolves ties deterministically: when multiple satellites achieve the same extremal value in any selection step, they are ordered by increasing satellite index and the smallest index is selected. This rule makes each selection step single-valued and the output reproducible for identical inputs. Third, a user-defined GDOP tolerance is introduced to limit the GDOP increase allowed when improving temporal stability. Smaller prioritizes geometric optimality, whereas larger enhances temporal continuity by accepting controlled GDOP degradation. These assumptions provide the foundation for subsequent problem formulation and theoretical analysis.

DC-FSS framework overview

Existing clustering methods exhibit three key limitations in LEO satellite selection. Tang et al.16 and Xu et al.17 used K-means and its variants, employing partitional clustering through iterative optimization of cluster center positions. These methods rely on random initialization, leading to non-repeatable results. K-means and K-means++ methods use azimuth and planar projection coordinates for clustering, respectively, but low-dimensional projections with Euclidean distance metrics are prone to celestial sphere angular distance distortion. Additionally, K-means requires multiple iterations to converge, increasing computational overhead. While the original Density Peaks Clustering (DPC)26 can identify cluster centers through local density, it requires manual selection from decision graphs, making it unsuitable for real-time automated systems. More importantly, existing methods operate independently at single epochs, neglecting temporal continuity and resulting in frequent satellite handovers.

Addressing these limitations, this paper proposes the Density-Clustered Fast Stable Selection (DC-FSS) method based on a key insight: LEO constellation orbital design creates non-uniform satellite density distributions on the celestial sphere, where high-density regions contain redundant geometric information. Density clustering can automatically identify these regions and select representative satellites without manual intervention or random initialization, achieving natural spatial partitioning through density distribution without iterative convergence.

DC-FSS provides three core innovations through a three-stage synergistic design. First, density-guided initialization introduces density clustering into satellite selection. It automatically identifies the natural structure of satellite distributions through local density calculation, achieving structured dimensionality reduction of the search space. Second, distance-constrained greedy selection provides determinism. It produces unique outputs given identical inputs and ensures that the angular distance between any two centers exceeds , providing geometric dispersion of celestial sphere coverage. In contrast, K-means and its variants, while improved in accuracy, still rely on random initialization. The Hungarian algorithm, though deterministic, may have multiple optimal solutions. This enables the algorithm to maximize historical satellite retention within a controlled precision loss range, superior to fixed-window methods and heuristic strategies. Stage 1: density-guided initialization

Density Peaks Clustering (DPC) identifies cluster centers through local density and distance to higher-density points. However, it requires manual selection from decision graphs, making it unsuitable for real-time automated systems. Therefore, DC-FSS adapts DPC principles to constrained k-center selection, achieving automatic center identification and spatial distribution guarantees through deterministic greedy strategies and distance suppression mechanisms.

Stage 2: hybrid optimization

Although density clustering initialization provides a spatially balanced starting point for satellite selection, density centers are not necessarily optimal in terms of GDOP. This is because local density only reflects the spatial clustering degree of satellites, while GDOP quality depends on satellite geometric configuration. Therefore, Stage 2 further minimizes the geometric dilution of precision through the synergy of intra-cluster local optimization and global greedy optimization, while maintaining spatial distribution characteristics.

Stage 3: forward stable strategy

After density clustering initialization and hybrid optimization, the algorithm can select satellite combinations with near-optimal GDOP for each epoch. However, pure GDOP-driven selection strategies have an inherent flaw: selection results between consecutive epochs may change frequently, leading to excessively high satellite handover rates. This problem is particularly prominent in Low Earth Orbit (LEO) constellations, where LEO satellites move at extremely high speeds and satellite-ground geometric configurations change rapidly. Even minor GDOP differences may trigger satellite replacements. Frequent handovers increase signal tracking overhead, reduce positioning continuity, and waste computational resources. Therefore, a controlled trade-off between positioning accuracy and selection stability is necessary.

Experimental setup

This section describes the experimental data, comparison methods, and evaluation metrics used to assess the DC-FSS satellite selection algorithm.

First, regarding the data, the experiments utilize the Starlink constellation TLE catalog from CelesTrak (epoch 2025-11-29), comprising 8,990 operational satellites. A simulation-based approach is adopted: a virtual receiver is positioned in the Beijing region (39.9N, 116.4E, altitude 50 m), with satellite orbits propagated via the SGP4 model to compute satellite ECEF positions at each epoch, from which elevation and azimuth angles relative to the receiver are derived. To meet the stable tracking requirements for LEO satellite signals such as Starlink27, a uniform 25 elevation mask is applied to filter out low-elevation satellites, thereby mitigating multipath effects and tropospheric delay impacts. After elevation filtering, the number of visible satellites ranged from 50 to 100, significantly exceeding the typical dozen satellites in traditional GNSS—a characteristic feature of LEO-SoOP scenarios. The experiments covered different time periods and latitudes to reflect the spatiotemporal variations in constellation geometry.

For performance benchmarking, DC-FSS is compared against three representative baseline methods: (1) Quasi-Optimal (QO) approach, which obtains near-optimal GDOP solutions through exhaustive search or iterative elimination in computationally tractable scenarios, serving as a geometric performance lower bound; (2) Genetic Algorithm (GA), representing typical metaheuristic global search strategies to assess the performance of stochastic optimization in high-dimensional combinatorial spaces; (3) Random selection, which uniformly samples a specified number of satellites from the candidate set, providing a performance baseline under unstructured strategies. These baselines are consistent with existing satellite selection literature, facilitating cross-study comparisons.

Regarding experimental configuration, considering both the satellite count required for positioning and typical receiver channel hardware constraints, the satellite selection scale is set to . To capture temporal variations in constellation geometry, 1,000 independent Monte Carlo scenarios are generated across different time periods, with each algorithm evaluated independently per scenario. For continuous tracking experiments, 1-hour simulations are conducted at 1 Hz sampling rate (3,600 epochs total) to assess handover frequency and computational stability under long-term operation. All algorithms are implemented and executed on identical hardware: Intel Core i7-11700K @2.30 GHz with 12 GB RAM. Statistical significance of pairwise algorithm performance differences is assessed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests or paired t-tests, with corresponding p-values and effect sizes reported.

Performance evaluation is conducted across three dimensions: geometric optimality, computational overhead, and system continuity. Geometric performance is characterized by GDOP mean, median, standard deviation, and 95% confidence intervals, with an ”approximation ratio” metric introduced to quantify each algorithm’s GDOP deviation from the exhaustive optimal value. Computational performance is measured by mean per-epoch processing latency and its variance to assess feasibility within the 10ms real-time budget. Results and discussion

This section systematically evaluates DC-FSS performance across eight dimensions: optimality verification, statistical robustness (single-constellation and multi-constellation), temporal continuity, geographic generalizability, environmental robustness, component contribution, and parameter sensitivity.

Optimality and statistical verification

Optimality verification

To rigorously quantify the geometric optimality of the algorithm, DC-FSS is compared against the global optimal solution obtained through exhaustive search under controlled conditions. Using a typical Starlink geometry as an example, exhaustive evaluation is performed on all candidate subsets under scenarios with fixed visible satellite count and selection scale . The results demonstrate that across all k values, the relative GDOP deviation of DC-FSS from the exhaustive optimal solution is maintained within approximately 3.9%–8.6%, with a mean approximation ratio of 1.055. The quasi-optimal algorithm, leveraging higher computational complexity, compresses the mean approximation ratio to 1.013. In contrast, typical greedy heuristics and random strategies not only exhibit higher mean GDOP values but also significantly increase variance, indicating frequent entrapment in local optima or even suboptimal regions within the combinatorial space.

Regarding computational overhead, the processing time of exhaustive search grows exponentially with k, rendering it infeasible for real-time constraints. In contrast, DC-FSS exhibits near-linear growth in computation time with k, consistently maintaining 3-5ms across all test cases—achieving approximately 4-7 orders of magnitude speedup over exhaustive search. Compared to other heuristic algorithms, DC-FSS also demonstrates substantial computational advantages: approximately 5–6 times faster than quasi-optimal algorithms, and approximately 2–3 times faster than genetic algorithms. This computational efficiency advantage enables DC-FSS to meet the millisecond-level latency requirements of real-time navigation systems while sacrificing only approximately 5–7% of geometric optimality.

These findings reveal that Starlink satellites exhibit relatively uniform angular distribution across the celestial sphere, naturally forming multiple density peaks. DC-FSS exploits density clustering to extract these high-density regions as representative points, then performs small-scale geometric optimization within and across clusters—effectively searching for extrema in a structured space with piecewise approximate uniformity. In other words, rather than blindly searching an entirely unstructured combinatorial space, the algorithm leverages the inherent angular uniformity characteristic of LEO constellations to significantly expand the solution space near the geometric optimum, thereby approaching the global optimum under polynomial complexity.

This robustness enabled DC-FSS to provide multiple-fold computational acceleration while sacrificing only approximately 6–7% geometric optimality, meeting the stringent millisecond-level latency requirements of real-time navigation systems.

Algorithm design verification

Ablation experiment: component synergy verification

The ablation experiment aimed to verify that DC-FSS was not a simple combination of modules, but rather a systematic adaptive design for the LEO satellite selection problem. The experiment compared five configurations: complete DC-FSS, without density clustering, without FSS mechanism, without geometric optimization, and random baseline, based on 30 Starlink epochs with selection scale k from 4 to 8.

Figure 13 presented the ablation experiment results.

Ablation experiment results. Panel (a) shows GDOP performance; panel (b) displays handover frequency; panel (c) presents computation time.

The ablation results revealed the critical role of density clustering adaptation. After removing density clustering, GDOP performance deteriorated drastically, increasing by 15,107% relative to the complete system at k = 4, and still increasing by 651% at k = 8. This catastrophic degradation stemmed from the algorithm degenerating into randomly initialized geometric optimization, frequently trapped in local optima. The original DPC used Euclidean distance and required manual center selection, making it inapplicable directly to real-time satellite selection. DC-FSS systematically adapted the density peak concept to celestial sphere geometry, adopting angular distance metrics and designing a deterministic greedy center selection algorithm. This adaptation was not a simple distance metric substitution, but rather a redesign of the center selection mechanism under spherical geometric constraints, introducing minimum angular spacing constraints to ensure geometric dispersion. The experimental results proved that this deep adaptation was critical to algorithm performance.

The design of the geometric optimization phase equally embodied systematic thinking. After removing geometric optimization, GDOP increased by 3,820% at k = 4 and 76% at k = 8. Although density centers provided spatially dispersed initial selection, they were not optimal in the GDOP sense, because local density only reflected satellite clustering degree, while GDOP quality depended on the global geometric configuration of satellites. DC-FSS achieved GDOP optimization while maintaining spatial distribution characteristics through a two-stage design of intra-cluster exhaustive search and global greedy refinement. This design leveraged the spatial region partitioning already completed by density clustering, reducing search space from exponential to polynomial level while avoiding local optima traps.

The FSS mechanism design targeted the temporal continuity problem of LEO constellations. Existing clustering methods operated independently at single epochs, leading to frequent handovers. Removal of the FSS mechanism had minimal impact on GDOP, with relative changes ranging from 0.02% to 0.69%, but handover frequency increased significantly. Taking k = 8 as an example, complete DC-FSS had 5.3 handovers, while the configuration without FSS increased to 7.0, an increase of 32.7%. The FSS mechanism achieved controllable trade-off between precision and stability through bounded GDOP loss constraints, representing the first systematic solution to temporal stability within a clustering framework.

Panel (c) demonstrated the computational efficiency trade-off. Complete DC-FSS computation time increased from 4ms to 5.4ms with k, exhibiting near-linear growth. After removing density clustering or geometric optimization, computation time decreased to 0.6-1.6ms, but GDOP performance degraded catastrophically. This indicated that DC-FSS computational overhead was a necessary cost for achieving near-optimal GDOP performance.

The ablation experiment validated the core design philosophy of DC-FSS. Through celestial sphere geometric adaptation of density clustering to achieve search space dimensionality reduction, through deterministic greedy strategy to guarantee result repeatability, and through bounded loss constraints to achieve temporal stability. The synergistic effect of the three stages produced overall advantages significantly superior to simple superposition of individual components, with removal of any component leading to significant performance degradation, proving the necessity of systematic adaptive design.

Parameter sensitivity analysis

Algorithm practicality depended not only on performance but also on parameter robustness. Metaheuristic algorithms typically required tuning multiple parameters, each with varying sensitivity to different problems, leading to deployment difficulties. DC-FSS design followed the principle of simplicity, containing only two core geometric parameters: density radius r and minimum distance factor . This experiment used 50 Starlink epochs to systematically evaluate parameter performance stability.

Figures 14 and 15 demonstrated the performance of density radius r and minimum distance factor across wide parameter ranges.

Density radius sensitivity analysis.

Minimum distance factor sensitivity analysis.

The results showed GDOP fluctuation of only 1.53% and CV of 0.40% across both parameters. This extremely low sensitivity first stemmed from the geometric characteristics of LEO constellations. The relatively uniform distribution of constellations like Starlink caused the GDOP landscape to exhibit multi-peak but relatively smooth characteristics, with small GDOP differences between local optimal solutions.

The algorithm effectively exploited this geometric characteristic through a hierarchical error correction mechanism. The density clustering phase, controlled by r and factor, was only responsible for providing coarse-grained geometric framework, rapidly identifying the main directions of satellite distribution. Smaller r generated more density peaks, larger r generated fewer density peaks; smaller factor allowed initial centers to cluster, larger factor forced centers to disperse. However, these initialization differences were systematically eliminated in subsequent optimization. Intra-cluster replacement optimization corrected density clustering deviations within local ranges, while global greedy optimization sought improvements from candidates far from current centers. This design of coarse initialization to local correction to global correction transferred parameter sensitivity from initialization to optimization phase, guaranteeing performance lower bound through rapid convergence to find optima.

From a practical deployment perspective, the extremely low parameter sensitivity enabled DC-FSS to generalize better, without requiring tedious parameter tuning for different application scenarios. It should be clarified that DC-FSS also included two user preference parameters: GDOP tolerance and handover penalty . These two parameters did not belong to the algorithm robustness examination category, but rather provided explicit trade-off control for users. Users could adjust parameters according to high-precision or high-dynamic application requirements, thereby flexibly adapting to application needs without affecting the robustness of core geometric parameters.

Discussion

The eight experiments collectively established that DC-FSS achieved near-optimal geometric accuracy, millisecond-level computation, and reduced satellite handovers across diverse constellations, geographic latitudes, and environmental conditions, three objectives that have historically been treated as competing constraints in satellite selection research. This joint achievement was not incidental but reflects a deliberate structural alignment between the algorithm design and the intrinsic geometric regularity of LEO mega-constellations.

The optimality verification and ablation study together revealed why this alignment was effective. Starlink’s orbital design produced a quasi-uniform, multi-peak density distribution on the celestial sphere, and DC-FSS was structurally matched to exploit this regularity. By transforming high-density sky regions into angularly dispersed representative points through the distance suppression mechanism, the algorithm reduced what was nominally a combinatorially explosive search into a structured subspace problem whose complexity grew polynomially with satellite count. The ablation results confirmed that neither density initialization nor geometric refinement alone was sufficient: the gain from their combination exceeded the sum of individual contributions, validating the three-stage synergy as the mechanism behind near-optimal performance.

The robustness experiments revealed a deeper property of the algorithm. In practice, navigation receivers operate over extended periods, requiring filter states to converge and tracking loops to maintain lock across successive epochs. To meet this requirement, the FSS mechanism retains previously selected satellites subject to a bounded GDOP tolerance at each epoch, yielding selection sequences that change less frequently at the cost of a controlled increase in GDOP. The robustness results indicate that this trade-off remains favorable across diverse operating conditions. Moreover, the deterministic and reproducible outputs of DC-FSS suggest that the method is well-suited for safety-critical domains such as aviation and autonomous driving, where consistent algorithmic behavior under identical inputs is required for validation and certification.

These properties suggest several practical implications for LEO navigation receiver design. The millisecond-level per-epoch latency preserves computational headroom for concurrent receiver functions such as signal tracking, integrity monitoring, and filter updates, which typically compete for the same processing budget in resource-constrained embedded platforms. The multi-constellation experiments indicated that computation time grew more slowly than the increase in satellite count, suggesting that DC-FSS is well suited to accommodate future constellation growth without necessitating fundamental algorithm redesign. While the current evaluation was based on simulation, these computational and behavioral properties provide a basis for anticipating deployment suitability in practical receiver architectures.

The latitude experiments revealed a meaningful applicability boundary for the algorithm. In sparse satellite environments such as near-polar regions, the density landscape degenerated and the preprocessing cost of density clustering was no longer offset by search-space reduction. This observation suggests that an adaptive mode-selection strategy, triggered by a satellite-count or geometry-quality threshold, could invoke a lighter-weight alternative in such degenerate scenarios while defaulting to DC-FSS under typical operating conditions, a hybrid approach worth exploring in future receiver implementations. The parameter sensitivity analysis further showed that, for most geographic regions and constellation configurations, default parameter settings transfer without requiring additional recalibration, though fine-tuning remains advisable in specialized or particularly demanding operational contexts.

More broadly, the results suggest that structured exploitation of constellation geometry can achieve competitive accuracy deterministically and efficiently, without resorting to exhaustive search or stochastic metaheuristics with extensive tuning. The structure-then-refine paradigm adopted by DC-FSS may hold applicability beyond satellite selection, extending to other real-time combinatorial resource assignment problems in navigation and communications where the problem structure exhibits exploitable geometric regularity.

Conclusion

This study presented DC-FSS for real-time satellite subset selection in large-scale LEO constellations, where geometric accuracy, computational efficiency, and temporal stability had historically been difficult to achieve simultaneously. Built on celestial sphere density modeling as prior knowledge, the three-stage framework of density clustering initialization, hybrid geometric refinement, and Forward Stable Strategy control transformed the combinatorially explosive selection task into a structured, real-time-deployable optimization, while providing an interpretable trade-off mechanism centered on GDOP tolerance to adapt to different precision and continuity requirements.

Experimental evaluation demonstrated that DC-FSS consistently delivered near-optimal GDOP within millisecond-level computation across single-constellation and multi-constellation scenarios, diverse geographic latitudes, and varying environmental conditions, while the FSS mechanism substantially reduced satellite handover frequency at bounded GDOP cost, collectively bridging the gap between instantaneous geometric performance and sustained navigational continuity. Ablation and parameter sensitivity analyses confirmed that these advantages stemmed from the synergistic interaction of all three stages and remained robust across wide parameter ranges, reducing the tuning burden at the deployment stage.

It should be noted that this paper’s evaluation was primarily based on TLE-driven geometric simulation and simplified signal assumptions, without explicitly introducing signal quality factors such as SNR, multipath, and interference in the model, nor systematically validating the impact of high-speed vehicle motion and end-to-end navigation filter closed-loop on satellite selection strategy. Future work will combine more realistic measurement error and occlusion statistical models to explore satellite selection strategies integrating signal quality, while studying mission-requirement-driven adaptive parameter setting and multi-source fusion collaborative satellite selection strategies.

Overall, DC-FSS offered a controllable and interpretable trade-off among geometric near-optimality, computational efficiency, and temporal stability, providing an algorithmically feasible framework for real-time satellite selection in next-generation LEO-SoOP navigation systems.
