Abaloparatide Increases Distal Femur Bone Mineral Density Post-Total Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: Brian Nickel, Diane Krueger, Gretta Borchardt, Samuel Mosiman, Paul A. Anderson, Neil Binkley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Osteoporosis is common in joint replacement patients and increases adverse outcome risk. Data indicate that total knee arthroplasty (TKA) causes distal femur bone loss. We hypothesized that abaloparatide (ABL) would mitigate this loss. The study’s purpose was to evaluate the effect of ABL initiated before TKA on distal femur bone mineral density (BMD) in osteoporotic patients.Methods:TKA candidates age ≥55 years were enrolled in this open-label, prospective, observational 18-month study. Those with clinical osteoporosis, defined as lowest T-score ≤ −2.5 or < −1.0 if prior low-trauma fracture, received daily ABL. TKA patients with osteopenia without fracture comprised an untreated control group. BMD was measured by dual energy x-ray absorptiometry at the L-spine, hip, radius and 2 distal femur regions of interest (ROIs) placed at 15% and 25% of femur length at screening (∼3 months pre-TKA), 1 week pre-TKA, and 6 months and 15 months post-TKA. Patient-reported outcomes were obtained at the same time points. Groups were compared at screening by t-test. Between group outcomes over time were evaluated by repeated measures mixed modelling.Results:At screening (n = 58; 29 ABL/29 control) sex, mean age and mean BMI did not differ between groups. ABL group screening BMD was lower (p ≤ 0.05) at all sites except the L-spine. ABL was dosed for a mean (SD) of 96 (60) days before TKA. Control group distal femur BMD was statistically unchanged, −1.0% and −2.4% at the 15% and 25% ROIs, respectively, at 15 months post-TKA, (p ≥ 0.34). ABL group distal femur BMD increased at the 15% and 25% ROIs and differed from control by 4.6% to 7.6% (all p < 0.01) at 6 and 15 months post-TKA. Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score for Joint Replacement demonstrated significantly worse (<0.05) function at baseline in the osteoporotic group and greater improvement over time compared with controls.Conclusion:ABL increases distal femur BMD in osteoporotic patients undergoing TKA. Unlike prior reports distal femur BMD did not decrease after TKA among controls; further study to clarify the effect of TKA on distal femur BMD is needed.Trial Registration:Clinical trial status—open-label prospective trial registered at ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04167163Level of Evidence:Level II, Therapeutic. Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is commonly performed to improve mobility and quality of life. Importantly, 60% to 80% of patients undergoing TKA have low bone mineral density (BMD) and many have osteoporosis1,2. Osteoporosis increases the risk of adverse outcomes post-TKA including implant subsidence, revision surgery, and periprosthetic fractures3. Such fractures are not rare, occurring in up to ∼5.5% of TKA patients, are challenging to repair or revise, and may have devastating consequences4-7. Notably, periprosthetic femur fractures are associated with functional decline, increased healthcare cost, frequent hospital readmission (>20% within 3 months), and >20% 1-year mortality8-11.

Reports suggest that TKA leads to distal femur bone loss12-16. This is logical, as multiple potential mechanisms present after TKA might cause bone loss including stress shielding, kinematic changes, immobilization, quadriceps muscle loss, and inflammatory changes17-19. This BMD decline is reportedly prompt and substantial; a meta-analysis found that distal femur BMD declined ∼15% within 6 months following TKA20. Importantly, osteoporosis consequences after TKA can be mitigated;21-23 osteoporosis treatment reduces periprosthetic fracture risk after total hip arthroplasty24 and bisphosphonate treatment after total hip or knee replacement reduces revision by over 50%25.

Anabolic bone-forming osteoporosis medications produce higher BMD increases and greater fracture risk reduction than achieved with antiresorptive agents26-28. Thus, anabolic therapy is preferred as initial treatment for those at very high fracture risk29. We hypothesized that treating osteoporotic patients with the bone-forming anabolic agent abaloparatide (ABL) beginning 3 months before and continuing for 15 months post-TKA would reduce distal femur bone loss measured by dual energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA). The purpose of this study was to compare distal femur BMD change in the ABL treated group with an untreated osteopenic group. Change in bone turnover markers (C-terminal telopeptide of type 1 collagen [CTX] and aminoterminal propeptide of type 1 procollagen [P1NP]) over time and patient-reported outcomes were evaluated as secondary/exploratory end points.

Methods

Study Design

In this prospective observational study, men and women age ≥ 55 years who were scheduled for elective primary TKA were considered for study participation. Their medical records were reviewed against the inclusion/exclusion criteria (Appendix 1) and study design (Supplement Figure 1). This study was performed by fellowship-trained arthroplasty surgeons at a large academic center which performs 2,500 arthroplasty surgeries annually. All TKAs used the Smith+Nephew LEGION total knee system. All patients were weight bearing as tolerated postoperatively without restrictions.

Outcomes of Interest

Study primary outcomes included change in distal femur BMD by DXA at 2 regions of interest (ROIs) (Supplement Figure 2) placed 15% and 25% of full femur length. Other outcomes of interest included bone formation and resorption markers (CTX and P1NP) and patient-reported outcomes as specified below.

BMD Measurement

Lumbar spine, bilateral femur/hip, forearm, and vertebral fracture assessment DXA were performed in routine clinical manner using a GE Healthcare iDXA (Madison, WI) densitometer. Distal femur BMD obtained as noted in Appendix 2. Scans were performed at screening, subsequently just before TKA, and at 6 and 15 months postsurgery (Supplement Figure 1).

Laboratory

A comprehensive chemistry panel, 25(OH)D, and parathyroid hormone were obtained at screening in a routine clinical manner. CTX and P1NP were batch measured using commercially available kits at screening, pre-TKA, and 6 months and 15 months post-TKA. All serum aliquots were stored at -80°C until thawed for analysis after study conclusion to minimize variability.

ABL Treatment

ABL was provided by Radius Health (Boston, MA). All treatment group participants were trained in administration at screening using the Radius instructions for use (https://radiuspharm.com/wp-content/uploads/tymlos/tymlos-instructions-for-use.pdf). All treated subjects received the approved dose, 80 mcg, subcutaneously daily. The mean ABL compliance was 96.6% (range 87%-108%).

Calcium and Vitamin D Supplementation

All subjects completed a dietary calcium intake questionnaire at screening. Those consuming <1,000 to 1,200 mg of calcium daily were advised to take a supplement to reach this target. If serum 25(OH)D was ≥30 ng/mL vitamin D supplementation was not modified, when 10.1 to 20.0 ng/mL, 4,000 IU of daily vitamin D3 was recommended; if 20.1 to 30.0 ng/mL, 2,000 IU daily was recommended.

Patient-Reported Outcome Measures

Widely used patient-reported outcome measures, Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score for Joint Replacement (KOOS JR) and VR-12 (Veterans RAND 12 Item Health Survey), were collected at screening, pre-TKA, and 6 and 12 months post-TKA30-32. At 6 and 15 months post-TKA, Forgotten Joint Survey data were collected33.

Statistical Analysis

Power calculation was based on our previous data estimating that osteoporotic patients would have an average (SD) distal femur BMD pre-TKA of 1.15 (0.2) g/cm2. We hypothesized minimal bone loss (<0.02 g/cm2) in the ABL group at the 15-month follow-up. With a SD of 0.2, follow-up data on at least 21 osteoporotic patients would provide 86% power to conclude that ABL effectively reduces distal femur bone loss to less than 0.16 g/cm2. To account for up to 26.5% attrition (as reported in the pivotal ABL trial),34 we enrolled 29 osteoporotic and 29 osteopenic subjects.

Descriptive statistics (mean [SD] or frequency [%]) were compiled. Characteristics between control and treatment groups at baseline were compared using independent t-tests for continuous variables and Fishers exact test for categorical variables because of low cell counts. Distal femur BMD change from screening to 15 months was the primary outcome measure. Repeated measures mixed models were used to evaluate raw BMD change and % BMD change with group, time, and their interaction as effects of interest. No covariates were included in the models as there were no significant demographic between group differences for which to control. No imputation was done for missing data as there were relatively few missing values, and these appeared to be missing at random. In this type of instance, the mixed model approach used provides valid estimates. In all instances, a p-value <0.05 was considered significant. All analyses were performed using SAS software.

Results

Study Cohort

Fifty-eight TKA patients participated in this study. Of 128 screened, 70 were excluded for a screen fail rate of 55%. At screening (n = 29 ABL/29 control), sex, mean (SD) age, and BMI did not differ between groups (Table I). One participant was Asian and all others were non-Hispanic White. Screening BMD was lower in the ABL group (p ≤ 0.05) at all sites except the L-spine (Table I). ABL was dosed for a mean (SD) of 96 (60) days before and 453 (20) days after TKA. Bone relevant serum chemistries and bone turnover markers did not differ between ABL and control groups (Table I). During the study, 1 participant was discontinued, 1 dropped out, and 3 were lost to follow-up; all but 1 lost to follow-up were in the ABL group.

Bone Mineral Density

In the control group, percent change in distal femur BMD was numerically, but not statistically, lower at 15 months post-TKA; −1% at the 15% ROI (p = 0.99) and −2.4% at the 25% ROI (p = 0.34; Figs. 1-A and 1-B). Distal femur BMD increased at the 15% and 25% ROIs in ABL group at 6 months post-TKA and differed from control (p < 0.01) by 5.7% and 4.6%, respectively. Similar ABL to control differences (p < 0.001) were observed at 15 months; 7.6% and 5.7% at the 15% and 25% ROIs, respectively (Figs. 1-A and 1-B). BMD increased by 6 months in the ABL group at the L-spine and differed from control by 6.0%; p < 0.0001 and by 9.1%; p < 0.0001 at 15 months post-TKA (Fig. 1-C). Similarly, BMD increased by 6 months in the ABL group at the total hip and differed from control by 4.6%; p < 0.0001 and by 6.2%; p < 0.0001 at 15 months post-TKA (Fig. 1-D).

Percent BMD change over time. Treatment with abaloparatide resulted in between group distal femur BMD change differences of 4.6% to 7.6% at 6 and 15 months at both the 15% and 25% distal femur ROIs (Figs. 1-A and 1-B). No significant BMD decline from screening was observed at either the 15% or 25% ROIs in the control group. BMD increased from screening in the ABL group and differed from control at 6 and 15 months at the lumbar spine (Fig. 1-C) and total hip (Fig. 1-D). Surgical Complications/Adverse Events

Medical and surgical adverse events were evaluated descriptively. No orthopaedic implant or surgical-related complications were noted including revision surgery or infection. Specific adverse events, including those classified as serious, and related study discontinuation are summarized in Appendix 3.

Discussion

In this study, ABL treatment initiated ∼3 months before TKA in osteoporotic patients improves BMD at the distal femur, lumbar spine, and total hip by 6 months postsurgery with improvements maintained at 15 months. BMD change differed between ABL-treated and control groups at 6 and 15 months post-TKA at all sites. BMD was unchanged in the control group and, unlike prior reports, distal femur BMD did not decline following TKA. Bone formation, as measured by P1NP, dramatically increased in the ABL-treated group by TKA surgery. In summary, ABL can be administered to osteoporotic TKA patients preoperatively resulting in significant BMD increase and improvement in PROs.

Length of preoperative medications before elective procedures remains unclear and an area in need of further study. Patient acceptance of the approach reported here, and compliance with parenteral anabolic therapy, was high; whether this generalizes to all populations and facilities is unknown.

ABL was well tolerated in this TKA cohort and, as it is an anabolic agent, is a logical preoperative bone health optimization choice in osteoporotic patients. Only 1 patient experienced hypercalcemia in this study; no other adverse events led to ABL discontinuation. Ideally, goals of preoperative medication include rapid onset of bone formation thereby facilitating increases in bone mass and potentially microarchitecture and potentially facilitating implant ingrowth. Rapid increases in bone formation and substantial BMD increases as demonstrated in this study are well documented34. Unsurprisingly, these lead to reduced fracture risk and improvements in biomechanical strength parameters35, of potential importance for orthopaedic procedures.

These findings raise fundamental questions and a potential area of further research; does osteoporosis in TKA patients affect function negatively and would osteoporosis treatment improve function? Fu, using a large database36, reported that patients with osteoporosis and newly diagnosed knee osteoarthritis who were taking bisphosphonates were 34% less likely to ever have a TKA and at 5 years were less likely to use pain medication. Regardless, arthroplasty surgeons will continue to evaluate patients with osteoporosis and end-stage knee arthritis. Increased awareness to the clinical benefit of preoperative bone optimization and/or postoperative treatment in these patients to prevent future fractures continues to be paramount.

The absence of statistically significant distal femur BMD decline was unexpected and differs from a prior meta-analysis of change after TKA20. It seems likely that the numerical BMD decline observed would become significant with a larger study number. Nonetheless, the magnitude of bone loss (∼1%-2.5%) is much less than the ∼15% previously reported. Multiple potential reasons for this result are plausible. Specifically, existing studies, date back up to 3 decades, have generally been small and used various BMD measurement techniques and a variety of ROIs for BMD measurement37-39. Small ROIs may have poor reproducibility thus confounding prior work. Moreover, both cemented and cementless implants have been studied, and some studies included cement in the ROIs40. In addition, but importantly, some existing studies did not obtain BMD measurements before TKA; it is plausible that surgical procedures and/or cement altered the BMD change reported41. Finally, it is plausible that improvements in surgical techniques, implants, and/or postoperative care may lead to less BMD loss than previously reported. Further study to clarify the effect of TKA on distal femur BMD is needed.

Study limitations include conduct at a single site with 1 manufacturer of densitometer and small sample size. This was not a true randomized control trial, rather an open-label study comparing osteoporotic patients with an osteopenic controls, it was not powered for patient-reported outcome measures (Appendix 4). Moreover, DXA distal femur ROIs remain nonstandardized; however, those chosen for this study have excellent precision and avoid hardware and cement. We believe this appropriate as randomizing osteoporotic patients to placebo is not ethically feasible. In addition, study participants are primarily female and overwhelmingly White, which limits generalizability. Study strengths include the prospective nature, rigorous DXA performance, excellent follow-up with minimal attrition, and high medication compliance.

In conclusion, ABL initiated approximately 3 months before TKA in osteoporotic patients was well tolerated and increases distal femur BMD. Further research evaluating how long ABL should be administered before TKA to optimize outcomes is indicated. Unlike prior reports, distal femur BMD did not decrease after TKA among controls; further study to clarify the effect of TKA on distal femur BMD is needed.
