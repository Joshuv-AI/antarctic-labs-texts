Optimizing initial biomechanical strength (“Time Zero”) of posterior repair in total hip arthroplasty: a biomechanical comparison of suture materials and knot configurations
Authors: Ming Chen, Xiang Liu, Dayi Chen, Xiaojing Li, Haoyuan Du, Yulong Sun, Hua Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPosterior capsule repair (PCR) is commonly performed during total hip arthroplasty (THA) via the posterolateral approach to improve postoperative stability. Because biological healing of the repaired posterior structures requires time, the repair construct must provide sufficient immediate mechanical support after surgery. Phase 1 evaluated the baseline tensile properties of three commonly used suture materials: #2 Ethibond, #0 Vicryl, and 3 − 0 PGLA. Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is a well-established procedure for relieving pain and restoring function in patients with advanced hip disease [1, 2]. With advances in bearing tribology and implant design, THA has achieved excellent long-term survivorship [3]. However, early postoperative dislocation remains an important complication because it may impair recovery, increase healthcare utilization, and require revision surgery [4–6]. Therefore, improving postoperative stability remains an important goal in arthroplasty research.

The surgical approach is closely related to postoperative stability. The posterolateral approach remains widely used because it provides good exposure, preserves the abductor mechanism, and facilitates component positioning [7]. However, this approach requires incision of the short external rotators and posterior capsule [4, 8]. The posterior capsule is an important stabilizing structure, particularly when the hip is placed in flexion, adduction, and internal rotation [9]. Loss of posterior capsular restraint may compromise stability and increase the risk of posterior subluxation or dislocation [10, 11]. Previous studies have reported dislocation rates of 4% to 9% when the posterior structures are not repaired [12–14].

Posterior capsule repair (PCR) is commonly used to improve stability after posterolateral THA. Previous clinical studies and meta-analyses suggest that PCR may reduce dislocation risk and improve postoperative stability [3, 11, 15]. However, some patients still experience early dislocation despite repair [10]. Biomechanical evidence suggests that failure may occur at the suture-knot interface rather than in the native tissue itself [16]. Therefore, the mechanical properties of the repair construct warrant biomechanical evaluation [17].

Biological healing of the repaired capsule and short external rotators requires time. During the early postoperative period, the repair site remains mechanically vulnerable because collagen remodeling and tissue integration are incomplete [18, 19]. Therefore, the suture material and knot configuration must provide sufficient immediate fixation before biological healing is established. In this context, the initial biomechanical performance of the repair construct, or “Time Zero” strength, becomes particularly important.

To investigate this issue, we used a standardized in vitro surrogate model for biomechanical tensile testing. The objectives were to (1) evaluate the baseline tensile properties of commonly used suture materials and (2) compare eight knot configurations. In Phase 2, the primary endpoint was maximum failure load, and the secondary endpoints were construct stiffness, load at 2 mm gap formation, and failure mode. Based on these findings, we aimed to identify a PCR construct with favorable initial biomechanical performance under controlled laboratory conditions.

Materials and methods

Overview of experimental design

This in vitro biomechanical testing protocol was conducted in two sequential phases to isolate specific mechanical variables. Phase 1 evaluated the baseline tensile properties of different suture materials independently, thereby establishing material-specific mechanical baselines without tissue-related confounding factors. Phase 2 used a standardized soft-tissue surrogate to compare the biomechanical performance of eight suture-knot configurations in a simulated surgical setting. All tests were performed in a climate-controlled biomechanics laboratory at 25 °C using a microcomputer-controlled universal testing machine (PDDW-2; Jinan Pinde Testing Machine Co., Ltd).

Three clinically prevalent sutures were tested to assess their intrinsic tensile performance and inherent knot security:

All specimens were manually tied into closed loops using a standardized surgical knot configuration featuring three locking throws. This technique was used to reduce the influence of operator-induced knot slippage and to allow assessment of the intrinsic tensile strength and friction-related characteristics of the materials.

Phase 2: biomechanical comparison of suture configurations

Obtaining fresh and homogeneous human cadaveric hip capsules is challenging, and such specimens may show substantial variability in tissue quality, including age-related degeneration and structural differences [20]. Therefore, porcine dermal tissue was used as a standardized surrogate model. Porcine dermis shares structural similarities with dense human capsular connective tissue and has been used in previous biomechanical studies of suture-tissue constructs [21, 22].

This study was conducted in the Biomechanics Laboratory of the Institute of Orthopedics, Shenzhen People’s Hospital. Fresh porcine dorsal skin was obtained from Guangzhou Yuexiu Agriculture and Animal Husbandry Food Technology Co., Ltd. The epidermis and dermis were separated from hair, subcutaneous fat, and other soft tissues, and then cut into 100 mm × 30 mm rectangular strips. Tissue thickness was controlled at 4.0 ± 0.2 mm using a digital caliper. All samples were bisected along the central axis to simulate a capsulotomy. The tissue edges were then approximated for suturing, and hydration was maintained with 0.9% saline spray throughout specimen preparation [23].

To reduce the influence of material-related variability during configuration testing, 3 − 0 PGLA was used uniformly across all eight groups in Phase 2. Based on the Phase 1 results, this approach allowed comparison of knot configurations under consistent material conditions.

Biomechanical testing protocol

Phase 2 constructs underwent immediate “Time Zero” tensile testing following preparation (Fig. 4). To reduce potential systematic bias related to tissue dehydration or environmental variation, the testing order of specimens across the eight groups was randomized using a computer-generated random sequence. Specimens were mounted vertically within the testing machine clamps. A 1.0 N preload was applied for 10 s to reduce viscoelastic slack and standardize the initial baseline. Quasi-static uniaxial tension was then applied at a constant displacement rate of 20 mm/min until construct failure occurred.

Core biomechanical parameters extracted from the continuous load-displacement curves included:

(1) Maximum Failure Load (Fmax, N): The absolute peak load recorded before construct failure.

(2) Load at 2 mm displacement (F(Load at 2 mm), N): The force required to induce a 2 mm gap at the repaired interface. Gap formation was tracked directly at the tissue interface using pre-marked optical reference points and manually measured with a high-precision digital caliper. Although a specific histological gap threshold for the hip capsule has not been firmly established, we adopted the 2 mm criterion based on prior repair literature and general principles of dense connective tissue healing [27].

(3) Construct Stiffness (N/mm): Calculated from the slope of the linear elastic region of the crosshead load-displacement curve generated by the testing software, reflecting the construct’s resistance to dynamic deformation.

(4) Failure Mode: Categorized macroscopically into suture breakage, knot slippage, tissue cut-through (cheese-wiring), or a mixed mode.

Due to the distinct visual differences between knot configurations, blinding the operator during specimen mounting was not feasible. However, all tensile data were captured and processed automatically by the universal testing machine software, ensuring objective data acquisition independent of operator bias.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test [28] and Levene’s test, respectively. An a priori power analysis was performed based on variance estimates obtained from preliminary testing, using the G*Power framework for one-way ANOVA [29]. At an alpha level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 80%, the minimum sample size was determined to be 7 specimens per group. Accordingly, Phase 1 included 7, 9, and 10 specimens in the three material groups, respectively, while Phase 2 included 8 specimens in each group; thus, all groups met or exceeded the predefined minimum sample size. For data meeting parametric assumptions, between-group differences were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. For data that did not meet parametric assumptions, the Kruskal–Wallis H test [30] followed by Dunn’s multiple-comparison test was used. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Phase 1: biomechanical properties of suture materials

Independent testing of the 26 suture specimens showed significant differences in ultimate tensile strength, structural stiffness, and failure behavior among the three materials (Figs. 5 and 6). Kruskal-Wallis analysis showed an overall difference in maximum failure load (P < 0.001). Post hoc testing indicated that non-absorbable #2 Ethibond (92.44 ± 8.72 N) had a higher failure load than absorbable #0 Vicryl (71.76 ± 4.38 N, P = 0.0005) and 3 − 0 PGLA (31.80 ± 2.90 N, P = 0.0003). Ethibond also showed the highest stiffness among the tested materials (Table 1). Regarding failure behavior, the PGLA group failed mainly by suture breakage, whereas Vicryl and Ethibond showed mixed failure modes. In the Vicryl group, knot slippage of up to 19 mm was observed before ultimate failure, which may reflect lower knot security under the present testing conditions.

Phase 2: biomechanical comparison of suture configurations

Phase 2 testing in the porcine surrogate model showed clear differences in biomechanical performance among the tested configurations (Table 2). Among the single-strand baseline groups, the Figure-of-8 technique (Group B) showed a higher maximum failure load than the simple interrupted technique (Group A) (60.87 N vs. 54.19 N) [31]. The two double-strand configurations (Groups C and D) showed higher failure loads than the single-strand groups. Although the absolute difference in maximum failure load between the two double-strand knots was small (3.37 N), differences were observed in secondary mechanical parameters. The Nice knot showed the highest construct stiffness among all groups (12.59 ± 1.21 N/mm, 95% CI: 11.58 to 13.60 N/mm), which was higher than that of the MRH knot (9.91 ± 0.92 N/mm, 95% CI: 9.14 to 10.68 N/mm; P < 0.05) (Fig. 7). Complex single-strand sliding knots, including the Duncan loop and SMC knot, did not show a clear mechanical advantage over simple interrupted suturing in this open-model setting. For the high-load double-strand configurations (Nice knot and MRH knot), failure most commonly occurred as suture breakage at the knot interface rather than tissue cut-through.

Discussion

Posterior capsule repair (PCR) is commonly used to improve stability after posterolateral THA [15]. Because biological healing of the repaired capsule requires several weeks, the early postoperative period remains mechanically vulnerable. In our in vitro model, the combination of #2 Ethibond and a double-strand Nice knot showed a favorable overall initial mechanical profile among the tested constructs.

Phase 1 data suggest that #2 non-absorbable braided polyester (Ethibond) may provide more favorable baseline mechanical properties for PCR in THA [32]. The concern with absorbable sutures is not limited to baseline tensile strength. Their hydrolytic degradation may reduce mechanical support during the healing phase of the posterior capsule [18, 19, 32]. In our tests, Vicryl also showed marked knot slippage. Because gap formation may interfere with tissue healing [33], this finding may be relevant when considering early construct stability.

The favorable biomechanical performance of the Nice knot observed in Phase 2 may be related to its double-strand, sliding, self-locking configuration [17, 25]. Under tension, this configuration may increase compression between the suture limbs and thereby reduce slippage. Compared with conventional static knots, this mechanism may contribute to greater resistance to elongation and higher construct stiffness in the present testing model.

Construct stiffness reflects resistance to elastic deformation. The double-strand architecture of the Nice knot may reduce elongation under tensile loading compared with single-strand constructs. In the present model, this feature was associated with a higher stiffness value and a higher Load at 2 mm displacement, suggesting better resistance to gap formation under controlled testing conditions.

Tissue cut-through (“cheese-wiring”) is a recognized mode of failure in capsular or tendon repair under tensile loading [34, 35]. One possible explanation is that a larger contact area between the suture and tissue may reduce local stress concentration. In this context, the Figure-of-8 and Nice knot configurations may distribute force over a broader area than simple single-strand constructs, which may contribute to improved resistance to tissue cut-through.

This study suggests that the assumption that more complex knotting techniques necessarily provide superior mechanical strength may not apply in the open THA setting. Arthroscopic sliding knots such as the SMC knot and Duncan loop were developed for use in confined arthroscopic spaces [36]. In the open THA setting, however, sustained and uniform axial tension on the sliding limb may be more difficult to maintain. This may lead to premature locking and reduce the expected mechanical advantage of these knot configurations. In our model, these knots did not outperform simple interrupted suturing.

From a biomechanical perspective, the combination of #2 Ethibond and the Nice knot showed favorable initial fixation properties in this experimental model. Given that load sharing across sutures may be uneven in vivo, constructs with higher individual failure loads may offer potential mechanical advantages. However, these findings should be interpreted within the limitations of this in vitro study and should not be taken as direct evidence of clinical superiority.

This study has several methodological limitations. First, Phase 2 used healthy porcine dermis as a surrogate model to reduce inter-specimen variability [21, 22]. However, human capsular tissue encountered during primary THA is often derived from older patients with degenerative changes [37], and the absolute failure loads observed in this model may therefore differ from those in clinical tissue. Second, construct strength was assessed using quasi-static uniaxial loading, which does not fully reproduce the multidirectional and cyclic forces acting on the posterior capsule in vivo. Third, construct stiffness was calculated based on crosshead displacement without compliance correction; accordingly, the reported values reflect the stiffness of the overall testing system rather than pure interfacial tissue stiffness. Because this was an in vitro biomechanical study, the findings should be interpreted as experimental evidence, and direct clinical extrapolation should be made with caution. Although the double-strand constructs showed higher initial strength in this setting, biological healing, surgical technique, and patient-related factors are also likely to influence clinical outcomes [9]. Accordingly, these bench findings may inform construct selection, but they do not replace clinical evidence or established postoperative precautions.

Conclusions

In this in vitro surrogate model, absorbable sutures and traditional static knots showed lower initial fixation strength than double-strand configurations. Under controlled laboratory conditions, the combination of #2 Ethibond and a double-strand Nice knot was associated with higher initial biomechanical strength and construct stiffness than the other tested methods. These findings provide experimental support for construct selection in posterior capsule repair, but further validation in cadaveric and clinical studies is needed.
