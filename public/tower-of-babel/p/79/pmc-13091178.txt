Medial Patellofemoral Ligament Reconstruction Using Autograft vs Allograft in Pediatric Patients: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Ethan Liu, Minseung Kang, Joshua T. Bram, Alexandra M. Olson, Julie Reed, Christopher J. DeFrancesco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMedial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction (MPFLR) is the most common treatment option for pediatric patients with recurrent patellar instability. However, there is a lack of consensus regarding the superiority of allograft or autograft MPFLR for young patients. This systematic review aims to evaluate the clinical outcomes of allograft and autograft MPFLR in the pediatric and adolescent population.MethodsStudies comparing allograft and autograft MPFLR in pediatric patients were identified from PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library. Inclusion criteria included studies: 1) evaluating pediatric patients (≤21 years of age), 2) comparing clinical outcomes between MPFLR allograft and autograft, 3) published after January 1, 2000, and 4) published in English. Exclusion criteria included 1) meta-analysis, chapters, conferences, abstracts, case reports, editorials, or reviews, 2) studies performed in animal models, and 3) studies lacking direct comparisons between allograft and autograft. Nonrandomized studies were assessed for quality using the methodological index for nonrandomized studies, while randomized studies were assessed using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool. A meta-analysis using the random-effects generalized linear mixed model was applied to compare the failure rates between the allograft and autograft treatment groups.ResultsFour studies met the final inclusion criteria (2 level 3 retrospective comparative studies, 1 level 4 case series, and 1 level 2 multicenter randomized controlled trial). The aggregate data from all 4 studies displayed a higher rate of failure and higher odds of failure in the autograft group (P = .004). Postoperative Kujala scores were high for both autograft and allograft groups. While 1 study found greater Kujala scores in the allograft treatment group, another study reported greater scores in the autograft treatment group. The remaining 2 studies did not find significant differences in the Kujala scores between groups.ConclusionAmong pediatric patients undergoing MPFLR, the use of autograft may be associated with higher failure rates compared to the use of allograft. Patient-reported outcomes improved with both autograft and allograft MPFLR, with no clear difference between the groups. Future studies should evaluate why autograft MPFLR may be associated with a higher risk of recurrent instability.Key Concepts(1)While graft choice for medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction (MPFLR) in the general population has been studied in the past, there is a gap in research for pediatric MPFLR, which this study aims to address.(2)This review found that outcomes were generally positive for MPFLR, whether allograft or autograft was used. However, autograft MPFLR had a higher rate of graft failure.(3)Given the synthesized evidence and the lack of donor site morbidity associated with the use of allograft, we recommend that pediatric sports surgeons consider using allograft when performing MPFLR.Level of EvidenceIV, Systematic Review

Introduction

Patellofemoral instability (PFI) is a common condition affecting pediatric and adolescent patients, with an incidence of 29 per 100,000 in individuals aged 10–17 years [1]. Conservative treatment remains the standard of care for first-time dislocations. However, the risk of recurrent instability is highly associated with young age, with a classic study by Cash and Hughston showing a 60% recurrence rate in patients aged 11–14 years at the time of first-time dislocation [2]. In the absence of bony deformity (e.g. genu valgum or severe trochlear dysplasia), surgical intervention with a medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) reconstruction is the treatment of choice for the patient with multiple prior instability events. In the first-time dislocator with a loose body, surgical intervention with MPFL reconstruction (MPFLR) is typically recommended in conjunction with procedures such as fragment removal, microfracture, and/or fragment fixation, given a six-fold increase in recurrent instability with loose-body removal alone [3,4]. MPFLR is also increasingly recommended for the first-time dislocator with epidemiological and/or anatomical factors putting the patient at high risk of recurrence [5,6]. A multitude of MPFLR techniques have been described including free-graft reconstructions using single-limb or double-limb patterns, reconstructions that include an attachment from the femoral footprint of the MPFL complex (MPFLC) to the distal quadriceps tendon (reconstituting the medial quadriceps tendon-femoral ligament), the quadriceps turndown technique, and more [[7], [8], [9]].

While the use of autograft is associated with lower failure rates in anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction for young patients [10], there is no clear consensus regarding the superiority of allograft or autograft in MPFLR, particularly among pediatric patients. Proponents of allograft may argue that the inferiority of allograft in ACL reconstruction should not be generalized to MPFLR as there are key anatomic and structural differences between the native ACL and the MPFL. Prior systematic reviews comparing the outcomes of MPFLR in patients of all ages based upon graft choice (autograft vs allograft) note similar clinical improvements but a higher rate of graft failure in patients undergoing MPFLR with autograft [11]. However, since the incidence of both primary and recurrent PFI is significantly higher in younger patients, an updated review focusing exclusively on pediatric and adolescent patients is warranted. The current review aimed to 1) provide an overview of clinical outcomes after MPFLR using autograft or allograft in pediatric and adolescent patients and 2) compare these outcomes by graft choice (autograft vs allograft). The results may guide clinicians in choosing an appropriate graft when performing MPFLR in young patients.

Methods

This study was exempt from institutional review board oversight. Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) methodology was used on November 11th, 2025, to search PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library for studies comparing outcomes of allograft and autograft MPFLR in pediatric patients. A Boolean search syntax for “pediatric” patients with “patellofemoral instability” undergoing “medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction” using “allograft” or “autograft” was used. The specific search strategies can be found in Supplement 1. Articles were imported into an online systematic review platform (Covidence systematic review software, Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia).

Identified studies were reviewed by 2 independent reviewers (E.L., M.K.). Study titles and abstracts were reviewed first for potential inclusion. Articles advanced through title/abstract review underwent a full-text review by the same reviewers. The inclusion criteria used were studies 1) evaluating pediatric patients (≤21 years of age), 2) reporting on the clinical outcomes of MPFL reconstructions with a comparison between autografts and allografts, 3) from the past 25 years (after January 1, 2000), and 4) that were published in English. Exclusion criteria included 1) meta-analysis, chapters, conference abstracts, case reports, editorials, or reviews, 2) studies performed in animal models, and 3) those without a direct comparison between allograft and autograft. In cases of disagreement at the title/abstract and full-text stages, a third reviewer (J.T.B.) served as the tiebreaker.

Data were subsequently extracted from each of the included studies for synthesis. In addition to demographic variables and follow-up time, data pertaining to surgical techniques were recorded. This included the procedure performed (eg MPFLR vs MPFL/MQTFL), graft source and size, concomitant procedures, and operative times. Clinical outcomes assessed were proportions of individuals with treatment failures as defined by the studies (ie recurrent instability), complications, and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) when available.

Study methodology assessment

The methodological index for nonrandomized studies (MINORS) was used to assess the quality of nonrandomized studies. Scores were assigned by 2 reviewers (E.L., M.K.), with discrepancies resolved by a third reviewer (J.T.B.). The MINORS has 12 criteria, and each criterion is scored out of two points for a total maximum score of 24 points [12]. To evaluate the 1 included randomized controlled trial, version two of the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials was employed [13].

Statistical analysis

A random-effects generalized linear mixed model meta-analysis using the meta package in R version 4.5.1 (R Core Team 2025, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was created to compare failure rates between the allograft and autograft groups. Results were reported as the pooled failure rates, odds ratio of graft failure between groups, and I2 statistic to evaluate heterogeneity across the included studies. The P-values ≤0.05 defined statistical significance.

Study methodology assessment

The three included nonrandomized articles were of moderate quality (scoring 15–22 points on the MINORS scale) [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18]], with the study by Hendawi et al. receiving a score of 17/24, and the articles by Kumar et al. and Parikh et al. receiving scores of 19/24 and 16/24, respectively.

The study by Matuszewski et al.–the single randomized study included–showed some concerns for risk of bias in domain 2 and four of the Cochrane risk-of-bias 2.0 tool due to the lack of blinding of the patients and the outcome assessors, respectively. The study demonstrated an adequate randomization process, sufficient outcome data availability, and a lack of selective reporting, showing a low risk of bias in the 3 other domains.

Patient demographics

Across all studies, there were no reported differences in baseline characteristics between groups [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. Hendawi et al. Kumar et al., and Matuszewski et al. found no significant differences between the allograft and autograft treatment groups in terms of patient age or sex. Hendawi et al. additionally reported no differences in the proportion of acute vs chronic patellar dislocations between the 2 groups, and Matuszewski et al. reported no differences in the mean number of patellar dislocations between the 2 groups. Hendawi et al. and Matuszewski et al. both found no difference in the proportion of left vs right knee operations between the 2 treatment groups. Matuszewski et al. and Kumar et al. provided information on patients’ involvement in sports, but the studies did not evaluate the distribution of sport participation between the 2 treatment groups. Though Parikh et al. assessed hypermobility via the Beighton score in the included patients, the authors did not stratify this trait between the 2 treatment groups.

Radiographic data

Hendawi et al. and Parikh et al. measured the tibial tubercle-trochlear groove distance (TT-TG) on preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies and found no significant difference between the successful and failed grafts. Additionally, Parikh et al. reported no significant difference in the proportion of high-grade trochlear dysplasia between the successful and failed groups but reported significantly higher preoperative patellar height (P = .0002) and patellar tilt (P = .002) in the failed grafts compared to the successful grafts. Matuszewski et al. measured preoperative TT-TG and preoperative and postoperative patellar tilt angle using MRI, and they found no significant difference between the autograft and allograft treatment groups. They also reported that both treatment groups demonstrated significant improvement to patellar tilt angle after surgery (P < .001). None of the included studies compared physeal status between the 2 treatment groups. Kumar et al. did not report radiographic data.

Follow-up

Only the study by Kumar et al. delineated follow-up time by graft type, reporting a significant difference between groups (allograft = 3.1 years, autograft = 5.7 years, P < .001). Hendawi et al. did not report a minimum follow-up in their study, although Kujala scores were collected at least >6 months postoperatively. The studies by Parikh et al. (mean follow-up of 7.2 years) and Matuszewski et al. (mean follow-up of 2 years) reported overall follow-up with no distinction between the 2 treatment groups.

Surgical techniques

In all 4 studies, a diagnostic arthroscopy was routinely performed prior to the MPFL reconstruction procedure [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. Hendawi et al. used allograft or autograft gracilis tendon (as determined by the surgeon) in both treatment groups, with average graft size of 5.7 mm and 5.3 mm for the allograft and autograft groups, respectively (P < .001). They performed their MPFLR with a tunnel in the patella and an interference screw on the femur. Graft choice was determined by patient preference in the study according to Kumar et al. using either a gracilis or semitendinosus tendon in the allograft group (goal of either 4–4.5 mm single or 5–5.5 mm doubled) or the gracilis tendon in the autograft group (graft size not provided). The surgical technique varied across the study, initially utilizing interference screws to secure the graft to the femur and 2 suture anchors on the patella. Later, they adopted an alternative approach, first securing the graft to the patella with an interference screw before fixing the graft to the femur with another interference screw.

Parikh et al. performed their MPFLR by drilling a single tunnel into the patella and securing the graft to the femur using an interference screw. In the allograft treatment group, unspecified hamstring tendons were used, whereas the autografts were either gracilis (17/31), semitendinosus (13/31), or quadriceps (1/31). Graft choice was determined by shared decision-making between the patient, the family, and the surgeon. Matuszewski et al. randomized graft choice, using fascia lata tendon (12 mm wide x 2 mm thick x 5–7 cm long strip) for the allograft group and gracilis tendon (5 mm thick x 8–10 cm long V-shaped grafts) for the autograft group. In the allograft group, the graft was secured to the patella with sutures passed through small bone tunnels and to the femur with a titanium anchor. In the autograft group, they similarly drilled a bone tunnel into the patella, but the graft was passed through this tunnel and then secured to the femur with an interference screw. Concomitant procedures

Performance of concomitant procedures was reported inconsistently across the included studies. Hendawi et al. reported various additional secondary procedures, including chondroplasty, loose body removal, microfracture, and meniscal repair, without specifically noting the distribution of these secondary procedures between the allograft and autograft treatment groups. Prior to MPFL reconstruction, Kumar et al. performed a tibial tubercle osteotomy (TTO) for skeletally mature patients with a tibial tubercle-trochlear groove (TT-TG) distance of greater than 20 mm (3 of 36 and 4 of 23 in the allograft and autograft groups, respectively). They also reported similar proportions of loose body removals (allograft = 5/36, autograft = 6/23) and lateral releases (allograft = 7/36, autograft = 2/23) between the groups. Parikh et al. reported a number of concomitant procedures without specifying the distribution of these procedures between the allograft and autograft treatment groups including 13 patellar chondroplasties, 5 lateral femoral condyle chondroplasty, 1 patellar osteochondral fracture fixation, 1 removal of hardware from previous surgery, 1 lateral retinacular lengthening, and 1 lateral meniscus debridement (20/31 patients total). Matuszewski et al. performed 1 medial meniscus tear treatment and 3 microfractures for grade II cartilage damage in the allograft group, as well as 3 microfractures for grade II cartilage damage in the autograft group.

Operative time and cost

Two studies compared the mean or median operation time between the allograft and autograft groups [15,18]. Hendawi et al. reported an average of 97.3 min and 134.5 min for the allograft and autograft operations, respectively (P < .001). Similarly, Matuszewski et al. reported decreased surgical time with use of an allograft (median 95 min) vs an autograft (median 115 min), with no statistical comparison made. Hendawi et al. also performed a cost analysis between the use of allograft and autograft. The longer operating time in the autograft group increased costs by approximately $445, whereas the allografts were estimated to cost $1,058 on average. Reoperation costs were reported as $34,740 to the payer and $4,500 to the hospital. Due to the significant difference in reoperation rates between the allograft and autograft groups, it was determined that use of an autograft was actually more costly than the use of an allograft.

Clinical failure meta-analysis

The definition of failure varied across the included studies. Hendawi et al. defined clinical failure as graft failure with recurrent patellar stability and the need for revision MPFL reconstruction. The authors reported zero graft failures in the allograft group and 6 graft failures (28.6%) in the autograft group (P = .004), occurring at a mean 13.8 months post-operatively. Kumar et al. similarly defined graft failure as recurrent patellar instability, observing 3 allograft (8.3%) and 6 autograft (26.1%) failures (P = .064). The definition of graft failure with the need for any subsequent intervention due to recurrent instability was used by Parikh et al. They reported 2 allograft (12.5%) and 7 autograft (22.6%) failures (P = .69). Among the autograft failures, six of 17 knees treated with the gracilis tendon failed, whereas none of the 13 knees treated with the semitendinosus graft failed (P = .02). Matuszewski et al. did not define failure but reported 1 recurrent dislocation event in the allograft treatment group due to loosening of a suture anchor. Using a random-effects generalized linear mixed model, the overall pooled failure rates for autograft and allograft were 17.7% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 8.0–34.5%) and 5.4 (95% CI: 2.3–12.4%), respectively. There were higher odds of graft failure with autograft versus allograft (odds ratio [OR]: 4.38, 95% CI: 1.6–11.9, P = .004, see Fig. 2. Patient reported outcome measures

PROM scores increased consistently after surgery across all studies evaluated for both allograft and autograft. Hendawi et al. reported greater postoperative Kujala scores for the allograft treatment group (92.1 vs 80.3, P = .003). On the other hand, Kumar et al. reported greater postoperative Kujala scores for the autograft group (97.4 vs 92.7, P = .02) but observed no differences in preoperative or 6-week, 3-month, or latest follow-up pain scores. Parikh et al. and Matuszewski et al. found no differences between the Kujala scores for patients treated with allograft or autograft. Parikh et al. also found no difference in any other PROM evaluated (pediatric version of the International Knee Documentation Committee [Pedi-IKDC], Hospital for Special Surgery Pediatric Functional Activity Brief Scale [HSS Pedi-FABS], Banff Patellofemoral Instability Instrument [BPII], Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score [KOOS]-pain score, KOOS-symptoms score, KOOS-activities of daily living score, KOOS-sport score, and the KOOS quality of life score) between the 2 treatment groups. Complications

Parikh et al. reported 9 unplanned surgeries after MPFLR due to complications other than recurrent instability, which included 1 patient with deep venous thrombosis, wound dehiscence, and arthrofibrosis necessitating wound closure and manipulation under anesthesia (autograft), 5 patients with pain that underwent patellar chondroplasty (all autograft), 1 patient with a postoperative deformity that required reversal of the TTO (allograft), 1 patient who underwent femur interference screw removal for persistent pain (allograft), and 1 individual with pain that had a saphenous neuroma excision (autograft). Matuszewski et al. reported a single complication from the allograft treatment group, in which a patient experienced a wound infection treated with oral antibiotics. Hendawi et al. and Kumar et al. did not report any complications including infections or stiffness.

Discussion

While the use of autograft is clearly superior in ACLR for young patients due to lower rates of graft failure and improved knee stability [[19], [20]], [[19], [20]] the choice is not as clear for pediatric MPFLR. Some surgeons may prefer the use of autograft for MPFLR for several reasons, including ease of supply, avoiding the cost of an allograft, circumventing any concerns regarding immunologic reaction or disease transmission, and more. On the other hand, allograft tissue may be preferred by others because it avoids donor site morbidity, streamlines the surgical encounter, and can provide a higher volume of collagen than a native tendon might. This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted to inform this discussion in pediatric patients and perhaps add some clarity for surgeons.

The results of the current work indicate that the use of either autograft or allograft tissue for MPFLR is safe and effective in treating the pediatric patient with PFI. Although postoperative PROMs were encouraging for both the autograft and allograft groups, recurrent instability was more common in patients treated with autograft (17.7% vs 5.4%, OR = 4.38). Additionally, the use of allograft was associated with both time and overall cost savings. The synthesized findings, therefore, suggest that surgeons should strongly consider the routine use of allograft for MPFLR in pediatric patients with PFI.

While small differences in Kujala scores favoring autograft have been reported in adult populations [21], the current study found mixed data regarding the superiority of Kujala scores between autograft and allograft groups for pediatric patients. Although, Hendawi et al. noted higher post-operative Kujala scores for patients treated with allograft (92.1 vs 80.3), Kumar et al. reported higher scores (97.4 vs 92.7) with autograft reconstructions. Meanwhile, Matuszewski et al. and Parikh et al. found no difference in postoperative Kujala scores or other PROMs between treatment groups. Overall, the findings of this study in pediatric patients are consistent with the results of prior literature in adult populations after MPFLR. Autograft MPFLR exhibits higher failure rates compared with allograft reconstructions, although graft choice does not greatly influence postoperative PROMs, which are positive with both allograft and autograft [11,21].

The authors recognize that there are several factors other than graft choice that influence failure rates and outcomes after MPFLR. In this review, overall graft diameter may have been a confounding factor in assessing graft failure. In particular, Hendawi et al. reported that autograft tended to be of smaller diameter. In addition, the studies by Parikh et al. and Kumar et al. did not include data about the autograft size, which may have influenced graft failure rates. Supporting this idea, Parikh et al. even noted that there was a higher risk of failure among autograft MPFLR cases when the gracilis tendon (typically smaller) was used rather than the semitendinosus tendon. It is also possible that autograft tendon was associated with higher rates of failure in this meta-analysis because the included autograft patients had a high prevalence of hypermobility, with collagen disorders perhaps making these patients’ autografts suboptimal. Additionally, the authors recognize that surgical technique varied substantially in the included cases, and it is possible that technical variations in autograft surgeries were partly to blame for the higher failure rate observed.

There are a number of limitations to the current study. First, there was no shared definition of graft failure, and follow-up time was not consistent across the studies. This latter finding may have led to missed failures in patients lost to follow-up, though failures were reported to occur at >1 year follow-up (eg mean 13.8 months in Hendawi et al.). Second, concomitant procedures were reported by all included studies, but the distribution of these interventions across the 2 groups was not reported in the studies by Hendawi et al. and Parikh et al. Additionally, we specifically assessed whether the higher failure rate with autograft MPFLR was driven by the results of a single study showing a significant difference (Hendawi et al.). Post-hoc analysis showed that, if that study were to be removed from the review, the reported odds ratio would be adjusted to 2.36 for autograft failure relative to allograft failure (P = .12). While the comparison would lose statistical significance, these results are expected, given the low number of included studies, and the directionality of results remains consistent. The authors also recognize that the results of the current review are based on findings from 4 studies of varying design and quality including 2 level 3 retrospective comparative studies [15,16], 1 level 2 multicenter randomized controlled trial [18], and 1 level 4 case series [17]. Lastly, several confounding factors may have contributed to the difference in failure rates noted. Baseline characteristic variables were inconsistently reported and compared between autograft and allograft groups, including an overall lack of reporting on physeal status and other radiographic parameters such as trochlear morphology, TT-TG (<20 mm in all patients in the study by Matuszewski et al.), and patella alta. Future prospective work or retrospective studies with propensity score matching may shed further light on what factors make allograft or autograft the better choice in particular patients.

In summary, this review showed no clear advantage in failure rate or postoperative PROMs to justify the use of autograft over allograft in MPFLR for pediatric patients with PFI. In addition to decreased operative time and overall cost, allograft reconstruction offers the advantage of no donor-site morbidity. Given that even careful hamstring harvesting is associated with a >40% rate of injury to branches of the saphenous nerve [22] and the possibility of associated wound problems related to harvest, this advantage should not be overlooked. This study showed similar postoperative PROMs, lower failure rates, and shorter operative time with the use of allograft (vs autograft) in MPFLR for pediatric patients, supporting the routine use of allograft in these operations.

Conclusion

Among pediatric patients undergoing MPFLR procedure, the use of autograft was associated with higher failure rates compared to the use of allograft in this small systematic sample of studies. Patient-reported outcomes improved with both autograft and allograft MPFLR, with no clear difference between the groups. Future studies should evaluate why autograft MPFLR may be associated with a higher risk of recurrent instability. Given the lack of evidence showing the superiority of autograft, the use of allograft may be preferable in MPFLR for pediatric patients with PFI, given its avoidance of donor-site morbidity, though future prospective studies are needed.
