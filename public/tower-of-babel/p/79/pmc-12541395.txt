Inequality in air pollution–attributable mortality by income level between and within countries
Authors: Chenliang Tao, Yuqiang Zhang, Drew Shindell, Hongliang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceAir pollution is one of the leading causes of adverse health impacts worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries with high exposure levels. However, impacts within countries are also uneven, being influenced by local factors, including exposures. This paper assesses premature mortality disparities across different income groups between and within countries. We provide evidence that aging populations exacerbate health risks in countries with low poverty rate, surpassing those in poorer countries by some measures. Moreover, health burdens are concentrated not only in affluent urban areas globally but also among poorer rural populations in low-income countries. These findings can help guide more equitable outcomes for currently underserved populations.

Ambient air pollution has emerged as one of the most significant environmental health challenges globally. In 2019, it contributed to approximately 4 to 8 million premature deaths (1, 2), yet the burden is unevenly distributed across different regions and demographic groups. Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) typically bear disproportionate air pollution exposure risks, largely due to their reliance on less advanced energy and urban transport technologies, and rapidly expanding industrial sectors (3). Additionally, emission associated with the production of consumer goods for international trade, a significant proportion of which occurs in LMICs, effectively shifts some of the health burden of consumption onto economically disadvantaged countries (4–6). This, compounded by less stringent emissions regulations in these regions, enhances the associated health risks (7, 8). As countries implement diverse socioeconomic development and air quality abatement measures, failure to recognize this inequality can lead policymakers to misidentify the income groups bearing the greatest health burdens, potentially exacerbating inequalities.

While substantial progress has been made in understanding inequalities in pollutant exposures at national scales, critical gaps persist in characterizing how these risks vary among different socioeconomic groups within individual countries. Early insights, drawn largely from studies in high-income settings, have established a conventional assumption that impoverished populations tend to be more exposed to ambient pollutants (9–13). In the United States, for example, inequality patterns often stem from historical and ongoing racial discrimination and class-based market dynamics (14–17). Yet this narrative does not hold universally. Recent analyses reveal that many comparatively affluent urban areas in LMICs experience PM2.5 burdens comparable to or even exceeding those of their poorer counterparts (18, 19). The lack of systematic characterization of the heterogeneous relationship between income level and pollution exposure risk within and across countries hinders effective policymaking to reduce vulnerability to this exposure.

Moreover, a predominant focus on broad urban hotspots has overshadowed critical nuances in rural or transitional zones, where heavy reliance on biomass fuels, frequent wildfires, and limited healthcare infrastructure can yield unexpectedly high pollution exposure and impact inequalities (20–22). Existing studies have also centered primarily on PM2.5 concentration inequality to advance our understanding, often neglecting other pollutants and disease-specific vulnerabilities. For instance, while numerous studies have confirmed that impoverished populations face greater health risks from air pollution, a comprehensive analysis noted that O3 did not follow the same pattern as other pollutants in the United States (13). Sparse fine-scale socioeconomic and multipollutant data, coupled with a dearth of granular analyses linking distinct pollutants to specific diseases, limit our ability to identify how economic inequities, healthcare accessibility, and cross-boundary pollution collectively shape global vulnerability. These constraints underscore a need to integrate more granular datasets and develop refined frameworks that capture pollutant- and disease-specific disparities within complex socioeconomic landscapes.

In this study, we quantify the distribution of premature mortality attributable to long-term exposures to air pollutants [O3, PM2.5, and formaldehyde (HCHO)] based on global high-resolution pollutant estimates obtained from satellite and in situ observations using a deep learning model. We combine these estimates of health burdens with subnational survey poverty data and spatially resolved Gross Domestic Product (GDP) to investigate the interaction between premature mortality and poverty. Health inequality was assessed as the extent to which socioeconomic groups disproportionately bear pollution-related mortality burdens across subnational regions and urban–rural areas. Our results reconcile the heterogeneous results from recent literature, clarifying that environmental health burdens are neither uniformly concentrated among the poorest nor solely confined to densely populated urban cores. Instead, a spectrum of vulnerabilities emerges from the interplay between local geography, economic development patterns, infrastructural disparities, and distinct pollution exposures. By providing a nuanced understanding of the pollutant-specific and disease-specific dimensions of environmental impacts, our work points toward the importance of fine-grained, regionally tailored policies.

Results

Subnational Premature Mortality and Poverty.

The combination of poverty level and air pollution mortality estimations enables us to reveal the vulnerability of the affected population. Fig. 1 illustrates the bivariate spatial distribution of premature mortality attributable to ambient air pollution, juxtaposed with subnational poverty rates, defined as populations living below the $3.65/d poverty threshold—a benchmark reflecting typical national poverty lines in lower- and middle-income countries. The results indicate that Southern Asia, parts of Amazonia, and Africa face higher vulnerability from air pollutants and poverty than other regions.

The statistical analyses show that 0.93, 0.34, and 0.14 million deaths of persons living on less than $3.65 per day are attributed to long-term unsafe PM2.5, O3, and HCHO exposure in 2019, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). For individuals experiencing extreme poverty, defined as those with an income below $2.15 per day, the respective estimates were 0.27, 0.11, and 0.04 million. About 6% of deaths attributed to unsafe air pollution occur among people in extreme poverty, below the 9% share of the global population classified as extremely poor. Under the assumption that poor and nonpoor individuals within the same country are equally susceptible to air pollution, we interpret this disparity as evidence that about one-third of extremely poor people are not geographically concentrated in regions with the highest mortality from outdoor air pollution. While the $2.15 threshold identifies individuals in extreme poverty, it is crucial to acknowledge that people with incomes slightly above this threshold, who remain economically vulnerable, are often classified as poor and face similar health risks from air pollution exposure. When we consider the higher poverty threshold of $6.85/d, which is representative of upper-middle-income countries, the attributable deaths increase significantly to approximately 2.17 million (PM2.5), 0.73 million (O3), and 0.31 million (HCHO). By overlaying poverty metrics onto air pollution mortality estimates, we effectively capture one of the ways in which poverty interacts with the health impacts of long-term exposure to air pollutants.

Of 0.42 million deaths in extreme poverty, roughly two-thirds (66%) are in India, Nigeria, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). This aligns with a previous study of PM2.5 exposure and poverty, which also identified these countries as having among the highest exposure and poverty level (7). However, the proportion of deaths in these countries in our analysis exceeds their proportion of the global exposed population (48.6%) by 17 percentage points, a disparity that suggests that the true health burden is far greater than what exposure-based models alone depict. Notably, deaths from chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) due to HCHO exposure in impoverished populations differ significantly from those linked to PM2.5 and O3, with India, Bangladesh, and Pakistan alone accounting for 90% of global deaths. While the ten countries with the highest pollution-related mortality among populations living in extreme poverty are predominantly located in Africa and South Asia, elevating the poverty threshold highlights substantial health burdens in the Middle East, East Asia, Indonesia, and Latin America.

Higher Mortality Rates in Affluent Countries.

We examined the disparities of health burdens between national and subnational regions stratified by poverty rate to explore whether economically disadvantaged regions disproportionately bear a higher premature mortality, consistent with their higher exposures. We categorized the population-weighted air pollutant concentrations and premature mortality of subnational regions into three groups based on the tertiles (33rd and 66th percentiles) of the $3.65/d poverty thresholds. These groups were designated as the low-poverty group (poverty rate < 2.5%, the 33rd percentile), the medium-poverty group (poverty rate between 2.5% and 41.8%, the latter being the 66th percentile), and the high-poverty group (poverty rate > 41.8%), respectively, as shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S2, where the medium-poverty group is predominantly located in South Asia, the Middle East, Southeast Asia, and Central/South America, while the poor group is mainly found in African countries.

Subsequent statistical results demonstrate that the population-weighted O3 concentration was highest in low-poverty countries, whereas population-weighted PM2.5 concentrations peaked in medium-poverty countries (Fig. 2A). When attributing mortality outcomes to these exposures, O3 patterns aligned with their respective exposure distributions (Fig. 2B). However, PM2.5 exhibited a statistically significant difference across the groups, with the highest mortality occurring in low-poverty countries and the lowest in high-poverty countries. Overall, significant and consistent disparities in pollution-induced premature mortality are observed between impoverished and affluent regions across various pollutants and causes of death, with relatively low-poverty areas bearing a greater share of mortality attributable to pollution exposure (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Using subnational baseline mortality data for the 15 countries with available estimates, we recalculate the inequality metrics and find the resulting patterns align closely with those based on national baseline mortality rates (SI Appendix, Fig. S4), confirming the robustness of the primary findings.

The positive Pearson correlation between GDP and premature mortality, along with the negative correlation between poverty rate and premature mortality, provides further evidence supporting this pattern (SI Appendix, Figs. S5 and S6). A decomposition of the underlying driving causes finds that per capita GDP is positively correlated with baseline mortality rates but negatively correlated with PM2.5. This indicates that although individuals living in affluent areas are exposed to the lowest concentrations of PM2.5, their elevated baseline mortality rates contribute to a more severe health burden. Analysis of inequality using age-stratified populations and baseline mortality rates demonstrates that this relationship results from the aging populations in low-poverty countries (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Subsequent sensitivity analyses that refined the poverty stratification to quintiles and deciles reproduced the same overarching patterns, underscoring the robustness of our conclusions (SI Appendix, Figs. S8 and S9). The quantitative decomposition of drivers in observed mortality rate disparities shows that both baseline mortality rate and population aging contribute to greater inequality in exposure-attributable mortality than exposure itself (Fig. 2C). We also present additional evidence, as shown in Fig. 2D, which illustrates that while health disparities characterized by baseline mortality rates exacerbate mortality in high poverty regions, aging contributes to higher mortality rate in low poverty groups.

Beyond intergroup disparities, it is also crucial to recognize substantial within-group inequalities. The Lorenz inequality curves of the low- and medium-poverty groups are more skewed than those of the poor group, indicating greater within-group inequality in the relatively affluent group (Fig. 2E and SI Appendix, Fig. S10), where 31% of the population living in high-risk regions accounts for 50% of the cumulative exposure-related deaths. Additional support comes from their higher Gini indices based on mortality rate (SI Appendix, Fig. S11), with some countries showing exceptionally high health burden, such as East Asia in the affluent group and South Asia in the medium-poverty group (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). Notably, when we aggregated the total premature deaths attributable to exposures from the two pollutants, the resulting inequality mirrored that of PM2.5 since PM2.5 contributed the majority of these mortality impacts. The decile specification also uncovers inequality that is masked within each tercile. The decline together in relative risk and mortality rate of the first two tercile groups indicate that the intergroup health inequalities of low- and medium-poverty groups stem mainly from differential exposure in pollution (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 B and F).

Examining cause-specific mortality burdens attributable to PM2.5 exposure provides further insight into drivers of inequality. We observed greater within-group inequality for conditions closely linked with aging populations and dietary habits high in salt and fat, such as ischemic heart disease (IHD), chronic stroke (Stroke), lower respiratory infections (LRI), and type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM), in countries at medium or lower levels of poverty. Thus this inequality may be driven by relatively “benign” factors such as an older population in a particular region, as well as international variations in lifestyles. In contrast, among countries at medium or higher poverty levels, the most pronounced inequality emerges for lung cancer and COPD, likely reflecting uneven industrial distribution and air pollution control measures across these countries. These findings highlight the need for more refined, region-specific assessments to fully capture the extent of environmental injustice.

Inequalities within Countries.

The pooled estimates mask substantial heterogeneity within nations and subnations. Leveraging the fine spatial scale of data, we can quantify the disparities of premature mortality rates within countries between economically disadvantaged and affluent segments of society [in instances where gridded poverty data were unavailable, gridded per capita GDP in 2005 purchasing power parity US dollars (USD) was utilized as a reliable microproxy for the inequality analyses (23)]. We conducted a sensitivity test aggregating gridded GDP to subnational levels and found similar inequality patterns across income groups as when using poverty data (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). To this end, the rich and the poor were categorized according to the median per capita GDP of the respective countries. We operationalize socioeconomic stratification at the geospatial grid level through a critical assumption of within-unit homogeneity, wherein all populations within a grid classified as affluent (or impoverished) are treated as representatives of their respective income cohorts to unmask the structural disproportionality in health burden allocation between economic populations.

At a more granular scale, our results show that within approximately 60% of countries, there are elevated air pollution-related health risks for economically advantaged populations, predominantly in more affluent countries. This is consistent with the highest pollution levels often occurring in the most affluent areas in these countries, such as the Po River valley in Italy or the environs of Shanghai in China. Conversely, higher mortality rates for impoverished populations are concentrated in high-poverty regions, including South Asia, and some countries in Southeast Asia and sub-Saharan Africa (Fig. 3 A and B). The observed pattern of mortality inequalities could be indicative of disparities in air pollution sources. Economically deprived areas in high-income countries may have fewer of the main emissions sources in those countries, such as vehicles or industry, relative to more affluent areas, whereas in low-income regions, economically deprived areas may rely on residential solid-fuel and biomass combustion that intensifies exposure. Globally, mortality rates show marginally higher burdens in affluent groups (58 per 100,000) versus impoverished groups (56 per 100,000) (Fig. 3C). This observed discrepancy aligns directionally with the trends observed in subnational administrative data, thereby reinforcing the robustness of the findings. Furthermore, we isolated the effects of urban–rural spatial stratification on the disparities among poor and affluent groups. The analysis indicates that the observed disparities in mortality rates were primarily attributable to elevated rates in urban and suburban areas among affluent populations compared to those living in poverty. A higher mortality rate is observed in low-poverty grids of urban, suburban, and rural areas across China, the United States, and Eastern Europe. In Northern and Southern Europe, the health burden is higher in both suburban and rural populations experiencing economic disadvantage (SI Appendix, Fig. S14).

In regions characterized by high vulnerability, particularly in South Asia, Southeast Asia, and Africa, where impoverished populations are confronted with a combination of social and environmental challenges, the extent of these disparities exhibits significant variations. These variations are primarily influenced by the distribution of pollution sources across rural and urban zones. As depicted in Fig. 3 D–F, urban and suburban affluent populations exhibited higher mortality rates than their affluent rural counterparts, aligned with global patterns, whereas rural impoverished groups disproportionately bore health burdens, a pattern dominating regional aggregates due to the larger rural population base. It is important to note that this outcome is reflective of the average results found within socioeconomic cohorts. A detailed examination of disparities within groups reveals that affluent populations in urban and suburban areas with elevated health risks worldwide exhibit elevated inequality indices, indicating the presence of localized disparities where intraurban variations (e.g., proximity to industrial clusters or green spaces) amplify this inequality (SI Appendix, Fig. S15). From urban to rural areas, global intragroup inequality increases within poor populations, a pattern likewise observed in Africa and South Asia, ultimately reaching levels comparable to those observed among the affluent groups. This suggests that broadly shared systemic condition such as limited infrastructure and use of traditional fuels, rather than income-related class differences, dominate the mortality inequalities in rural areas.

We estimated grid-specific correlations between poverty and mortality in South and Southeast Asia and Africa. We focus on these regions as they are characterized by the co-occurrence of elevated poverty and pollution-related mortality, with rural impoverished populations experiencing disproportionate health burdens. Comparing the joint distribution of poverty and mortality emphasizes that peripheral areas of urban agglomerations, industrial belts, or economically prosperous transportation corridors (blue) frequently exhibit co-occurrences of elevated mortality and relatively high poverty (Fig. 4). In Africa, such regions often cluster around a few major cities or resource extraction sites, whereas in South and Southeast Asia, they are predominantly concentrated along heavily urbanized coastal plains, metropolitan zones, or special economic districts. By contrast, the central (purple) zones represent hotspots characterized by high mortality coupled with high income level. These areas, while relatively affluent, face intensified pollution from industrial emissions, vehicular exhaust, and concentrated energy usage, compounded by dense populations, leading to pronounced pollution-related premature mortality. This conjunction of economic development and heightened health risks epitomizes a “cost of opportunity” when air pollutant regulations are weak. The term “opportunity” here refers to the concentration of employment opportunities, services, and infrastructure around urban centers. As shown in the Insets of Fig. 4 and SI Appendix, Fig. S16, populations inhabiting transitional fringes between urban and rural areas are economically marginalized and bear disproportionately elevated health burdens due to cross-boundary pollutant transport, especially for poorer individuals, illustrating the pervasive impacts of industrial and urban growth on vulnerable populations.

Discussion

We investigated the relationship between poverty and mortality attributed to the long-term exposure of air pollutants within and between subnational areas. Our analysis reveals that there is substantial spatial heterogeneity in premature mortality, along with disparities in economic inequity of premature mortality across spatial scales. Building on Sager’s findings that PM2.5-related mortality is even more unequal than pollution exposure itself (24), we demonstrate that baseline mortality and aging exacerbate observed mortality inequalities. Contrary to the relationship that impoverished populations systematically bear the heaviest exposure to pollution, (8, 12, 25) which is found across nations globally and across communities in North America, our findings indicate that premature mortality rates attributable to air pollutants are elevated in affluent areas due to population aging compared to underdeveloped subnational regions. Moreover, the mortality rate of affluent populations in developed countries exceeds that of impoverished populations, whereas the reverse is true for relatively poor countries in Africa and parts of Asia, where impoverished individuals experience a higher health risk from pollution exposure. These results highlight the limitation of aggregated data at the administration level in capturing localized hotspots of pollution and poverty (26–28). Aggregated national metrics can conceal stark local inequities by glossing over subregional differences in industrial siting, population density, and social infrastructure (19), thereby diluting critical heterogeneities, whether in densely populated cities or diffuse rural settlements. In particular, proximity to economic opportunities in more affluent urban centers of low-income countries can come at the cost of living closer to large emission sources. Such an uneven burden underscores the importance of region-specific dynamics. Local geography, economic structure, and social infrastructure can all profoundly affect where pollution is generated and who is most exposed.

When addressing the multifaceted character of pollution-induced vulnerabilities, it is important to clarify the spatial disparities of pollution. Previous research has provided limited insights into spatially explicit health-poverty hotspots and urban–rural variations. When stratifying the differential health risks between affluent and impoverished populations into urban–rural contexts, it becomes evident that regional patterns are generally dominated by rural areas due to their larger spatial coverage, as previously discussed. Urban and suburban areas within both high-income and low-income countries exhibit a consistent pattern, with affluent populations typically experiencing higher premature mortality rates than their impoverished counterparts. Despite their relative economic advantages, residents often experience higher pollution exposure due to proximity to pollution-intensive areas (e.g., dense urban cores and transportation hubs) (29, 30). Divergent development trajectories between affluent and low-income nations result in notable spatial disparities in mortality rates. Suburban areas exhibit the highest mortality burden, reflecting the progressive decentralization of industrial activities to suburban zones in high-income countries (31–33). In contrast, urban areas within low-income countries experience the highest mortality rates due to concentrated industrial activities, insufficient regulatory frameworks, and inadequate emission control practices (34).

Regions in Africa and South Asia characterized by intersecting high poverty rates and elevated premature mortality from pollution exposure illustrate the compounded socioenvironmental challenges faced by those impoverished communities. Analysis of the grid-scale underscores the relative neglect of urban–rural transition zones, where economically disadvantaged populations face significant risks from high pollution exposures. Traditionally, urban environments have been conceptualized as pollution “hotspots” with elevated health burdens related to air quality due to higher densities of anthropogenic activities (35–37). Our results show that the highest dual risk of mortality and poverty is predominantly located in marginalized communities located in industrial corridors, urban peripheries, or major transportation routes. Further analyses show that exposure inequality is lower in urban areas compared to rural areas, with the affluent group experiencing greater exposure inequality than the poor, while this difference is eliminated in rural areas. Uneven infrastructure development in rural areas can concentrate marginalized communities near pollution-emitting facilities, exacerbating health disparities. Meanwhile, the urban-centric narrative is complicated by the vulnerability of rural populations to both local and transboundary pollution (38, 39). While rural areas may appear to have fewer direct anthropogenic pollution sources, reliance on biomass fuels and frequent wildfires can significantly elevate ambient pollutant concentrations, an issue often overshadowed by the prevailing urban-centric focus (20–22, 40). In Africa, poorer rural communities not only experience risks from biomass burning pollution but also endure limited healthcare access and the added impact of distant industrial emissions (41, 42), all of which contribute to notably higher pollution inequalities.

Additionally, socioeconomic inequalities in mortality burdens vary significantly by pollutant type and disease cause, and these disparities in attributable mortality differ from exposure patterns. For instance, population-weighted O3 concentrations are higher for affluent groups, aligning with previous multipollutant socioeconomic inequality research that found O3 uniquely higher among higher-income populations compared to the other five conventional pollutants (13). This higher exposure in affluent areas persists when translated into ozone-attributable mortality. Conversely, PM2.5 population-weighted concentrations are typically higher in LMICs. However, when attributed to ultimate health outcomes (all-cause mortality), the relationship reverses, showing a negative correlation with poverty, with higher mortality burdens in high-income countries. This reversal can be attributed to factors such as higher population aging and a greater chronic disease burden in affluent countries. It is important to recognize that reducing PM2.5 in an affluent location might provide more benefit than an equivalent reduction in a poorer place because the older population living in the lower-poverty areas are more sensitive (43). Except for LRI, specific PM2.5-related causes of mortality align with the economic inequality patterns observed for all-cause mortality. These findings emphasize the need for region- and pollutant-specific assessments to better inform policies, including those aimed at providing equitable or preferential support to historically underserved populations.

While our results offer valuable insights, reliance on secondary pollution modeling and limited poverty proxies introduces uncertainties. Fine-scale socioeconomic data, including baseline mortality rates, remain scarce, including in many rapidly urbanizing areas. The assumption that baseline mortality rates are consistent across income groups within countries may also underestimate inequality by obscuring subnational disparities in healthcare access and population vulnerability. Health risks might be higher for low-income populations relative to the national average since they are poor and therefore in many countries do not have as much access to high-quality health care. However, they may also be younger than the high-income people in the same area if they are migrants to those areas looking for work, for example, which would mean their baseline mortality rates could be lower. While the use of national rates may not fully capture the variations in baseline vulnerability, it remains a defensible approach given the limited available data. Future research would benefit from disaggregating baseline mortality rates by income group and robust longitudinal studies incorporating mobility, healthcare accessibility, and behavioral factors, as well as examining additional impacts such as disability-adjusted life years. Such efforts would enrich our understanding of the cost of opportunity, wherein at least the initial phases of economic growth correlate with amplified environmental health risks, and provide a blueprint for more effective and equitable pollution control measures.

Overall, there are disparities between countries, within countries, and across urban–rural divides, underscoring the need for policy interventions that balance economic growth with comprehensive and equitable public health priorities. The success of future air quality management should be measured not only by overall pollutant reductions, but by how such reductions protect vulnerable groups including impoverished communities that lack robust healthcare, affluent enclaves that endure heavy pollutant loads due to industrial clustering, and older individuals with escalating health risks from aging. Strengthening healthcare infrastructure, enhancing emission standards, and investing in clean energy transitions may collectively alleviate these burdens and address the nuanced realities of how air pollution intersects with social and economic vulnerabilities.

Methods

Transformer-Based Reconstruction of Air Pollutants.

Initially, we integrate diverse datasets encompassing satellite observations of trace gases and aerosols, meteorological data, and auxiliary variables indicative of anthropogenic emissions. This fusion creates a comprehensive and robust dataset to support the learning of the complexities of global air pollution dynamics (SI Appendix). Utilizing the fused data, we employed a Transformer-based deep-learning model, known as Air Transformer (AiT) (44), to construct and understand the potential nonlinear associations between column densities and near-surface observations. This model is specifically designed to estimate global ground-level O3, PM2.5, and HCHO concentrations, leveraging advanced algorithms to handle the spatial and temporal variability inherent in the multisource data.

The model utilizes an encoder–decoder structure, which is particularly effective in capturing the spatial dependencies among input variables from surrounding grids. The encoder is composed of a multilayer Transformer layer stacked with spatial self-attention and channel self-attention for mining hidden associations between predictor variables. The decoder implements the interactions between the target pixel and adjacent grids through the original self-attention design. This approach enables a robust reconstruction of daily pollutant concentrations at a 10 km resolution, accounting for variables linked to emission, transport, chemical transformation, and column density of air pollutants within approximately 20 km of the target grid. In addition, by incorporating a multitask learning strategy, the model allows simultaneous estimation of multiple pollutants, enhancing generalizability and regularization (SI Appendix).

Recent emerging evidence suggests that ground-level HCHO level is associated with respiratory toxicity. Our global surface HCHO concentration reconstruction from HCHO satellite retrievals provides a valuable opportunity to quantify long-term health impacts that have been largely overlooked due to the scarcity of surface HCHO observations. Because the damage to health caused by inhaled formaldehyde is most notable in the respiratory tract, and the relative risk of long-term HCHO exposure caused by other causes has not been quantified, our study only assessed premature mortality in COPD. The coefficient of γ between HCHO exposure and COPD mortality risk was calculated from the results of a previous study (SI Appendix) (48). Other pollutants, such as nitrogen dioxide, may also be important but are not included here in part because of their high degree of correlation with ozone or PM2.5.

Long-Term Exposure Inequality Measurement.

Premature mortality attributed to air pollutants is aggregated with data on subnational poverty headcount ratio, gridded GDP, and urban–rural classification, along with age-stratified populations, to evaluate health inequality between and within countries. The inequality analysis of mortality focuses on PM2.5 and O3 because HCHO-attributable COPD mortality accounts for only a small share of total mortality. We stratify the observations into terciles of the poverty-headcount ratio to dampen the leverage of outliers and to provide an easily interpretable, quantitative results of inequality across socioeconomic groups. We test robustness using alternative binning schemes (quintiles and deciles) and find that the main conclusions are insensitive to the number of bins (SI Appendix). In addition, regression analysis and correlation analysis of premature-mortality rates against poverty and other covariates are reported as independent validity tests. The decomposition of air pollutant–attributable mortality is accomplished by introducing two additional sensitivity analysis. Specifically, the exposure effect is evaluated by calculating the mortality rate using a globally unified baseline mortality rate and age structure. The baseline mortality effect is then quantified as the difference between the mortality rate based on the actual baseline mortality rate with a unified age structure and the exposure effect. Finally, the population aging effect is assessed by comparing it to the full model, which incorporates both the actual baseline mortality rate and the age structure.

In this study, exposure refers solely to the spatial distribution of pollutant concentrations. Vulnerability is defined as the heterogeneity in the combination of poverty level and air pollution attributable mortality. Poverty headcount ratio for about 1,600 subnational regions among more than 168 countries, defined as the percentage of the subnational population living in households with consumption or income per person below the $2.15, $3.65, and $6.85 poverty line at 2017 international prices, are from the Word Bank’s Global Subnational Poverty Atlas. The gridded population data were obtained from WorldPop datasets (49). Global urban–rural classification data at a resolution of 0.01° × 0.01° for 2020 are from the Global Human Settlement Model Layers (GHS-SMOD) project (50). Each grid cell was categorized based on both population density and built-up area characteristics. Detailed information can be found in SI Appendix.

We quantify the inequality of premature mortality rates for the groups classified by GDP (Ig) using Gini Coefficient as the inequality index. By measuring the relative mean absolute difference in mortality rate between pairs of observations, we derive the inequality index as[3]G=∑i∑jxi−xj2n2x−,

where xi and xj represent the mortality rates of regions i and j, respectively. The Gini coefficient normalizes the average pairwise mortality rate difference by the mean mortality rate (x-) and total number of comparisons (n), yielding a value between 0 (perfect equality) and 1 (maximum inequality).
