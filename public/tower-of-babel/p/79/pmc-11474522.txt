Evaluation of Biosurfactants to Preflush for Offshore Wells
Authors: Fubin Xin, Zhiqiang Wu, Xingjie Ling, Xingquan Zhang, Bing Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
With the improvement of environmental protection requirements in offshore oil and gas exploration, there is a great demand to find environmentally friendly surfactants for cementing preflush fluids. In the present paper, the application potential of three biosurfactants of rhamnolipid, lipopeptide, and sophorolipid in the preflush fluid was evaluated through a series of experiments. The experiments studied the interfacial tension (IFT) of the biosurfactants, resistance to temperature, salt resistance (sodium chloride and sea salt), and the influences of the studied biosurfactants on the rheological compatibility and capabilities to convert the wettability of the formation from oil-wet to water-wet, mud cake removal efficiency, and to improve the interfacial bonding properties of the preflush fluid. The results showed that rhamnolipid, lipopeptide, and sophorolipid exhibited excellent surface activities in high-temperature and sodium chloride (NaCl) environments. When exposed to sea salt, lipopeptides lost their function due to agglomeration, while rhamnolipids and sophorolipids maintained structural stabilities and high surface activities. When rhamnolipid was mixed with sophorolipid in a ratio of 2:1 at the same concentration, the combined biosurfactant had the best surface activity among the studied surfactants. The combined biosurfactant not only had good rheological compatibility with the drilling fluid and cement slurry but also showed excellent properties in the removal of mud cake. In addition, the effect of the removal of the mud cake was verified by the gas channeling experiment. The experimental results show that the presence of mud cake can reduce the bonding strength. When rhamnolipid was mixed with sophorolipid in a ratio of 2:1 at the same concentration, the removal efficiency of mud cake was the highest, and the interfacial bonding property of the cement sheath can be improved.

Introduction

The wellbore obtained through the construction of a well is the only channel for the outflow of underground oil and gas. Meanwhile, the cementing job is one of the key processes during the construction of a well. Its main objective is to inject a cement slurry into the annular between the casing and the formation. The long-term safe exploitation of oil and gas resources can be ensured only after the cement slurry has solidified and formed an effective zonal isolation. In order to achieve this objective, the drilling fluid should be largely removed from the annulus, and the entire annulus should be filled with appropriate cement. In addition, the cement sheath should have good bonding properties with the casing and formation. However, if the drilling fluid is not compatible with the cement slurry, it will adhere and leave mud cake on the wall of the wellbore. Contact between the residual drilling fluid and the mud cake with cement slurry can lead to contamination. This in turn has the potential to shorten the thickening time of the cement slurry, as well as reduce its compressive strength.1−4 Consequently, the quality of the cement sheath is likely to deteriorate over time, ultimately affecting the quality of the cementing. To address these challenges, a functional fluid called the preflush fluid is used in cementing engineering for oil and gas wells. As illustrated in Figure 1, the preflush fluid is located between the drilling fluid and the cement slurry. Its main functions are to prevent contamination between the drilling fluid and the cement slurry and to efficiently remove the mud cake during the displacement process. In addition, when nonaqueous fluids, such as oil-based mud (OBM), are utilized in drilling operations, preflush fluid can alter the wettability of the casing and formation.

Flushing of the filter cake during the displacement process.

Water, a viscosifier, surfactants, and a weighting agent such as barite make up a typical preflush fluid. Surfactant is one of the most important components of a preflush fluid and endows the preflush fluid with good compatibility with the drilling fluid/cement slurry, mud cake washing performance, and interfacial wetting reversal performance. For instance, Curbelo et al.5 selected vegetable oil microemulsion as the oil phase and developed a preflush fluid with high wettability conversion capability. The results demonstrated that the flushing solution was capable of effectively removing the mud cake while offering certain environmental advantages. Neto et al.6 devised a rinsing solution using brine as the water phase and cationic surfactant as the surfactant. The results demonstrated that the preflush fluid efficiently removed the mud cake formed by nonaqueous drilling fluid and exhibited good wettability conversion capability. Furthermore, the chemical properties of the solution were compatible with the cement slurry and could reduce the environmental impact of the cementing process. The environment-friendly preflush fluid proposed by Henry et al.7 was based on the anionic surfactant of methyl ester sulfonate. Their experimental results demonstrated that the preflush fluid not only exhibited good rheology and washing efficiency, with no adverse impact on the environment, but also played a crucial and multifaceted role in ensuring cementing quality and wellbore integrity over the operational lifespan of the well.

With the continuous improvement of environmental protection requirements in the offshore oil and gas industry, the search for environmentally friendly wellbore working fluids is becoming more and more important. The commonly used long-chain alkane surfactants are derived from petroleum products and their derivatives, all of which exhibit certain biological toxicity. Therefore, there is an urgency to find environmentally friendly surfactants for cementing preflush fluids. Biosurfactants are metabolites with certain surface activity secreted by microorganisms during metabolism under certain conditions, such as glycolipids, polysaccharide lipids, lipopeptides, and their derivatives. Compared with other surfactants produced by chemical synthesis or petroleum refining, biosurfactants not only have the properties of solubilizing, emulsifying, wetting, foaming, dispersing, and reducing surface tension but also have the advantages of being nontoxic, biodegradable, ecologically safe, and highly surface-active.

In this paper, the applicability of three biosurfactants of rhamnolipid, lipopeptide, and sophorolipid in the preflush fluid is evaluated through a series of experiments. The experiments include temperature resistance, salt resistance (sodium chloride and sea salt), and interfacial tension (IFT) of the biosurfactants. The influences of the studied biosurfactants on the rheological compatibility, mud cake removal efficiency, and capabilities to convert the wettability of the formation from oil-wet to water-wet and to improve the interfacial bonding properties of the preflush fluid are also studied. Therefore, this study focuses on the efficient and green development of offshore oil and gas resources and strives to develop a set of environmentally friendly preflush fluid systems with comprehensive performance from the characteristics of surfactants themselves.

Experimental Section

Materials

The studied biosurfactants included rhamnolipid, lipopeptide, and sophorolipid (supplied by Beijing Yanwei Technology Co., Ltd., China). The manufacturer tested the biological toxicity of the three biosurfactants according to the Chinese national standard “Biological Toxicity of Pollutants in Offshore Oil Exploration and Development, Part 2: Test Method” (GB/T 18420.2-2009). The results demonstrated that the allowable values of biotoxicity of the three biosurfactants exceeded the stipulated threshold value of 15,000 mg/L for the first-class marine zone and exhibited a lack of toxicity to marine organisms. The other materials used to prepare the preflush fluid included a suspension agent, antifoaming agent, and weighing agent (Barite) (Weihui Chemical Co., Ltd., China).

The materials used to prepare the cement slurry included Class G oil well cement (Leshan Jiahua Cement Co., China), fluid loss control additive G33S, and dispersant USZ (Weihui Chemical Co., Ltd., China).

The drilling fluid came from an offshore rig in the South China Sea and had a density of 2.2 g/cm3. Other materials included NaCl (Kelong Chemical Co., China), industrial grade sea salt (obtained from the South China Sea), surface tensiometer (obtained from Shanghai Fangrui Instrument Co., Ltd., China), medium-pressure filtration instrument (obtained from Qingdaohengtaida Electromechanical Equipment Co., Ltd., China), and a custom-made permeability testing instrument. Electric heating constant-temperature blast drying oven was obtained from Shanghai Qixin Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., China. Mud cake washing and two interfacial cementation comprehensive test instruments were also used in the current work.

Mechanism of Surfactants

Infiltration and swelling effect: the hydrophobic end of the biosurfactant molecule is adsorbed on the surface of the mud cake, and the hydrophilic end goes deeper into the water. As the hydrophilic end mixes with water, it makes the surface of the mud cake hydrophilic, and the water molecules and reagents gradually infiltrate to produce the swelling effect, which weakens the structural and cohesive forces within the mud cake and the force between the mud cake and the casing. The mud cake is stripped and rolled up under the scouring action of the preflush fluid. In addition, new biosurfactant molecules are readsorbed onto the surface of the mud cake, creating a new penetration effect, which ultimately results in a complete wash of the mud cake.

Dispersion effect: surfactants have good dispersion ability and can wrap the mud cake surface particles in a micellar structure, which helps to disperse the mud cake surface particles suspended in the preflush fluid and improve the washing efficiency.

Methods

Instruments and Methods for Testing Surface Tension

Measurement of the Surface Tension

The test reagent was poured into clean glassware, and platinum was hung from the hook of the test sensor. After being immersed in the liquid, the platinum board was affected by the surface tension. The surface tension of the test reagent pulls down the platinum board as much as possible. When the surface tension of the test reagent or other related forces were balanced, the platinum board stopped immersing into the test reagent. At this time, the sensor of the balance measures the depth of immersion and converts it into the surface tension of the test reagent.

Measurement of the Critical Micelle Concentration (CMC)

After preparing a series of different surfactant reagent concentrations (0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 1, 1.2, and 1.5%) using an electronic balance at by mass ratio, the surface tension of each concentration was measured using a surface tensiometer. The critical micelle concentration (CMC) was determined from these measurements.

Evaluation of the Temperature Resistance of Surfactants

The surfactants were electrically and sequentially heated to temperatures of 25, 80, 120, and 150 °C in an electric thermostatic drying oven. The heated surfactants were tested using a surface tension meter. Finally, the surface tension at different temperatures was analyzed to determine whether the temperature had a significant influence on the surfactants or not.

Evaluation of the Salt Tolerance of Surfactants

In the actual cementing operation, a certain amount of sodium chloride should be added to the preflush fluid to prevent the formation from dissolution and collapse when drilling into the salt paste formation. In addition, in cases in which fresh water is not readily available, seawater is sometimes used to configure the surfactants. Therefore, in the above-mentioned cases, a surfactant was required to have a high salt resistance.

Using an electronic balance, different masses of salt (NaCl) and sea salt were weighed and then mixed by using a stirrer to prepare various concentrations of solutions of surfactants. During the stirring process, an equal mass of surfactant was added to create surfactant solutions. Finally, the surface tensions of prepared solutions of surfactants were measured by using a surface tension meter. Further analysis was conducted to assess their tolerance to salt and sea salt.

Contact Angle (Wettability)

A clean microscope slide was immersed in diesel for 30 min to remove the lipids. Subsequently, solutions of various biosurfactants with the same concentrations were prepared using a precision balance. The wettabilities of these biosurfactants were evaluated by measuring the contact angles of droplets formed on the surface of the slide. Smaller contact angles indicate the superior wettability performance of the surfactant and vice versa.

Compatibility (Including Preparation of Preflush Fluid and Cement Slurry)

Rheological Compatibility

According to the recommendations of the American Petroleum Institute (API), rheological experiments such as 95/5, 75/25, 50/50, 25/75, and 5/95 involving different proportions of cement or drilling fluid and preflush fluid were conducted. Different proportions of reagents were analyzed by using a six-speed rotary viscometer, and the readings were recorded. The rheological compatibility of the fluid was evaluated by calculating the R-index value (R). If the R-index was within the range of 0–40, the fluid was deemed compatible. If the R-index was within the range of 41–70, the fluid was deemed slightly incompatible, and additional testing was required. A truncated cone round die of 60 mm, top diameter of 36 mm, and height of 60 mm was prepared. The truncated cone round die was shaped and placed on a flat horizontal glass surface. To prevent the formation of bubbles, the fluid was mixed well. The mixed clean pulp is rapidly injected into the truncated cone round mold and scraped with a scraper, and the truncated cone round mold is lifted vertically while the stopwatch is activated. The fluid is then allowed to flow on the glass plate until 30 s have elapsed. The maximum diameter of the flowing part in two perpendicular directions is then measured with a ruler, and the average value is taken as the flow of the cement paste. Moreover, it is necessary to measure and record the flow rate. The rheology of the material was evaluated by using these two methods.

Thickening Compatibility

The prepared preflush fluid and cement slurry mixture was put into the cement slurry cup. The thickening time periods of the cement slurry and the mixture of the preflush fluid were compared to determine whether there was a phenomenon of accelerating or retarding of the solidification.

Compressive Strength Compatibility

Relevant experiments were carried out to determine the compressive strength’s compatibility. The cement slurry, preflush fluid, and cement slurry mixture were poured into the mold and cured in a water bath at 80 °C. After the mixture solidified, the compressive strength was measured by using the compression test.

Mud Cake Washing

The artificial rock core was placed in the drilling fluid to attach the oil-based drilling fluid to the surface. The core was fixed on the casing at 60 °C and 3.5 MPa for 30 min. The sleeve was fixed on the stirrer, and the paddle cup containing the preflush fluid was preheated in the water bath to control the temperature. Finally, the preflush fluid underwent the same speed for different times and solution concentrations. The mud cake washing efficiency was calculated by changing the thickness of the mud cake. Results and Discussion

Surfactant Test

Critical Micelle Concentration (CMC)

The application of surfactants with reduced surface tensions results in enhanced washing efficiency and a more facile wetting and penetration.8 The term “critical micelle concentration” is defined as the saturation of surfactant molecules at the interface, accompanied by the aggregation of excess molecules in the solution to form micelles.9−12 The main states of critical micelle concentration involve spherical and lamellar states and so on, as shown in Figure 5. To achieve the minimum solubilization concentration, oil could be dissolved in micelles.13 Consequently, three distinct biosurfactant concentrations were evaluated, and CMC values were obtained. Moreover, the surface tensions of three biosurfactant solutions with different concentrations were evaluated. It was observed that the minimum surface tension and CMC of the surfactants were attained at the same concentration, as shown in Figure 6.

Spherical and layered micelle structure of the surfactant.

Changes in the surface tension of rhamnolipid, lipopeptide, and sophorolipid with concentration.

As shown in Figure 6, the surface tension exhibited a decreasing trend with an increase in the concentration of the surfactant solution. When the concentration was held constant, the surface tension underwent a gradual decrease until it reached equilibrium. The lowest surface tension values corresponding to these three biosurfactants were found to be as follows: γ(Rhamnolipid) = 29.1 mN/m, γ(Lipopeptide) = 29.1 mN/m, and γ(Sophorolipid) = 28.5 mN/m. The corresponding critical micelle concentrations were found to be as follows: CMC(Rhamnolipid) = 3%, CMC(Lipopeptide) = 2.5, and CMC(Sophorolipid) = 3%. The closer the surface tension of a substance was to the critical micelle concentration, the greater the wetting permeability of the surface, which was conducive to the washing of mud cake.

Temperature Resistance of Surfactants

With the further exploration and development of oil and gas resources, a greater number of high-temperature and high-pressure deep-sea oil and gas fields have been discovered. This is accompanied by an increase in temperature, which presents significant challenges to cementing fluids.14 As the temperature increases, the solubility of general surfactants may be enhanced, while the washing effect of surfactant solution may diminish. This may not meet the demands of deep-sea and high-temperature underground operations. Therefore, it is essential to assess the temperature resistance of the chosen biosurfactants. Consequently, the interfacial tensions of rhamnolipid, lipopeptide, and sophorolipid were examined under different temperatures (25, 80, 120, and 150 °C).

As shown in Figure 7, with the increase in temperature, there was little change in the surface tension of the studied surfactants. At 150 °C, there appeared to be a stabilizing effect on the change in surface tension due to the increase in temperature. The findings indicated that the three biosurfactants exhibited robust high-temperature stability.

Effect of temperature on the surface tension of solutions of surfactants.

Salt Resistance of Surfactants

Seawater has an inherently high salt content, which may impact the efficacy and stability of drilling fluids, preflush fluids, and cement slurry. Furthermore, it can also lead to the deterioration of solutions of surfactants, which represents a significant challenge for surfactants. Therefore, it is crucial to select surfactants that demonstrate robust salt (NaCl) resistance to ensure the integrity and performance of the preflush fluid.15,16 The NaCl solution was selected as the base solution, and its salt resistance was subsequently tested. As shown in Figure 8, the surface tension of the solution of surfactants fluctuated slightly with an increase in the NaCl concentration. The surface tension gradually stabilized. Accordingly, the experimental results indicated that these three biosurfactants exhibited some degree of salt resistance.

Effect of NaCl Concentration on the surface tension of solutions of surfactants.

In the context of offshore drilling engineering, the preparation of reagents from seawater constitutes the majority of the process, and therefore, it is of great importance that these reagents exhibit a high level of sea salt resistance. For example, the South China Sea has a high salinity, and its salinity reaches 35%. The results are compared with an ordinary surfactant prepared using tap water as a reference and reagents prepared from seawater with a salinity of 35%.

Composition of Surfactants

The process of combining surfactants with each other or with other compounds is known as the recombination of surfactants. This combination has the objective of creating a synergistic impact among the surfactants, which implies that the combined capabilities will be more effective in certain ways than the capabilities of the individual surfactants.17,18 Subsequently, the suitable surfactant is combined with the selected reagent, and the surface tension is evaluated using the platinum plate method. This procedure was employed to identify an optimal surfactant reagent for further experimentation. Following the procedure of the salt resistance test, the lipotropic peptide was rendered inoperable following the preparation of seawater, due to which it was not used in the subsequent test.

As shown in Figure 10, the surface tension values of the three distinct proportions of surfactants were less than those of the single reagent. The lower the surface tension, the better the wettability, which is conducive to an improvement in washing results. Therefore, the combined use of rhamnolipid as the main agent and sophorolipid as the auxiliary agent was expected to result in a reduction in surface tension, thereby leading to a noticeable enhancement in washing efficiency.

Wettability Reversal

Wettability is defined as the capacity of a surface to interact with a solution. During the removal of mud cake, the wettability of the wall of the well can be altered from one state of interaction to another by the application of a specific technique.20,21 In the present study, the contact angle methodology was used to ascertain the wettability characteristics of the preflush fluid.22,23 It can be demonstrated that the wetting capability of a solution is inversely proportional to the wetting angle. This means that the smaller the angle is, the stronger the wetting capability. Figure 11a depicts the wetting angle of the untreated water. Figure 11b illustrates the wetting angle of water treated with sophorolipids. Figure 11c displays the wetting angle of water treated with rhamnolipids. Figure 11d shows the wetting angle of water treated with both rhamnolipids and sophorolipids. All treatments were conducted at the same concentration of each solution. As evidenced by the results, the incorporation of surfactants appeared to enhance the wetting efficacy. For the same concentration, rhamnolipid demonstrated a greater wetting effect than did sophorolipid. The optimal ratio of rhamnolipids to sophorolipids was determined to be 2:1. This ratio yielded the highest degree of wetting.

Gas Channeling Test

The residual mud cake in the wellbore was closely related to the gas channeling of the well cementing, which seriously affected the quality of the cementing.25−27 The presence of mud cake will reduce the bonding and lead to gas channeling, which ultimately affects the quality of cementing. Therefore, the rock core attached to the mud cake was washed, and the cement slurry was poured and maintained. When the cement slurry maintenance was complete, a gas channeling test was carried out. To prevent gas channeling, the size of the gas channeling should be judged by the amount of sufficient resistance formed inside the cement slurry.

Figure 19 shows gas channeling at the interface of cementation without washing the mud cake. Figure 20 shows the gas channeling in the cementation of the interface after the mud cake was washed. The results showed that under the same curing conditions and the temperature of cement slurry, the internal resistance after washing with the preflush fluid and curing with cement slurry was greater than that after washing. The greater the resistance, the shorter the gas channeling distance and the less harm in the system. Therefore, good washing efficiency could improve the cementation quality, seal the gas reservoir well, effectively prevent gas channeling, and improve the overall quality of cementing.

Conclusions

In this study, the use of biosurfactants as a cementing preflush fluid in offshore oil and gas wells was explored. To ascertain the characteristics of the studied biosurfactants, related experiments were carried out, and the efficacy of the preflush fluid was evaluated using a series of experiments. Based on the results, the following conclusions are drawn.(1)The surface tension of the three biosurfactants was evaluated. A series of tests were conducted to evaluate the environmental protection, temperature resistance, and NaCl resistance, as well as the sea salt resistance of the biosurfactant solutions. The findings demonstrated that the three agents exhibited commendable characteristics. However, the lipopeptide displayed a notable limitation in its capacity to withstand the presence of sea salt.(2)The experiments were conducted to evaluate the rheological, thickening, and pressure compatibility properties of the preflush fluid. The results indicated that the configured preflush fluid was compatible with the aforementioned characteristics.(3)An experiment was conducted to evaluate the efficiency of washing the mud cake. This was followed by the conception and perfecting of a mud cake washing experiment. The washing experiments were conducted using a blender with compound biosurfactant solution and single biosurfactant solution, and the biosurfactant was optimally selected according to the results. The results indicated that the optimum washing efficiency was achieved when rhamnolipid and sophorolipid were mixed in a 2:1 ratio.(4)The results of the bonding strength test, conducted alongside a gas channeling test, indicated that the presence of mud cake could result in a reduction of bonding strength. A further gas channeling test was conducted under the same curing conditions and temperature of the cement slurry. The results showed that the internal resistance was greater after washing with the preflush fluid and subsequent curing with cement slurry than it was without washing. Therefore, it was inferred that the antigas channeling capability of the rock core was superior to that of the unwashed specimen.
