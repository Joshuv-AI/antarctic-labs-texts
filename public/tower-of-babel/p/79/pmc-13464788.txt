Association of emergency department stroke care processes with rehabilitation outcomes after first-ever stroke
Authors: Tuba Erdem Sultanoğlu, Hasan Sultanoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo examine the association between emergency department (ED) processes and acute interventions after first-ever stroke and the functional and motor gains achieved by the end of a rehabilitation program in a physical medicine and rehabilitation clinic.MethodsThis single-center retrospective observational cohort study included 257 patients who presented to the ED with a first-ever stroke and subsequently underwent a rehabilitation program in a physical medicine and rehabilitation clinic. Demographic and clinical characteristics, ED process/intervention variables [large vessel occlusion (LVO), stroke code activation, intensive care unit admission, intravenous thrombolysis (IVT), endovascular therapy (EVT), initiation of antithrombotic therapy, and time metrics], and rehabilitation outcomes were extracted from electronic records. Functional and motor outcomes were assessed using the Functional Independence Measure (FIM), Functional Ambulation Scale (FAS), and Brunnstrom staging. The primary outcome was clinically meaningful functional improvement (ΔFIM ≥ 22). Secondary outcomes included ΔFIM, FIM efficiency, ΔFAS ≥ 1, independent ambulation at the end of the rehabilitation episode (FAS ≥ 4), and ≥ 1-stage gain in Brunnstrom staging for the upper extremity, hand, and lower extremity. Multivariable logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed.ResultsThe mean age was 65.02 ± 13.55 years, and 38.9% of patients were female. Significant improvement was observed across functional and motor outcomes during rehabilitation. Total FIM increased from 75.65 ± 15.90 to 96.42 ± 17.02 (ΔFIM 20.77 ± 13.70; p < 0.001; Cohen’s dz = 1.52), median FAS increased from 4 to 5 (p < 0.001), and 78.6% of patients achieved independent ambulation at the end of the rehabilitation episode (FAS ≥ 4). Clinically meaningful functional improvement (ΔFIM ≥ 22) was observed in 45.9% of patients. Brunnstrom stages improved significantly in all domains (all p < 0.001), with ≥ 1-stage gain rates of 58.6% for the upper extremity, 55.5% for the hand, and 64.5% for the lower extremity. In multivariable analyses, stroke code activation and IVT were associated with several rehabilitation outcomes, whereas LVO was associated with less favorable outcomes in selected domains. For clinically meaningful functional improvement, female sex, stroke code activation, IVT, and initiation of antithrombotic therapy were positively associated, whereas NIHSS, baseline FIM, and LVO were negatively associated. ROC analysis showed modest discrimination for the ΔFIM ≥ 22 model (AUC = 0.688) and good discrimination for the FAS ≥ 4 model (AUC = 0.878).ConclusionsEmergency department organization and acute stroke care processes may be associated not only with early clinical outcomes but also with functional and motor gains achieved during rehabilitation. In particular, stroke code activation and IVT were associated with more favorable rehabilitation outcomes in several models, whereas LVO was associated with less favorable gains in selected domains. These findings are consistent with the value of integrated stroke care pathways linking emergency care and rehabilitation.

Background

Stroke is a major public health problem that affects a large proportion of the population owing to its high incidence and mortality and substantially reduces quality of life by causing persistent disability among survivors. According to recent World Stroke Organization data, stroke continues to impose a major clinical and economic burden, and rehabilitation remains a core component of stroke care [1, 2]. Contemporary approaches emphasize that acute treatment and rehabilitation should be integrated within a continuous care pathway planned from the early phase onward [3–6]. In acute ischemic stroke, time intervals from symptom onset to hospital presentation, hospital arrival to neuroimaging, and neuroimaging to reperfusion therapy are key determinants of outcomes. Evidence increasingly supports that streamlined emergency department workflows, systematic tracking of time metrics, and “stroke code” activation can reduce treatment delays. Yet, most studies focus on short-term outcomes, and the relationship between acute-phase process indicators/interventions and subsequent rehabilitation gains remains less well characterized [7, 8]. In this context, evaluating the impact of early emergency department management on the rehabilitation trajectory and functional outcomes is important for refining clinical strategies and strengthening multidisciplinary care. Predicting rehabilitation outcomes is essential for goal setting, patient–family counseling, resource planning, and clinical decision-making. Admission-based predictive models highlight the prognostic value of baseline function, neurological severity, and multidimensional assessments [9–12]. However, few studies have examined ED interventions and process time metrics within the same framework in relation to rehabilitation outcomes [7–12]. Because functional recovery is multidimensional—encompassing activities of daily living, ambulation, and motor recovery—there is a need for studies using multidomain outcomes to clarify which functional domains are most strongly related to acute emergency department management. The aim of this study was to examine the association between emergency department time-performance indicators and acute treatment practices after first-ever stroke and the functional and motor gains achieved by the end of a rehabilitation program delivered in a physical medicine and rehabilitation (PM&R) clinic.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was designed and conducted as a single-center, retrospective, observational cohort study. Reporting followed the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) recommendations for observational research. Patients who presented to the emergency department with a first-ever stroke and subsequently received a rehabilitation program in the PM&R clinic after acute-phase management were included. Both ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke were included because the aim was to evaluate emergency department stroke-care processes and subsequent rehabilitation outcomes across the real-world first-ever stroke population referred for PM&R-based rehabilitation in our center. The study period spanned from January 2022 to July 2025. Data were extracted from the hospital electronic health records, emergency department records, and rehabilitation assessment forms.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. Ethical approval was obtained from the local institutional review board prior to study initiation (Düzce University, 2025/178; 28 July 2025). Given the retrospective design of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. All data were anonymized prior to analysis.

Participants

Patients were eligible if they (i) presented to the emergency department with a first-ever stroke, (ii) subsequently received a stroke rehabilitation program in the PM&R clinic after acute-phase management, (iii) had at least one functional or motor assessment recorded at rehabilitation baseline and at the end of the rehabilitation episode (or at discharge for inpatients) (FIM, FAS, and/or Brunnstrom staging), and (iv) had available data for the emergency department process/intervention variables defined in the study. Patients were excluded if they had a history of recurrent stroke (i.e., not first-ever), missing baseline or end-of-episode/discharge rehabilitation assessments, missing key time/intervention data in acute emergency department records, or a final diagnosis of a non-stroke neurological condition.

Data collection and variables

Demographic and clinical variables included age (years), sex, stroke subtype (ischemic/hemorrhagic), neurological severity at presentation assessed using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), comorbidities, and hand dominance. Emergency department process and intervention variables comprised the presence of large vessel occlusion (LVO), “stroke code” activation, intensive care unit admission, intravenous thrombolysis (IVT), endovascular treatment (EVT), initiation of antithrombotic therapy, onset-to-emergency department arrival time (minutes), door-to-imaging time (minutes), and emergency department length of stay (minutes). For interpretability in statistical models, time variables were scaled per 10-minute increase.

Rehabilitation assessments

Patients received rehabilitation either as inpatients or outpatients according to routine clinical practice, based on functional status, medical stability, and rehabilitation needs as determined by the treating PM&R team. To improve comparability across settings, outcomes were defined using the rehabilitation baseline assessment and the end-of-episode/discharge assessment. In our setting, the rehabilitation episode was based on a 30-day program, and the PM&R baseline assessment corresponded to the rehabilitation baseline evaluation. Rehabilitation duration was additionally reflected by FIM efficiency (ΔFIM/day). Data on the FIM, FAS, and Brunnstrom staging were extracted from standardized assessment forms completed at rehabilitation baseline and at the end of the rehabilitation episode (or discharge, as applicable). Total FIM scores were recorded at baseline and end point; functional change was calculated as ΔFIM = FIM_end − FIM_baseline. FIM efficiency was calculated as ΔFIM per rehabilitation duration (days) (ΔFIM/day) [13]. Ambulation level was recorded using FAS at baseline and end point, and ΔFAS (category change) was calculated. Ambulation outcomes were defined as at least one-category improvement (ΔFAS ≥ 1) and independent walking at the end of the rehabilitation episode (FAS ≥ 4) [14]. Brunnstrom motor recovery stages were recorded at baseline and end point for the upper extremity, hand, and lower extremity. ΔBrunnstrom (stage change) was computed for each domain, and at least one-stage gain (≥ 1 stage) was defined as a binary outcome [15]. Brunnstrom staging was used to characterize segment-specific motor recovery trajectories rather than as a stand-alone indicator of clinically meaningful functional improvement or functional independence.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was clinically meaningful functional improvement, defined using the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) threshold. MCID refers to the smallest change in an outcome measure considered clinically meaningful. Based on the reported MCID for change in total FIM score in stroke rehabilitation, clinically meaningful improvement was defined as ΔFIM ≥ 22 (0 = no, 1 = yes) [16, 17]. Secondary outcomes included ΔFIM (continuous), FIM efficiency (ΔFIM/day), ΔFAS ≥ 1, independent walking at the end of the rehabilitation episode (FAS ≥ 4), and at least one-stage gain in Brunnstrom staging for the upper extremity, hand, and lower extremity [16, 17]. Brunnstrom-based outcomes were considered exploratory motor recovery measures and were interpreted separately from functional outcomes derived from FIM and FAS.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation when normally distributed and as median (interquartile range [IQR]) when non-normally distributed. Categorical variables are reported as counts (n) and percentages (%). Changes between rehabilitation baseline and the end of the rehabilitation episode were evaluated using a paired-samples t-test for total FIM scores and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for ordinal measures (FAS and Brunnstrom stages). The effect size of the paired change in FIM was reported as Cohen’s dz. To identify factors associated with clinically meaningful improvement (ΔFIM ≥ 22), a multivariable logistic regression model was fitted, and results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values. The model included a priori selected covariates: age, sex, NIHSS score at presentation, baseline FIM, presence of LVO, stroke code activation, intensive care unit admission, IVT, EVT, initiation of antithrombotic therapy, and time variables scaled per 10-minute increase. Variable selection was conducted using a clinically driven reduction approach, without stepwise procedures. To assess the association between emergency department factors and functional gains on a continuous scale, multivariable linear regression was performed with ΔFIM as the dependent variable; regression coefficients (B), standard errors, 95% CIs, and p-values are reported. Separate multivariable logistic regression models were constructed for ambulation and motor recovery outcomes, including ΔFAS ≥ 1, independent walking at the end of the rehabilitation episode (FAS ≥ 4), and ≥ 1-stage improvement in Brunnstrom staging for the upper extremity, hand, and lower extremity. For each model, ORs, 95% CIs, p-values, and Nagelkerke’s pseudo-R² are reported. Discrimination of logistic models for binary outcomes was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with the area under the curve (AUC) and 95% CIs reported. Where applicable, the optimal cut-off was determined using the Youden index, and sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and accuracy were calculated at the selected threshold. Reporting of prediction-model results was guided by the TRIPOD principles. For missing data, analyses were conducted on complete cases (listwise deletion) for variables included in each model, and the sample size (n) is reported for each analysis. For descriptive change analyses, the number of patients contributing paired baseline and end-of-episode data was reported separately for each rehabilitation measure. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 257 patients were included (mean age 65.02 ± 13.55 years; 38.9% female). The number of patients included in each rehabilitation outcome analysis is presented in Table 2, and the number of complete cases included in the multivariable models is reported in the relevant tables. Baseline demographic, clinical, and rehabilitation characteristics are summarized in Table 1. In an exploratory subgroup comparison, clinically meaningful functional improvement did not differ significantly between ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke, whereas independent ambulation at the end of the rehabilitation episode was less frequent in patients with hemorrhagic stroke. Exploratory analyses according to hand dominance and lesion laterality did not show clear differences in the main rehabilitation outcomes. Significant improvement was observed in functional measures, and changes were also observed in motor recovery stage measures, by the end of the rehabilitation episode. Total FIM and ambulation improved significantly during rehabilitation (Table 2). Independent walking was achieved by 78.6% of patients, and 45.9% showed clinically meaningful functional improvement. In our setting, this efficiency measure reflected change over a rehabilitation episode based on a 30-day program. Brunnstrom stages changed from a median of 3 at baseline to 4 at the end point for the upper extremity, hand, and lower extremity (all p < 0.001). The proportions achieving at least a one-stage gain were 58.6% for the upper extremity, 55.5% for the hand, and 64.5% for the lower extremity (Table 2). These findings were interpreted as motor recovery stage changes rather than direct measures of functional independence. Due to missing data, multivariable models were fitted in 224 patients. In the ΔFIM ≥ 22 model, female sex, stroke code activation, IVT, and antithrombotic initiation were positively associated with clinically meaningful improvement. NIHSS, baseline FIM, and LVO were negatively associated (Table 3). For ambulation improvement (ΔFAS ≥ 1), NIHSS was positively associated, while female sex and LVO were negatively associated; stroke code activation, IVT, and initiation of antithrombotic therapy were positively associated (Table 4). In Brunnstrom gain models, stroke code activation was associated with upper-extremity and hand gains, whereas IVT was associated with lower-extremity gains (Table 5). Accordingly, stroke code activation and IVT emerged as factors consistently associated with better outcomes across global functional improvement as well as ambulation and motor recovery domains. In exploratory unadjusted comparisons, outcome differences between thrombolysed and non-thrombolysed patients were not consistently evident; however, IVT remained positively associated with several outcomes in the adjusted models. The discriminative performance of the model for clinically meaningful FIM improvement was modest (AUC = 0.688), whereas the model for independent ambulation at the end of the rehabilitation episode showed good discriminative ability (AUC = 0.878). This suggests that ambulation outcomes were captured more clearly than global FIM change by the selected acute-phase variables (Figs. 1 and 2).

Discussion

The main finding of this study is that patients demonstrated significant improvements in global functional independence, ambulation, and motor recovery by the end of the rehabilitation episode. Several acute-phase ED variables, particularly stroke code activation and IVT, were associated with better rehabilitation outcomes. In contrast, LVO was associated with less favorable outcomes in selected models. These findings should not be interpreted causally. They likely reflect the combined influence of stroke severity, patient selection, system organization, timely treatment, and continuity of care. The positive association between NIHSS and ΔFAS ≥ 1 should be interpreted cautiously. Patients with greater initial impairment may have had more room for measurable improvement in ambulation. This finding may therefore reflect, at least in part, a greater potential for measurable gain among patients with poorer baseline ambulation status rather than a more favorable overall recovery profile. Exploratory comparison by stroke subtype also suggested some heterogeneity in ambulation outcomes, although the relatively small hemorrhagic subgroup limits firm interpretation. Previous studies have shown that structured prehospital and in-hospital stroke workflows, including time-sensitive protocols, are associated with improved acute stroke care performance and selected clinical outcomes [7, 18]. Our findings extend this concept by suggesting that organized acute-phase processes may also be associated with rehabilitation gains. Nevertheless, the impact of acute stroke care pathways may not always translate directly into functional outcomes. For example, in a study evaluating a nationally standardized stroke care pathway, some process indicators and discharge patterns improved, yet no marked difference was observed in 90-day functional outcome [19]. This is important when interpreting our findings. Associations between ED process variables and rehabilitation gains do not necessarily imply a direct cause–effect relationship. They may also reflect patient selection, stroke severity, organizational quality, team experience, and continuity of care [7, 8, 19]. In particular, stroke code activation can be considered not merely a process marker but a system-level indicator that facilitates rapid evaluation, imaging prioritization, team coordination, and greater access to timely treatment [8]. The finding that IV thrombolysis and initiation of antithrombotic therapy were positively associated with some models is also clinically meaningful. Reperfusion therapies are known to support early neurological improvement and better long-term functional outcomes. Our study suggests that this relationship may also be reflected in rehabilitation gains [2, 3, 6]. However, findings related to EVT should be interpreted with particular caution, because the EVT subgroup in our cohort was very small and the corresponding estimates were imprecise. Contemporary observational data regarding rehabilitation practices and outcomes among patients receiving reperfusion therapy emphasize that rehabilitation is critical for both access and outcomes in this population [20, 21]. IV thrombolysis may improve ambulation and some motor outcomes by preserving tissue in the acute phase and providing an early advantage for neuroplasticity. However, this explanation requires confirmation in prospective time-dependent studies [20, 21].

In our study, the stronger ROC performance of the FAS ≥ 4 (independent ambulation) model compared with the ΔFIM ≥ 22 model (higher AUC) may be explained by several factors. The stronger discrimination of the independent ambulation model may reflect the more concrete and homogeneous nature of FAS ≥ 4 compared with the broader and more composite structure of total FIM. In addition, the inverse association of baseline FIM suggests that ceiling and floor effects may have influenced the ΔFIM ≥ 22 model [9, 13, 14]. Differences across Brunnstrom subdomains (upper extremity, hand, and lower extremity) are clinically expected, as distinct predictors may show varying significance across these outcomes. However, these Brunnstrom-based outcomes should be interpreted as indicators of motor recovery pattern rather than direct evidence of functional gain, particularly because early stage transitions may not necessarily translate into meaningful real-world functional improvement. Brunnstrom staging is practical and widely used in clinical settings, and the ability to track upper extremity, hand, and lower extremity components separately is valuable for demonstrating heterogeneity of motor recovery across segments [12, 15]. Studies addressing the clinical utility, validation, and responsiveness of the Brunnstrom scale also support this approach [12, 15, 22]. Hand function depends on more complex corticospinal control. In contrast, lower-extremity and gait gains are influenced more by balance, trunk control, compensation, and task-specific repetition. This may explain why some acute variables were associated more strongly with certain subdomains [23, 24]. Thus, analyzing Brunnstrom in three separate subdomains may yield more clinically meaningful information than using a single composite motor score.

This study has several limitations. First, its single-center retrospective design introduces the possibility of selection bias and information bias related to record quality. In addition, the cohort consisted only of patients who survived the acute phase and were subsequently referred for rehabilitation, which may have introduced survivor bias and referral selection bias.In addition, inclusion of both ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke may have introduced clinical heterogeneity, and this should be considered when interpreting the pooled analyses. Second, complete-case analysis reduced the sample size of multivariable models to 224 patients and may have affected statistical power. Third, confounding by indication is likely for some ED variables; for example, stroke code activation or IV thrombolysis may reflect both system efficiency and appropriate patient selection rather than the isolated effect of a specific intervention. Fourth, functional and motor assessments were performed by different evaluators in routine practice, which may have introduced measurement variability. Fifth, the inclusion of both inpatient and outpatient rehabilitation pathways may have introduced heterogeneity related to treatment intensity, duration, and discharge criteria. In addition, although the rehabilitation episode in our setting was based on a 30-day program, standardized data on the exact time from acute stroke care to the rehabilitation baseline assessment were not consistently available, which limited more detailed interpretation of rehabilitation timing. Finally, the lack of external validating outcomes such as 3- or 6-month modified Rankin Scale scores or quality-of-life measures limits interpretation of rehabilitation gains within the broader post-stroke prognosis.

Clinically, this study provides a framework that “bridges” organizational quality indicators in acute stroke care and rehabilitation outcomes. In particular, stroke code activation, access to reperfusion therapy (IVT), and early medical management indicators may relate not only to acute mortality/morbidity but also to subacute rehabilitation gains [7, 8, 18–21]. Accordingly, the ED and rehabilitation phases of the stroke care continuum should be considered as parts of an integrated process rather than independent stages [2–6]. Future multicenter, prospective studies incorporating standardized time metrics, early neurological improvement indices, rehabilitation dose/intensity, and long-term post-discharge functional outcomes are needed to clarify the causal structure of these relationships. In addition, incorporating more detailed motor scales and patient-reported outcome measures alongside FIM, FAS, and Brunnstrom may enable more sensitive assessment of upper extremity/hand recovery in particular [23, 24].

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that emergency department organization and acute treatment processes in the early stroke phase may be associated with functional and motor gains achieved during rehabilitation. Although significant improvements were observed across FIM, FAS, and Brunnstrom measures, these associations should be interpreted cautiously because of the retrospective single-center design and possible residual confounding. Overall, the results support the importance of continuity-based stroke care models linking emergency care with rehabilitation and warrant confirmation in multicenter prospective studies.
