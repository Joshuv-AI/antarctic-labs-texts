Cross-border collaboration in a major incident: emergency medical services response to a train crash
Authors: Peter Martin Hansen, Dana Raub, Sanne Bjerre, Jesper Broesby, Trond Nuland Fedog, Jakob Kjersgaard Johansen, Søren Mikkelsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOn 15 August 2025, a fatal train crash happened in Southern Denmark following a collision with a truck at an unsecured railway crossing, resulting in derailment and one overturned carriage. One person was killed, and 29 people were injured. This case report aims to give a detailed description of the response of emergency medical services to evaluate cross-border and inter-authority cooperation as well as adherence to major incident guidelines and established communication pathways.Case presentationInitial calls indicated several critical patients and numerous school children on the train. Nineteen Danish and German units, including four helicopters, were dispatched to the scene. Access to the site was restricted to a single narrow countryside road, requiring careful organization of staging areas, access/egress routes, and casualty clearing stations. A Joint Incident Command was formed, and patients were triaged, treated, and registered using an electronic prehospital system allowing for continuous coordination with the dispatch center for hospital allocation. Two critically injured patients were transported by helicopter to two trauma centers in Denmark and Germany, and the remaining patients were distributed across three regional hospitals. The response to this incident largely aligned with national guidelines and cross-border cooperation agreements. However, delays in incident management were identified, and responders criticized infrequent training opportunities for major incidents. The rate of unauthorized radio shifts was lower than previously described.ConclusionsThis report describes the response to a major incident, characterized by effective inter-authority cooperation and successful cross-border collaboration between Danish and German emergency services. Routine collaboration during everyday responses proved crucial to ensure efficient collaboration during crises. While adherence to major incident protocols was generally high, challenges remained regarding the site organization and timely establishment of command structures. The observed delays may reflect limited experience with major incident management among regular responders, prompting the need for comprehensive incident command training of prehospital physicians. Compared to previous incidents, communication consistency was notably improved, likely due to dedicated training efforts after prior communication failure. The incident underscored the importance of joint inter-authority response concepts and the central coordinating role of the dispatch center. Background

A major incident (MI) is characterized by resource demands that exceed those readily available [1, 2]. Scandinavian MIs are infrequent, and therefore, MI reporting is important [3] to enhance knowledge and preparedness. Although trains are generally considered safe [4], train crashes often develop into a MI [5].

Case reports about MI management consistently identify interagency collaboration, particularly communication and early joint coordination, among the key determinants of successful incident management [6]. These challenges may be further exacerbated in border regions, where MI scenarios require effective collaboration not only between responding agencies but also across national jurisdictions [7].

In this report, we describe the emergency medical service (EMS) response to a MI that developed from a train crash, with a particular focus on incident site organization, communication, and inter-authority as well as cross-border collaboration. The objectives of this report are to evaluate adherence to current national MI guidelines, assess the effectiveness of inter-agency and cross-border collaboration, and identify key insights that may inform future MI responses.

Case presentation

On August 15, 2025, a train crash occurred in rural Southern Denmark when a train collided with a truck at an unsecured railway crossing. The incident resulted in one fatality and 29 injured individuals.

Study design

This case report adheres to the CONFIDE (CONsensus guidelines on Reports of Field Observations in Disasters and Emergencies) concept [8] (Additional file 1).

Emergency medical services in Denmark and Germany

The Danish three-tiered prehospital system consists of ambulances, rapid response units, and physician-staffed mobile emergency care units (MECUs) or helicopter emergency medical services (HEMS). It has been thoroughly described in scientific literature [5, 7–13]. Further, the Royal Danish Air Force 722nd squadron may assist civil authorities in MI scenarios. National response and MI guidelines are uniform in all of Denmark.

The incident presented in this report was managed by the emergency medical communication center (EMCC) of Southern Denmark, based in Odense. In addition to the dispatch team, a prehospital physician is present at the EMCC during daytime on weekdays and on call in case of a MI outside of these hours. Their tasks include the allocation of patients to hospitals from the incident site, based on precise knowledge of hospital capacities and capabilities as well as prehospital triage results.

In Germany, the responsibility for providing prehospital services rests with the individual sixteen federal states. Typically, this responsibility is delegated to the municipalities, which either offer EMS through local fire services or contract non-profit organizations and privately owned companies. Consequently, response models and organizational frameworks may vary considerably across federal states and even municipalities.

German emergency calls are handled by advanced life support (ALS) ambulances, typically staffed by one emergency medical technician (EMT) with 520 h of training (versus Danish EMTs: 3 years and 7 months of training) and one paramedic with three years of training (versus Danish paramedics: EMT training plus 1.5 years of experience and 6 months of additional training). By contrast, German basic life support (BLS) ambulances handle planned transfers and are commonly staffed with two EMTs, or one EMT accompanied by an entry-level EMS provider (160–320 h of training). If deemed necessary, an additional MECU (or HEMS) may be deployed to the scene, carrying a paramedic and a licensed prehospital emergency physician. Most commonly, German prehospital physicians are anesthesiologists, trauma surgeons, or internists.

During MIs, additional resources include an on-call organizational EMS commander (paramedic with leadership training) and an on-call commanding prehospital physician. Until these specialized resources arrive, the crew of the first MECU assumes provisional command.

Infrastructure in the border region

Denmark and Germany share a 68 km land border. Along this border, Denmark commands ten ALS ambulances, one paramedic-manned rapid response car, and two anesthesiologist-staffed MECUs. The nearest HEMS base is located at Billund Airport (96 km from the border). In the corresponding border region, Germany provides 47 ALS ambulances, seven BLS ambulances, and eight MECUs. In addition, HEMS is provided from a base at Niebüll Hospital within 20 km of the border. All prehospital services in this area are coordinated by the regional EMCC Nord in Harrislee. Both the German and Danish border regions conduct radio communication via Terrestrial Trunked Radio (TETRA) [14].

Trauma center classification in Denmark and Germany follows a hierarchical structure similar to the level-based system defined by the American College of Surgeons Committee on Trauma [15]. The Region of Southern Denmark provides seven hospitals (Fig. 1). Three of those are trauma units at level 2 or lower, and Odense University Hospital is the level 1 trauma center. The catchment population of this trauma center is approximately 1,240,000 people. The corresponding German region with its three municipalities and a total of 455,000 permanent residents hosts seven hospitals, of which the Diako Hospital Flensburg serves as a level 1 trauma center.

Major incident guidelines and preparedness

The Danish framework for MI management includes guidelines for joint incident command (JIC) [16], describing the roles of authorities involved in a MI. Built on sector responsibility and inter- authority cooperation, the framework is divided into the strategic, operational, and tactical levels (Additional file 2). Incident commanders undergo a joint three-week training together and, thus, apply shared concepts during MI response. The course incorporates extensive training in MI management and radio communication, as well as several table-top and full-scale exercises.

When a MI is declared in Denmark, temporary inter-authority radio communication channels are issued by Danish police for mutual use by police, fire services, and EMS. During MI management, EMS use additional radio channels for direct communication with the EMCC and the involved hospitals.

This report assesses adherence to the key pillars of Danish MI guidelines in the presented scenario, i.e., timely recognition of the MI and activation of resources, site organization, inter-authority cooperation, cross-border collaboration, communication on site and with external resources, adequate resource allocation, early downscaling and return to normal state, as well as defusing/debriefing of involved staff.

Alarm and dispatch

At 16:45, the Danish national distress number received the first call from the incident site. The call was relayed to the EMCC in Odense at 16:47, and a MI was declared at 16:50. The EMCC promptly dispatched five ambulances, two MECUs, and three HEMS units (Table 1). Shortly after, the Search-and-Rescue (SAR) helicopter from the Royal Danish Air Force was commissioned on in-flight request from the first arriving Danish HEMS physician. As per request of the arriving ambulance officer, five additional ambulances from Germany were dispatched to the incident. During the ongoing management of the incident, one additional ambulance was commissioned from the neighboring region for tasks not related to the MI. Further, the on-call prehospital physician was summoned to the EMCC.

Over the course of the incident, a total of eleven ambulances, three MECUs, one paramedic-manned rapid response unit, three HEMS units, and one SAR helicopter were dispatched to the incident site (Table 1).

Scene description

The train crash happened in a rural area between Tinglev and Kliplev in the southern part of Denmark, 11 km from the German border. At an unsecured railway crossing near a cornfield, an express train collided with a 40-ton tank truck carrying animal waste (manure) that was crossing the tracks. The train was of the IR4 type, carrying 95 passengers and two crew members, with a speed of 100 km/h at the time of the incident. Upon impact, two of the four train wagons derailed, overturning one of them. The truck was thrusted into the cornfield. The temperature at the incident site was 24.1 degrees centigrade with no precipitation, and the wind speed was 6.3 m/s.

Site access and organization

The incident site was only accessible from the narrow countryside road crossing the railway tracks, from both the southwest and northeast (Fig. 2, Additional file 3). The area around the partly derailed train and the manure-loaded tank truck was declared the hot zone (i.e., exclusive to fire service personnel) by the fire brigade, establishing inner and outer cordons around the incident scene.

Incident site organization

Upon arrival, the ambulance officer declared the southwestern access as the primary ambulance staging area. Due to the narrow road and the arriving vehicles, passing the incident site was difficult, and it was decided to use the northeastern part of the road as a second staging area. Once the police incident commander arrived at the scene, their vehicle was used for incident command meetings, documentation, and registration of resources. Two interim casualty clearing stations (CCSs) were appointed by police at each end of the incident site. An evacuation point for uninjured passengers was established in a large private building, roughly 900 m northeast of the CCSs.

By this time, German HEMS had landed on the road at the pilot’s discretion. To avoid crowding at the scene, the first Danish HEMS landed south of the incident site at a nearby farm’s parking lot. This landing zone was also used by the next two arriving helicopters. Several people had evacuated themselves using the train doors and escaping from the overturned coach through windows using emergency hammers.

Assisted by uninjured passengers and bystanders, fire brigade personnel evacuated passengers from the train to the roadside, while EMS personnel initiated assessment and registration of the evacuees at the two CCSs, thus outside of the hazardous zone.

Hospital preparedness

After the initial assessment, the EMCC physician alerted four Danish hospitals and one German hospital in and close to the affected region, requesting that they prepare for incoming patients. Communication with the receiving hospitals was conducted via a digital tool providing text-based messaging and an overview of available resources at each facility. As events unfolded, one of the four Danish hospitals activated their mass casualty protocols. Further, the Diako Hospital in Flensburg did so independently, in line with local guidelines.

Medical incident command

The first arriving Danish HEMS physician assumed the role of Medical Incident Commander (MIC) after consulting with the MECU physicians already on site. The HEMS crew assisted the MIC in providing an overview of the incident site and the approximate number of suspected casualties, while continuing the treatment of critically injured patients. Upon arrival of commanders from the police and fire & rescue, the JIC convened.

Pre-defined inter-authority communication lines were issued by police, adhering to the established framework. All upcoming tasks and resources relevant to each sector were documented on a whiteboard. At this point, the estimated number of 95 passengers (and two crew members) on the train was relayed to the JIC from Danish State Railways, based on the electronic scanning of passenger tickets prior to the crash.

The first German HEMS on site treated the first critical patient, and the two MECU teams were joined by the second Danish HEMS team, taking care of the second critical patient and conducting triage and registration of the remaining patients. When the third MECU arrived, this physician was appointed CCS officer. Thirty-five minutes after the incident occurred, the SAR helicopter arrived and was tasked by the MIC with the search for a missing person, using the forward-looking infrared (FLIR) camera capabilities of the helicopter.

Patients were evaluated and treated by EMS personnel and registered in the electronic prehospital medical record system which can also integrate photographs (Dedalus Healthcare, Aarhus, Denmark) [17, 18]. The EMCC physician received continuous updates on the progress of registration, assessment of each casualty’s condition and injuries. In collaboration with the MIC and the ambulance officer, the EMCC physician determined the most appropriate receiving hospital for each patient. The decision was based on hospital capability and capacity, made electronically available to the EMCC physician.

The JIC reconvened regularly to coordinate the collaboration between authorities. The MIC released the SAR helicopter after the FLIR search confirmed there were no casualties inside the cornfield. As resource requirements were progressively clarified, ambulances and MECUs were released accordingly. Once the last patient had left the incident site, commanders were released from the incident site (Additional file 4). A first defusing session was offered to responders involved in the incident immediately after the on-site incident management had concluded, followed by a technical debriefing four days after the incident.

Radio communication

As per MI guideline, police relayed a default channel destined for the JIC and issued specific interdisciplinary channels. In total, there were 28 radios in use by EMS in the incident. 25 (89.3%) of those were switched to the designated channels. Records indicate that all non-standard channel switches were directed to the default JIC channel reserved for authorized units and personnel.

Patient characteristics and management

As per MI guideline, initial patient registration was conducted using the electronic prehospital medical record system [17, 18]. The system enables the ambulance officer, CCS officer, and the MIC to obtain a complete overview of patients assessed by EMS. After a wristband has been placed on the patient, it is scanned into a tablet and serves as a unique identifier for the duration of EMS involvement in the patient pathway.

Triage was performed by the treating physicians, the CCS officer and EMS personnel using the Danish Emergency Process Triage (DEPT), a five-category algorithm [19]. During the initial triage, one individual on the train was declared dead at the scene, while two patients were identified as critically injured (red), four as moderately injured (yellow/orange), and 23 as lightly injured (green). Correspondingly, each patient was assigned a transport priority. The EMCC physician distributed patients to a total of five different hospitals across Southern Denmark (Aabenraa, Kolding, Esbjerg, Odense) and Germany (Flensburg) based on the on-site triage category documented in the prehospital patient record system and verbal report from the ambulance officer.

The two patients in critical condition suffered from blunt trauma, including neurotrauma and limb fractures. Due to their injuries and subsequent transport priority, these patients were transported by HEMS and admitted to two level one trauma centers in Germany and Denmark, 20 and 136 km away, respectively.

Most other patients suffered from fractured limbs or bruises and minor cuts from blunt trauma. The four moderately injured and all lightly injured patients were distributed among three regional trauma units in Aabenraa, Kolding, and Esbjerg, located 17, 70 and 101 km from the incident site, respectively. This included a group of fifteen school pupils and their two lightly injured teachers without immediate treatment requirements who were admitted as a group to the emergency department in Kolding. All were transported in a chartered bus and were accompanied by a paramedic carrying standard medical equipment.

Psychosocial support services were established at receiving facilities in Aabenraa and Kolding.

Chemical, biological, radiological, nuclear and explosives (CBRNE) aspects

Due to the hazardous properties of the animal waste in the tanker, fire services established capabilities to enable the cleansing of affected individuals prior to boarding the ambulances. However, decontamination was ultimately not required. Five uninjured passengers cleansed themselves at the facility. Manure emits methane (CH4), ammonia (NH3) and hydrogen sulphide (H2S), and therefore, the use of personal protective equipment is recommended [20]. In this case - outdoors - boots, impregnated outerwear and rubber gloves were used.

Discussion

The incident described in this report resulted in one fatality and 29 injured patients. The response involved not only EMS, fire services, and police personnel, but also effective collaboration between Danish and German dispatch centers and providers on scene. In addition, a military helicopter was deployed to assist with a civilian major incident.

Adherence to existing MI protocols was generally acceptable. The following paragraphs will elaborate on distinct key factors for success and lessons identified for future incidents.

Activation and deactivation of resources

There was an interval of approximately three minutes between the initial emergency call to the dispatch center and the formal declaration of a MI. This short delay may reflect the inherent difficulty of establishing situational awareness based on early and often fragmented information, as a comprehensive understanding of an MI typically requires multiple incoming reports. Additionally, the relative rarity of MIs may contribute to a lower initial level of suspicion among dispatch personnel, potentially delaying recognition. These considerations underscore the importance of regular awareness and training initiatives for dispatch center staff to support timely identification of such events.

In line with the definition of a MI, the resources initially dispatched through the automated protocol were insufficient. Upon arrival of the ambulance officer, additional resources, including units from Germany through established cross-border agreements, were rapidly mobilized.

According to the action principle in Danish crisis management [21], the initial response to a MI should incorporate a certain degree of overtriage. In this case, the number of injured individuals was ultimately lower than initially anticipated, and EMS units were thus progressively released from the MI scenario.

This incident reflects the inherent challenge of MI management, requiring a balance between under-activation of resources and the risk of depleting capacity needed to cater to concurrent routine emergencies. In conclusion, continuous and dynamic reassessment of resource requirements, along with the timely release of excess EMS units, should be prioritized by both the commanders on scene and the EMCC.

Inter-authority collaboration

Once established, the JIC functioned in accordance with the national MI management guidelines [16] (Additional file 2). In line with the principle of sector responsibility, a shared situational awareness among the involved authorities was effectively established and maintained. Joint management of this MI was facilitated through regular coordination meetings and the involvement of liaison personnel with specialized expertise from, for example, Danish State Railways, the Danish Emergency Management Agency, and Banedanmark (Danish governmental agency responsible for developing, maintaining, and operating state railway infrastructure).

The effectiveness of the JIC structure is likely attributable to the Danish model of training designated personnel for incident command roles through a mandatory, three-week interagency training program. This approach fosters not only a shared response framework and terminology, but also familiarity among responders within this specific region. While such joint training is not yet standard across all of Europe [22], existing scientific literature indicates that shared concepts and terminology enhance inter-authority collaboration, while personal familiarity among responders increases trust and effective coordination [22, 23]. Joint training programs should therefore be considered a quality standard for MI preparedness and management.

Nevertheless, the establishment and initial meeting of the JIC were delayed for up to twenty minutes. This observation echoes the need for incident command training of all Danish prehospital physicians, as the delay was likely attributable to the initial complexity of tasks inherent in major incidents, as well as the time taken for the arrival of a prehospital physician with the aforementioned MI training.

Communication

Contrasting previous MIs in Denmark [5, 24, 25], radio communication within and between emergency authorities was consistent with guidelines for the majority of radio users in this incident. Non-standard shifts were likely due to unfamiliarity with MI communication standards and task overload. Communication difficulties are commonly described in sudden-onset MI scenarios, related to factors such as lack of training, compromised inter-authority cooperation and unintuitive interface design [25–27]. It has been well described in scientific literature that, under stress, responders tend to revert to behaviors and practices that are most familiar to them, typically those reinforced through frequent training or regular activity [25, 28]. The improved rate of radio grid adherence reported for this incident is likely due to increased radio training efforts within EMS. After radio communication failure in previous incidents, frequent micro training of Danish EMS providers has been established [25].

The use of forced steering or patching of radios, i.e. the technical process of interconnecting multiple radio systems from a control room without direct human input, may further enhance communication success. This approach is used by several Danish authorities, i.e., during special operations. However, there is reluctance towards patching among Danish emergency authorities, mostly related to concerns regarding information security [29]. Thus, forced steering/patching was not employed in the management of this scenario.

Furthermore, this incident presented the particular challenge of a narrow road serving as the sole access route, meaning that mismanagement of incoming forces could have easily compromised the overall response. Maintaining clear access and egress routes is of critical importance in MI scenarios. Standardized communication concepts, such as the METHANE acronym [30], facilitate the communication of such essential information about the incident site to incoming resources.

EMCC perspective

As per MI guideline, the on-call physician was summoned to the EMCC immediately after the MI was identified. To ensure full operational functionality even outside standard working hours, it is of utmost importance that both pre-defined response concepts and established lines of communication, including radio systems, are available to ensure the initial coordination of a MI.

The prehospital medical record system proved to be essential for maintaining an overview of patients and resources both on site and at the EMCC. However, reliance on the electronic prehospital patient journal as the sole tool of communication in MI events is generally not advisable. It is imperative that there are several ways of communication to ensure redundancy. The EMCC digital tool for communication with hospitals provided a secure backbone for relaying information from different sources.

Centralizing responsibility for communication with receiving hospitals to the EMCC physician may further reduce the inherently high organizational burden on on-site medical personnel, thereby allowing for greater focus on immediate tasks and clinical decision-making on scene.

Resource allocation

Of the two casualties triaged red, both were allocated the highest available medical resources on site and transported by HEMS to level one trauma facilities, respectively.

Lightly injured patients were transported to regional trauma centers via ground-based ambulances. The group of school pupils were transported together with their teachers in a bus accompanied by a paramedic, enabling dynamic re-triage and treatment if needed. This approach allowed for the transport of the involved children without exposing them to additional stress caused by separation from their peers. Whenever possible, the authors of this report strongly encourage context-sensitive solutions to avoid secondary traumatization of vulnerable subgroups.

The five-category triage algorithm (DEPT) [19] applied in this incident may appear more complex to providers accustomed to algorithms with fewer categories. However, its longstanding use as the Danish standard highlights the importance of both familiarity with established concepts and adherence to one standardized system, promoting consistency across responders and supporting its effective use in high-stress situations [31].

MI may compromise hospital surge capacity, and therefore, hospital preparedness is of major importance [32]. Despite early notification from the EMCC physician to the receiving hospitals, only two of the five involved institutions activated their mass casualty protocol. Such a decision typically rests with hospital management and senior physicians, considering that the activation of these protocols puts all regular hospital activities on hold. Thus, this decision should be made based on careful weighing of all relevant factors.

Cross-border collaboration

Collaboration with German EMS and ambulance crews was perceived as effective and consistent with established guidelines. One key factor contributing to successful collaboration was the ambulance officer’s proficiency in German. As a native German now living in Denmark, he served as the main point of contact for German colleagues. Language and mutual cultural understanding play crucial roles in cross-border operations.

Collaborative efforts were undertaken between the Danish EMCC in Odense and the German EMCC Nord in Harrislee. Given their relative proximity, the two EMCCs have been maintaining a permanent operational connection. Recently, a dedicated radio connection between the two dispatch centers has been tested to further facilitate cooperation. Cross-border collaboration is reinforced through regular joint exercises and operations, as well as aligned response regulations between Danish and German fire services.

Formal contracts enabling prehospital collaboration between Denmark’s and Germany’s border regions have been in place and regularly updated since 1999. Based on these contracts, the Niebüll HEMS service Christoph Europa 5 routinely responds to missions in Denmark. German ground ambulances are also regularly dispatched to Danish territory. Cross-border collaboration has been monitored and facilitated by the Interreg program - more particularly, the DanGer 112 project [33], an EU initiative fostering cross-border collaboration in various sectors, including health and rescue services, infrastructure and digitalization.

In summary, cross-border collaboration in rural border regions should not be regarded as an exceptional arrangement reserved for MIs, but rather as a fundamental component of routine emergency preparedness and everyday operations.

EMS major incident preparedness

In Danish national crisis management, the command structure within the organization is well described (Additional file 2) [16, 21]. The nationwide applicability of standardized MI management guidelines in Denmark, in contrast to German guidelines which may vary between regions, represents a significant advantage in reducing the potential for misunderstandings.

During the post-incident debriefings, EMS personnel voiced concerns regarding limited opportunities for regular MI training. This was partly attributed to staff shortages which often prevent full-time personnel from participating in large-scale exercises. In contrast, voluntary providers may have greater flexibility to attend such training activities. This phenomenon has been described in previous literature [23] and seems common in countries that experience staff shortages in the medical sector. The subsequent disparity may result in uneven preparedness levels across provider groups and highlights the need for more accessible and sustainable training strategies.

Continued assessment of EMS performance in MI is important to adapt guidelines and preparedness concepts to real-life circumstances. Therefore, case reports and official evaluations of MI are pivotal [3, 34] for exercises and training to reflect real-life scenarios [35].

Strengths and limitations of the study

Comprehensive EMS data access constitutes one of the main strengths of this case report. Notably, several authors served in crucial roles during the incident, providing a precise picture of the MI and the subsequent management. The investigators are confident that transferability to similar organizations is substantive. However, there are limitations to the case report as well, such as recall and publication bias. Another limitation of this study may be the lack of reliable data on concurrent routine EMS calls in the affected region, which prevents assessment of the incident’s impact on the overall system. Further, the nature of this case report does not allow for the establishment of a causal relationship between the reported response measures and the outcomes of this incident.

Conclusion

This major incident resulting from a fatal train crash was managed largely in alignment with national guidelines, demonstrating effective inter-authority and cross-border collaboration. The restricted access via a rural road presented unique challenges for the organization of the incident site. Overall, the strong performance of personnel underscores the value of training skills specific to MI. Structured training, standardized communication, and early establishment of joint incident command remain key challenges during MI response efforts. The EMCC played a central role in coordination and should continue to be leveraged to reduce on-scene burden. Ongoing investment in accessible training and interoperable systems is essential to strengthen preparedness for future incidents. Joint training programs across responding agencies, particularly for incident command roles, should be institutionalized and integrated into routine preparedness efforts.
