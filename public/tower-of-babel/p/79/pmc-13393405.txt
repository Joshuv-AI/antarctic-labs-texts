Effect of 1% Schizochytrium limacinum supplementation on omega-3 enrichment of broiler meat under commercial field conditions☆
Authors: Kenan Muratović, Vedad Šakić, Dinada Tahirović, Jasmin Katica, Ćazim Crnkić
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the effects of dietary supplementation with 1% dried Schizochytrium limacinum on fatty acid composition and omega-3 (ω-3) enrichment of broiler meat under commercial field conditions. The aim was to assess ω-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) enrichment across different meat portions and to determine whether the resulting levels may meet criteria for nutritional labeling. A total of 36,905 Cobb 500 broilers were assigned to a control group fed a basal diet and an algae-supplemented group receiving the same diet with added microalgae. Production performance parameters were recorded, and breast, leg, and wing meat samples were analyzed for fatty acid composition using gas chromatography. Supplementation did not affect body weight, feed intake, feed conversion ratio, or mortality. However, supplemented birds exhibited higher total ω-3 PUFA content in all meat portions, mainly due to enhanced deposition of docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and, to a lesser extent, eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA). DHA increased approximately 11-, 16-, and 13-fold in breast, leg, and wing meat, respectively. Total ω-6 fatty acids were not affected, resulting in lower ω-6/ω-3 and arachidonic acid (ARA)/EPA ratios. The total EPA + DHA content in fresh tissue reached approximately 26 mg/100 g in breast and 90 mg/100 g in leg and wing portions. Leg and wing portions, analyzed with skin, met the criteria for labeling as “high in omega-3 fatty acids,” whereas breast meat remained below the threshold required for the “source of omega-3 fatty acids” claim. Overall, the present study suggests that dietary supplementation with 1% Schizochytrium limacinum may enhance ω-3 PUFA content in broiler meat under commercial production conditions without compromising performance, and may contribute to improved nutritional value of poultry products.Keywords: Schizochytrium, Broiler meat, Omega-3, DHA enrichment, Commercial

Introduction

Recent studies indicate that suboptimal levels of omega-3 (ω−3) fatty acids and imbalanced ω−6/ω−3 ratios remain widespread worldwide, highlighting the need to increase ω−3 intake to mitigate global health challenges (Torrissen et al., 2025). A recent narrative review by Lewis et al. (2025) emphasizes that long-chain ω−3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), particularly eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), play key roles in human health by supporting cardiovascular function, normal central nervous system development, and exerting anti-inflammatory effects. Nevertheless, global intake of EPA and DHA remains below recommended levels due to insufficient consumption of fatty fish and fish oil, the primary natural sources of these acids. This highlights the importance of sustainable alternative sources, including fortified foods.

Fortifying poultry products offers a practical approach to increase ω−3 intake, as poultry is widely consumed, affordable, and its composition can be modified through diet. Cartoni Mancinelli et al. (2022) reviewed the importance of poultry products as an alternative source of long-chain ω−3 PUFAs for human nutrition. Poultry meat typically contains low levels of EPA and DHA, but supplementation with appropriate additives, including fish oil or marine algae, can markedly increase these levels.

Fish oil and algal biomass are among the most effective dietary supplements for enriching poultry meat with long-chain ω−3 PUFAs (Rymer et al., 2010). However, the use of fish oil is increasingly considered unsustainable, whereas DHA-rich marine algae represent a viable and sustainable alternative. Schizochytrium limacinum is one of the marine microalgae that is particularly rich in DHA and has proven highly effective in increasing the content of long-chain ω−3 PUFAs in poultry meat. At the same time, its inclusion in feed should be limited due to potential adverse effects on oxidative stability and sensory characteristics of the meat. Even at 2% dietary inclusion, algae can increase meat susceptibility to oxidation, occasionally causing off-flavors in thigh meat (Baéza et al., 2015), though lipid oxidation may remain within acceptable consumption limits (Jeon et al., 2022). Therefore, inclusion is generally recommended below 2% to avoid negative effects on meat oxidative stability (Baéza et al., 2015). Accordingly, research is ongoing to determine a dietary inclusion level that maximizes the enrichment of poultry meat with ω−3 PUFA without compromising meat quality.

Studies have shown that effective enrichment of poultry meat can be achieved with dietary inclusion of Schizochytrium algae below 2%. At a dietary inclusion level of 1%, Schizochytrium algae can, under controlled experimental conditions, lead to a substantial increase in DHA and EPA content in broiler meat without compromising meat quality or production performance (Long et al., 2018; Moran et al., 2018a; Keegan et al., 2019a,b; Petrolli et al., 2019). Based on these findings, Keegan et al. (2019b) reported that 1% algae would need to be added to broiler feed so that the commercially most valuable meat portions could be labeled as a source of ω−3 fatty acids. However, responses vary considerably among studies, reflecting differences in diet formulation, supplementation duration, muscle type, lipid content, and the presence of subcutaneous fat.

Despite the growing body of literature, further data are needed to clarify how moderate inclusion levels of dried Schizochytrium limacinum influence fatty acid deposition across different broiler meat portions under practical production conditions, including breast, legs, and wings, and how these changes translate into improvements in ω−6/ω−3 and arachidonic acid (ARA)/EPA ratios. Most previous studies have been conducted under controlled experimental conditions, whereas less information is available from commercial production systems. In this context, it has been consistently shown that such inclusion levels of Schizochytrium algae (around 1%) do not adversely affect oxidative stability of broiler meat; thus, the present study focused primarily on evaluating the efficiency of ω−3 PUFA enrichment under commercial conditions. Accordingly, the aim of the present field study was to evaluate the effects of dietary supplementation with 1% dried Schizochytrium limacinum under commercial field conditions on the fatty acid composition of broiler meat, with particular emphasis on total ω−3 PUFA, EPA and DHA deposition, and the resulting ω−6/ω−3 and ARA/EPA ratios in different meat portions. The study also evaluated whether this level of inclusion would allow broiler meat to meet the criteria for labeling as a source of ω−3 fatty acids, thereby contributing to the development of nutritionally enhanced poultry products.

Materials and methods

All procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with the European Union Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes and complied with standard commercial farming practices. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Sarajevo - Veterinary Faculty (approval number 07-03-391-2/26). The study was conducted under commercial field conditions without procedures causing additional stress, pain, or discomfort beyond routine farm management. Birds were handled in accordance with good husbandry practices to ensure animal welfare throughout the study.

Animals, diets and experimental design

The field experiment was conducted at a commercial poultry farm operated by Brovis d.d. company (Visoko, Bosnia and Herzegovina). A total of 36,905 one-day-old Cobb 500 broilers (initial body weight 41.7 ± 1.8 g) originating from the same parent flock and hatched in the company’s hatchery were distributed into two dietary groups according to the farm’s commercial production practices: a control group (CON; 17,525 birds) and an algae-supplemented group (ALG; 19,380 birds). The groups were reared simultaneously within the same commercial poultry house, physically separated according to the farm's routine production practices and managed under the same environmental and husbandry conditions, including space allowance, temperature, humidity, and lighting program. Each dietary treatment was applied to a single dietary group. Consequently, the dietary group represented the experimental unit for production performance, whereas individual meat samples were analytical subsamples collected to characterize the fatty acid composition within each dietary group. Feed and water were available ad libitum throughout the experimental period. On day 40, the birds were slaughtered at the company’s slaughterhouse.

A three-phase feeding program (starter, grower, and finisher) was used with corn-soybean basal diets prepared in the company's mixer. Ingredient composition and nutrient content of basal diets are shown in Table 1. Broilers were fed a starter diet from day 1 to 21, a grower diet from day 22 to 35, and a finisher diet from day 36 to 40. All diets were provided in mash form. The same feeding program and diets were used for the ALG group but were supplemented with 1% dried biomass of the heterotrophic microalga Schizochytrium limacinum. The microalgal product was commercially sourced and, according to the manufacturer’s specifications, contained 27.2% DHA and 0.28% EPA of total fat (±10%), with negligible amounts (<0.1%) of linoleic acid (LA), ARA and alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), and a minimum fat content of 50%.

Production performance

Approximately 1% of birds were randomly sampled for weekly body weight measurements from day 1 to day 35, while final weighing was performed on day 40 before transport to the slaughterhouse. Initial weighing included 20 randomly selected groups of 10 chicks, whereas subsequent weighings involved more sampling groups with fewer birds per group, while maintaining an overall sampling proportion of approximately 1% of the flock. Final weighing included 40 groups of 4 birds in each treatment. Mortality was recorded daily. Body weight (BW) gain, total feed intake (FI), and feed conversion ratio (FCR), corrected for the body weight of dead birds, were calculated for each dietary group at the end of the experiment.

Sampling procedures

Feed samples for chemical analysis were collected on the farm during silo filling. Meat samples were obtained at the slaughter line from three portions: breast, whole leg (drumstick and thigh), and wing. From each dietary group, 10 samples per meat portion were randomly collected as analytical subsamples to characterize fatty acid composition rather than as independent biological replicates of the dietary treatment. Breast samples were collected without skin and bone, whereas whole legs and wings were sampled with skin to reflect their typical market form. All meat samples were frozen at −18°C and submitted to an accredited laboratory (SP Laboratorija AD, Bečej, Serbia; ISO/IEC 17025:2017) for analysis of ω−3 and ω−6 PUFA content.

Chemical analysis of feed

Crude nutrient content (moisture, protein, fat, fiber, and ash) was determined by the standard Weende method (proximate analysis). Crude protein (N × 6.25) was analyzed using a Kjeltec Auto 1030 automatic analyzer (Tecator, Sweden), and other parameters were analyzed using standard ISO methods. Calcium content was determined by flame atomic absorption spectrophotometry using a PinAAcle 900T (PerkinElmer Corp., USA) after dilution of the sample with strontium chloride (1:50, w/v), and total phosphorus content was determined colorimetrically.

Fatty acid analysis of meat

Fatty acid methyl esters in meat samples were determined after extraction and methylation on a gas chromatograph (Agilent 6890) with a flame ionization detector (GC/FID). Before analysis, the samples were thawed at room temperature, then deboned and ground and homogenized together with the skin (except for the breast samples) in a water-cooled mill (Kniftec model 1095).

The extraction of fat from the samples was performed in two ways: extraction after acid hydrolysis and cold extraction (Nordic Committee on Food Analysis NMKL, 160:1998). The fat obtained after acid hydrolysis was used to recalculate the results (total fat), while the fat obtained by cold extraction was used for the identification and quantification of fatty acid methyl esters. Fat extraction after acid hydrolysis was carried out by hydrolysis of the sample with hydrochloric acid, then cooling and filtration of the solution, followed by continuous extraction with chloroform in the extraction apparatus, and removal of the solvent, distillation, drying and measurement of the residue. Cold extraction is based on extracting the sample (between 50 and 100 g, depending on the fat content of the sample) with chloroform at room temperature for 4 to 5 hours with constant mixing (horizontal homogenizer).

After fat extraction, methylation of fatty acids was performed (ISO, 12966-2:2011). A portion of 100 (± 0.1) mg of cold-extracted fat was weighed, and 2 mL of isooctane was added, followed by gentle heating until complete dissolution. Subsequently, 100 µL of methanolic potassium hydroxide solution was added, and the mixture was vigorously shaken for approximately 1 min. The mixture was then allowed to separate into layers, after which 2 mL of sodium chloride solution (approximately 40%) was added and the mixture was shaken again. Subsequently, 1 g of sodium bisulfate was added and the mixture was vigorously shaken. After phase separation, 1 mL of the upper isooctane layer which contains fatty acid methyl esters was transferred into a vial using a pipette.

The samples prepared as described were analyzed on a gas chromatograph with a flame ionization detector - GC/FID (EN ISO, 12966-1:2015) and an autosampler (G1513A, Agilent). An HP-88 column was used (length = 100 m, inner diameter = 0.25 mm, film thickness = 0.2 µm; Agilent). The following temperature program was applied: an initial temperature of 120°C for 1 min, then heating continues at 7°C/min up to 175°C which was maintained for 10 min. After that, heating continues at 5°C/min up to 210°C which was maintained for 5 min, then further heating at a rate of 5°C/min up to 230°C, which is maintained for 15 min. The carrier gas was helium.

The injected sample volume was 1 µL at a split/splitless ratio of 50:1, with helium flow of 43.2 mL/min and a temperature of 250°C. The temperature of the FID was 300°C with a flow rate of H2 of 40 mL/min, air 400 mL/min, and nitrogen 25 mL/min. The frequency of recording the chromatogram was 29 Hz. The analysis duration was 47.50 min.

Quantification of fatty acid methyl esters was done by comparison with the standard solution FAME 37 (Supelco, Sigma-Aldrich Ltd.). The validated limit of quantification (LOQ) for EPA was 0.004 g/100 g fat, and the repeatability of the method, expressed as coefficient of variation (CV), was 4.7%.

The fatty acid composition of fat extracted from meat samples included four ω−3 and four ω−6 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs): linoleic acid (LA; C18:2ω−6), gamma-linolenic acid (GLA; C18:3ω−6), alpha-linolenic acid (ALA; C18:3ω−3), dihomo-gamma-linolenic acid (DGLA; C20:3ω−6), homo-alpha-linolenic acid (HALA; C20:3ω−3), arachidonic acid (ARA; C20:4ω−6), eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA; C20:5ω−3) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA; C22:6ω−3). Their content is expressed as g/100 g of extracted fat. EPA + DHA content was expressed as mg/100 g of fresh tissue for comparison with the regulatory thresholds specified in Regulation (EC) No. 1924/2006. It was calculated for each meat sample from the fatty acid profile of the extracted fat and the corresponding fat content before averaging for each dietary group.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Minitab 17 statistical software (Minitab Inc., State College, PA, USA). Differences between CON and ALG groups were evaluated using Student’s t-test. Mortality data were analyzed using the Chi-square test with Yates’ correction. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used to evaluate differences in fatty acid composition among meat portions within dietary groups. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05. Because each dietary treatment was applied to a single dietary group, statistical analyses of fatty acid composition were performed on analytical subsamples. Therefore, the resulting P-values should not be interpreted as evidence of independent treatment-level replication. Since BW gain, FI, and FCR were calculated at the group level, the results are presented descriptively without inferential statistical testing, with relative differences between groups reported.

Production performance parameters, including BW, BW gain, FI, FCR, and mortality rate, are summarized in Table 2. Birds in the ALG group achieved a slightly higher BW gain, while consuming approximately 60 g less feed than birds in the CON group. Consequently, FCR was lower in the ALG group, indicating approximately 2.2% lower feed consumption per unit of weight gain. The ALG group also recorded a higher mortality rate, with 118 more birds lost compared to the CON group.

No appreciable differences in total ω−3 PUFA content among meat portions were observed in the CON group, whereas in the ALG group it was higher in breast fat than in the other meat portions. In the CON group, ALA content was similar among meat portions, whereas in the ALG group a small difference was observed between breast and leg meat. HALA and DHA contents were consistently higher in breast meat than in the remaining meat portions, with DHA showing the most pronounced positional variation, exhibiting three- to fourfold higher concentrations in breast meat than in leg and wing portions in both dietary groups. EPA differences were evident only in the CON group, where breast meat contained approximately twofold higher levels than the other portions. Consequently, both ω−6/ω−3 PUFA and ARA/EPA ratios varied among meat portions in the ALG group. The ω−6/ω−3 PUFA ratio was lower in breast meat compared with legs and wings, whereas the opposite pattern was observed for the ARA/EPA ratio. In the CON group, both ratios were more uniform across meat portions, with a difference in ARA/EPA observed only between breast and wing meat.

Discussion

Study design limitation

A limitation of the present study is that each dietary treatment was represented by a single dietary group. Therefore, the observed differences cannot be interpreted independently of possible group-specific effects. Nevertheless, the study was designed as a commercial-scale field validation under practical production conditions, providing evidence of the feasibility of dietary ω−3 enrichment rather than definitive estimates of treatment effects.

Production performance

Average BW, BW gain, feed intake, FCR, and mortality were similar between the CON and ALG groups. Overall, the findings suggest that supplementation with 1% dried Schizochytrium limacinum does not appear to negatively affect the production parameters evaluated under the commercial conditions of this study. Even a 2.2% reduction in FCR may have practical relevance under large-scale production. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that low to moderate dietary inclusion of Schizochytrium algae does not adversely affect key production performance parameters in broilers (Rymer et al., 2010; Baéza et al., 2015; Jeon et al., 2022; Abd El-Wahab et al., 2025) and may indicate a potential improvement (Long et al., 2018).

Omega-3 PUFA

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the potential for enhancing ω−3 PUFA deposition in broiler meat through dietary supplementation under commercial field conditions. Supplementation with 1% dried Schizochytrium limacinum was associated with increased ω−3 PUFA content across all meat portions, with content in the extracted fat being 53% higher in breast meat, 26% higher in leg meat, and 18% higher in wing meat compared with control birds. The increase in total ω−3 PUFA observed in this study was mainly associated with increased DHA deposition, followed by EPA, whereas ALA and HALA contents remained unchanged. This is relevant because individual ω−3 fatty acids exert distinct physiological functions; therefore, total ω−3 content alone is not always a reliable indicator of the nutritional quality of fat (Calder, 2012). Similar findings have been reported in previous studies, indicating that increases in total ω−3 PUFA in broiler meat following Schizochytrium supplementation are driven mainly by DHA (Yan and Kim, 2013; Ribeiro et al., 2013; Long et al., 2018; Jeon et al., 2022), as other ω−3 fatty acids occur in these algae at very low or negligible amounts (Petrolli et al., 2019; Islam et al., 2025). Studies using 1% Schizochytrium generally report increases in total ω−3 PUFA of approximately 85–100% in breast and thigh meat (Long et al., 2018; Petrolli et al., 2019). In contrast, the increase observed in the present study at the same inclusion level was more modest. This difference may reflect variations among studies in diet formulation, duration of algal supplementation, lipid deposition patterns across tissues, as well as differences in the type of meat samples analyzed.

DHA plays a key role in the development and function of the brain and retina, while both DHA and EPA contribute to the maintenance of normal cardiac function (EFSA, 2010). Its role in human health is therefore widely recognized. DHA can be synthesized endogenously from ALA or obtained directly from dietary sources; however, the conversion of ALA to DHA is relatively inefficient, underscoring the importance of dietary DHA intake (Calder, 2012).

In this study, dietary inclusion of 1% dried Schizochytrium limacinum was associated with a substantial increase in DHA content across all analyzed meat portions. Compared with the CON group, DHA levels in the fat extracted from breast, leg, and wing meats of the ALG group were approximately 11-, 16-, and 13-fold higher, respectively.

Earlier studies have likewise confirmed the high efficiency of DHA transfer from feed to meat when Schizochytrium algae are included in broiler diets (Abril and Barclay, 1998; Mooney et al., 1998; Ribeiro et al., 2013; Yan and Kim, 2013). Rymer et al. (2010) reported that supplementation of broiler feed with 1.1% Schizochytrium biomass increased DHA concentrations by 4.5-fold in breast meat and 11-fold in leg meat. Thus, at a comparable supplementation level, the DHA enrichment observed in this study appears to be comparable or in some cases higher in both breast and leg meat. Our findings also exceeded the DHA increases reported in other studies using 1% of similar algae in broiler diets (Long et al., 2018; Keegan et al., 2019a,b; Petrolli et al., 2019). However, Moran et al. (2018a) reported substantially greater DHA enrichment, achieving a 21-fold increase in breast meat, a 29-fold increase in thigh meat, and a 36-fold increase in skin following the inclusion of 1% Aurantiochytrium limacinum, a microalga closely related to Schizochytrium. Notably, their study is among the few that quantified DHA deposition in skin with adhering fat, where enrichment was most pronounced. The same authors also highlighted considerable variability in DHA responses among studies, even at identical inclusion levels. For example, 0.5% algae increased DHA content in breast and thigh meat by approximately 12-fold in one experiment (Moran et al., 2018a), whereas in another study by the same group, the same inclusion level resulted in only a threefold increase (Moran et al., 2018b).

In the present study, dietary inclusion of 1% Schizochytrium algae resulted in a relatively moderate increase in DHA content in breast meat, but approximately twofold higher DHA deposition in thigh meat compared with some previous studies (Long et al., 2018; Keegan et al., 2019a,b; Petrolli et al., 2019). This outcome is likely attributable to the analytical approach used, in which leg and wing samples were analyzed together with the skin, as substantially higher amounts of DHA can accumulate in skin with adhering fat than in skinless breast and thigh meat (Gonzalez-Esquerra and Leeson, 2000; Moran et al., 2018a; Keegan et al., 2019b).

EPA shares several physiological functions with DHA, although it also exerts distinct biological roles. Notably, EPA plays a specific role in inflammatory pathways, serving as a precursor for series-3 prostaglandins, series-5 leukotrienes, and series-3 thromboxanes (Calder, 2006). While the primary dietary sources of EPA for humans remain fish and fish oil, its concentration in poultry meat can be effectively increased through the incorporation of natural algal or marine ingredients into broiler feed (Keegan et al., 2019a,b; Jeon et al., 2022).

The dried Schizochytrium limacinum used in this study contained only 0.28% EPA in its lipid fraction. Nevertheless, EPA levels increased consistently across all analyzed meat portions in the ALG group compared with the control. EPA content in the extracted fat was approximately threefold, sixfold, and fivefold higher in breast, leg, and wing meat, respectively. These findings may suggest that EPA detected in poultry meat does not necessarily originate directly from the diet but may also arise through endogenous metabolic processes. Although a small proportion of ALA, typically less than 5-10%, can be converted to EPA and DHA, with conversion to EPA generally being more efficient (Gibbs et al., 2010; Calder, 2012), this pathway alone is unlikely to fully explain the present findings. ALA content in meat was similar between treatments, and the ALG diet did not provide an additional source of ALA. Therefore, a potential explanation may involve retroconversion of DHA to EPA, as previously suggested in the literature (Sprecher et al., 1995). Supporting this mechanism, Mooney et al. (1998) reported increased EPA levels in the white meat of broilers fed 2.8% or 5.5% dried Schizochytrium algae, despite the absence of EPA in both the algal supplement and the meat of control birds.

Considering the fat content of the analyzed meat portions, total EPA + DHA content in breast meat of supplemented birds was approximately 26 mg/100 g of fresh tissue, whereas values in leg and wing meat were approximately 90 mg/100 g. According to EU Regulation (EC No., 1924/2006), poultry meat may be labeled as a “source of ω−3 fatty acids” if it contains at least 40 mg of EPA + DHA per 100 g of product, whereas twice this amount is required for the claim “high in ω−3 fatty acids.” Accordingly, total EPA + DHA content in leg and wing meat of supplemented birds reached levels sufficient for labeling as “high in ω−3 fatty acids” on fresh tissue basis, whereas breast meat did not reach the threshold for even the “source” claim. Other authors (Keegan et al., 2019a,b), using 1% of similar algae (Aurantiochytrium) in broiler diets reported comparable results for thigh meat and substantially higher EPA + DHA levels in breast meat than those observed in the present study. Obtained under commercial field conditions, these findings suggest potential practical applicability of this supplementation strategy in broiler production. However, because breast meat was analyzed without skin, whereas leg and wing samples included skin, the higher EPA + DHA levels observed in leg and wing portions may partly reflect differences in total lipid content among tissues. Skin contains higher lipid content and may contribute to increased absolute ω−3 fatty acid levels (Gonzalez-Esquerra and Leeson, 2000; Moran et al., 2018a; Keegan et al., 2019b). Therefore, comparisons among meat portions should be interpreted with caution.

Omega-6 PUFA

Omega-6 fatty acids are generally abundant in the human diet, with essential LA required in the largest amounts. Ensuring adequate LA intake is generally not limiting, as it is widely present in plant-based foods and animal fats, particularly in poultry fat where it constitutes the predominant proportion of total ω−6 PUFA (Crespo and Esteve-Garcia, 2001; Rymer et al., 2010; Ribeiro et al., 2013). In this study, LA accounted for more than 90% of total ω−6 PUFA in all meat portions, with no differences between algae-supplemented and control birds, which was expected given the negligible LA content of the algae used (<0.1% of fat). ARA ranged from 0.29 to 0.69 g/100 g fat in leg and wing meat and was approximately 2.5-fold higher in breast meat, while values were similar between the CON and ALG groups. As ARA can be synthesized endogenously from LA (Calder, 2012), increasing dietary LA generally promotes ARA deposition in poultry tissues (Crespo and Esteve-Garcia, 2001; Rymer et al., 2010). However, because the algae used contained negligible amounts of LA, the ALG diet did not provide additional LA, which likely explains the absence of treatment effects on ARA content. The higher ARA content in breast meat supports its preferential localization in phospholipids (Simopoulos, 2016), which are more abundant in breast muscle than in other meat portions (Rymer and Givens, 2005).

PUFA ratios

Supplementation of broiler diets with Schizochytrium limacinum was associated with a lower ω−6/ω−3 PUFA ratio in all examined meat portions, primarily due to higher ω−3 PUFA levels without changes in ω−6 PUFA content, consistent with previous studies. The ratio decreased by approximately 33% in breast fat and by around 20% in legs and wings. The more pronounced reduction in breast fat likely reflects the greater increase in total ω−3 PUFA compared with other meat portions. Studies using similar inclusion levels of algae have reported even greater reductions, in some cases halving the ω−6/ω−3 ratio in both breast and leg meat (Rymer et al., 2010; Long et al., 2018; Keegan et al., 2019a,b; Petrolli et al., 2019). Dietary supplementation was also associated with a reduced ARA/EPA ratio. Lowering this ratio in meat may influence the ARA/EPA ratio in the blood of consumers, which has been proposed as a more specific marker of inflammatory balance than the overall ω−6/ω−3 ratio (Sears and Saha, 2021). In breast meat, this ratio decreased by 65%, while in leg and wing meat it was reduced by approximately 80% compared with control group. These favorable changes resulted from a marked increase in EPA content across all examined meat portions. ARA promotes inflammation through pro-inflammatory mediators, whereas EPA supports the production of anti-inflammatory mediators and limits endogenous ARA synthesis from LA (Calder, 2006). An optimal blood ARA/EPA ratio of approximately 1.5–3 has been proposed (Sears and Saha, 2021). Particularly unfavorable ratios of these fatty acids, as well as an overall unfavorable ω−6/ω−3 ratio, have been reported in populations of Southeastern European countries (Torrissen et al., 2025).

Although the observed effects of Schizochytrium limacinum supplementation on ω−3 fatty acid deposition are generally consistent with previous experimental studies, the present work extends the available evidence by demonstrating these responses under large-scale commercial production conditions. Unlike most published trials conducted with relatively small experimental flocks under controlled research settings, this study evaluated dietary supplementation in a commercial flock comprising more than 36,000 broilers managed under routine production practices. Furthermore, the inclusion of skinless breast meat together with commercially relevant skin-on leg and wing portions provides practical information regarding the nutritional value of different retail products. Consequently, the present findings provide field-scale evidence supporting the feasibility of ω−3 enrichment strategies under routine broiler production conditions, while acknowledging the limitations inherent to the study design.

In addition to the design limitation, the present study is also limited by the lack of oxidative stability and sensory quality assessments, such as TBARS analysis and sensory evaluation. Although the study demonstrates differences in fatty acid composition associated with dietary supplementation under commercial production conditions, potential effects on lipid oxidation and off-flavour development were not assessed. Therefore, conclusions regarding the practical implications of the dietary supplementation should be interpreted within this context.

Conclusion

Dietary supplementation of broiler feed with 1% dried Schizochytrium limacinum may enhance ω−3 PUFA content across all analyzed meat portions, primarily through increased DHA and EPA deposition, while leaving ω−6 PUFA levels unaffected. These changes resulted in lower ω−6/ω−3 and ARA/EPA ratios. Total EPA + DHA content reached levels sufficient for the “high in ω−3 fatty acids” claim in leg and wing portions analyzed with skin, whereas skinless breast meat remained below the threshold required for the “source” claim. No adverse effects on production performance were observed. However, interpretation of the results should consider the experimental design (single group per treatment) and the absence of oxidative stability and sensory quality assessments. Overall, the findings suggest that this supplementation strategy may have the potential to improve the nutritional profile of broiler meat under the conditions of this study.
