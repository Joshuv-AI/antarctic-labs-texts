Beyond Biofeedback: Expanding Perspectives on Pelvic Floor Physical Therapy for Evacuation Disorders
Authors: Mira M Yang, Cayla Bales, Luis Felipe Carvao, Jenna Iberle, Joy Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePelvic floor physical therapy (PFPT) is recommended for conditions including constipation and fecal incontinence. There is limited evidence exploring characteristics of physical therapy practices, attitudes toward treating evacuation disorders, and potential barriers to care that may influence treatment outcomes. Therefore, a better understanding of these aspects of PFPT is needed. The purpose of this study was to survey PFPT practices, using the Chicagoland area as a microcosm. We aimed to explore practice characteristics, available treatment modalities, and attitudes toward treatment of evacuation disorders.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional phone and email-based survey of pelvic floor physical therapists across 17 counties in the Chicagoland area. Of 149 eligible contacts, there were 48 respondents representing 58 total PFPT practices. Mixed methods were used to aggregate data quantitatively and perform mapping analysis and thematic analysis.ResultsThere were geographic disparities in PFPT provider density. Most practices served all gender identities but consisted primarily of female therapists. Levels of PFPT-specific training and certification varied. Respondents felt that education on lifestyle, including diet, was an important therapeutic goal. Biofeedback in a variety of forms was used by most respondents (81%); some suggested that biofeedback was not as effective for chronic constipation as other therapies. Themes of potential barriers to satisfactory outcomes included delayed and vague referrals from other clinicians, as well as patient misinformation and inappropriate expectations.ConclusionsThis study provides preliminary insights to guide further work on improving access to PFPT and improving treatment outcomes for bowel symptoms. To strengthen collaboration, we suggest that referring clinicians actively communicate and develop networks with local PFPT offices, and offer earlier, more detailed PFPT referrals to patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10620-026-10089-3.Keywords: Pelvic floor, Physical therapy, Evacuation disorders, Biofeedback

Introduction

Pelvic floor physical therapy (PFPT), also referred to as pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT), is a specialized form of physical therapy in which a trained physical therapist provides instruction on the coordination, strengthening, and relaxation of the pelvic floor using physical and behavioral therapy modalities [1, 2]. Gastrointestinal (GI) societies have recommended PFPT as a treatment option for bowel disorders including fecal incontinence, chronic proctalgia and dyssynergic defecation; biofeedback is particularly recommended for the latter [3–5]. The American Physical Therapy Association (APTA’s) Academy of Pelvic Health Physical Therapy has also published a clinical practice guideline for the treatment of functional constipation that discusses different forms of biofeedback, manual therapy, and electrical stimulation [6]. There is not a shared consensus on the utility of diagnostic tools such as anorectal manometry (ARM) in the evaluation of constipation.

Preliminary evidence also shows that PFPT can increase complete spontaneous weekly bowel movements and symptoms even in patients with constipation who do not have a dyssynergia diagnosis or are laxative-refractory [7]. Despite this, PFPT remains relatively underutilized by both patients and providers. Prior work has shown that the majority of patients seeking PFPT have symptoms that are chronic, and patients are often not referred until at least 5 years after diagnosis [8, 9]. We speculate based on anecdotal experience that many referring clinicians may not be familiar with specific treatment modalities used in PFPT and cannot reasonably set patient expectations for treatment. Many patients referred to our academic gastroenterology clinic for medical evaluation of refractory constipation had previously been told to “find a PFPT office” without a referral order or understanding of what PFPT entailed.

Furthermore, patient experiences and treatment outcomes may be affected by PFPT availability based on geographic density and distance, patient-therapist gender concordance, provider expertise and therapies offered, and attitudes toward treating evacuation disorders, including the use and interpretation of diagnostic tools such as ARM and various forms of rectal biofeedback [10–12]. There is limited evidence regarding how these aspects of PFPT practices influence treatment of evacuation disorders. Therefore, we aimed to survey PFPT practices in our region (the Chicagoland area) to analyze the landscape of access to practices, practice patterns, perspectives on treatment, and perceived barriers to care. We hoped to explore how to improve referring clinicians’ understanding of and utilization of PFPT for GI symptoms.

Methods

We conducted a cross-section survey of PFPT providers in the Chicagoland area using convenience sampling. Our research group developed a 15-item homegrown questionnaire. The questionnaire was designed to take 10–15 min to answer by telephone. We performed a single round of pilot testing with an expert PT and subsequently made appropriate revisions. The survey included questions on practitioner and patient characteristics, training and certifications, scope of practice including available treatment modalities, and perceived misconceptions regarding PFPT from referring providers. Survey responses were recorded in REDCap, with respondent names and emails only collected if the respondent indicated interest in being contacted in future for clinical purposes or follow up of this research study. The questions are available as Supplemental Document 1. Given the lack of protected health information or patient information being collected, the Northwestern University Institutional Review Board deemed this study exempt.

Physical therapy practices that offered PFPT were identified via word of mouth and systematic search of a professional physical therapy directory (the Academy of Pelvic Health Physical Therapy). Practices employing licensed pelvic floor physical therapists with at least one year of experience within 50-air miles of downtown Chicago were eligible for participation. Practices were excluded if they did not offer PFPT, did not employ a licensed PT, were outside of the 50-air mile radius, were determined to be permanently closed, or if they did not have a working method of contact (Fig. 1).

Consort diagram detailing contacted and surveyed practice numbers

168 practices were contacted between October and November 2024 by phone and/or email (if the PT stated a preference for email communication or did not respond by phone), with a maximum of 2 follow ups sent over the course of 1–2 months. There was no pre-specified number of responses set for statistical significance, given the lack of literature available for assumptions and the exploratory nature of this study. A “response” was defined as an answer to any of the survey questions received; surveys with missing data were included and contributed to the overall response rate. Respondents were required to provide or confirm practice name and address to present duplicate responses; however, one respondent who worked at several practices was permitted to share multiple responses to descriptive questions regarding each individual practice. The response rate was defined as the number of responses divided by the number of eligible participants. Reasons for non-response, defined as no response to initial phone call or follow up phone calls or emails, were not collected from the 101 practices that did not respond.

Descriptive statistics of quantitative data were performed in Microsoft Excel. No additional techniques to account for missing data, propensity scoring, or sensitivity analysis were used. Surveyed practices were mapped using R/R Studio by location and number of therapists using Leaflet open-source mapping library. United States Census data were used to map population density. Select free-response survey responses were independently coded by two team members using thematic analysis methodology with a defined codebook on Atlas.ti. Code categories included “Barriers to Care,” “Lifestyle Counseling,” “Provider Misconceptions,” “Provider Tools,” “Psychosocial Health,” “Physical Therapist Modalities”, and “Referrals.” After systematic code reconciliation, themes and subthemes were confirmed and exemplary quotations were selected. Overall, the Checklist for Reporting Survey Studies (CROSS) is reported as Supplemental Table 2.

The mean number of therapists per practice was 3, although not all surveyed practices reported number of therapists. The majority of responding practices (n = 40, 83%) consisted entirely of female physical therapists (PTs). However, most practices (n = 43, 90%) accepted patients of all genders and gender identities, with only 10% (n = 5) only serving patients who identified as female. Portions of 17 distinct counties were included within the 50-air mile radius (Fig. 2), with approximately 8 counties with over 50% area included and 5 counties with over 90% inclusion (Cook, DuPage, and Lake Counties in Illinois, and Lake and Porter Counties in Indiana).

Regarding practice location, most surveyed practices were in Illinois within DuPage, Cook, and Lake counties (Fig. 3).

The highest respondent-patient ratio was present in DuPage County, at 1.30 providers/100,000 individuals, followed by Lake County (IL) with 1.00 respondent/100,000 individuals. Southern and Western counties within the Chicagoland area had low response rates (one PT each practicing west and south of Cook County where the city of Chicago is located). Although a small portion of the county was included within the 50-air mile radius (Fig. 2), DeKalb County in Illinois; Newton, Jasper, and LaPorte Counties in Indiana; and Berrien County in Michigan were excluded from Fig. 3 to improve the visualization of distribution of practices. There was a zero percent response rate from these counties, despite our protocol for 2 follow-up attempts.

Treatment modalities offered at surveyed practices varied. Nearly all PTs (n = 44, 92%) reported use of digital rectal exams within their practice. A large proportion (n = 39, 81%) endorsed reviewing ARM results, with the same proportion offering biofeedback. The most common type of biofeedback used was rectal balloon training (n = 26, 54%) followed by EMG-guided biofeedback (n = 22, 46%) and tactile/digital biofeedback (n = 13, 27%). Distribution of biofeedback and/or balloon training was similar to the overall distribution of surveyed practices (Figs. 3 and 4). Access to biofeedback of some form was primarily available in Cook, DuPage, and Lake Counties within Illinois (Fig. 4). Manual therapy, including visceral mobilization and massage, was offered by most practices (n = 30, 63%). Dietary and fluid intake recommendations, intended to supplement existing guidelines from referring physicians, were offered by nearly three-fourths of survey practices (n = 35, 73%); exercise counseling was offered by a much smaller proportion (n = 10, 21%).

Availability of PTs with specialist certifications varied widely among practices. The most common certification was the Women’s Health Clinical Specialist Board-Certification offered by the American Board of Physical Therapist Specialties (ABPTS), with 15 practices endorsing one or more PTs with this certification (31%), followed by the Pelvic Rehabilitation Practitioner certification offered by the Herman & Wallace Pelvic Rehabilitation Institute (n = 12, 25%). However, most practices reported having at least one PT with formal or informal trauma-informed training (n = 40, 83%). Most practices (n = 33, 69%) also offered telehealth as a potential appointment format.

Thematic Analysis

Thematic analysis of select responses identified 3 major themes and 11 subthemes (Table 2).

The first major theme supported earlier adoption of PFPT as a parallel, adjunct treatment modality to medical therapy, including use for patients with complex co-morbidities. One practitioner stated: “I wish that referring providers… knew that even if the source of symptoms is more GI in nature, physical therapy can still provide patients relief of symptoms (especially pain, leakage, and hemorrhoids)” (Table 2).

The second major theme focused on the holistic approach of PFPT with scope of practice including lifestyle counseling and expanding beyond biofeedback. One provider stated, “Biofeedback machines are not the optimal way to teach patients how to relax their pelvic floors. It is easiest to be taught through rectal palpation. Failed anorectal manometry does not indicate a patient cannot relax his or her pelvic floor.” Another stated, “Biofeedback machines are not the end all, be all. There are a wide variety of techniques and a lot of important education to help support & empower patients to manage their conditions. PTs’ main job is to figure out how each patient learns best, what words they need to hear & what techniques work best for their unique situation.”

Regarding the scope of lifestyle counseling, one PT provided an example: “[We provide] recommendations regarding eating, including sitting, chewing food, breathing during meals, [and] avoiding tight pants all day as well as at mealtime.” Others mentioned expanding on over-the-counter recommendations provided by referring providers. Some practitioners also emphasized the potential benefit of PFPT before considering invasive surgical options, stating, “I wish referring providers knew how beneficial just even 1 or 2 sessions with a pelvic floor PT could be for a patient before they undergo more invasive treatment options such as surgery.” These two themes together provide context and specificity for a sentiment common among several PTs practicing PFPT that “providers don’t know we exist” and/or “don’t know what we do.”

The third theme explored barriers to accessing PFPT beyond geography/distance including lack of patient general health education, referral gaps, and psychosocial barriers to care including lack of mental health support and stress management. According to one PT: “Many times, constipated patients have poor education regarding healthy bowel habits… a lot of symptoms can be improved through proper education, not medications” (Table 2). Another concern among PTs was that many patients did not understand how the pelvic floor worked or did not establish a relationship in their minds between their bowel symptoms and pelvic floor dysfunction. One provider stated, “I often have patients complete a bladder and/or bowel diary to make correlations between symptoms and their diet.”

Regarding patient referral status, one PT stated: “PT does not typically have GI providers refer; usually patients self-refer or find us on google and present for other pathology,” and this potentially can lead to frustration among patients. Although distributions of practices were not found to be uniform (Fig. 3), it is evident that additional barriers exist for patients that can stem both from lack of information and provider referrals.

While our survey did not explicitly ask about suggestions for improving referral recommendations, several PTs commented on concomitant referrals to other team members for the integrated management of bowel disorders. The most common referral suggestion was for a dietitian or nutritionist (n = 9, 19%). Several (n = 6, 12.5%) suggested referrals to a behavioral health specialist, therapist, or psychologist, with one respondent stating “gastrointestinal psychologist” specifically.

Discussion

This mixed-methods survey of PFPT providers in the Chicagoland area provides useful contextual findings regarding the state of PFPT practice in one region of the United States. While our geographical scope was limited, we found local variability and disparities in geographic access to PFPT, PT perception of treating evacuation disorders, PFPT-specific certifications, and therapeutic modalities. While conclusions regarding geographic access are limited to the Chicagoland area, other findings regarding PT perceptions and therapeutic modalities may be generalizable to other regions within the United States given significant variability of modalities across practices and studies in other regions demonstrating poor awareness of PFPT services [11, 13, 14].

Notable survey findings include the large proportion of female PT providers and those with trauma-informed training (with various forms of training), the philosophical emphasis on patient education and earlier PFPT referrals, and the perceived over-emphasis on biofeedback to manage constipation (as well as the range of biofeedback modalities available by practice).

We found differences in the geographical distribution of PFPT offices within our region that appeared to correlate with overall population density. Geographical distribution and density of offices could affect a patient’s ability to access expert PFPT in a timely manner. Gastroenterologists practicing in areas with a lower density or no PFPT offices might consider incorporating aspects of patient education that would be provided in a PT office, such as virtual PFPT consultation or in-office biofeedback through a trained nurse or advanced practice provider. There was a trend toward correlation between PFPT office location and population income level, although this study lacked power to establish statistical significance. Of the 48 surveyed practices, the highest density of respondents was in DuPage and Lake Counties in Illinois, which are the two wealthiest counties in Illinois and within the 17 counties included in our study (Fig. 3) [15].

We found that among the 48 respondents, there were differences in levels of training, type of training, and treatment modalities offered. Most, but not all, PTs reported some form of trauma-informed training, which is critical for patients with a trauma history undergoing PFPT given the sensitivity of exams and exercises. Trauma-informed care can also contribute to a more patient-centered, safer therapy experience, which is especially relevant given potential higher prevalence of childhood and adulthood sexual abuse and traumatic experiences in patients with pelvic floor disorders and complex pelvic pain [16–19].

Specialty certification may be important both as a marker of knowledge acquisition (it has been suggested that while general PT can perform elements of the exam and treatment required for slow transit constipation, a pelvic floor specialist may be required to treat other defecation disorders) and as a signifier of personal achievement that promotes reputation [20]. The majority of PTs had completed some form of specialty certification, although as Table 1 shows, there was no dominant certification among our respondents. It is unclear whether completion of certain certificates and additional training programs after certification affects treatment outcomes; this could be explored in future research.

In addition, we found variation in types of therapies offered by respondents. Taking biofeedback as an example, respondents differed in whether they offered balloon-guided, sEMG-guided, or digital biofeedback (the form offered by 27% of respondents), and 20% of respondents did not offer it at all. This is an important distinction as studies that support the use of biofeedback for dyssynergic defecation utilize the former two forms of biofeedback, and the gastroenterology literature has focused on biofeedback provided using anorectal manometry catheters [21, 22]. We suggest that if referring clinicians feel that biofeedback is indicated as part of a treatment plan, they should counsel their patients to inquire about whether biofeedback is offered, and if so, the preferred form of biofeedback. Respondents perceived that biofeedback was often the only PFPT therapy discussed by referring clinicians (particularly gastroenterologists), despite evidence for visceral mobilization and exercise regimens in evacuation disorders [23–25]. Respondents also offered dry-needling, which has shown promise in animal models [26–28], and a large proportion (73%) reported providing dietary and/or lifestyle counseling, often as an adjunct to medical provider recommendations. Future research might clarify the nature of this dietary and lifestyle counseling, as anecdotally this counseling might include adequate hydration and fiber intake, or less evidence-based recommendations for supplements like probiotics and other commercial products.

Respondents in our study perceived additional barriers to care that were elucidated through our qualitative analysis, including low patient understanding and poor education on bowel function and apprehension of PFPT. These findings indicate the importance of educating patients on the nature and role of PFPT in treating GI symptoms prior to referral, which should improve patient expectations and goal-setting during therapy. Studies have emphasized the importance of a therapeutic relationship between patients and clinicians to facilitate open discussion of experiences, especially given the potential stigma surrounding evacuation disorders [29]. Previous studies have identified difficulty with recalling when and how to do exercises, lack of time, and patient expectations as being associated with poor engagement in PFPT [30, 31]. One systematic review and meta-analysis identified health professional counseling, provision of information, and delivery/teaching as potential constructs impacting participation in PFPT [30].

Our qualitative responses also support existing evidence surrounding earlier adoption of PFPT for a wider range of conditions without requiring extensive treatment failures [7, 32]. Several therapists discussed concerns regarding PFPT being seen as a “last option prior to surgery” or wishing they saw a patient before he/she underwent surgery (Table 2). While some research has demonstrated symptomatic improvement post-operatively in patients completing PFPT, it is important to consider the potential for earlier PFPT to improve symptoms, and patients requiring surgical management may not always benefit from PFPT as an adjunct treatment to surgery [33–36]. It is also important to recognize potential limitations of biofeedback and pelvic floor muscle training, as some literature has demonstrated decreased or short-term benefits in patients with isolated structural pelvic floor anomalies or slow transit constipation [37–39]. Hence, referring clinicians should still ensure specific referral indications with careful patient selection.

There are several significant limitations to our study. We chose to focus on the Chicagoland area given the need to manually identify and reach out to individual practices for telephone or email follow up, but our geographic area may not be representative of PFPT availability and treatment modalities in the United States as a whole. While we had a good response rate (32%), we did not collect reasons for non-response, which might have identified biases in methods of contacting clinics. The non-response rate might have affected our ability to accurately assess disparities in access. Selection bias could have influenced our response rate due to the increased ease of directly reaching PTs at smaller, private clinics compared to PTs working in large hospital systems that utilize a central phone room. Despite using a professional database and word-of-mouth sources to identify clinics, this study may not have identified all relevant PFPT practices given the lack of a single comprehensive directory listing. Finally, as this was a telephone/email survey, we deliberately pared down the survey to not impose a significant time burden on participants, but this may have missed certain important factors such as insurance coverage and cost of treatment, and richer qualitative data that a different methodology, such as in-person or virtual interviews, may have identified, which is relevant given the importance of identifying concepts for future work.

In summary, this survey demonstrated geographic disparity in access to PFPT as well as variations between PFPT practices in terms of certification, treatment philosophy, and therapeutic approach that referring clinicians should consider when referring patients for PFPT. We suggest, based on our findings, that referring clinicians build direct lines of communication with local PFPT offices to which they refer, solicit feedback about appropriateness of referrals and patient outcomes, and engage in discussion about the specific treatment modalities offered at these offices to ensure a good fit for patients. We believe that expanding a survey of this nature at a national level would shed light on areas that would benefit from improved interdisciplinary management of evacuation disorders and serve as a foundation for creating educational opportunities for referring clinicians and PTs.
