Ecological validity framework and neurodevelopmental disabilities: applications for parent training programmes
Authors: Paul De Luca, Soyun Chae, Miranda D’Amico
Published: 2026

Caring for a child with neurodevelopmental disabilities (eg, autism spectrum disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, learning disabilities and intellectual disability) may be strenuous for many families, a reality that is often more pronounced for those from racial, ethnic and/or cultural minority groups. Existing parent-mediated and parent training interventions are largely developed, piloted and tested with White, middle-class families, with a persistent dearth of culturally responsive interventions tailored to the unique needs of minoritised groups.1 As a result, families whose cultural values, communication styles and caregiving norms diverge from existing services may be offered programmes that, though technically ‘evidence-based’, may be misaligned with their lived contexts and needs. In response to this gap, our viewpoint is a call to researchers, clinicians and advocates alike to integrate the core tenets of the ecological validity framework (EVF).2 These recommendations are intended for clinician-scientists conducting community-based clinical research or leading parent training programmes to strengthen cultural responsiveness in their work.

The EVF (table 1) identifies eight interrelated domains: language, persons, metaphors, content, concepts, goals, methods and context. We highlight how these domains may reorient parent training programmes towards equity, as well as provide recommendations to guide the design, adaptation and evaluation of parent training interventions.

The ecological validity framework: from theory to practice

First, language demands more than translating manuals. Instead, we argue interventions must be designed with linguistically diverse populations from their inception. Canada’s substantial growth in ethnocultural diversity sheds light on the urgency of adequate representation and consideration for culturally responsive practices. Since the 1990s, Canada welcomes approximately 235,000 new immigrants annually from over 175 countries.3 Recently Canada admitted 104,256 new immigrants within the first quarter of 2025 alone4. While 98% of Canadians speak English or French, 10.7 million Canadians have a non-official language as their mother tongue (eg, Spanish, Punjabi and Mandarin).5 Nearly 2% of Canadians (roughly 800,000) do not speak either French or English,3 with this figure likely being higher in 2025. Thus, with current data indicating an increase in language minority speakers in Canada, it is imperative that clinicians consider whether interventions are offered in multiple languages.

Second, persons consider the dynamic between the client and interventionist. Researchers and clinicians are encouraged to reflect on power dynamics between interventionists and participants and contemplate whether the programme may be delivered by ‘insiders’ (eg, peer leaders) from the same community. This is modelled in the Parents Taking Action programme,6 in which ‘promotoras’ are trained to deliver parent education to Latinx parents of autistic children. Such an approach fosters a sense of security by eliminating perceived power differentials. A prudent clinician-scientist is one who practises within their scope; as such, they are encouraged to reflect on whether their positionality imposes a power differential or if they are otherwise ill-equipped to effectively respond to the culture-specific needs of diverse groups.

Understanding metaphors entails understanding how key ideas within concepts (eg, ‘independence’ and ‘behaviour management’) are framed in a way that resonates with families’ values, traditions and beliefs about neurodivergence, rather than assuming Western ideals as the default. Moreover, symbols and concepts that are shared within the community of interest represent a shared understanding among members that outsiders may not be privy to. Thus, it is advised that clinician-scientists take the time to build rapport and understanding, as well as building in windows of structured reflection so clinicians can reflect on whether the intervention is aligned with participating families.

Bernal and colleagues2 indeed treat content and concepts as distinct concepts. However, within the concept of community-based parent training, we ascertain it is practical and conceptually appropriate to merge the two, as they are interrelated. Content (ie, stories, examples and materials used in intervention) gains meaning only through the cultural concepts the families used to interpret outcomes (eg, parenting, child development and service- and support-seeking). For many families, content and concept operate together as a singular meaning-making system. Thus, clinician-scientists must move beyond surface-level cultural tailoring and instead attend to how within- and between-cultural worldviews shape parents’ understandings of programmes, their willingness to engage and the perceived legitimacy of the intervention. In practical terms, this may entail eliciting families’ narratives or perspectives on parenting during intake to intentionally weave these frameworks into session materials. This will ensure content is anchored within a conceptual worldview that the family holds.

Sixth, we believe the goals of the intervention are culturally sensitive and developed alongside members of the community. This entails a shift in approach from one that is prescriptive to collaborative. Such a shift will ensure their needs and interests are properly addressed. Despite the literature being in its infancy, there are several exemplary programmes that demonstrate what effective collaboration looks like, such as ‘Getting to Know Cerebral Palsy’6 ‘The Social ABCs’ programme7 and the aforementioned ‘Parents Taking Action’.8 These programmes include materials (eg, manuals, modules and protocols) that have been developed in consultation with community members and allied professionals (eg, social workers), an iterative process (ie, one that integrates immediate feedback from community members) and emphasise collaborative problem-solving.

We also argue that cultural responsiveness requires methods with embedded accessibility and feasibility. In short, even the benefits of the most robust and effective interventions are null if families cannot consistently attend sessions, understand materials, or effectively implement strategies within the constraints of their daily lives. Clinician-scientists conducting community-based work must partner with communities to design interventions with potential challenges in mind to minimise burden, account for structural barriers, and ensure that skills can be implemented amid stressors (eg, work schedules, acculturative stress, childcare needs, language and mistrust of services).

Finally, we encourage active reflection of context, as it is a critical piece of the broader ecological forces that shape families’ lives (eg, migration history, socioeconomic conditions, discrimination, family structure and community norms). Thus, clinicians must perform ‘contextual assessments’. This entails consistent monitoring and check-ins to alleviate barriers to attendance and engagement with the programme. We argue that treating context as a dynamic force, rather than a static baseline variable, ensures that interventions will be delivered in a way that is equitable.

Looking ahead towards next steps

While caring for a child with neurodevelopmental disabilities requires unwavering strength and resilience, it is also both rewarding and empowering for many parents. To support these families, it is the responsibility of clinician-scientists to uphold the core tenets of cultural responsiveness. We believe the process by which we culturally adapt parent training should not be regarded as a superficial exercise in translation but rather a core element of intervention development. Cultural differences and systemic inequalities should no longer be seen as peripheral in the field of neurodevelopmental disabilities. The solution is not merely to provide more services in response to a growing need for support, but rather ensuring the right kinds of programmes are offered. Thus, as Canada (and North America broadly) continues to diversify, service providers and advocates must move beyond considering whether an intervention works but consider for whom, under what conditions and in what culture-specific contexts it is most efficacious.
