Rapidly progressive pseudomembranous Aspergillus tracheobronchitis diagnosed by bronchoscopy in a mechanically ventilated immunocompromised patient: A case report
Authors: Ryusuke Maruta, Genki Yoshimura, Kazuya Okada, Jun Makino
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aspergillus tracheobronchitis (AT) is a rare and severe manifestation of invasive aspergillosis, primarily affecting immunocompromised patients. The pseudomembranous subtype is associated with high mortality, particularly among those requiring mechanical ventilation. We report the case of an 86-year-old woman with nephrotic syndrome receiving corticosteroid and cyclosporine therapy who developed respiratory failure following emergency surgery for sigmoid colon perforation. After being admitted to the intensive care unit and placed on mechanical ventilation, she developed worsening oxygenation on ICU day 7. Chest computed tomography revealed bilateral ground-glass opacities, and serum β-D-glucan levels were elevated, initially raising suspicion for Pneumocystis pneumonia. Bronchoscopy on ICU day 12 showed no airway abnormalities, but repeat bronchoscopy on day 14 demonstrated extensive tracheal pseudomembranes and a yellowish endobronchial plaque. Cytological and microbiological examinations confirmed Aspergillus fumigatus. Intravenous voriconazole was promptly administered, but her respiratory failure progressed, and she died on ICU day 16. Clinicians should consider AT in patients with progressive pneumonia after major surgery regardless of immune status, particularly when bacterial infection is excluded or antibiotic response is poor. Early and repeated bronchoscopy is crucial for timely diagnosis, and empirical antifungal therapy should be promptly considered to improve outcomes in suspected cases.

Introduction

Aspergillus tracheobronchitis (AT) is a rare subtype of invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA), characterized by fungal invasion confined to the tracheobronchial tree [1]. AT predominantly affects immunocompromised individuals, such as those with hematologic malignancies, solid organ transplants, or under corticosteroid or calcineurin inhibitor therapy [1], [2]. Although AT accounts for only 7–8 % of IPA cases, it is recognized as one of the most severe forms, with mortality rates reaching up to 78 %, especially in patients requiring mechanical ventilation [2], [3]. Among its four subtypes—obstructive, ulcerative, pseudomembranous, and mixed—the pseudomembranous form is the most lethal [4].

Diagnosis is often delayed due to the nonspecific nature of clinical symptoms, which frequently mimic conditions such as ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) or acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). Chest imaging is usually inconclusive, and serum biomarkers such as β-D-glucan or galactomannan may not reflect localized airway disease [5]. Bronchoscopy plays a central role in the diagnosis, allowing for direct visualization and microbiological sampling. Histopathology and microbiological testing are useful for definitive diagnosis. Aspergillus fumigatus is the most commonly identified pathogen. Despite the availability of antifungal agents, outcomes remain poor in patients presenting with neutropenia or acute respiratory failure.

We present a fatal case of rapidly progressing pseudomembranous AT in a mechanically ventilated elderly patient with nephrotic syndrome under dual immunosuppressive therapy, emphasizing the importance of early and repeated bronchoscopy in high-risk patients.

Case report

An 86-year-old woman with a medical history of hypertension and progressive bilateral leg edema was admitted for evaluation. Laboratory and imaging studies led to a diagnosis of nephrotic syndrome. She was initiated on oral prednisone 40 mg/day. Due to inadequate response, cyclosporine (100 mg/day) was added one month later, and the corticosteroid dose was gradually tapered during hospitalization.

Several months into treatment, the patient developed severe abdominal pain. Imaging revealed sigmoid colon perforation, necessitating emergency surgery. Postoperatively, she was transferred to the ICU and initiated on mechanical ventilation. Empirical broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy with meropenem was started for presumed postoperative peritonitis.

On ICU day 7, her oxygenation deteriorated. Chest computed tomography (CT) demonstrated bilateral ground-glass opacities without nodules or cavitation (Fig. 1). Discussion

Aspergillus tracheobronchitis (AT) is an uncommon manifestation of invasive aspergillosis limited to the airways. AT is classified into four subtypes—obstructive, ulcerative, pseudomembranous, and mixed—with the pseudomembranous form associated with the highest mortality [4]. The disease predominantly affects immunocompromised individuals, including patients with hematologic malignancies, solid organ transplants, or those receiving prolonged corticosteroid or immunosuppressive therapy [1], [2]. However, AT has occasionally been reported in apparently immunocompetent individuals who experience transient immune suppression due to conditions such as critical illness, major surgery, or prolonged antibiotic therapy [6], [7].

In critically ill patients, the diagnosis of AT is complicated by overlapping clinical presentations with VAP or ARDS, as well as by the limited sensitivity of non-invasive diagnostic tools. Imaging findings are nonspecific, and serum β-D-glucan, although often elevated, lacks specificity for airway-invasive disease [8].

Gastrointestinal (GI) surgery can create such a transient immunocompromised state through multiple mechanisms. Postoperative stress, temporary suppression of cellular immunity, and prolonged exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics can disrupt the host’s microbial balance and weaken mucosal defense [9], [10]. Additionally, airway instrumentation and mechanical ventilation during the perioperative period may cause local mucosal injury and impair airway clearance, facilitating Aspergillus colonization and invasion [7]. Several studies have shown that postoperative fungal infections―including invasive aspergillosis and intra-abdominal candidiasis―are not uncommon in surgical patients, particularly following upper GI or abdominal procedures [11], [12], [13].

Bronchoscopy remains the cornerstone of diagnosis, enabling both visual assessment and sample collection, particularly in immunocompromised patients with respiratory symptoms [5], [14]. In our case, pseudomembranes were absent on ICU day 12 but became prominent by day 14, underscoring the rapid progression and the necessity for repeat bronchoscopic evaluation in deteriorating patients. Histopathological and cytological identification of fungal hyphae and culture confirmation of Aspergillus fumigatus established the diagnosis [14], [15].

Several reports have suggested that mucous plug cytology offers high sensitivity and specificity for AT diagnosis, even in the absence of deep tissue biopsy [15]. Prompt administration of antifungal therapy, typically with voriconazole, is critical. Nonetheless, in advanced cases like ours, the therapeutic window may be too narrow to reverse fulminant airway involvement [16].

Advanced age, combined with dual immunosuppressive therapy and postoperative status, likely contributed to our patient’s susceptibility. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for AT in patients presenting with progressive pneumonia following GI surgery, regardless of their immune status. When radiological or clinical findings suggest pneumonia, early bronchoscopy should be performed to evaluate for airway fungal involvement, and prompt empirical antifungal therapy―particularly with voriconazole―can lead to a favorable outcome [17].

Conclusion

Aspergillus tracheobronchitis occur in not only immunocompromised but also immunocompetent hosts following GI surgery, likely due to transient postoperative immune dysfunction and airway injury. Clinicians should be aware of this potential complication when encountering non-resolving pneumonia in postoperative patients. Early bronchoscopy is essential for prompt diagnosis, and empirical antifungal therapy should be considered when bacterial infection is excluded or antibiotic response is inadequate, as early intervention can significantly improve clinical outcomes.
