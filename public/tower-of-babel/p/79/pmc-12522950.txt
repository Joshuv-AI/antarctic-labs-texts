TRPM2: a pivotal player in tumor progression and a promising therapeutic target
Authors: Zhigang Chen, Zhenheng Wu, Tianhan Xu, Fuqian Yu, Dongmei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
TRPM2, a member of the TRPM family, is a ubiquitously expressed non-selective cation channel located on cellular membranes. Activated by ADP-ribose (ADPR), this channel is prevalent across various normal tissues and plays a critical role in numerous physiological and pharmacological processes, particularly in maintaining calcium homeostasis. A substantial body of evidence indicates that elevated expression of TRPM2 is associated with various cancer types. Increased TRPM2 expression has been significantly correlated with enhanced cellular proliferation, invasion, and poor prognosis in cancer patients. Furthermore, TRPM2 is pivotal in regulating Ca2+ levels, maintaining the mitochondrial function, and inducing cell cycle arrest, all contributing to tumor progression. This review examines the significance of the TRPM2 channel in tumor development and highlights its potential as a therapeutic target for inhibiting cell growth, apoptosis, migration, invasion, angiogenesis, and resistance to chemotherapy drugs. The evidence suggests that the targeted modulation of the TRPM2 channel could be invaluable in diagnosing and treating tumors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-03974-w.

Introduction

So far, over 100 distinct types of cancer have been identified [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), there will be 15 million new cancer cases by 2024 [2]. The annual global cancer burden is expected to increase to more than 28 million cases by 2040 [3]. The leading cause of cancer is the abnormal proliferation of cancer cells, which is closely related to ion channels [4]. Ion channels are integral membrane proteins that play an essential role in cancer cell proliferation, apoptosis, autophagy, and differentiation [5, 6]. Among them, the transient receptor potential (TRP) channel is the best-studied thermionic channel [7]. In 1969, Cosens et al. first discovered that fruit flies with impaired visual mutations could replace wild-type fruit flies and had TRP in response to stable light [8]. It was not until 1989 that Montell et al. identified the TRP gene and described its molecular characteristics [9]. In fact, the TRP superfamily can run through the evolutionary process from nematodes to flies to humans [10]. Currently, the TRP superfamily is recognized to comprise over 100 TRP genes across various species, with 27 identified in humans [11]. At the cellular level, the expression of some TRPs (such as TRPC1) is even ubiquitous, while others are localized to specific organs (such as TRPV5, which is only expressed in the human kidney) [9].

The TRPM subfamily represents the largest and most diverse group within the TRP channel [12–14]. Cumulative studies from morphology, biochemistry, and genetics have demonstrated that TRPM plays a role in many aspects of human malignancy [15]. TRPM2, the second member of the TRPM ion channel subfamily, is ubiquitously expressed across various cell types, including cardiac, hematological, and neural cells [16]. Following its unveiling, TRPM2 has demonstrated a pivotal function in managing oxidative strain and contributing to the aberrant activity of cancerous cells in conjunction with its facilitation of Ca2+ signaling through TRPM2-regulated channels [17]. According to the analysis of the GEPIA database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/), the expression of TRPM2 was highly expressed in various tumors (Supplementary Fig. 1). Based on this, we will summarize the structure and function of TRPM2 in the following section to further analyze the primary pathological functions regulated by TRPM2 in the occurrence and progression of tumor diseases.

Structure of TRPMchannel

The TRP channels, a diverse superfamily of non-selective cationic channels, play a crucial role in a broad spectrum of physiological and pathological processes [18, 19]. In mammals, these channels are categorized into six distinct subgroups based on sequence homology: TRPA (ankyrin), TRPC (canonical), TRPP (polycystin), TRPV (vanilloid), TRPM (melastatin), and TRPML (mucolipin) [20, 21] (Table 1). Most of these channels are capable of enabling calcium ions to flow through, while some also permit the passage of sodium ions [22]. Activation of these channels typically occurs via cationic signaling pathways, resulting in the depolarization of the cell membrane. TRP channels serve as environmental and chemical detectors that play a role in various physiological processes, including the reabsorption of calcium in the intestines, perceiving heat, and even detecting pain. Additionally, they may also be implicated in pathways that contribute to the formation of tumors [23].

The TRPM channel is a group of non-selective cation channels comprising eight members (TRPM1 to TRPM8). These channels are involved in various physiological functions, as well as cancer and other pathological diseases [24]. The cytoplasmic region of each TRPM channel subunit spans 732 to 1611 amino acids [25]. The TRPM homologous domain consists of roughly 700 amino acids located at the N-terminal. It forms a transmembrane structure composed of 6 transmembrane segments and 1/4 pores [23]. However, the C-terminal domain varies among members, ranging from 1000 to 2000 amino acids [26]. The TRPM subfamily is categorized into four subgroups based on structural variability: TRPM1/TRPM3, TRPM2/TRPM8, TRPM4/TRPM5, and TRPM6/TRPM7 [27]. TRPM channels exhibit variability in allowing calcium ions to pass through. For instance, the TRPM4/5 channel is impermeable to calcium, whereas the TRPM6/TRPM7 channel exhibits substantial permeability to calcium [28].

In the following section, we will shift our attention to TRPM2, a protein initially identified by Nagamine et al. and was previously known as TRPC7 and LTRPC [29]. The TRPM2 gene is located on chromosome 21q22.3 and consists of 32 exons that cover a distance of 90 kb [30]. The TRPM2 channel comprises 1503 amino acid residues organized in a tetrameric structure [31]. Each subunit consists of six transmembrane segments (S1-S6) and intracellular N-terminal and C-terminal regions (Fig. 1). The N-terminal portion of the protein contains four areas of the major homology region (MHR) exclusive to the TRPM subfamily. These regions show conservation within this particular subfamily [32]. In addition, the second MHR displays a pattern that resembles IQ. This motif consists of a transmembrane tract that contains a ring of pore sections positioned between S5 and S6. These pore regions are critical for determining the selectivity of TRPM2 and TRPM8 for cations and anions [33]. The C-terminal section of these channels has a TRP box, a helical domain, and an adenosine diphosphate ribose pyrophosphatase homologous domain that are highly conserved [14]. ADPR has been found to attach to the NUDT9-H domain in the C-terminal region of TRPM2, which is widely accepted. However, new studies have revealed the existence of another ADPR binding site in the MHR1/2 domain [34]. The ligand-binding site of the MHR1/2 domain has a higher degree of conservation compared to the NUDT9-H domain. Consequently, the MHR1/2 domain serves as the primary site for ligand binding in the TRPM2 channel [35, 36]. Further research is needed to explore the specific affinity between these two domains and certain Ligands, such as cyclic adenosine 5’-diphosphate ribose (cADPR).

In addition to the full-length TRPM2 isoform (TRPM2-L), several physiological splicing variants have been discovered (Supplementary Fig. 2), namely TRPM2-S, TRPM2-N, TRPM2-C, and TRPM2-TE [37]. The interaction between these alternative splicing variants and the whole TRPM2 isoform could potentially affect the overall functionality of TRPM2 [38]. For example, it has been discovered that TRPM2-S interacts with TRPM2-L, reducing calcium influx and a dominating negative impact [39]. Nevertheless, the specific process via which the alternative splicing of TRPM2 contributes to disease pathophysiology remains poorly understood.

Activation and Inhibition of TRPM2 channel

TRPM2 can be stimulated by niacinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD), cADPR, and substances that produce reactive oxygen species such as H2O2, intracellular Ca2+, and phosphatidylinositol diphosphate (PIP2) [40, 41] (Fig. 2). Furthermore, intracellular ADPR and its derivatives possess the capacity to directly initiate activating the TRPM2 channel [30, 35]. Moreover, several external signals can also stimulate TRPM2 by enhancing the synthesis of intracellular ADPR. This ADPR then binds to the TRPM2 C-terminal NUDT9-H domain. Examples of such signals include oxidative stress, tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF alpha), and amyloid B-peptide [42, 43]. cADPR potentiates the impact of ADPR and independently controls the opening and closing of TRPM2 channels. ADPR is primarily synthesized by mitochondria or by activating poly ADP ribose polymerase (PARP) [44]. TRPM2 is regulated in a positive manner by intracellular Ca2+ and calmodulin, and the interaction between ADPR and TRPM2 helps facilitate the entry of Ca2+ [43, 45]. This interaction results in an elevation of Ca2+-binding calmodulin, which strengthens the binding of calmodulin to the n-terminal IQ motifs of TRPM2. As a result, this provides a positive feedback mechanism for activating TRPM2 and the influx of Ca2+ [46]. In addition, the TRPM2, which has a binding site mutation in ADPR, can be activated directly by increasing the concentration of intracellular calcium ([Ca2+] i) [47].

In contrast, TRPM2 was found to be inhibited by N-(p-amyl cinnamyl) o-aminobenzoic acid (ACA), 2-aminoethoxy diphenyl borate (2-APB), nifedipine, flufenoxime acid (FFA), and conazole [48]. Notably, ACA, 2-APB, and FFA have shown substantial inhibitory effects on the entry of Ca2+ through the TRPM2 channel. In addition, suppressing TRPM2 activation by PARP inhibitors, such as adenosine monophosphate and 8-Bromo-cADP-Ribose (8-Br-cADPR), is believed to be due to the hindrance of ADPR binding to NUDT9-H domains [49]. Remarkably, extracellular and intracellular protons exhibited more effectiveness compared to [Ca2+] i and [ADPR] i, perhaps suppressing TRPM2 activation by diminishing [Ca2+] o sensitivity [50]. Furthermore, there have been reports indicating that the activity of the TRPM2 channel diminishes when temperatures drop below 40℃ [51]. Moreover, both extracellular and intracellular acidification also hinders the function of the TRPM2 channel.

Pathological functions of the TRPM2 channel in human disease

TRPM2 channels are widely expressed in various tissues and cells, including the brain, pancreas, lung tissue, and immune cells such as astrocytes, microglia, neutrophils, and macrophages [17]. Futhermore, TRPM2 has been shown to play a role in various human diseases such as inflammation, ischemia-reperfusion, and tumors play a complex and vital role [30, 52–54]. In inflammation, activation of TRPM2 channels not only promotes the inflammatory response but also inhibits inflammation by regulating the reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and bactericidal capacity of macrophages and neutrophils [53]. Activation of TRPM2 channels in ischemia-reperfusion injury may exacerbate damage in a variety of organs. However, in some cases, TRPM2 may also protect myocardium from I/R damage by regulating calcium inflow and phosphorylating proline-rich tyrosine kinase 2 (Pyk2) [54]. TRPM2 is highly expressed across a range of cancer types. Research indicates that activation of TRPM2 is associated with the proliferation and survival of diverse cancer cells [30]. In more specific tumors, such as neuroblastoma, high expression of TRPM2 is associated with unfavorable patient prognoses. Studies have shown that neuroblastoma cells exhibiting high TRPM2 expression possess enhanced migratory and invasive capabilities [55].

Synergistic effects of TRPM2 and TRPM2-AS in cancer and epigenetic regulation of TRPM2

Similar to TRPM2, TRPM2-AS is situated on chromosome 21q22.3. TRPM2-AS is a long non-coding RNA characterized by its antisense properties and is notably overexpressed in various human tumors (Supplementary Fig. 3) [56]. Studies have shown that antisense RNA is an RNA molecule that complements the target mRNA sequence and can form a double-stranded RNA complex with the target mRNA, thereby preventing its translation into protein [57]. For example, TRPM2-AS may increase the stability of TAF15-mediated TRPM2 mRNA, thereby promoting the proliferation of colon cancer cells [58]. We suspected that the role of TRPM2-AS and TRPM2 in cancer development might be related, but this has not been demonstrated in experiments. In future studies, TRPM2-AS may be used to knockdown the expression of TRPM2 channels to study the roles of both cell signaling and pathological processes.

In addition to antisense RNA regulation, epigenetic mechanisms such as DNA methylation and histone modification have more complex regulatory effects on TRPM2 expression. In some cancers, TRPM2 promoter hypermethylation leads to transcriptional silencing, while hypomethylation in aggressive tumors is associated with TRPM2 overexpression and poor prognosis [59]. At the same time, histone deacetylases (HDACs) affect the expression of TRPM2 by dynamically regulating the level of histone acetylation. For example, HDAC inhibitor trichostatin A can up-regulate the expression of TRPM2 and enhance oxidative stress-induced apoptosis in prostate cancer cells [60]. Moreover, the proteasome degradation pathway may further refine the function of TRPM2 by regulating its protein stability. However, specific experiments are needed to confirm this in the future. Together, these mechanisms form a complex regulatory network to maintain cell homeostasis by balancing TRPM2 activity, and its dysregulation may drive tumorigenesis [61].

Pathological functions of the TRPM2 channel in tumor

TRPM2 is implicated in numerous human pathologies, including various cancers such as lung [62], bladder [17], breast [63], pancreatic [64], gastric [65], and prostate cancers [66]. The specific involvement of TRPM2 in different cancer types is detailed in Fig. 3. The study elucidated the involvement of TRPM2 in key pathological mechanisms of cancer, including proliferation, cell death, migration, invasion, angiogenesis, and resistance to chemotherapy drugs. This research will now explore the cancer-related consequences of TRPM2’s oncogenic properties. Ultimately, people with different types of tumors that have an excessive amount of this channel tend to have a worse prognosis. This has prompted a discussion about the process behind the formation of this channel in tumor.

TRPM2 and proliferation

Notably, the loss of TRPM2 led to reduced proliferation of various tumor cells such as lung, breast, and pancreatic cancer, suggesting that TRPM2 plays a key role in regulating the cell cycle [67–69]. Downregulation of TRPM2 in lung cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells has been shown to significantly inhibit cell proliferation [67]. In human breast cancer cells, TRPM2 shows a protective effect in reducing DNA damage, while inhibiting TRPM2 through 2-APB or TRPM2 mRNA silencing reduces cell proliferation [68]. In terms of pancreatic cancer, studies have shown that overexpression of TRPM2 can promote the proliferation of pancreatic cancer in both cell models and nude mouse tumor-bearing models [69]. In addition, this overexpression demonstrated an inverse correlation with patient survival. In studies of brain tumor cell lines, the TRPM2 channel has been identified as a key factor in maintaining cell proliferation, and its overexpression is positively correlated with cell proliferation. Similarly, studies using CRISPR/Cas9 technology to knock out TRPM2 in U937 cells found that a lack of TRPM2 inhibited the proliferation of leukemia cells and increased their sensitivity to adriamycin [70]. These findings indicate that TRPM2 is integral to tumor cell proliferation and tumor formation. Nonetheless, the precise regulatory mechanism through which TRPM2 influences the cell cycle remains unclear and may involve the activation of cell cycle checkpoints.

TRPM2-AS has been identified as an oncogene in various cancers, including colorectal cancer [71], glioma [72], prostate cancer [73], and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCC) [74]. TRPM2-AS knockdown can inhibit the proliferation of colorectal cancer cells [71]. Mechanistically, TRPM2-AS interacts directly with the RNA binding protein (RBP) TAF15 to maintain the stability of TRPM2 mRNA [58] (Fig. 4). This suggests that TRPM2-AS promotes the proliferation of colorectal cancer cells by enhancing TAF15-mediated TRPM2 mRNA stability. In glioma [72] and prostate cancer [73], overexpression of TRPM2-AS is closely related to cell proliferation. When TRPM2-AS was down-regulated by siRNA, the proliferation of glioma cells decreased. Futhermore, TRPM2-AS promotes the proliferation of endometrial cancer (EC) cells by releasing SPP1 through sponging miR-497-5p [72, 75]. Moreover, TRPM2-AS knockdown has been shown to inhibit tumor proliferation in xenograft mouse models. In fact, little has been reported about TRPM2-AS. The intricacy surrounding the function of non-coding RNAs in the context of cancer remains elusive. It is postulated that TRPM2-AS could serve as a mechanism to temper the excessive activation of TRPM2 rather than entirely abolishing its activity [76].

TRPM2 and cell death

Among all malignant tumor behaviors, TRPM2 is the most well studied concerning its role in the cell death of cancer cells, which may involve the interaction of TRPM2 with apoptosis, autophagy, oxidative stress, and irradiation. In fact, TRPM2-mediated cell death involves multiple mechanisms, which have been demonstrated in studies of a variety of tumors, including brain tumors, prostate cancer, and stomach cancer [72].

In the context of neuroblastoma, TRPM2 protects cell viability by maintaining mitochondrial function, cell bioenergy, and reducing ROS levels [77, 78]. After inhibition of TRPM2, defects in autophagy/mitochondrial autophagy have been shown to lead to the accumulation of ROS and mitochondrial damage and may lead to reduced cell viability [76, 79]. In addition, when TRPM2-L was inhibited by TRPM2-S, two proteins, Hsp60 and Tom20, typically removed via mitophagy, have been observed [76]. Reduced expression of HIF-1/2α in these cells correlates with reduced expression of downstream mitochondrial target BNIP3, which plays an essential role in autophagy. Cells expressing TRPM2-S showed a large number of malformed mitochondria with swollen and degraded cristae, while wild-type cells expressing TRPM2-S had normal cristae. Cells depleted of TRPM2 by CRISPR also showed a reduction in autophagy.

Studies in prostate and gastric cancer have shown that blocking or knocking down TRPM2 can lead to decreased levels of autophagy-associated proteins (ATG3, ATG5, ATG6, ATG7, ATG12, and LC3-I to LC3-II) [80]. Moreover, TRPM2 is overexpressed in human tongue cancer specimens and tongue cancer cell lines [81]. Within SCC9 tongue carcinoma cells, exposure to hydrogen peroxide at 0.5 or 1 mM concentrations significantly increased apoptotic activity. Additionally, the suppression of TRPM2 gene expression through siRNA techniques led to an elevated rate of apoptosis in SCC9 tongue cancer cells. TRPM2 is also notably overexpressed in urothelial carcinoma of the bladder [76]. Within T24 bladder cancer cell lines, the augmented expression of TRPM2 accelerated the process of apoptosis, whereas the reduction of TRPM2 activity suppressed histone deacetylase function and prevented the stimulation of cell apoptosis [82, 83]. The findings suggest that inhibition of histone deacetylase results in increased acetylation of H3K9 and elevated expression of the TRPM2 gene within its promoter regions, thereby promoting enhanced programmed cell death. Apoptosis and autophagy are well-established mechanisms of cell death induced by anti-tumor agents, as evidenced by certain anti-tumor drugs. For instance, the activation of TRPM2 channels by resveratrol and eicosapentaenoic acid has been shown to augment apoptosis in glioma cells treated with paclitaxel or cisplatin [84].

The TRPM2 channel functions as a sensor of cellular oxidative stress and exhibits a dual role [85–87]. On the one hand, excessive Ca2+ inflow caused by TRPM2 channel activation may exacerbate cellular damage [87]. On the other hand, activation of TRPM2 channels can have a protective effect on cells under certain conditions [86]. However, oxidative stress can be a disadvantage for cell survival because its occurrence increases the probability of cell death. TRPM2 protects cells from oxidative stress by regulating Nrf2 (nuclear factor E2 related factor 2) mediated antioxidant pathways and cofactors, thereby maintaining cell survival [78]. Low levels of ROS can enhance cell proliferation through cancer metabolic pathways [88]. However, with the increase of ROS levels, TRPM2 activates cell death pathways through ferroptosis, lipid peroxidation, and DNA oxidation [89]. In certain malignancies, the manifestation of TRPM2 within neoplastic cells correlates with an elevated vulnerability to oxidative damage and enhanced prognostic indicators for patients, contrary to fostering cellular viability [76]. In fact, selenium supplementation has been shown to reduce oxidative stress. In brain tumor, the effect of selenium on docetaxel (DTX) -resistant glioblastoma (GBM) cells may be due to oxidative stress activation of TRPM2, thereby increasing the effectiveness of DTX-induced apoptosis [90].

The TRPM2 channel is notable for its ability to allow the passage of Ca2+ ions [91]. In HeLa cells, oxidative stress-induced TRPM2-mediated Ca2+ influx leads to phosphorylation and activation of calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CAMKII), followed by phosphorylation of BECN1/Beclin-1 [76]. BECN1 separates from PIK3C3, binds B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2), and inhibits autophagy, making cells more susceptible to death. Ishii et al. found that wild-type glioblastoma A172 cells were found to have undetectable endogenous TRPM2 levels [92]. The introduction of TRPM2 channels into glioma cell A172 leads to an increase in cell mortality induced by H2O2, and this increase is dependent on Ca2+ entry. At hydrogen peroxide doses of 100 and 250 µM, a hydrogen peroxide dose-dependent increase in intracellular calcium ions and an increase in sensitivity to cell death were observed. More recently, activation of TRPM2 has led to intracellular oxidative stress and higher concentrations of Ca2+ in different glioma cells (DBTRG). This, in turn, promotes GBM cell death through a process called apoptosis [93].

Interestingly, irradiation can also stimulate Ca2+ entry into cells via TRPM2. TRPM2 has been shown to be a radiation sensor protein [94] that mediates ATP release and DNA repair to improve cell survival [95]. In T-cell leukemia, inhibition or suppression of TRPM2 after irradiation reduced the number of cells accumulated in G2/M and increased the number of dead cells. In addition, TRPM2 is higher in T-cell leukemia cells that overexpress Bcl-2 and contributes to cdc2 inactivation, G2/M cell cycle arrest, and cell survival [95].

TRPM2 and migration/invasion

TRP channels in mammals significantly influence various cellular processes by interacting directly or indirectly with macromolecular complexes and actin cytoskeletons [96]. TRPM2 dysfunction is associated with many diseases, such as tumor migration and invasion. Studies have shown that ROS also can enhance tumor cell movement and invasion by influencing changes in actin cytoskeleton dynamics [97, 98]. ROS specifically regulates actin cytoskeleton dynamics by activating TRPM2 channels, thereby initiating or prolonging calcium signaling pathways.

In cancer cells, such as DBTRG glioblastoma cells, TRPM2-mediated calcium signaling promotes increased cell migration. Activating TRPM2 increases the levels of calcium and zinc ions in cells [99, 100]. These ions then interact to control the dynamics of the actin cytoskeleton and focal adhesion. The regulation of cytoskeletal structure depends on the interaction between calcium and zinc ions, suggesting that the inflow of ions through channels is involved in regulating this process through interactions with the cell substrate. In addition, activation of TRPM2 by hydrogen peroxide induces the formation of filopodia, loss of actin stress fibers, and disintegration of focal adhesions, leading to increased migration of HeLa cells [96] and prostate cancer cells [101]. Activation of TRPM2 increases intracellular Ca2+ and zinc levels, but zinc and Ca2+ interact to regulate actin cytoskeleton and adhesion dynamics. The migration of zinc-rich lysosomes to the front of the cell indicates that zinc plays a leading role in promoting cell migration [76]. These data suggest that either TRPM2 inhibition or free Zn2+ chelating agents may reduce or prevent metastasis progression.

A recent investigation analyzed the role of regulators of g-protein signaling 4 (RGS4) and mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) in cell migration and invasion mechanisms. The study found that TRPM2-AS increased the expression of RGS4, a protein that activates GTPase, and its expression changes affected the migration and invasion ability of glioma cells [72]. In addition, RGS4 was found to reduce phosphorylation levels of signaling molecules, such as c-Jun and JNK, which are associated with a reduced propensity to migrate and invade glioma cells. Finally, TRPM2 channels play a role in the movement and infiltration of glioma cells by interacting with the actin cytoskeleton to activate the phosphorylation of RGS4 and MAPK signaling pathways.

In addition to participating in cytoskeletal interactions, TRPM2 is also directly involved in the signaling regulation of various tumor cells, such as lung cancer and gastric cancer. Studies by Almasi S et al. have shown that inhibiting TRPM2 can hinder the invasive ability of lung cancer cells and trigger changes in the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) process [62]. Similarly, inhibition of TRPM2 modulates gastric cancer cell invasion via the PTEN/Akt signaling pathway [65]. In endometrial carcinoma (EC) tissues, TRPM2-AS is overexpressed, and SPP1 is released by sponge adsorption of miR-497-5p, thus promoting migration and invasion [75]. Particular cancers exhibit heightened levels of TRPM2 in the affected cells, which is linked to an increased susceptibility to oxidative harm and improved predictive markers of patient outcomes, in contrast to promoting cell survival [75]. In neuroblastoma, high expression of TRPM2 is associated with poorer patient outcomes [55]. TRPM2 increases neuroblastoma cells’ migration and invasion ability by regulating integrin expression. This regulatory effect involves an increase in the transcription factors HIF-1α, E2F1, and FOXM1, which bind to the promoter/enhancer region of the integrin gene.

TRPM2 and angiogenesis

The expression of TRPM2 may play a positive role in tumor angiogenesis. For example, the presence of TRPM2 channels in brain arteries hints at a potential function of TRPM2 to promote angiogenesis in GBM [90]. Studies have found that high expression of TRPM2-AS is closely related to angiogenesis and poor prognosis in patients with gallbladder cancer (GBC) and EC [75]. In GBC, TRPM2-AS activates the NOTCH1 signaling pathway by interacting with PABPC1, thereby promoting angiogenesis [102]. In EC, TRPM2-AS can release SPP1 from sponge miR-497-5p, thereby promoting the angiogenesis of EC cells as well as HUVECs. Similar results were obtained in xenograft mouse models. There is a close interaction between tumor angiogenesis and tumor immune microenvironment. By analyzing the immune microenvironment, overexpression of TRPM2-AS in EC cells stimulated the angiogenesis of M2 macrophages by secreting SPP1-enriched exosomes [75].

Currently, the precise regulatory mechanism of TRPM2 in tumor angiogenesis has not been fully elucidated. TRPM2 is known to modulate calcium influx and the phosphorylation of specific enzymes, influencing angiogenesis, a process that is likely associated with the activity of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [103, 104].

TRPM2 and drug resistance

TRPM2 shows specific resistance characteristics across various tumor types such as melanoma [105], breast cancer [106], neuroblastoma [16], gastric cancer [107], prostate cancer [108], lung cancer [59], leukemia [109], and so on. In melanoma, TRPM2 antagonists have significant anti-tumor effects on cells carrying B-raf or N-ras mutations and can restore chemosensitivity by inhibiting drug efflux pumps or apoptotic escape mechanisms [105]. In breast cancer, TRPM2 is associated with doxorubicin and tamoxifen resistance, while pharmacological and genetic inhibition of TRPC5-mediated autophagy increases the cytotoxic effect of doxorubicin [106]. In neuroblastoma, TRPM2 enhanced the growth of neuroblastoma xenografts and reduced the sensitivity of neuroblastoma xenografts to doxorubicin, while inhibition of TRPM2 significantly enhanced the cytotoxicity of doxorubicin [16]. Knockdown of TRPM2 in gastric cancer can inhibit the autophagy process and increase the sensitivity of gastric cancer cells to paclitaxel and doxorubicin [107]. In prostate cancer cells, overexpression of TRPM2 leads to resistance to cytotoxic chemotherapy, while AS TRPM-2 ODN combined with chemotherapy drugs can significantly improve efficacy [108]. Rebound increased TRPM2 expression in acquired osimertinib-resistant EGFRm NSCLC and in tissues that relapsed after EGFR-TKI treatment is a key mechanism leading to acquired osimertinib resistance [59]. Knockdown of TRPM2 in osimertinib-resistant EGFRm NSCLC cells resulted in ROS production and DNA damage. After osimertinib treatment, cells in vivo undergo apoptosis and slow growth. In addition, TRPM2-AS knockdown could reverse cisplatin resistance in NSCLC by activating the p53-p66shc pathway [110].

Notably, the role of TRPM2 is divergent in leukemia. Deletion of TRPM2 increased the sensitivity of leukemia cells to chemotherapy [109]. However, other studies confirmed that TRPM2 plays only a minor role in AML, that its loss can be easily compensated, and that TRPM2 loss does not sensitize leukemic cells to chemotherapy [111]. We analyzed that the drug resistance regulation of TRPM2 may be leukemia subtype specific, and its functional differences displayed in different models may result from differences in the activation of compensatory mechanisms in the tumor microenvironment. In summary, most current studies have found that overexpression of TRPM2 can lead to resistance to various chemotherapy drugs, and its knockdown can restore sensitivity.

TRPM2 channel is a therapeutic target for tumor

The TRPM2 channel substantially impacts various cancer-causing processes in tumor cells, such as cell growth, programmed cell death, cell movement, tissue invasion, and the formation of new blood vessels. These functions have generated considerable interest in the channel. Due to the lack of specific objectives, existing treatments have difficulties in efficiently combating many tumors, especially advanced malignant tumor. Increasing evidence indicates that focusing on the TRPM2 pathway could be a promising approach for treating tumor [72, 112]. This could explain the development of novel and efficient treatments that specifically target the activity of the TRPM2 channel. DTX, botulinum neurotoxin, resveratrol, and paclitaxel are among the pharmacological agents known to activate the TRPM2 channel. DTX, a partially synthesized taxane, is well described in scientific literature as an antineoplastic agent [113]. It is typically provided together with selenium, a vitamin recognized for its anti-cancer and chemoprotective properties [114]. Previous studies indicate that selenium has the potential to increase the apoptotic effects of DTX via activating TRPM2 through oxidative stress. In addition, studies have demonstrated that selenium effectively hinders the proliferation and survival of tumor cells (GBM) in laboratory settings [90]. A study revealed the mechanism of how selenium and DTX work by showing that these compounds activate TRPM2, leading to an increase in the intensity of intracellular Ca2+ fluorescence. This provides valuable information about the pharmacological mechanism of these drugs [115]. Botulinum neurotoxin, a protein neurotoxin consisting of seven serotypes (A-G), is recognized for its anti-cancer properties [116]. Botulinum neurotoxin A (BTX-A) has been found to increase the production of ROS and the entry of Ca2+ into tumor cells by activating TRPM2. This leads to malfunction of the mitochondria and, ultimately, cell death [117]. Resveratrol, a naturally occurring polyphenol known for its beneficial benefits in cancer and cardiovascular disease, has been shown to enhance the sensitivity of cancer cells to different medicines [118]. Paclitaxel, a taxane similar to DTX, similarly interferes with the movement of microtubules and triggers programmed cell death. In a recent clinical trial for glioma, the concurrent administration of resveratrol and paclitaxel resulted in an enhanced effectiveness of paclitaxel [119]. Prior studies have shown that resveratrol and paclitaxel cause mitochondrial malfunction and death by increasing the amounts of ROS inside cells. This suggests that TRPM2 may play a role in regulating apoptosis in GBM [120]. Further research has confirmed this theory, demonstrating that when paclitaxel and resveratrol are given together, they work together to activate the TRPM2 channel, resulting in oxidative stress and apoptosis [84]. These data indicate that resveratrol has the potential to be used alongside other chemotherapeutic drugs to improve the effectiveness of treatment.

ACA, 2-APB, curcumin, and other pharmacological drugs can efficiently inhibit the TRPM2 channel. Significantly, 2-APB has been identified as a powerful suppressor for different types of cancer since its initial detection. It acts as a broad-spectrum inhibitor of ion channels in various types of cells, including TRPM2. Nevertheless, contradictory reports have addressed the specificity of 2-APB in its ability to block the TRPM2 channel at doses lower than 10 µM [76]. Additional research is necessary to clarify the precise processes that cause these chemicals to have inhibitory effects in cellular settings. Calcium ions, specifically in the form of Ca2+, play a crucial role in the process of TRPM2-mediated cell death. In the case of glioma, it has been demonstrated that the use of ACA (with an IC50 of 1.7µM) and 2-APB (with an IC50 of 1.2 µM) can reduce the increase in cytoplasmic free Ca2+ levels that occur during TRPM2-induced cell death in BTX-induced GBM cells [121]. Both antioxidants and oxidants can either hinder or facilitate the development of tumors, and the TRPM2 protein plays a vital function in controlling the level of ROS within cells [122]. As a result, antioxidants and oxidants have emerged as potential therapeutic options for tumors [123]. Moreover, the amalgamation of curcumin and cisplatin has exhibited potential in tumor treatment. The efficacy of cisplatin, a platinum-based chemotherapy drug employed in managing many malignancies, can be enhanced by including curcumin as a complementary therapeutic approach [124]. Recently, researchers have produced silver nanoparticles and humus toxins for the treatment of tumors. These substances have shown the capacity to successfully destroy tumor cells by activating the TRPM2 channel and enhancing the oxidizing activity of cisplatin [125]. The findings provide evidence for the importance of TRPM2 in tumor cells and indicate its potential as a target for therapeutic intervention.

Concluding remarks and perspectives

The identification of TRPM2 a decade ago has yielded numerous significant discoveries that have deepened our comprehension of its structure and mechanisms. This channel possesses many fascinating attributes, establishing it as one of the most adaptable and appealing ion channels. TRPM2 facilitates the passage of Ca2+ ions and can be triggered by several causes, such as ADPR and ROS. It works as a detector of oxidative stress. The importance of identifying TRPM2 as a crucial element in the interaction between Ca2+ signaling and oxidative stress is highlighted by its involvement in causing cellular damage and contributing to the development of human illnesses. Previous studies have shown that TRPM2 has a role in various physiological and pathological processes, such as insulin secretion, cardiovascular failure, and cancer cell metastasis [17, 126].

TRPM2 channels are widely present in various cancer cell types and tissues and affect cancer cells’ proliferation and metastasis [107]. Moreover, research suggests that the absence of the TRPM2 channel in mice does not cause notable physiological harm or changes in development, weight, behavior, or other factors [127]. Therefore, suppressing TRPM2 channels does not present a risk to survival. Due to the cell-specific characteristics of TRPM2 channel function, it has the potential to be a valuable target for therapeutic development in specific situations. In the future, researchers should conduct additional investigations into the regulatory mechanisms that control the expression and activity of the TRPM2 channel.

The TRPM2 channel exhibits increased expression in multiple types of tumors and is prominently distributed throughout the whole body system [128]. Accumulating evidence indicates that TRPM2 may have a crucial role in maintaining the survival of cancer cells. Studies have shown that the TRPM2 channel supports the survival of cancer cells by aiding in the regulation of autophagy and DNA repair [17]. Moreover, heightened levels of TRPM2 are associated with raised levels of ROS and play a role in the advancement of tumors by activating transcription factors, including HIF-1/2α and other signaling pathways [129]. Therefore, focusing on medications that control the redox state may offer a more efficient treatment strategy. Nevertheless, research is scarce concerning the role of TRPM2 in tumor pathophysiology, indicating the need for further investigation.

While we provides a comprehensive discussion on the role of the TRPM2 channel in tumor progression and its potential as a therapeutic target, several limitations persist. Firstly, the tissue-specific variations in TRPM2 regulation, such as its role in promoting apoptosis in bladder cancer versus maintaining cell survival in neuroblastoma, remain inadequately elucidated. Secondly, there is a notable paucity of research literature concerning DNA methylation and histone modification, which constrains the scope of this review. Lastly, although the review underscores the potential of TRPM2 as a therapeutic target, it lacks detailed information on dosing, routes of administration, and patient selection criteria for both existing and prospective drugs.

In summary, ion channels have emerged as significant drug targets following enzymes and receptors. While TRPM2 is already recognized as involved in physiological processes, increasing evidence indicates that it could be a valuable target for various types of cancers. TRPM2 has multiple functions in cancer cells, including promoting cell survival, increasing sensitivity to chemotherapy, and inducing cell cycle arrest and death. These functions offer prospective opportunities for developing new therapeutic approaches. Future research should prioritize the investigation of TRPM2-associated signaling pathways to elucidate the precise mechanisms involved in ADPR mobilization. The advancement of novel pharmacological agents is expected to contribute significantly to a comprehensive understanding of TRPM2 and augment its therapeutic potential.
