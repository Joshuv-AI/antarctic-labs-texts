Introduction

Addressing commercially-driven health-harms offers a substantial opportunity to improve health: analysis from the Lancet series on the Commercial Determinants of Health (CDoH) suggests that health-harming industries contribute to at least a third of deaths globally [1]. Another paper in the series indicates ways to interrupt the different mechanisms by which harm is perpetuated, including by preventing undue influence on policy making processes [2].

In the UK, local authorities are natural agents of change, shaping determinants of health through their varied powers, statutory duties, assets and resources. To build on the opportunities available in these settings, we developed a Research-Derived Actionable Tool (RDAT) [3] that aimed to support UK local authorities to maximise benefits and minimise harms to public health by improving governance of commercial interactions. This toolkit fills a gap by distilling academic knowledge into clear, actionable and pragmatic guidance that can be aligned to local values [4].

The objective of this paper is to share learning from the development of this toolkit.

Methods

We used four principles to guide the development of the toolkit:

1)Concise sufficiency: only include material that directly helps local authority colleagues tackle local issues.

Concise sufficiency: only include material that directly helps local authority colleagues tackle local issues.

2)Getting started over perfection: prioritise a timely, actional output that can be iterated and improved through practice and evaluative research.

3)Guidance rather than direction: aim to help local authorities, which are independent, varied and democratically accountable, to develop the right policy for their organisation and the population they serve.

4)Evidence-informed: draw from existing evidence on harms and how to address them in different contexts.

Document search and analysis was designed to prioritise materials applicable to local authority settings (Principle 1) and to use stakeholder knowledge of the field to safety-net our non-exhaustive approach (Principle 2). We considered the framing, style and structure of existing materials as well as the content (Principle 1,4) in order to model our resource on others that stakeholders reported to be useful and acceptable in local authority settings.

The workshop drew from a range of facilitation methods (see for instance Liberating Structures [5]) to seek expert input on a clearly defined set of questions that could not be addressed by the existing published evidence base (Principle 1, 2). By focusing on open discussion rather than consensus, we were able to draw out a range of sometimes conflicting expert views. When resolving the workshop findings, we used both our guiding principles (Principle 1, 3, 4) and consideration of domains of expertise.

The methods described in Table 1 were not implemented in a linear way. For example, individual expert input was invaluable throughout. It was initially sought in the planning stages, then it was drawn on again during the analysis and synthesis of data to inform editorial decision-making where gaps in the evidence base meant judgement calls were needed and to gather views on the balance of competing risks and how to manage these. Finally, it was used towards the end for feedback on content and format.

Drafting and revision provided opportunities to refine, test and check our approach in an iterative manner. When drafting, we were mindful of our intended local authority audience, who experience strong time- and resource-pressures (Principle 1, 2). We emphasised readability and usability. For instance, separating the guidance from the supporting evidence allowed readers to understand the key points as quickly as possible, whilst providing them with a clear evidence-base behind each recommendation (Principle 4). The toolkit’s modular structure was intended to allow readers to pick and choose which parts of the toolkit to use depending on their context and needs (Principles 1, 2, 3).

Results

We developed a modular set of documents: the Good Governance Toolkit [6], which aims to support UK local authorities to ‘improve governance of commercial interactions, relationships and influence … to maximise benefits and minimise risk for population health.’ This toolkit has been published by the Association of Directors of Public Health and includes seven sections, a foreword and an accompanying set of flow-charts to inform policy and decision-making. The sections cover how to use the toolkit, the case for using good governance to protect health, issues to consider and evidence to support managing private-sector engagement in governance policy development, benefits and key issues to be aware of, principles and content considerations for policy and process, FAQs, and a glossary.

An evaluation of the process and impact of any implementation is underway and being led by the Local Health & Global Profits Research consortium [7].

The methods we used enabled us to make decisions about scope and resolve conflicts. One example that illustrates how these methods supported decision-making relates to differing stakeholder views about scope. Some recommended starting with a set of specific health-harming industries, where evidence of harm related to conflicting interests is strongest. However, academic evidence highlighted the risks that ‘tobacco exceptionalism’ may bring to progress in other policy areas [8]. The workshop format enabled us to explore and understand the different perspectives and the reasoning underpinning them. Through analysis using reflective discussions, we decided to prioritise a broad approach rather than focusing on specific industries, whilst working to mitigate concerns raised by some stakeholders. Our reasoning was partly based on prioritising research evidence for content decisions (Principle 4 and expertise weighting); partly about adding value to existing approaches, which had tended to focus on single industries or groups of them; and partly about prioritising democratic accountability and providing local authorities with the tools to develop approaches to suit their context and needs (Principle 3).

Our iterative approach enabled us to check proposed mitigations to address concerns with expert stakeholders. For example, to mitigate concerns that a broad approach may feel overwhelming and lead to less progress, we used design (a modular structure) and dissemination plans (for example explicitly stating the intention for people to use the toolkit in different ways, suggesting some starting points that could include choosing a limited number of industries or policy processes). The effectiveness of these mitigations can be pursued as a line of enquiry through evaluation.

Discussion

We theorised that this toolkit could contribute to improving population health through changing ways of thinking about governance and conflicting interests, supporting decision-making or introducing new policy. Policy change depends on local factors including political will and local authorities vary in readiness for action on CDoH. Therefore, whilst the toolkit’s intended use-case is to support policy development, we have also recommended its use for learning material, developing a case for change, and ad-hoc decision-making. In some cases, wider use of this material may help open the policy window to enable the full use-case.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of the toolkit are that it clearly describes the target audience and setting, is adaptable to context, prioritises action, provides a suitable range of supporting evidence, takes account of existing structures and systems, and is considered by our expert panel to be practically feasible and support implementation. It therefore meets many criteria considered highly relevant for planning system-level health promotion [9] and enabling factors considered important in RDATs [3].

The key limitation of this toolkit is that, at time of publication, it had not been fully implemented. This means that its feasibility had not been tested in the real world, and experiences from direct application or testimonies could not be incorporated. The planned evaluation will enable lessons from implementation (including resource implications) to be built into future iterations of the toolkit.

Conclusions

It was possible to develop an RDAT in advance of practice to support action in an emerging field of public health. This report presents a pragmatic, real-world approach to translating academic evidence into local practice. Toolkits for public health practice are subject to a different set of standards [3] and priorities to academic work, and so the methods for producing them must also differ [10]. By publishing our methods, we aim to encourage timely and appropriate evidence-informed action across emerging fields of public health where more rigorous approaches may lag behind. We also hope to contribute to thinking and debate about methods and standards for RDATs.
