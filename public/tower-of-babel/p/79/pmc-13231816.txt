Threat processing and PTSD symptom progression over repeated combat deployments
Authors: Tom Zalmenson, Noga Yair, Omer Azriel, Dana Shamai-Leshem, Yaron Alon, Yoav Levinstein, Ariel Ben-Yehuda, Lucian Tetse-Laur, Jacob Rotschield, Zivan Aviad Beer, Anat Talmon, Daniel S Pine, Paul D Bliese, Yair Bar-Haim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Psychological reaction to traumatic exposure is heterogeneous and is typically reflected in distinct longitudinal patterns of symptoms development. Various pre-deployment factors have been found to play a role in determining subsequent PTSD symptom trajectories.Objective: Here we delineate the role of threat-related information processing in predicting trajectories of trauma-related PTSD symptom scores conditional on endorsed exposure among combat-deployed infantry soldiers.Methods: A cohort of infantry soldiers (N = 596) was followed over multiple combat deployments to assess the predictive role of baseline threat processing (attention and interpretation) in the trajectories of trauma-related PTSD symptom scores conditional on endorsed exposure. Data were collected at 4-time-points: (1) shortly after enlistment and before deployment; (2) approximately 15 months into service following one deployment; (3) One year later, after further deployments; and (4) another year later, six months following honourable discharge. Behavioural and subjective threat-related attention, and threat interpretation were assessed alongside PTSD symptom severity and traumatic exposure at each time-point.Results: Three distinct symptom trajectories were identified: ‘Resilient’ (83.7%); ‘Worsening’ (10.9%), and ‘Improving’ (5.4%). Elevated subjective attention bias to threat and faster threat interpretation predicted both ‘Worsening’ and ‘Improving’ class membership. Notably, while subjective attention bias increased over time across all classes, it was consistently elevated in the non-resilient classes.Conclusions: The results highlight the predictive association between threat processing biases and trajectories of trauma-related PTSD symptom scores conditional on endorsed exposure, offering insights into cognitive mechanisms that may perpetuate or mitigate PTSD symptoms in combat-exposed populations.Trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04651192..KEYWORDS: PTSD, trajectories, threat processing, soldiers, resilience

Introduction

Combat deployment entails exposure to potentially traumatic events that increase the risk for posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (Hoge et al., 2004, 2006; Milliken et al., 2007; Richardson et al., 2010). Longitudinal studies of deployed soldiers have identified several latent trajectories of PTSD symptom development (Andersen et al., 2014; Berntsen et al., 2012; Bonanno et al., 2012; Dickstein et al., 2010; Donoho et al., 2017; Polusny et al., 2017; Van Der Wal et al., 2020). Four trajectories tend to commonly emerge: (a) ‘Resilient’, showing low levels of symptom severity over time; (b) ‘Improving’, showing high pre-deployment level of symptom severity followed by a gradual reduction; (c) ‘Worsening’, showing low levels of symptoms severity pre-deployment followed by a gradual increase in symptoms; and (d) ‘Chronic’, showing high levels of symptoms over time. Pre-deployment predictors of membership in non-resilient trajectory classes include younger age, higher neuroticism, lower education level, military occupation, and lower perception of preparedness for deployment (Bonanno et al., 2012; Donoho et al., 2017; Polusny et al., 2017; Van Der Wal et al., 2020). Here, we examine the role of threat-related information processing in predicting PTSD symptom trajectories in combat-deployed infantry soldiers.

Survival in combat environments requires tuned processing of potential threats. Unlike typical civilian contexts, where minor threats rarely signal actual danger, in combat, minor threats can predict high probability of real danger. It is therefore essential for deploying soldiers to effectively attend to and categorize ambiguous stimuli. Disrupted threat vigilance and categorization have been noted in relation to PTSD symptoms in cross-sectional studies. For example, greater attention to threats has been linked to greater PTSD symptom severity (Bryant & Harvey, 1997; DePierro et al., 2013; El Khoury-Malhame et al., 2011; Naim et al., 2014), and relative to healthy participants, those with PTSD tend to apply a lower criterion for categorizing ambiguous stimuli as threatening (Amir et al., 2002; Bomyea et al., 2017; Kimble et al., 2012; Vasterling & Arditte Hall, 2018). However, cross-sectional studies cannot determine whether cognitive biases manifest in individuals prone to PTSD prior to trauma exposure. In a longitudinal study, Wald et al. (2013) assessed threat-related attention patterns and PTSD symptoms in soldiers at three time points, two before deployment and one following six months of combat. Soldiers who displayed attentional threat vigilance before combat deployment were at a greater risk for elevated PTSD symptoms one year later during their first combat tour (Wald et al., 2013). This Wald et al study carried several limitations that the current study seeks to address: (a) Limited one-year time frame with a single post-exposure measurement, leaving open questions about how PTSD symptoms and attentional threat processing unfold across the full military career cycle, multiple combat deployments, and into the post-discharge period; (b) A limited focus on behavioural attentional bias measures, not including measures of subjective threat-related attentional biases (Azriel et al., 2022) and threat interpretation. The current study combines a longer follow-up period and more assessment points, with a more comprehensive assessment of potential threat-related cognitive biases.

Here we report findings from a longitudinal study that measured PTSD symptoms and threat processing in a cohort of infantry soldiers at four time-points over a period of 39 months, starting at enlistment (before combat deployment), 15 months later following first combat deployment, 12 months later following additional deployments, and ending 12 months later (6-months post honourable discharge). Threat-related attention, interpretation, and PTSD symptoms were measured at each time point. We expected to find similar trajectory classes of trauma-related PTSD symptom severity over time to those reported in prior studies of deployed cohorts (Andersen et al., 2014; Berntsen et al., 2012; Bonanno et al., 2012; Dickstein et al., 2010; Donoho et al., 2017; Polusny et al., 2017; Van Der Wal et al., 2020). Based on the reported associations between threat processing and PTSD symptoms (Amir et al., 2002; Azriel et al., 2022; Bomyea et al., 2017; Bryant & Harvey, 1997; DePierro et al., 2013; El Khoury-Malhame et al., 2011; Kimble et al., 2012; Naim et al., 2014; Vasterling & Arditte Hall, 2018; Wald et al., 2013), we expected that relative to participants with a ‘Resilient’ trajectory, participants with a ‘Worsening’ trajectory would exhibit higher baseline attention bias to threat, faster threat interpretation of ambiguous stimuli, and a higher proportion of threat interpretations of ambiguous stimuli.

Methods

Participants

Participants were male infantry soldiers (N = 596; Mage at study enrollment = 19.6 years, SD = 0.80; range = 18–27), representing a single cohort of recruits to an Israel Defense Force (IDF) infantry maneuver brigade. The participants were deployed to multiple combat and security missions in military conflict zones, involving routine patrols and border defense activities. The full sampling range consisted of 654 eligible soldiers (2% declined participation; 7% were excluded for insufficient fluency in Hebrew; and 2.2% were not available during the baseline measurement). Fifteen participants that were unavailable during the baseline measurement were enrolled in the study during the second measurement time-point. The only inclusion criterion was fluency in the Hebrew language. Due to high troop mobility and operational demands only subsamples of the full cohort participated at each data collection point. Baseline assessment occurred within 2 weeks of enlistment (n = 581). Measurement-point 2 occurred 15 months later, after completion of basic training and a first deployment (n = 484). Measurement-point 3 occurred 12 months later following 2–4 additional deployments (n = 456). The last measurement-point occurred 12 months later, 6 months after honourable discharge (n = 335). Data collection occurred between March, 2019 and June, 2022. None of the study variables predicted attrition at subsequent time points (all ps > .05).

All participants provided a written informed consent. Given the sensitivity of research in military settings, research staff worked with the IDF to empower participants to decline participation. The study was approved by the Tel Aviv University Institutional Review Board and the IDF Medical Corps Ethics Committee (Clinicaltrials.gov identifier NCT04651192).

Measures

PTSD symptoms

Symptoms in relation to the most recent month were measured using the PTSD Checklist (PCL-5, specific stressor version; Blevins et al., 2015). Participants rated symptoms on a 5-point scale (0 = not at all, 4 = very much). Cronbach's αs for the four time-points of data collection were 0.929, 0.930, 0.928, and 0.940, respectively. To ensure that symptoms reported reflect post-traumatic distress linked to a reported Criterion A event, participants who did not endorse a traumatic exposure prior-to, or at a current assessment point were assigned a PCL-5 score of zero for that assessment.

Combat exposure

The Combat Experiences Scale (Hoge et al., 2004; Wald et al., 2013) was used to track combat exposure. This 20-item questionnaire describes different combat events that soldiers may encounter during deployment. Items were yes/no questions concerning the experience of each of the combat event types. A total score reflected a count of the endorsed combat events.

Traumatic experiences before military enlistment

Pre-military traumatic exposure was assessed using an 8-item checklist adapted for the Israeli context and previously utilized in longitudinal studies with IDF soldiers (Gober Dykan et al., 2025; Wald et al., 2013). Participants where asked: ‘Have any of the following events ever happened to you?’. Probed events included: (1) being present and/or injured in a terrorist attack; (2) being in an area attacked by missiles or artillery or injured by such an attack; (3) being present and/or injured in a motor vehicle accident; or (4) experiencing sexual or physical assault. The total score reflects a count of the endorsed events.

Behavioural threat-related attention

Threat-related attention was assessed using a variant of the dot-probe task (MacLeod et al., 1986), which included 152 threat-neutral trials, each beginning with a fixation display (500 ms), followed by a vertically oriented pair of words, one threat-related and one neutral (500 ms), then a target arrowhead appeared in one of the words locations (Figure 1a). Participants were instructed to identify the direction of the arrowhead as quickly and accurately as possible. Attentional threat bias was calculated as the difference between the mean reaction time to targets presented at neutral locations and those presented at threat locations.

Illustrations of the behavioural threat-processing tasks: (a) illustration of a trial in the dot-probe attentional threat processing task; (b) an example of a morphed sequence in the emotion recognition task; (c) illustration of a trial in the emotion recognition task.

Subjective threat-related attention

Subjective reports on threat vigilance and difficulty disengaging from threats were derived from the attention bias questionnaire (ABQ; Azriel et al., 2022). This 9-item questionnaire probes subjective experience of attentional threat prioritization. Higher scores reflect a more intense subjective feeling of alertness to threats in the environment. The total score of the ABQ has been shown to positively correlate with PTSD symptom severity and behaviourally measured threat-related attention bias (Azriel et al., 2022). Cronbach’s αs for the four time-points of data collection in the current sample were 0.912, 0.933, 0.931, and 0.928, respectively.

Threat interpretation

Threat interpretation of ambiguous stimuli was assessed using the emotion recognition task (Maoz et al., 2016). The task displays 15 morphed faces equally spaced on a continuum between happy and angry expression endpoints of the same actor (Figure 1b). Each of the 15 faces was presented three times, for a total of 45 trials displayed in a random order. Each trial began with a fixation cross (800-1200 ms), followed by a morphed face picture. The face was displayed for 200 ms and then masked by a scrambled face display for 200 ms. Then, a question mark appeared on the screen until response. Participants were asked to press one of two buttons to indicate whether the presented face was ‘angry’ or ‘happy’. Two measures were derived: (1) the proportion of ‘angry’ responses, indexing the extent of the tendency to interpret the faces as negative; and (2) response time (RT) bias, calculated as the difference between RTs to angry-interpreted and happy-interpreted faces. Positive RT-bias scores indicate a tendency to make negative interpretations more quickly than positive ones. Negative RT-bias scores reflect the opposite pattern (Maoz et al., 2016, 2017).

Procedure

Baseline pre-deployment data were collected within two weeks of enlistment in the IDF, 15 months later, 12 months later, and another 12 months later. Traumatic exposure measured at baseline referred to traumatic experience that occurred before military enlistment and baseline PTSD symptoms were reported in relation to pre-enlistment events. PTSD symptom scores conditional on endorsed exposure, behavioural and subjective threat-related attention, and threat interpretation were measured at all four time points. Combat exposure was assessed at time points 2–4.

Data analysis

To identify homogeneous groups of individuals with similar patterns of trajectories of trauma-related PTSD symptom scores conditional on endorsed exposure, a Latent Class Analysis (LCA) based on PTSD symptom severity data (PCL-5) over time was conducted (full analysis on the original non-imputed data is reported in Supplementary Materials). Analyses were caried out using MPlus 8 software (Muthén & Muthén, 2017). Participants with at least one observed PCL-5 score across the four time-points were included in the LCA (N = 596) Missing values were handled using Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML) estimation (Enders & Bandalos, 2001). The LCA characterizes multidimensional discrete latent variables based on a cross-classification of observed variables (Schreiber, 2017). The model fit for the optimal number of classes was determined according to the following criteria: (a) the lowest Bayesian information criterion (BIC), sample-size adjusted BIC, and Akaike information criterion (AIC) scores; (b) a significant Lo–Mendell–Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMR-LRT), and bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT); and (c) high latent class membership probabilities, indicated by entropy values approaching 1 (Jung & Wickrama, 2008). The importance of fit indices was ranked as follows: BLRT, BIC, SSABIC, AIC, and LMR-A. We tested the fit of a one-class model and systematically increased the number of classes until adding more classes was no longer warranted. The LMR-A and the BLRT were used to compare the fit of the specified class solutions to models with one less class. Analyses were conducted without imputation.

To discover baseline cognitive predictors of trajectories class membership of trauma-related PTSD symptom scores conditional on endorsed exposure, the collected cognitive variables (behavioural attentional threat bias, subjective attentional threat bias, threat interpretation RT bias, and threat interpretation bias as the proportion of ‘angry’ responses) were applied in a multinomial logistic regression model with class membership as the dependent variable. All predictors were Z-standardized prior to analysis. Pre-military traumatic experience was entered as a covariate. This model included participants with complete baseline threat-processing measures and pre-military trauma reports (N = 544). The cognitive variables that significantly predicted class membership were included in follow-up analyses.

To describe the dynamic relations between the identified cognitive threat-processing predictors and trajectories of trauma-related PTSD symptom scores conditional on endorsed exposure, we computed repeated-measures ANOVAs with Time (4 levels) as a within-subject factor and Class as a between-subjects factor, followed by simple effects analyses to clarify the nature of observed interactions. Bonferroni correction was applied to the simple effects analysis, adjusting for multiple comparisons either between classes at each time point or between time points within each class. When the assumption of sphericity was violated, the Huynh-Feldt correction to the degrees of freedom was applied.

A sensitivity power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 (Faul et al., 2009) to determine the minimum detectable effect (MDE) for the binary contrasts within the multinomial logistic regression (α = 0.05, Power = 0.80, r2 other predictors = 0.2). For the ‘Worsening’ vs. ‘Resilient’ contrasts (analytic n = 519; base rate ‘worsening’ = 11.4%) the model was sufficiently powered to detect an Odds Ratio of 1.54, and for the ‘Improving’ vs. ‘Resilient’ contrasts (analytic n = 485; base rate ‘Improving’ = 5.2%) the model was sufficiently powered to detect an Odds Ratio of 1.86. Additional sensitivity analyses were conducted for the repeated-measures ANOVAs (α = 0.05, Power = 0.80, 4 measurements, correlation of r = .5 among repeated measures). The study was powered to detect small effects at a Cohen’s f of 0.08 for Threat Interpretation (RT bias and Percent ‘Angry’; analytic N = 265) and 0.088 for ABQ (analytic N = 224).

Mean combat exposure events were 1.4 (SD = 2.05) at time-point 2, 4.28 (SD = 2.94) at time-point 3, and 5.2 (SD = 3.3) at time-point 4, reflecting an accumulation of combat exposure over time. Mean pre-military traumatic experiences was 0.84 (SD = 1.01). Combat exposure and PTSD symptoms

A significant correlation emerged between post-discharge (time-point 4) combat exposure levels and PTSD symptom scores conditional on endorsed exposure (PCL-5), r = .33, p < .001, indicating elevated symptoms for soldiers with higher levels of combat exposure. A univariate analysis of variance (ANOVA) on combat exposure levels at time-point 4 with trajectory class of PTSD symptom scores conditional on endorsed exposure as the between-subjects factor revealed a main effect of class, F(2,334) = 3.97, p = .02, ηp2 = .02. Post-hoc comparisons using Tukey’s HSD indicated a significant difference between the ‘Resilient’ class (M = 4.99, SD = 3.15) and the ‘worsening’ class (M = 6.54, SD = 3.92), p = .02. No other contrasts reached statistical significance (all ps > .05).

Baseline threat processing predictors of trajectory class membership of PTSD symptom scores conditional on endorsed exposure

The multinomial logistic regression indicated that pre-deployment baseline threat-related cognitive processing predicts symptom trajectory class membership, controlling for pre-military traumatic experience. The overall model was significant, χ2(10) = 86.87, p < .001, explaining 23% of the variance in class participation. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals for all predictors are presented in Table 3. Participants in the ‘Worsening’ class were more likely to report greater subjective attention bias to threat at recruitment (OR = 1.40, 95% CI = 1.05-1.85, p < .05), and apply faster interpretations of threat (OR = 1.41, 95% CI = 1.05-1.90, p < .05) relative to participants in the ‘Resilient’ class. Contrary to our expectation ‘Worsening’ participants in the current analysis reported less ‘angry’ interpretations (OR = .73, 95% CI = .55−.98, p < .05) relative to participants in the ‘Resilient’ class. Participants in the ‘Improving’ class reported greater initial subjective attention bias to threat (OR = 3.12, 95% CI = 1.85-5.28, p < .001), and applied faster interpretations of threat (OR = 1.69, 95% CI = 1.03-2.79, p < .05) relative to participants in the ‘Resilient’ class.

Threat-processing over time and class membership

Follow-up analyses were conducted on the three variables that predicted class membership (subjective attention bias to threat and RT and percent of ‘angry’ interpretation bias in the emotion interpretation task). Subjective attention bias to threat

The repeated-measures ANOVA on ABQ scores revealed a main effect for time, F(2.96,654.73) = 14.97, p < .001, ηp2 = .06, and a main effect of class, F(2,221) = 18.92, p < .001, ηp2 = .15, without a time-by-class interaction, F(5.93,654.73) = 1.69, p = .12, ηp2 = .02 (Figure 3a). Post-hoc comparisons using Tukey’s HSD indicated that the ‘Resilient’ class (M = 18.13, SD = 6.58) exhibited significantly lower subjective attention bias to threat than both the ‘Worsening’ (M = 24.80, SD = 3.38, p < .001) and ‘Improving’ classes (M = 28.34, SD = 7.75, p < .001), with no significant difference between the ‘Worsening’ and ‘Improving’ classes, p = .38.

Pairwise comparisons using Bonferroni-adjusted estimated marginal means showed a progressive increase in subjective attention bias to threat over time across the total sample. Specifically, subjective attention bias was lower at time-point 1 (M = 14.69, SD = 8.50) compared to time-point 2 (M = 17.08, SD = 9.42, p < .05), 3 (M = 21.63, SD = 9.10, p < .001) and 4 (M = 23.08, SD = 8.60, p < .001). Subjective attention bias at time-point 4 was higher than at time-point 2, p < .05.

Threat interpretation – RT bias

Analysis of threat interpretation RT bias scores revealed no main effects of class and time, nor a class-by-time interaction (ps > .05).

Threat interpretation – percent ‘angry’

The repeated-measures ANOVA on percent of ‘angry’ interpretation revealed a main effect of class, F(2, 262) = 3.79, p < .05, ηp2 = .03, without a main effect of time or an interaction effect (ps > .05; Figure 3b). Post-hoc Tukey’s HSD comparisons indicated that the ‘Improving’ class (M = .57, SD = .06) presented higher percentage of ‘angry’ interpretations compared to the ‘Worsening’ class (M = .51, SD = .04), p < .05.

Discussion

This study generated four key findings: (1) Three latent classes of symptom trajectories were identified: ‘Resilient’, ‘Worsening’, and ‘Improving’; (2) Elevated subjective attention bias to threat predicted belonging to non-resilient trajectories; (3) Faster threat interpretation predicted belonging to non-resilient trajectories; and (4) Levels of threat-related subjective-attention and interpretation biases differed over time between the three symptom trajectory classes.

The current findings align with prior research on PTSD symptom trajectories in military populations, identifying three of the four most observed classes in previous studies: ‘Resilient’, ‘Worsening’, and ‘Improving’ (Andersen et al., 2014; Berntsen et al., 2012; Bonanno et al., 2012; Dickstein et al., 2010). The current results are most similar to Bonanno et al. (2012), who reported a four-classes model and found resilience to be the predominant trajectory (∼84% of participants). Our ‘Worsening’ class (∼11%), is almost double the size of Bonanno et al.’s, suggesting potentially greater combat exposure in the current cohort. Unlike Bonanno et al. (2012) and other studies (Andersen et al., 2014; Dickstein et al., 2010; Polusny et al., 2017) we did not observe a ‘Chronic’ trajectory. This divergence may be partially explained by differences in enlistment practices between the US professional military and the IDF where soldiers are subject to mandatory service. In the IDF, those with high and potentially stable levels of symptoms are less likely to be enlisted as infantry combatants or to be retained in active-duty roles.

Elevated subjective attention to threats predicted belonging to non-resilient trajectories, consistent with theoretical models emphasizing the impact of threat-processing on emotional distress (e.g. Ehlers & Clark, 2000). Heightened attention to threat likely amplifies exposure to fear-evoking stimuli, thereby perpetuating distress (Bar-Haim et al., 2007; Shechner & Bar-Haim, 2016). This finding parallels the pattern reported by Wald et al. (2013), contributing to current knowledge by emphasizing the long-term association between pre-deployment threat-related attention bias and PTSD symptoms across the whole duration of military service and following discharge. Notably, behavioural attention bias did not predict symptom trajectories, a finding that diverges from the results of Wald et al. (2013). This may suggest that this specific measure is less sensitive for detecting long-term symptom trajectories spanning several years (but see the results of the non-imputed data in Supplementary Material).

Faster threat interpretation also predicted belonging to non-resilient trajectories in line with previous research linking threat categorization to elevated PTSD symptoms (Amir et al., 2002; Bomyea et al., 2017; Kimble et al., 2012; Vasterling & Arditte Hall, 2018). To our knowledge, these finding provide the first longitudinal evidence identifying threat-related interpretation bias as a pre-deployment predictor of non-resilient trajectories. Within combat environments, this elevated cognitive sensitivity may reinforce maladaptive appraisals of danger, contributing to symptom exacerbation over time (Woud et al., 2012). This effect was larger in the ‘Improving’ class than in the ‘Worsening’ class, yet for both falling below our calculated sensitivity threshold. Thus, the role of interpretation RT bias as a prospective predictor of non-resilient trajectories should be viewed as preliminary and interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, taken together with increased attention to threats, this pattern suggests an indicative cognitive profile that may characterize individuals who are more susceptible to symptom development, a pattern that accounted for a substantial portion of the variance (23%) in trajectory classification. Such biases might foster a reinforcing cycle of hypervigilance and threat misperception that has the potential to fuel and sustain symptoms over time (Fani et al., 2012; Meiser-Stedman et al., 2009; Woud et al., 2012).

Evidence regarding the proportion of ‘angry’ interpretations was more complex. At baseline, the ‘Worsening’ group reported a lower proportion of ‘angry’ interpretations relative to the ‘Resilient’ group, going against evidence for elevated threat interpretation in this population. Notably, this finding is based on an effect size that falls below our sensitivity threshold, and was not detected in the following ANOVA, thus necessitating a cautious interpretation. The longitudinal analyses did reveal a distinct divergence between the two non-resilient groups, with the ‘Improving’ class maintaining a significantly higher percentage of ‘angry’ interpretations compared to the ‘Worsening’ class over time. This sustained interpretative bias in the ‘Improving’ group might suggest that the combination of pre-deployment vulnerabilities with the acute stressors of a combat environment contributes to a more overall biased threat-processing pattern that persists even as symptoms reduce. However, more data is needed to determine the reliability and nature of this longitudinal divergence.

Analysis of the temporal dynamics of subjective attention to threat within each trajectory class indicated that levels of subjective attention to threat increased over time in both the ‘Resilient’ and non-resilient trajectories, with the latter presenting consistently higher levels of subjective attention to threat across time. On the one hand, this potentially highlights adaptive plasticity of the threat monitoring system (Shechner & Bar-Haim, 2016), reacting to increased environmental danger during combat deployment. On the other hand, it is also of interest that participants in non-resilient classes exhibited consistently higher levels of subjective attention to threats compared to participants in the ‘Resilient’ class, suggesting that while moderate alertness may be adaptive, excessive vigilance can undermine the mental health benefits of this mechanism, potentially exacerbating adverse psychological outcomes. These findings further suggest that effective coping in stressful environments is associated with flexibility and regulation within the threat-attention system. The ability to maintain alertness at an adaptive level rather than remaining in a persistently heightened vigilance state, may be key to balancing situational awareness with emotional well-being. Soldiers able to modulate their attention to threat in response to threatening situational demands may be better equipped to navigate high-risk environments without experiencing long-term negative psychological consequences. Furthermore, the persistent elevation of threat monitoring after discharge from service indicates that this system may struggle to readjust to civilian life. Follow-up research could examine whether recalibration of threat monitoring occurs naturally over time, and under what circumstances it might take place (Metcalf et al., 2022).

The results of the current study should be interpreted considering some limitations. First, due to the inherent challenges of data collection from combat deploying soldiers, the conditions during data collection were not fully uniform and could have contributed to ‘noise’ in the data. It is worth noting that the significant findings reported here were obtained despite this potential noise. Second, although research indicates that self-reported PTSD symptoms on the PCL-5 strongly correlate with clinician ratings through a structured diagnostic interview (CAPS-5) and therefore provide an accurate measure of PTSD severity (Bovin et al., 2016), symptoms measurement in the current study relied on self-reports. Therefore, implications for clinical PTSD should be considered with caution. Third, pre-military traumatic exposure was measured using an 8-item checklist previously utilized in longitudinal large-scale studies of IDF soldiers (Gober Dykan et al., 2025; Wald et al., 2013). Future studies could utilize standardized inventories such as the Life Events Checklist for DSM-5 (LEC-5; Weathers et al., 2013) to control for additional possible pre-military traumatic events. Fourth, we did not track whether the index Criterion A trauma shifted between pre-military and combat-related events across assessment waves. Although we controlled for pre-enlistment trauma to isolate deployment-related vulnerability, symptom reports for some participants may reflect a cumulative or shifting impact of both pre-military and military stressors. Furthermore, future studies should track the specific ‘worst event’ identified by participants at each assessment wave to detect shifts in exposure relevant to reported symptoms. This would provide further data regarding the persistence of symptoms anchored to a single index trauma versus the evolving impact of polytrauma or cumulative trauma. Fifth, although combat deployment in the current context implies a very high likelihood of combat exposure, in our main analyses we only considered exposure where it was explicitly reported. However, it is possible that self-report measures do not capture the full scope or cumulative impact of all Criterion A events encountered during deployment. Future research could benefit from integrating objective deployment data with subjective reports to better delineate how specific types of trauma exposure interact with threat processing biases. Finally, although the current study aligns with the PTSD symptom trajectories reported in previous studies, it is the first to offer predictive links between cognitive processes and symptom trajectory classes. The study’s focus on male infantry IDF soldiers limits generalizability to other combat roles, female soldiers, and different militaries. Replication research on diverse populations could enhance generalizability and confidence in the current results.

In conclusion, the current study indicates that baseline attentional and interpretational threat processing mechanisms can predict a significant portion of combat deployed soldiers’ trajectory class membership of PTSD Symptom scores conditional on endorsed exposure. By highlighting the predictive and dynamic roles of attention and interpretation biases in combat deploying soldiers, the findings contribute to a nuanced understanding of the cognitive mechanisms underlying resilience and vulnerability to PTSD. The current study suggests that brief cognitive assessments before deployment can offer valuable insights into soldiers’ symptom trajectories over multiple deployments and reintegration into civilian life. The findings highlight the potential of such tools to identify individuals at greater risk for adverse psychological reactions to combat exposure. Further research in additional contexts and that explicitly tracks specific index traumas across time-points is needed to evaluate the practical value of such markers in military settings.
