The increased co-expression of SLC7A11 and PD-L1 was associated with poor prognosis in extranodal natural killer T-cell lymphoma patients
Authors: Cuiying He, Yingzhen Yao, Xiaolin Wu, Meng Yue, Lingling Zhang, Xiaojie Fan, Yuan Li, Chen Zhao, Haisheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Extranodal natural killer/T‑cell lymphoma (ENKTL) is a highly aggressive type of mature T and NK cell lymphoma that faces treatment challenges. Although immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have shown clinical activity in ENKTL, primary resistance and limited durable responses remain major obstacles. Our previous study demonstrated that the natural diterpenoid compound kayadiol exerted antitumor activity via p53/SLC7A11‑mediated ferroptosis in ENKTL cells. Cystine, through the solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11)—a cystine/glutamate antiporter—serves as the primary regulator of cystine uptake for glutathione biosynthesis. To evaluate the expression patterns of SLC7A11 in ENKTL, we conducted immunohistochemistry (IHC) analyses on formalin‑fixed paraffin‑embedded (FFPE) specimens obtained from 42 newly diagnosed ENKTL patients. The results indicated that high expression of SLC7A11 was associated with decreased overall survival (OS) and progression‑free survival (PFS). Furthermore, the simultaneous detection of PD‑L1 expression revealed a correlation between the expressions of SLC7A11 and PD‑L1, with their mutual high expression also linked to an unfavorable prognosis. To further assess the co‑expression pattern of SLC7A11 and PD‑L1 in tumor cells, we performed fluorescent multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC). Notably, both SLC7A11 and PD‑L1 were confirmed to be predominantly localized to CD56+ neoplastic cells. The mIHC analysis further substantiated that elevated co‑expression of SLC7A11 and PD‑L1 was associated with a poor prognosis for ENKTL patients. These findings were externally validated in the GSE90597 cohort, where high SLC7A11 expression and high co‑expression were both associated with poorer OS, whereas PD‑L1 alone did not reach significance. Conversely, CD8+PD‑1+ tumor‑infiltrating T cells showed no statistically significant association with clinical outcomes. Our findings demonstrate that tumor cell‑specific co‑expression of SLC7A11/PD‑L1 is associated with a poor prognosis in ENKTL, suggesting that dual‑targeted therapy may enhance efficacy for these individuals.

Introduction

Extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma (ENKTL), a neoplasm derived from mature T or NK cells, is a highly aggressive lymphoma with a distinct geographic predilection for East Asia and South America [1]. Presently, asparaginase-based regimens combined with radiotherapy yield favorable outcomes in early-stage ENKTL, with 5-year overall survival (OS) exceeding 80% [2]. However, patients with advanced-stage or relapsed/refractory (R/R) disease continue to face dismal prognoses, with a 5-year OS of less than 30% [3]. Data from the Chinese Lymphoma Collaborative Group show that ENKTL patients who fail to achieve PFS24 (no disease progression within 24 months after first-line treatment) have a median OS of only 5.3 months after disease progression and a 5-year OS rate of 19.2% [4].

Immune evasion is a hallmark of ENKTL pathogenesis. The programmed death-1/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) axis is a central mediator of tumor immune suppression. Binding of PD-L1 on tumor cells to PD-1 on T cells dampens T-cell activity and proliferation, facilitating immune escape [5]. ENKTL tumor cells frequently exhibit elevated PD-L1 expression, and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes also express more PD-1 than healthy controls, providing a rationale for PD-1 blockade [6–8]. Indeed, anti-PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy has demonstrated efficacy in R/R ENKTL, with objective response rates ranging from 31.8% to 75% in reported clinical studies [9–12]. Despite these advances, challenges such as primary resistance, early disease progression, and insufficient depth of remission remain common, underscoring the need to identify additional therapeutic vulnerabilities and biomarkers that can guide more effective combination strategies [13, 14].

Our research team is dedicated to exploring lymphoma drug-resistance mechanisms and developing new therapeutic targets. Our previous study demonstrated that the natural diterpenoid compound kayadiol exerted antitumor activity via p53/SLC7A11-mediated ferroptosis in ENKTL cells [15]. Cystine through the Solute Carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11), a cystine/glutamate antiporter, serves as the primary regulator of cystine uptake for glutathione biosynthesis [16]. Data from TCGA and GTEx indicate that the expression of SLC7A11 correlates with immune checkpoints such as (programmed death-ligand 1) PD-L1 across a range of tumors [17]. Notably, recent studies have revealed complex interactions between SLC7A11-mediated ferroptosis and the programmed death-1/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) axis. For instance, targeting SLC7A11 was shown to induce ferroptosis in tumor-associated macrophages and enhance the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapy in hepatocellular carcinoma [18]. In thyroid cancer, pharmacological inhibition of the MDM2-p53 interaction upregulated PD-L1 expression while downregulating SLC7A11, thereby linking ferroptosis induction with immune checkpoint modulation [19]. Conversely, PD-L1 was found to protect tumor-associated dendritic cells from ferroptosis during immunogenic chemotherapy by sustaining SLC7A11 expression, suggesting a cell type‑dependent regulatory role of PD-L1 in ferroptosis susceptibility [20]. In the context of infection, SLC7A11 deficiency in macrophages promoted bactericidal activity and activated PD-L1 expression via the ROS-NF-κB axis, further supporting a functional crosstalk between SLC7A11 and PD-L1 in immune cells [21]. Despite these emerging insights, the co-expression patterns and clinical significance of SLC7A11 and PD-L1 in extranodal natural killer/T-cell lymphoma (ENKTL) remain largely unexplored. Given the aggressive nature of ENKTL and the limited efficacy of current immunotherapies, understanding whether SLC7A11 and PD-L1 are coordinately expressed in tumor cells, and how their co-expression impacts patient prognosis, may provide a rationale for novel combination strategies targeting both ferroptosis and immune checkpoints.

To validate the potential interaction between SLC7A11 and PD-L1 in NK/T-cell lymphoma, we performed a comprehensive analysis using immunohistochemistry (IHC) and fluorescent multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) of PD-L1 and SLC7A11 protein expression in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) biopsies from newly diagnosed extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma patients. Immunofluorescence mapping revealed predominant localization of SLC7A11 and PD-L1 within ENKTL cellular compartments. Quantitative analyses demonstrated that both individual overexpression and concurrent co-expression served as adverse prognostic indicators.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

All human tissue samples in this study were collected and used with the approval of the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University (2023KS273) and in accordance with ethical standards. Pathological archives were reviewed for patients diagnosed with ENKTL at the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University between January 1, 2015, and June 30, 2024. A total of forty-two patients were identified as having archival formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues obtained through diagnostic biopsies conducted prior to treatment. The diagnosis of ENKTL was made according to the criterion outlined by the revised 4th edition of the WHO Classification of Tumors of Hematopoietic and Lymphoid Tissue.

Immunohistochemistry

IHC was performed on FFPE tissue sections using a Leica RM2245—Semi Motorized Rotary Microtome (Leica, Germany). Antigen retrieval was conducted by incubating the sections in a Tris–EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) at 95–98 °C for 20 min. Subsequently, the sections were cooled to room temperature, washed in distilled water and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual buffer. Non-specific binding was blocked with a blocking solution. Then, the sections were incubated with the primary antibody: PD-L1 (CST, 13684, 1:200), SLC7A11 (Abcam, ab37185, 1:100). After washing, the slides were incubated with secondary antibody (MXB biotechnologies, Inc., KIT-5020) and stained with DAB.

IHC staining intensity was scored as 0 (none), 1 (weak), or 2 (strong); percentage of positive cells as 0 (none), 1 (1–49%), or 2 (50–100%). The total IHC score was calculated by intensity × percentage, ranging from 0 to 4. A slide was recorded as low for PD-L1 or SLC7A11 expression when the total IHC score was ≤ 1 and defined as high when the total IHC score was ≥ 2. The total IHC score was determined independently by two pathologists under blinded conditions. The interobserver agreement, measured by Cohen's kappa, was 0.737 for SLC7A11 and 0.708 for PD-L1.

Fluorescent multiplex immunohistochemistry

Fluorescent multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) was performed with 3 µm paraffin sections of patient tumor tissue using 6-color AlphaTSA Multiplex IHC Kit (Alphaxbio, Beijing, China), which comprises sequential steps: dewaxing, antigen retrieval, and cyclic labeling (five cycles) involving primary antibody incubation followed by species-specific secondary antibody/fluorophore conjugation, concluding with DAPI counterstaining. All procedures were automated using the AlphaXPainter® platform with precisely regulated incubation parameters (time/temperature). Initial dewaxing and heat-mediated antigen retrieval unmask target epitopes. Primary antibodies PD-L1 (CST, 13684, 1:200), SLC7A11 (Abcam, ab37185, 1:100), CD56 (ZSGB-BIO, ZM0057, 1:100), CD8 (ZSGB-BIO, ZA0508, 1:100), and PD-1 (ZSGB-BIO, ZM0381, 1:100) specifically recognized targeted antigen in tissue section. Species-matched secondary antibodies from the multiplex staining kit subsequently bind to primary antibodies, with fluorophore conjugation enabling target visualization. Following iterative labeling cycles and DAPI nuclear counterstaining, the multiplex staining protocol is completed. The stained sections, after being mounted with an anti-fade medium, were scanned using the Axioscan 7 imaging system (ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany).

Statistical analysis

Comparisons of clinical data were performed by Pearson’s χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Comparisons of continuous variables between two groups or among multiple groups in continuous variable were determined by Wilcoxon rank sum test or two-tailed Student’s t-test or analysis of variance (ANOVA). The correlation between the two groups was analyzed by Spearman’s correlation. Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method, and survival curves were compared using the log-rank test. Overall survival (OS) was calculated from the date of initial diagnosis to the date of death from any cause, or date of the last follow-up. Progression-free survival (PFS) was calculated from the date of initial diagnosis to disease progression, relapse, death from any cause, or date of last follow-up. To determine the optimal cut‑off values for continuous variables, the surv_cutpoint function from the survminer package was applied, which is based on maximally selected rank statistics. Patients were then divided into high‑expression and low‑expression groups according to the obtained cut‑off values. To account for multiple testing across these predefined biomarker comparisons, log‑rank p‑values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure; FDR‑adjusted P‑values (q values) < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to evaluate the impact of variables on prognosis, and results are presented as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Variables included in the multivariate models were selected based on clinically recognized prognostic factors. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

High expression of SLC7A11 and PD-L1 was associated with worse prognosis

Subsequently, Kaplan‒Meier analyses were performed to explore the potential effects and relationships between the expression status of SLC7A11 or PD-L1 and clinical outcomes. Patients exhibiting high levels of SLC7A11 expression were associated with significantly shorter OS (Fig. 2A) and PFS (Fig. 2D), and similar results were observed for high PD‑L1 expression (Fig. 2B and E). When patients were stratified by co‑expression of both markers, those with high co‑expression had the worst OS (Fig. 2C) and PFS (Fig. 2F). These findings were externally validated in the GSE90597 cohort: high SLC7A11 expression and high co‑expression were both associated with poorer OS, whereas PD‑L1 alone did not reach significance (Fig. 2G–I). To account for multiple testing, the log‑rank P values were adjusted using the Benjamini‑Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. PD-1 expression on CD8+ cells and the ratio of CD8+ cells to tumor cells were not found to affect patient survival

To evaluate the impact of T‑cell distribution and PD‑1 expression on the outcomes of ENKTL patients, we further analyzed the mIHC results. PD‑1+CD8+ T cells were defined as cells exhibiting membranous staining for both CD8 and PD‑1, with a positive threshold of ≥ 1% of cells. As shown in Fig. 4C and D, the proportion of PD‑1+CD8+ T cells in tumor tissue showed no significant correlation with overall survival.

Subsequently, we assessed whether the ratio of CD8+ T cells to tumor cells was associated with overall patient survival. Patients were divided into high and low groups using the optimal cutoff value determined by maximally selected rank statistics. No significant association with overall survival was observed (Fig. 4E and F). The correlation between the expression levels of SLC7A11 or PD‑L1 on CD56+ cells and the degree of CD8+ T‑cell infiltration was further analyzed, and Spearman correlation analysis revealed no significant correlation.

Discussion

Extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma represents a distinct clinicopathological entity within non-Hodgkin lymphomas, characterized by molecular heterogeneity, immunobiological complexity, and aggressive clinical progression patterns that frequently demonstrate treatment resistance.

Over the past decade, L-asparaginase-based combination chemotherapy has significantly improved survival outcomes for early-stage NK/T-cell lymphoma patients. Nevertheless, the clinical efficacy for advanced-stage patients remains unsatisfactory, with over 70% experiencing disease progression within five years after diagnosis [22]. Recent clinical investigations in extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma have evaluated novel therapeutic strategies encompassing immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), cell-surface-targeted monoclonal antibodies, EBV-specific targeted cytotoxic T lymphocyte, chimeric antigen receptor T cells, immunomodulatory agents, epigenetic modulators and signaling pathway inhibitors. Notably, immunotherapy, especially ICIs, has shown promising results [9, 23, 24].

PD-L1 is ubiquitously expressed in tumor cells and serves as a validated predictive biomarker for ICI therapy. The reported prevalence of PD‑L1 positivity or high expression in ENKTL varies significantly across studies, with estimates ranging from 33.8% to 100% [6–8, 25–30]. This variability can be largely attributed to discrepancies in antibody clones, scoring algorithms, and cutoff thresholds. Notably, PD-L1 immunohistochemistry assays have not been systematically validated in lymphomas, and a standardized scoring system remains unavailable [31]. In our cohort, more than 90% of cases exhibited PD-L1 expression in ≥ 10% of tumor cells. Based on this observation, we applied a pragmatic product scoring method to categorize ENKTL patients into high‑ and low‑expression groups. Using this classification, high PD‑L1 expression was significantly correlated with poor prognosis. This prognostic association is consistent with most published studies, supporting high PD‑L1 expression as an independent predictor of adverse outcomes in this aggressive lymphoma subtype.

Conventional IHC assay for PD-L1 expression lacks cellular specificity, unable to distinguish tumor-intrinsic PD-L1 from stromal or immune cell sources. To address this limitation, we developed a dual-marker analytical approach employing mIHC to concurrently assess PD-L1 expression and CD56+ tumor cell localization. Quantitative analysis revealed tumor cell-specific PD-L1 overexpression independently predicts inferior PFS and OS in ENKTL patients. Notably, PD‑L1 is also expressed on immune cells within the ENKTL microenvironment, particularly tumor‑associated macrophages [27, 30, 32]. Consistent with this, our mIHC analysis demonstrated PD‑L1‑positive signals in CD56‑negative regions. Although the limited marker panel in this study precluded definitive identification of PD‑L1 expression on non-neoplastic cells, this technical constraint does not affect our central finding that tumor cell-specific PD‑L1 expression is an independent prognostic factor in ENKTL.

PD-1/PD-L1 blockade demonstrates clinical activity in extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma, yet monotherapy offers suboptimal survival outcomes, evidenced by a 1-year PFS rate of 66% in clinical cohorts [9]. In our cohort of 29 ENKTL patients analyzed by mIHC, 8 received first-line PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor-based combination therapy. Three cases exhibiting primary resistance to therapy and 2 demonstrating disease relapse following complete remission. No statistically significant association was found between clinical response patterns and tumor cell-specific PD-L1 expression levels, possibly due to the limited statistical power resulting from the small sample size. The prognostic-predictive correlation between PD-L1 expression and PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor efficacy remains controversial in ENKTL [31]. Consistent with our findings, a subset of extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma patients with elevated tumor PD-L1 expression exhibit disease progression following PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor therapy [31, 33]. This indicates compensatory pathway activation as a mechanism of immune evasion, highlighting the urgent requirement for more effective combination therapies in this study.

Our previous research findings suggest that SLC7A11 is a potential therapeutic target for ENKTL [15]. SLC7A11, a critical subunit of the cystine/glutamate antiporter (system Xc⁻), is essential for tumor cell survival and therapeutic resistance. By mediating cystine uptake and glutamate export, SLC7A11 supports glutathione (GSH) synthesis, thereby safeguarding cancer cells against ferroptosis[34]. In addition, under conditions of glucose deprivation, when NADPH supply is limited, high SLC7A11 expression drives excessive cystine uptake, leading to NADPH depletion, disulfide accumulation, and ultimately disulfidptosis [35].

Emerging evidence indicates a reciprocal crosstalk between SLC7A11 and PD-L1 in tumor cells. Studies have reported a correlation between SLC7A11 and PD-L1 expression in tumor tissues such as hepatocellular carcinoma, lung adenocarcinoma, and mesothelioma [17, 20, 21]. Experimental data from melanoma cell models show that inhibition of SLC7A11 upregulates the transcription factors IRF4 and EGR1, resulting in increased PD‑L1 expression [36]. In thyroid cancer models, the MDM2‑p53 inhibitor APG‑115 has been shown to downregulate SLC7A11, thereby promoting lipid peroxidation and enhancing PD‑L1 expression [19]. Conversely, PD‑L1 can suppress SLC7A11 expression through preferential activation of mTORC2 (RICTOR), leading to reduced GSH levels and increased ferroptosis sensitivity in vitro [37]. Collectively, these observations reveal a complex, bidirectional regulatory network between SLC7A11 and PD-L1.

Beyond direct molecular interactions within tumor cells, SLC7A11 also modulates the tumor immune microenvironment, influencing PD‑L1‑mediated immune tolerance, and providing a strong theoretical rationale for dual‑targeted therapy. By sustaining GSH synthesis, SLC7A11 inhibits ferroptosis and concurrently promotes the accumulation of myeloid‑derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) in the tumor microenvironment, thereby suppressing CD8+ T‑cell activity and attenuating antitumor immunity [38, 39]. Conversely, tumor immunotherapy may synergize with SLC7A11‑mediated ferroptosis to enhance anti‑tumor effects. Studies have found that immune checkpoint inhibitors can activate CD8+ T cells to release interferon‑γ (IFN‑γ), and IFN‑γ downregulates SLC7A11, thereby promoting ferroptosis in tumor cells [40].

This feed‑forward loop suggests that the combination of SLC7A11 inhibition with PD‑1/PD‑L1 blockade may produce therapeutic synergy. Increasing evidence supports the synergistic effects of ferroptosis inducers and PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. In hepatocellular carcinoma, MerTK upregulates SLC7A11 via the ERK/SP1 pathway, thereby suppressing ferroptosis in tumor cells and fostering an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, which leads to resistance to anti‑PD‑1/PD‑L1 therapy. Treatment with the MerTK inhibitor sitravatinib restores sensitivity to anti‑PD‑L1 therapy by promoting ferroptosis and reducing the infiltration of myeloid‑derived suppressor cells [39]. In pancreatic cancer, the epigenetic agent JIB04 induces ferroptosis by inhibiting the Nrf2-Slc40a1 axis, thereby transforming the immunosuppressive microenvironment into an immune-active state and sensitizing tumors to anti-PD-1 therapy [41]. In non-small cell lung cancer, the combination of the ferroptosis inducer IKE with anti-PD-1 effectively overcomes PD-L1-mediated resistance through the PI3K-AKT-HIF-1α pathway [42]. These findings urther substantiate the synergistic interaction between ferroptosis inducers and PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, highlighting their potential to reverse immune exclusion and promote an immunocompetent tumor microenvironment.

In accordance with this rationale, our study revealed that ENKTL patients with high co‑expression of SLC7A11 and PD‑L1 have significantly poorer prognosis, indicating that these two molecules co‑operate to promote tumor progression and immune evasion. Collectively, converging mechanistic and preclinical evidence across multiple cancer types strongly supports the dual targeting of SLC7A11 and the PD‑1/PD‑L1 axis as a rational and promising therapeutic strategy for SLC7A11‑high malignancies, including ENKTL.

Extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma exhibits significant immune microenvironmental heterogeneity. A multi-omics study by Zhao et al. classified NK/T-cell lymphomas into three distinct immune phenotypes: immune-desert, immune-deficient, and immune-inflamed subtypes [43]. In our cohort, mIHC analysis revealed sparse CD8+ T-cell infiltration across most ENKTL specimens, with substantial interpatient variability. Evidence indicates that the immune-inflamed subtype, characterized by abundant CD8+ T cells, is generally associated with more favorable outcomes. However, when CD8+ T cells express high levels of exhaustion markers such as PD-1 and CTLA-4, their cytotoxic function is impaired, limiting their capacity to mount effective anti-tumor responses [44].

Feng et al. reported that T cells from ENKTL patients exhibit elevated expression of multiple inhibitory receptors, accompanied by diminished proliferation and IFN‑γ production [45]. Cho et al. proposed a four‑subtype immune classification of ENKTL, demonstrating that CD8+ T‑cell‑rich immune evasion tumors express high PD‑L1 yet show no survival advantage, whereas Treg‑rich immune tolerant tumors are mostly early‑stage with better prognosis[32], consistent with our observation that CD8+ T‑cell density does not correlate with clinical outcome. The absence of prognostic significance of CD8+ T‑cell counts may reflect not only functional exhaustion but also spatial segregation from tumor cells. Single‑cell and spatial transcriptomic studies have shown that tumor‑associated macrophages in ENKTL promote immune evasion and suppress T‑cell activity within the tumor microenvironment via ligand-receptor interactions, underscoring the importance of spatial organization in shaping immune responses [46].

Furthermore, the frequent association of ENKTL with Epstein-Barr virus infection contributes to its immunosuppressive nature, as the virus can induce expression of immunosuppressive molecules including PD-L1, IL-10, and TGF-β, thereby compromising cytotoxic T-cell function [47]. In addition, the presence of dense fibrotic stroma and altered chemokine profiles within ENKTL lesions may impose physical and chemical barriers that hinder T-cell infiltration. Collectively, these factors provide a plausible explanation for the absence of correlation between quantitative measures of CD8+ T cells and PD-1+ cells with clinical outcomes in our cohort. Given the high heterogeneity of the ENKTL immune microenvironment, future efforts to develop dual‑targeted therapeutic strategies against SLC7A11 and PD‑1 should comprehensively evaluate their effects on immune cell subset function and the underlying molecular mechanisms.

In summary, our study provides the first confirmation of a correlation between the high expression levels of SLC7A11 and PD-L1 in ENKTL cells and a poor prognosis. While dual targeting of these pathways may represent a promising therapeutic approach, this hypothesis requires confirmation in functional studies. The interpretability of our findings is constrained by the limited sample size and technical constraints that precluded a detailed analyses of immune cell subsets and their functional states. Nonetheless, our results establish tumor‑cell‑specific SLC7A11/PD‑L1 co‑expression as an independent adverse prognostic factor in ENKT and provide a strong rationale for further mechanistic investigations and the development of dual‑targeted therapeutic strategies.
