Text mining and machine learning reveal global determinants of food insecurity
Authors: Bia Carneiro, Giuliano Resce, Nicola Caravaggio, Agapito Emanuele Santangelo, Giosuè Ruscica, Giulia Tucci, Grazia Pacillo, Gary Eilerts, Peter Läderach, Kevin Coffey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study applies Natural Language Processing (NLP) and Machine Learning (ML) to investigate global historical trends in food security. Using USAID’s Famine Early Warning Systems Network’s (FEWS NET) comprehensive reports spanning over two dozen countries, it explores prevalent dimensions such as shocks, outcomes, and coping capacities, offering insights into long-term food security conditions. Results highlight the prevalence of market and climate impacts across the countries and period considered. Based on results from the topic classification, ML models were applied to determine the most important factors that predict food insecurity. The analysis confirmed market shocks as the main predictors of food insecurity globally. The approach demonstrates the potential for extracting valuable insights from narrative sources that can support decision-making and strategic planning. This integrated approach not only enhances understanding of food security but also presents a versatile tool applicable beyond the context of humanitarian aid.Subject terms: Socioeconomic scenarios, Computational science, Computer science, Information technology

Introduction

Despite decades of decline, food insecurity is currently on the rise1. Tackling this challenge requires understanding the compounding factors that drive food insecurity2,3. Yet, although food security hazards such as climate shocks, conflicts, or market disruptions are widely documented in the literature, obstacles remain for systematically monitoring these potential risks4 as well as their interconnections.

Traditionally, Early Warning Systems (EWS) for food insecurity have been designed to detect and interpret early signals of potential food crises, enabling timely interventions and reducing hunger. These systems are considered essential tools for policymakers and humanitarian organisations5. EWS have evolved significantly to incorporate new approaches, stakeholder networks, and advancements in technology, adapting effectively to the changing dynamics of food insecurity5,6. For instance, in regions such as the Horn of Africa, remote sensing-based EWS have been implemented to complement national initiatives, providing extensive coverage and cost-effective monitoring of vegetation and crop yields7. A critical aspect of these systems is the understanding of vulnerability to shocks, notably climatic shocks, which significantly influence the effectiveness of EWS to accurately assess a community’s sensitivity and adaptive capacity to climate variability and its impacts on food and livelihoods8.

Data-driven methodologies utilized in contemporary EWS typically follow structured steps, including defining scope, identifying change drivers, generating scenarios, assessing implications, and continuous monitoring6. These methods leverage statistical models and readily accessible data such as food prices, weather patterns, and demographic information, generating frequent and localized forecasts9. This approach contrasts with traditional “convergence-of-evidence” methods, demonstrating superior accuracy by enhancing spatial detail and incorporating diverse data types, ultimately surpassing reliance on traditional methodologies like the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC)9.

Current EWS aspire to augment existing frameworks like the IPC by delivering more frequent, geographically detailed predictions. The employment of open data and transparent models addresses issues related to the “black box” nature of some existing systems, thus bolstering humanitarian response capabilities and improving governance in food security9,10. Furthermore, the establishment of comprehensive food safety risk early-warning index systems from a risk governance perspective provides a robust framework to enhance food safety risk prevention and control capabilities, thereby supporting government regulatory bodies in identifying and mitigating potential food safety risks effectively11. Such enhancements in predictive modeling significantly strengthen early detection, enabling more proactive and precise interventions in food crises.

Created in 1985 by the United States Agency for International Development (USAID), the Famine Early Warning Systems Network (FEWS NET) is a pioneering program that aims to provide governments, humanitarian agencies, and practitioners with comprehensive monitoring and forecasting of food security across more than thirty countries. Operating as a multi-agency initiative, FEWS NET collaborates with key partners within the US government as well as research and humanitarian organizations to integrate diverse data sources and expertise. Its analysis combines existing quantitative data with extensive qualitative field data collection across multiple domains, including agroclimatology, markets and trade, conflict, health, nutrition, and livelihoods, offering localized insights unavailable in purely statistical analyses.

By leveraging this extensive, multi-source data approach, FEWS NET provides context-specific and detailed scenario-based projections of food security conditions months in advance. These insights enable governments, humanitarian organizations, and policymakers to take proactive measures, such as pre-positioning food aid, implementing resilience programs, or adjusting policy responses to mitigate the impacts of emerging food crises.

At the same time, this accumulated wealth of knowledge is publicly available through documents in the program’s repository, providing a comprehensive historical overview of food insecurity drivers and outcomes in the global south. For instance, more than four thousand country-level food security outlook reports have been published since the turn of the millennium. Such an extensive repository presents a valuable opportunity for advanced analytical techniques such as natural language processing (NLP) and machine learning to extract deeper insights into food security trends. Analyzing report information over time enables the identification of recurring themes, shifting and emerging threats, and key drivers of food insecurity, as well as the detection of hidden relationships between food security indicators and external variables, uncovering potential future crises based on historical patterns.

However, as is common within large institutions, operational activities, competing priorities, and time and resource constraints mean that such longitudinally and programmatically rich repositories of information are underutilized as sources of knowledge. To address this issue, the integration of big data analytics and machine learning for systematic analysis of comprehensive institutional documentation provides an innovative strategy, as evidenced by recent studies leveraging underexplored organizational sources12–19. Specifically, textual content from institutional reporting mechanisms, mined using advanced techniques, provide valuable insights for macroeconomic trends17.

As Early Warning Systems continue to evolve toward greater transparency, predictive accuracy, and data integration, FEWS NET’s historical textual data remains an underutilized resource that, when leveraged through advanced analytics, can enhance the predictive power of food security monitoring and improve early intervention strategies. This study proposes an integrated, data-driven approach to systematically analyze historical trends in food insecurity indicators using the Famine Early Warning Systems Network (FEWS NET) country analyses as a case study, repurposing their periodic food security reports for a novel application. Considering the high effectiveness of FEWS NET’s scenario development and projections (Backer and Billing, 2021), the integrated approach seeks to unlock valuable insights into the complex factors influencing food insecurity, fostering a more comprehensive understanding of historical developments. Specifically, we aim to address two research questions:

What historical trends and insight can be identified from FEWS NET reporting, regarding the prevalence and dynamics of various dimensions of food insecurity drivers and outcomes?

Results

A framework for text mining food insecurity

Content analysis is widely used in the social sciences to discern patterns and changes in political agendas across time and geography20. Specifically, text mining is used to convert unstructured text from various sources into structured and normalized data21, facilitating the extraction of meaningful information. Moreover, the integration of interpretative appraisal and statistical techniques has the potential to generate novel insights for evidence-based policymaking22.

This study employed a data-driven approach integrating supervised text mining and machine learning to analyze a vast, unstructured dataset of publicly available textual data from FEWS NET’s country reporting. The goal was to investigate food security shocks, indicators, and outcomes, and to quantify relationships among these elements. The analysis followed a machine learning process involving data gathering, processing, and the application of machine learning models to derive insights23.

Once the data was collected and processed (see Materials and Methods), the first step was to verify the level of homogeneity within the body of text analyzed and identify any unexpected trends related to how particular terms were associated with different geographic locations. For this, a semantic network was created to visually present associations between countries and words in reports. Figure 1 presents the resulting network, which employed the “Force Atlas 2” force-directed algorithm to spatialize nodes, mapping the proximity and authority of entities in relation to each other24. This algorithm draws linked nodes closer together and pushes unrelated nodes farther apart, providing a visual interpretation of the dynamics between entities in the network.

A modularity algorithm was applied to identify community structures, grouping nodes into clusters based on the level of interaction between them, using the Louvain method25. The modularity coefficient, a metric indicating homogeneity within groups, measured 0.133 on a scale from -1 to +1, suggesting the absence of significant clusters. In other words, there were no notably different associations between groups of words and specific countries in the terminology used in reports across FEWS NET geographies. This finding is supported by the node spatialization analysis where countries form the center of the network, and terms are distributed around it in a regular circular pattern without noteworthy outliers or groups.

These results affirm the consistency of FEWS NET reporting, both across time and across geographies. This consistency is an intentional strategy within the FEWS NET scenario development process, which uses a standard methodology to assess the current and projected food security situation of areas of concern26. As such, it was expected that the documentation would be uniform, supporting the supervised text mining strategy to uncover representations of strategic topics.

The first component of supervised topic identification is the development of a custom taxonomy for food insecurity. This taxonomy presents the basis for the detection of relevant issues within the text of documents, as well as the starting point for the creation of a food insecurity ontology i.e., a representational vocabulary for a shared domain of discourse27.

Based on FEWS NET’s dimensions of food security, an analytical framework comprising three overarching themes and 11 sub-themes was constructed (see Fig. 2 for a summarized framework, and Supplementary Materials S1 for the complete version). The theme “Shocks and hazards” describes events which pose a potential threat to life, health, property, physical security, or the environment, and can have a significant impact on food security. These were grouped into the sub-themes: Climate, Conflict, Markets, Diseases, and Governance. The theme “Food Security Indicators” contains indicators related to three of the four pillars of food security28: food availability, food access, and food utilization. The fourth pillar stability is represented through the shocks from the first theme and the outcomes in the third theme, “Food, nutrition, and livelihood security outcomes.” The latter comprises sub-themes related to food security status, health, and nutrition indicators, as well as concepts related to livelihood strategies.

Source: Authors’ elaboration.

Based on the definitions of these analytical categories, key words for each concept were identified and manually included in order to establish a terminology from which to map the data against. A total of 118 key words, or topics, were identified across the sub-themes.

A central element of textual analysis is semantic association, as topics specified in a conceptual framework may be represented differently in the various sources. Expanding the terminology to identify similar words such as synonyms, hypernyms, and hyponyms improves the ability to uncover patterns in the corpora and effectively establishes a taxonomy for categorical classification. These potential term variations were tackled through a mixed method approach combining machine and expert-driven keyword expansion. In total, the final 118 concepts contain 9,758 associated terms. A validation was conducted that resulted in 105 topics with an accuracy above 70%, of which 66 are above the mean value of 84.53% (see Materials and Methods for a detailed discussion on the taxonomy development and validation process).

Determinants of food insecurity

Accurate prediction and forecasting are essential to implement efficient policies, but traditional econometric models fall short in addressing this challenge adequately. It has been demonstrated how conventional econometric models are limited in solving predictive tasks, especially those associated with policy problems, as they are tailored to yield unbiased coefficient estimates, rather than minimizing prediction errors12,29. Machine Learning (ML) algorithms have demonstrated significant potential in tackling predictive problems15,30 and are on the rise for solving issues connected to poverty targeting31 and the effectiveness of public programs and spending32.

In this light, and considering the reliability of our topic identification model in the systematic analysis of food insecurity trends, ML is a valuable approach to explore within the proposed framework. Specifically, we aim to identify which algorithms are best suited to predict food insecurity and to uncover the most important predictors.

The graphical representation in Fig. 5 enhances the understanding of the core determinants in predicting food insecurity. All features’ importance were standardized and then ordered based on a weighted average, constructed by multiplying each model’s feature by the relative model’s accuracy. Notice that the approach proposed identifies the total features’ effect in predicting food insecurity by considering simultaneously direct and indirect effects33.

Each model has a different algorithm to estimate the feature importance indices34. With Random Forest, feature importance is computed as the mean gain produced by the feature over all the trees, where the gain is measured by the Gini index. Using Extreme Gradient Boosting and Gradient Boosting Machine, feature importance is computed as the average improvement of the splitting on the features across all the trees generated by the boosting algorithm (a detailed discussion of the models is available in the Materials and Methods section).

The top 10 features able to predict food insecurity are “Commodity scarcity,” “Flooding,” “Rainfall,” “Market failure,” “Food prices,” “Inflation,” “Market prices,” “Drought,” “Grain prices,” and “Disease.” Six of them fall within the market framework, the most prominent one, while three are climate-oriented, and one is related to disease.

For those major predictors it is useful to stress in which countries they play a predominant role within our matrix. Table 2 reports the country-average value of our top 10 features within the Term Document Matrix (TDM) constructed on FEWS NET reports. For each feature the top five countries are reported. Burundi, Malawi, Senegal, and Madagascar stand out as countries where those drivers are more prominent, each appearing in five or more topic areas. These nations face overlapping challenges, particularly related to commodity scarcity, market instability, inflation, and food prices, suggesting heightened vulnerability to both economic and food system shocks. Mozambique and Somalia are also prominent, especially in climate-related topics as flooding and drought. Guinea and the Democratic Republic of Congo show a high average presence in disease-related reports, stressing out persistent public health concerns, instead. It must be stressed that we did not consider only country-specific reports in our analysis but also region-specific ones. Among those, the one focused on Central America and the Caribbean are the one with the highest average importance within the drought topic.

Regarding the features related to market dynamics, “Food prices” show a clearly positive pattern, meaning that the higher the presence of food prices within the reports, the higher the probability of the presence of food insecurity as well. This result is in line with the literature, which identifies food prices as a major driver of food insecurity35. “Grain prices,” linked to the previous features, show a non-linear pattern where, after an initial declining trend, it rises again, stressing how even in this case, a higher presence of grain prices would be associated with food insecurity. Conversely, while “Inflation” and “Market prices” show a negative relationship with our target variable, this may be because they are both broader market drivers that are not necessarily associated with food-related issues.

Moreover, “Commodity scarcity” has a bell-shaped curve, although the decreasing pattern after the turning point is moderate. “Market failure” shows an N-shape relationship instead.

Those features are all somewhat related to the agricultural sector and its link with both local and international markets. This is not surprising considering the larger presence of sub-Saharan Africa countries within the FEWS NET monitoring system. This area is in fact characterized by export-oriented agricultural policies which expose the sector to international market fluctuations and decrease in food consumption36.

By looking at the variables that fall under the umbrella of climate, “Flooding” shows a positive and concave relationship with food insecurity. For “Rainfall,” after an initial positive correlation, the pattern is decreasing thereafter, which could be explained by the fact that a higher presence of rainfall may reduce agricultural risks. Nonetheless, the slight uprising at the end of the PDP is not surprising, since even the excess of rainfall perhaps associated with extreme events due to climate change may undoubtedly affect harvests and livelihoods negatively. A similar pattern is observed for “Drought,” despite the initial positive relationship being far more pronounced. Finally, for “Disease,” the pattern has an initial positive association, followed by a U-shaped relationship with food insecurity.

Discussion

Every day, organizations generate knowledge through their operational and reporting mechanisms. Despite this wealth of information, the textual content in their knowledge base is often fragmented and not easily accessible to analysts and policymakers. This study aims to address these challenges by proposing an analytical framework and a model to transform bodies of text into structured data suitable for systematic analyses, applying this to the context of food security and using FEWS NET as a case study.

FEWS NET, a leading provider of global early warning and food security analysis, possesses extensive, consistent data spanning decades for over two dozen countries. Through supervised methods applied to their food security reporting, the research identifies the distribution of strategic topics across regions and their evolution. Furthermore, the machine learning approach enabled the identification of the most important predictors of food insecurity.

The topic classification shows that the most prevalent topics represented in FEWS NET’s reporting, globally, are connected to markets (such as food prices), humanitarian assistance, household income, rainfall, conflict, and harvest outcomes. The most important predictors for food insecurity are commodity scarcity, flooding, rainfall, market failure, food prices, and inflation. The machine learning analysis enables disentangling their non-linear relationship with the probability of food insecurity occurrence.

It is sobering to reflect on the results of this analysis, which underline that the most common features associated with instances of food insecurity in FEWS NET countries are market and climate-related conditions. Both these hazards have progressively become more notable as threats not only in FEWS NET-covered countries, but across the rest of the world due to global climate change and the globalization of markets and commodity trade seen over the recent decades.

Moreover, this study highlights the transformative role of natural language processing (NLP) and text mining in Early Warning Systems by systematically analyzing unstructured textual data and extracting meaningful patterns. These methodologies allow for a granular understanding of food security narratives by identifying key themes, detecting shifts in risk factors, and enhancing the predictive capacity of existing models. The application of feature selection techniques within machine learning further refines the analysis by pinpointing the most significant determinants of food insecurity, reducing noise, and improving model interpretability. By integrating these computational approaches, Early Warning Systems such as FEWS NET can move beyond traditional monitoring frameworks, leveraging rich textual repositories to enhance situational awareness, refine intervention strategies, and better anticipate emerging food crises. The findings showcase the potential for humanitarian aid institutions, such as USAID, to leverage diverse information sources effectively and optimize institutional data to address emerging questions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the potential of integrating text mining and machine learning to analyze large-scale, narrative-based early warning data in the domain of food security. By applying a custom analytical framework to over a decade of FEWS NET reporting, the research identifies critical trends and reveals consistent topic patterns across time and geography. The supervised topic classification confirmed the dominant role of market-related shocks and climate conditions—such as food prices, commodity scarcity, rainfall, and flooding—as recurrent themes and primary predictors of food insecurity. These findings underscore the robustness and internal consistency of FEWS NET documentation and the value of systematizing unstructured reporting for strategic insight.

The integration of machine learning algorithms not only enabled the identification of the most influential predictors of food insecurity but also allowed for a nuanced analysis of their functional relationships with food security outcomes. Feature importance and partial dependence analyses revealed non-linear associations between predictors and the likelihood of food insecurity, offering more refined perspectives than traditional models. This methodological innovation provides a pathway to disentangle the complexity of food crises and supports more targeted, evidence-driven intervention planning. Importantly, the analytical framework developed here demonstrates how institutional repositories like FEWS NET’s can be transformed into predictive tools, enhancing both the monitoring and anticipatory capacities of food security Early Warning Systems.

Looking ahead, the study highlights a critical opportunity for humanitarian organizations, governments, and donors to adopt similar approaches, combining computational methods with rich, underutilized textual data to strengthen their foresight and response capabilities. As global food systems continue to face unprecedented disruptions due to climate change, geopolitical instability, and economic volatility, the ability to predict and act on early signals of food insecurity will be increasingly vital. Future research could drill down to national and sub-national level analysis, further expand the potentiality of machine learning through causal approaches, as well as expand this framework to include real-time data streams and explore dynamic modeling to forecast evolving risk landscapes.

Methods

The full dataset comprises text and metadata from 4028 FEWS NET documents distributed across 36 countries. The documents included Food Security Outlook reports (1044) and Food Security Outlook Update reports (2984). These periodic reports provide ongoing monitoring evidence and are used to prioritize food security assistance needs. In 2009, FEWS NET’s tri-annual reporting adopted a common framework for analysis and classification of food insecurity, enhancing comparability across countries and over time. Therefore, the models developed, analysis, and validation focused on reports published under the latest template since 2013, totaling 1414 documents from 33 countries.The flow chart in Fig. 7 presents the research design.

Research design

Data extraction involved extracting relevant portions of text using a custom algorithm. Drawing on the standardized template that FEWS NET reports adopted from 2013 onwards, our analysis focused on the “National Overview” section. A database was generated for each country, containing report metadata and extracted textual sections. Supplementary materials S2 – S3 present the distribution of the documents and the process of extracting text from documents.

Semantic network analysis

A strategic component of this study is to assess the cohesion within the analyzed text and unearth any unforeseen trends in how specific terms are associated with diverse geographic locations. To achieve this goal, a network analysis approach was employed. Drawing inspiration from linguistics and cognitive sciences, which utilize semantic networks to represent relationships between concepts, languages, or words37,38, this study generated a network graph. In this graph, country names and words served as nodes, with each report forming an edge connecting a country to a word present in that document.

The study utilized a Term Document Matrix (TDM) for analysis, prepared through the R package tm39,40. The TDM underwent a preprocessing phase where punctuation, stop words (e.g., “the,” “is,” “of”), and numbers were removed. Additionally, words were converted to extract their base forms by removing affixes. The resulting TDM table distributed terms by rows and documents by columns, with matrix cells indicating the frequency of each term in each document. For the construction of the graph, the TDM table was imported into the open-source software Gephi41. This approach facilitated the visualization of the relationships between country names and associated terms, providing valuable insights into the semantic connections within the body of text under examination.

Topic classification

The analysis involved the development of a supervised model, employed to analyze extensive unstructured text, seeking implicit patterns, crucial for decision-making42. The initial step was constructing a custom analytical framework and subsequent taxonomy. This framework served as the foundation for identifying strategic issues and creating a food insecurity ontology27. Following the dimensions considered by FEWS NET in their reporting, the analytical framework was developed with three themes and 11 sub-themes, as previously discussed. Keywords for each topic were identified and included in the framework. After an iterative validation, 118 topics across sub-themes were identified. A complete version of the analytical framework is available in Supplementary Materials S1.

Taxonomy development

To enhance the analysis, expanding the terminology in the analytical framework to include similar words, such as synonyms, is essential for pattern discovery and effective categorical classification. This expansion involved a mixed approach, combining both machine-driven and expert-driven keyword expansion methods. The taxonomy development process started from the analytical framework, where a machine-driven step utilized a Word2Vec (W2V) to expand the initial 128 topics from the analytical framework. Word2Vec43–45, produces a distributed representation of similar words. It identifies semantically similar words based on similarity measures in a large unlabeled corpus, where words with similar contexts are considered similar. The skip-gram model was applied in this study, representing each word as a bag of character-grams46.

In the first step of the analysis, a vector of words for each topic was generated and qualitatively assessed to verify their adherence to the topic. Out of the initial 128 topics, 51 were positively evaluated and deemed suitable for food security analysis. For the remaining 77 topics that did not meet the criteria, a term expansion was conducted through a dictionary-based algorithm using AGROVOC, the Food and Agriculture Organization’s comprehensive multilingual vocabulary16. AGROVOC encompasses over 40,000 concepts across various areas of interest, including food, nutrition, agriculture, fisheries, forestry, and the environment, available in up to 37 languages. The related terminology was then extracted into JSON files, and a vector of words was generated. Following another round of expert validation, an additional 54 topics were accepted. As 22 topics still fell short through machine-driven solutions, extensive discussions with food security specialists ensued, resulting in manual adjustments. Some topics were merged or excluded, while others were added, culminating in 16 manually adjusted topics. The final taxonomy for food insecurity comprises 118 concepts and 9,758 key words.

Taxonomy validation

To determine the accuracy of the topic classification model, a validation strategy was implemented. A stratified sample from the dataset was randomly selected,1 comprising 20% of reports (276 out of 1431). A food security specialist and two climate security visiting researchers from the Alliance of Bioversity and CIAT read and manually classified the reports using the topics from the analytical framework.

The results from the manual classification were compared to the results from the machine classification using a confusion matrix, in which the accuracy was measured according to the frequency of corresponding classifications. In addition, the matrix enables identification of issues arising from misclassification by the machine, i.e., low specificity that leads to over classification (high number of false positives) or low sensitivity that leads to under classification (high number of false negatives), and points to ways in which the model can be boosted.

In cases of low specificity, they were resolved by determining the optimal threshold for classification of the topic. While in most cases the presence of any word associated with a particular topic in the taxonomy was classified as a topic presence, for the cases of over classification, a quartile function was used to detect the optimal threshold. This function divides the data into equal halves, where the median acts as the middle (50th percentile), with the lower part as the lower quartile (25th percentile) and the upper part as the upper quartile (75th percentile). In most cases, when the threshold is set at the upper quartile, accuracy improves. Regarding low sensitivity, the issue was tackled through consultation with the manual coders and food security specialists to improve the taxonomy. Model validation

In this study, validating the analytical framework for food insecurity and its taxonomy is as crucial as their development. The taxonomy serves as the structural foundation for systematic analysis and meaningful comparisons, ensuring a clear and standardized framework for interpretation. Incorporating external data sources enhances the research process by providing depth and authenticity against real-world events and trends, reinforcing the overall reliability of the analysis.

The validation relied on external data sources from the World Development Indicators (WDI), chosen for their relevance to research questions. As the World Bank’s primary collection of development indicators, comprising 1400 time series indicators across 217 countries over 50 years, the WDI datasets offer a comprehensive outlook on global socio-economic trends and provide critical insights into various dimensions relevant to food security48,49.

Validation begins by applying the taxonomy for food insecurity to the WDI dataset. Topic frequencies are made comparable by normalizing and scaling into a measure of prevalence. To determine if specific topics present in FEWS NET’s food security reporting align with World Bank development indicators, Pearson’s correlation tests were employed on the two datasets. Only indicators with a p-value below 1% were considered, emphasizing their significance in validating the taxonomy. The analysis focused on the correlation between topics “Conflict,” “Disease,” “Inflation,” and “Starvation” with various WDI indicators (see Fig. 9 for correlation values). For instance, the topic “Starvation” correlates positively with the WDI variables “Average deprivation share” and “Stillbirth rates” but negatively with “Freshwater availability”. Similar associations are found for the topics “Inflation” and “Disease,” indicating that increased topic frequency corresponds to upward trends in relevant WDI measures.

The topic “Conflict” shows a positive correlation with the WDI variables “Internally displaced persons” and “New displacement cases due to conflict or violence”. This suggests that a higher prevalence of conflict-related mentions in reports corresponds to increased displacement. With a 1% p-value, these statistically significant findings support the use of text from FEWS NET’s comprehensive reporting as source data for the characterization of food insecurity dimensions across countries.

The machine learning model for identifying food insecurity predictors

The analytical framework for food security includes a dichotomous variable of “Food insecurity” (Y), which is equal to one when a FEWS NET report had at least one occurrence for the topic with the same name, and zero otherwise. As features, all topics within the theme “Hazards/Shocks” were selected. We relied only on this theme as it represents drivers of food insecurity, while the other two groups comprise indicators or potential outcomes (see Fig. 2).

Furthermore, we excluded all features under the umbrella of disease (except for the more general feature “Disease”) due to their geographic specificity, which makes them difficult to generalize.2 Consequently, we had a total of 61 features, as reported in Table S1. Of these, 45 remained in the analysis, while 16 were either absent in the reports or had no variability. For the remaining features, values were normalized for comparison among individual reports.

The ROC curve serves as a visual representation of a classifier’s performance across various discrimination thresholds by illustrating the trade-off between the true positive rate (sensitivity) and the false positive rate (1-specificity)61. In ML, particularly in classification tasks, the ROC curve is a valuable tool for assessing the diagnostic capability of a model. In scenarios where the classification task is entirely unpredictable, the ROC curve aligns with the diagonal line, resulting in an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.5. A model that performs perfectly would yield an AUC of 1, indicating flawless discrimination between classes. In essence, a higher AUC signifies a more predictive model.

The AUC in Table 6 shows that the RF model outperforms all the other models. This is confirmed also by looking at other measures of performance shown in Table 4. In particular, the accuracy of RF is statistically higher than the no information rate. This is in line with previous empirical applications, confirming that tree-based models are the more competitive methods for structured binary tasks62,63. The second-best performing model is GBM, while the third place is taken by EGB. Nonetheless, it should be stressed that, for all models, the accuracy is statistically higher than the no information rate.
