Adipose tissue-derived mesenchymal stem cells combined with prednisone synergistically ameliorate autoimmune hepatitis in mice
Authors: Jinfeng Kong, Xiuhui Liang, Geng Chen, Xiaolong Liu, Naishun Liao, Dongliang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe combination of adipose tissue–derived mesenchymal stem cell (ADSC) transplantation with conventional therapy for autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) represents a critical step toward the clinical translation of ADSC-based therapeutics. However, no preclinical studies have yet reported on such combination therapy for AIH. This study aims to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of ADSC–prednisone (AP) combined therapy in an AIH mouse model and to investigate the underlying mechanisms.MethodsConA-induced and CFA/S100-induced AIH mice were treated with prednisone, ADSCs, or AP combination therapy. Serum biochemistry, histopathology, immune cell infiltration, cytokine profiles, MAPK signaling pathways (p38/MAPK, JNK/ERK), and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) were analyzed.ResultsBoth ADSC and prednisone monotherapies significantly reduced serum transaminases (ALT, AST, ALP, TBIL) and hepatic inflammation compared with untreated controls. Notably, AP combination therapy demonstrated superior protection, with greater reductions in immune cell infiltration (CD4⁺, CD8⁺, CD11b⁺), pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, IL-12), and autoantibody production (IgG). Mechanistically, ADSCs suppressed p38/MAPK activation, while AP further enhanced inhibition of JNK/p38 signaling. scRNA-seq revealed that AP therapy reshaped the liver cellular landscape, reduced immune cell abundance, and promoted regenerative signaling networks involving VEGF, TGF-β, and FGF pathways.ConclusionADSCs combined with prednisone synergistically ameliorate immune-mediated liver injury through dual immunosuppression and modulation of key signaling pathways, promoting immune tolerance and tissue repair. This strategy offers a promising therapeutic approach for AIH.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-05069-3.

Background

Autoimmune liver diseases, comprising autoimmune hepatitis, primary biliary cholangitis, and primary sclerosing cholangitis, are a heterogeneous group of chronic inflammatory disorders driven by the loss of immune tolerance to self-antigens expressed in the liver [1]. This breakdown in immune regulation results in sustained activation of autoreactive T cells, B-cell hyperactivity, autoantibody production, and persistent inflammation that targets hepatocytes or biliary epithelial cells [2]. The progressive hepatocellular injury, fibrosis, and eventual cirrhosis resulting from autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) can culminate in liver failure if not adequately treated. For decades, first-line immunosuppressive therapy—typically prednisone has formed the cornerstone of AIH management [3]. However, a substantial number of patients show incomplete biochemical or histologic responses, experience frequent relapses during dose reduction, or cannot tolerate long-term treatment [4]. Furthermore, extended corticosteroid use carries a well-known burden of adverse effects, such as osteoporosis, diabetes, hypertension, cataracts, weight gain, and heightened infection risk [5, 6]. These challenges highlight the pressing demand for novel therapeutic approaches capable of inducing deeper, more sustained remission while minimizing systemic toxicity through targeted immunomodulation rather than generalized immunosuppression.

Emerging preclinical and early-phase clinical evidence strongly supports the therapeutic potential of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) in immune-mediated disorders, owing to their multifaceted biological properties—including potent immunomodulatory effects on both innate and adaptive immunity [7], secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β, PGE2) [8–10], suppression of pathogenic T-cell subsets (such as Th1 and Th17) [11], promotion of regulatory T cells (Tregs) [12], inhibition of dendritic cell maturation [13], and stimulation of tissue repair through paracrine signaling and mitochondrial transfer. Although MSC therapy shows promise for autoimmune hepatitis (AIH), it remains firmly in the preclinical stage [14]. To accelerate clinical translation, initial clinical trials should therefore evaluate MSCs as an adjunct, not a replacement for standard immunosuppressive therapy, both for scientific rigor and ethical considerations, given the risk of rapid disease progression, liver failure, or death if AIH is inadequately controlled. Particularly, the limitations of monotherapy with either MSCs or corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone) in AIH should be carefully considered. On the one hand, MSC monotherapy may be limited in severe, chronic, or fibrotic stages of autoimmune liver disease, where entrenched immune dysregulation, dense inflammatory infiltrates, and advanced architectural remodeling create a hostile microenvironment that can impair MSC survival, engraftment, and functional activity [15, 16]. On the other hand, prednisone monotherapy, while providing rapid immunosuppression, offers only transient control and fails to restore long-term immune tolerance or promote intrinsic tissue repair [17]. Therefore, a combined therapeutic approach may offer synergistic benefits: prednisone could initially “condition” the hepatic niche to improve the survival and function of administered MSCs, while MSCs, in turn, could promote sustained immunomodulation and tissue regeneration, potentially facilitating steroid dose reduction or withdrawal.

In this study, we systematically evaluated the therapeutic efficacy and underlying molecular mechanisms of adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADSCs) in combination with prednisone in the murine AIH model. The results suggest that combined treatment offers a better efficacy than ADSC or prednisone monotherapy. Hence, this study provides experimental evidence for the subsequent clinical evaluation of MSC therapy for autoimmune liver diseases.

Methods

Animals

Adult male specific pathogen-free (SPF)-grade C57BL/6 mice (18–20 g; aged 5–6 weeks) were sourced from Xiamen Fudexin Biotech Co., Ltd. (Animal Production License No.: SCXK(Min)2025-0001). Animals were housed in IVC (Individually Ventilated Cages) (Five mice per cage), and maintained in a SPF barrier facility (Animal Use License No.: SYXK(Min)2025-0004) under controlled conditions: temperature 22– 25°C, relative humidity 40%–70%, and a 12-h light/dark cycle.

This study complies with ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. and was conducted under ethical approval from Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Mengchao Hepatobiliary Hospital, Fujian Medical University(Project Title: Exploring the mechanism of prednisone combined with mesenchymal stem cells in the treatment of autoimmune hepatitis; Approval No.: MCHH-AEC-2025-016; Approval Date: Apr 6, 2025).

ADSC isolation and culture

ADSCs were isolated as previously described [18, 19]. In brief, subcutaneous adipose tissue was harvested from the inguinal region of 4-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (n = 10), minced into ~ 0.1 mm³ fragments, and digested with 0.1% collagenase type I (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in HBSS supplemented with calcium and magnesium (HyClone, USA) at 37 °C for 60 min. The digestion was terminated by adding α-MEM (HyClone, USA) containing 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS; ExCell, China). The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 100-µm cell strainer and subjected to red blood cell lysis using an osmotic lysing buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). The remaining nucleated cells were collected, resuspended, and seeded at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL in T-75 flasks with α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA). Upon reaching confluence, cells were detached with 0.25% trypsin–EDTA (Gibco, USA) and passaged at a 1:3 ratio.

AIH model and treatment

Concanavalin A(Con A)–induced AIH model was induced by a single intravenous injection of Con A (Sigma-Aldrich) via the tail vein at 15 mg/kg, and CFA/S100-induced AIH model was established by intraperitoneal injection of 0.5 mL of a Complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA)-S100 emulsion (containing 2 g/L murine S100 protein in complete Freund’s adjuvant) on days 1 and 7. The AIH mice were randomly (using random table method) assigned to four groups receiving PBS, prednisone (5 mg/kg, intragastric administration), ADSCs (106 ADSCs/mouse in 0.2 mL PBS), or the combination of prednisone and ADSCs. The MAPK pathway inhibitor (SB203580, 5 mg/kg) and MAPK pathway activator (dehydrocorydaline, 300 mg/kg) were also administered intragastrically in the combined treatment regimen. The normal mice were set as the control group. Each group consisted of 6 animals, and a total of 90 mice were used. All animals were anesthetized and euthanized at predetermined time points, and serum and liver tissues were collected for subsequent therapeutic evaluation (Table 1).

Sample collection and processing

Euthanasia was performed after the completion of cell and drug therapy. Animals received intraperitoneal anesthesi with 1% pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg). Blood was collected from the orbital vein and liver tissue was collected for pathological examination and transcriptome sequencing using standard protocols.

Liver function assay and ELISA

Serum was obtained by centrifuging whole blood at 3,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and total bilirubin (TBIL) were quantified using commercial biochemical assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The serum Levels of IgG were quantified using commercial ELISA assay kits (MultiSciences Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Histopathological examination

Liver tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature (RT) for 24 h, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) for histopathological assessment and examined under a Carl Zeiss light microscope (Germany).

Immunofluorescence histochemistry

Fresh liver tissue was embedded in OCT and cryosectioned (10 μm). Sections were fixed in 4% PFA for 20 min at RT, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: α-SMA (1:200), p-P38(1:300), p-JNK(1:300), ERK5(1:300), CD4 (1:200), CD11b (1:300), or CD8 (1:200). After three PBS washes, sections were probed with Alexa Fluor 488 labeled secondary antibodies (1:1000) for 1 h at RT, followed by DAPI nuclear staining (50 µg/mL, 10 min). Fluorescence imaging was performed on a Carl Zeiss microscope (Germany).

Immunohistochemistry

Following initial tissue section preparation, antigen retrieval was performed by immersing sections in citrate-based retrieval buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) and heating in a pressure cooker for 2 min, followed by natural cooling to RT and three PBS washes. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% H2O2 for 10 min, and sections were then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 30 min at RT. Sections were subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CK18, HMGB1, CD68, ERK5, ERK1/2, p-ERK1/2, p38, p-p38, JNK, and p-JNK at dilutions of 1:200, 1:200, 1:200, 1:300, 1:200, 1:400, 1:300, 1:300, 1:300, and 1:300, respectively. After three PBS washes, sections were probed with appropriate secondary antibodies for 2 h at RT. Following an additional three PBS washes, DAB staining was performed, and the protein expression was evaluated using a Carl Zeiss fluorescence microscope (Germany).

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from liver tissues using TRIzol reagent (TransGen, China). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the Transcriptor First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Roche Applied Science, Mannheim, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was carried out on an ABI StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA, USA) with the following cycling conditions: 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 70 °C for 30 s. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1. Gene expression levels were normalized to β-actin, and relative mRNA abundance was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blot analysis

Liver tissues were homogenized in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 1 mM EDTA, pH 7.4) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, USA). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (TransGen Biotech, China). Equal amounts of protein (40 µg/lane) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose (NC) membranes (Pall, USA) in transfer buffer (25 mM Tris, 192 mM glycine, 20% methanol, pH 8.3). The NC membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in TBST for 2 h at RT, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ERK5, p38, p-p38, JNK, and p-JNK (all 1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, USA), and β-actin (1:1000; TransGen, China). After three washes with TBST, membranes were probed with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:8000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA) for 1 h at RT. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and detected by chemiluminescent imaging.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from liver tissues using a commercial kit (TransGen Biotech, China) and subsequently subjected to poly(A)-selected RNA sequencing on the Illumina HiSeq X10 platform.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)

Fresh liver tissues were preserved in GEXSCOPE Tissue Preservation Solution, and processed at Novogene Lab (China). After standard preparation (washing, filtration, RBC lysis), single-cell suspensions were loaded onto microfluidic devices (~ 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL). scRNA-seq libraries were generated using the 10×Genomics® protocol and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (150 bp paired-end).

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). Differences between two groups were assessed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s T-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

ADSC transplantation combined with prednisone ameliorate liver injuries in ConA-induced AIH mice

To evaluate the therapeutic effects of ADSC transplantation in combination with prednisone on AIH, we established a murine model of ConA-induced AIH, followed by treatment with prednisone, ADSCs, or both at the indicated time points (Fig. 1A). Comparing with the ConA group, both prednisone and ADSC treatments significantly reduced ALT, AST (Fig. 1C), ALP, and total bilirubin (Tbil) levels, with the ADSC-prednisone (AP) combined therapy showing the most pronounced protective effect (Fig. 1B and E). Histopathological examination via HE staining revealed severe hepatocellular necrosis and inflammatory infiltration in the ConA group, whereas the ADSC and prednisone groups exhibited marked improvement, and AP combined therapy further attenuated liver damage, preserving liver architecture (Fig. 1F). Additionally, we also found that AP combined therapy can further reduce the expression of CK18 in liver tissues (Fig. 1G). Therefore, these results suggesting that ADSC transplantation combined with prednisone could ameliorate liver injury in ConA-induced AIH mice.

ADSCs combined with prednisone attenuate immune cell infiltration and pro-inflammatory responses in ConA-induced AIH mice

To investigate the immunomodulatory effects of AP combined therapy in ConA-induced AIH mice, we analyzed hepatic immune cell infiltration and inflammatory profiles. Immunofluorescence staining revealed significantly increased infiltration of CD4⁺ T cells, CD11b⁺ immune cells, and CD8⁺ T cells in the ConA group compared to normal controls (Fig. 2A). Both prednisone and ADSC treatment reduced the accumulation of these immune cells, with the AP combined therapy showing the most potent suppressive effect (Fig. 2B). Consistently, serum IgG levels were markedly elevated after ConA challenge and were significantly reduced by ADSC or prednisone monotherapy, with further suppression observed in the combination group (Fig. 2C). The mRNA levels of key pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-12, were dramatically upregulated in the ConA group, while all treatments effectively downregulated their expression, with the greatest reduction shown in the combination group (Fig. 2D). Additionally, we also found that AP combined therapy can further reduce the expression of CD68 and HMGB1 in liver tissues (Figure S1). These findings indicate that the synergistic action of ADSCs and prednisone exerts robust anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects in ConA-induced AIH mice.

ADSC transplantation combined with prednisone ameliorate liver injuries in CFA/S100-induced AIH mice

We next investigated the therapeutic effects of ADSC transplantation in combination with prednisone on CFA/S100-induced AIH mice (Fig. 3A). Macroscopic examination revealed liver swelling and surface irregularities in CFA/S100 group, which were markedly improved by both ADSC and prednisone monotherapy, with the AP combined therapy yielding the best preservation of liver morphology (Fig. 3B). Biochemical analyses showed that serum levels of ALT, AST, ALP, and TBIL were significantly elevated in the CFA/S100 group compared to normal controls, whereas all treatments reduced these markers, with the most pronounced effect observed in the combination group (Fig. 3C, F). Histopathological evaluation via HE staining confirmed severe hepatocellular necrosis and inflammatory infiltration in the CFA/S100 group, while treatment groups exhibited reduced inflammation and structural preservation, particularly in the combination group (Fig. 3G). These results demonstrate that ADSCs and prednisone synergistically ameliorate liver injury and restore hepatic function in CFA/S100-induced AIH mice.

ADSCs combined with prednisone reduce inflammation in CFA/S100-induced AIH mice

We further investigated the immunomodulatory effects of AP combined therapy in the CFA/S100 model. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed massive infiltration of CD4⁺, CD11b⁺, and CD8⁺ immune cells in the livers of CFA/S100-treated mice (Fig. 4A). Both ADSC and prednisone treatments significantly reduced the number of infiltrating immune cells, with AP combined therapy achieving the greatest suppression (Fig. 4B). Notably, serum IgG levels, a marker of humoral immunity, were markedly elevated in the CFA/S100 group, and were significantly lowered by both monotherapies, with the strongest reduction observed in the combination group (Fig. 4C). These findings indicate that the combination therapy exerts potent immunosuppressive effects by dampening both cellular and humoral immune responses.

ADSCs combined with prednisone ameliorate liver injuries by targeting MAPK signaling

RNA-seq analysis was next used to explore the potential mechanism of the AP combined therapy for AIH. The volcano plot data suggested that the expression of 1216 genes was different between ADSCs and model group (ConA vs. ADSC), 661 gene expression was different between combination and model group (ConA vs. ADSC + Prednisone), 691 gene expression was different between combination and prednisone group (Prednisone vs. ADSC + Prednisone), and the expression of 336 genes as different between combination and ADSCs group (ADSC vs. ADSC + Prednisone) (Fig. 5A). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the MAPK signaling pathway was significantly enriched across all groups (Fig. 5B), suggesting that AP combined therapy ameliorates AIH-related liver injury, at least in part, by modulating MAPK signaling. IHC and Western blot confirmed reduced phosphorylation of p-P38, p-JNK, and ERK5 in treated groups; notably, the combination showed the lowest p-P38 levels, suggesting synergistic inhibition of P38/MAPK signaling (Fig. 5C–F). To further confirm the above result, a p-P38 inhibitor (SB203580) was further used in ConA-induced AIH mice. As expect, the p-P38 in liver tissues could be effectively inhibited by SB203580 administration, and the inhibition of p-P38 and p-P38/P38 could be further enhanced by the combined treatment of SB203580, ADSCs and prednisone (Fig. 6A and B), indicating the synergistic effect with the SB203580 co-treatment. Furthermore, a slight decreased serum levels of ALT, AST, ALP, TBIL, and IgG after SB203580 co-treatment, although there is no statistical difference between ADSC+Prednisone and SB203580 co-treatment group (Fig. 6C). Histologically, liver architecture was nearly restored both in ADSC+Prednisone and SB203580 co-treatment group (Fig. 6D). Significantly, the increased serum levels (ALT, AST, ALP, TBIL) and the reduced inflammatory infiltration (in histopathological examination) were clearly shown after using the p-P38 activator (dehydrocorydaline) in the AIH mice, indicating the reversal of therapeutic benefits from the AP combined therapy (Figure S2). Taken together, the p38/MAPK signaling pathway is involved in the protective effects of ADSCs and prednisone in AIH.

ScRNA-seq reveals altered cellular landscape and signaling networks

To gain deeper insight into the cellular and molecular mechanisms of the combination therapy, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on liver tissues after combination therapy. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) plots revealed distinct clustering patterns across groups, with the combined group showing a shift toward immune quiescence and hepatocyte recovery (Fig. 7A). Cell type proportion analysis indicated a significant reduction in immune cells (especially T cells and macrophages) and an increase in hepatocytes and endothelial cells following treatment (Fig. 7B). Gene set enrichment analysis identified key signaling pathways modulated by the AP combined therapy, including downregulation of pro-inflammatory signals (e.g., Sema3a-Nrp1, Lgals9) and upregulation of regenerative and angiogenic signals (e.g., Vegfa-Vegfr, Tgfβ-Fgfr2) (Fig. 7C). Moreover, intercellular communication networks revealed enhanced cross-talk between Kupffer cells, hepatocytes, and T cells through pathways such as NRG, PARs, FGF, SEMA3, VEGF, IGF, CSF, and TGF-β signaling (Fig. 7D), suggesting a coordinated immune resolution and tissue repair process. Functional enrichment analysis further highlighted the involvement of pathways related to immune regulation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and metabolic homeostasis (Fig. 7E). In particular, the cell subpopulation analysis in hepatic stellate cells (HSC) enriched the MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 7E). Immunofluorescence co-localization analysis further confirmed that AP combined therapy could reduce p-P38 and p-JNK in the HSC (marking with α-SMA) cells (Figue S3). Collectively, scRNA-seq data provide comprehensive evidence that the combined therapy reshapes liver cellular landscape and promotes a reparative microenvironment.

Discussion

Although corticosteroids such as prednisone are effective in inducing remission in AIH, their long-term use is frequently complicated by metabolic, skeletal, and infectious adverse events, particularly in elderly or comorbid patients [5, 6], making AIH a persistent therapeutic challenge. ADSCs have emerged as a promising adjunctive therapy in liver diseases, owing to their potent immunomodulatory, anti-fibrotic, and tissue-reparative properties [20, 21]. In particular, the ADSC-secretome has been shown protective effect in liver ischemia-reperfusion and partial hepatectomy [22–24]. In the clinical studies for treating steroid-refractory acute graft-versus-host disease, researchers have found that MSC treatment can reduce the dosage of glucocorticoids (such as prednisone) [25]. However, whether ADSCs can be rationally combined with conventional immunosuppressants to enhance efficacy in AIH has remained unclear. In this study, we provide compelling evidence that the combination of ADSCs and prednisone exerts synergistic therapeutic effects in AIH mice. Our multimodal approach, integrating biochemical, histological, molecular, and single-cell transcriptomic analyses, reveals a coordinated mechanism involving suppression of inflammatory cell infiltration, downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, inhibition of MAPK signaling (particularly p-P38), and reprogramming of intercellular communication networks toward tissue repair.

Notably, although ADSC monotherapy demonstrated appreciable anti-inflammatory activity as evidenced by reduced hepatic infiltration of CD4+T cells and CD11b+myeloid populations, it fell short in achieving complete histological or functional recovery in the CFA/S100-induced AIH model. Specifically, although serum transaminases (ALT and AST) were modestly lowered compared to untreated controls, IgG levels remained elevated, and architectural disarray, including interface hepatitis and lobular inflammation, persisted. This partial efficacy likely reflects the inability of ADSCs alone to fully suppress the robust, antigen-driven adaptive immune response that characterizes AIH. Unlike prednisone, which directly targets glucocorticoid receptors to broadly inhibit T-cell activation and B-cell-mediated autoantibody production, ADSCs primarily modulate the immune milieu through paracrine signaling and cell-cell interactions that favor tolerance over rapid immunosuppression. Consequently, while ADSCs create a microenvironment conducive to repair, by enhancing Treg frequencies, dampening dendritic cell function, and secreting trophic factors such as HGF and VEGF, their impact on established humoral and cytotoxic effector arms may be insufficient as a standalone intervention in acute or severe AIH. This limitation underscores the rationale for AP combined therapy. The synergy likely arises from complementary mechanisms: prednisone rapidly suppresses T-cell activation and cytokine production via glucocorticoid receptor–mediated transcriptional repression [26, 27], while ADSCs exert paracrine and contact-dependent immunoregulation by promoting regulatory T-cell (Treg) expansion, inhibiting dendritic cell maturation, and secreting anti-inflammatory mediators such as IL-10, TGF-β, and PGE₂ [28, 29]. This dual action not only mitigates ongoing inflammation but also promotes an immune-tolerant microenvironment conducive to tissue regeneration.

It has been previously reported that pharmacological inhibition of the P38/MAPK pathway can attenuate liver injury in experimental models of AIH [30]. Consistent with this report, we found that treatment with the p-P38 inhibitor SB203580 alone moderately reduced serum transaminase levels (ALT, AST, TBIL and ALP) and attenuated hepatic inflammation, confirming that targeting P38/MAPK signaling provides a measurable therapeutic benefit in AIH. In the context of ADSC-prednisone (AP) combined therapy, SB203580 co-treatment resulted in a slight further improvement in liver function markers (e.g., ALT, AST) compared to AP combination alone. However, this incremental benefit was marginal and did not translate into significant histological or immunological advantages (such as reduced immune cell infiltration or IgG levels), suggesting a lack of true synergistic interaction between p-P38 inhibition and AP combination regimen. Additionally, despite similar degree of p-P38 inhibition between AP combination and p-P38 inhibition alone, the latter did not demonstrate superior overall efficacy. This apparent discrepancy strongly implies that the therapeutic superiority of AP combined therapy cannot be fully attributed to down-regulation of P38/MAPK signaling alone. Instead, it likely arises from additional, parallel mechanisms, such as modulation of T-cell differentiation, induction of regulatory immune subsets, secretion of trophic factors (e.g., HGF, VEGF, IL-10), and restoration of intercellular communication networks, as evidenced by our single-cell RNA sequencing data (Fig. 7).

Indeed, scRNA-seq provided unprecedented resolution into how combination therapy reshapes the hepatic cellular ecosystem. Untreated AIH livers were enriched in pathogenic populations, including inflammatory CD8⁺ T cells, activated Kupffer cells, and profibrotic stellate cells, which are hallmarks of immune-mediated parenchymal destruction. In contrast, AP combined therapy shifted the landscape toward homeostasis by increasing proportions of quiescent hepatocytes and endothelial cells, reducing immune cell burden, and upregulating regenerative ligand–receptor pairs (e.g., Vegfa–Flt1, Tgfb1–Tgfbr2, Fgf2–Fgfr1). Critically, intercellular communication analysis revealed enhanced crosstalk between parenchymal and non-parenchymal cells via VEGF and TGF-β signaling pathways known to promote angiogenesis, metabolic recovery, and matrix remodeling [31, 32]. These findings underscore that effective therapy does not merely suppress inflammation but actively reconstructs a reparative niche. Moreover, intercellular communication networks revealed enhanced cross-talk between Kupffer cells, hepatocytes, and T cells through pathways such as NRG, PARs, FGF, SEMA3, VEGF, IGF, CSF, and TGF-β signaling (Fig. 7D), suggesting a coordinated immune resolution and tissue repair process. This multi-pathway dialogue between innate immune cells, parenchymal cells, and adaptive immune cells likely reflects a complex, albeit potentially dysregulated, attempt to restore hepatic homeostasis. The concomitant activation of pathways like TGF-β and CSF may drive pro-fibrotic and inflammatory responses, respectively, while the engagement of FGF and IGF signaling could indicate concurrent regenerative efforts. This simultaneous push-and-pull between injury and repair signals underscores the dynamic and competing processes within the AIH microenvironment. Future studies should aim to temporally resolve these interactions to determine whether they represent a failed resolution program or an imbalanced but persistent reparative response, which could reveal novel therapeutic targets for rebalancing immune-stromal crosstalk in AIH.

Collectively, our findings position ADSC–prednisone combination therapy not merely as an additive regimen, but as a synergistic strategy that simultaneously dampens pathogenic immunity and actively fosters a pro-regenerative hepatic niche.

Conclusion

In summary, ADSCs combined with prednisone provide a powerful therapeutic strategy for liver function restoration in AIH, acting through multifaceted mechanisms including immune suppression, MAPK pathway inhibition, and restoration of intercellular communication. This approach provide a key experimental evidence for further clinical translation of ADSC therapy for AIH.
