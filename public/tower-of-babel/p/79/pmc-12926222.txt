Mechanisms of fosfomycin resistance observed in clinical isolates of Escherichia coli from Canada: CANWARD 2007–2022
Authors: Laura F Mataseje, Cole Lysak, Nicole Lerminiaux, Melanie Baxter, James A Karlowsky, George G Zhanel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral fosfomycin is commonly prescribed as a single 3 g dose to empirically treat uncomplicated cystitis. This study investigated the mechanisms and frequency of fosfomycin resistance among clinical isolates of Escherichia coli from Canada.MethodsIn total, 5012 clinical isolates of E. coli collected by the CANWARD surveillance programme from 2007 to 2022 were tested for susceptibility to fosfomycin using the CLSI reference agar dilution method. MICs were interpreted by 2024 CLSI M100 breakpoints. The 19 fosfomycin-resistant (MIC ≥256 mg/L) and 45 fosfomycin-intermediate (MIC 128 mg/L) isolates identified, and 69 fosfomycin-susceptible (MIC ≤1–64 mg/L) control isolates underwent WGS.ResultsIn total, 98.7% (4948/5012) of all E. coli isolates tested were fosfomycin-susceptible; 0.4% were fosfomycin-resistant and 0.9% were fosfomycin-intermediate. ST131 accounted for 42.1%, 40.0% and 20.3%, respectively, of fosfomycin-resistant, fosfomycin-intermediate and fosfomycin-susceptible isolates. Six (31.6%) fosfomycin-resistant isolates and one (2.2%) fosfomycin-intermediate isolate carried a fosA gene. All fosA genes were plasmid mediated and all but one fosA-carrying plasmid harboured multiple other antimicrobial resistance genes. A nonsense mutation in, or complete deletion of, one or more genes in the fosfomycin transport systems GlpT and UhpT were observed in 63.2% (37/57) of fosA-negative fosfomycin-resistant and fosfomycin-intermediate isolates but were not detected in any of the 69 fosfomycin-susceptible isolates tested.ConclusionsIn Canada, the occurrence of mobile fosfomycin resistance determinants (fosA genes) in clinical isolates of E. coli is low (∼0.14% of all isolates). Our results highlight the importance of the functional transporter systems GlpT and UhpT for the in vitro activity of fosfomycin.

Introduction

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are a common affliction, particularly of reproductive age women. Estimates indicate that approximately half of all women will experience at least one UTI during their lifetime.1 Escherichia coli accounts for ∼80% of community-acquired UTIs in otherwise healthy women, and ∼50% of hospital-associated infections.2 In vitro resistance to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, β-lactams and fluoroquinolones, and MDR are frequently observed phenotypes when antimicrobial susceptibility testing of urinary isolates of E. coli is performed.3

Oral fosfomycin is a first-line empirical agent for the treatment of uncomplicated cystitis and is typically prescribed as a single 3 g dose. Its use may warrant particular consideration when MDR E. coli is suspected or proven to be the aetiological agent of uncomplicated cystitis.4 Fosfomycin imitates both glycerol-3-phosphate (G3P) and glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) to gain access into the cytoplasm of E. coli cells via GlpT, the G3P transporter, and UhpT, the G6P transporter.5 In the cytoplasm, fosfomycin acts as a phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) analogue, where it binds to and inhibits MurA (UDP-N-acetylglucosamine enolpyruvyl transferase), the enzyme that catalyses the initial reaction in the peptidoglycan synthesis pathway.6 Fosfomycin activity is not diminished by mechanisms commonly responsible for resistance to β-lactams (e.g. β-lactamases, PBP mutations), which target later steps in peptidoglycan synthesis.6

Three primary mechanisms of fosfomycin resistance are known: target (MurA) modification, enzymatic inactivation of fosfomycin, and impaired uptake of fosfomycin into bacterial cells.5 Modification of MurA, preventing fosfomycin binding to it, is a common mechanism of resistance identified by in vitro selection experiments; however, this mechanism is rarely present in clinical isolates resistant to fosfomycin.7 Recently, the enzymatic inactivation of fosfomycin through plasmid-mediated fosA genes has been the focus of many studies.8,9 More than 10 different fosA variants have been identified to date, with fosA3 being the most common.8,9 New variants continue to be reported, such as fosA11 in Providencia rettgeri.10 The third, and currently the most common mechanism of resistance in clinical isolates, is reduced fosfomycin uptake. Chromosomal mutations in the genes encoding GlpT and UhpT transport system proteins reduce fosfomycin permeability. Furthermore, chromosomal mutations of local regulators of transporter genes, such as uhpA, uhpB and uhpC, are also known to result in reduced fosfomycin uptake. Earlier studies have also shown that mutations in the chromosomal genes cyaA and ptsI, which are responsible for cAMP levels, can play a role in fosfomycin resistance because low cAMP levels down-regulate expression of the two fosfomycin transporters.11,12

A previous study of 868 urinary isolates of E. coli from Canadian hospital laboratories collected between 2010 and 2013, reported that resistance to fosfomycin was rare, with only 0.1% of isolates being resistant.13 The current study reports the frequency of fosfomycin resistance among 5012 clinical isolates of E. coli collected by the CANWARD surveillance programme from 2007 to 2022, and investigates mechanisms of fosfomycin resistance in all fosfomycin-resistant and fosfomycin-intermediate isolates identified, as well as a subset of fosfomycin-susceptible isolates, using WGS. To enrich our cohort of fosfomycin-resistant and fosfomycin-intermediate resistant isolates we chose to test urinary isolates as well as isolates from non-urinary sites (i.e. blood, respiratory and wound isolates).

Materials and methods

Bacterial isolates

The CANWARD surveillance programme is an annual Canadian Antimicrobial Resistance Alliance (CARA)/Health Canada–partnered study that collects bacterial pathogens cultured in tertiary-care hospital laboratories in 8 of the 10 Canadian provinces.14 Isolates are collected consecutively, one per patient, from both inpatients and outpatients attending emergency rooms, hospital clinics, medical/surgical wards and ICUs. Each laboratory submits annual quotas of respiratory, wound, urine and bloodstream isolates. CANWARD receives annual approval by the University of Manitoba Research Ethics Board (H2009:059).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

From 2007 to 2022, the CANWARD surveillance programme tested 5012 clinical isolates of E. coli for susceptibility to fosfomycin using the CLSI reference agar dilution method on Mueller–Hinton agar supplemented with 25 mg/L glucose-6-phosphate.15–17 The isolates were cultured from respiratory, wound, urine and blood specimens. Fosfomycin was supplied by Paladin Labs (Montreal, Canada). Stock solutions and dilutions were prepared in CAMHB as described by the CLSI.15 Quality control was performed following CLSI recommendations, and fosfomycin MICs were interpreted using 2024 CLSI M100 breakpoints (resistant ≥256 mg/L; intermediate 128 mg/L; susceptible ≤64 mg/L) for urinary isolates of E. coli.16,17 All isolates deemed fosfomycin resistant (n = 19), all isolates deemed fosfomycin intermediate (n = 45) and a similarly sized set of fosfomycin-susceptible isolates with MICs ranging from ≤1 to 64 mg/L (n = 69), to function as controls, were chosen for WGS. To enrich our cohort of fosfomycin-resistant and fosfomycin-intermediate resistant isolates we chose to test urinary isolates as well as isolates from non-urinary sites (i.e. blood, respiratory and wound isolates). For simplicity we chose to apply urinary CLSI breakpoints (SIR) to urinary and non-urinary isolates. The 69 control E. coli isolates with fosfomycin MICs ranging from ≤1 to 64 mg/L were chosen to represent isolates with varying fosfomycin MICs and were randomly selected albeit representing all of the geographical areas of CANWARD.

Statistics were conducted using a 2 × 2 contingency table and Fisher’s exact test.

WGS

Genomic DNA was extracted using Qiagen DNeasy 96 Blood and Tissue Kits (Hilden, Germany). Short-read libraries were created with TruSeq Nano DNA HT sample preparation kits (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Isolates were sequenced using an Illumina NextSeq platform. Paired-end, 151 bp indexed reads were generated using the Illumina NextSeq platform. FastQC v0.11.9 (https://github.com/s-andrews/FastQC) was used to assess quality control metrics for Illumina reads. Contigs were assembled using Shovill v1.1.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/shovill). Oxford Nanopore Technologies sequencing libraries were prepped using the Rapid Barcoding kit (SQK-RBK004) and R9.4.1 flowcells. Reads were basecalled using the super accurate model (SUP) with Guppy v6.5.7. De novo assemblies were completed using Unicycler v0.5.0 (https://github.com/rrwick/Unicycler). Bacterial species were confirmed using the RefSeq Masher Matches tool (https://github.com/phac-nml/refseq_masher). StarAMR v0.11.018 was used to detect antimicrobial resistance genes using the ResFinder database v2024-08-0619 and STs using the MLST database v2.23.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/mlst).

A custom script was used to locate 18 genes of interest [murA, crp, crr, cyaA, pta, ptsH, ptsI, glpT, uhpT, uhpA, uhpB, uhpC, fruK, plsX, purB, hflD, mutL and mutS from E. coli K12 MG1655 (NC_000913.3)] along with fosA genes (fosL1, fosA2, fosA3, fosA4, fosA5, fosA6, fosA7, fosA7.5 and fosA8) in the assembled sequence data of each isolate. Briefly, reference sequences were blasted against isolate assemblies using blastn v2.15.020 with a minimum length of 100 bases and minimum 92% sequence identity. The nucleotide hits were then extracted, re-oriented to be on the same strand, and translated to protein sequences. Stop codons resulting in premature truncation of proteins were identified; if no stop codons were found, the protein sequences were aligned to the reference using MUSCLE v5.121 and analysed for amino acid mutations relative to the reference (substitutions, deletions or insertions). Nucleotide hits near the edges of contigs were investigated manually. For gene targets that were not detected in the assembly based on the above search criteria we refer to them in the text as ‘complete deletions’.

Plots were created in R v4.4.222 using the tidyverse packages23 and ComplexHeatmap v2.22.24 Sequencing read data are available on NCBI SRA under BioProject number PRJNA1303168 (reviewer link: https://dataview.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/object/PRJNA1303168?reviewer=68qmummpp0jh6amm97n2hnc737).

Results

Of the 5012 clinical isolates of E. coli collected by the CANWARD surveillance programme from 2007 to 2022 and tested for susceptibility to fosfomycin, 0.4% (n = 19) were fosfomycin resistant; nine isolates had a fosfomycin MIC of 256 mg/L, two isolates had a fosfomycin MIC of 512 mg/L and eight isolates had a fosfomycin MIC >512 mg/L. Forty-five isolates (0.9%) were fosfomycin intermediate (MIC 128 mg/L). The 69 fosfomycin-susceptible isolates chosen for WGS comprised 29, 11, 5, 3, 7, 4 and 10 isolates with, respectively, fosfomycin MICs of ≤1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 and 64 mg/L. Of the 19 fosfomycin-resistant isolates, 1 was collected in each of 2007, 2008, 2009, 2010, 2014, 2015 and 2016; 2 isolates were collected in each of 2017, 2018 and 2022; and 6 isolates were collected in 2021. The 45 fosfomycin-intermediate isolates were obtained as follows: 2007, 3; 2008, 3; 2009, 4; 2010, 4; 2011, 2; 2012, 3; 2013, 3; 2014, 2; 2015, 4; 2016, 3; 2017, 3; 2018, 4; 2019, 0; 2020, 1; 2021, 4 and 2022, 2. Fosfomycin MICs and full descriptions of the WGS data for all 133 E. coli isolates included in this study are listed in Table S1 (available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online).

Among fosfomycin-resistant isolates, ST131 (n = 8; 42.1%) was the most common ST, followed by ST648 (n = 2; 10.5%); other STs accounted for only single isolates of fosfomycin-resistant E. coli (Table S1). ST131 was also the most common ST among both fosfomycin-intermediate (n = 18; 40.0%) and fosfomycin-susceptible isolates (n = 14; 20.3%). We looked at the association of ST131 with ESBLs as a whole and then if there were any associations with either ST131 or ESBLs between fosfomycin-susceptible or non-susceptible (intermediate or resistant) isolates. We observed that ST131 was significantly more associated with an ESBL compared with non-ST131 isolates (P < 0.0001). ST131 was significantly associated with fosfomycin non-susceptible isolates compared with susceptible isolates (P = 0.0138). Finally, the presence of an ESBL was also significantly associated with fosfomycin non-susceptible isolates compared with susceptible isolates (P = 0.0075).

Fosfomycin-resistant isolates tended to carry more antimicrobial resistance genes than fosfomycin-intermediate or fosfomycin-susceptible isolates (Figure 1). Among the 19 fosfomycin-resistant isolates, 89.5% also carried resistance genes to aminoglycosides, β-lactams and quinolones; 78.9% carried tetracycline resistance genes; 73.7% carried sulfonamide resistance genes; 63.2% carried trimethoprim resistance genes; and 47.4% carried macrolide resistance genes. Common resistance genes across both fosfomycin-resistant and fosfomycin-intermediate isolates included: sul1 (47.4% of fosfomycin-resistant isolates; 46.7% of fosfomycin-intermediate isolates), sul2 (52.6%; 20.0%), blaTEM-1B/C (47.4%; 31.1%), blaCTX-M (57.9%; 30.6%), tetA/tetB/tetC/tetM (84.2%; 48.9%), aph(3″)-Ib (52.6%; 22.2%), aph(6)-Id (52.6%; 22.2%), mph(A) (47.4%; 31.1%), dfrA (63.2%; 48.9%) and aadA (68.4%; 37.8%). blaCTX-M-14 and blaCTX-M-15 were the two most common blaCTX-M types among both fosfomycin-resistant and fosfomycin-intermediate isolates (Table S1).

Proportions of E. coli isolates encoding antimicrobial resistance genes categorized by antimicrobial class and stratified by fosfomycin-susceptible (S), -intermediate (I) and -resistant (R) phenotypes. Fosfomycin MIC categories were defined by 2024 CLSI M100 breakpoints: susceptible, ≤64 mg/L; intermediate, 128 mg/L; and resistant, ≥256 mg/L. The ‘other’ antimicrobial class includes rpoB (n = 1) and ampC promoter (n = 1) mutations.

Six (31.6%) fosfomycin-resistant isolates and one (2.2%) fosfomycin-intermediate isolate carried a fosA gene (Table S2). The fosA genes present in fosfomycin-resistant isolates were fosA3 (n = 2), fosA (n = 1), fosA4 (n = 1), fosA7.5 (n = 1) and fosA8 (n = 1); fosA7.5 was identified in one fosfomycin-intermediate isolate. Using long-read sequencing, FosA-type plasmids carrying fosA genes were resolved (Table S2). The plasmids ranged in size from 47 to 232 kb. Plasmid replicon types were variable and included IncN, IncC, IncI1, IncFII, IncFII/N and IncHI2/HI2A. Only replicon type IncFII was observed in multiple plasmids (carrying fosA, fosA3 and fosA8 genes), which were not closely related (data not shown). All but one fos gene –containing plasmid also carried other antimicrobial resistance genes; at least one of these additional genes was always a β-lactamase (i.e. blaCTX-M-65, blaCTX-M-55, blaTEM-1B, blaTEM-232, blaTEM-214 or blaCMY-2).

Amino acid mutations in murA, crp, crr, pta, glpT, uhpT, cyaA, ptsH, ptsI, uhpA, uhpB, uhpC, fruK, plsX, purB, hflD, mutL and mutS were determined by aligning protein sequences to the reference sequences from E. coli K12 MG1655 (Table S1); proportions of isolates (excluding isolates carrying a fosA gene) with amino acid mutations in six genes (murA, glpT, uhpT, uhpA, uhpB, uhpC) stratified by fosfomycin MIC are shown in Figure 2.

Prevalence of amino acid mutations identified for six major genes (murA, glpT, uphT, uphA, uphB and uphC) stratified by fosfomycin MIC for 126 isolates of E. coli. The figure excludes the seven isolates of E. coli identified with a fosA. Mutations are annotated with the amino acid change position relative to the reference E. coli K12 MG1655 gene. ‘Stop’ indicates a premature stop codon; ‘NA’ indicates the gene was missing from the assembly. Isolate proportion values of 0 (darkest shade of blue) indicate that the mutation was not present in any isolates, and values of 1 (darkest shade of red) indicate that the mutation was present in all isolates. Large mutations in the uhpC and uhpA genes were excluded for three isolates and one isolate, respectively, to improve figure readability.

Among the 69 fosfomycin-susceptible isolates, there were only 6 isolates with notable gene mutations or alterations; each of the 6 isolates exhibited only a single gene mutation or alteration. Two isolates had a Pro99→Ser substitution within murA, which has been loosely associated with fosfomycin resistance.25 However, Seok et al.25 reported this mutation in combination with fosA3. In our report, isolates with a Pro99→Ser change had a fosfomycin MIC of ≤1 mg/L or 8 mg/L, suggesting that this mutation alone does not mediate fosfomycin resistance. The remaining four isolates had MICs of 16 to 32 mg/L and contained a premature stop codon in one of ptsI, uhpA, uhpB or uhpC. These alterations may elevate fosfomycin MICs by affecting the activation of cyaA or expression of uhpT. Of note, 16 isolates with fosfomycin MICs of 16 to 64 mg/L had no mutations in any of the genes we characterized (Table S2).

For the 57 isolates of E. coli that were fosfomycin resistant or fosfomycin intermediate and did not carry a fosA gene, we focused our analysis on alterations in the GlpT and UhpT transporters and the UhpABC system. Amino acid substitutions were detected in GlpT in 5 of the 57 isolates; the specific substitutions were: Gly389→Glu (n = 2), Gly395→Arg (n = 1), Pro218→Leu (n = 1) and Arg45→His (n = 1) (Table S1). None of these mutations have previously been reported, nor has their impact been characterized; these amino acid substitutions were only observed in isolates with fosfomycin MICs ≥128 mg/L. Previously described amino acid substitutions were commonly found (e.g. Glu448→Lys; Leu297→Phe; Glu443→Gln; Gln444→Glu; Ala16→Thr)12 but these have been previously reported to have no impact on protein function.26 Additionally, seven isolates contained nonsense mutations in glpT (all at different positions). Less variability was observed in amino acid substitutions within UhpT. The substitution Glu350→Gln (n = 23; 40.4%), previously described as having no impact on protein function,26 was also identified in 36.6% of fosfomycin-susceptible isolates in this study. The only other substitution detected among isolates not susceptible to fosfomycin was Asp320→Tyr (n = 1), which has not been previously described. Four other isolates had nonsense mutations, each at a unique position. Two additional isolates had 38 and 33 amino acid deletions within UhpT. In 13 isolates, the uhpT gene was missing entirely; in all but 2 of these 13 isolates, the uhpA, uhpB and uphC genes were also missing. Additional nonsense mutations in uhpA, uhpB and uphC genes were commonly observed in isolates not susceptible to fosfomycin (n = 13). Overall, 37 of 57 (63.2%) isolates not susceptible to fosfomycin had a nonsense mutation or complete deletion of one or more of glpT, uhpT or uhpABC. These mutations or deletions were not observed in any fosfomycin-susceptible isolates and were found in every instance but three, in non-FosA-containing fosfomycin-resistant isolates. Of interest, these three fosA-negative fosfomycin-resistant isolates contained undescribed mutations in GlpT [Gly389→Glu (n = 2) and Arg45→His (n = 1)] but had no other detected mutations within the GlpT/UhpT transporters and UhpABC system. Future work on these specific mutations should be conducted to determine whether they contributed meaningfully to fosfomycin resistance in these isolates.

Genes required for cAMP regulation were investigated (Table S1). Transcription of glpT and uhpT is under control of CyaA.7 Mutations in CyaA and their effects on fosfomycin MICs remain unclear.12 One study suggested substitution in Ser142→Asn contributed to increased MICs,27 another indicated that Gly222→Ser, Ser356→Leu and Gly359→Glu changes resulted in loss of protein structure or function.26 In our study, we found 119 of all 133 (89.5%) isolates studied had one or more of these substitutions and did not correlate with fosfomycin MICs. Activation of CyaA requires the presence of G6P and the phosphotransferase system (PTS) system, which includes ptsH and ptsI. A study on amino acid substitutions within ptsI revealed a number of positions that were described as having no impact on protein function and subsequently did not contribute to the fosfomycin MIC (i.e. Arg367→Lys, Ala306→Thr and Val25→Ile).26 In our study, we did not find any additional amino acid substitutions among the fosfomycin-resistant and fosfomycin-intermediate isolates studied. Additionally, we did not observe mutations or substitutions in ptsH. Data on pta have suggested that deletion of this gene decreases susceptibility to fosfomycin.27,28 We did not observe any deletions in this gene; however, we did note a limited number of amino acid substitutions in fosfomycin-resistant and fosfomycin-intermediate isolates, specifically Ala454→Thr (n = 1), Arg401→Cys (n = 1) and Val450→Thr (n = 1), but the implications of these mutations are unknown. We did not observe deletions or mutations in crr or crp.

Previous work has shown that deletion of purB, hflD, mutL, mutS, fruK or plsX genes is associated with an increased fosfomycin MIC.29 In our study, among all 133 isolates studied, we observed only a single instance where one of these genes (mutS) was absent from an isolate (Table S1); that isolate had a fosfomycin MIC of 128 mg/L and no additional mutations associated with an increased fosfomycin MIC. We found that amino acid substitutions in purB, hflD, mutL, mutS, fruK and plsX genes were common; however, some changes were only observed in fosfomycin-resistant and fosfomycin-intermediate isolates. Those changes were plsX, Pro286→Ser (n = 1); hflD, Asp7→Asn (n = 1); mutL, Ala306→Gly (n = 1), Ile195→Val (n = 1) and Ser505→Pro (n = 1); and mutS, Gly696→Glu (n = 1) and Thr414→Ala (n = 1). The implications of amino acid substitutions in these genes remain to be elucidated.

Discussion

The frequency of fosfomycin resistance among clinical isolates of E. coli in Canada remains low (<1%) when resistance is defined by interpreting reference method agar dilution MICs with current CLSI breakpoints for fosfomycin tested against urinary isolates of E. coli.15,16 In the current study of 5012 clinical isolates of E. coli from 2007 to 2022, only 0.4% were fosfomycin resistant (MIC ≥256 mg/L) and 0.9% were fosfomycin intermediate (MIC 128 mg/L). Only 6 of the 19 (31.6%) fosfomycin-resistant isolates carried a fosA gene. FosA plasmid size and replicon types were variable, with only IncFII observed in multiple plasmids; however, none of the replicon types were related, likely indicating multiple introductions of plasmid-mediated fosA genes into E. coli isolates over the study period. Several studies have examined FosA3 and reported its association with multiple plasmid replicon types, including IncN and IncFII,30–32 as we observed in our fosfomycin-resistant isolates. Additionally, FosA3 is known to be associated with CTX-M types, including CTX-M-55 and CTX-M-65, which we have confirmed with our data.30–32 FosA8 was first described in 2019 on an IncN plasmid and is believed to have originated from Leclercia adecarboxylata.33 The FosA8 plasmid we identified was also on an IncN plasmid. With the exception of one isolate, all FosA-containing plasmids were associated with additional antimicrobial resistance genes that included at least one β-lactamase gene. Therefore, the risk of selection for fosA-carrying isolates of E. coli by use of antimicrobial agents other than fosfomycin clearly exists and requires monitoring. Furthermore, the association of E. coli ST131 and ESBLs, specifically blaCTX-M types, has been well described.34 Here we report on a significant association between fosfomycin non-susceptible E. coli ST131 as well as ESBL production compared with fosfomycin-susceptible isolates. This combination of β-lactamase and clonal type may be contributing to increasing fosfomycin non-susceptible isolates in Canada.

Our results highlight the importance of the functional transporter systems, GlpT and UhpT, for fosfomycin activity. Several substitution mutations in GlpT were found solely in fosfomycin-resistant isolates and have not previously been reported, specifically Arg45→His, Gly395→Arg, Gly389→Glu and Pro218→Leu. Huang et al.35 have previously shown that when Arg45, a conserved amino acid in bacterial GlpT proteins, is replaced by lysine, GlpT substrate transport activity is significantly decreased. This finding suggests that another amino acid substitution for Arg45, as we observed, has the potential to limit fosfomycin transport by GlpT and result in resistance. Similarly, a substitution mutation at amino acid Gly389, a backbone amino acid in GlpT known to recognize and bind the glycerol moiety of G3P, may also limit fosfomycin binding to and transport by GlpT.36 Future work is needed to confirm these mutations and their direct association with elevated fosfomycin MICs. Nonsense mutations were also observed in some glpT genes resulting in a truncated, non-functional gene. There were nonsense mutations in five fosfomycin-resistant isolates; the locations of these mutations were dissimilar for all isolates. Previous studies have shown that premature stop codons resulting in the loss of function of GlpT are linked to fosfomycin-resistant and fosfomycin-intermediate phenotypes.12 One study reported that the deletion of nucleotide 401 resulted in a premature stop codon for glpT and was associated with fosfomycin resistance in E. coli.37 Further work is needed to better describe the effects of nonsense mutations in GlpT on fosfomycin resistance.

In our investigation, the inducible transport system UhpT, coupled with regulatory genes uhpA, uhpB and uhpC, harboured crucial mutations impacting fosfomycin activity in both fosfomycin-resistant and -intermediate isolates of E. coli. The function of the UhpT system is intricately regulated by the phosphorelay system, UhpABC. Consequently, mutations affecting UhpABC function influence the overall expression levels of uhpT.8 uhpA, uhpB and uhpC exhibit a high mutation frequency,38 a trend corroborated by our sequencing results. Mutations in the UhpT system leading to fosfomycin resistance predominantly manifest as nonsense mutations, complete operon deletions or partial operon deletions. Cattoir et al.8 previously reported that isolates of E. coli with such nonsense mutations and deleted operons displayed fosfomycin MICs >256 mg/L. In the isolates we studied, we did not observe discernible patterns among these mutations in the UhpABC system. However, 22.8% of fosfomycin-resistant and fosfomycin-intermediate isolates had a missing uhpT gene in combination with a partial or completely missing uhpA, uhpB or uhpC gene.

This study is not without limitations. To enrich our cohort of fosfomycin-resistant and fosfomycin intermediate-resistant isolates we chose to test urinary isolates as well as isolates from non-urinary sites (i.e. blood, respiratory and wound isolates). We chose to supplement our urinary isolates with non-urinary isolates as fosfomycin-resistant E. coli are so uncommon (only 0.4% in our study) in Canada. We also specifically chose to apply urinary CLSI breakpoints (SIR) to urinary and non-urinary isolates. This was performed for simplicity as we were most interested in investigating fosfomycin resistance mechanisms in E. coli isolates with very high MICs to fosfomycin (≥128 mg/L) to assess resistance mechanisms and particularly fosA genes. It should be mentioned that there is a contrast between EUCAST and CLSI fosfomycin breakpoints, with EUCAST’s limited breakpoints and cautions around non-urinary sources. If we had used EUCAST fosfomycin breakpoints, the number of E. coli isolates deemed resistant and intermediate isolates would have been higher.

We conclude that fosfomycin-resistant (0.4%; MIC ≥256 mg/L) and fosfomycin-intermediate (0.9%; MIC 128 mg/L) isolates of E. coli remain rare in Canada and that the occurrence of FosA mobile resistance elements is low (0.14%; 7/5012). Our results highlight the importance of GlpT and UhpT transport systems for the activity of fosfomycin. Research elucidating the importance of the novel mutations associated with fosfomycin resistance identified in the current study may be important.
