The biochemical function of bivalent aptamer assemblies against B cell markers CD19 and CD20
Authors: Yilam Ng Cen, Nicole B. Williams, Nicolás Di Siervi, Raja Mohanrao, Lexi Chen, Leandro Cerchietti, Prabodhika Mallikaratchy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
B cells, essential components of the adaptive immune system, are central to the pathology of B cell malignancies and autoimmune disorders. These cells can be specifically targeted using ligands that recognize B cell surface receptors such as CD19 and CD20. Utilizing ligand-guided selection (LIGS), a variation of the systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment (SELEX) method, we previously identified high-affinity, high-specificity aptamers targeting CD19 and CD20. In this study, we report the functional characterization of dimeric aptamer assemblies designed to target CD19 and CD20 on B cell lymphomas, reinforcing the potential of LIGS-derived aptamers as versatile tools for targeting cell surface receptors. Functional studies in CD21-negative B cells using the most optimized dimeric aptamers revealed that the dimeric CD19 aptamers were selectively internalized, imitating the behavior of anti-CD19 antibodies. Additionally, dimeric CD20 aptamers exhibited enhanced antigen binding compared to their monomeric counterparts, without inducing calcium release. These findings underscore the promise of LIGS-generated aptamers as cost-effective, stable, and precise therapeutic agents for B cell-associated malignancies such as diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL), advancing the development of aptamer-based synthetic therapeutics with strong clinical potential.

Introduction

Central to the adaptive immune system, B cells are implicated in many diseases, including B cell malignances, such as non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (NHL) and Hodgkin’s lymphoma, and autoimmune diseases, such as systemic lupus erythematosus, multiple sclerosis (MS), and rheumatoid arthritis (RA).1,2 Thus, markers of B cells have been recognized as critical antigens in the development of therapeutic strategies.2 Two widely targeted B cell-specific antigens are CD19 and CD20. CD20 is a surface protein expressed on B cells and plays a key role in B cell development, differentiation, and B cell receptor (BCR) signaling.3 Rituximab, a monoclonal antibody targeting CD20, has been widely used in the treatment of B cell-associated malignancies.3 However, CD19 has a broader expression profile across B cell populations, making it a particularly valuable target for the development of precision therapies. Notably, antibodies targeting CD19 have demonstrated therapeutic success in treating CD20-negative B cell disorders.4 Current therapeutic strategies targeting CD19 include chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells, bispecific antibodies, and anti-CD19 antibody-drug conjugates, all of which have shown promise in the treatment of B cell malignancies.5,6 Nevertheless, the complexity and cost associated with engineered cell therapies and monoclonal antibody-drug conjugates production have limited their widespread application, underscoring the need for alternative therapeutic targeting agents.7,8,9

Aptamers, consisting of short single-stranded DNA or RNA sequences that fold into well-defined three-dimensional conformations enabling high-affinity and specific target recognition, have demonstrated significant therapeutic potential.10,11 The small size and synthetic nature of aptamers can facilitate the introduction of a wide-range of chemical modifications that elevate therapeutic success.12,13,14 In 2004, pegaptanib, an aptamer against vascular endothelial growth factor was approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of age-related macular degeneration (AMD).15 A second aptamer against AMD was approved by the FDA in 2023, and clinical trials are currently investigating several promising aptamer candidates against various disease targets.12,15 Employing a novel method called multi-target ligand-guided selection (LIGS), we recently identified multiple aptamer candidates against CD20 and CD19 with high specificity using a cell-SELEX (systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment) library enriched against Toledo, a B lymphocyte cell line.16,17 Several observations have indicated that anti-CD19 antibody internalization depends on the expression levels of CD19’s co-receptor CD21.18 Yet, there have been no reported investigations detailing the their co-receptor dependency of anti-CD19 and anti-CD20 aptamers or their biological function. Herein, to address these gaps, we generated anti-CD20 and anti-CD19 dimeric aptamers explored their function. Since the structure-activity-relationship (SAR) of aptamers is important for optimizing aptamer length, we first truncated anti-CD20 and anti-CD19 aptamers into shorter variants by removing nonessential regions, while preserving structural integrity and increasing binding affinity. Truncated aptamer variants with the highest affinity and specificity were selected for the design of dimeric constructs. Dimeric aptamers incorporating the most optimized polyethylene glycol (PEG) linkers were evaluated for their internalization and target-specific functionality against CD19 and CD20. Our results showed that dimeric aptamers targeting CD19 underwent slow internalization into CD21-negative, CD19-positive lymphoma cells. Through a series of experiments, we demonstrated that the presence of the co-receptor CD21 impairs CD19 aptamer internalization, mirroring the behavior observed with anti-CD19 monoclonal antibodies. In contrast, CD21 expression did not affect the binding or function of the anti-CD20 aptamer. Moreover, the dimeric anti-CD20 aptamer exhibited enhanced antigen binding compared to its monomeric counterpart but did not trigger calcium release upon binding to the tested B cell lines. These findings underscore that LIGS-generated aptamers can replicate key functional characteristics of monoclonal antibodies, supporting their potential as effective and targeted therapeutic alternatives to antibody-based treatments.

Results

Truncation and binding affinity analysis of anti-CD19 and anti-CD20 aptamers

We identified two aptamers against CD19, namely WB15.CD19 and WB17.CD19, with two mutations distinguishing the two.17 Interestingly, in a separate study, we used LIGS to generate anti-CD3 aptamers and found a similar trend of a family of aptamers originating from a single sequence with mutations that caused variable affinities.19 Based on this observation, we focused on WB15.CD19 and WB17.CD19 for functional studies. To optimize aptamer length, we truncated 8 bases from the 3′-end and 4 bases from the 5′-end of both WB15.CD19 and WB17.CD19, removing a total of 12 bases to generate the WB15.CD19.1 and WB17.CD19.1 variants, respectively (Figures S1A–S1C and S1F). Assuming that the aptamer’s functional region forms a hairpin, we further removed 21 bases to generate WB15.CD19.2 and removed 25 bases to generate WB17.CD19.2 (Figures S1C and S1G). All variants were assessed for their apparent affinity constants. Both WB15.CD19.1 and WB17.CD19.1 variants exhibited a slightly lower affinity than the parent variants, while WB15.CD19.2 and WB17.CD19.2 completely lost their binding capability toward CD19 expressed on Raji cells (Table 1; Figures S2A–S2D). These findings suggest that critical secondary or tertiary structures necessary for CD19 recognition were disrupted upon truncation, despite partial retention of the predicted structure. We then tested whether retention of the second loop consisting of 5′-C (54) to G (62)-3′ reinstated CD19 recognition (Figures S1H and S1I) but found no improvement in binding. Based on these observations, we suggest that the aptamer binding mechanism likely follows an “induced-fit” model, in which target engagement promotes conformational adaptation of the aptamer, rather than a “fold-and-bind” model, where the aptamer adapts a fixed structure prior to binding. An alternative explanation is that the full-length aptamer is required to stabilize the folded secondary structure necessary for binding, hindering truncations. Next, we truncated a previously reported CD20 aptamer, WB1.CD20. Using an approach similar to that described previously, we first removed 5 bases from the 3′-end and 8 bases from the 5′-end to generate WB1.CD20.1 (Table 1; Figures S3A, S3B, S4A, and S4B). We further truncated 9 bases to generate WB1.CD20.2, followed by an additional 5 bases truncation to produce WB1.CD20.3. Specificity of truncated anti-CD19 and anti-CD20 aptamer variants

The truncated variants were then assessed for their specificity using CD19/CD20-positive and CD19/CD20-negative cells, including knockout CD19 and CD20 Burkitt’s lymphoma CA46 cells. Since only the first truncation showed considerable binding affinity, we focused on assessing the specificity of the WB1.CD20.1 aptamer against CD20-positive cell lines, including the Burkitt’s lymphoma cell lines Raji, BJAB, and SKLY-16 and the NHL cell line Toledo. Negative control cells included T cell leukemia Jurkat.E6 and CD20-knockout (CD20KO) CA46 cells (Burkitt’s lymphoma). The binding patterns of the WB1.CD20.1 variant in these B cell lines suggested that it retained its binding specificity toward surface CD20 (Figures S5A–S5G).

Then, we utilized the same cell lines to assess binding specificity of CD19 variants WB15.CD19.1 and WB17.CD19.1. While we observed slightly decreased binding affinity of truncated CD19 variants compared to their parent aptamers, the specificity of the aptamers toward CD19 was unaffected by truncation, as demonstrated by specific binding of both variants toward the CD19-positive cell lines Raji, Toledo, BJAB, and SKLY-16 but not toward CD19KO CA46 cells or CD19-negative Jurkat E6.1 cells (Figures S5H–S5N and S5O–S5U). Finally, using fluorescence microscopy, we assessed whether the truncated CD19 aptamer WB17.CD19.1 and truncated CD20 aptamer WB1.CD20.1 would colocalize with their respective mAbs. As expected, both aptamers colocalized with their respective antibodies, further demonstrating target specificity (Figure S6).

Dimerization of anti-CD19 and anti-CD20 aptamers

Since the apparent affinities of the monomeric aptamers are insufficient for functional cellular assays, we dimerized both anti-CD20 and anti-CD19 aptamers using PEG linkers to enhance their apparent affinity. The generation of dimeric aptamers to improve avidity and induce biological effect on cell receptor proteins is well established.20 Dimeric aptamers with monomers of 35–45 bases have been reported to require longer PEG linkers than aptamers with more bases to achieve desired avidity.21 Since both anti-CD19 and anti-CD20 aptamers are 65 bases long, we used spacer 9 to optimize the linker length between the two aptamers. Four dimeric variants of WB1.CD20.1 were designed and synthesized with linker lengths ranging from one spacer (∼1.32 nm) to four spacers (∼5.28 nm) (Table 1; Figure S3G). The first dimeric variant, WB1/1.CD20.1_1S, with a single spacer exhibited reduced binding avidity (Kd = 83 ± 58 nanomolar [nM]) compared to the monomeric variants (Table 1; Figures S3G and S7A). The second variant, WB1/1.CD20.1_2S, with two spacers exhibited similar binding affinity to WB1/1.CD20.1_1S (Kd = 53 ± 40 nM) (Table 1; Figure S7B). Interestingly, the best binding affinity was observed with WB1/1.CD20.1_3S, which contained three spacers (Kd = 15 ± 7.4 nM) (Table 1; Figure S7C). In contrast, the fourth variant, WB1/1.CD20.1_4S, with four spacers, demonstrated a weaker binding affinity than the monomeric variants (Kd = 25 ± 12 nM) (Table 1; Figure S7D), suggesting that the optimal spacer length is ∼3.96 nm, corresponding to three spacers. When apparent binding affinities were assessed at physiological temperatures, variants with 1 or 4 spacers (WB1/1.CD20.1_1S and WB1/1.CD20.1_4S) exhibited reduced affinity, indicating decreased aptamer stability at higher temperatures. In contrast, variants with 2 or 3 spacers showed slightly improved affinity compared to their binding at 4°C (Table 1; Figures S7E–S7H). For example, WB1/1.CD20.1_1S exhibited no binding at 37°C (Figure S7E). However, apparent affinity gradually improved with longer spacer lengths, as observed with WB1/1.CD20.1_2S, which showed moderate binding at 37°C (Kd = 50 ± 2.3 nM) (Figure S7F). WB1/1.CD20.1_3S maintained strong binding at 37°C (Kd = 14 ± 8.3 nM) (Figure S7G), confirming its superior stability and performance across temperatures. Finally, WB1/1.CD20.1_4S displayed weaker affinity than the monomeric variants at 37°C (Kd = 65 ± 38 nM) (Figure S7H) suggesting that excessively long spacers may diminish the cooperative binding advantage of dimeric aptamers, causing them to behave more like monomers.

Next, we used two truncated aptamer variants, WB15.CD19.1 and WB17.CD19.1, targeting CD19, and synthesized a total of seven dimeric aptamers. Specifically, we generated three dimeric constructs based on WB15.CD19.1 and four based on WB17.CD19.1. First, we designed two homodimeric aptamers, each consisting of either two WB15.CD19.1 or two WB17.CD19.1 aptamers. Following a linker optimization approach similar to that used for anti-CD20 aptamers, we optimized the linker length by varying the number of PEG spacer units between the aptamers (Table 1; Figures S1D, S1J, and S8). The homodimeric aptamer WB17/17.CD19.1_1S showed no affinity saturation at 4°C, indicating that shorter linker length may have destabilized aptamer binding forming intramolecular interactions between the two aptamers. However, at 37°C, the same dimeric aptamer construct showed improved avidity (Kd = 56 ± 49 nM), suggesting that higher temperatures might have destabilized the intramolecular interactions between the two aptamers formed at 4°C. We observed a similar binding pattern for WB15/15.CD19.1_1S, in which the avidity was 125 ± 68 nM at 4°C but improved to 11 ± 6.1 nM at physiological temperature (Table 1; Figures S8I and S8K). We did not observe this temperature-dependent discrepancy in avidity when 2, 3, and 4 spacers were used, further suggesting that the shorter linker length facilitates intramolecular interactions between the aptamers that destabilize the functional aptamer structure (Table 1; Figures S8B/S8F, S8C/S8G, S8D/S8H, and S8J/S8L). A temperature dependence of aptamer binding has been reported before; for example, the TD05 aptamer selected at 4°C did not show similar affinity at physiological temperatures, and anti-CD16α aptamers showed their highest affinity at 25°C but not at 4°C.22,23 The optimal configuration with the highest apparent affinity was achieved with three spacers, resulting in a Kd of 17 ± 5.5 nM for WB17/17.CD19.1_3S and 4.3 ± 2.4 nM for WB15/15.CD19.1_3S (Table 1; Figures S8G and S8M). In contrast, the use of four spacers led to a slight decrease in affinity (Table 1; Figures S8D/S8H). We also explored the possibility of tethering WB15.CD19.1 and WB17.CD19.1 aptamers to create heterodimeric aptamers. Using the same linker optimization strategy, we synthesized three variants with one, two, and three spacers (Table 1; Figures S8N–S8R). However, the apparent binding affinities of the WB15/17 dimers were lower than those of the WB17/17 or WB15/15 dimers, suggesting that the single-base difference between the aptamer sequences might lead to a less favorable fold mediated by intramolecular interactions between the aptamers, thus lowering the avidity.

Internalization kinetics of dimeric CD19 aptamers

CD19 is a rapidly internalizing antigen.24 Given that dimeric CD19 aptamers could serve as highly effective synthetic drug delivery agents for the treatment of CD19-positive human lymphoma, we next evaluated whether the aptamers internalize into CD19-positive B cells. To explore aptamer internalization, Xiao et al. used partial digestion of the extracellular portion of the receptor protein with trypsin.25 However, the use of trypsin is limited by its potential to act as a B cell stimulator.26 Therefore, as an alternative approach to assess aptamer internalization in B cells, we used proteinase K, a cleaving reagent used to remove membrane-bound protein receptors.27 To partially digest the extracellular membrane portion of cells after incubation and then performed flow cytometry to detect internalized aptamers. This method has been successfully used in aptamer binding analysis but has not been employed to explore aptamer internalization.28 Since cell lines vary in the concentration of antigens on their surface, we first optimized proteinase K cleavage of aptamers on the cell surface. We incubated Raji or Ramos cells with aptamers or antibodies against CD19 or CD20, washed the cells, treated them twice with varying concentrations of proteinase K at 37°C for 10 min each, and quantified the fluorescence intensity related to the binding of the fluorescently labeled anti-CD19 mAb or CD19 aptamer to the partially digested Ramos cells. CD19 aptamer/antibody fluorescence intensity was significantly lower after treatment with proteinase K, demonstrating that we developed an effective platform by which to assess the internalization of dimeric anti-CD19 aptamers (Figure S9). Additionally, we verified aptamer stability under physiological conditions by incubating the aptamers for 24 h in 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) complete medium at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator and using gel electrophoresis to confirm that the aptamers remained intact and were not degraded by serum proteinases (Figure S10).

Having demonstrated an effective internalization platform and aptamer stability in cell culture media, the internalization of three aptamers, WB15/15.CD19.1_3S, WB17/17.CD19.1_3S, and WB15/17.CD19.1_3S, was assessed using an anti-CD19 mAb as a positive control and a mutated dimeric aptamer as a negative control. Here, we incubated the aptamers and controls on ice for 50 min and analyzed binding using flow cytometry. We then incubated the samples at 37°C for 0.5, 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h, treated the samples with proteinase K, and monitored the internalized signal at each time point. Interestingly, neither the anti-CD19 antibody nor any of the three tested dimeric aptamers internalized into Burkitt’s lymphoma Raji cells (percent internalization ∼<1%) (Figures 2B, 2D, 2F, 2H, S11B, S11D, S11F, and S11H). A similar pattern was observed for Toledo cells. In contrast, the anti-CD19 antibody rapidly internalized into Ramos cells within 30 min, aligning with previously observed rapid internalization of the anti-CD19 mAb (Figures 2A and S11A). All aptamer constructs, however, showed slow internalization until 6 h. We tested if the allophycocyanin (APC) label on the anti-CD19 antibody or fluorescein (FAM)/cyanine 3 (Cy3) labels on the aptamers play a role in the internalization; however, as no internalized APC signal was observed when the isotype and CY3/FAM-labeled random DNA control were used, demonstrating that the dye does not play a role in internalization kinetics (Figure S11). The lack of internalized signal observed for negative controls suggests that the internalization was driven by specific binding of the anti-CD19 antibody and the dimeric CD19 aptamers (Figures 2C, 2E, 2G, S11C, S11E, and S11G). Finally, we observed potential receptor recycling. Beyond 36 h, the percentage of internalized anti-CD19 mAb dropped by ∼50%. Dimeric anti-CD20 aptamer does not induce intracellular calcium release in DLBCL cell lines

We investigated the potential of aptamers to induce intracellular calcium release by using three DLBCL cell lines: OCI-LY7, HBL-1, and Karpas 422. With application of random DNA control, WB15/15.CD19.1_3S, WB17/17.CD19.1_3S, and WB1/1.CD20.1_3S aptamers using 1% Triton X-100 as a positive control. We monitored intracellular calcium levels using the fluorescent calcium indicator Fluo-4 AM with fluorescence intensity recorded at 535 nm following excitation at 485 nm. Across all three DLBCL cell lines, the aptamers did not induce significant changes in calcium mobilization compared to the random negative DNA control and positive control 1% Triton X-100, (Figures S14A–S14F). This finding suggests that the aptamers do not modulate calcium signaling under the conditions tested.

Discussion

Expressed in late pre-B lymphocytes, CD20 is a non-glycosylated protein that belongs to the membrane-spanning 4-domains protein family.31,32 CD20 plays a crucial role in B cell activation, differentiation, and calcium ion regulation.33 Owing to its restricted expression on B cells and absence on stem cells or plasma cells, CD20 has become a key therapeutic target in the treatment of B cell malignancies.34 Although the actual biological role of CD20 remains unclear, rituximab, an antibody developed against CD20, is one of the major therapeutic modalities in the treatment of B cell malignancies and several autoimmune diseases, with over a million doses of rituximab administered to treat B cell-related diseases.3 Thus, CD20 remains a crucial target for the development of novel therapeutic strategies, including bispecific antibodies and antibody-drug conjugates.35 The antigen CD19, also known as B4, on the other hand, is a type 1 transmembrane glycoprotein that belongs to the immunoglobulin superfamily. CD19 is specifically expressed in normal and neoplastic B cells and follicular dendritic cells.36 CD19, categorized as an essential biomarker in B cells, is expressed in pre-B cells and mature B cells37 and plays a key role in BCR signaling, as well as B cell activation, differentiation, and survival.38,39 CD19 is also widely expressed in most B cell malignancies, including acute lymphoblastic leukemia and NHL, making it an important therapeutic target.40,41,42

Given the significance of CD19 and CD20 in B cell-related diseases, several aptamers targeting these markers have been developed. For instance, Esposito et al. designed an RNA aptamer and delivered miRNA, while Mayer et al. identified DNA aptamers against CD19 using FACS-SELEX.43,44 However, a systematic biological investigation of reported aptamers against CD19 and CD20 is lacking, particularly focusing on co-receptor dependency on aptamer internalization, limiting their applications in the design of therapeutics and diagnostics. In previous studies, we demonstrated that the dimeric aptamer R1.2 against mIgM displayed specificity toward both soluble and membrane-bound IgM similar to that of anti-IgM antibodies.45 Similarly, the dimeric anti-CD3 aptamer activated Jurkat.E6 cells.46 In this study, we investigated the biochemical functionality of dimeric CD19 and CD20 aptamers.22 We found that, similar to internalization of the B4 variant of the anti-CD19 antibody, selective internalization of the dimeric anti-CD19 aptamer into B cells was negatively correlated with CD21 expression levels. In our findings, all tested dimeric anti-CD19 aptamers internalized into Ramos, OCl-LY7, and HBL-1 cells, which do not express high levels of CD21. In contrast, neither aptamers nor the B4 antibody internalized into Raji cells, which are known to express high levels of CD21. Notably, approximately 30% of DLBCL cases lack CD21 expression, while another 30% exhibit low CD21 levels. These observations suggest that the selective uptake of anti-CD19 aptamers may be useful for engineering targeted therapeutics against DLBCL. Furthermore, the lack of uptake by dimeric anti-CD20 aptamers suggesting that CD20 aptamers might be effective in the design of bispecific aptamers.

Several such aptamers are currently in clinical trials.15 Antisense oligonucleotides and small interfering RNAs are nucleic acid-based therapeutics that target specific RNA sequences and have shown potential in treating B cell lymphoma including DLBCL.47,48,49 Current treatment options for DLBCL typically include combination chemoimmunotherapy regimens such as R-CHOP (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone), with emerging strategies involving CAR T cell therapy and antibody-drug conjugates for relapsed or refractory cases.50 Similarly, aptamers offer a promising alternative as they can be engineered to selectively bind and internalize into target cells. This makes them attractive candidates for the development of nucleic acid-based therapeutics against DLBCL, particularly when both the targeting agent and the therapeutic cargo are composed of nucleic acids simplifying large-scale synthesis and purification while maintaining specificity and efficacy. In conclusion, the dimeric nucleic acid aptamers reported herein hold strong potential for development into targeted therapies for DLBCL and other B cell-related malignancies, expanding the repertoire of available therapeutic molecules and offering a cost-effective, scalable alternative to conventional biologics.

Antibody staining assay

Cells were prepared by washing three times with 3 mL of RPMI-1640 medium, followed by centrifugation at 1,000 rpm for 3 min. Antibodies were added at a final concentration of 0.03 μg per tube, and the samples were incubated on ice for 20–30 min for staining. Subsequently, the cells were washed with 3 mL RPMI-1640 medium, resuspended in 250 μL RPMI-1640, and analyzed using a Cytek NL-2000 flow cytometer. Preparation of solutions and folding conditions

After the purification of monomers (WB1.CD20, WB1.CD20.1, WB1.CD20.2, WB1.CD20.3, WB15.CD19, WB15.CD19.1, WB15.CD19.2, WB17.CD19, WB17.CD19.1, and WB17.CD19.2) and dimers (WB1/1.CD20.1_1S, WB15/15.CD19.1_1S, WB15/17.CD19.1_1S, WB17/17.CD19.1_1S, WB1/1.CD20.1_2S, WB15/15.CD19.1_2S, WB15/17.CD19.1_2S, WB17/17.CD19.1_2S, WB1/1.CD20.1_3S, WB15/15.CD19.1_3S, WB15/17.CD19.1_3S, WB17/17.CD19.1_3S, WB1/1.CD20.1_4S, and WB17/17.CD19.1_4S), stock solution concentration was determined by using a UV-vis spectrophotometer (Cary 60 UV-Vis, Agilent). Then, 10 μM solutions of the monomeric and dimeric molecules were prepared by dilution of the respective stock solutions with RPMI-1640 medium (+25 mM HEPES, +L-glutamine). These 10 μM solutions were then used to prepare 2 μM, 500 nM, and 250 nM working solutions by using RPMI-1640 medium (+25 mM HEPES, +L-glutamine). Random controls were prepared in a manner similar to that of each monomeric and dimeric molecule. The folding conditions differed between CD19 and CD20 aptamers to optimize their structural integrity. For CD19-targeting monomeric and dimeric aptamer, as well as the random control, solutions were denatured at 95°C for 10 min and then incubated at 25°C in the dark (under aluminum foil) for 45 min to allow proper folding. In contrast, CD20-targeting aptamers were denatured at 95°C for 10 min and immediately snap-cooled on ice (4°), followed by a 45 min folding period on ice. There folding protocols were strictly followed because aptamer binding diminishes if the folded aptamer is kept on ice any longer.

Determination of apparent binding affinity at 4°C

Aptamer dilutions and folding were performed as described in the previous section. Apparent binding affinities of monomers and dimers toward target cells were determined by using Raji (1.5 × 105) cells. Both CD20 and CD19 fluorescently-labeled aptamer concentrations were used (500 nM–0.5 nM), and cells were incubated in 50 μL of CSB and 50 μL of aptamer solutions in RPMI-1640 for 1 h on ice. After 1 h, 3 mL RPMI-1640 medium were added for one wash, and the cells were reconstituted in 250 μL RPMI-1640 medium. Aptamer binding toward targets cells was analyzed with flow cytometry for each concentration by recording 5,000 events. Kd values were obtained by plotting the aptamer’s apparent fraction of bound aptamers, which was calculated as specific mean fluorescence = aptamer mean fluorescence − random DNA mean fluorescence, against the concentration of aptamers with GraphPad Prism software using a nonlinear regression (curve fit) and one site-specific binding.

Determination of apparent binding affinity at 37°C

The apparent affinity of target-specific aptamers against Raji cells was determined using a range of aptamer concentrations from 250 to 0.25 nM. Both CD19 and CD20 aptamers were prepared as described in the previous section. Before the aptamer binding assays, cells were washed three times with 3 mL RMPI-1640 medium. Each concentration of aptamers was incubated with 1.5 × 105 Raji cells at 37°C for 1 h in a final volume of 100 μL. Cells were then washed once using 3 mL RPMI-1640 medium, and binding events were analyzed by flow cytometry. Kd values were obtained by plotting the aptamer’s specific mean fluorescence against nM concentration of aptamers with GraphPad Prism software, using a nonlinear regression (curve fit), one site-specific binding.

Specificity assays

Specificity assays were conducted with individual aptamers against HPLC-purified random DNA (monomeric and dimeric) control purchased from IDT DNA Technologies. The specificity of aptamer sequences was evaluated by incubating parents (full length), truncated analogues, and dimeric analogues separately with six different cell lines accompanying the B cell lines Toledo, Raji, BJAB, Ramos, SKLY-16, OCl-Ly7, HBL-1, and Karpas 422, while the negative cell lines were Jurkat.E6 and CA46 knockdown. These assays were performed by incubating 50 μL of either monomeric or dimeric aptamer (500 or 250 nM) or random control with 2.0 × 105 cells in 50 μL of CSB at 37°C for 45 min, followed by washing once with 3 mL RPMI-1640 medium. Cells were reconstituted in 250 μL RPMI-1640. Finally, binding was analyzed by flow cytometry by counting 10,000 events. Using FlowJo software (version 10.10.0), we plotted the mean fluorescence intensity in GraphPad Prism software as a measure of site-specific binding. Three independent experiments, each performed in triplicate, against each cell line were performed.

Fluorescence microscopy

Toledo cells were washed three times by centrifugation at 1,000 rpm for 3 min with 3 mL of RPMI-1640 medium between each wash. The cells were then counted, and 2 × 105 cells were resuspended in 100 μL CSB. For binding studies, 2 × 105 cells in 100 μL were incubated with 2 μM of either Cy3-labeled WB1.CD20.1, WB17.CD19.1 aptamer, or random DNA control in 100 μL to achieve a final aptamer concentration of 1 μM. Incubations were carried out on ice for 45 min. Following this, 0.125 μg of APC-labeled anti-CD20 antibody was added to the tubes incubated with WB1.CD20.1 aptamer or random DNA control. Similarly, 0.125 μg of APC-labeled anti-CD19 antibody was added to the tubes incubated with WB17.CD19.1 aptamer or random DNA control. For controls, 0.125 μg of APC-labeled isotype control antibody was added to tubes incubated with either WB1.CD20.1 or WB17.CD19.1 aptamer. After antibody incubation, binding was disrupted by adding 2 mL of RPMI-1640 medium, followed by centrifugation. The supernatant was decanted, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 500 μL of 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, catalog no. SH30256.01). The resuspended cells were mixed thoroughly and gently transferred to the center of a glass coverslip in a 35 mm × 10 mm style cell culture dish (catalog no. 430165), ensuring that the cell suspension covered only the glass coverslip by surface tension. Cells were allowed to adhere by gravity sedimentation at room temperature for 30 min. Following adhesion, cells were fixed by carefully adding 500 μL of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) prepared in 1× PBS to the dish, breaking the surface tension and resulting in a final PFA concentration of 2%. Fixation was allowed to proceed for 10 min at room temperature. After fixation, the fixative was aspirated, and the cells were washed with 1.5 mL of 1× PBS to remove any non-adherent cells. After washing, 1 mL of fresh 1× PBS was added to the sides of the culture dish to maintain hydration. Coverslips with adherent cells were gently lifted from the dish using sharp-tipped tweezers. Excess liquid from the backside of the coverslip was removed with a Kimwipe, and residual liquid on the cell side was gently absorbed by touching the corners of the coverslip with a Kimwipe. Coverslips were mounted cell-side down onto microscope slides pretreated with one drop of Fluoromount-G mounting medium. Slides were allowed to dry for 15 min in a light-protected area before imaging with a Nikon TiE inverted microscope (Nikon Inc., Melville, NY). Images were acquired using a Neo sCMOS camera (6.45 μm pixels, 560 MHz, Andor Technology) and a 40× dry objective. This protocol ensured high-quality imaging of Cy3-labeled aptamer and APC-labeled antibody colocalization in Toledo cells.

Proteinase K treatment

Following the binding or internalization assay, Raji, Toledo, Ramos, OCl-Ly7, and HBL-1 cells (2.0 × 105 cells per sample) were washed with 2 mL of 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, calcium- and magnesium-free; catalog no. SH30256.01, HyClone) to remove unbound material. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with 100 μL of proteinase K solution prepared in PBS. Raji, Toledo, and Ramos cells were treated with 1 mg/mL proteinase K (catalog no. EO0491, Thermo Fisher Scientific), while OCl-Ly7 and HBL-1 cells were treated with 3 mg/mL proteinase K. Incubation was performed at 37°C for 10 min. After the initial incubation, Raji, Toledo, and Ramos cells were washed with 1 mL PBS at room temperature and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 3 min; then, the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in 100 μL of 1 mg/mL proteinase K in PBS and incubated again at 37°C for 10 min. Similarly, OCl-Ly7 and HBL-1 cells were washed with 1 mL of PBS at room temperature and centrifuged at 350 rcf for 5 min; then, the supernatant was discarded. These cells were resuspended in 100 μL of 3 mg/mL proteinase K in PBS and incubated at 37°C for an additional 10 min. To terminate proteinase K activity, 100 μL of 20% FBS-containing medium was added to each sample and incubated for 1 min. The cells were then washed with 3 mL of RPMI-1640 medium. Raji, Toledo, and Ramos cells were centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 3 min, while OCl-Ly7 and HBL-1 cells were centrifuged at 350 rcf for 5 min. After the supernatant was discarded, the cell pellets were resuspended in 250 μL of RPMI-1640 medium. Samples were analyzed using flow cytometry, and data were processed using FlowJo software (version 10.10.0). Graphical representations, including bar graphs, were generated using GraphPad Prism software.

Internalization assays

The dimeric aptamer molecules WB15/15.CD19.1_3S, WB15/17.CD19.1_3S, and WB17/17.CD19.1_3S were prepared as previously described and used at a final concentration of 500 , 250 , 125 nM in a total volume of 100 μL in CSB buffer, alongside dimeric random controls prepared under identical conditions. Raji, Ramos, OCl-Ly7, and HBL-1 cell lines were washed three times with 3 mL RPMI-1640 medium, resuspended in CSB buffer, and incubated at 25°C for 15 min before the assay. For the internalization assay, 2.0 × 105 cells per sample were incubated with aptamer solutions in a final volume of 200 μL per well at 4°C or on ice for 1 h. APC-conjugated CD19 antibody (Clone SJ25C1) and APC-conjugated isotype control were added as positive controls after 20 min of incubation at a final concentration of 0.5 , 0.25 0.125 , 0.0625 , 0.031 , 0.015 , 0.003 , 0.0015 , and 0.00075 μg in 100 μL. Among these, the optimal condition was determined to be 250 nM for aptamer and 0.00015 μg for antibody. After the initial 1-h incubation, 100 μL of each sample were transferred into flow cytometry tubes and kept on ice, while the remaining wells received 100 μL of 20% FBS-containing media and were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 for time points of 0.17, 0.25, 0.67, 0.42, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h. For each sample in flow cytometry, tubes were subsequently washed with cold PBS, and proteinase K treatment was performed on half the volume to remove surface-bound aptamers, while the other half was left untreated to assess total aptamer binding. This step ensured discrimination between internalized and surface-bound aptamers. All treatments and washes were conducted as described in prior sections to maintain consistency. This protocol enabled precise and reproducible quantification of aptamer internalization dynamics under various experimental conditions.

Confocal imaging

Cellular fluorescence imaging was conducted using a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope (Zeiss, USA) equipped with a Plan-Apochromat 63×/1.40 oil immersion DIC objective (63× oil immersion objective). The excitation wavelengths and emission detection ranges were optimized for each fluorophore. APC-conjugated antibodies were imaged using a 633-nm laser line for excitation and a spectral detection range of 660–690 nm, while Cy3-labeled aptamers and random controls were imaged using a 543-nm laser line for excitation and a spectral detection range of 560–590 nm. To halt internalization, cells were placed on ice immediately following incubation and washed with 2 mL of cold PBS containing BD Hoechst 33342 solution (catalog no. 561908, BD Pharmingen) to stain nuclei, followed by a 15-min incubation on ice. After staining, cells were washed again with 3 mL of PBS without Hoechst 33342 solution. No proteinase K treatment was applied. Before imaging, cells were resuspended in 100 μL of Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS, calcium chloride-free, magnesium chloride-free, and magnesium sulfate-free; catalog no. 14175-095, Gibco) and transferred onto poly-D-lysine-coated 35-mm glass-bottom dishes (part no. P35GC-1.0-14-C, MatTek Corporation). Cells were allowed to settle on the glass surface for 3 min on ice. For colocalization experiments, aptamers and random controls were prepared as described previously, and mAbs were added at a final concentration of 0.03 μg. Aptamers (or random controls) and antibodies were simultaneously incubated with cells on ice for 1 h. Following incubation, cells were washed with 2 mL of cold PBS containing BD Hoechst 33342 solution for nuclear staining and incubated for 15 min on ice. A final wash with 3 mL of PBS without Hoechst 33342 solution was performed before imaging. Cells were then resuspended in 100 μL of HBSS and transferred onto poly-D-lysine-coated glass-bottom dishes, allowing them to settle for 3 min on ice. Images were acquired using Zeiss ZEN 2 software, and post-acquisition analyses, including colocalization assessments, were conducted using ImageJ software.

Gel electrophoresis

A 3.5% agarose gel was prepared by combining 1.75 g of agarose (Fisher Bioreagents, Agarose Genetic Analysis Grade, catalog no. BP1356-100) with 50 mL of 1× Tris-borate-EDTA (TBE) electrophoresis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. B52, 10× TBE). The mixture was heated in a microwave until the agarose was completely dissolved. The molten agarose was poured into a 7 × 7 cm tray and allowed to solidify using the Mini-Sub Cell GT Horizontal Electrophoresis System (Bio-Rad) with casting gates. Electrophoresis was performed at 120 V for 45 min. Following electrophoresis, the gel was post-stained with SYBR Gold nucleic acid gel stain (Invitrogen, catalog no. S11494) for 45 min in the dark. The gel was then rinsed with Milli-Q water to remove excess stain. Samples were prepared at a final concentration of 200 nM in PBS before loading onto the gel. Imaging was performed using a GelDoc Go Gel Imaging System (Bio-Rad) to assess purity and electrophoretic mobility of monovalent and dimeric aptamers.

Denaturing PAGE

A 7.5% denaturing PAGE gel was prepared by combining 16.25 mL of Milli-Q water, 2.5 mL of 10× TBE buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. B52), 6.25 mL of acrylamide/bisacrylamide solution (40%, 37.5:1; Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. J60868.AP), 250 μL of ammonium peroxodisulfate (APS; TCI America, catalog no. A2098), and 12.5 μL of N,N,N′,N′-tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED; molecular biology reagent, MP Biomedicals, catalog no. 194019) to a final volume of 25 mL. The mixture was used to cast two gels in the SureCast Gel Handcast Station (Invitrogen by Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. HC1000) and allowed to polymerize for 15–20 min. While the gels polymerized, a degradation buffer was prepared by mixing 50 mL of 10× TBE, 5 mL of 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate (UltraPure SDS Solution, Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 15553027), and 445 mL of Milli-Q water. Once polymerized, the gels were pre-run at 250 V for 30 min in the Invitrogen XCell SureLock Mini-Cell system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. EI0001). Following the pre-run, the wells were washed with buffer to ensure cleanliness before sample loading. Sample preparation and gel loading: samples were prepared by incubating aptamers with cells in 20% FBS media (1:1 ratio, 10% FBS media) for 24 h in wells of a 96-well plate. After incubation, the samples were transferred to 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes and denatured at 95°C for 10 min. Following denaturation, the samples were centrifuged to spin down the cells into pellets. The cell pellet was discarded, and the supernatant containing the aptamers was collected for further analysis. A 1:1 ratio of denaturing loading buffer (without dye) was added to the supernatant. For gel loading, 20 μL of the prepared supernatant sample and 10 μL of DNA ladder were loaded into the wells. The gel ran at 200 V for 20 min.

Calcium release assays

Cells were incubated with 1 μM Fluo-4 AM (F14217, Invitrogen) for 30 min at 37°C. The fluorescent indicator was then washed out with fresh, warm media, and then cells were allowed to recover for an additional 30 min at 37°C. After washing, the cells were seeded into wells of a 96-well plate at a density of 50,000 cells per well in 100 μL. Next, we added 50 μL of aptamer (WB15/15.CD19.1_3S, WB17/17.CD19.1_3S, and WB1/1.CD20.1_3S) and Random DNA oligo (negative control for the aptamers) three times their final concentration (final aptamer concentration: 250 nM). A positive control consisting of 1% Triton X-100 in assay medium was included in all experiments. Triton X-100 permeabilizes the plasma membrane, allowing Ca2+ stored intracellular organelles such as the endoplasmic reticulum to be released into the cytosol, and enabling extracellular Ca2+ influx. This control was used to generate the maximum fluorescence signal (Fmax) and to verify the functional integrity of the assay. Fluorescence emissions from all 96 wells were monitored simultaneously at an emission wavelength of 535 nm after excitation at 485 nm (F488). Fluorescence was recorded using a microplate reader (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices) with excitation at 488 nm and emission detected at 535 nm. Real-time fluorescence at 535 nm was monitored before and after the addition of the aptamer (WB15/15.CD19.1_3S, WB17/17.CD19.1_3S, and WB1/1.CD20.1_3S) and Random DNA (negative control for the aptamers). Data are presented as the mean ± SD from three independent experiments, each performed in triplicate. Statistical analysis was conducted using one-way ANOVA in GraphPad software.
