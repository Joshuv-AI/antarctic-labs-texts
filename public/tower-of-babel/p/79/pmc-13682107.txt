Large mitogenome of Gunnera prorepens reveals extensive structural expansion alongside ancestral gene retention
Authors: Zhaowu Zhang, Ruoyu Liu, Shanshan Dong, Yang Liu, Chen Xiayi, Tengbo Huang, Hui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGunnerales represent an early-diverging lineage sister to the Pentapetalae; however, mitochondrial genome evolution in this group remains uncharacterized. Characterizing the mitogenome structure and gene content in Gunnerales provides critical insights into early core eudicot evolution.ResultsWe assembled and annotated the complete mitogenome of Gunnera prorepens using PacBio HiFi sequencing. The 1,374,480 bp genome, larger than those of many basal angiosperms and early-diverging eudicots. The coding gene set remains largely conserved, including 39 protein-coding genes, 23 unique tRNA genes, and three rRNA genes. The sdh3 and sdh4, which are frequently lost in other early-diverging eudicots, are retained. Genome expansion is primarily associated with repeat accumulation, including 1,066 dispersed repeats and several large inverted repeats that may represent potential recombination sites in the mitogenome. In addition, plastid-derived sequences account for 6.33% of the genome. We predicted 380 RNA editing sites, including an editing event predicted to restore the start codon of nad1. Comparative analyses indicate extensive structural rearrangements and genome expansion while maintaining an ancestral like coding gene complement.ConclusionsThe mitogenome of G. prorepens provides additional evidence that mitochondrial genome expansion in angiosperms can occur without substantial loss of protein-coding genes. As a representative of Gunnerales, G. prorepens retains a largely ancestral mitochondrial gene complement despite its relatively large genome size. Background

Angiosperm mitochondrial genomes are characterized by extensive variation in size, structure, and sequence composition [1–4]. Compared with plastid genomes, which are generally conserved in gene order and content, plant mitogenomes frequently exhibit structural rearrangements, repeat-mediated recombination, and lineage-specific gene loss [5]. Genome sizes among angiosperms vary substantially, largely reflecting differential accumulation of non-coding sequences, ranging from the highly reduced mitogenome of Viscum scurruloideum [6] to the significantly expanded genome of Silene conica [7]. The simultaneous occurrence of extensive structural reorganization and highly conserved sequence evolution in plant mitochondrial genomes defines the classic mutation rate paradox [8–11]. This structural dynamism paired with sequence conservation implies partially independent evolutionary trajectories during angiosperm diversification.

Resolving genomic evolution within a phylogenetic framework is particularly important for understanding major radiations in angiosperms. The origin and early diversification of Pentapetalae (core eudicots) represent a major evolutionary transition [12]. Large-scale nuclear phylogenomic studies have substantially improved resolution of deep eudicot relationships, and generally recover Gunnerales as sister to Pentapetalae [13, 14]. However, relationships among early-diverging eudicot lineages, including Ranunculales, Proteales, Trochodendrales, Buxales, and Gunnerales, have not been consistently resolved with strong support across different datasets [14, 15]. Short internal branches at the base of core eudicots have been interpreted as evidence of a rapid early radiation, potentially contributing to incomplete lineage sorting and discordant phylogenetic relationships [16].

While plastid and nuclear genomes have been extensively sampled to address early eudicot relationships [17], mitochondrial genomic data for this phylogenetic grade remain relatively limited. Available mitogenomes of early-diverging eudicots include representatives of Proteales and Trochodendrales, such as Nelumbo nucifera [18] and Tetracentron sinense [19]. These studies documented variation in genome size and gene content, including independent losses of specific respiratory genes, such as sdh3 and mttB. However, a complete mitogenomic characterization of Gunnerales, the lineage immediately sister to Pentapetalae, is still lacking.

Nuclear genomic studies have revealed large-scale evolutionary transitions linked to early core eudicot diversification, including whole-genome triplication events [20]. It remains unclear whether the mitochondrial genome expansion, repeat proliferation, and gene loss observed in many core eudicots originated on the stem lineage leading to Pentapetalae, or evolved later within the crown core eudicots. Resolving this issue requires mitogenome sequences from taxa at this critical phylogenetic position.

Here, we report the complete mitochondrial genome of G. prorepens assembled from PacBio HiFi reads. By characterizing its genomic architecture, repeat composition, and RNA editing landscape, we conducted comparative analyses with representative basal and core eudicots, including Arabidopsis thaliana [21] and Ranunculus cassubicifolius [22]. By integrating these results within a phylogenetic context, we evaluate mitochondrial genome evolution at the base of core eudicots and assess the extent to which structural reorganization and coding sequence conservation are decoupled during early eudicot diversification.

Materials and methods

Plant sampling, DNA extraction and sequencing

Leaf tissue of G. prorepens was collected from the Shenzhen Fairy Lake Botanical Garden (Shenzhen, China). Identification of the species was conducted by Professor Liu Yang, and a voucher specimen (SZG00108924) was deposited in the SZG herbarium (Herbarium of Shenzhen Fairy Lake Botanical Garden). Total genomic DNA was extracted using CTAB protocol [23]. DNA integrity was assessed via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA concentration was quantified using a Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

For high fidelity long read sequencing, high quality genomic DNA was used to construct SMRTbell libraries according to the manufacturer’s instructions [24]. The resulting PacBio HiFi data were utilized for the de novo assembly of the G. prorepens mitochondrial genome.

Genome assembly and annotation

The mitochondrial genome of G. prorepens was assembled from HiFi reads using HiMT(1.1.0) [25]. The initial assembly generated a multi-branched closed graph structure (Fig. S1a), which accurately reflects the in vivo coexistence of multipartite structural isomers driven by repeat-mediated homologous recombination. However, to facilitate downstream genome annotation, comparative genomics, and standardizing the sequence for database submission, we manually untangled this branched graph into a single, continuous master circle (Fig. S1b). This untangling process was based strictly on the linkage relationships (supported by completely spanning and junction-spanning long reads) and the sequencing depths of each contig. By resolving the bifurcations at the high-depth repetitive nodes, the branches were successfully concatenated into a standard circular sequence that captures the maximum sequence capacity without redundancy. The finalized closed-loop path is perfectly resolved as follows: 139(+)—> 141(+)—> 142(-)—> 646(-)—> 139(-)—> 140(+)—> 148(-)—> 645(-)—> 139(+)—> 646(+)—> 142(+)—> 144(+)—> 143(-)—> 646(-)—> 139(-)—> 140(+)—> 649(-)—> 146(+)—> 650(+)—> 645(-)—> 139(+)—> 646(+)—> 143(+)—> 147(-)—> 146(-)—> 648(-)—> 647(-)—> 145(+)—> 647(-)—> 644(+)—> (back to 139(+)) (where + and—indicate forward and reverse complement strands). Additionally, a continuous read coverage plot was generated using bam2plot (https://zenodo.org/records/15052225) to validate the sequencing depth across the finalized master genome (Fig. S2).

Protein-coding genes (PCGs), ribosomal RNA genes (rRNAs), and transfer RNA genes (tRNAs) were annotated using PMGA [26], with reference to published mitochondrial genomes of closely related taxa. Genome structure and gene distribution were visualized using Organellar Genome DRAW (OGDRAW) [27]. Non-canonical start or stop codons and potential frameshift annotations were manually inspected and corrected based on sequence homology using Geneious [28].

Genome characteristics and repeat analysis

Genome size and GC content were calculated using custom Perl scripts. To minimize algorithmic bias and ensure the robustness of repeat identification, we employed a dual-software cross-validation strategy [29].

Simple sequence repeats (SSRs) were detected and cross-validated using MISA [30] and Pytrf (v1.4.2) [31]. Identical minimum repeat thresholds were strictly applied across both platforms: 10 for mononucleotides, 6 for dinucleotides, and 5 for tri- to hexa-nucleotides.

Tandem repeats were identified using Tandem Repeats Finder (TRF, v4.09) [32] and Pytrf. For TRF, the alignment parameters were set to 2, 5, 7, 80, 10, 50, and 2000. Pytrf was run in parallel using matched stringent parameters (motif length 1–2000 bp, identity ≥ 80%, and total length ≥ 25 bp). A high-confidence consensus set of tandem repeats was defined by intersecting the genomic coordinates from both tools.

For dispersed repeats, we utilized the suffix-tree-based algorithm Vmatch (2.3.1) [33] to identify forward and palindromic repeats, alongside a self-comparison approach using BLASTn [34] with an e-value threshold of 1e − 5. Trivial self-matches and reciprocal duplicates were strictly filtered. A consensus dispersed repeat dataset was generated by merging overlapping regions using BEDTools(v2.30.0) [35] to estimate total repeat length and genomic proportion. Finally, overlaps between the consensus tandem repeats and dispersed repeats were also evaluated using BEDTools to prevent redundancy.

Whole-genome synteny analysis

Syntenic relationships between the mitochondrial genomes of G. prorepens and representative comparative species were examined using MUMmer(v4.0.0beta2) [36]. Conserved syntenic blocks and genome rearrangements were identified using the nucmer and delta-filter modules. The resulting alignments were visualized with mummerplot. Additional synteny analyses were conducted using Mauve [37] to identify locally collinear blocks (LCBs).

Codon usage bias analysis and RNA editing

Codon usage patterns were analyzed based on all annotated mitochondrial protein-coding genes of G. prorepens. Relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values were calculated using MEGA12 [38] under the standard genetic code. RSCU was defined as the ratio of observed codon frequency to the expected frequency assuming equal usage of synonymous codons. Codons with RSCU values greater than 1.0 were considered overrepresented, whereas those with values less than 1.0 were underrepresented. Nucleotide composition at the first, second, and third codon positions (GC1, GC2, and GC3) as well as overall GC content was calculated to assess compositional bias.

To accurately predict C-to-U RNA editing sites within the mitochondrial protein-coding genes (PCGs) of G. prorepens, and to avoid the phylogenetic bias and potential false positives/negatives associated with relying on a single distant evolutionary reference, an ensemble prediction strategy was employed. We utilized two state of the art: PlantC2U [39] and MitoRED (https://gitcode.com/jll2026/MitoRED). MitoRED, an optimized model built upon the Deepred-Mt framework, incorporates RNA editing data curated from 25 diverse angiosperm species and utilizes extended context sequences. This multi species training approach significantly enhances the identification of conserved editing sites while reducing the false positive rate. PlantC2U was employed in parallel to provide a complementary and predictive framework. To ensure the stringency and reliability, the final high-confidence C-to-U RNA editing sites were defined as the intersection of the independent predictions generated by both the PlantC2U and MitoRED models.

Substitution rates and selective pressure analysis

To accurately estimate the substitution rates and evolutionary constraints across different lineages, a total of 31 shared mitochondrial protein-coding genes among G. prorepens (assembled in this study), A. thaliana (GenBank: BK010421), and R. cassubicifolius (GenBank: PP657143) were extracted.

To preserve the reading frame integrity, orthologous sequences were strictly aligned at the codon level using ParaAT [40] (v2.0) utilizing MAFFT [41] as the backend aligner. Based on the established core eudicot phylogeny, an unrooted species tree topology (R. cassubicifolius, G. prorepens, A. thaliana) was used as the structural framework. The evolutionary rates were then calculated using the codeml program within the PAML [42]. We employed the mutation-selection model (FMutSel) [43] as described by Yang and Nielsen (2008) by setting CodonFreq = 6. The free-ratio model (model = 1) was applied to independently estimate the branch-specific non-synonymous substitution rate (dN), synonymous substitution rate (dS), and their ratio (ω = dN/dS) for each lineage. Statistical significance among different branches was assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction.

Phylogenetic analysis and gene presence absence assessment

Phylogenetic relationships were reconstructed using mitochondrial genome data from 21 representative seed plant species, including gymnosperms, basal angiosperms, monocots, and eudicots. The sampled taxa comprised Ginkgo biloba [44], Cycas taitungensis [45], Amborella. trichopoda [46], Nymphaea colorata [47], Schisandra chinensis [48], Liriodendron. tulipifera [49], Cinnamomum camphora [50], Chloranthus spicatus(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/OR643818.1/), Oryza sativa [51], Zea mays [52], Polygonatum kingianum [53], R. cassubicifolius [22], N. nucifera [18], T. sinense [19], G. prorepens, V. vinifera [54], A. thaliana [55], Populus tremula [56], Gossypium raimondii [57], Solanum lycopersicum [58], and Helianthus annuus [59].

The gymnosperms G. biloba and C. taitungensis were used as outgroups to root the phylogenetic tree.

For phylogenetic inference, we implemented a rigorous data-quality control and partitioned-model approach to resolve previous phylogenetic instabilities. Initially, 41 mitochondrial protein-coding genes (PCGs) were extracted from the 21 sampled species. To ensure orthology and signal-to-noise ratio, genes were filtered based on two criteria: (1) presence in ≥ 18 of the 21 species, and (2) total tree length < 2.0 to exclude evolutionary outliers that might cause long-branch attraction (Table S8). Ultimately, a set of 26 PCGs was retained (atp1, atp4, atp6, atp8, atp9, ccmB, ccmC, ccmFC, ccmFN, cob, cox2, cox3, matR, nad2, nad3, nad4, nad4L, nad5, nad6, nad7, nad9, rpl16, rpl5, rps1, rps12, and rps4).

Amino acid sequences were standardized to a single-line format and aligned individually using MAFFT [41] (v7). Alignments were trimmed using TrimAl [60] with a gap threshold of 0.8 (-gt 0.8) to remove poorly aligned regions. For each gene, the best-fit evolutionary model was independently selected using ModelFinder [61] based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). A supermatrix was then constructed, and a maximum likelihood (ML) tree was reconstructed using a partitioned-model approach in IQ-TREE 2 [62], allowing each gene to utilize its specific optimal substitution model.

To quantitatively assess topological consistency and genealogical conflict, we calculated the Gene Concordance Factor (gCF) and Site Concordance Factor (sCF) (Table S9) in addition to 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap (UFBoot) replicates. The final phylogeny was visualized using the iTOL [63]. Patterns of gene presence and absence were mapped onto the resulting tree based on a binary matrix derived from the original annotations to illustrate mitochondrial gene content evolution across the sampled lineages.

Result

General characteristics and atypical size expansion

Initially assembled as a complex multi-branched graph, the mitochondrial genome of G. prorepens was subsequently resolved into a circular master sequence of 1,374,480 bp based on sequencing depth and read linkage information (Fig. 1) and an overall GC content of 46.04% (27.02% A, 26.94% T, 22.95% C, 23.09% G). As a member of the Gunnerales, the sister group to the core eudicots, G. prorepens exhibits an expanded mitogenome size relative to other early-diverging eudicots (Fig. S3), such as N. nucifera [18] (~ 516 kb) and T. sinense [19, 64] (~ 856 kb). While several basal angiosperm lineages are characterized by relatively small and structurally stable mitogenomes, as exemplified by L. tulipifera [49] (~ 554 kb), the 1.37 Mb genome of G. prorepens suggests a lineage specific accumulation of non-coding sequences and repetitive elements. Although this size is considerably smaller than the multi megabase genomes found in some eudicot lineages (e.g., Selenicereus. monacanthus [65] at 2.29 Mb), it represents a notable departure from the more compact ancestral state typically associated with early-diverging angiosperms (Fig. S4).

Gene content and conservation

The annotation of the G. prorepens mitogenome identified 65 unique gene species, comprising 39 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 23 unique transfer RNA (tRNA) isotypes, and 3 ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes (Fig. 1,Table S1). The PCGs repertoire is relatively comprehensive, retaining a nearly complete ancestral suite of respiratory complex genes. This includes nine NADH dehydrogenase subunits (nad1–7, nad4L, nad9), two succinate dehydrogenase subunits (sdh3, sdh4), one ubiquinol cytochrome c reductase (cob), three cytochrome c oxidase subunits (cox1–3), and five ATP synthase subunits (atp1, 4, 6, 8, 9). Several genes frequently lost or transferred to the nucleus in more derived eudicot lineages—such as those involved in cytochrome c biogenesis (ccmB, C, FC, FN), maturase (matR), and transport protein (mttB)—are preserved in this genome. G. prorepens retains 13 ribosomal protein genes (rpl2, 5, 10, 16 and rps1, 3, 4, 7, 10, 12, 13, 14, 19), representing an ancestral gene content comparable to that of A. trichopoda [46] and L. tulipifera [49].

Analysis of gene structure revealed that 9 PCGs contain introns, with a total of 21 group II introns identified. The genes nad1, nad5, and nad7 exhibit the highest structural complexity, each containing four introns. Consistent with observations in other angiosperms, nad1 and nad5 appear to require trans-splicing for maturation, as their exons are distributed across non-contiguous genomic regions [66, 67]. Analysis of the tRNA repertoire identified a total of 43 tRNA genes were identified, representing 23 unique isotypes. High redundancy was observed for certain tRNAs, trnfM-CAU (7 copies), trnD-GUC (3 copies), and trnY-GUA (3 copies), likely contributing to the overall expansion of the mitogenome.

Repeat sequence analysis

Repetitive sequences, encompassing simple sequence repeats (SSRs), tandem repeats, and dispersed repeats, are characteristic features of the G. prorepens mitogenome. A total of 80 SSR loci were identified (Table S2), with a distribution pattern dominated by mononucleotide repeats, which account for 71.25% (57/80) of the total, followed by dinucleotide (19, 23.75%) and trinucleotide repeats (4, 5.00%). No tetrameric, pentameric, or hexameric motifs were detected. In terms of base composition, a strong A/T bias was observed, with 96.5% of mononucleotide repeats consisting of A/T motifs, and dinucleotide repeats were similarly dominated by AT/TA motifs. Given the total genome size of 1.37 Mb, the density of SSRs in G. prorepens is relatively low compared to many other angiosperm lineages, yet these loci provide potential molecular markers for genetic diversity studies.

Tandem repeat analysis identified 108 arrays with consensus lengths ranging from 5 to 74 bp (Table S3). While the majority of these repeats occur in low copy numbers, certain loci exhibited moderate amplification; for instance, a 9-bp motif (ATCAATATC) and a 5-bp motif (TATAG) were present in 8.4 and 6.8 copies, respectively. These A/T-rich tandem repeats contribute to the local sequence variation within intergenic spacers and are factors in the structural complexity of the non-coding regions.

The dispersed repeat landscape of G. prorepens exhibits a high frequency of elements (Table S4), primarily categorized into Large Repeats (> 1 kb) and dispersed repeats. Three pairs of Large Inverted Repeats (LIRs) exceeding 1 kb were identified, with lengths of 6,765 bp, 1,691 bp, and 1,449 bp, respectively. These sequences may serve as potential sites for homologous recombination and genomic isomerization. Additionally, the genome contains 1,066 dispersed repeat elements, with an average length of 49.70 bp and an average copy number of 5.76. The frequent occurrence of these small-to-medium dispersed repeats constitutes a noteworthy portion of the non-coding regions. Although individual dispersed repeat elements are relatively short, their high density likely facilitates structural flux and contributes to the overall genomic expansion observed in the G. prorepens mitogenome.

Genomic synteny and structural rearrangements

Comparative collinearity analysis using Mauve alignments of G. prorepens, A. thaliana, and R. cassubicifolius demonstrates extensive structural divergence. While homologous gene clusters are identified as Locally Collinear Blocks (LCBs) across the three species, their genomic arrangement differs substantially, indicative of frequent rearrangement events such as translocations and inversions (Fig. S5). In G. prorepens, these conserved blocks appear more dispersed, separated by expanded intergenic regions. This contrasts with the relatively more compact arrangement observed in A. thaliana, consistent with the significant difference in total genome size between the two species. These observations support the view that structural evolution in the Gunnera lineage operates largely independently of coding sequence evolution; the proliferation of non-coding DNA and structural rearrangements have proceeded without compromising the integrity of the coding sequences.

PCG codon usage analysis

The codon usage bias (CUB) of the 39 protein-coding genes (PCGs) in the G. prorepens mitogenome was characterized to reflect the functional translational environment. To account for the extensive post-transcriptional modifications, all Relative Synonymous Codon Usage (RSCU) values were calculated based on mRNA sequences. In the functional transcripts, translation initiation is uniform, with AUG serving as the start codon for all 39 genes.

RSCU values (Fig. 2) for the functional codons demonstrate a systematic preference for A or U at the third codon position (GC3 = 41.7%), reflecting the overall A/T richness of the mitogenome. UUG (Leu, RSCU 1.23) is a highly preferred codon, reflecting the high demand for Leucine in mitochondrial transmembrane proteins such as cox and nad subunits. Unlike other G-ending codons (e.g., CUG, 0.68) which are avoided, the preference for UUG may be linked to translational efficiency for hydrophobic peptides. Other strongly preferred codons include CAU (His, 1.45), GCU (Ala, 1.44), and UAU (Tyr, 1.41). These results indicate that codon selection in G. prorepens is a product of both nucleotide mutation pressure and the requisite post-transcriptional editing that restores conserved protein functions.

RNA editing and functional modifications

Post-transcriptional C-to-U RNA editing was systematically mapped across the protein-coding genes of the G. prorepens mitogenome. A total of 380 editing sites were confidently identified utilizing the predictive tools MitoRED and PlantC2U, with the vast majority of sites exhibiting high probability scores approaching 1.000 (Table S5). The distribution of these editing events was highly uneven among loci (Fig. 3).

Summary of mitochondrial RNA editing in G. prorepens

Editing sites were predominantly concentrated in genes encoding subunits of the respiratory chain and cytochrome c maturation complexes. The nad gene family (Complex I) exhibited the highest editing frequencies, led by nad4 (45 sites), nad7 (27 sites), nad5 (27 sites), and nad1 (21 sites). Genes involved in cytochrome c biogenesis also harbored numerous edits, notably ccmC (22 sites), ccmFN (19 sites), and ccmB (16 sites). High editing activity was further observed in the transport-related gene mttB (22 sites) and the cytochrome oxidase subunit cox1 (20 sites). This unequal distribution aligns with the general tendency for core membrane-bound respiratory genes in angiosperms to require extensive RNA editing for transcript maturation.

Analysis of the edited nucleotide coordinates within the coding sequences revealed a strong bias toward the first and second codon positions, rendering third-position edits extremely rare. Consequently, nearly all identified edits resulted in non-synonymous substitutions. Deduced from the local sequence motifs, the most frequent amino acid conversions included Serine-to-Leucine, Proline-to-Leucine, and Serine-to-Phenylalanine. These specific substitutions typically increase the hydrophobicity of the translated residues, a biochemical shift essential for stabilizing the transmembrane helices of membrane-associated respiratory proteins [68].

Furthermore, specific editing events are required to generate functionally indispensable codons. A prominent example is a C-to-U conversion identified at position 2 of the nad1 transcript (ACG to AUG), which creates the canonical initiation codon required for successful translation. Internal editing events across multiple loci also function to restore evolutionarily conserved amino acid residues relative to orthologs in other land plants. Together, RNA editing in the G. prorepens mitogenome serves as a crucial post-transcriptional mechanism to maintain the functional and structural integrity of the oxidative phosphorylation machinery.

Extensive integration of plastid-derived sequences (MTPTs)

Sequence analysis identified 76 plastid-derived fragments (MTPTs) totaling 87,067 bp, representing approximately 6.33% of the G. prorepens mitogenome. These exogenous sequences range from 48 bp to 6,258 bp in length, with sequence identities to the plastid genome ranging from 73.90% to 100%. The distribution of these transfer events, visualized via genomic collinearity analysis, shows alignment chords connecting the plastid genome to widely dispersed loci across the mitochondrial circle (Fig. 4). This pattern suggests that MTPTs originated from multiple independent integration events or underwent extensive post-integration rearrangements, reflecting the dynamic structural evolution of this lineage.

The largest integrated fragment (6,258 bp) encompasses the trnC-GCA, petN, and rpoB genes, along with a partial sequence of rpoC1. Other substantial segments include a 4,481 bp fragment containing trnH-GUG and matK, and a 4,035 bp segment containing ndhA and ndhH (Table S6).

Annotation reveals divergent evolutionary fates for plastid-derived protein-coding sequences versus transfer RNAs. Plastid-derived protein-coding regions are predominantly fragmented, truncated, or contain frameshifts, suggesting they exist as non-functional pseudogenes. Remnants were identified for several photosynthesis-related genes (e.g., psaI, psbB, petA), which are essential for the plastid thylakoid electron transport chain but lack functional relevance in the heterotrophic mitochondrial context. Similarly, truncated sequences of the plastid NAD(P)H dehydrogenase complex (ndhC, ndhK) and RNA polymerase (rpoC1, rpoC2) were detected. The degeneration of these coding sequences is expected, as mitochondria utilize their own distinct respiratory and transcriptional machineries, leading to a relaxation of selective pressure on these exogenous fragments.

In contrast, several plastid-derived tRNA genes retain complete secondary structures and high sequence identity to their plastid progenitors, suggesting potential functional preservation. Identified candidates include trnM-CAU (elongator tRNA), trnD-GUC, trnW-CCA, trnV-GAC, trnN-GUU, and trnE-UUC. The elongator trnM-CAU is present in multiple copies with 98.4% sequence identity. In many angiosperm lineages, native mitochondrial tRNA genes are frequently lost and functionally replaced by their plastid-derived counterparts, a process known as tRNA substitution. The structural integrity of these integrated plastid tRNAs suggests they are potentially competent for recruitment into the mitochondrial translation machinery. The plastid-derived trnM-CAU likely functions during the elongation phase of protein synthesis, providing the necessary methionyl-tRNA for nascent peptide growth. This recruitment would supplement the mitochondrial tRNA pool, ensuring the stable translation of the 39 protein-coding genes.

Substitution rates and selective pressure dynamics

To accurately evaluate the evolutionary dynamics and selective constraints on the mitogenome, branch-specific non-synonymous (dN) and synonymous (dS) substitution rates, along with their ratio (ω = dN/dS) (Table S7), were calculated for 31 shared protein-coding genes. This analysis rigorously separates the underlying mutation processes from selective pressures.

Overall, the majority of the analyzed genes exhibited ω values substantially less than 1 across all three lineages (Fig. 5a), underscoring that strong purifying selection remains the predominant evolutionary force maintaining mitochondrial functional integrity. However, significant variation in substitution rates was observed among lineages. Notably, the non-synonymous substitution rate (dN) was significantly elevated in the terminal branch of A. thaliana compared to both G. prorepens and R. cassubicifolius (Fig. 5b). This accelerated amino acid substitution rate is consistent with the well-documented rapid sequence evolution characteristic of mitogenomes in the Brassicaceae family.

Despite its massive structural expansion, the G. prorepens mitogenome maintains relatively low baseline substitution rates, mirroring the conservative nature of the early-diverging eudicot R. cassubicifolius. Nevertheless, specific genes displayed elevated ω values (often > 1) on the G. prorepens branch, indicating localized episodes of relaxed purifying selection or potential adaptive evolution. These genes primarily encode components of the ATP synthase complex, cytochrome c oxidase/reductase, and transport proteins.

Accelerated evolution was prominently observed in ccmFN (ω = 1.49), a gene encoding a heme-lyase subunit essential for the covalent attachment of heme to apocytochromes. This specific shift in selective pressure within the cytochrome c maturation (CCM) pathway suggests that it may represent an evolutionary hotspot in G. prorepens. Such sequence diversification could potentially influence the efficiency of heme processing, reflecting metabolic fine-tuning or compensatory mutations required to maintain the functional integrity of the respiratory chain in the face of extensive genomic structural rearrangements.

Phylogenetic placement and gene content evolution

Phylogenetic reconstruction was performed using a partitioned-model approach based on a refined supermatrix of 26 mitochondrial PCGs, after excluding evolutionary outliers and genes with low taxon occupancy. This analysis supports Gunnerales as the sister lineage to the Pentapetalae (Fig. 6), confirming its suggested position at the base of the core eudicots. Mapping mitochondrial gene content across 21 representative seed plants reveals that G. prorepens maintains an exceptionally conservative gene repertoire. The presence/absence matrix demonstrates that G. prorepens retains 39 protein-coding genes (PCGs), missing only rps2 and rps11 (Fig. 6). This high degree of retention (approx. 95%, 39/41) is similar to the ancestral patterns observed in basal angiosperms like L. tulipifera and C. camphora, which retain nearly full gene sets.

This conservation stands in sharp contrast to the extensive gene erosion observed in more derived core eudicots. For instance, A. thaliana and H. annuus have lost numerous genes (e.g., multiple sdh, ccm, and rps genes) through functional transfer to the nucleus or outright deletion. G. prorepens retains both sdh3 and sdh4, as well as the cytochrome c maturation gene ccmFN, which are absent in Arabidopsis. The preservation of sdh3 and sdh4 is functionally critical, as these subunits serve as essential membrane anchors for Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase), bridging the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and the respiratory electron transport chain. While basal eudicots like R. cassubicifolius and N. nucifera also exhibit high conservation, they have nonetheless lost sdh3, further highlighting the ancestral genic state of G. prorepens.

G. prorepens preserves a nearly complete suite of ribosomal protein genes (13 out of 15), including several (e.g., rps1, rps10, rps13, rps14, rps19) that are frequently missing in rosid and asterid lineages. The retention of these genes suggests a high level of autonomy in the mitochondrial translation apparatus, mirroring the characteristics of early-diverging angiosperm lineages. These results indicate that despite the substantial expansion of its mitogenome to 1.37 Mb, G. prorepens has resisted the genic reduction typical of the Pentapetalae, demonstrating that genomic size proliferation and gene loss are independent evolutionary trajectories at the base of the core eudicots.

Discussion

G. prorepens is recovered as sister to the Pentapetalae in our mitochondrial phylogeny based on conserved protein-coding genes. This placement is consistent with nuclear phylogenomic analyses[13, 69, 70]and provides independent organellar support for the phylogenetic position of Gunnerales. Mitochondrial protein sequences are particularly valuable for resolving deep phylogenetic relationships owing to their low evolutionary rates and low substitution saturation [71]. Relationships among early-diverging eudicots have been difficult to resolve due to short internal branches and widespread gene tree discordance, thus additional evidence from distinct genomic compartments provide critical information for evaluating this key node.

The mitochondrial genome of G. prorepens (1.37 Mb) is larger than those reported for most early-diverging eudicots. Size variation in angiosperm mitogenomes is typically associated with differences in intergenic content rather than expansion of coding regions [5, 58]. In G. prorepens, dispersed repeats, large inverted repeats, and plastid-derived insertions together account for a substantial fraction of the genome. In contrast to A. trichopoda, where genome expansion has been linked to extensive horizontal gene transfer [46], the increase in genome size in Gunnera appears to be primarily associated with endogenous repetitive sequences and retention of non-coding DNA.

Despite extensive structural rearrangement and repeat accumulation, the set of protein-coding genes in G. prorepens remains largely conserved. The genome retains 39 protein-coding genes, including sdh3 and sdh4, as well as 13 ribosomal protein genes. Losses of sdh3 and sdh4 have been reported in N. nucifera and R. cassubicifolius, and progressive mitochondrial gene loss has been documented in several derived core eudicot lineages [5, 72]. Comparative gene presence–absence patterns therefore indicate that genome expansion in Gunnera is not accompanied by substantial reduction of coding capacity. These observations suggest that structural reorganization and gene loss are not necessarily tightly linked during mitochondrial genome evolution.

The identification of 380 predicted C-to-U RNA editing sites indicates that post-transcriptional modification remains active in this lineage. RNA editing is a common feature of plant mitochondria [1], and editing-dependent restoration of conserved residues has been described in multiple taxa [73]. Most shared protein-coding genes show ω ratios below 1.0, consistent with predominant purifying selection. This pattern accords with the generally low nucleotide substitution rates reported for plant mitochondrial genomes despite frequent structural rearrangements [7, 74]. Although a subset of genes exhibits relatively elevated ratios, particularly within the cytochrome c maturation pathway, the overall signal indicates maintenance of core respiratory functions.

The mitogenome of G. prorepens combines substantial intergenic expansion and structural rearrangement with retention of a largely conserved coding gene set. Given its phylogenetic position near the divergence of Pentapetalae, this genome provides an additional comparative reference for understanding the diversity of mitochondrial genome organization among early eudicots. The results indicate that repeat-associated expansion can occur without concurrent extensive gene loss, contributing to heterogeneity in mitochondrial genome architecture across angiosperms.

Conclusion

The expanded 1.37 Mb mitochondrial genome of G. prorepens, assembled here via long-read sequencing, provides a critical data point for the sister lineage to Pentapetalae. The coexistence of massive repeat-driven expansion and a highly conserved protein coding repertoire highlights that structural reorganization and functional gene erosion are uncoupled at the base of the core eudicots. These genomic resources will facilitate future investigations into the mechanisms of organellar evolution during major angiosperm radiations.
