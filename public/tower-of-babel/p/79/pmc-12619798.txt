Routine interventions failed to provide survival benefits for early-stage major salivary gland marginal zone lymphoma in a population-based analysis
Authors: Jiaqi Qin, Hailing Liu, Xiaojing Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Major salivary gland marginal zone lymphoma (MSGMZL) is a rare malignant tumor, predominantly presenting with early-stage (I/II) lesions. Given the scarcity of evidence for treatment efficacy, a comprehensive analysis of the long-term outcomes of various therapeutic approaches in early-stage MSGMZL is warranted. A population-based cohort study was conducted to include patients diagnosed with MSGMZL from 1992 to 2016, using data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression, propensity score matching (PSM), and stratified analyses were employed. A total of 1,091 early-stage MSGMZL cases were identified, with a median follow-up of 81.0 months. Key prognostic factors for overall survival included age over 60 years at diagnosis (hazard ratio [HR], 5.99; 95% confidence interval [CI], 4.36–8.24), diagnosis before 2005 (HR, 1.46; 95% CI, 1.10–1.95), and the presence of coexisting malignancy (HR, 1.61; 95% CI, 1.27–2.04). Disease-specific survival was adversely affected by age over 60 years at diagnosis (HR, 4.25; 95% CI, 2.34–7.75) and by the year of diagnosis before 2005 (HR, 1.98; 95% CI, 1.06–3.67). PSM revealed no differences in survival outcomes among conventional interventions. Subgroup analyses indicated that patients diagnosed after 2005 exhibited improved survival curves, suggesting the potential impact of novel therapeutic strategies. Our retrospective analysis indicates that conventional treatments do not confer a survival advantage for patients with early-stage MSGMZL. These findings underscore the urgent need for innovative therapeutic approaches and highlight the direction for future research aimed at improving patient outcomes.

Introduction

Marginal-zone B-cell lymphoma (MZL) comprises a heterogeneous group of malignant tumors that can occur in extra-nodal organs, such as the gastrointestinal tract, ocular adnexa, skin, soft tissue, salivary gland, thyroid gland, lung, bladder, and others [1–3]. The major salivary gland (MSG) is an uncommon primary site for MZL, with the most frequent location being the parotid glands, followed by the submandibular glands and sublingual glands [4]. The etiology and pathogenesis of major salivary gland marginal zone lymphoma (MSGMZL) remain elusive to date. Environment, autoimmune disorders and infectious agents may have a role in the development of extra-nodal MZL (EMZL). A close association between MSGMZL and Sjögren’s Syndrome (SS) has repeatedly been published [5–8]. The risk of developing lymphoma especially EMZL, prevalently affecting the major salivary glands, is as high as 44-fold for individuals with SS compared with that for healthy control populations [8]. The clinical manifestations of MSGMZL are typically nonspecific, often presenting as localized, painless lumps (stage I-II), with a small tumor burden and a favorable Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status [9–11]. Invasive biopsy and pathological analyses are pivotal for making a diagnosis. Data in the literature suggest surgery (Sx), antibiotic therapy, radiotherapy (RT), chemotherapy (CT), immunotherapy, and various combinations of these modalities have shown efficacy in the treatment of non-gastric EMZL [7, 12–14]. For those with gastric MALT lymphoma associated with Helicobacter pylori, eradication results in lymphoma regression in 75% of cases [12]. Involved site radiotherapy has favorable outcomes in localized MZL, with a 5-year PFS and OS of 79 and 95%, respectively [15]. And those with advanced stage, where observation is not an option, chemoimmunotherapy is the standard front-line therapy [16]. However, MSGMZL is an extremely rare subtype of EMZL. There is little evidence to suggest that any one of these therapies is superior to others for patients with early-stage MSGMZL [3, 17–19]. Current treatment recommendations are mainly based on small retrospective studies and expert consensus. The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database is a valuable resource that compiles data on cancer prevalence, treatment, and survival from population-based cancer registries. It currently encompasses information on 28% of all cancer cases in the United States, offering a robust platform for analyzing a large and diverse patient population. This extensive data set is vital for a comprehensive understanding of the epidemiology and outcomes of MSGMZL. Thus, we conducted a population-based cohort study utilizing the SEER database on a total of 1,091 patients to compare long-term outcomes of conventional approaches in early-stage MSGMZL.

Patients and methods

Data collection

We obtained data from the custom SEER database [Incidence-SEER 18 Regs Custom Data (with additional treatment fields), Nov 2018 Sub (1975–2016 varying)], which collects information on cancer prevalence, treatment, and survival from population-based cancer registries and currently represents 28% of cancer cases in the United States population [20]. Permission to use these data for analysis from the SEER program of the National Cancer Institute (NCI).

We identified MSGMZL cases using International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, 3rd Edition (ICD-O-3) histology code 9699/3 for MZL and anatomical site codes C07.9, C08.0, and C08.1 for the parotid and other major salivary glands. These standardized codes ensure precise case identification, which is crucial for maintaining internal consistency and enhancing external comparability within the oncological research community. Subsequently, we refined our study population by excluding patients who were younger than 18 years old, as well as those with incomplete information or those whose lesions were classified as advanced stages III-IV. All items collected included age of onset, sex, race, primary site, Ann Arbor stage, coexisting malignancy, treatment parameters, survival months, causes of death, and year of diagnosis. The study complies with the Helsinki declaration and the ethical standards of the institutional committee on human experimentation.

Statistical methods

We used NCI SEER*Stat version 8.3.6.1, R-Studio version 4.0.2 for statistical analyses. The primary endpoints of our study were overall survival (OS) and disease-specific survival (DSS). OS was measured from the time of diagnosis to the date of death or the last follow-up. DSS was measured from the date of diagnosis to the date of death due to MSGMZL or the last follow-up. We employed the Log-rank test to assess the survival curves for trends. Univariate Cox regression analysis identified potential prognostic factors associated with survival outcomes. Variables with a P-value less than 0.1 in the univariate analysis or those of clinical significance were included in the multivariate model, a common practice to prevent the premature exclusion of important variables. The multivariate Cox regression analysis was conducted to control for confounding factors and to ascertain the independent prognostic significance of each variable. We constructed forest plots to represent the hazard ratio (HR) and the 95% confidence interval (CI) for risk variants. Propensity score matching (PSM), using a 1:1 nearest neighbor technique with a caliper width of 0.02, was applied to re-evaluate the impact of different interventions on survival, adjusting for covariates such as age at onset, sex, race, primary site, Ann Arbor stage, year of diagnosis, coexisting malignancies, and other treatment modalities. A caliper width of 0.02 was chosen to achieve an optimal balance between a high rate of successful matches and minimal data loss. Finally, we performed stratified analyses by prognostic factors to further validate our findings. All tests were two-sided and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics

We identified 1,091 eligible cases of early-stage MSGMZL recorded between 1992 and 2016 for the study. Table 1 details the demographic and clinical characteristics of the cohort. The age of diagnosis ranged from 18 to 95 years, with a median age of 63.0 years, highlighting the wide age distribution of MSGMZL. There was a marked female predominance, with a female-to-male ratio of 2.54:1, reflecting a sex disparity in disease incidence. The majority of the cohort was Caucasian (81.9%), likely mirroring the demographic composition of the study regions. Notably, 59.2% of the patients were diagnosed after 2005, suggesting potential temporal trends in diagnostic practices. Most patients exhibited stage I lesions (73.8%), while stage II was present in 26.2% of cases. MSGMZL predominantly affected the parotid glands (87.6%), with the submandibular (11.8%) and sublingual glands (5.6%) less commonly involved. The incidence of unilateral lesions was significantly higher at 96.2% compared to bilateral lesions. Survival outcomes for the entire cohort

After a median follow-up time of 81 months, neither median OS nor DSS for the entire cohort had been reached. The OS rates were 86.3% (95% CI, 80.2-90.7) at 5 years and 73.0% (95%CI, 61.7-81.4) at 10 years. The DSS rates were 96.5% (95% CI, 92.7-98.3) at 5 years and 95.3% (95%CI, 90.2-97.8) at 10 years.

Figure 1 depicts the results of univariate and multivariate analyses of potential prognostic factors for OS in early-stage MSGMZL patients. In Cox univariate analysis, clinical variables with P-values less than 0.1 were the age of onset, race, year of diagnosis, concomitant malignancy, and treatment strategy. When including the above covariates, age over 60 years at diagnosis, diagnosis before 2005, and coexisting malignancy were unfavorable independent predictors for OS in multivariate analysis. As shown in Fig. 2, age of onset and year of diagnosis were potential prognostic factors for DSS. Discussion

Herein, we present the most extensive and longest-followed retrospective case series on early-stage MSGMZL to date, encompassing a comprehensive dataset that spans a wide array of patient demographics, clinical presentations, and treatment outcomes. By analyzing a larger and more diverse patient cohort than any previous study, we have been able to identify novel trends and correlations that were previously obscured by smaller sample sizes. The patient cohort exhibits an indolent behavior, with a 10-year OS rate of 73.0% and a 10-year DSS rate of 95.3%. Only about one in four of these patients presented with stage II lesions. Parotid glands were the most common primary sites, with lesions typically being unilateral at onset. Women and middle-aged individuals were found to be more susceptible to the disease. This study provides a detailed description of the clinical characteristics of early-stage MSGMZL, highlighting both similarities and differences when compared to other subtypes of EMZL [21–23]

Our study’s findings regarding the long-term outcomes in early-stage MSGMZL patients carry significant clinical implications. Firstly, the similarity in survival rates between Sx and other routine interventions suggests that Sx may not confer a survival advantage, prompting clinicians to consider a broader range of treatment options and their individual benefits and risks for each patient. This finding is also consistent across other subtypes of EMZL [24]. While RT is recommended by international guidelines for localized EMZL [25, 26], including local relapse, our analysis indicates that it did not provide survival benefits for early-stage MSGMZL. This discrepancy may be attributed to the side effects of RT, which can negatively impact patients’ quality of life and potentially affect survival outcomes. The high incidence of xerostomia and other oral complications associated with RT in head and neck cancers, as reported in the literature, underscores the importance of considering patient-reported outcomes in treatment decision-making. These findings highlight the need for careful patient selection for RT and the potential for dose and fractionation optimization to minimize side effects while maintaining efficacy. Moreover, our study’s observation that adjuvant chemotherapy does not improve OS and DSS in early-stage MSGMZL patients aligns with the findings of the multicenter IELSG 41 trial [11]. The lack of significant differences in outcomes between patients receiving first-line antibiotic treatment and those receiving conventional treatment, as found in another domestic study, further supports the need for a personalized approach to treatment [27]. Overall, our findings underscore the importance of adopting innovative strategies for the treatment of early-stage MSGMZL, while considering the potential benefits and side effects of established therapeutic approaches.

Several studies have indicated that HP eradication can lead to the regression of extragastric MALT lymphoma. The proposed mechanism involves B-lymphocytes in the gastric microenvironment that are continuously stimulated by HP through interactions with HP-specific T cells. This chronic stimulation may drive the transformation of these B-lymphocytes into malignant B-cell clones [27–29]. These clones have the potential to migrate to extragastric mucosal sites, where they can evolve into MALT lymphoma. Importantly, conventional therapies for MZL do not target or eradicate HP infection, which may partially explain the lack of survival benefit observed in these patients. Additionally, recurrent somatic mutations in the G-protein–coupled receptor 34 (GPR34) have been identified in a subset of MALT lymphomas of the salivary gland. GPR34 mutations induce B-cell receptor signaling and CD40 receptor activation in B cells, leading to the activation of the NF-kB pathway. This activation triggers local proliferation of B cells and confers increased resistance to apoptosis, enhancing the transformation potential of these cells [30]. MZL has the potential to transform into a more aggressive histology, most commonly diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL), with an overall annual rate estimated at 1%. Florindez et al.‘s analysis of the SEER database for follicular lymphoma grades 1–2 and MZL patients revealed that while upfront treatment reduced the risk of transformation in FL, it did not have the same effect in MZL [31]. These biological insights underscore the complexity of MZL. The identification of specific molecular pathways and genetic mutations involved in treatment resistance and transformation may pave the way for the development of targeted therapies that could potentially improve outcomes for MSGMZL patients.

Anacak Y et al. [32] reported that under conventional treatment modalities, up to 35% of patients may experience relapse within 5 years. These recurrences can occur within the radiation field, on the untreated contralateral side of the paired organ, or even in distant epithelial tissues and mucosa, indicating that localized treatment alone might not meet the therapeutic demands. Rituximab monotherapy has demonstrated significant efficacy in a phase II study for both early-stage and advanced EMZL, with an overall response rate (ORR) of 73% [33]. Furthermore, Salar et al. [34] reported on the use of bendamustine plus rituximab as initial treatment for MZL in a multicenter phase II study. This regimen showed an ORR of 100%, with a complete response rate of 98% among 57 evaluable patients. Other targeted agents, such as bortezomib, venetoclax, ibrutinib, and idelalisib, have also shown promise in the treatment of EMZL and warrant further study [35–37]. Looking ahead, future research should explore the synergistic effects of drug combinations and focus on identifying predictive biomarkers for response to such therapies. There is a pressing need for more detailed clinical trials, particularly those centered on MSGMZL.

Our results indicate that patients over 60 years old at the time of diagnosis exhibit a significantly higher hazard ratio for both OS and DSS. This is consistent with the biological understanding that older age is often associated with a less robust response to treatment and a greater prevalence of comorbidities, which can complicate the course of the disease and its management. The impact of the year of diagnosis on survival outcomes is likely a reflection of the evolution in therapeutic approaches and improvements in supportive care over time. The presence of a coexisting malignancy has a notable adverse effect on survival, with a higher HR observed in our study. This is likely due to the increased burden on the patient’s system, the potential for treatment interactions, and the cumulative impact on overall health and resilience. The incidence of 25.2% for coexisting malignancies in our cohort underscores the importance of comprehensive baseline assessments and the need for integrated oncological care. Our study did not attempt to construct a prognostic model for early-stage MSGMZL due to the lack of baseline examinations and laboratory data.

The present study, while leveraging the extensive data available in the SEER registry, is subject to certain inherent limitations. A significant limitation is the absence of granular details regarding treatment protocols, including the specifics of chemotherapy regimens, radiation therapy techniques, and surgical approaches. Additionally, the database does not capture comprehensive information on comorbidities and their potential impact on treatment decisions and patient outcomes. The lack of data on lymphoma progression or recurrence also precludes the assessment of progression-free survival, an important oncological endpoint. Another limitation is the potential for selection bias within the database, which predominantly includes populations from specific geographic regions and may not be fully representative of the broader, more diverse patient populations. The homogeneity of our study cohort, with a majority of White subjects (81.9%), may limit the generalizability of our findings to other ethnically diverse populations. We acknowledge these limitations and suggest that future research could benefit from incorporating data from diverse patient populations and utilizing more detailed clinical information to enhance the external validity of the study findings. Future studies might also consider the integration of molecular profiling to better understand treatment resistance and response in early-stage MSGMZL.

Conclusion

In summary, our results represent an important validation and supplement of the clinical experience with treatment in early-stage MSGMZL patients. Conventional interventions failed to provide any survival benefit for early-stage MSGMZL patients in the study. A novel therapy might be a better alternative.
