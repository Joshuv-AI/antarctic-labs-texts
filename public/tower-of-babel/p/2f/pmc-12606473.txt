ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCardiovascular risk factor management (ie, hypertension, dyslipidaemia and diabetes) in post-coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) patients is suboptimal, with a high prevalence and low control rate due to various barriers, including a lack of self-management awareness and inadequate healthcare resources. Artificial intelligence (AI) interventions are promising for improving lifestyle management and secondary prevention; however, their effectiveness in post-CABG patients remains unclear. We aimed to describe the protocol of the artificial intelligence dialogue system-assisted comprehensive management of secondary prevention among post-coronary artery bypass graft patients (SMART) assessing the efficacy and safety of an AI-based dialogue system, namely ‘Smart Family Doctor’, on blood pressure, lipids and glucose control.Methods and analysisThe SMART study is an open-label, parallel-group, randomised controlled trial. 536 post-CABG patients with uncontrolled hypertension, diabetes or dyslipidaemia are 1:1 randomly assigned to either the health manager or the intervention group with the implementation of the ‘Smart Family Doctor’ and followed up for 6 months postoperatively. The ‘Smart Family Doctor’ runs on the WeChat service platform, which includes an AI-based dialogue system of individual intelligent questions and answers, physiological indicators monitoring, medication reminders and automated message sending. Data on physical examinations, biological samples and self-management behaviour will be collected at baseline, 3 and 6 months after randomisation. The primary outcome was the change from baseline to 6-month follow-up in cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, dyslipidaemia and diabetes) control rates. For the primary analysis, we will use generalised mixed-effects models to compare the control rate at the 6-month follow-up between the intervention and control groups, accounting for study sites as random effects.Ethics and disseminationThe study was registered at http://www.clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06615609). The central ethics committee of Fuwai Hospital approved the SMART trial (2024–2410). All collaborating sites obtained local approval or accepted central ethics approval. The findings of this study will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, presentations at national and international conferences, and potentially public engagement initiatives.Trial registration numberNCT06615609.Keywords: Artificial Intelligence, Clinical Trial, CARDIOLOGY

Introduction

Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) is a primary surgical procedure for treating coronary artery disease (CAD). Recent studies indicate that approximately 50 000 patients undergo CABG procedures annually in China, accounting for 20% of all cardiac surgeries,1 and these patients often have coexisting conditions such as hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidaemia.2 3 Substantial evidence from clinical research demonstrates that long-term control of blood pressure, lipids and glucose, along with lifestyle modifications, significantly reduces the risk of CAD-related mortality and major cardiovascular events.4,6 Therefore, effective secondary prevention through proper management of these risk factors plays a key role in the maintenance of native and graft vessel patency, delay of CAD progression and prevention of adverse cardiovascular outcomes.7 However, the control of these risk factors post-CABG remains suboptimal. Previous studies have reported that adherence to long-term secondary prevention in patients undergoing CABG is below 50%,8,10 contributing to higher rates of disease recurrence, poor clinical outcomes and substantial healthcare costs. One study conducted in China involving 2877 community-dwelling patients with self-reported ischaemic heart disease found that 90.0% had at least one of the cardiovascular risk factors, with low control rates for hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidaemia of 11.3%, 10.0% and 13.9%, respectively. Moreover, the reported use of antiplatelet agents or statins was only approximately 28.8%.11 Another study on patients with type 2 diabetes revealed that 29.8% had concurrent hypertension and dyslipidaemia, but only 5.6% achieved simultaneous control of the risk factors.12 Other studies have indicated that post-CABG patients exhibit lower adherence to secondary prevention compared with those receiving percutaneous coronary intervention or medical therapy alone.13 14 However, conventional secondary prevention procedures (eg, organising patient education sessions, making follow-up calls, prescribing long-acting medications) often require significant resources, limiting their real-world implementation. Thus, exploring effective strategies and tools to enhance patient self-management and adherence to managing these risk factors is a priority in secondary prevention.

With the rapid advancement of digital health technologies, particularly mobile applications and smartphone-based interventions, cost-effective and scalable solutions for improving adherence are becoming feasible. Several studies have shown promising results in using such tools to support self-management and medication adherence in CAD patients.1015,18 However, despite these advancements, current tools often provide simple, generic feedback and lack personalised, adaptive interventions. Recently, artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a powerful tool in the healthcare domain with potential applications in cardiovascular disease prevention and management. AI-assisted interventions have shown promise in improving lifestyle behaviours, such as diet and physical activity, as well as in diagnosing and managing conditions such as hypertension, CAD and atrial fibrillation.19,21 Interactive AI systems can facilitate patient education, address patients’ concerns and improve communication between patients and surgeons.22 Given these advantages, AI-driven procedures for post-CABG management warrant further investigation, although the safety and effectiveness of these applications in the context of secondary prevention and guidance for post-CABG patients have yet to be fully validated.

In this context, we aimed to develop and evaluate the efficacy and safety of an AI-based dialogue system, namely ‘Smart Family Doctor’, for managing post-CABG patients. This study aims to build a comprehensive knowledge system guided by clinical guidelines, develop a digital health management tool, and conduct a randomised controlled trial to assess the tool’s impact on risk factor control. The findings will provide evidence-based strategies for improving secondary prevention and long-term outcomes in patients undergoing CABG.

Methods and analysis

Patient and public involvement

Patients, participant surgeons and the public will not get involved in the development of research questions, study design or any other part of this protocol.

Objective

The primary objective of the SMART trial is to assess the efficacy and safety of the ‘Smart Family Doctor’ tool, an AI dialogue system designed for secondary prevention education, in improving risk factor control among post-CABG patients over a 6-month follow-up period.

Trial design and setting

This is an open-label, two-arm parallel-group, randomised controlled trial that assesses the efficacy and safety of an AI dialogue system which integrates a comprehensive knowledge system guided by clinical guidelines for AI-driven communication among post-CABG patients. Participants from nine tertiary hospitals in mainland China will be randomly allocated 1:1 to either the intervention or control arm after the patient screening period. Participants in the intervention group receive support from the SMART system and usual care, and those in the control group receive usual care. Enrolled participants will be required to attend face-to-face follow-ups every 3 months from the baseline visit and will be followed up for 6 months. All participants will provide written informed consent at the initial trial screening visit by local investigators (online supplemental file 1).

The study was registered at http://www.clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06615609). The central ethics committee of Fuwai Hospital approved the SMART trial (2024–2410). All collaborating sites obtained local approval or accepted central ethics approval. The project commenced in January 2025, with patient recruitment starting in September 2025. The anticipated completion date for the 6-month follow-up is September 2026.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria for participants

Participants are eligible for the trial if they were aged ≥18 years, had a history of prior CABG surgery, hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidaemia, uncontrolled risk factors, access to a smartphone, the ability to download and use mobile applications proficiently, and provided written informed consent. Uncontrolled risk factors are defined at the time of screening, such that participants must meet at least one of the following conditions: systolic blood pressure ≥130 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure ≥80 mm Hg; glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥7% and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) ≥1.8 mmol/L.

Exclusion criteria for participants

We exclude patients who are inappropriate to attend this trial; the detailed exclusion criteria included (1) history of heart failure or severe cardiac arrhythmias; (2) presence of other serious underlying conditions, such as malignancies or impaired liver or kidney function; (3) pregnant, breastfeeding or planning to conceive within the next year and (4) cognitive or communication impairments, or restricted daily mobility.

Randomisation

The SMART trial will employ a centralised stratified block randomisation method to assign participants to the control or intervention arm. Each participant will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either the control or intervention arm, stratified by site, age (≥65 years, <65 years) and sex (male, female). Within each stratum, block randomisation will be conducted using a fixed block size of 10, meaning that each block will consist of 10 consecutively enrolled participants. A fixed-block randomisation sequence will be used to ensure proper allocation. Given the nature of the study design and intervention, the study doctors and participants could not be blinded to treatment allocation. Assessments at the end of the trial will be performed by independent outcome assessors who will remain blinded throughout the study until the database is locked to minimise bias, at which point the study will be fully unblinded.

SMART intervention

Control

Participants in the control arm will receive weekly health management guidance from health professionals. Each session will last for 3 hours and will include advice on a healthy lifestyle, pharmacological therapy and recommendations for rehabilitation. Participants will be provided with a smart wristband to wear daily throughout the 6-month study period and will be required to synchronise their health data via the mobile app for the wristband.

Intervention

Patient profiling

Patient profiling

Once the patient is enrolled, the system will create a personalised health profile for each patient using electronic data collection (EDC) data, including sex, age, medical history, surgical history and key biological indicators related to cardiovascular risk factors, and continuously updated in real time based on the historical interactions.

Dialogue interactions

Dialogue interactions

When the system receives a patient query or response, it is translated into zero-shot prompts to identify the patient’s intent based on the dialogue context, patient profile and current query using the model. Query intents are classified as symptom consultation, wound care, lifestyle advice, report interpretation, medical check-ups, medication inquiries and non-medical questions. The system performs vector-based searches in the knowledge base using query intents to gather relevant information for response generation. Finally, for each patient query or response, the system uses the patient profile, retrieved knowledge, dialogue history and current query to generate contextually appropriate responses. It ensures safety by restricting the provision of responses that may pose health risks and adapting the response logic based on the type of query. Specifically, we set a safety threshold during the dialogues. Questions that exceed this threshold (such as those related to medication use, disease diagnosis or recommendations for specific doctors or hospitals) will prompt the system to advise the participants to consult our health professionals at the weekly health management session.

If the participants initiate a conversation, the ‘SMART Family Doctor’ system will provide the following content according to the query intents and the patients’ characteristics: (1) basic knowledge on diseases, risk factors and treatment; (2) specific guidance on blood pressure, lipid control, blood glucose control, medication adherence, physical activity and smoking cessation and (3) customised reminders for medication, diet, physical activity and medical appointments. Patients can upload their latest blood pressure, blood lipids and blood glucose data, as well as medication adherence, to receive feedback and guidance from the ‘SMART Family Doctor’. The ‘SMART Family Doctor’ will open access to conversation for 24 hours per day.

Knowledge feed

Knowledge feed

The system has been preloaded with nearly 500 pieces of manually reviewed knowledge or reminder notifications for post-CABG secondary prevention. The content encompasses hypertension knowledge, lifestyle advice, hyperglycaemia knowledge, medication reminders, outpatient follow-up reminders, blood pressure measurement reminders, blood glucose measurement reminders, and related videos or articles.23,27 The ‘SMART Family Doctor’ will randomly select one or two pieces and automatically push them to participants every Monday, Wednesday and Friday.

Quality feedback

Quality feedback

The ‘SMART Family Doctor’ system includes an active follow-up function. It proactively inquires about patients who have not engaged with the system for an extended period (eg, more than 1 week), with three automated re-engagement messages. A list of these patients will be provided to the research team for further attention.

Development of AI-driven dialogue system

The development of the AI-driven dialogue system was informed by the current guidelines for CAD secondary prevention and common post-CABG management issues (figure 2).623,27 A knowledge base for secondary prevention and healthy lifestyle of patients with coronary heart disease is automatically constructed by mining relevant diagnostic and treatment experiences from authoritative guidelines, medical books, expert consensus and 4000 anonymised consultation records obtained from post-CABG patient consultation platforms (eg, WeChat groups).

The procedure for knowledge base construction.

An optical character recognition combined with a natural language processing information extraction model was used to build a module for understanding patient medical record photos. A multiround question-and-answer robot model powered by the open-source Qwen large language model (Alibaba Group, Hangzhou, China) and our proprietary knowledge base was developed as the core processing module of an intelligent physician assistant system. The system will facilitate interactions to assist patients in resolving postsurgical concerns and advising them to adhere to secondary prevention and healthy lifestyles.

During predeployment testing, the AI system was evaluated against 200 clinically curated scenarios, with each scenario involving an average of 5–10 AI-to-’patient’ question-and-answer interactions (totalling 891 rounds). The pilot testing demonstrated 100% physician-adjudicated accuracy, correctly answering all 879 rounds related to post-CABG secondary prevention and routine care, while also accurately identifying and escalating all 12 prohibited questions to participating physicians for further review. Additionally, in a separate validation set, the system successfully detected and flagged all 100 predefined prohibited questions, confirming its ability to recognise and handle restricted inquiries as intended.

The system employs a multitiered safety framework beginning with strict rule-based protocols that automatically escalate all medication-related queries, diagnostic interpretations and care recommendations to clinicians through keyword detection and intent classification. All AI-patient dialogues will undergo weekly audits by a research team. High-risk interactions (eg, medication, acute symptoms) will be fully reviewed, while a random sample of lower-risk inquiries will be checked for quality. Errors will be logged, categorised by severity, and corrected through model fine-tuning or clinician follow-up as appropriate. Audit findings will be used to update the knowledge base and system rules, ensuring continuous quality improvement. Patients are also able to report potential errors directly to their clinicians. The research team gathers these reports from clinicians weekly and implements necessary updates.

Pilot study

To ensure that the intervention strategies work properly and improve users’ experience, we piloted the ‘SMART Family Doctor’ in one hospital in Beijing between January and February 2025. A total of 12 post-CABG participants were enrolled. The mean age was 60.75 (SD 6.24) years. All participants attended the ‘SMART Family Doctor’ programme and completed questionnaires during the pilot period. At the end of the pilot study, almost all participants (n=11, 91.67%) reported that the ‘SMART Family Doctor’ was helpful and easy to understand for managing diseases. 10 of the 12 (83.33%) participants reported that ‘SMART Family Doctor’ was user-friendly. In general, 91.67% of the participants were satisfied with the ‘SMART Family Doctor’ app.

In-depth interviews regarding the perceived strengths and barriers were also conducted. 11 of 12 participants commented that ‘SMART Family Doctor’ was convenient for them. One participant (male, 58 years) said that ‘‘SMART Family Doctor’ is convenient, as it enables patients to seek medical advice remotely and timely, eliminating the need for in-person consultations with doctors’. The participant also suggested that ‘The response time is long, and the medical terms used are difficult to understand. It would be helpful to reduce the use of medical terms and present the information in simpler, more straightforward language that is easier for older adults to understand.

Outcomes

The primary outcome is the change from baseline to 6-month follow-up in cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, dyslipidaemia and diabetes) control rates, which is defined as achieving all the following: (1) systolic blood pressure <130 mm Hg and diastolic blood pressure <80 mm Hg; (2) LDL-C <1.8 mmol/L and (3) HbA1 c<7%.

The secondary outcomes include the blood pressure control rate, change in lipids (total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), LDL-C, small dense LDL-C (sdLDL-C), lipoprotein(a) Lp(a)), change in glycaemic metabolism (fasting plasma glucose (FPG), haemoglobin A1c), changes in physical activity, smoking rate at 6 months, and change in patient-reported outcomes (health status, coronary disease-related quality of life (QoL), depression and cognitive function).

The exploratory outcomes include the proportions of patients with major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), such as death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, stroke and any rehospitalisation, changes in inflammatory biomarkers, vascular endothelial function indicators, renal function and anthropometric indicators. We will also conduct the cost-effectiveness analysis, with the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio expressed as the additional cost per patient achieving target control of hypertension, dyslipidaemia or diabetes.

The safety outcomes include adverse events of interest and serious adverse events that require or prolong hospitalisation, resulting in persistent or significant disability or loss of function, resulting in congenital abnormalities or birth defects, or are other important medical events as determined by the study physician (ie, events that, while not life-threatening or requiring hospitalisation, may pose a risk to the participant or require intervention to prevent one or more of the outcomes listed above). Adverse events of interest include those considered potentially related to the study intervention, symptomatic hypotension, syncope, hypoglycaemia (FPG <3.9 mmol/L), any muscle weakness, unexplained myalgia or symptoms suggestive of hepatitis (eg, loss of appetite, nausea, vomiting, jaundice, somnolence or fatigue).

Data collection

Sociodemographic information (age, sex, education, employment), medical history, family history of CAD, lifestyle behaviours (smoking status, alcohol use, physical activity), anthropometric data (height, weight, waist circumference, blood pressure, heart rate), current medications, CAD-related QoL, health-related QoL, depression, cognitive function, adverse events and local laboratory tests are collected via a customised EDC system (table 1).

Data collected during screening, baseline and follow-up visits

The interview will be conducted in a purposive sample of 20–30 participants in ‘SMART Family Doctor’ group (balanced between high/low engagers).

Blood pressure and heart rate are measured using a validated sphygmomanometer (Omron HBP 1120U, Tokyo, Japan) after a 5 min rest at each visit. The values are measured for three consecutive seated readings at 1 min intervals by a trained study assistant or physician. The real-time blood pressure data is automatically uploaded and simultaneously displayed on the EDC system.

Local laboratory tests for aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, creatine, TC, TG, HDL-C, LDL-C, haemoglobin A1c, FPG and electrolytes are collected by the study assistant at the screening, 3-month and 6-month follow-up visits. The estimated glomerular filtration rate is calculated using the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation.28 Likewise, blood and urine samples are collected by the study assistant at the randomisation, 3-month and 6-month follow-up visits using standardised sampling kits and then transported to the central laboratory for analysis of TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, sdLDL-C, Lp(a), haemoglobin A1c, FPG, C reactive protein, interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-10 (IL-10), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), intercellular adhesion molecule-1, von Willebrand factor, prostaglandins and urine markers (sodium, potassium, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, urinary sodium excretion rate).

Self-reported lifestyle behaviours of smoking, drinking and physical activity are collected at screening and 3-month and 6-month follow-up visits. Physical activity is measured using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire,29 which contains seven items to measure the duration and frequency of vigorous, moderate and light intensity activities. Self-reported adverse events are collected at randomisation and 3-month and 6-month follow-up visits. A safety committee will review these adverse events.

A series of participant-reported outcomes (PRO) are collected to better estimate the effect of the intervention on not only the objective measures of cardiovascular risk factors but also the subjective health status. The PROs include CAD-related QoL, health status, depression and cognitive function. CAD-related QoL was measured using the Seattle Angina Questionnaire (SAQ),30 which contains five domains: physical limitation, angina stability, angina frequency, treatment satisfaction and disease perception. All SAQ domain and summary scores ranged from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating a better QoL. Health status is measured using the Short Form 12-item Health Survey (SF-12v2).31 The SF-12v2 consists of physical component score, mental component score and scale scores for physical function, role physical, bodily pain, general health, vitality, social function, role emotion and mental health. Higher scores indicate better health status. Depression is measured using the nine-item Patient Health Questionnaire.32 Each item counts 0–3 points according to the frequency of symptoms, and the total score for the questionnaire is 27 points, with higher scores indicating more severe depression. Cognitive function is measured by the Mini-Cog test with scores ranging from 0 to 5 (scores ≤2 indicating cognitive impairment).33

Participant feedback is collected at 3 and 6 months using the 10-item system usability scale and optional questions focusing on potential errors, barriers to engagement and suggestions for improvement.34 Feedback will be reviewed by the Steering Committee to inform in-time refinements. Although we use a fixed intervention protocol, we permit limited real-time adjustments including urgent safety modifications (eg, correcting AI response errors) and non-core content updates (eg, improving user-friendly interactions, refining educational materials). Feedback involving other major changes or adjustments related to intervention frequency, content, scope, etc, will not be adopted in this study. However, after the study ends, we will still refer to such feedback to improve ‘SMART Family Doctor’.

On trial completion, we will conduct semistructured qualitative interviews with a purposive sample of 20–30 participants (balanced between high/low engagers) in ‘SMART Family Doctor’ group including perceived effectiveness, programme appeal, acceptability, usability and participant satisfaction.

Data management

A customised EDC system was developed by a third-party team for data collection, data verification, monitoring trial progress and quality control, and providing a platform for participants and material management. Data transmission will be encrypted, and all data will be securely stored in a database. Accessing the data system requires a unique username and password each time. The system will limit the functionalities and data access permissions based on the researcher’s role in the study. Any modification to the data will require the user to enter their username and password as an electronic signature. To ensure the validity and reliability of the data, built-in checks are embedded in the system to ensure that the records are complete and meet predefined formats and ranges. The data manager and study investigator from the study centre will monthly conduct remote or on-site monitoring of recruitment, retention, intervention delivery, document review and adverse event reporting to ensure data quality. The study analysis data will be exported by the data manager and provided to the study investigators. The data export process will ensure participant anonymity. The exported database will be stored separately on computers that are not connected to external networks to ensure that the database remains secure and prevents any data leakage. An independent Data Monitoring Committee (DMC), comprising clinical experts, statisticians and an ethicist with no competing interests, will oversee the safety of participants and the integrity of the trial data. The DMC will operate independently from the sponsor and funder and will periodically review unblinded safety and efficacy data. It will provide recommendations to the trial steering committee regarding continuation, modification or termination of the trial.

Sample size calculation

Based on a review of the literature and interviews with clinical experts,1735,37 we anticipate that the control rate of cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidaemia) will increase by 10% in the intervention group compared with the control group. Enrolling 218 participants in each group (a total of 536 participants) will yield 90% power with a type I error of 0.05, allowing for a 10% dropout rate and no less than 90% intervention adherence rate of participants during the follow-up.

Statistical analysis

The main analysis will align with the statistical analysis plan developed and approved by the study team before the final data lock and analysis stage. Data analysis will be conducted according to the intention-to-treat principles. All randomised participants will be analysed according to their original randomisation allocation, regardless of their actual adherence to the protocol.

For baseline analysis in our study, the mean (SD) of quantitative variables with normal distribution, the median (IQR) of quantitative variables with skewed distribution, or frequency of categorical variables will be calculated for the intervention and control groups, and between-group differences will be evaluated using the Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney non-parametric test for continuous variables, and the χ² test for categorical variables when appropriate. The number of missing values will be clearly indicated.

For the primary analysis, we will use generalised mixed-effects models to compare the control rate of cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidaemia) at the 6-month follow-up between the intervention and control groups, accounting for study sites as random effects. The same models will be used to analyse the categorical secondary and exploratory outcomes. Continuous outcomes will be analysed using linear mixed-effects models. Subgroup analyses were prespecified for the primary outcome. Subgroup variables collected at the screening visit included age, sex, education, LDL-C and haemoglobin A1c levels. Other subgroup variables collected at the randomisation visit included smoking and blood pressure levels. To test the significance of the interaction between subgroups and treatment groups, we will include the interaction term in the generalised mixed-effects models of the main analysis.

Secondary and exploratory outcomes will be assessed without adjustments for multiplicity. No interim analysis will be performed for this trial. Missing data will be handled using multiple imputations when applicable. All tests of significance will be two-sided, and statistical significance is defined as p<0.05. Statistical analyses will be performed using SAS software (V.9.4; SAS Institute).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, the SMART study is the first randomised trial that represents a significant advancement in the field of secondary prevention for post-CABG patients and specifically addresses the management of concurrent cardiovascular risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidaemia. Given the high prevalence of these risk factors and their adverse effects on CAD progression, the development of a scalable, AI-driven intervention holds substantial promise for improving clinical outcomes. The ‘Smart Family Doctor’ system, which combines personalised medical guidance, healthy lifestyle interventions and wearable health monitoring, provides a comprehensive strategy for overcoming the existing barriers to effective secondary prevention.

The SMART trial emphasised the composite control of hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidaemia, which represents a holistic and pragmatic strategy for secondary prevention in post-CABG patients. This composite control rate for cardiovascular risk factors reflects the multifaceted nature of risk management in post-CABG patients. Achieving control of these risk factors is critical for reducing the risk of recurrent cardiovascular events and improving long-term clinical outcomes.38 The rationale stems from the compelling evidence that inadequate management of cardiovascular risk factors significantly increases the likelihood of recurrent cardiovascular events, graft failure and poor long-term outcomes.39 40 Despite the well-documented benefits of achieving target levels for risk factors, real-world evidence highlights the concerningly low rate of comprehensive risk factor control in this population.41 By addressing the interplay between hypertension, diabetes and dyslipidaemia, the SMART trial seeks to generate evidence for a scalable, AI-driven intervention capable of bridging real-world care gaps and improving long-term cardiovascular outcomes.

Previous studies have emphasised the low adherence to secondary prevention in post-CABG patients.41 42 Factors contributing to poor adherence include limited patient education, forgetfulness, concerns about medication side effects and lack of motivation.43 44 Additionally, surgeons often face challenges in delivering consistent, personalised care due to resource constraints and patient overload.45 The SMART trial intervention was designed to address these issues by providing patients with timely, personalised suggestions and educational content, while also equipping providers with a structured framework for patient management.

The ‘Smart Family Doctor’ integrates a robust knowledge base derived from the current guidelines for secondary prevention of CAD. By leveraging AI-driven dialogue systems, the intervention offers real-time support to patients, helping them navigate post-CABG care, manage their medications and adopt healthier lifestyles. The inclusion of both general and targeted educational content ensures that patients receive information tailored to their specific conditions, such as blood pressure control, blood glucose and lipid management, physical activity and smoking or alcohol cessation. One of the innovative aspects of the SMART trial is the embedded image recognition technology within the ‘Smart Family Doctor’ platform. This technology enables patients to upload their laboratory reports directly to the platform, where an embedded image recognition system extracts the relevant laboratory information. Based on this data, the platform provides tailored, evidence-based recommendations to support patient-specific management strategies. This approach not only enhances accessibility and convenience for patients but is also fine-tuned to each person’s unique health profile.46 The similar technologies were also used in dietary tracking, exemplified by the Keenoa app, which facilitates real-time tracking and assessment of dietary intake through photos, making dietary monitoring more accessible and accurate.47

The SMART trial had several strengths. The SMART trial employed a rigorous randomised controlled design with stratified block randomisation, ensuring a balanced distribution of participants across the intervention and control groups. Stratification factors, including site, age and sex, account for potential confounding variables and enhance the internal validity of the study. The blinding of outcome assessors is another strength of the study design, as it reduces the risk of outcome measurement bias. Given the nature of the intervention, complete blinding of the participants and surgeons is not feasible; however, objective outcome measures, such as blood pressure, lipid profiles and HbA1c levels, help mitigate potential biases associated with subjective assessments. Other outcome measures, such as physical activity levels, smoking rates, QoL assessments, cognitive functions, MACEs and inflammatory biomarkers will also offer valuable insights for potential mechanisms and broader benefits of the ‘Smart Family Doctor’ intervention on clinical and patient-reported outcomes. While longer-term follow-up would provide valuable insights into sustained treatment effects, this falls beyond the scope of the current proof-of-concept study focused on establishing initial efficacy. The 6-month time frame was selected to align with the primary outcome assessment while allowing timely clinical translation. Evaluation of long-term MACE endpoints will be addressed in our subsequent longitudinal study. Furthermore, participants will be provided with smart wristbands to track vital health metrics, including physical activity and cardiovascular-related indicators. This real-time objective data is synchronised with a mobile application, allowing patients to stay informed about their health status and lifestyle.

The findings of the SMART trial have the potential to optimise the management of post-CABG patients by providing an evidence-based, scalable solution for improving adherence to secondary prevention strategies in patients with CAD. The use of AI-driven ‘Smart Family Doctor’ reduces the need for intensive clinicians and primary care provider resources, making the intervention more feasible for widespread implementation, especially in resource-constrained settings. Such digital health interventions can bridge gaps in healthcare delivery, providing consistent support to patients, regardless of their geographic location. Moreover, the study’s emphasis on patient empowerment and self-management aligns with contemporary models of personalised care.48 By fostering active patient engagement, the intervention may lead to sustained improvements in health outcomes and QoL. The integration of AI technology in post-CABG care paves the way for future innovations in personalised medicine and digital therapeutics.

This study had some limitations. This open-label design may introduce performance bias, as participants and investigators were aware of treatment allocation. However, blinding for study participants and surgeons is not feasible given the nature of the intervention, and blinding of outcome assessors helps mitigate this concern. Reliance on self-reported data for measures such as physical activity and health status may introduce recall and social desirability biases. However, we believe that such bias would be balanced between the intervention and control groups, and the metrics have been widely used in previous studies. Future research is warranted to explore the long-term sustainability of the intervention and its impact on clinical outcomes beyond the 6-month study period. Additionally, further studies could assess its applicability to diverse populations with other chronic conditions.

Conclusions

The SMART trial was a randomised controlled trial that assessed the effectiveness of the ‘Smart Family Doctor’ tool for risk factor control among post-CABG patients in China over 6 months. By employing an AI-driven personalised intervention, the trial provides a multidisciplinary approach targeting patient self-management and has the potential to significantly reduce the burden of CVD and improve patient outcomes. The findings will provide valuable evidence for the implementation of AI-driven tools in clinical practice, paving the way for more effective and scalable solutions in the field of cardiovascular care.

Ethics and dissemination

The study was registered at http://www.clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06615609). The central ethics committee of Fuwai Hospital approved the SMART trial (2024–2410). All collaborating sites obtained local approval or accepted central ethics approval. All participants provided written informed consent before enrolment. The study adheres to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national ethical regulations.

Data are collected using a secure EDC system with encrypted transmission and role-based access control to ensure participant confidentiality and data integrity. Data management follows predefined standard operating procedures, including quality checks, consistency monitoring and audit trails. Curated datasets are de-identified, standardised and stored in secure servers to enable long-term preservation and secondary analyses.

The findings of this study will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, presentations at national and international conferences, and potentially public engagement initiatives. Deidentified data may be available on reasonable request to the corresponding author, in accordance with data sharing policies.
