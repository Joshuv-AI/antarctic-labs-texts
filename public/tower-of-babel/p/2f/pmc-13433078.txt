Leveraging Coaching in Plastic Surgery: An Upgrade From See One, Do One, Teach One
Authors: Ogonna N. Nnamani Silva, Natalie B. Baxter, Ovya Ganesan, Simon G. Talbot, Kavitha L. Ranganathan, Eleanor R.G. Tomczyk, Charlene P. Chand, Lydia A. Helliwell, Dennis P. Orgill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Coaching is a concept often associated with sports. The perceived benefits of coaching in the athletics realm have led to its increased appreciation in business and medicine. Coaching drives high performance by providing personalized guidance, strategic insights, and real-time feedback. Despite its established benefits in sports and business, it remains underused in medicine, particularly in plastic surgery. Drawing on evidence from other industries and specialties, this article explores the potential of coaching to enhance technical proficiency, accelerate skill acquisition, and improve patient outcomes in plastic surgery. By integrating core coaching principles, the processes used to develop and actualize a plastic surgeon’s potential can move beyond traditional methods toward a structured, innovative approach that prepares surgeons to meet the demands of an evolving and increasingly complex healthcare landscape.

Takeaways

Question: How can structured coaching frameworks be used to accelerate skill acquisition, enhance technical proficiency, and sustain excellence in plastic surgery across all stages of practice, from trainee to senior surgeon?

Findings: We propose a framework that adapts coaching principles from performance-driven fields for surgeons at all levels to improve technical performance and shorten learning curves while fostering a culture of continuous improvement.

Meaning: Coaching offers an essential, achievable upgrade that can strengthen plastic surgery education, improve patient outcomes, and uphold the specialty’s standing in an evolving healthcare landscape.

INTRODUCTION

The greatest athletes of our generation credit coaching as the key to their success. Without Richard Williams and Bill Belichick, there would be no Serena Williams and Tom Brady, respectively. Although talent and motivation laid the foundation, expert coaching propelled these athletes to extraordinary levels (Table 1). Coaching extends beyond mere oversight, encompassing personalized guidance, strategic advice, and tailored instruction to promote development. Such support enables athletes to navigate obstacles, refine their skills, and ultimately reach their full potential. Similar to elite sports, the practice of plastic surgery demands precision, teamwork, and sound decision-making. Nevertheless, despite these similarities, coaching remains underused in plastic surgery. This article highlights how integrating coaching frameworks could transform the field of plastic surgery.

Some may argue that residency training already incorporates coaching elements, as trainees work closely with attending surgeons to learn their craft. Traditional residency training follows “see one, do one, teach one” paradigms and typically falls along a spectrum or represents a blend of apprenticeship and Halstedian models,1 where learning occurs through a mix of observation and hands-on experience with variable and inconsistent feedback.2 In the current training models, skill acquisition is often passive. Trainees observe, assist, and perform portions of cases, but progression is often driven by exposure rather than goal-directed practice with consistent feedback. Conversely, coaching models emphasize deliberate practice. Deliberate practice consists of performing highly structured tasks with specific goals, immediate feedback, and repeated refinement under expert guidance.3 This methodology ensures intentional skill development, accelerating mastery in plastic surgery. Although many surgeons attribute their training to working with a specific mentor, formal coaching would systematize this process to create consistent, high-quality skill acquisition.

Coaching is a synergistic combination of teaching and mentorship. Teaching emphasizes knowledge transfer, and mentorship focuses on career development. Coaching deliberately hones concrete skills and promotes growth through collaborative, consistent, and goal-oriented feedback. In more traditional teaching models, a senior surgeon may take over a case to demonstrate a technique, even when patient safety concerns are low. In contrast, a coach would pause, identify the specific deficit, and guide the trainee through deliberate correction before advancing.

The general surgery literature has found that coaching is beneficial for technical development and well-being.4 Given the extensive skill set required for plastic surgeons, the specialty should formally adopt coaching to enhance surgical education and professional growth.

The main objective of this article is to demonstrate the utility of coaching principles in plastic surgery. We first explore the history of coaching, tracing its origins to ancient Greece. We then examine the current role of coaching in various fields, including sports, business, medicine, and surgery. Drawing on our own experiences as former athletes and musicians, we believe that coaching can drive meaningful advancements in the field of plastic surgery. With this context, we highlight the growing need for coaching in the specialty and review barriers to its implementation. Finally, we propose a structured framework for integrating coaching at the medical student, resident, and attending levels. We hypothesize that this framework will foster improvement and elevate surgical practice.

THE HISTORY OF COACHING

In ancient Greece, initial coaching paradigms focused on the pursuit of Olympic glory, inspiring new methods to maximize physical strength and endurance.5 To find the greatest athletes, coaches recruited individuals from all corners of Greece to participate in the Olympics.5,6 As the Olympics grew, specialized preparation became essential.7 What one would retrospectively characterize as coaching evolved significantly during the Renaissance, coinciding with the revival of sports, art, and learning.8 Scholars promoted a holistic approach with trainees, reflecting the Latin ideal of mens sana in corpore sano: a sound mind in a sound body.9 Apprenticeship models flourished, with artists and musicians honing their craft under the guidance of experts.8 The word coach ultimately originated in the 15th-century Hungarian village of Kocs, known for its horse-drawn carriage production, which evolved into the English term coach. By the 1830s, students at the University of Oxford adopted the word coach as slang to describe tutors who guided them through complex studies, marking the beginning of modern coaching.10

HISTORY OF COACHING IN BUSINESS

In the late 20th century, coaching expanded beyond education and sports, becoming a valuable tool for enhancing performance within the corporate landscape. William V. Campbell, affectionately known as “Coach,” mentored influential figures, including Steve Jobs and the founders of Google, Sergey Brin and Larry Page. Drawing on his roots in Pennsylvania’s steel country and the gridiron, Campbell’s approach centered on communication, trust, and empathy. He reshaped how executives understood their roles, teaching them to focus not only on products but also on cultivating relationships within their organizations. Campbell demonstrated how effective coaching can transform innovative thinkers into global leaders.11 Today, coaching is a cornerstone of success in business. With a return on investment of executive coaching as high as 788%,12 an estimated 80% of Fortune 500 companies use executive coaches to enhance decision-making and refine leadership skills.13

HISTORY OF COACHING IN MEDICINE

Within clinical science, the earliest accessible literature on coaching appeared in a psychology study from 1952, which applied coaching principles to the care of “delinquent” populations in state institutions.14 A decade later, Hospital Topics introduced coaching in medicine, outlining the benefits of healthcare coaches in improving both the skills and morale of hospital staff. In 1999, Dr. Souba15,16 became one of the first doctors to address the use of coaching in surgery. He described coaching as an empowering and directive process that helps surgeons gain new perspectives in situations where such insights cannot be achieved independently.15,16

In recent years, the prevalence of surgical coaching in the literature has increased; however, plastic surgery remains a late adopter. Two articles have discussed the potential of coaching applications in plastic surgery,17,18 and just 1 plastic surgery study, a randomized controlled trial (RCT), has examined the benefits of coaching. The RCT demonstrated that surgeons performing cleft palate repairs who received intensive coaching (one-on-one coaching, intraoperative coaching, video review, and technical guidance) had significantly lower fistula rates compared with those who received standard feedback.19 Entrustable professional activities and Society for Improving Medical Professional Learning tools have standardized trainee feedback.20 Coaching adds what those systems cannot: active, immediate feedback that enables on-the-spot remediation. Although the plastic surgery literature has adopted learning theories and frameworks,21–24 coaching remains a separate, practice-driven discipline. Our article provides the next step, a coaching approach that integrates real-time guidance and deliberate practice, upgrading current educational tools to accelerate performance. Plastic surgery stands to benefit from coaching principles that have transformed outcomes in other performance-driven fields.

COACHING: THE SURGEON’S PATH TO MASTERY

Mastery in elite sports and surgery is achieved through experience, practice, and repetition. Usain Bolt, the fastest sprinter in history, stated: “Skill is only developed by hours and hours of work.”25 In plastic surgery, the learning curve is steep: a 2015 study found that surgeons reach peak efficiency in breast reductions after an average of 15 years.26 Although consistent practice is essential, practice alone is insufficient. For novices who “don’t know what they don’t know,” solo practice is beneficial for kinesthetic development, but it can also cement poor habits rather than build expertise. Coaching addresses this gap. Consider a swimmer perfecting their stroke: a coach might isolate an inefficiency, such as improper hand entry in the water, and design drills to correct it. Improvements do not arise from merely swimming more laps. Instead, coaching creates the conditions for deliberate practice, in which repetition is guided by targeted feedback.27 This dynamic allows the learner to reevaluate and refine technique toward reliable automaticity. This coaching style has been applied in surgical training; an RCT of residents found that those who received coaching with performance analysis and debriefing made fewer technical errors than those in conventional training.28 Across disciplines, coaches foster a practice of intentional skill development and self-awareness, enabling trainees to understand the rationale behind each action and adjustment, accelerating progress toward mastery.27,29

Coaching remains valuable beyond the formative years of training. In sports, athletes rely on coaches to enhance their skills throughout their careers. Serena Williams, for example, turned to a new coach, Patrick Mouratoglou, after a 2-year Grand Slam drought. “The tennis world is constantly changing. What is true today may be wrong tomorrow. If you don’t make progress, others will, and you will be overtaken,” Mouratoglou observed.30 With his coaching, Williams returned to dominance, securing 10 more Grand Slam titles.31 A similar urgency for improvement exists in medicine. A 2021 analysis of digit replantation outcomes revealed that surgeon proficiency was more strongly correlated with successful replantation than procedural volume. Alarmingly, more years of practice were associated with higher failure rates.32 Although this finding must be interpreted cautiously, it suggests that experience does not guarantee technical proficiency. For ongoing mastery, surgeons must continually strive to maintain their skills.

As medicine evolves, surgeons who fail to make adjustments to their clinical approach not only risk stagnation but also the loss of technical command in procedures with significant specialty overlap. Plastic surgeons face mounting pressure as other specialties encroach on areas traditionally led by plastic surgeons, a trend known as “specialty creep.”33 As anatomical domains blur and referral patterns shift,34 operations once defined by plastic surgery (ie, cleft palate repair, head and neck reconstruction, hand surgery, and facial aesthetic procedures) are increasingly performed by surgeons in other fields.33 In this climate, it is no longer enough to rely on reputation or legacy. Plastic surgeons must actively evolve to retain their expertise and leadership in such complex, high-stakes procedures. However, few surgeons engage in coaching to enrich their practice, a missed opportunity.

Coaching offers practical solutions to help surgeons confront both external competition and the pitfalls of career stagnation. From novices to experts, coaches identify blind spots and accelerate growth. Whether refining a tennis swing or improving a resident’s flap elevation, structured feedback and deliberate practice are essential to achieving and sustaining excellence. This raises the question: if world-class athletes have coaches to hold them accountable throughout their careers, why don’t plastic surgeons?

PLASTIC SURGEONS AS COACHES: CURRENT CHALLENGES

Despite evidence supporting coaching in performance-driven fields, plastic surgeons’ hesitancy to implement it positions them in the laggard cohort of adopters. This reluctance likely arises from implementation barriers; most notably, the lack of standardized definitions and measurable outcomes for evaluating coaching interventions. Although effective coaches should drive measurable improvements in objective skills, without clear metrics, they can default to imparting personal opinions and reinforcing idiosyncratic practice styles. For example, coaching that emphasizes using fewer sutures to increase speed rather than focusing on precise technique and tissue handling limits meaningful skill development. Unlike sports, where success is easily defined (running faster, scoring more, or winning), outcomes are more challenging to measure. Within plastic surgery, this complexity is intensified. Each case begins with a unique baseline, and outcomes often depend on the patient’s perspective.

Traditional feedback hierarchies within surgery pose challenges, as concerns over competence or autonomy may discourage senior surgeons from fully engaging in coaching.35 Endocrine surgeon and author Atul Gawande recounted his experience hiring a former mentor to coach him in mid-career. Although he gained invaluable insights from having an expert critique his decisions and technique, he also faced moments of intense discomfort. Reflecting on a procedure that went poorly, he wrote, “My cheeks burned; I was mortified. I wished I’d never asked him along…I had let Osteen [the coach] see my judgment fail; I’d let him see that I may not be who I want to be.”36 The emotional weight of feedback extends beyond the surgical setting. Former athletes and musicians who became physicians found that most participants became more receptive to feedback over time, learning to separate criticism of performance from their self-worth.37

Logistical challenges further complicate the implementation of surgical coaching, including the availability of high-quality coaches. In athletics and business, top performers are not always the most effective coaches, and the same may hold true in surgery. Research shows that coaching can be taught and assessed.38 However, poor coaching may have no effect or even worsen surgical outcomes. Although businesses can hire external coaches, this approach is more suitable for corporate leadership training than for technical skills requiring surgical expertise. As a solution, senior or retired surgeons could be invaluable coaching resources. Drawing on their accumulated wisdom to mentor without the demands of active operating, they could impart nuanced insights on intraoperative decisions to trainees, much like Coach Bill Bowerman’s impact on some of America’s greatest long-distance runners.39 To mitigate potentially ineffective guidance, we advocate for structured competency evaluations using tools such as the Wisconsin Surgical Coaching Rubric, which strive to increase access to coaching development, leading to more skilled cohorts of coaches.38 We encourage broader adoption of coaches via structured workshops across national conferences to foster greater participation and to create opportunities for establishing coaching certifications.

Supporting the development of current and future plastic surgeons through coaching demands a collective approach. Although surgical coaching may begin as a volunteer effort, long-term sustainability requires buy-in from key stakeholders, including division chiefs, department chairs, and deans. Institutions that recognize the value of coaching have instituted protected time and financial compensation for faculty coaches. The financial feasibility of coaching can be improved by incorporating simulations and video reviews to limit operating room disruption, with institutions allocating compensation in the form of full-time effort for coaches. Further, implementing volunteer pilot programs in various settings can demonstrate the benefits of coaching, ultimately yielding improvements that justify broader investment.40 For those in private practice, where institutional structures may be absent, virtual coaching networks can be formalized through national society platforms and operationalized to provide services akin to business executive coaching, as a way to sustain growth, counter isolation, and tackle mid-career challenges such as burnout or technique refinement.

Effective coaching begins with meeting trainees where they are and committing to methodical execution in their technical development. This approach requires recognizing how skills can be broken down to support deliberate and progressive improvement, while rejecting the “they’ll get it eventually” mindset in favor of relentless refinement. When there is genuine investment in skill transfer and trainee growth, coaching naturally improves, as repeated instruction and feedback reveal which methodologies are most effective. The magic of coaching begins with true buy-in, when the trainee is driven to improve, committed to execution, and trusts their coach enough to believe in the process. In addition to personal development, coaching skills can be advanced nationally by creating pedagogically grounded workshops at conferences, offering a structured framework for integrating effective techniques across all levels. For example, Caprice Greenberg’s contributions through the Academy for Surgical Coaching have illuminated peer-based models that enhance operative performance, offering a blueprint that we believe plastic surgery can adopt to encourage accelerated technical and leadership growth.41,42

ENHANCING SURGICAL PERFORMANCE: A FRAMEWORK FOR COACHING IN PLASTIC SURGERY

Implementing coaching within plastic surgery requires a thoughtful, multistep approach. First, we must recognize that coaching is not simply providing feedback or allowing a trainee to do their part of the case; it involves creating an environment that fosters continuous improvement. In surgery, this means integrating coaching into every aspect of training, from the junior surgeon–trainee relationship to the interactions between senior surgeons. Coaching students and residents begins with a focus on skill execution. As they progress, coaching expands to include intraoperative decision-making. CONCLUSIONS: THE TIME FOR SURGICAL COACHING IS NOW

The demand for efficient, innovative, and skilled plastic surgeons continues to grow. To meet this need, we must explore new models for surgical education and performance enhancement. As discussed, mastery of a bilateral breast reduction, a bread-and-butter procedure, can take nearly 15 years.26 In most fields, such a timeframe for proficiency would be inconceivable. We believe that coaching presents an effective solution to such challenges. Integrating coaching into surgery can accelerate learning and improve patient outcomes, while also driving financial sustainability and innovation within the specialty. The effect of coaching has a profound impact on the trainee, and as in sports, we anticipate the development of “coaching trees,” where generations of people trained under a master coach begin to evolve and develop.

Coaching trees, whether based on a shared philosophy, mentoring relationships, or specific strategies, play a critical role in the development and evolution of sports. These legacies create a flow of knowledge, success, and innovation across generations. In this view, successful coaching legacies are not only marked by wins, but also by the enduring impact of coaching principles that shape individuals, communities, and the future of the sport. Plastic surgery stands to benefit profoundly from such traditions. There is no better time to embrace the principles that have made coaching a foundation of excellence in other disciplines. The introduction of coaching into plastic surgery is not just an investment in the individual surgeon’s success; it is an investment in the field’s future. By providing surgeons with the tools, insights, and support they need to grow, we can ensure that the next generation of plastic surgeons is better prepared to meet the challenges of a rapidly evolving healthcare landscape.

Coaching can unlock new levels of performance in plastic surgery. A coach’s trained eye can identify areas for improvement to accelerate the learning curve, whether in the execution of a delicate procedure or the optimization of surgical workflow. Integrating coaching into plastic surgery has the potential to transform the field, enabling trainees to master complex procedures with greater efficiency and precision. Institutionally, junior attendings could progress more quickly toward proficiency, whereas senior attendings can embrace lifelong learning. Ultimately, formalizing coaching structures within plastic surgery will establish a new standard of surgical excellence and patient care, upholding the specialty’s role as a leader in innovation and collaboration.
