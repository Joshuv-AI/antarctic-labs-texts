Hippocampal mediation in the lipid–Alzheimer's disease pathway: A high‐dimensional mediation analysis stratified by APOE and sex
Authors: Yunjia Cao, Yuwei Fan, Qingtao Meng, Jiajia Wang, Rui Chen, for the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, the Alzheimer's Disease Metabolomics Consortium
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONSystemic lipid dysregulation is a modifiable risk factor for Alzheimer's disease (AD), yet the neuroanatomic pathways through which specific lipids influence AD progression remain poorly delineated. We investigated whether brain structure mediates the relationship between plasma lipids and AD progression, and whether this pathway is modified by apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotype and sex.METHODSThis retrospective cohort study included 1019 non‐demented participants from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI). Baseline plasma lipidomics (781 species) and subsequent T1 magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)–derived brain volumes and cortical thickness (198 features) were analyzed. Cox models identified lipids and brain regions associated with time‐to‐AD progression. High‐dimensional mediation analysis (HIMA) was employed to identify significant mediators, followed by stratified analyses by APOE ε4 carrier status and gender and sensitivity analyses adjusting for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers and comorbidities.RESULTSThirty‐five lipids were associated with AD progression. High‐dimensional mediation analysis across all 198 imaging features uniquely identified left hippocampus volume as the sole significant mediator for 14 specific lipids, including lysophosphatidylcholines and phosphoinositols. Stratified analyses revealed that this mediation pathway was predominantly driven by APOE ε4 carriers. Furthermore, gender differences were observed: female participants exhibited a more isolated indirect effect through the hippocampus for key lipids, whereas male participants often showed concurrent direct and indirect effects. Sensitivity analyses confirmed robustness after adjusting for CSF amyloid beta (Aβ)42/phosphorylated tau (p‐tau) and revealed white matter hyperintensities as an additional mediator when accounting for comorbidities, supporting a dual neurodegenerative–vascular pathway.DISCUSSIONThe left hippocampus is a critical mediator linking specific plasma lipids to AD progression, with an additional vascular pathway via white matter hyperintensities emerging after comorbidity adjustment. This pathway is strongly modified by both APOE genotype and sex, highlighting the potential for precision‐medicine interventions targeting lipid metabolism to preserve brain structure and slow disease progression, particularly in genetically susceptible individuals.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a severe public health crisis and the leading cause of dementia in the aging population, characterized by amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques and hyperphosphorylated tau tangles. 1 , 2 Since limited disease‐modifying therapies are available, identifying modifiable risk factors and intervention pathways in early AD is critical. 2 Age is the strongest risk factor, and the apolipoprotein (APOE) ε4 allele is the major genetic risk for sporadic AD, influencing lipid homeostasis, neuroinflammation, and Aβ aggregation 1 , 2 .

Mounting evidence establishes dysregulated lipid metabolism as a key player in AD pathogenesis. 1 , 3 The lipid‐rich brain relies on strict lipid homeostasis for integrity and synaptic function. 4 Large‐scale lipidomic studies reveal consistent alterations in glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, and fatty acids in AD brains and plasma, often manifesting preclinically. 1 , 3 Numerous Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI)–based studies further link peripheral lipids (ether lipids, gangliosides, sphingolipids, glycerophospholipids, acylcarnitines) to AD diagnosis, progression, and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) conversion. 1 , 3 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Lipid abnormalities can induce lipid droplet accumulation and neurodegeneration. 11 Systemic metabolic stress from modern high‐fat diets—inducing hyperlipidemia and insulin resistance—accelerates AD pathology and cognitive decline in animal models. 11 Meanwhile, specific brain atrophy patterns, particularly in medial temporal lobe structures (entorhinal cortex, hippocampus), are robust neuroimaging biomarkers and prodromal signs of AD. 2 , 12 , 13 Earliest atrophy occurs in the medial temporal lobe and spreads to parietal, frontal, and cingulate cortices with disease progression. 14 Multiple studies have linked these lipid alterations to neuroimaging phenotypes (hippocampal atrophy, white matter microstructure, brain metabolism) and to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers of amyloid and tau pathology. 2 , 12 , 15 , 16

Although lipid metabolism disorders and brain structural integrity are independently linked to AD, their interaction in disease progression remains poorly understood. 17 Large‐scale metabolomic studies have identified circulating lipids and metabolites associated with increased risk of all‐cause dementia, AD, and vascular dementia, which further show widespread correlations with neuroimaging markers of brain atrophy and white matter hyperintensities (WMHs). 17 The APOE ε4 allele, the strongest genetic risk for sporadic AD, plays a pivotal role in brain lipid homeostasis; ApoE4 impairs lipid transport and promotes cholesterol ester accumulation in astrocytes, a hallmark of AD pathology. 2 , 18 This genetic risk interacts with age and modifiable factors. 19 Genetic and sex‐specific analyses reveal that APOE genotype and sex modify lipid–AD relationships, with lipids mediating up to 30% of APOE ε2 protection and showing divergent male–female patterns. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 Mendelian randomization supports causal roles for specific lipid subspecies. 2 , 4 Although some studies suggest that the hippocampus mediates fatty acid–cognition relationships, 25 most prior research has investigated lipids and brain structure in isolation; collinearity among brain regions further confounds identification of neural pathways. Collectively, the literature establishes peripheral lipid dysregulation in AD, but few studies have formally tested whether brain structure mediates the lipid–AD pathway.

To address these limitations, our study employs a high‐dimensional mediation analysis framework to control for inter‐correlations among a wide range of neuroimaging‐derived phenotypes. This approach goes beyond simple association and rigorously tests the mediating role of brain structures in the lipid–AD pathway. By first establishing the association between lipidomics and AD progression, and then investigating brain regions as potential mediators, this study aims to delineate the specific neuroanatomic routes through which lipids exert their influence. Identifying vulnerable brain structures such as the hippocampus that mediate the effects of particular lipids could facilitate the development of targeted dietary or pharmacological strategies. The ultimate goal is to slow AD progression by protecting these key brain regions, thereby preserving cognitive function and improving quality of life for at‐risk individuals.

METHODS

Study Population and Data Source

Data used in the preparation of this article were obtained from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (or ADNI) database (adni.loni.usc.edu). The ADNI was launched in 2003 as a public–private partnership, led by principal investigator Michael W. Weiner. The primary goal of ADNI has been to test whether serial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET), other biological markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessment can be combined to measure the progression of MCI and early AD. We integrated three primary data sources: lipidomics data from The Alzheimer's Disease Metabolomics Consortium (ADMC) Lipidomics Meikle Lab Longitudinal Data Matrix, radiomics data from the Alzheimer's Disease Sequencing Project (ADSP) Phenotype Harmonization Consortium (PHC)—Composite T1 FreeSurfer Scores, and diagnostic and covariate data from the ADNI database.

The participant inclusion criteria were: (1) availability of baseline lipidomics data; (2) availability of T1 MRI radiomics data collected after the baseline lipidomics assessment; (3) availability of a subsequent clinical diagnosis recorded after the MRI scan to determine disease progression status; (4) being free of AD dementia at the baseline timepoint; and (5) complete data on key demographics and APOE genotype. To ensure data quality, participants with extreme outliers in any radiomics variable (values less than –3) were excluded.

The final cohort included 1019 eligible individuals. Disease progression was defined as any decline in clinical diagnosis, including transitions from cognitively normal (CN) to MCI, MCI to AD, or CN to AD. And participants who experienced a reversion (e.g., from MCI to CN) during follow‐up were treated as not having progressed. During follow‐up, 374 participants (36.7%) experienced disease progression, whereas 645 (63.3%) remained stable.

Lipidomics and radiomics assessment

Lipidomics profiling

The baseline lipidomic measures were derived from the ADMC Meikle Lab dataset. This targeted lipidomics platform quantified 781 lipid species from plasma samples via Liquid Chromatography‐Mass Spectrometry (LC‐MS). Internal standards were used for normalization, and the dataset was aligned across batches and ADNI phases using National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) 1950 reference plasma. 26

Radiomics feature extraction

Radiomics features were obtained from the ADSP PHC—Composite T1 FreeSurfer Scores. T1‐weighted MRI scans were processed using FreeSurfer version 6.0 with rigorous quality control. The derived volumetric and cortical thickness measures were harmonized across different scanner platforms, models, and field strengths using longitudinal ComBat to remove non‐biological technical variances.

Statistical analysis

All analysis were conducted using R statistical software (version 4.3.1). A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Baseline characteristics

Descriptive statistics for baseline demographics and clinical characteristics were generated for the entire cohort and compared between progression and non‐progression groups using tableone package. Independent samples t‐tests were used for continuous variables and chi‐square tests for categorical variables.

Data preprocessing

Both lipidomics and radiomics data were log‐transformed (with pre‐shifting for radiomics data containing negative values) and auto‐scaled to approximate normal distributions and standardize the scales for analysis.

Univariable association analysis

Univariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were fitted to identify lipidomic and radiomic features individually associated with time‐to‐disease progression, adjusting for confounders including sex, age, marital status, education years, handedness, and residence type. The false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to p‐values to account for multiple testing. The survival package was used to conduct the models.

Simple mediation analysis

For lipids and imaging variables associated with AD progression, simple mediation effect analysis was conducted using mediation package to quantify direct, indirect, and total effects. Significance was evaluated via confidence intervals (CI) estimated by the bootstrap method. This method was also used for APOE genotype and gender stratified analyses of key pathways.

High‐dimensional mediation analysis

To systematically explore the mediating effects of all radiomic features on the pathway between lipidomic species and disease progression, we employed a high‐dimensional mediation analysis method. 27 This approach addresses the challenge of numerous potential mediators relative to sample size by first using Sure Independence Screening for dimensionality reduction, followed by variable selection via Minimax Concave Penalty (MCP), and a joint significance test with Bonferroni correction to identify significant mediation pathways while controlling for false positives. Analyses were conducted using the HIMA package in R, adjusting for the confounders mentioned.

Subsequent analysis were based on Model 1 metabolites to ensure statistical power and mechanisms coverage. Two lipids—deDE(18:2) and deDE(20:4)—were excluded, as their associations with AD are driven mainly by AD‐related drugs. 28

Enrichment analysis (Table S2) showed that 35 lipids were enriched in acylcarnitine (AC), ganglioside, and lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) after FDR adjustment. Association between radiomics and AD progression

Univariable Cox models (Model 1, adjusted for confounders mentioned above) identified 146 imaging variables significantly associated with AD progression after FDR adjustment (Table S3). Increases in right inferior lateral ventricle, left inferior lateral ventricle, WMHs, left lateral ventricle, right lateral ventricle, right choroid plexus, third ventricle, CSF, left choroid plexus, right hemisphere surface holes, optic chiasm, surface holes, and left hemisphere surface holes accelerated AD progression, whereas other variables were protective.

After further adjusting for APOE genotypes (Table S4), 142 variables overlapped with Model 1 results.

Mediation analysis

Simple mediation analysis

Simple mediation analysis identified significant mediation effects for multiple brain regions across lipid exposures (Table S5). Key mediating regions included the hippocampus, amygdala, entorhinal cortex, and various temporal and parietal cortical thickness and volume measures. The proportion mediated (PropMed) ranged widely, with some regions accounting for over 30% of the total effect (e.g., ≈46% for left hippocampus under LPE(20:4) [SN1]).

Most effects were statistically significant (p < 0.05 for both average causal mediation effect [ACME] and average direct effect [ADE]), although some mediators showed non‐significant or weak effects. For certain lipid–brain region pathways, ACME was significant (ACME_p < 0.05) but ADE was not (ADE_p ≥ 0.05), suggesting that lipids influence AD progression primarily through mediating brain regions, rather than through a direct biological pathway.

Identification of left hippocampus volume as a sole mediator in the lipid–AD pathway

To address collinearity among brain regions, high‑dimensional mediation analysis was performed with 35 lipid metabolites as exposures, 198 neuroimaging variables as potential mediators, and AD progression as the outcome. Among the 198 imaging phenotypes, only left hippocampus volume emerged as a significant mediator for 14 lipid species (Table 3). These lipids showed significant indirect effects on AD progression via left hippocampus volume (both α and β paths p < 0.05, consistently negative coefficients yielding positive indirect effect estimates). Results for the LPC subclass are visualized in Figure 1. Key findings: Indirect effect estimates ranged 0.047–0.084; proportion mediated (PropMed) varied, with LPC(O‑22:1) highest at 7.1% and others between 4.4% and 6.6%. Lipids with the largest indirect effects were LPC(O‑22:1) (IDE = 0.084, PropMed = 7.1%), PI (Phosphatidylinositol) (18:0_20:3) (IDE [indirect effect] = 0.078, PropMed = 6.6%), and PI(38:5) (IDE = 0.071, PropMed = 6.0%). These 14 lipids included LPCs, ACs, and PIs, indicating that they influence AD progression primarily through associations with left hippocampal volume.

Sensitivity analysis

Stratified by APOE genotype

Stratified mediation analysis revealed that left hippocampus mediation pathways were driven predominantly by APOE ε4 carriers (APOE 1 group) (Table S6). In the APOE 1 group, lipids such as LPC(22:5), LPC(P‐20:0), LPC(18:1), and PI(38:5) showed significant ACME but non‐significant ADE, indicating their effects on AD progression are almost entirely mediated through the left hippocampus. In non‐carriers (APOE 0 group), this pattern was rare; only a few lipids (e.g., TG(52:3), GM3 (Monosialoganglioside) (d18:1/24:1)) showed significant ACME with less consistent effects. These findings highlight that the APOE genotype strongly modifies the mediating role of the left hippocampus in the lipid–AD pathway.

Stratified by gender

Mediation pathways through the left hippocampus exhibited gender differences (Table S7). In male participants, both ACME and ADE were frequently significant for the same lipid (e.g., AC(16:1), AC(20:4), GM3(d18:1/24:1)), indicating that lipids influence AD progression both directly and indirectly via the left hippocampus. In female participants, a more pronounced mediation pattern was observed for specific lipids (e.g., LPC(O‐24:1), LPC(18:1), PI(38:5)), with highly significant ACME but non‐significant ADE, suggesting that their effects are almost exclusively mediated through the left hippocampus. In addition, LPC(22:5) showed a strong significant total effect in female participants, composed of both significant direct and indirect effects. These results indicate that gender modifies the mediation mechanism, with male participants showing concurrent direct and indirect effects and female participants exhibiting stronger, more isolated indirect effects for specific lipids.

Adjustment for CSF Aβ42 and phosphorylated tau (p‐tau)

To assess whether the observed mediation pathways are independent of core AD neuropathology, we repeated the high‑dimensional mediation analysis in a subset with CSF Aβ42 and p‑tau data (n = 630, collecting before the plasma samples), including these biomarkers as additional covariates. After adjusting for Aβ42, three lipid species showed significant indirect effects on AD progression via left hippocampus volume. Among these, LPC(16:1)[sn1] was not identified in the primary analysis (Table S8), representing a novel finding under stricter adjustment for amyloid pathology. After adjusting for p‑tau, seven lipids remained significant mediators via the left hippocampus, with GM3(d18:1/20:0) emerging as a new mediator (Table S9). These results indicate that the hippocampal mediation pathway is robust to adjustment for core AD pathology, while also revealing that additional lipid mediators may be uncovered when accounting for specific aspects of AD pathology.

Adjustment for comorbidities

To adjust for potential confounding by pre‑existing metabolic conditions, we included baseline cardiovascular disease, endocrine–metabolic diseases, and mental disorders as additional covariates. After comorbidity adjustment, high‑dimensional mediation analysis identified two significant mediators: left hippocampus volume and white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) (Table 4). Two distinct patterns emerged. First, for several lipids originally mediated solely by the left hippocampus (e.g., LPC(22:5)[sn1], AC(16:1), AC(18:0)), the significant mediator shifted from left hippocampus to WMHs after adjustment, suggesting that their effects on AD progression may be partially mediated through vascular pathways previously masked by comorbidities. Second, several lipids that were not significant in the primary analysis became significant mediators via the left hippocampus after comorbidity adjustment, including GM1(monosialortetrahexosylganglioside) (d18:1/16:0), CE (cholesteryl esters) (22:5), LPC(22:5)[sn1]/LPC(22:5)[sn2], and LPC(O‐24:2), indicating that adjusting for comorbidities may unmask previously suppressed hippocampal pathways.

DISCUSSION

Our study systematically delineates a pathway from systemic lipid dysregulation to AD progression, with left hippocampal volume as a critical neural intermediary. High‐dimensional mediation analysis identified the left hippocampus as the sole significant mediator for 14 plasma lipid species, including LPCs, ACs, and PIs. Significant indirect effects indicate that these lipids influence AD progression substantially through this vulnerable brain structure. Stratified analyses revealed that this pathway is driven predominantly by APOE ε4 carriers and exhibits gender‐specific patterns: female participants show more isolated indirect effects through the hippocampus for specific lipids, whereas males often have concurrent direct and indirect effects.

Current findings on cholesterol levels in AD are inconsistent, although hypercholesterolemia is still considered an early risk factor associated with memory impairment. 2 Strong evidence for lipid involvement includes alterations in phospholipids, plasmalogens, ceramide, gangliosides, and brain sulfatases, 1 , 22 consistent with our results. Some studies have reported changes in blood phospholipid metabolism in AD, 1 , 2 , 3 and although certain PCs were associated with AD, 29 , 30 we did not identify them. Our identification of GM3 gangliosides, acylcarnitines, and phospholipids as risk factors aligns with prior cohort studies 1 ; for example, elevated plasma GM3 links to AD diagnosis and pathology, and ACs (reflecting mitochondrial dysfunction) are early cognitive impairment biomarkers. 31 The attenuation of most lipid–AD associations after adjusting for APOE ε4 status reinforces the role of APOE as a master lipid carrier and the strongest genetic risk factor for AD, profoundly influencing lipid metabolism. 32 Our study extends these findings by proposing a specific neuroanatomic pathway for lipid action.

LPCs and PIs are implicated in AD pathogenesis through various mechanisms. LPCs are bioactive lipids involved in neuroinflammation and apoptosis; elevated levels are linked to blood–brain barrier disruption and neuronal injury. 33 Our finding of negative associations between these lipids and left hippocampal volume aligns with studies showing that certain LPC species correlate with greater brain atrophy and cognitive decline. 34 Alterations in PI (a key membrane component and signaling precursor) may disrupt intracellular signaling and synaptic function, contributing to neurodegeneration. 35 Our mediation results suggest that these lipids exert pro‑AD effects partly by accelerating atrophy in the vulnerable left hippocampus.

Although numerous studies have identified brain atrophy as a mediator between genetic risk factors (e.g., APOE) and AD 36 or between cardiovascular risk and cognition, 37 few have integrated high‑dimensional lipidomics with neuroimaging within a formal mediation framework. Prior AD mediation studies typically focused on single or few candidate mediators. 25 Our approach addresses significant collinearity among brain regions, a common confounder. 38 Similar robust methods (e.g., high‐dimendional multi‐omics mediation analysis with latent confounding (HILAMA) 39 ) have identified proteins mediating effects on memory through regions like the left hippocampus. We apply this philosophy to the lipidome, confirming that high‑dimensional mediation is a powerful tool for disentangling complex AD pathways. This rigorous approach distilled the brain atrophy landscape to identify the left hippocampus as the most robust mediator for a specific lipid set, consistent with its central role in AD and vulnerability to metabolic dysregulation. 40 Our work thus adds a new, lipid‑focused dimension to the existing literature on brain mediation in AD.

Our sensitivity analyses confirm the robustness and complexity of the hippocampal mediation pathway. Adjusting for CSF Aβ42 and p‑tau did not abolish the left hippocampus's mediating role but instead revealed additional lipid mediators (LPC(16:1)[sn1] and GM3(d18:1/20:0)) not significant in the primary model, aligning with emerging evidence linking lipid metabolism to tau pathology. 13

More strikingly, adjusting for metabolic comorbidities revealed WMHs as an additional mediator, and for several lipids the mediator switched entirely from hippocampus to WMHs, indicating a vascular pathway initially masked by comorbidity‐related confounding. Independent evidence has shown that WMHs associate with complement and high‐density lipoproteins (HDLs) independent of tau and hippocampal atrophy, with specific lipid variations correlating with slower progression and reduced inflammation; that cardiometabolic conditions drive polypathology and cognitive decline; and that cardiovascular risk burden modulates plasma biomarker associations with AD and cerebrovascular neuropathology. 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 Collectively, these findings corroborate our dual‑pathway model: a neurodegenerative route via hippocampal atrophy (robust to AD pathology adjustment) and a vascular route via WMHs (unmasked after comorbidity adjustment). Both pathways are potentially targetable by lipid‑modifying interventions, with relative contributions depending on genetic background and comorbid conditions.

Another relevant study 45 explored associations between lipid metabolism and functional brain networks. Although their focus was on dynamic functional connectivity, our study provides a piece of the puzzle by focusing on brain structure. Together, these studies highlight that lipid dysregulation impacts the brain at multiple levels.

Stratified analyses revealed effect modifications by APOE genotype and sex, informing personalized prevention. Stronger, more isolated hippocampal mediation in APOE ε4 carriers suggests that this subgroup is particularly susceptible to adverse lipid effects on hippocampal integrity, likely due to the impaired lipid transport and neuronal repair of APOE ε4. 32 Targeting specific lipids (e.g., LPC(P‐20:0), PI(38:5)) in APOE ε4 carriers could represent a genetically targeted prevention strategy.

Gender differences are equally compelling. In female participants, exclusive hippocampal mediation for specific lipids suggests heightened hippocampal vulnerability to lipidomic influences, potentially due to post‑menopausal hormonal changes affecting lipid metabolism and brain bioenergetics. 46 In male participants, concurrent direct and indirect effects point to additional non‑hippocampal pathways. These findings underscore the need for gender‑tailored dietary or pharmacological interventions.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the observational design precludes definitive causal inference; unmeasured confounding remains possible despite temporal sequencing. Second, plasma lipids may not fully reflect the brain lipid environment, although peripheral lipids correlate with brain pathology. 47 Third, the primarily Caucasian, highly educated sample limits generalizability. Fourth, we lacked sensitive measures of chronic neuroinflammation; CSF white blood cell count is insensitive to low‐grade inflammation in AD. Fifth, insufficient sample size prevented integration of multimodal imaging (diffusion tensor imaging [DTI], rs‐functional MRI [fMRI], fluorodeoxyglucose–positron emission tomography [FDG‑PET]) or proteomics for stable high‑dimensional mediation analysis. Sixth, recent methodological advances such as TransHDM (Pan et al. 48 ) offer transfer learning approaches for smaller or diverse cohorts, which we did not employ. In addition, our model does not account for genetically determined selective vulnerability of brain regions (e.g., hippocampus) due to genetic programming, high metabolic activity, and lipid composition. 49 , 50 Furthermore, limited sample size prevents the incorporation of interim CSF tau data, hindering mediation assessment of tau in the lipid–brain pathway. Finally, the proportion of mediated effects, although significant, was modest, indicating that other pathways and mediators contribute.

In conclusion, our study provides evidence that a defined set of plasma lipids influences AD progression risk primarily through associations with left hippocampal volume. This pathway is modified by APOE genotype and sex, suggesting precision‑medicine approaches. Lipid levels are modifiable, pinpointing a tractable intervention pathway. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the hippocampal pathway's robustness after adjusting for CSF Aβ42/p‑tau and revealed an additional vascular mediation pathway via WMHs when accounting for comorbidities. Future research should include experimental studies to confirm causality and randomized trials testing to determine whether interventions targeting these lipids can slow hippocampal atrophy and delay AD progression, especially in high‑risk subgroups.
