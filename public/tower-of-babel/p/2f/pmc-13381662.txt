Saliva, urine, and anorectal swabs as viable alternatives to lesion swabs for molecular diagnosis of mpox
Authors: Andrew Nsawotebba, Valeria Nakintu, Jonathan Kabazzi, Rodney Kisuule, Misaki Wayengera, Alisen Ayitewala, Caroline Makoha, Samuel Jefferson Mutyaba, Edward Kiyonga, Sulaiman Ikoba, Caroline Achola, Aloysious Ssemaganda, Isaac Ssewanyana, Susan Nabadda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mpox diagnosis relies on lesion swabs, which are infeasible when lesions are absent or inaccessible, limiting case detection during outbreaks and surveillance in resource-limited settings. This cross-sectional paired diagnostic study evaluated saliva, anorectal swabs, and urine as alternatives to lesion swabs (the reference standard) at the Entebbe Isolation Unit during Uganda’s 2024–2025 clade 1b outbreak. Among 120 participants (52.5% female; median age 28), 76.7% were PCR-positive. Positivity was highest for lesion swabs (83.3%), followed by saliva (81.7%), anorectal swabs (78.3%), and urine (75.8%). Viral loads did not differ significantly across specimen types, and saliva met the predefined diagnostic equivalence criteria relative to lesion swabs. Urine showed significant directional discordance. No sex-based differences in detection were observed. Here, we show that saliva is a noninvasive alternative to lesion swabs for mpox PCR diagnosis; anorectal swabs offer high sensitivity in mucosal exposure contexts; and urine is suboptimal as a standalone specimen.

Introduction

Mpox virus (MPXV), the causative agent of mpox disease, is a zoonotic virus in the Orthopoxvirus genus of the Poxviridae family. First identified in 1958 in laboratory monkeys and later in humans in 1970, mpox has since emerged as a significant public health concern, especially in endemic regions of Central and West Africa1,2. The World Health Organization (WHO) declared mpox a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) on 23 July 2022, following the rapid global spread of clade IIb MPXV across more than 115 previously unaffected countries3. A second PHEIC was declared on 14 August 2024, driven by the emergence and cross-border spread of a novel clade Ib strain from the Democratic Republic of the Congo into Burundi, Kenya, Rwanda, and Uganda4. As of 15 March 2026, 30 countries in Africa had reported 46,476 confirmed mpox cases and 214 deaths5. In Uganda, clade Ib transmission has been sustained and widespread: as of September 2025, 8167 confirmed cases and 50 cumulative deaths had been reported, with high transmission concentrated in urban centres including Kampala, Wakiso, and Mbarara6.

Human-to-human transmission occurs primarily through close contact with infectious lesions, bodily fluids, and respiratory secretions, and through contact with contaminated fomites7,8. Cases of animal-to-human transmission have been previously reported9; however, in the current Ugandan outbreak, human-to-human transmission, including sexual contact, appears to be the dominant mode of spread, with a distribution of frequent genital and perianal lesions consistent with this route7,8. Mpox clinical presentation is variable but commonly includes skin eruption, fever, lymphadenopathy, sore throat, headache, cough, myalgia, and mouth ulcers10. These nonspecific symptoms overlap substantially with those of other febrile illnesses endemic to Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), including malaria, dengue fever, measles, and other viral exanthemas, creating a significant diagnostic challenge11. Accurate and timely laboratory confirmation is therefore central to effective patient management and outbreak containment12.

Both consecutive PHEICs exposed the structural inadequacy of mpox diagnostic strategies that rely exclusively on lesion swabbing. During the 2022 clade IIb global outbreak and the 2024 clade Ib outbreak in Africa, access to testing was severely constrained by shortages of specimen-collection materials, viral transport media, and personal protective equipment (PPE), as well as insufficient trained laboratory personnel and weak cold-chain infrastructure across affected settings13,14. These logistical barriers were compounded by the frequency of atypical clinical presentations, cases with few or absent lesions, lesions confined to inaccessible genital or perianal sites, or no visible rash, in which lesion swab collection is either technically impractical or not clinically initiated15,16. Serological surveillance conducted during the 2022 outbreak confirmed that mpox infection occurs in the complete absence of reportable skin findings, identifying a population entirely invisible to lesion-dependent case detection17. Collectively, these challenges led to substantial underdetection and hampered outbreak responses across successive emergencies13,14.

While lesion swabs remain the preferred specimen for mpox PCR owing to the high viral burden at active lesion sites7, relying solely on them creates two important gaps that alternative specimens are well-positioned to address. The first is clinical: lesion sampling is not feasible when lesions are absent during the early prodromal phase, dry or crusted without expressible material, or located at anatomically difficult sites such as the cervix, urethra, or deep intra-anal mucosa15,16. In such scenarios, common in Uganda’s current outbreak, given its predominance of genital and perianal clade Ib involvement, saliva, anorectal swabs, or urine are not optional supplements but may be the only technically viable specimens. Stigma surrounding mpox further narrows the window for lesion-based testing: fewer than 55% of at-risk individuals with a suspicious lesion sought healthcare in some cohorts, and more than 84% of affected communities identified mpox-related discrimination as a barrier to testing18,19. Patient-acceptable, potentially self-collected specimen types offer a distinct advantage here: not replacing the clinical encounter but enabling case detection in individuals who would otherwise remain entirely undiagnosed.

The second gap is epidemiological. Effective outbreak surveillance requires specimen collection that does not depend on visible lesions, invasive examination, or clinical staffing. Saliva, urine, and self-collected anorectal swabs can be obtained through community outreach, field-based surveillance, or postal self-sampling models, and evidence from mpox and analogous STI surveillance programs supports their feasibility and acceptability in these formats20,21. Detection of MPXV DNA in non-lesion specimens from individuals without recognized mpox symptoms further supports the value of these matrices for identifying transmission that lesion-based testing would miss entirely17,21. In resource-limited settings such as SSA, where cold-chain systems, trained personnel, and PPE are intermittently unavailable, specimens that are more tolerant of ambient conditions could also meaningfully expand the geographic and demographic reach of testing programs12–14.

Previous research has established biological proof-of-concept for non-lesion mpox diagnostics: MPXV DNA has been detected in saliva, semen, and urine of acutely infected patients22, and orthopoxviruses have been identified in the saliva, urine, faeces, and neural tissue of experimentally infected macaques23. However, published data on the diagnostic sensitivity and reliability of these specimen types remain limited in depth and geographic scope, with most studies reporting small cohorts from non-African settings, limited paired comparisons, and no data from clade Ib outbreaks. Rigorous, large-sample validation of alternative specimen performance in active outbreak conditions is therefore a prerequisite for evidence-based changes to diagnostic algorithms.

However, not all alternative specimens should be assumed to be uniformly superior to, or logistically simpler than, lesion swabs, and their limitations require explicit acknowledgement. Saliva is genuinely non-invasive and has demonstrated acceptable stability at ambient temperatures, but whole-saliva quality varies with hydration status and time since last oral intake, and collection standardization remains an active area of investigation24. Anorectal swabs require the same collection technique and viral transport media as lesion swabs; their advantage is anatomical rather than procedural, as they sample a mucosal site directly relevant to the dominant transmission routes in this outbreak. Self-collection has shown high concordance with clinician-collected swabs in sexually transmitted infection (STI) surveillance settings, supporting their potential role in outreach-based testing20,21. Urine specimens, while straightforward to obtain, are vulnerable to viral DNA degradation at ambient temperature; the optimal collection fraction midstream versus first-void has not been standardized across mpox studies, and the analytical limit of detection (LOD) for urine is substantially higher than for swab-based specimens in transport media, increasing the probability of missed low-viral-load infections25,26. These trade-offs underscore the need for rigorous, paired diagnostic performance evaluations before any alternative specimen is recommended for routine clinical or surveillance use.

To address this evidence gap, we conducted a cross-sectional paired diagnostic evaluation of saliva, anorectal swabs, and urine against lesion swabs as the reference standard in 120 participants during Uganda’s 2024–2025 clade Ib mpox outbreak. Here, we show that saliva is diagnostically equivalent to lesion swabs within prespecified ±10% bounds; anorectal swabs show high sensitivity; and urine is highly specific but shows significant directional discordance. These findings collectively support the integration of multi-site sampling into mpox diagnostic algorithms.

Equivalence testing

Paired bootstrap equivalence testing demonstrated diagnostic equivalence between saliva and lesion swabs, with Δ = −0.017, and the 90% CI was fully contained within ±0.10. Anorectal swabs and urine did not meet the equivalence criteria because their 90% CIs crossed the lower equivalence bound.

Regression analyses

In adjusted GEE logistic regression models accounting for within-participant correlation, urine specimens were associated with significantly lower odds of PCR positivity relative to lesion swabs (OR 0.40, 95% CI 0.22–0.74; p = 0.004), while no significant difference was observed for anorectal swabs (OR 0.68, 95% CI 0.41–1.13; p = 0.134) or saliva (OR 0.82, 95% CI 0.52–1.30; p = 0.403). Confirmed cases showed substantially higher odds of positivity than alert cases (OR 49.5, 95% CI 20.1–121.8; p < 0.0001). In GEE models restricted to PCR-positive specimens, mean Cq values did not differ significantly by specimen type after adjustment (Source data). Confirmed case status was independently associated with lower Cq values, consistent with a higher viral burden in confirmed infection. Sex was not significantly associated with PCR positivity (OR 1.05, 95% CI 0.72–1.53; p = 0.806) or Cq values across specimen types, indicating that detection performance was not meaningfully modified by sex and that the findings apply across both sexes.

Discussion

This study provides a systematically paired diagnostic evaluation of alternative specimen types, saliva, anorectal swabs, and urine, against lesion swabs for Mpox PCR detection using methodologically rigorous comparative analyses. Our findings demonstrate that saliva and anorectal swabs are effective alternatives to lesion swabs, particularly in multi-site sampling strategies. Notably, saliva achieved diagnostic equivalence to skin lesion swabs under the prespecified ±10% bounds, with PCR positivity of 97% in confirmed cases, compared with 98% for lesions. Anorectal swabs showed high sensitivity but narrowly missed the equivalence thresholds, whereas urine samples exhibited high specificity but significant directional discordance and lower detection odds. Together, these results offer critical quantitative refinement to the expanding evidence base on multi-site mpox diagnostics and have direct implications for patient-centred testing strategies and outbreak response27–30.

Lesion swabs remain the most reliable single specimen, consistent with mpox pathophysiology as a vesiculopustular disease characterized by high local viral burden. However, the near-equivalent performance of saliva underscores that viral shedding is not confined to cutaneous lesions. Saliva’s high sensitivity and near-complete concordance with lesion swabs suggest that oral fluids capture meaningful viral signals during acute infection and can identify infections that would otherwise have been missed by lesion sampling alone. Anorectal swabs similarly demonstrated high sensitivity, consistent with mucosal involvement and exposure routes, whereas urine showed lower yield, limiting its standalone diagnostic value27,28,31–33.

Paired Cq analyses showed no significant differences between lesion swabs and alternatives among PCR-positive pairs, and Bland–Altman plots showed no systematic bias despite wide limits of agreement. These findings align with prior reports indicating that when multiple specimens are positive, viral loads are broadly comparable across matrices27–29. Accordingly, practical performance differences appear to be driven primarily by differences in binary detection probability rather than by systematic Cq displacement, reflecting heterogeneity in viral shedding across sites and over time.

Detection of MPXV DNA in saliva, urine, and anorectal swabs confirms widespread viral dissemination and multi-site shedding during acute infection. This has important implications for transmission and infection control. The presence of viral DNA in saliva raises concern for oral-to-oral transmission, whereas detection in anorectal and urinary matrices highlights the potential for exposure via other bodily fluids33. Although PCR positivity does not equate to infectivity34, recent research reporting successful live virus culture from approximately 66% of PCR-positive saliva samples underscores the epidemiological relevance of these findings35. Our results contribute to this understanding by confirming that mpox patients shed virus in multiple bodily fluids and align with the WHO’s recommendation to collect additional specimen types (such as oral/pharyngeal swabs, urine, semen, blood, and rectal swabs) to better characterize disease progression, viral dynamics, and transmission risk30.

These findings are consistent with evidence from the 2022–2023 multi-country mpox outbreaks. Although early outbreak investigations consistently found lesion swabs to be the best diagnostic specimens, studies in Barcelona, Spain, and Ontario, Canada, have detected MPXV DNA across diverse matrices, underscoring multisite shedding, with some cohorts showing 100% positivity in saliva27,29,34. Lower mpox saliva positivity (16.7%) has been observed in patients without rash using saliva PCR, compared with higher positivity (88.9%) in patients confirmed mpox-positive by lesion swab28, likely reflecting differences in clinical stage, symptom presentation, and collection method. While some prior reports suggested that throat swabs might miss a substantial portion of cases30, the high yield in this study may be attributed to whole saliva sampling, which captures viral shedding from both the oropharynx and any present oral lesions. Additionally, saliva’s high sensitivity and diagnostic equivalence to lesion swabs within prespecified ±10% bounds observed in this study further support the recommendation that noninvasive oral sampling can be a practical diagnostic approach, particularly when lesion sampling is impractical or unacceptable31,32,36.

For anorectal swabs, our results align with prior work demonstrating frequent detection in populations with sexual exposures at anorectal sites. The high sensitivity with moderate specificity suggests that anorectal sampling effectively captures true positives while occasionally yielding discordant positives relative to lesion-negative reference status32,37. Urine specimens, by contrast, demonstrated high specificity and PPV; however, they also showed notable diagnostic discordance and lower detection odds compared to lesion swabs. This reinforces prior caution: although urine can harbour viral DNA in a substantial proportion of cases, it remains more likely to miss positives than lesion and mucosal sites. The high specificity reflects a low false-positive rate, while the observed discordance was driven predominantly by lesion-positive/urine-negative pairs, indicating reduced sensitivity rather than any inherent contradiction between specimen types.

Importantly, these findings do not undermine or challenge the central role of lesion swabs, which remain the gold standard in current WHO and CDC guidelines38,39. Rather, they augment the diagnostic toolkit, and any shift in diagnostic algorithms should be made cautiously and supported by rigorous validation. Nevertheless, the accumulating data, including this study, make a compelling case for broadening the official guidance. It would be prudent for health authorities to consider expanding mpox sampling recommendations to include saliva and other alternative specimen types as acceptable diagnostic specimens, especially for epidemiological surveillance, particularly when skin lesions are absent, inaccessible, or when the collection of swabs is logistically constrained, or additional confirmation is needed40. Our results, consistent with prior studies, underscore that such changes could improve case-finding and patient care without compromising diagnostic accuracy.

From a practical standpoint, saliva is a patient-friendly, non-invasive specimen with diagnostic performance equivalent to lesion swabs under conservative bounds, and it is highly sensitive. This strengthens the case for scaled oral/oropharyngeal sampling in clinics or community testing programs when lesion sampling is limited, declined, or infeasible27,28,34. Anorectal swabs are particularly relevant in contexts of anorectal exposure or symptoms, consistent with recent assay validations on oropharyngeal and anorectal matrices29,32. Urine’s diagnostic performance profile makes it unsuitable as a standalone diagnostic specimen; its utility is best considered supplementary within a composite algorithm that includes a higher-yield site, provided that clinicians understand the risk of false negatives relative to lesion or oral/anorectal sampling27,29. These specimen-level recommendations apply equally across sexes: no significant difference in detection odds was observed between female and male participants (adjusted OR = 1.05; p = 0.806), and sex-stratified positivity rates differed by no more than 8.3 percentage points across specimen types. One caveat specific to urine is that biological differences in urogenital anatomy may influence viral yield and collection standardization differently across sexes, a question that warrants prospective evaluation with pre-specified sex-stratified analyses. Based on these findings, targeted multi-site strategies, lesion combined with saliva, or lesion combined with anorectal swab, are recommended to maximize sensitivity, particularly in atypical presentations or when initial results are discordant with clinical suspicion.

This pattern aligns with broader evidence that MPXV shedding varies across body sites and that detection timelines can vary by matrix and time from symptom onset, reinforcing multi-specimen strategies when feasible, particularly in research, surveillance, or among patients with ambiguous clinical presentations. Moreover, multicenter analyses of assay sensitivity underscore that platform and assay choice also matter, with analytical LODs varying across kits and potentially shaping detection probabilities in low‑load contexts41,42.

Several limitations merit consideration. Participants were recruited from a single national isolation unit, thereby enriching the cohort with moderate-to-severe cases likely to have peak viral shedding; sensitivity estimates for alternative specimen types may therefore be higher than those observed in mild or early-stage community presentations. The alert cohort partially broadens the clinical spectrum but cannot fully correct for this. Specificity estimates have limited precision where lesion-negative strata are small, and the absence of serial sampling precludes characterization of site-specific positivity trajectories over time. Many case investigation forms lacked symptom onset data, preventing adjustment for time since onset or lesion stage in GEE models; observed differences in specimen performance may therefore partly reflect underlying disease stage rather than intrinsic differences in viral yield. PCR positivity alone does not confirm transmissibility, as culture was not performed. Sex-disaggregated diagnostic metrics should be treated as descriptive and hypothesis-generating: subgroup sizes of 57–63 per sex yielded statistical power below 23% to detect a 10 percentage-point difference in positivity, and gender identity data were not collected. Urine collection fraction was not standardized, potentially introducing variability in viral yield and contributing to the higher discordance observed for this specimen type. Finally, a uniform extraction protocol was applied across all specimen matrices without matrix-specific optimization, and all testing was performed on a single RT-PCR platform; detection rates and analytical sensitivity may differ across other extraction methods or assay systems.

Building on our findings, we recommend several avenues for future research and improvements in Mpox diagnosis: Future research should prioritize prospective, longitudinal, multi-site sampling to quantify positivity windows and infectiousness across matrices, particularly saliva and anorectal sites. Future studies should pre-specify sex-stratified analyses of specimen diagnostic performance, with sample sizes powered for subgroup comparisons. In particular, the potential influence of urogenital anatomy on urine specimen yield and collection standardization across sexes warrants prospective evaluation. Optimizing collection methods, such as comparing patient-collected drool versus clinician-collected swabs, will be vital for real-world implementation. From a public health perspective, while lesion swabs are the current gold standard, integrating patient-friendly, non-invasive saliva testing could greatly expand access to screening and improve case-finding in community settings where lesion sampling is impractical. Finally, studies should consider composite testing algorithms to balance diagnostic yield with patient acceptability, especially where patient flow, stigma, or limited resources restrict lesion sampling.

In conclusion, saliva and other alternative specimens offer high diagnostic accuracy for mpox and meaningfully complement lesion-based testing. Saliva is diagnostically equivalent to lesion swabs for mpox PCR detection, and multi-site sampling improves sensitivity, thereby reducing the risk of missed cases due to sampling limitations or atypical presentation. These insights, supported by emerging research and consistent with early guideline recommendations, pave the way for more robust and flexible diagnostic approaches. By strategically incorporating alternative specimens and multi-sample protocols while acknowledging their limitations, healthcare providers can enhance mpox case detection and patient outcomes during current and future outbreak responses. Existing evidence indicates that with additional validation, diagnostic methods for mpox could become more comprehensive by including non-lesional specimens, ultimately strengthening our public health response to this re-emerging disease.

Method

Study design and setting

This was a cross-sectional study, conducted from February 26 to March 31, 2025, at the Entebbe Isolation unit and the Central Emergency Response and Surveillance Laboratory (CERSL) in Uganda. We evaluated the diagnostic performance of alternative samples (anorectal swabs, urine, and saliva) compared with lesion swabs as the reference standard. The study enroled 120 participants, including 92 PCR-confirmed cases and 28 clinical alerts admitted to the Entebbe Isolation Unit from selected hospitals throughout Uganda.

Participants included individuals who provided verbal consent and were undergoing treatment for PCR-confirmed Mpox at the Entebbe isolation unit, as well as symptomatic alerts with complete laboratory documentation (alert cohort). Clinical alerts were symptomatic individuals who met the WHO case definition for suspected mpox but without laboratory confirmation at enrolment. Demographic and clinical variables, including age, sex, district of residence, symptom onset, and clinical presentation, were obtained via structured interviews for admitted patients and extracted from case investigation forms for clinical alerts. Biological sex was recorded as self-reported by each participant during the structured interview or extracted from the case investigation form for the clinical alert cases. Gender identity data were not collected as part of this study. Alerts were excluded if request forms lacked essential metadata (sample type, collection date, demographics, or clinical history).

Sample collection and transport

The study enroled a consecutive available sample, and four matched specimens were collected per participant. Collection techniques were standardized across all specimen types to ensure reproducibility. Lesion swabs were obtained by a healthcare professional by swabbing the base of unroofed vesicles or pustules, or by scraping crust material with flocked swabs, and specimens were immediately transferred to viral transport media (VTM). Anorectal swabs were collected by a healthcare professional or a self-collecting patient under instruction, using a woven swab inserted approximately 3–4 cm into the anal canal without lubrication and rotated 360° against the rectal walls, then immediately placed into VTM and broken at the scoreline. Saliva was self-collected via passive drool or active spitting, where possible, into a conical Falcon tube for 2–5 minutes, with participants instructed to refrain from oral intake for at least 30 minutes prior to collection, where feasible. Urine specimens were collected in sterile wide-mouthed universal containers. All specimens were triple-packaged and transported to CERSL in Kampala (approximately 43 km from the Entebbe isolation unit) under cold-chain conditions (2–8 °C), totalling 480 specimens.

Laboratory analysis

Upon arrival at CERSL, samples were either processed and tested immediately or stored at 2–8 °C pending analysis, with all specimens tested within 48 hours of collection. Sample extraction was performed using the QIAamp viral DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 400 µL of the sample was lysed with 400 µL of AL buffer and 20 µL of protease, and then ethanol-mediated binding to a silica membrane was performed. Sequential washing with 500 µL of washes 1 and 2 was performed, followed by elution with 150 µL of AE buffer. A negative extraction control was included in each run to check for contamination. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using the Bioperfectus Monkeypox Virus Fast Real-Time PCR Kit (Jiangsu Bioperfectus Technologies Co., Ltd., catalogue number JC70203-1NW-25T) targeting conserved F3L gene regions of the MPXV genome on a Bio-Rad CFX 96 system. Specific primers and probes are designed based on the F3L gene region of MPXV, as previously described25. qPCR was performed in 25 μL reactions containing 10 μL sample DNA extract, 10 μL PCR Reaction Mix, 5 μL Detection Mix, 10 μL inhibition controls (negative and positive), and a negative extraction control. qPCR cycling conditions were: UDG enzyme processing and denaturation at 37 °C for 2 min, followed by denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, then 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, followed by annealing, extension, and fluorescence collection at 58 °C for 30 s. Fluorescence was collected through FAM, VIC, and CY5 channels. FAM and VIC detected MPXV; CY5 detected RNase P, the internal control. For MPXV, Cq ≤ 40 was positive, >40 negative. For RNase P, Cq ≤ 37.0 was positive, >37.0 negative.

Data analysis

The data was organized and checked for completeness in Microsoft Excel 2016. It included patient ID, age, gender, sample collection date, sample type, Cq values, and Mpox PCR result. Quality checks ensured data consistency and removed duplicate entries. Participant characteristics and test outcomes were summarized with descriptive statistics. Categorical variables (sex, case category, sample type, PCR results) were reported as frequencies and proportions. Continuous variables (Cq values) were summarized with means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges. Distributional assumptions for Cq values were assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test43. Diagnostic performance was evaluated by calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) from 2 × 2 contingency tables comparing each alternative specimen against lesion swabs as the reference. Sensitivity was defined as true positives/(true positives + false negatives) and specificity as true negatives/(true negatives + false positives). 95% confidence intervals for all performance metrics were estimated using the Wilson method44. Cohen’s kappa (κ) and Gwet’s AC1 were assessed to account for chance agreement45. Kappa values were interpreted as moderate (0.41–0.60) or substantial (0.61–0.80) agreement.

Pairwise diagnostic comparisons between lesion swabs and each alternative specimen type used exact McNemar tests to assess discordance in paired binary outcomes; p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons with the Holm correction46. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs on the log scale were used to measure magnitude and direction discordance. Differences in Cq values among paired PCR-positive specimens were analysed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Median differences and effect sizes (r) were calculated and classified as small, medium, or large. Bland–Altman plots were generated to assess agreement in Cq values47. Diagnostic equivalence was assessed using two one-sided tests (TOST)48 on paired differences in positivity proportions (alternative specimen vs. lesion swab), with prespecified bounds of ±0.10%, a priori for the maximum difference in PCR positivity that would not alter clinical decision-making, such as case isolation, initiate contact tracing, or substitute specimen type in a resource-limited or lesion-inaccessible setting. Paired bootstrap resampling (20,000 iterations) was used to derive 90% confidence intervals (CI). Equivalence was concluded if the 90% CI lay entirely within these bounds. This margin was chosen to provide a conservative and clinically interpretable threshold for interchangeability in a binary diagnostic outcome49. To account for within-participant correlation across specimen types, GEE models with an exchangeable correlation structure were fitted for regression analysis50. A binomial full-cohort GEE model was fitted to estimate the effect of specimen type, sex, and case category on PCR positivity. A Gaussian GEE was used to model Cq values among PCR-positive specimens, adjusting for the same covariates. Results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) or mean differences with 95% CIs.

Statistical analysis

All tests were two-sided with p < 0.05, except equivalence testing, which used 90% confidence intervals. Analyses were conducted in Anaconda Navigator (Jupyter Notebook 7.3.2) using Python 3.13.7 (statsmodels, numpy, SciPy for correlation and significance testing, and Matplotlib for visualization) (https://www.python.org/).

Ethical statement

Permission to conduct this study was obtained from the Ministry of Health’s Departments of Integrated Epidemiology, Surveillance, and Public Health Emergencies (IES & PHE) and the National Health Laboratory and Diagnostic Services (NHLDS). The data were collected as part of a notifiable disease outbreak response conducted under the Ministry of Health’s public health surveillance mandate. All activities were conducted within this emergency response framework, classified as relevant to public health practice for disease surveillance and control, and not considered prospective human subjects research requiring additional clearance from the Uganda National Council of Science and Technology (UNCST). Verbal informed consent was obtained from participants in isolation and from clinical alerts whenever feasible, in accordance with outbreak response procedures. Ethical oversight for the use of these data was provided by the Uganda National Health Laboratory Services Research and Ethics Committee (Approval number: UNHLS-2025-133), which granted a waiver of written individualized informed consent. All personal identifiers for records from February 26, 2025, to March 31, 2025, corresponding to patients screened and tested at the CESRL, were redacted before the dataset was released to the study team. All procedures were carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, relevant institutional policies, and national guidelines governing the use of de-identified public health surveillance data.
