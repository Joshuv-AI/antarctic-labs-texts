Postoperative Sleep Interventions and Cardiac Surgery: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Alexander Lombardo, James Tsimiklis, Ammar Zaka, Alasdair Leslie, Brandon Stretton, Joshua Kovoor, Pramesh Kovoor, Stephen Bacchi, Aashray Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionSleep disturbance is a common issue affecting nearly 78% of cardiothoracic postoperative patients. There are no systematic reviews of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) that evaluate the impact of sleep interventions within this population. This study aims to evaluate the sleeping patterns in post‐operative cardiac surgery patients and identify if sleep interventions improve sleep quality. The primary outcomes were subjective and objective measures of sleep using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) in addition to the duration of intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital admission.MethodsSearches on PubMed, Medline, Embase, Scopus and Cochrane were undertaken from database inception to 24 February 2023. Only RCTs that recorded a sleep intervention in addition to standardised sleep scoring measures in adult cardiac surgical patients were included.ResultsA total of 26 studies were included (n = 2323). Total PSQI score had a statistically significant standardised mean difference (SMD) between intervention and control groups for improved scores as indicated by the SMD of −0.73 (95% CI; −0.93; −0.52, p = 0.04). The duration of ICU admission had a SMD of −0.23 (95% CI; −0.50 to 0.03, p = 0.19) and was not statistically significant. Similarly, the duration of hospital admission had a SMD of −1.10 (95% CI; −2.56 to 0.37, p = < 0.01) and was not statistically significant.ConclusionWhilst sleep interventions did not have a statistically significant effect on the duration of ICU or hospital admission, they did show a significant improvement in sleep quality as reported through the PSQI total score.Keywords: cardiac surgery, post‐operative, sleep interventions, sleep quality, systematic review

Introduction

Sleep disturbance affects approximately 78% of patients undergoing cardiothoracic surgery, exacerbated by more invasive and prolonged surgery [1, 2]. Often recognised clinically, sleep disturbances typically present with signs including sleep deprivation, fragmentation of sleep and poor sleep quality. Post‐operative sleep disturbances in the intensive care setting are recognised to delay recovery, prolong hospitalisation, impair cognitive function and increase the risk of life‐threatening illness [3]. Sleep deprivation has been associated with an increased risk of myocardial injury, atrial fibrillation, thromboembolism, heart failure, stroke and death within 30 days postoperatively [4].

Despite the recognised sequelae of post‐operative sleep disturbance, we were unable to identify any existing evidence‐based cardiac surgery protocols or guidelines on sleep interventions. Based on primarily observational data, previous meta‐analyses have consistently revealed a dearth of sufficient evidence supporting the efficacy of pharmacological interventions in ameliorating sleep disturbances amongst hospitalised patients [5, 6, 7]. Despite this, peri‐operative melatonin, eye masks and earplugs have demonstrated incremental benefit in the surgical setting [8, 9]. The efficacy of sleep interventions in patients undergoing cardiac surgery is inconsistent and largely comprised of low‐quality evidence [8, 9]. To update our current understanding, we performed a systematic review and meta‐analysis to evaluate the efficacy of pharmacological and non‐pharmacological sleep interventions in patients undergoing cardiac surgery.

Methods

The review was performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Figure 1) [10]. The protocol was prospectively registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews in Health and Social Care (PROSPERO, ID = CRD42023408325).

PRISMA flowchart.

Study Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

To maintain the highest quality of evidence, only randomised controlled trials of sleep interventions in patients undergoing only cardiac surgery were included. The following studies were excluded: observational studies including cohort studies, case–control studies and case reports/series; non‐English studies when translation was unavailable; patients below 18 years of age; pre‐existing sleep apnoea; and animal studies. Full‐text review was conducted by two authors (A.J.L. and J.T.) independently with a high inter‐reviewer agreement (Cohen's k: 0.82) and disagreements resolved by a third reviewer (A.W.L.).

Search Strategy

Searches on PubMed, Medline, Embase, Scopus and Cochrane were undertaken from database inception to 24 February 2023. The key search terms used included ‘sleep’, ‘cardiothoracic surgery’, ‘postoperative’, ‘sleep scale’ and other related terms. A detailed search strategy is shown in Appendix A. The results of the literature search were downloaded into EndNote and duplicate citations were identified and removed. All titles and abstracts were screened against the inclusion and exclusion criteria with potentially eligible studies selected for full‐text analysis.

Data Extraction

Two reviewers (A.J.L. and J.T.) independently screened all the titles and abstracts. This was performed with a free‐to‐use web application (Rayyan, Qatar Computing Research Institute, Ar‐Rayyan, Qatar) [11]. Conflicts were resolved by the inclusion of a third reviewer (A.W.L.). This was followed by the full‐text review of the selected articles by the two independent reviewers (A.J.L. and J.T.). We then extracted the data from selected studies using a standardised, pilot‐tested extraction template. The following data was extracted: research design, population characteristics (sex, mean age, comorbidities) and intervention type (e.g., massage, music therapy, etc.). Similarly, quantitative and qualitative outcomes, source of funding and reported conflicts of interest and methodological quality information were included.

Quality Assessment

Two reviewers (A.J.L. and J.T.) assessed the quality of the included studies by using the Downs and Black checklist [12]. Downs and Black score ranges were given corresponding quality levels as previously reported: excellent (26–28); good (20–25); fair (15–19); and poor (≤ 14). We evaluated potential biases using quality classifications of ‘low risk of bias’ when data for the criterion were described, ‘high risk of bias’ when data were not stated and ‘unclear risk of bias’ when the criterion was not relevant to the study design. Any conflicting classification was resolved by discussion with a third reviewer (A.W.L.).

Outcomes

Primary outcomes were subjective measures (patient‐reported quality) and objective measures of sleep (duration and disturbances) through standardised scoring measures (e.g., Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, Epworth Sleepiness Scale, Uppsala Sleep Inventory, etc.) [13]. Secondary outcomes were major adverse cardiac events (MACE), hospital length of stay (in days) and duration in ICU (in days). The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) consists of 19 self‐reported items across seven domains: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleeping medication and daytime dysfunction [14]. A global score is generated by summing the equally weighted domains (scale of 0–3) for a total score of 0–21. Higher scores signify worse sleep quality, with scores > 5 representative of poor sleep quality (sensitivity of 89.6%, specificity of 86.5%) [15, 16]. The PSQI is shown to have a high degree of internal consistency with Cronbach's alpha of 0.85 and has been validated for the diagnosis of good versus poor sleep quality [17].

Statistical Analysis

Unadjusted risk estimates were pooled from studies that provided sufficient data for calculation using a random‐effects model for continuous data. Heterogeneity between studies was assessed by a combination of I 2 statistic, Cochran's Q test, and data observation for each outcome. Significant interaction was considered when p ≤ 0.05 [18]. All calculations were performed using R. Heterogeneity was classified using the I 2 statistic as ‘might not be important’ (0% to 40%), moderate (30% to 60%), substantial (50% to 90%) and considerable (75% to 100%).

Results

Study Characteristics and Baseline Demographics

The characteristics of the included 26 RCTs (n = 2323 patients) are outlined in Table 1. The mean age was 57.2 years (SD = 4.70) and 56.6 years (SD = 5.16) in the intervention and control groups respectively, with 69.2% of all participants being male. The most common surgery performed was coronary artery bypass graft surgery (74.99%), followed by valvular surgery (16.92%) and other/non‐specified cardiac surgery (8.09%). The mean length of surgery was 235 min (SD = 51.7) and 224 min (SD = 57.1) in the control and intervention groups respectively. Of the total cohort, 25.53% had hypertension, 14.03% had diabetes, and 25.70% had a smoking history. The included studies investigated a multitude of different pharmacological and non‐pharmacological interventions. The most common interventions included muscle‐related therapy (eight interventions), herbal extracts (five interventions) and self‐care education (five interventions). There was insufficient data to perform meta‐analysis by intervention.

Statistical Analysis

Patients receiving sleep interventions demonstrated a reduced PSQI score of −0.73 with moderate heterogeneity (95% CI; −0.93; −0.52, p = 0.04, I 2 = 49%) (Figure 2). The duration of ICU admission (in days) had a SMD of −0.23 with moderate heterogeneity (95% CI; −0.50 to 0.03, p = 0.19, I 2 = 33%) (Figure 3) and was not statistically significant. Similarly, the duration of hospital admission (in days) had a SMD of −1.10 with substantial heterogeneity (95% CI; −2.56 to 0.37, p = < 0.01, I 2 = 92%) (Figure 4) and was not statistically significant. There was insufficient data to allow meta‐analysis for further selected outcomes.

Methodological Quality and Risk of Bias

As shown in Table 1, the studies were deemed as high quality overall with calculated mean scores as follows: total mean score 26 out of 32 (SD 2.35); reported subscale mean score 9.02 out of 11 (SD 0.99); external validity sub‐scale mean score 2.71 out of 3 (SD 0.68); bias sub‐scale mean score 4.73 out of 7 (SD 0.97); confounding sub‐scale mean score 4.54 out of 6 (SD 0.66); power sub‐scale mean score 5 out of 5 (SD 0).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta‐analysis to date to evaluate the existing evidence base on sleep interventions in cardiac surgery. This study comprised several RCTs, which included a diverse spectrum of interventions. The most recent relevant systematic review was in 2011 and included 13 studies without interventions and identified poor sleep outcomes in cardiothoracic patients [19]. Whilst there are no existing guidelines on the management of sleep in cardiac surgical patients, this study demonstrates the presence of a multitude of RCTs trialling various interventions that have been published reviewing the effect of a single intervention on a sleep outcome. Based on low‐quality evidence, sleep interventions were associated with statistically significant improved sleep quality and shorter ICU and hospital admissions, which were not statistically significant. This study corroborates with existing literature which has identified worse PSQI scores following cardiac surgery compared to scores following implementation of sleep interventions [20, 21]. Similarly, the existing literature supports an improvement in sleep quality following sleep intervention (PSQI SMD 2.49) without significant change to the duration of ICU or hospital admission [22, 23].

Upon review of the duration of ICU and hospitalisation, these findings must be viewed with caution given their low quality of evidence, substantial heterogeneity and potential confounders. This significant heterogeneity is potentially secondary to peri/post‐operative complications, individual surgeon and hospital practise and the absence of standardised interventions. The RCTs included in our study were not powered to identify significant differences in clinical endpoints. Previous observational data show poor sleep has been associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes [3, 24]. As such, future larger‐scale prospective trials should be powered to identify significant differences in clinical endpoints including all‐cause mortality, myocardial infarction and stroke [4]. The majority of included studies did not report MACE and there was insufficient data for meta‐analysis of intervention type and other cardiac outcomes.

Limitations

Unfortunately, a scarcity of major adverse cardiovascular events and surgical complications was recorded in the included studies. In addition, this study features considerably heterogeneous data due to the inclusion of a multitude of varied sleep interventions which were insufficient in number to run an appropriate subgroup analysis. For this reason, further long‐term research is required to identify if direct relationships between specific sleep interventions and subsequent impact on complications can be identified.

The existing evidence base exhibits heterogeneity in terms of the types and components of sleep interventions employed across studies. This heterogeneity makes it challenging to compare and generalise the findings. Standardisation of interventions and protocols would help ensure consistency and enable more robust comparisons. Different studies utilise various outcome measures to assess postoperative sleep quality, such as the PSQI score or subjective patient‐reported measures. This variability in outcome measures limits the ability to generalise these findings. Many studies focus on immediate postoperative sleep quality rather than examining the long‐term effects of sleep interventions. Long‐term follow‐up would provide insights into the sustainability of the intervention effects on sleep quality beyond the immediate recovery period. A key limitation identified in the current literature is the inconsistent reporting of the timing and duration of sleep interventions, as well as the timing of outcome assessment, which limits comparability across studies. Future research should aim to provide detailed information on when and how long interventions are administered to better understand their effects within different periods of postoperative care.

It is crucial to acknowledge the potential presence of biases and confounding factors in the existing evidence base. RCTs should aim to minimise selection bias, performance bias and detection bias through rigorous study design, randomisation, blinding and appropriate statistical analyses. Although guidelines for sleep interventions in cardiac surgery patients exist, they are not yet well‐established and standardised. Further consensus and collaboration amongst experts are needed to develop evidence‐based guidelines that can guide clinical practise and facilitate more consistent implementation of sleep interventions. Based on the findings and limitations of this study, there are multiple potential areas for future research. One area is the need to investigate the long‐term impacts of sleep interventions as this study predominantly assessed the immediate postoperative period. Given that ICU length of stay is influenced by numerous factors such as preoperative functional status, surgical complexity and institutional protocols, future studies may yield more meaningful insights by focusing on outcomes such as postoperative sleep quality, quality of life and MACE. Another important limitation of our study is the lack of standardised pain assessment data. Given the relationship between pain control and sleep quality, future studies should incorporate regular pain scoring and analyse its interaction with sleep outcome to better understand the potential confounding or mediating effects of analgesia. Additionally, further analysing the impact of patient demographics on sleep interventions could provide valuable insights regarding how healthcare providers may tailor interventions to better meet the needs of different patient populations.

Conclusion

Whilst sleep interventions did not have a statistically significant effect on the ICU or hospital admission duration, they did show a significant improvement in overall sleep quality as reported through the PSQI total score. The included studies have demonstrated preliminary evidence that sleep interventions may improve sleep quality for cardiac surgery patients. These findings provide support for future research in this area, particularly given the association between poor sleep and cardiovascular outcomes.
