Model-Informed Precision Dosing of Protein Kinase Inhibitors: Evaluation of Model-Averaging and Flattened Priors Methods
Authors: Félicien Le Louedec, Laura Morvan, Loïc Mourey, Maud Maillard, Christelle Vachoux, Malika Yakoubi, Diego Tosi, Gwenaelle Gravis, Guilhem Roubaud, Frédéric Thuillier, Helen Boyle, Fabienne Thomas, Mélanie White-Koning, Florent Puisset, Étienne Chatelut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesTherapeutic drug monitoring of protein kinase inhibitors (PKIs) usually relies on the measure of a single trough concentration at steady-state (Cmin,ss). When the sampling time differs from the trough, it is theoretically possible to predict Cmin,ss from maximum a posteriori (MAP) Bayesian estimates of PK parameters. However, several questions remain with regards to model-informed precision dosing (MIPD) of PKIs, such as choosing which model to use when several are available in the literature. Alternative techniques, such as flattened priors and model averaging may outperform standard analyses. The aim of this work is to report a comprehensive fit-for-purpose validation of MIPD for sunitinib and pazopanib.MethodsConcentration data from 41 renal cancer patients included in the SUP-R trial (NCT02555748) measured 2 and 6 hours after an intake were analyzed (MAP-Bayesian estimation of PK parameters) in order to predict Cmin,ss at the current cycle and at the next cycle. Different models from the literature were tested, as well as the model-averaging and flattened priors features available in the R package ‘mapbayr’.ResultsThe quality of Cmin,ss predictions depended on the model used. Flattening priors rarely improved or worsened the predictions. Model averaging was robust across the different scenarios tested and should be preferred to using a single model. Overall, a precision of 20% to 25% was achieved, with a minimal bias (< 5%).ConclusionThe benefit of the model-averaging method for the model-informed precision dosing of sunitinib and pazopanib is likely applicable to other protein kinase inhibitors. Thanks to ‘mapbayr’, this framework was implemented as a standalone shiny application to be used in clinical settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40262-026-01628-2.

Key Points

The choice and condition of use of population pharmacokinetics model when single concentration data from therapeutic drug monitoring is analyzed to individualize the dose is crucial.Considering a weighted average of predictions of Cmin,ss (i.e., model averaging) is superior to the choice of a single model for sunitinib and pazopanib. Decreasing the information a priori (flattening priors) has a limited influence.This framework was implemented in clinical applications thanks to the R package ‘mapbayr’.

Introduction

Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) of oral protein kinase inhibitors (PKIs) generally consists of measuring the trough concentration of the drug in plasma at steady state (Cmin,ss), assessing whether it falls within a target interval and adapting the dose accordingly [1–4]. TDM is performed in order to control the large inter-individual variability (IIV) in pharmacokinetics (PK) of these drugs. Indeed, PKIs present common PK characteristics which are sources of variability between individuals: pH-dependent resorption [5], sensitivity to food effect and difference in the extent of CYP3A4 metabolic capacities in the intestine and the liver [6]. This variability may result in disparities in drug exposure, with clinical consequences such as inefficacy in case of underexposure or adverse events in the opposite situation.

Sunitinib and pazopanib are antiangiogenic PKIs mainly used in the treatment of metastatic renal cell carcinoma, but also of gastro-intestinal stromal tumors and pancreatic neuro-endocrine tumors for sunitinib and soft-tissue sarcomas for pazopanib. The level of proof for the benefit of TDM of these drugs is high [7]. Monitoring the sum of sunitinib and N-desethyl-sunitinib (ND-sunitinib, the active metabolite) Cmin,ss was found to be associated with better clinical outcomes, with an efficacy target of 50–100 ng/mL in renal cell cancer [8]. For pazopanib, the interest in and the feasibility of TDM has also been proved, with an efficacy threshold of 20 mg/L in the same indication [9]. The choice of Cmin,ss rather than the area under the concentration curve between two administrations at steady state (AUCτ,ss) as the exposure metric is mainly motivated by the feasibility of the practice with outpatients [10, 11].

The main constraint to the practice of TDM of PKIs is the need to respect the sampling time (i.e., 24 h after last intake) in order to ensure the interpretation of the result against the established target trough concentrations. In case of non-compliance to the defined sampling time, Cmin,ss may be estimated using log-linear extrapolation based on the drug’s average elimination half-life and the time between sampling and dosing [12, 13]. An alternative and more robust method is to analyze the measured concentration jointly with a population PK model in order to make a Bayesian estimation of individual PK parameters and to predict Cmin,ss [14]. A population PK model describes the course of concentrations as a function of time in the population and allows the estimation of individual PK parameters even though the number of observations is low. This process corresponds to a model-informed precision dosing approach (MIPD), where all a priori and observed information about the drug PK are analyzed within a Bayesian framework in order to optimize the dose recommendation [15, 16]. This approach has been particularly successful in the fields of antibiotics such as vancomycin [17].

Several challenges arise for MIPD of PKIs such as sunitinib and pazopanib. First, the choice of model used for the estimation of parameters and for the prediction of Cmin,ss must be validated. Recently, model averaging has been suggested as a method to avoid having to choose a single model, and to potentially improve the quality of individual predictions. The idea is to analyze the same data using different models and to average their predictions with a weight calculated from the performance of each fit [18, 19]. Within this framework, models that poorly predict concentration data are penalized compared with models with a better goodness-of-fit.

Secondly, the analysis of a single concentration increases the risk of parameter shrinkage. Indeed, a population PK model informs the prior distribution and allows the estimation of parameters from sparse data designs with values within the range of values from which the PK model was initially constructed. However, even when a model is well specified at the population level in terms of structural, statistic and covariate models, its ability to estimate individual parameters might not be optimal. When a single concentration is analyzed (which is the case with PKIs), the parameter likelihood informed by observations may be too flat. At the estimation step, any deviation from the mode of the likelihood might not be penalized enough as compared with a deviation from the typical parameter value. This results in an inaccurate estimation of the individual PK parameters, with estimates too close to the typical values and far from the actual ones. One solution to deal with the shrinkage is to decrease the weight of the prior information in the calculation of the objective function value (OFV). This method, so-called ‘flattened priors’, was recently applied to vancomycin data and improved predictions [20].

Overall, this question of a ‘fit-for-purpose’ validation has already been addressed for other drugs whose doses are individualized using PK models, such as methotrexate [21] or vancomycin [22, 23] for instance, but not so far for PKIs with the exception of imatinib [24].

In the current report, PK data collected during the SUP-R clinical trial were analyzed in order to validate a MIPD approach for sunitinib (and its metabolite) and pazopanib. The capacity to predict the Cmin,ss when the sampling time does not correspond to trough concentration, as well as the capacity to predict Cmin,ss at the next occasion were investigated.

Methods

Patient and Data Collection

Concentration data were collected in the context of the SUP-R study (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT02555748). This study aimed to examine the feasibility of sunitinib and pazopanib dose individualization based on TDM. Patients were treated based on 6-week cycles until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity. Sunitinib patients received 50 mg/day for 4 weeks, followed by 2 weeks of washout. Pazopanib patients received 800 mg/day continuously. The protocol allowed dose reduction based on observed toxicities according to recommendations stated in the summary of product characteristics [25, 26]. Patients were sampled on day 15 of cycle 1 and day 15 of cycle 2, before (T0, trough concentration), 2 (T2) and 6 (T6) hours after drug intake. Concentrations of sunitinib, ND-sunitinib and pazopanib were determined with a validated ultra-high performance liquid chromatography method coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) [27]. Lower limits of quantification were 0.98 ng/mL for sunitinib and ND-sunitinib, and 0.01 µg/L for pazopanib.

MAP-Bayesian Analysis

Our analysis consisted of predicting the individual trough concentrations at day 15 of cycle 1 (C1T0) and at day 15 of cycle 2 (C2T0) from maximum a posteriori (MAP) Bayesian estimates of PK parameters based on concentrations observed at day 15 of cycle 1, 2 h (C1T2) or 6 h (C1T6) after the intake (Fig. 1). Several scenarios were tested. First, five population PK models of sunitinib + ND-sunitinib and six models of pazopanib from the literature were used. Then a model-averaging procedure based on the OFV was implemented [18]. Finally, four different weights were applied on prior information [20]. For each condition, the performance to predict the trough concentration at the current (C1T0, as a measure of actual exposure) and at the next cycle (C2T0, to measure the relevance of suggesting an alternative dosing based on MAP-Bayesian estimates of PK parameters) was quantified. Models

The five previously published models describing the pharmacokinetics of sunitinib and ND-sunitinib in adults are summarized in Table S1 of the electronic supplementary material (ESM). The model of Houk et al. [28] is made of two independent bicompartmental models (one for the parent drug, one for the metabolite), each describing a first-order absorption and elimination. Sunitinib and ND-sunitinib clearances (CL) are influenced by sex, race, tumor type and ECOG score, and volume of distribution is influenced by body weight. The model of Khosravan et al. [29] is similar to the previous one, with an additional lag time for sunitinib absorption. Age, sex, race and tumor type influenced CL, while body weight, sex and tumor type influenced central volume of distribution. The model of Yu et al. [30], as well as both remaining models, described the PK of parent drug and metabolite in a single model. This model takes into account non-linear metabolism of sunitinib. There is a peripheral volume for ND-sunitinib but not for sunitinib, and body weight influences the CL and the volumes of distribution of both molecules. The model of Diekstra et al. [31] is similar to that of Yu et al., but adds a peripheral volume for sunitinib. The model of Lindauer et al. [32] is the most complex, with delayed absorption with transit compartments and implementation of both systemic and pre-systemic metabolism. Distribution is bicompartmental for sunitinib but monocompartmental for ND-sunitinib.

The six models describing the PK of pazopanib are summarized in Table S2 (ESM). The models of Imbs et al. (2014) [33] and Imbs et al. (2016) [34] were both monocompartmental with a delayed first-order absorption and linear elimination. Inter-occasion variability (IOV) was implemented in CL and volume. There was no covariate in these models. The model of Ozbey et al. [35] was similar to the models of Imbs et al., but included an inverse relationship between aspartate aminotransferase levels (AST) and volume of distribution. The model of Yu et al. [36] was bicompartmental with a linear elimination; absorption was sequential and composed of two first-order phenomena, with a relative bioavailability (rF) that decreased as a function of time and dose. An IOV was set on rF. The model of Mourey et al. [37] is similar to the previous one, except that rF only decreased as a function of time (and not dose anymore), and that CL decreased as a function of age. The model of Baneyx et al. [38] is bicompartmental, with first order absorption and elimination, and with a decreased rF as a function of dose, and if the day of treatment is not the first day (categorical covariate).

Of note, because a single concentration–time point per patient was analyzed in the present work, IOV was not identifiable [39, 40]. Therefore, no cycle identification was used in our dataset.

Software

Data management, statistical analyses—including the MAP-Bayesian estimation of parameters, model averaging and prior flattening—and reporting were performed in R version 4.2 using the packages mrgsolve [43], mapbayr [44] and tidyverse [45].

The effect of flattening priors (up to λ = 0.03) differed between models. On the one hand, for Khosravan et al., the rRMSE decreased from roughly 110–120% to 20–45% and rMPE from roughly + 65% to 0–10% across the different scenarios. On the other hand, for other models some performances worsened slightly; for instance, the prediction of C1T0 from C1T6 with the model of Yu et al. (rRMSE increased from 15.6% to 18.2%). With other models, predictions slightly improved or stayed the same with an rRMSE of about 17–20% for the prediction of C1T0, and 20–26% for C2T0.

Model averaging gave predictions which were similar to the most performant models, especially when priors were flattened (λ = 0.03). The predictions of C1T0 from the C1T2 or the C1T6 concentration were precise (rRMSE = 17.1% and 18.1%, respectively) and unbiased (rMPE = + 2.3% and + 5.4%, respectively), as well as the prediction of C2T0 (rRMSE = 20.6% and 22.8%, rMPE = − 5.8% and − 3.0%, respectively).

At the individual level, the best model was not the same for every patient, as shown by Figure S1 in ESM.

The model-averaging procedure outperformed original models, even more so when priors were flattened, and whatever the analyzed or predicted concentration. For instance, with λ = 0.03, from a C1T2 concentration, the C1T0 was estimated with rRMSE = 25.7% and rMPE = +1.7%, and the C2T0 with rRMSE = 24.2% and rMPE = +0.2%.

As observed for sunitinib, at the individual level, the best model was not the same for every patient (Fig. S1, ESM).

Discussion

In this work, concentration data of sunitinib and pazopanib collected at a time different from the trough were analyzed in order to predict two concentrations of interest for TDM—the Cmin,ss just before intake, and Cmin,ss 6 weeks later. The motivation was two-fold; on one hand, to evaluate the ability to predict exposure at the current cycle, on the other hand, to see whether this performance would still be valid at the next cycle with a possibly modified dose based on the currently estimated PK parameters. Different population PK models from the literature were tested, and the impacts of a model-averaging procedure and of flattened priors were explored. For sunitinib, there were substantial differences in performance between models. There is no straightforward explanation for these discrepancies. The models of Houk et al. and Yu et al. frequently demonstrated superior performances across scenarios, despite strong structural differences. The first describes the PK of sunitinib and ND-sunitinib with two independent bicompartmental linear models, while the second uses a single model with a non-linear transformation of parent drug to metabolite. Conversely, the reason why the models of Diekstra et al. and Khosravan et al. performed poorly is probably related to their RUV, with a proportional error around 30–40%, while the other models had RUV around 10–20%. Therefore, their inability to fit an observed concentration is not penalized enough in the calculation of the OFV. Thus, as expected, they benefit the most from the flattened priors method, whose characteristic is precisely to give more weight to the data in the estimation of parameters. Flattening priors did not markedly improve the performance of models that performed well initially (i.e., Houk et al. and Yu et al.); the ratio between the IIV and the RUV originally reported is probably balanced enough to avoid an important shrinkage phenomenon. Model averaging did not outperform any original models in terms of rRMSE, but showed good and consistent performances across sampling times and prediction cycle. Moreover, bias was acceptable whatever the scenario (roughly ±5%).

For pazopanib, predictions from C1T6 concentrations tended to yield worse estimations than from C1T2, whatever the model. This could be explained by the highest variability in pazopanib absorption rate or by statistical fluctuations due to the low number of patients (Table 1). As for sunitinib, discrepancies were observed between models. Baneyx et al. was systematically associated with the poorest performance. This is most likely due to its smaller volumes of distribution and the lack of IIV on these parameters, as opposed to the other models. Model averaging could outperform any original model across the estimation scenarios. For every model, flattening priors hardly improved predictions, probably due to a high IIV in the original models that enables a good fit of a single concentration.

Overall, for both sunitinib and pazopanib, model averaging should be preferred over a single original model in the context of model-informed precision dosing of PKIs, mainly because it performs sufficiently well and consistently across sampling and predicting scenarios. At the individual level, supplementary results showed that the best model was not the same for every patient (Fig. S1, ESM). Model averaging offers a solution to this dilemma when performing TDM for a specific patient, since there are no objective arguments to choose a priori which would be the best single model. One could argue that model averaging does not improve performances compared with the use of a single model. Indeed, on the one hand, at the individual level, model averaging rarely emerged as the top-performing method compared with a single model (Fig. S1, ESM). On the other hand, on summarized data, rRMSE and rMPE did not improve with sunitinib data (Fig. 2), and only slightly with pazopanib data (Fig. 3) but this should be interpreted with care due to the limited sample size (n = 8–11). The reason why a major improvement in performance was not seen with model averaging might be that, for PKIs, only one concentration at steady state per patient was analyzed. These problems share similarities with the simple determination of CL and AUCτ,ss from an administration rate and a steady-state concentration. Thus, taking into account the differences of structures between models may not be as relevant as with IV drugs like vancomycin, where the model must capture both peak and trough concentrations [18].

Flattening priors did not improve the performance of estimations overall, precluding the recommendation of a systematic application of this methodology with PKIs. However, one could have expected that over-flattening priors would over-fit the observations at the expense of a worsened prediction of Cmin,ss. Our results did not show this, thus giving us confidence to use flattened priors in case of poor goodness of fit of a particular concentration. One could also argue that a crude application of flattened priors systematically to every patient is inadequate, since some patients might benefit from it, and some might not. Hughes and Keizer trained a machine learning (ML) algorithm to identify, a priori, the situation when priors should be flatten or not with vancomycin data [20]. This was not feasible here due to the low number of patients in our analysis.

It could be risky to extrapolate our results to other PKIs since our study was limited to sunitinib and pazopanib data. However, regarding the differences between these two drugs (metabolite data for sunitinib, but not for pazopanib; oral bioavailability limited by poor solubility in the digestive tract for pazopanib, but a first-pass hepatic effect for sunitinib…), it is reasonable to think that the recommendation to use model averaging, but not the flattening priors, should be applied to other PKIs.

More complex methods have also been suggested to deal with parameter-shrinkage. They comprise performing a classical MAP-Bayes estimation and correcting the estimated CL afterwards. The function applied to correct the estimate of CL would be dependent on the design of the problem and be determined with simulations. Baklouti et al. suggested a method where n profiles are simulated (with n different CL) and n MAP estimations are obtained; the smoothing function of the actual CL versus the estimated CL would be used as a correction function of shrinkage [42]. Destere et al. proposed a similar approach, but used ML to identify the correction function; the most relevant predictors in this ML algorithm were variables related to the difference between observed and predicted concentrations [46]. For both methods, the correction function must be identified for every individual problem (i.e., individual dosing records, covariate data and times of observation), which is time-consuming and has not been adapted to clinical settings yet.

Overall, for sunitinib and pazopanib, the predictions of Cmin,ss at the next cycle were associated with a precision (rRMSE) of approximately 20–25%, which could arguably be considered high in the context of ‘precision medicine’. TDM is expected to be associated with this 20–25% value or even a lower value for most patients; it is thus here a mean value, resulting in about half of the patients with a larger (worse) value. However, these values are close to the reported values of RUV (Table S1, Table S2, ESM), which could mean that the predictive limits of the models were reached. Furthermore, the difficulty in predicting the Cmin,ss at the next cycle even more precisely can be explained by the random IOV. This phenomenon is well documented for these drugs (Table S1, Table S2, ESM), and is hard to capture in MIPD [47, 48], especially when only one concentration per occasion is available, which precludes the statistical identifiability of IOV [40]. This is particularly the case in our sunitinib data, where the prediction of Cmin,ss at the next cycle is systematically worse than at the current cycle. Poor documentation of the dosing and sampling time in the data set can also contribute to degradation of the model performances.

In addition, MAP-Bayesian estimations return the point estimates of PK parameters, and the uncertainty about estimation is ignored. A proper estimation of parameter uncertainty could be propagated to concentrations (e.g., with simulations or the delta method [49]) and this would result in the calculation of a confidence interval of concentrations (instead of a single value) and of the associated probability of being within the target exposure (instead of a binary response). Although theoretically mapbayr can approximate the parameter uncertainty thanks to the Hessian matrix, it has been shown that more advanced Bayesian methods that leverage stochastic algorithms should be used for this purpose [50–52].

The final purpose of the current work is to use this framework in a clinical setting within MIPD software. From drug concentrations measured in the context of TDM, one could determine the dose regimen associated with the best probability of target attainment and individualize the patient dose. From a technical perspective, the MAP-Bayesian estimation of PK parameters with several models at once, the averaging of their predictions and the use of various degrees of prior flattening are possible using the R package mapbayr [44]. All these features are natively embedded and documented in the package, which facilitates research work as in the present article, and implementation in a clinical setting within a shiny application [53]. Conclusion

The present work defined the conditions necessary to apply model-informed precision dosing for sunitinib and pazopanib. We observed that the best specific population PK model to predict the Cmin,ss is not the same for all patients. We emphasized the benefit of using the model-averaging method for the model-informed precision dosing of sunitinib and pazopanib, and likely of other PKIs. In our laboratory, this framework was implemented within a shiny application, where parameter estimations, concentration predictions and averaging are done simultaneously thanks to the mapbayr R package we developed.
