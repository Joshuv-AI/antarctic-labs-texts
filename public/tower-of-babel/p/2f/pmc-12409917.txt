Development and validation of a new yearning induction task: a cross-cultural study of Chinese and Swiss bereaved
Authors: Jun Wen, Guangyuan Shi, Xin Xu, David Eberle, Wenli Qian, Jianping Wang, Andreas Maercker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Yearning is a core symptom of prolonged grief disorder (PGD), typically manifesting as vivid counterfactual thoughts and bittersweet emotions. However, current self-report measures fail to capture the dynamic nature of yearning and are often compromised by social desirability bias. This highlights the critical need for accurate and objective tools to assess this complex emotional experience.Objective: This study aimed to develop and validate the Yearning Induction Task (YIT), an innovative experimental paradigm designed to induce yearning in bereaved individuals from China and Switzerland. We also sought to explore how cultural differences shape emotional and cognitive responses following experimentally induced yearning.Methods: A total of 231 bereaved participants (130 from China and 101 from Switzerland) were recruited. After completing baseline measures, participants were randomly assigned to either the yearning or control condition of the YIT. Momentary yearning and emotion responses were assessed both pre- and post-induction. Data were analysed using ANCOVA to examine cultural differences in the induction effect of the task.Results: The task demonstrated good interrater reliability across cultures but did not significantly alter momentary yearning levels. However, significant cultural differences emerged in emotional responses. Swiss participants predominantly showed increases in negative emotions, such as sadness, anger, and loneliness, following the yearning induction. In contrast, Chinese participants experienced a decrease in positive emotions, including happiness. In the control condition, Swiss participants reported declines in positive emotions (e.g. gratitude, love), while Chinese participants experienced an increase in happiness and a reduction in overall negative emotions.Conclusion: This study provided preliminary evidence for the reliability and validity of a novel task to induce yearning among Swiss and Chinese bereaved individuals. Cultural differences emerged in emotional responses to induced yearning, although construct validity was only partially supported. These findings highlight the importance of culturally sensitive assessment tools and interventions for yearning in PGD.KEYWORDS: Yearning, prolonged grief disorder, cross-cultural, experimental induction, assessment

Yearning as a core symptom of PGD

Yearning is a complex cognitive-emotional phenomenon that extends beyond a mere emotional response to loss (Holm, 1999; Kaplan et al., 2018). On the cognitive side, it involves repetitive and uncontrolled counterfactual thoughts, as well as vivid mental images of the deceased. These thoughts often lead the bereaved to dwell on ‘what if’ scenarios, emphasizing the unrealized potential of the continued presence of the deceased (Robinaugh et al., 2016). While sadness and depression are common emotional responses to loss, yearning is a distinctive expression of the emotional bonds that persist after death (Bowlby, 1980). Individuals experiencing yearning often find themselves oscillating between the comfort of cherished memories and the anguish of irrevocable loss, leading to a bittersweet emotional state (Eisma et al., 2020). These cognitive and emotional elements integrate to characterize yearning, marking it as a unique aspect of the grieving process (Waters, 2023).

The extant literature consistently identifies yearning as the core symptom of prolonged grief disorder (PGD), distinguishing it from other conditions that occur after loss (e.g. Maercker et al., 2013; Prigerson et al., 2021). The inclusion of PGD in the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11; World Health Organization, 2024) and the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, text revision (DSM-5-TR; American Psychiatric Association, 2022) further underscores its clinical importance. The prevalence of this symptom is notable, with evidence suggesting that it might persist for up to two years following bereavement (Maciejewski et al., 2007). In addition, yearning has been found to interact with other symptoms of PGD and exacerbate the overall condition (Maccallum et al., 2017; Malgaroli et al., 2018). Neurobiological studies suggest that yearning in PGD may be linked to the brain’s reward system, indicating potential dysfunction in neural pathways related to attachment and reward (O’Connor et al., 2008). This finding aligns with the conceptualization of PGD as a reward disorder, in which an inability to reconcile loss leads to persistent and maladaptive yearning (Kakarala et al., 2020).

Limitations of self-report instruments

The development of single-item and multi-item measures to assess yearning has garnered considerable attention in bereavement research. For example, single-item measures, such as items from the Inventory of Complicated Grief (ICG; Prigerson et al., 1995) or the Prolonged Grief Disorder-13 (PG-13; Prigerson et al., 2009), are straightforward and easy to administer. However, despite their strong face validity, these measures may oversimplify the complex and multifaceted nature of yearning (Waters, 2023). Conversely, multi-item measures, such as the Yearning in Situations of Loss Scale (YSL; O’Connor & Sussman, 2014) and its short form (YSL-SF; Eisma et al., 2020), provide a more comprehensive evaluation by capturing multiple dimensions of yearning simultaneously. These instruments have consistently demonstrated strong psychometric properties across various loss contexts, including bereavement, romantic breakups, and homesickness (O’Connor & Sussman, 2014; Robinaugh et al., 2016). Moreover, the multi-item approach allows researchers and clinicians to differentiate between cognitive and emotional components of yearning, enhancing diagnostic accuracy and informing targeted intervention strategies (Eisma et al., 2020; Robinaugh et al., 2016).

However, self-report measures are susceptible to biases such as social desirability and recall inaccuracies, which may compromise the validity of research findings (Dang et al., 2020). For example, openly expressing feelings may be perceived as a sign of vulnerability or inadequate self-regulation. This could lead individuals, especially within cultures like the Chinese context that value emotional restraint, to underreport intense experiences of yearning (Zhou, Smith, et al., 2023). Additionally, these instruments often conceptualize yearning as a static condition, thereby overlooking its dynamic nature influenced by memories, triggers, and contexts (Micheli, 2022). Assuming a universally uniform yearning experience also neglects cultural variations in emotional expression and cognitive processing styles (Mazza et al., 2025; Silverman et al., 2021). Similarly, Eisma et al. (2020) highlighted inconsistencies in the factor structure of the YSL-SF between community and treatment-seeking samples, indicating that current self-report instruments might not reliably generalize to different populations. Consequently, there is a pressing need for more nuanced, dynamic, and culturally sensitive methods to assess yearning among bereaved individuals.

Insights from experimental induction paradigms

Experimental approaches designed to induce rumination and worry have been widely employed and validated in psychological research. A conventional method for inducing rumination, developed by Nolen-Hoeksema and Morrow (1993), involves structured prompts that guide participants through repetitive thoughts about past events. This approach is particularly effective at eliciting state rumination, especially regarding unmet personal goals (Michel-Kröhler et al., 2023). Conversely, traditional worry induction methods typically ask participants to focus on personally relevant worrisome topics without providing explicit guidance (Skodzik et al., 2016). Despite their differing forms, both reliably produce negative emotions, with worry primarily eliciting anxiety and rumination primarily eliciting depression (Spinhoven et al., 2018). In addition, these methods are frequently used to examine the causal effects of rumination and worry on outcomes such as physiological changes, attentional biases, and emotional processing (e.g. Grol et al., 2015; McLaughlin et al., 2007; Ottaviani et al., 2016). To facilitate direct comparisons between rumination and worry, Lewis et al. (2019) introduced a structured worry induction method that parallels the rumination induction procedure. This approach effectively induces both rumination and worry while minimizing confounds such as mind-wandering (Lewis et al., 2019).

Yearning shares conceptual similarities with rumination and worry, particularly in its repetitive, self-referential, and counterfactual nature, which is emotionally charged and difficult to disengage from (Kaplan et al., 2018). Inspired by experimental paradigms that induce rumination and worry in depression and anxiety research, we are interested in developing a novel Yearning Induction Task (YIT) to experimentally evoke yearning in bereaved individuals. This task is designed to create a structured yet emotionally evocative experience that closely mirrors the natural cognitive and emotional processes of yearning within a controlled experimental setting. The YIT is grounded in techniques previously validated in related contexts (Lewis et al., 2019). It consists of three components: (1) statements that direct attention towards the deceased; (2) prompts that elicit complex emotional memories; and (3) scenarios that encourage temporal reflection spanning past, present, and future experiences. These elements align with the theoretical conceptualization of yearning as a multifaceted phenomenon (Eisma et al., 2020; Kaplan et al., 2018). Detailed procedures for developing the YIT statements are provided in the Methods section. We posit that the YIT might effectively capture the interplay between cognitive and emotional aspects of yearning by prompting participants to recall specific memories and reflect upon their emotional significance. This experimental approach represents a significant advancement beyond traditional self-report measures, offering a more dynamic and contextually relevant assessment of yearning among bereaved individuals.

Cultural influences on yearning experience

Cultural contexts significantly shape the experience and expression of yearning. For instance, the German concept Sehnsucht, which reflects a deep longing for unattainable or utopian states, is reported to be more prominent among German adults than among those in the US (Scheibe et al., 2011). Similarly, nostalgia, a universally recognized bittersweet emotion closely related to yearning but typically more positive in nature, serves distinct psychological and social functions in different cultures (Sedikides & Wildschut, 2022). Yearning has also been identified as a central feature of maladaptive daydreaming. However, its interpretation differs across diverse cultural contexts, including populations from the US, Italy, Turkey, and the UK (Soffer-Dudek et al., 2021). In bereavement research, findings suggest that yearning is best conceptualized using a two-factor model among treatment-seeking samples in the US, whereas a one-factor model better represents community samples from the Netherlands and Germany (Eisma et al., 2020). It is noteworthy that yearning emerges as a particularly salient symptom among Chinese participants when culturally relevant grief symptoms are incorporated into comparative network analyses of PGD symptoms in Chinese and Swiss German-speaking bereaved individuals (Stelzer, Zhou, Merzhvynska, et al., 2020). The findings collectively emphasize that yearning is a multifaceted phenomenon deeply embedded within specific cultural frameworks.

However, comparative research on yearning between Eastern and Western cultures remains limited, except for recent work by Mazza et al. (2025) that used a single-item measure for comparison. Therefore, to uncover more systematic and nuanced cultural differences, we seek to compare yearning experience between Chinese and Swiss bereaved individuals using a structured experimental paradigm. The cultural underpinnings of these two societies are marked by notable differences. Chinese cultural traditions, heavily influenced by Confucian values and collectivism, place significant emphasis on emotional restraint, strong familial connections, and ancestor worship (Hsu et al., 2009; Shi et al., 2019). In contrast, Swiss culture, shaped by Western individualistic principles, is known to promote open emotional expression and prioritize personal autonomy (Potthoff et al., 2016; Xiu et al., 2017). These fundamental cultural distinctions underscore the necessity of adapting assessment tools to accurately capture the unique cognitive and emotional processes underlying yearning across different cultural contexts.

The present study

Given the central role of yearning in PGD and the limitations of existing assessments, this study aimed to develop and validate a new structured induction task on yearning among bereaved individuals from Switzerland and China. First, we assessed interrater reliability by examining whether raters consistently identified specific emotional and cognitive responses elicited by the YIT. Specifically, raters distinguished between yearning directed towards deceased loved ones and general longing for temporarily absent living individuals. Second, given the exploratory nature of this study, we investigated whether cultural contexts influence emotional and cognitive reactions following experimentally induced yearning, thereby providing initial evidence for the task's validity. Based on established cross-cultural theories (e.g. Markus & Kitayama, 1991; Schwartz, 2006), we expected culturally specific response patterns. Swiss participants, embedded within an individualistic culture that encourages open emotional expression, were anticipated to show significant increases in both negative and positive emotions after the yearning induction compared to a neutral control condition. In contrast, Chinese participants, influenced by collectivistic cultural norms promoting emotional restraint, were expected to exhibit emotional suppression, particularly through decreased positive emotions, rather than notable increases in negative emotions. Additionally, consistent with previous research using similar induction tasks (e.g. Lewis et al., 2019), we hypothesized that state yearning would significantly increase following the yearning induction compared to the control condition in both cultural groups. We further evaluated cross-cultural validity, construct validity, and discriminant validity through analyzing these culturally specific emotional and cognitive response patterns. Finally, to ensure ecological validity and practical relevance of the YIT, we examined task-related indicators such as perceived effort, difficulty, and success in both cultural samples. These insights aim to enhance culturally sensitive assessment methods and guide tailored therapeutic approaches for addressing yearning among diverse bereaved populations.

Method

Participants

Between April and December 2023, participants were recruited through social media, mailing lists, and grief support networks. Eligibility criteria included: (1) current enrollment as a university student; (2) fluency in Chinese or Swiss German; and (3) having experienced the loss of a loved one between 6 months and 5 years prior. Additionally, all Chinese participants were residing in mainland China, and all Swiss participants were residing in Switzerland at the time of the study. International students or individuals living abroad were excluded. The minimum duration of 6 months aligned with ICD-11 guidelines for diagnosing PGD (World Health Organization, 2024), while the maximum limit of 5 years ensured a diverse yet targeted timeframe. Participants not meeting these criteria were excluded. Participants in Switzerland received either course credit or a CHF 10 gift card, whereas Chinese participants were compensated with CNY 30. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Boards of the University of Zurich and Beijing Normal University.

A total of 244 bereaved individuals took part in the online Qualtrics survey. Thirteen participants who did not complete the YIT were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 231 (130 from China; 101 from Switzerland). As shown in Table 1, the proportion of males was higher in the Chinese sample (46.2%) compared to the Swiss sample (19.8%), p < .001. Swiss participants were significantly older (M=22.96,SD=3.48) than Chinese participants (M=21.25,SD=1.62), p < .001. Additionally, Chinese participants reported significantly higher baseline yearning (M=28.55,SD=6.82) and PGD severity scores (M=37.88,SD=10.69) than did their Swiss counterparts (yearning: M=19.93,SD=7.58; PGD: M=23.99,SD=9.75), ps < .001.

Measures

Sociodemographic and loss-related questionnaire

A self-designed questionnaire collected data on participants’ sociodemographic and loss-related information, including gender, age, education level, relationship to the deceased, cause of death, and time since loss. Relationships to the deceased were categorized into three groups based on kinship: immediate family member (i.e. parents, siblings, children, or partners), extended family member (i.e. grandparents, aunts/uncles, cousins), and non-family member (i.e. friends, colleagues, or significant others outside familial relationships).

International Prolonged Grief Disorder Scale (IPGDS)

The IPGDS assesses PGD symptoms according to ICD-11 criteria (Killikelly et al., 2020). It includes a 13-item standard scale and a 20-item cultural supplement, rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘Almost never’) to 5 (‘Always’). The scale has demonstrated strong psychometric properties across cultures and languages (Killikelly & Maercker, 2023). In this study, we used only the standard IPGDS scale, which demonstrated excellent reliability for both the Swiss German (ω = .92) and Chinese (ω = .93) versions.

Yearning in Situations of Loss Scale-Short Form (YSL-SF)

The YSL-SF (Eisma et al., 2020) comprises eight items assessing the frequency of yearning-related thoughts and feelings over the past month on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = ‘Never’ to 5 = ‘Always’). Its psychometric validity has been established across diverse populations (Eisma et al., 2020; O’Connor & Sussman, 2014). Internal consistency in the current study was ω = .91 for the Swiss sample and ω = .92 for the Chinese sample.

State emotion measure (SEM)

A scale was developed to capture momentary emotions related to loss, consisting of five positive (happiness, love, gratitude, hope, relief) and five negative emotions (anger, guilt, sadness, stress, loneliness). Participants rated their emotional state on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = ‘Not at all’ to 4 = ‘Extremely’), administered both pre- and post-induction. Reliability was acceptable to good for both groups across time points. For Chinese participants, omega was .88 for Positive Emotions Subscale at both pre- and post-induction, and .85 (pre) and .81 (post) for the Negative Emotions Subscale. For Swiss participants, omega was .86 (pre) and .89 (post) for Positive Emotions Subscale, and .79 (pre) and .80 (post) for the Negative Emotions Subscale.

State yearning measure (SYM)

Developed specifically for this study, the SYM assesses momentary yearning with five items adapted from the YSL-SF based on their high item-total correlations from the original YSL-SF validation study, in combination with expert panel discussions informed by theoretical considerations (Eisma et al., 2020). Additionally, the wording of these items was adapted to present tense (‘right now … ’) to better align with the experimental context. The full SYM was presented in Appendix 2. Participants rated their current yearning on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = ‘Not at all’ to 6 = ‘Very much’) both before and after induction. Internal consistency was good for both groups at all time points (pre-induction ω = .83 and post-induction ω = .86 for Chinese participants; pre-induction ω = .81, post-induction ω = .84 for the Swiss participants).

Task process measure (TPM)

Participants’ cognitive costs for the induction task were measured using three self-report items adapted from Troy et al. (2018). The items included: (1) perceived effort (‘How hard did you try to follow the task instructions?’), (2) perceived difficulty (‘How difficult did you find it to follow the task instructions?’), and (3) perceived success (‘How successful did you feel in following the task instructions?’). Items were rated on a 9-point Likert scale (1 = ‘Not at all’ to 9 = ‘Completely’).

Yearning induction task (YIT)

The YIT was developed based on established protocols (Lewis et al., 2019) and informed by a content analysis of existing definitions, scales, and theoretical models related to yearning (Eisma et al., 2020; Kaplan et al., 2018). The induction includes five sequential statements designed to progressively deepen participants’ experiences of yearning.

Participants were initially instructed to vividly recall sensory details and relational qualities of the deceased individual. This aimed to explicitly direct their attention towards the object of yearning, as recommended by Lewis et al. (2019). Next, they reflected on a meaningful, positive memory involving the deceased to evoke warm, nostalgic feelings and reinforce the enduring emotional bond (Sedikides et al., 2015). Following this positive recollection, the task shifted towards recalling an unpleasant memory associated with the deceased. This step was intended to capture the emotional complexity and ambivalence inherent in yearning, aligning with contemporary grief literature that identifies yearning as inclusive of negative affect related to unresolved relational dynamics (Keser et al., 2024; Malgaroli et al., 2018; Xu et al., 2025). Subsequently, participants imagined the deceased returning to life in the present, thereby promoting counterfactual thinking and intensifying longing through contemplation of an unattainable alternative reality (Eisma et al., 2021). The task concluded by prompting participants to envision the deceased within a future context. This final step highlighted the loss of anticipated shared experiences, reinforcing the temporal dimension of yearning (Prigerson et al., 2021).

In the control condition, statements were adapted to focus participants’ reflections on a living but temporarily absent individual. This maintained comparable reflective writing and emotional engagement while excluding bereavement-specific elements of yearning. Complete statements and instructions are provided in Appendix 1.

Procedure

After providing informed consent, participants completed baseline self-report measures. They were then randomly assigned to either the yearning or control condition within their respective countries. Participants completed measures of current yearning and emotional states both before and after the induction phase. During the induction, each statement was presented sequentially for two minutes. Participants read each statement, clicked ‘Start,’ and typed their responses into provided text boxes. Upon completion, they were debriefed and given psychoeducational resources and relaxation exercises to address potential emotional distress. The whole procedure lasted approximately 30 min.

Data analyses

All analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2; R Core Team, 2024), with statistical significance set at p < .05. Independent t-tests and chi-square tests were conducted to compare demographic and baseline characteristics between the Chinese and Swiss groups. Internal consistency was assessed using McDonald’s omega (Hayes & Rockwood, 2020). Reliability of the YIT was evaluated via intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC), with ICC > .75 indicating good reliability (Cicchetti, 1994).

Prior to the main analyses, assumptions of normality, homogeneity of variances, sphericity, and potential outliers were examined and addressed accordingly. No extreme outliers were identified. Normality was evaluated through visual inspection of Q-Q plots, indicating approximately normal distributions within each cell. Homogeneity of variances was confirmed by Levene’s test (all ps > .05). Sphericity was examined using Mauchly's test, and corrections were applied where necessary (e.g. Greenhouse–Geisser adjustments). Analyses of covariance (ANCOVAs) were conducted to examine main and interaction effects of culture (China vs. Switzerland), induction condition (yearning vs. control), and time (pre- vs. post-induction), as well as relevant interaction terms, while controlling for gender, loss type, cause of death, baseline yearning, and baseline PGD severity. Significant interactions were further explored with univariate ANCOVAs and paired-samples t-tests using Bonferroni corrections for multiple comparisons. Interaction plots were used to visually illustrate the nature and direction of effects across cultural groups and conditions.

Results

Manipulation check

To assess the effectiveness of the manipulation, two independent raters (native speakers of Chinese and German, respectively) were recruited from each participating culture. Raters underwent a standardized training session led by an expert researcher, which included instruction on the operational definitions of yearning, guided practice with coding sample responses, group discussions to resolve discrepancies, and independent coding practice. Both raters were blinded to the study hypotheses. They reviewed each participant’s written responses to the five induction prompts and rated the extent to which the participant exhibited corresponding features (e.g. vividness, positivity, negativity, focus on the present, and focus on the future) on a five-point Likert scale ranging from ‘Not at all’ to ‘Almost entirely’. To maintain the integrity of the blinding process, raters were not provided with the original induction prompts, following methodological practices from prior research (e.g. LeMoult & Joormann, 2014). After coding each of the five individual responses, the raters provided a final categorical judgment about which condition (deceased vs. living) best reflected the participant’s yearning experiences. For reliability assessment, the first author independently coded 33% of the participants’ responses. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) between the blinded raters was calculated to evaluate interrater reliability, yielding an ICC of .81, F(129,258)=16.45,p<.001, 95%CI[.71,.87] for the Chinese raters, and an ICC of .78, F(100,200)=12.56,p<.001,95%CI[.69,.84] for the Swiss raters. According to established benchmarks (Cicchetti, 1994), this represents good interrater reliability, confirming that the induction conditions effectively elicited distinct types of yearning across cultures. These findings suggest that the structured induction procedure consistently evoked yearning among participants.

Effects of induction on emotion

Pre- and post-induction differences on emotion valence

A 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 ANCOVA was conducted to examine the effects of culture (China vs. Switzerland), induction condition (yearning vs. control), time (pre-induction vs. post-induction), and valence (positive vs. negative) on emotion scores, while controlling for gender, loss type, cause of death, baseline yearning, and baseline PGD severity. There were no significant main effects of culture, induction condition, time, or valence, all ps > .05. However, a significant interaction between culture and valence emerged, F(1,221)=16.69,p<.001, ηp2=.07, as well as a three-way interaction among culture, time, and valence, F(1,221)=6.91,p= .009,ηp2=.03. Most importantly, the four-way interaction among culture, induction condition, time, and valence was significant, F(1,221)=8.90,p=.003,ηp2=.04. These findings indicate that the pattern of emotion scores across time and valence differed as a function of both cultural background and induction condition.

Therefore, separate 2 (culture) × 2 (induction condition) × 2 (time) ANCOVAs for each valence group were conducted. There was a statistically significant three-way interaction between culture, induction condition, and time for the positive emotion group, F(1,221)=11.05,p= .001,ηp2=.05, but not for the negative emotion group, F(1,221)=2.09,p=.150,ηp2=.01. However, there was a significant two-way interaction between culture and time for the negative emotion group, F(1,221)= 8.72,p=.003,ηp2=.04. The simple main effect of time on emotion scores was statistically significant in the Swiss control condition for both the positive emotion group, F(1,43)=4.42,p=.041,ηp2=.09, and the negative emotion group, F(1, 43) = 4.19, p=.047,ηp2=.09 as well as in the Swiss yearning condition negative emotion group, (F(1,44)=6.03,p=.018,ηp2=.12.

Pairwise comparisons between pre- and post-induction time points, with Bonferroni correction, revealed a significant decrease in positive emotions in the Swiss control condition (t(221)=−2.19, p=.030,95%CI[−0.28,−0.01]) and the Chinese yearning condition (t(221)=−2.13,p=.034, 95%CI[−0.28,−0.01]), while the Chinese control condition showed a marginal increase (t(221)= 1.94,p=.053,95%CI[−0.00,0.26]). No significant change was observed in the Swiss yearning condition (p=.435). For negative emotions, significant decreases were found in the Chinese control condition (t(221)=−2.17,p=.031,95%CI[−0.28,−0.01]), while the Swiss yearning condition (t(221)=2.18, p=.030,95%CI[0.01,0.28]) and the Swiss control condition (t(221)=1.98,p=.049, 95% CI[0.00,0.27]) showed increases. No significant change occurred in the Chinese yearning condition (p=.689). These results highlight cultural differences in emotional responses to yearning induction. Effects of induction on momentary yearning

A 2 × 2 × 2 ANCOVA was conducted, with culture (China vs. Switzerland) and induction condition (yearning vs. control) as between-subjects variables, and time (pre-induction vs. post-induction) as a within-subject variable, to assess group differences in momentary yearning scores. The main effect of culture was significant, F(1,221)=11.77,p=.001,ηp2=.05. However, the three-way interaction among culture, condition, and time was not significant, p=.760. This suggests that the combined effects of culture, condition, and time on momentary yearning responses did not differ significantly across groups.

Discussion

The study of yearning is an emerging focus in research on PGD, along with the psychology of emotions and cultural comparisons (e.g. Mazza et al., 2025). To address critical methodological gaps in existing assessments of yearning, the present study developed and validated a novel experimental induction task for yearning. Specifically, the research had two primary objectives: (1) to examine the reliability of the YIT in differentiating yearning for deceased loved ones from general longing, as well as its cross-cultural applicability among Swiss and Chinese bereaved individuals, and (2) to explore how cultural context influences emotional and cognitive responses elicited by experimentally induced yearning, providing initial insights into the construct, discriminant, cross-cultural, and ecological validity of the YIT. Results indicated good interrater reliability, confirming the task’s effectiveness in eliciting yearning in both cultural contexts. Importantly, significant cultural differences emerged in emotional responses to the induction task. When induced to yearn for deceased loved ones, Swiss participants predominantly experienced increases negative emotions (e.g. sadness, anger, loneliness), whereas Chinese participants primarily showed reductions in positive emotions (e.g. happiness). Conversely, yearning for temporarily separated living individuals led Swiss participants to report declines in positive emotions such as gratitude and love, while Chinese participants reported increased happiness. Task-related indicators, including participants’ perceived effort, difficulty, and success across both cultural samples, further supported the ecological validity and practical relevance of the YIT. However, despite rigorous theoretical grounding and structured procedures, the construct validity specific to yearning was only partially supported, as the induction did not significantly alter momentary yearning ratings. These findings suggest potential universal and culturally specific emotional experiences related to yearning, highlighting the nuanced role of cultural contexts in grief-related emotional processes.

A notable strength of this study was the use of a controlled experimental paradigm specifically designed to induce yearning. This approach provided preliminary methodological advancement over traditional self-report instruments. Established self-report tools, such as the YSL and its short form (Eisma et al., 2020; O’Connor & Sussman, 2014), frequently neglect the dynamic and nuanced qualities of yearning and are prone to social desirability bias (Dang et al., 2020). In contrast, the structured induction method elicited immediate behavioural expressions through participants’ narrative responses, thereby minimizing biases inherent in retrospective measures. The high interrater reliability further supported the effectiveness of this approach, highlighting its ability to evoke reflective states involving vivid recall of personal memories and emotions associated with yearning. Previous laboratory-based grief research has used visual stimuli (e.g. photographs of the deceased) to provoke intense grief reactions (e.g. O’Connor et al., 2008; Schneck et al., 2019). However, such stimuli may foster passive emotional engagement or mind-wandering, thereby hindering sustained cognitive processing (Akshita et al., 2015; Mitchell & Greening, 2012). Conversely, structured induction tasks encourage active cognitive engagement and deeper introspection (Arnold et al., 2017). This allows participants to become fully immersed in the personal experience of yearning. This study also extended existing laboratory paradigms that examine emotional and cognitive processes associated with grief. For example, tasks like the Grief Recall Task (Karl et al., 2018; Palitsky et al., 2023) have broadly assessed grief through recall of loss-related memories. However, the task developed in this study uniquely focused on yearning by systematically guiding participants through both positive and negative autobiographical memories, as well as counterfactual imaginings of reunions with the deceased. Furthermore, the structure of the YIT parallels certain elements of the Autobiographical Memory Task (AMT) and Future Event Task (FET; Robinaugh & McNally, 2013). While these tasks separately explore autobiographical memory specificity and prospective cognition, the YIT combines these dimensions into a unified approach. This integration represents a novel methodological advancement for capturing the dynamic interplay between past-oriented memories and future-oriented counterfactual thoughts that are central to the experience of yearning.

Moreover, the structured induction approach aligns with established paradigms used to study other repetitive cognitive processes, such as rumination, worry, and nostalgia (e.g. Lewis et al., 2019; Michel-Kröhler et al., 2023; Sedikides et al., 2015). While rumination induction tasks focus on repetitive negative thoughts linked to depressive symptoms, and worry induction tasks emphasize anticipatory concerns characteristic of anxiety (Samtani & Moulds, 2017), nostalgia induction typically evokes bittersweet but primarily positive emotions associated with past memories without the attachment-driven counterfactual elements inherent in yearning (van Tilburg, 2023). The yearning induction task uniquely integrates past-focused memories, counterfactual thoughts, and future-oriented desires about the deceased, thereby eliciting complex bittersweet emotional states driven by attachment loss (Kaplan et al., 2018). Additionally, by incorporating a control condition involving longing for temporarily absent living individuals, the task effectively distinguished grief-specific yearning from general longing. Bereavement-related yearning frequently involves complex emotions, including negative affect related to unresolved relational dynamics, distinguishing it from simpler forms of longing (Malgaroli et al., 2018; Xu et al., 2025). Research indicates that individuals with secure attachments may experience yearning predominantly linked to positive memories, whereas those with insecure attachments may experience yearning interwoven with distress and regret, reflecting unresolved relational dynamics (Fu et al., 2020). Despite differences in the object of yearning, both conditions effectively elicited yearning-like responses. This suggests the potential applicability of this structured induction paradigm beyond bereavement contexts. This distinction provides preliminary evidence supporting the discriminant validity of the YIT as a tool specifically targeting bereavement-related yearning, which may enhance the methodological rigour of using control conditions to manage potential confounders (Hallford et al., 2021).

Despite the exploratory nature of this study, the findings revealed a complex interplay among cultural background, induction condition, time, and emotional responses. Specifically, among Swiss participants, yearning for deceased loved ones significantly increased negative emotions such as loneliness, anger, and sadness without altering overall positive emotions or specific positive emotion types. This pattern suggests the emotional compartmentalization characteristic of yearning, whereby positive and negative emotions can coexist (Bonanno et al., 2002). The heightened loneliness aligns with prior research indicating that emotional loneliness is pronounced among Western widowed populations (Szabó et al., 2020). Anger observed in this context likely stems from perceived injustice or abandonment linked to unresolved grief, intensifying emotional distress (Harrison et al., 2022; Sveen et al., 2018). Importantly, these negative emotions might also be exacerbated in Swiss contexts, where social environments provide limited buffering against intense affect, increasing vulnerability to prolonged yearning (Xiu et al., 2017). Additionally, neural mechanisms underlying yearning involve activation of both reward and negative-affect brain regions, which may heighten negative emotions (e.g. sadness) while inhibiting positive emotional processing (Bryant et al., 2021; Kakarala et al., 2020). In the control condition, Swiss participants exhibited decreased positive emotions, notably love and gratitude, alongside increased overall negative emotions. This response aligns with the concept of ambiguous loss, a state characterized by uncertainty and lack of closure, which is associated with anxiety, frustration, and diminished positive affect (Testoni et al., 2020). Thus, anticipating future reunions during temporary separations might paradoxically reduce immediate positive emotions and amplify negative reactions among Swiss individuals. Collectively, these findings emphasize that yearning – whether directed towards deceased or temporarily separated individuals – is associated with intensified negative emotional responses in individualistic Western cultures, which value emotional authenticity and open expression of distress (Potthoff et al., 2016; Scherer & Wallbott, 1994).

In contrast, Chinese participants showed subtler emotional shifts during the yearning condition. Specifically, they exhibited a marked reduction in positive emotions, particularly happiness, without corresponding increases in negative emotions. This differs notably from induction tasks targeting rumination or worry, which explicitly amplify negative affect (Samtani & Moulds, 2017). The present findings suggest that among Chinese bereaved individuals, yearning for the deceased primarily affects positive rather than negative emotional states. This pattern likely reflects Confucian cultural values emphasizing emotional moderation and collective harmony (Kim & Sasaki, 2024; Schwartz, 2006), which encourage suppression of explicit negative emotional expressions during intense grief (Hsu et al., 2009; Shi et al., 2019). The absence of pronounced negative emotional fluctuations aligns with previous research indicating that East Asian cultural contexts foster dialectical emotional approaches, characterized by internal acceptance of negative affect alongside external emotional restraint (Miyamoto & Ryff, 2011; Nagulendran & Jobson, 2020; Song et al., 2024). Additionally, the significant decrease in happiness among Chinese participants may reflect culturally influenced tendencies to avoid overt displays of positive emotions during mourning due to norms linking such displays to disruptions of social cohesion or disrespect towards grief processes (Liang et al., 2024; Zhou, Zhao, et al., 2023). Interestingly, Chinese participants in the control condition reported increased positive emotions, notably happiness, and significant reductions in overall negative emotions, contrasting sharply with Swiss participants’ emotional responses. This optimism is likely rooted in cultural narratives emphasizing hope, enduring relational connections, and the normalization of temporary separations (Ruby et al., 2012; Sai et al., 2016). These culturally embedded narratives might foster resilience by strengthening collective identity and social bonds, even without physical proximity (Liu et al., 2016; Mobbs et al., 2021). Furthermore, although prior research highlights guilt as a critical emotion linked to PGD severity (Joa & Newberg, 2023), the current induction task did not significantly alter guilt in either cultural groups. This discrepancy suggests that the complexity of yearning experiences might overshadow guilt-related emotional reflections (Dulberg et al., 2025).

Despite these methodological strengths, the construct validity of yearning was somewhat limited, as the induction did not significantly influence momentary yearning ratings. This result suggests that baseline differences in yearning intensity between cultural groups may be relatively stable and largely unaffected by short-term experimental manipulations. However, the absence of significant interaction effects does not necessarily imply that the yearning induction failed entirely. According to Lorah (2020), a lack of interaction effects in the presence of significant main effects may reflect differential responsiveness to manipulations due to baseline group differences or boundary conditions (e.g. pre-existing levels of yearning), rather than indicating manipulation failure. In addition, given the profound roots of yearning in long-term attachment bonds, brief manipulations, such as the 10-minute induction utilized in this study, might elicit immediate emotional responses without substantially influencing the underlying cognitive state of yearning (Paulk et al., 2019). This supports prior evidence indicating that momentary emotional reactions can fluctuate independently of more enduring cognitive states (Huggins et al., 2019). For instance, individuals might experience transient emotions such as sadness or happiness without corresponding changes in their cognitive appraisal or awareness of these feelings. Prior research also highlights that emotionally induced experiences require adequate stimulus intensity to elicit meaningful responses (Roberts & Levenson, 2006). Consequently, participants who did not strongly identify with the induced yearning state might have provided emotional assessments reflecting cognitive disengagement, resulting in minimal variation in yearning scores across conditions. However, these interpretations should be considered with caution and require further investigation. Importantly, these findings align with existing cross-cultural research on yearning and grief. Consistent with Stelzer, Zhou, Maercker, et al. (2020), Chinese bereaved individuals in this study also reported stronger yearning and more severe prolonged-grief symptoms than their Swiss counterparts. This suggests that cultural predispositions may enhance grief intensity. Similarly, Zhou, Zhao, et al. (2023) showed that traditional mourning practices and existential beliefs distinctively shape grief experiences among Chinese bereaved, potentially amplifying yearning and other PGD symptoms. These findings reflect culturally rooted norms in Chinese culture that emphasize enduring emotional bonds with the deceased and internalized emotional expression (Joshanloo & Weijers, 2014).

Assessment of task-related cognitive demands revealed moderate and comparable levels of perceived difficulty, accompanied by consistently high ratings of effort and perceived success across both cultural groups. These findings provide further evidence that the structured induction task is both appropriate and effective. These results closely align with those reported in previous experimental research and demonstrate acceptable ecological validity (e.g. Troy et al., 2018). Interestingly, within the control condition, Swiss participants reported higher perceived effort relative to their Chinese counterparts. This difference may reflect greater cognitive engagement when processing temporary separation scenarios, potentially driven by Western cultural expectations that encourage explicit emotional involvement and introspection (Potthoff et al., 2016). By contrast, Chinese participants in the yearning condition reported higher levels of perceived success compared to Swiss participants. This pattern might reflect culturally distinct norms related to comfort and acceptance of structured introspective tasks. For example, Swiss participants might hold heightened expectations for emotional catharsis or explicit emotional insight, consistent with Western cultural values that prioritize emotional authenticity, self-reflection, and emotional expressiveness (Scherer & Wallbott, 1994).

Clinical implications

The findings of this study offer several important clinical implications. First, the good reliability of the YIT supports its potential use as a valuable clinical tool for assessing yearning in diverse populations. Integrating the YIT into clinical settings might improve the identification and therapeutic targeting of yearning in PGD interventions, thereby enhancing personalized care across culturally diverse populations (Eisma et al., 2020; Stelzer, Zhou, Maercker, et al., 2020). Second, the emotionally diverse responses evoked by the YIT underscore the need for culturally sensitive interventions that acknowledge and accommodate distinctive grieving patterns within specific cultural groups. For instance, emotion regulation techniques might prove particularly advantageous for Swiss bereaved individuals, who frequently encounter heightened negative emotions (Knowles et al., 2021). Conversely, interventions targeting Chinese individuals might more effectively prioritize re-establishing positive emotions and maintaining culturally appropriate connections with the deceased (Chentsova-Dutton et al., 2010). Moreover, acknowledging the interplay between cultural norms and specific grief expressions has the potential to enhance therapeutic effectiveness by enabling clinicians to build stronger connections with clients and provide culturally sensitive support (Zhou, Wei, et al., 2023).

Limitations and future direction

Despite the strengths of this study, several limitations warrant consideration. Firstly, demographic factors may have confounded the results, potentially obscuring genuine cultural influences. Specifically, higher baseline yearning and PGD scores among Chinese participants may reflect inherent demographic or clinical disparities rather than purely cultural distinctions. Prior evidence suggests repetitive thinking patterns intensify grief symptoms (Eisma & Lenferink, 2023). This possibly leads to elevated yearning responses in Chinese participants compared to Swiss participants, whose cultural norms may encourage less intense mourning responses. As emphasized by Miller and Chapman (2001), a critical limitation of employing ANCOVA is that statistical adjustment for covariates does not inherently create equivalence between naturally unequal groups. Thus, observed group differences cannot be confidently attributed solely to cultural factors, given underlying demographic and clinical disparities. Future research should carefully match demographic characteristics and loss-related variables across samples, thereby isolating cultural factors more effectively (Matsumoto & Van De Vijver, 2010). Additionally, investigating yearning responses in clinically diagnosed PGD populations might clarify the clinical implications of these findings.

Secondly, methodological limitations include the complexity and potential interpretative ambiguity of the YIT. While capturing multiple dimensions of yearning, its complexity may hinder clear interpretation. Future research should refine or develop simplified comparative induction tasks to enhance methodological clarity and interpretability. Additionally, the incorporation of negative relational memories diverges from traditional conceptualizations of yearning, which emphasize positive nostalgia. Empirical studies assessing the incremental validity of including negative memories in yearning inductions are needed to establish clearer methodological guidelines.

Thirdly, ecological validity constraints arise from both the laboratory-based induction setting and the immediate assessment of emotional responses. While the controlled laboratory setting of the YIT allows for standardization, it may inadequately capture spontaneous yearning experienced in daily life. Future studies employing ecological momentary assessment (EMA) methodologies could advance understanding by measuring yearning in naturalistic settings (Fritz et al., 2024). Moreover, the focus on immediate emotional reactions limits insight into longitudinal dynamics. Longitudinal research is essential to explore how yearning evolves over time, informing its long-term effects on grief trajectories and psychological adjustment (e.g. Smith & Ehlers, 2020).

Fourthly, the generalizability of findings is limited by the high proportion of participants grieving distant relatives or non-relatives. This restricts the applicability of the findings to normative bereavement experiences among college-aged individuals. Replication studies should target individuals experiencing the loss of close relatives to enhance ecological relevance. Additionally, the study did not directly assess participants’ emotional closeness or intensity of relationships with the deceased. These factors may vary substantially across cultures. Future studies should explicitly measure these dimensions to better interpret cross-cultural differences in yearning.

Finally, future research should broaden cultural diversity to strengthen generalizability and advance understanding of yearning as a globally diverse phenomenon (Hilberdink et al., 2023). Furthermore, integrating physiological and neurobiological measures with behavioural assessments represents a promising direction. For example, employing telemetric heartbeat monitors to assess heart rate variability post-induction could objectively measure physiological responses (Grol et al., 2015). Similarly, EEG monitoring of brainwave changes may elucidate neurobiological mechanisms underlying yearning across cultural contexts (Kakarala et al., 2020). Such multidimensional approaches may enhance culturally sensitive assessment and clinical interventions addressing the complex, multifaceted nature of yearning.

Conclusion

The present study provides preliminary evidence for the assessment of yearning by introducing and validating a novel yearning induction task among Swiss and Chinese bereaved individuals. The findings indicated good interrater reliability, thereby supporting the task's effectiveness in eliciting yearning across both cultural groups. Significant cultural differences emerged in emotional responses following yearning induction. Swiss participants predominantly experienced heightened negative emotions (e.g. sadness, loneliness, anger) when yearning was induced for deceased loved ones. This finding is consistent with cultural norms that promote emotional expressiveness. Conversely, Chinese participants primarily showed reductions in positive emotions (e.g. happiness) without corresponding increases in negative affect, aligning with cultural values that emphasize emotional moderation and dialectical emotional processing. Additionally, responses to yearning for temporarily separated individuals differed between cultures, with Swiss participants experiencing decreased positive emotions (e.g. gratitude, love) and heightened overall negative emotions, whereas Chinese participants reported increased happiness. Despite rigorous theoretical grounding, the construct validity specifically related to yearning was only partially supported, as the induction did not significantly alter momentary yearning ratings. Clinically, these results highlight the need for culturally sensitive interventions for yearning experiences. Emotion-regulation techniques targeting negative emotions may benefit Swiss individuals, while interventions for Chinese individuals might focus on enhancing positive emotional states. Future research should incorporate ecological, longitudinal, and neurophysiological methods to further advance culturally tailored assessments and interventions for yearning.
