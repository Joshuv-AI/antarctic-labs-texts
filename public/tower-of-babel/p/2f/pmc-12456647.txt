Real-time ultrasound-guided lung recruitment maneuver for atelectasis treatment after one lung ventilation
Authors: Miki Misawa, Tanat Chotijarumaneewong, Koichi Yoshinaga, Akihiro Suzuki
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Point-of-care ultrasound is emerging as a valuable tool for guiding lung recruitment maneuver.[1] With institutional review board approval and patient consent, we present a case demonstrating the utility of real-time ultrasound guidance for managing postthoracoscopic atelectasis.

A 73-year-old woman with a medical history of hypertension underwent thoracoscopic segmentectomy for left upper lobe lung cancer. The surgery was conducted in the right lateral decubitus position, with one-lung ventilation facilitated by a double-lumen tube. There was no oxygenation problem observed during the surgery. Postoperative supine chest X-ray revealed decreased radiographic opacity in the right upper lung field accompanied by rightward tracheal deviation [Figure 1a]. Point-of-care lung ultrasound examination revealed extensive subpleural consolidation in the right anterior chest region (Zone R1), indicative of atelectasis [Figure 1b]. After bronchoscopic removal of secretions from the right upper lobe bronchus, recruitment maneuver only for right lung was performed using anesthetic apparatus program (GE HealthCare, Vital Capacity, pressure hold: 30 cmH2O, hold time: 10 seconds). The recruitment maneuvers were performed twice until ultrasound confirmation of atelectasis resolution [Figure 1c and e]. Following anesthesia emergence and successful extubation, follow-up chest X-ray in the ICU demonstrated significant radiographic improvement in the right upper lung field’s transparency, with resolution of atelectasis [Figure 1d]. The patient’s postoperative recovery was uneventful, and she was discharged on postoperative day 5.

Perioperative imaging. a: Postoperative chest X-ray showing right upper lung atelectasis. b: Ultrasound confirming subpleural consolidation. c: Persistent consolidation after initial treatment. d: Improved aeration on postrecruitment X-ray. e: Minimal residual consolidation on final ultrasound

Perioperative pulmonary complications, including atelectasis, pneumothorax, and aspiration pneumonia, are associated with high mortality rates, necessitating prompt detection and appropriate management.[2] The effectiveness of the lung recruitment maneuver can be evaluated through two main approaches: direct lung imaging and clinical/physiological monitoring. Direct lung imaging includes computed tomography, chest radiography, and electrical impedance tomography, while clinical and physiological monitoring encompasses pressure-volume loops and arterial blood gas analysis.[3] However, these modalities often present practical limitations in the perioperative setting due to their complexity, cost, or spatial constraints.

Lung ultrasound offers advantages in guiding recruitment maneuver through its capacity for real-time feedback. During the recruitment process, ultrasound can assess alveolar expansion through the evaluation of artifacts such as A-lines, allowing clinicians to immediately assess the effectiveness of their interventions and adjust the recruitment pressure accordingly. This immediate feedback feature is particularly valuable in thoracic surgery, where maintaining an optimal balance between effective recruitment and avoiding excessive airway pressure is crucial for preventing potential complications such as anastomotic leakage. Previous studies have demonstrated that ultrasound-guided recruitment maneuver, leveraging this real-time feedback capability, achieve superior outcomes compared to conventional manual inflation technique in both thoracic and laparoscopic gynecological surgeries.[45]

From a practical standpoint, lung ultrasound’s bedside applicability, noninvasive nature, and absence of radiation exposure make it particularly well-suited for perioperative use. The technique requires minimal setup time and can be readily repeated throughout the perioperative period, allowing for continuous monitoring and adjustment of recruitment strategies.

In our case, lung ultrasound facilitated both the confirmation of atelectasis in the nonoperative lung and guided the subsequent recruitment maneuver, leading to the successful resolution of the condition. This case highlights the practical utility of real-time ultrasound guidance for lung recruitment maneuver in postthoracoscopic atelectasis.
