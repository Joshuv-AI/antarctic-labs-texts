Osteolectin/CLEC11A‐Related Signaling Molecules in Periodontitis and Peri‐Implantitis
Authors: Gözde Işıker Kara, Feyzanur Caldiran, Hatice Balci Yuce, Busenur Sahin, Ercan Cacan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivePeriodontal and peri‐implant tissues differ histologically and biomechanically, which may be reflected in local osteogenic and mechanosensitive signaling. This exploratory cross‐sectional study aimed to compare expression profiles of Osteolectin (CLEC11A) and related signaling molecules across periodontal health, peri‐implant health, periodontitis, and peri‐implantitis.MethodsThere were four groups: Periodontal Health (PH), Peri‐Implant Health (PIH), Periodontitis (P), and Peri‐Implantitis (PID). CLEC11A, integrin alpha‐11 (ITGA11), glycogen synthase kinase‐3β (GSK‐3β), and piezo‐type mechanosensitive ion channel component‐1 (PIEZO1) levels in gingival/peri‐implant crevicular fluids (GCF/PICF) were assessed using ELISA. CLEC11A, ITGA11, GSK‐3β, β‐catenin, runt‐related transcription factor‐2 (RUNX2), and PIEZO1 expressions in gingival and peri‐implant mucosa tissues were assessed using qRT‐PCR.ResultsGCF/PICF CLEC11A levels differed significantly among the groups, with higher levels in P and PID than in PH. Similarly, ITGA11 expression was significantly higher in P and PID relative to PH. β‐catenin expression varied significantly, with higher levels in PIH and P than in PH. Additionally, RUNX2 expression differed significantly, with higher expression in PIH, P, and PID than in PH. Furthermore, PIEZO1 expression was significantly higher in P compared to PIH.ConclusionWithin the limitations of this study, CLEC11A‐related signals appear to be differentially associated with periodontal and peri‐implant inflammatory conditions.

Introduction

Periodontitis and peri‐implantitis are both chronic inflammatory diseases with a common multifactorial etiology that leads to destruction of the supporting alveolar bone (Heitz‐Mayfield and Lang 2010; Papapanou et al. 2018). Peri‐implantitis causes rapid tissue destruction and a dense inflammatory infiltrate dominated by T lymphocytes (Bullon et al. 2004; Easter et al. 2025) with critical histopathological differences between the two diseases, as periodontal and peri‐implant tissues differ in many respects (Heitz‐Mayfield and Lang 2010; Schwarz et al. 2018). The absence of periodontal ligament around the implant leads to decreased vascular support, altered wound healing, and altered tactile sensitivity (Emecen‐Huja et al. 2013; Ivanovski and Lee 2018; Jacobs and van Steenberghe 1994), and bone tissue is also expected to respond biomechanically differently to occlusal forces (Kim et al. 2005).

Bone is a unique tissue that adapts to changing mechanical, biochemical, and neural signals by remodeling (Lafage‐Proust et al. 2015), which involves the removal of old or damaged bone by osteoclasts, followed by its formation by osteoblasts (Feng and McDonald 2011). Osteoblasts produce new bone throughout life for bone remodeling and repair. The primary source of new osteoblasts is leptin receptor‐expressing (LepR+) stromal cells found in adult bone marrow. These cells are normally quiescent but proliferate to regenerate bone after injury (Zhou et al. 2014).

A growth factor synthesized by LepR+ bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs), osteoblasts, osteocytes, and hypertrophic chondrocytes has been identified. This growth factor is osteolectin (CLEC11A), the member A glycoprotein from the C‐type lectin domain (Shen et al. 2019; Yue et al. 2016). Osteolectin stimulates osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs and promotes bone formation (Shen et al. 2019). It may be a candidate molecule for age‐related osteoporosis and fracture healing (Shen et al. 2019; Yue et al. 2016). Binding to its receptor, integrin subunit alpha 11 (ITGA11), activates the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway via glycogen synthase kinase‐3β (GSK‐3β), which subsequently induces the expression of osteoblast‐associated gene transcripts, including runt‐related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) (Logan and Nusse 2004; Shen et al. 2019). Additionally, there is evidence that mechanical stimulation‐mediated bone formation by osteolectin is mediated by the mechanosensitive piezo type mechanosensitive ion channel component 1 (PIEZO1) (Ehnert et al. 2021; Shen et al. 2021). CLEC11A and PIEZO1 are potential key molecules responsible for the remodeling process in bones exposed to mechanical stimulation.

The presence of leptin (Lep) and leptin receptor (LepR) in human periodontal ligament cells, gingival fibroblasts, and gingival epithelial cells has been previously demonstrated (Li et al. 2014). In animal models, Lep and LepR are upregulated in inflamed periodontal tissues. LepR+ cells contribute to newly formed alveolar bone and are required for periodontal homeostasis in adulthood (Li et al. 2015; Xu et al. 2024). Moreover, LepR+ periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs) proliferate following injury and can differentiate into osteoblasts, cementoblasts, and fibroblasts in vivo, while physiological mechanical loading is necessary to maintain LepR+ PDLSCs through PIEZO1‐mediated mechanotransduction (Zhang et al. 2023).

Although decreased osteolectin expression has been reported in gingival tissue from individuals with periodontitis compared with healthy controls (Yin et al. 2024), clinical evidence integrating osteolectin with mechanotransduction and osteogenesis‐related pathways across periodontal and peri‐implant settings remains limited. Because periodontal and peri‐implant tissues differ in their structural, biological, and biomechanical characteristics, osteolectin‐related signaling may also vary between these environments (Berglundh et al. 2018; Delgado‐Ruiz et al. 2019; Ivanovski and Lee 2018). Therefore, the present exploratory cross‐sectional study aimed to evaluate mechanotransduction, Wnt signaling, and osteogenesis‐related processes through CLEC11A and related biomarkers—CLEC11A, ITGA11, GSK‐3β, PIEZO1, β‐catenin, and RUNX2—in gingival/peri‐implant crevicular fluids and corresponding gingival/peri‐implant tissues under health and disease conditions.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

This cross‐sectional observational study included 80 individuals recruited from the Department of Periodontology at the Faculty of Dentistry, Tokat Gaziosmanpaşa University (Tokat, Turkey) between May 2023 and July 2024. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Tokat Gaziosmanpaşa University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Project number: 22‐KAEK‐138). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Participants were eligible if they were 25–70 years of age, systemically healthy, had at least 24 functional teeth, met the clinical criteria of one of the study groups, and provided informed consent. Exclusion criteria included a history of periodontal surgery within the previous 6 months; use of immunosuppressive, anti‐inflammatory, antiresorptive, antibiotic, antihypertensive, or hormonal medications within the last 3 months; current smoking or use of tobacco/alcohol; pregnancy/lactation; undergoing orthodontic therapy; and having clenching/bruxism.

Periodontal and peri‐implant diagnoses were established according to the 2017 World Workshop Classification of Periodontal and Peri‐Implant Diseases and Conditions (Caton et al. 2018). Based on these criteria, participants were allocated to four groups: Periodontal Health (PH, n = 20): No clinical signs of inflammation, bleeding on probing (BOP) < 10%, and probing depth (PD) ≤ 3 mm (Lang and Bartold 2018).Peri‐Implant Health (PIH, n = 20): No clinical signs of inflammation, no profuse BOP, PD ≤ 5 mm, and no further bone loss following initial healing after the first year of functional loading (Renvert et al. 2018).Periodontitis—Stage III Grade C (P, n = 20): Interdental clinical attachment loss (CAL) at the site of greatest loss ≥ 5 mm, radiographic bone loss extending to the mid/apical third of the root, ≤ 4 teeth lost due to periodontitis, and complexity features (PD ≥ 6 mm, vertical bone loss ≥ 3 mm, Class II/III furcation defect, ridge defects) with rapid progression (bone loss/age ratio > 1) (Tonetti et al. 2018). The determination of both stage and grade was based on the most severely affected diagnostic sites (Tonetti et al. 2018). While smoking and systemic diseases, such as diabetes mellitus, were exclusion criteria, the grading of periodontitis was assessed using parameters of disease progression, specifically radiographic bone loss relative to age, rather than grade modifiers.Peri‐Implantitis (PID, n = 20): Inflamed peri‐implant mucosa with profuse bleeding on probing (BOP) and/or suppuration, accompanied by radiographic evidence of bone loss occurring after the first year of functional loading. In the absence of baseline radiographs, bone loss ≥ 3 mm in conjunction with BOP and a probing depth (PD) ≥ 6 mm was used as the diagnostic criterion (Renvert et al. 2018).

Clinical Examination

All clinical measurements were recorded by a single calibrated examiner at six sites per tooth/implant (mesiobuccal, midbuccal, distobuccal, mesiolingual, midlingual, distolingual). The assessed clinical parameters included plaque index (PI), gingival index (GI), PD, BOP, and CAL (Ainamo and Bay 1975; Löe and Silness 1963; Silness and Löe 1964). A Williams periodontal probe (Hu‐Friedy, Chicago, IL, USA) was used for teeth, whereas a plastic periodontal probe (Hu‐Friedy, Chicago, IL, USA) was used for peri‐implant sites.

For each group, one tooth/implant located in the posterior region and had antagonist teeth, showing group characteristics was selected as the sample tooth/implant. Samples were obtained from the deepest pockets of the sample tooth/implant in periodontitis and peri‐implantitis groups.

Collection of Gingival and Peri‐Implant Crevicular Fluids

Gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) and peri‐implant crevicular fluid (PICF) samples were collected at least one week after clinical evaluation using standardized paper strips (Periopaper; Proflow Inc., Amityville, NY, USA). The sampling area was isolated with cotton rolls, and supragingival plaque was gently removed prior to sampling. The strips were then inserted into the sulcus until mild resistance was encountered and retained for 30 s (Awad et al. 2025). Six strips were collected per participant. Contaminated samples (saliva/blood) were excluded. All strips were stored in Eppendorf tubes at −80°C until analysis.

Collection of Gingival and Peri‐Implant Mucosal Tissue Samples

Due to ethical considerations, tissue samples were collected only from patients requiring surgical interventions (e.g., resective gingival surgery, flap procedures, or mucogingival surgeries). A total of 44 gingival and peri‐implant mucosal tissue samples were collected and distributed as follows: PH (n = 8), PIH (n = 13), P (n = 13), and PID (n = 10). All samples were stored at −80°C in Eppendorf tubes until further analysis.

Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of CLEC11A, ITGA11, GSK‐3β, and PIEZO1 in GCF/PICF were quantified using human‐specific ELISA kits (Sunred Biotechnology, Shanghai, China; Cat. Nos. 201‐12‐3544, SRB‐T86936, 201‐12‐5661, and 201‐12‐8368, respectively). Prior to analysis, samples were thawed at room temperature for 1 h. To elute crevicular fluid from the paper strips, 600 μL of phosphate‐buffered saline was added to each tube (100 μL per strip) followed by vortexing for 1 min. ELISA procedures were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Concentrations were calculated from standard curves generated using known concentrations of each analyte. Assay sensitivity and intra‐ and inter‐assay coefficients of variation were recorded as reported by the manufacturer. All target proteins were detectable in all GCF/PICF samples included in the final analyses. No value was below or above the detection limit of the corresponding assay, and all concentrations were within the quantifiable range of the standard curves; therefore, no sample exclusion, substitution, or imputation was required.

RNA Extraction and Quantitative Real‐Time PCR

Transcripts of genes encoding CLEC11A, ITGA11, GSK‐3β, β‐catenin, RUNX2, and PIEZO1 were analyzed using real‐time polymerase chain reaction (qReal‐Time PCR). Total RNA was extracted using QIAzol lysis reagent. Briefly, 500 μL of the sample was mixed with QIAzol, transferred to Eppendorf tubes, and then chloroform was added, followed by centrifugation at 4°C. The aqueous phase was collected, mixed with isopropanol, and centrifuged. The resulting RNA pellet was washed with 70% ethanol, air‐dried, and resuspended in RNase‐free water. RNA purity and concentration were assessed spectrophotometrically; samples with an A260/A280 ratio of 1.8–2.2 were used for downstream applications.

Total RNA was reverse transcribed and amplified using the Luna Universal One‐Step RT‐qPCR Kit (New England Biolabs, USA) on an Analytik Jena qPCR system. Gene‐specific primers (Macrogen, Seoul, South Korea; 100 pmol/μL) were used, as listed in Table S1.

Thermal cycling conditions were as follows: reverse transcription at 55°C for 15 min, initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s, annealing at Tm—5°C, and extension at 72°C for 30 s. A final cooling step was performed at 37°C for 30 s. Relative gene expression levels were determined using the 2−∆∆Ct method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001), with β‐actin as the endogenous reference. ΔC t values were calculated by subtracting the C t of β‐actin from the Ct of the target gene. ΔΔC t values were obtained by comparing experimental and control ΔC t values.

The laboratory analysis was performed by investigators who were blinded to the sample groups.

Statistical Analysis

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power 3.1 software based on pilot study data, with ITGA11 levels in GCF as the primary outcome. Based on a one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (α = 0.05, power = 80%, effect size = 0.379), a total sample size of 80 participants (20 per group) was required to achieve adequate statistical power. In the tissue subgroup (n = 44), one‐way ANOVA revealed a significant difference among the groups, with a large effect size (F(3,40) = 3.89, η2 = 0.23).

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v.20.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA) with a significance level of 0.05 and a 95% confidence interval. Pearson's chi‐square test was used for categorical comparisons. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to evaluate the normality of the data.

For normally distributed variables, one‐way ANOVA (or Welch ANOVA when the homogeneity of variance assumption was violated), followed by Tukey's HSD or Bonferroni post hoc tests (or Tamhane's T2 post hoc test when variances were unequal), was used. For non‐normally distributed variables, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used with Dunn's post hoc test. Correlations between biomarkers, gene expression levels, age, and clinical periodontal parameters were assessed using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients, depending on data distribution.

The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate for multiple comparisons, with an FDR level set at 0.05.

Periodontal Clinical Measurements

Table 1 summarizes the average periodontal clinical measurements for both the full cohort and the tissue subset.

In the full cohort, PI, GI, PD, BOP, and CAL were significantly higher in the P and PID groups than in the PH and PIH groups (all p < 0.001; Table 1).

The Correlation Analyses Among Groups

In the ELISA analysis, CLEC11a was positively correlated with ITGA11 (r = 0.315, p =0.004), GSK‐3β (r = 0.382, p < 0.001), and PIEZO1 (r = 0.442, p < 0.001). ITGA11 was positively correlated with GSK‐3β (r = 0.490, p < 0.001) and PIEZO1 (r = 0.357, p = 0.001), and GSK‐3β was positively correlated with PIEZO1 (r = 0.467, p < 0.001) (Table S2).

In the gen expression analysis, CLEC11A expression correlated positively with ITGA11 (r = 0.351, p = 0.019), RUNX2 (r = 0.346, p = 0.022), and PIEZO1 (r = 0.448, p = 0.002). ITGA11 expression correlated positively with GSK‐3β (r = 0.325, p = 0.031), β‐catenin (r = 0.438, p = 0.003), and PIEZO1 (r = 0.414, p = 0.005). β‐catenin correlated positively with RUNX2 (r = 0.386, p = 0.01) and PIEZO1 (r = 0.365, p = 0.015) (Table S3).

No significant correlations were observed between age and any ELISA biomarker or gene expression level (all p > 0.05) (Tables S4 and S5).

Regarding clinical parameters, CLEC11a levels in GCF/PICF correlated positively with PI (r = 0.220, p = 0.05), GI (r = 0.317, p = 0.004), PD (r = 0.392, p < 0.001), BOP (r = 0.371, p = 0.001), and CAL (r = 0.296, p = 0.008), whereas ITGA11, GSK‐3β, and PIEZO1 in GCF/PICF did not show significant correlations with clinical parameters (Table S4).

For gene expression, CLEC11A correlated positively with GI (r = 0.312, p = 0.039), PD (r = 0.380, p = 0.011), BOP (r = 0.428, p = 0.004), and CAL (r = 0.405, p = 0.006), but not with PI. In addition, ITGA11 expression correlated positively with GI (r = 0.423, p = 0.004), PD (r = 0.484, p = 0.001), BOP (r = 0.396, p = 0.008), and CAL (r = 0.378, p = 0.011). Additionally, RUNX2 expression correlated positively with BOP (r = 0.342, p = 0.040), and PIEZO1 expression correlated positively with CAL (r = 0.302, p = 0.046) (Table S5).

Discussion

The present exploratory cross‐sectional study investigated the expression of CLEC11A and related molecules (ITGA11, GSK‐3β, β‐catenin, RUNX2, and PIEZO1) in periodontal health, peri‐implant health, periodontitis, and peri‐implantitis. The most consistent finding was an elevation of crevicular‐fluid CLEC11A in disease, particularly in periodontitis, together with higher tissue expression of ITGA11, β‐catenin, and RUNX2 in some diseased or peri‐implant groups. However, the pattern was not uniform across protein and gene‐expression analyses, and not all components of the proposed pathway differed significantly. Therefore, the current data support differential associations rather than direct evidence of coordinated activation of a single signaling axis.

Given that periodontitis is characterized by connective‐tissue breakdown and active bone turnover, elevated CLEC11A may reflect inflammatory exudation and ongoing remodeling rather than a simple increase in local anabolic activity (Shen et al. 2019; Xu et al. 2024; Yue et al. 2016). The positive correlations of GCF CLEC11A with GI, PD, BOP, and CAL support an association with disease severity, but they do not establish whether CLEC11A contributes to, compensates for, or merely accompanies periodontal breakdown.

In this context, decreased osteolectin expression in human and experimental periodontitis tissues has been demonstrated using immunohistochemistry and transcript/protein assays (Yin et al. 2024). Although these findings differ from the present observation of elevated crevicular‐fluid CLEC11A, the lack of a parallel increase in our tissue‐expression data suggests that fluid‐phase CLEC11A may be influenced by exudative and multicompartment contributions rather than by local transcription alone (Subbarao et al. 2019). While tissue mRNA reflects localized intracellular transcription, GCF is a complex exudate that pools proteins from various systemic and local sources (Duarte et al. 2019). Differences in sampling strategies (fluid versus tissue), cohort characteristics, and disease heterogeneity may further contribute to between‐study variation.

CLEC11A promotes the differentiation of LepR+ BMSCs into osteoblasts (Shen et al. 2019). LepR+ PDLCs have been reported to be activated by injury and mechanical stimulation, and these cells have been linked to periodontal regeneration (Li et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2023). The absence of periodontal ligament in peri‐implant tissues may alter CLEC11A regulation, likely affecting mechanical‐stimulation‐mediated bone formation (Shen et al. 2021). In the present study, CLEC11A levels did not differ significantly between periodontitis and peri‐implantitis, suggesting a lower or more variable biological response in peri‐implant tissues during disease (Belibasakis et al. 2015; Casarin et al. 2025).

Beyond CLEC11A‐centered osteogenic and mechanotransduction‐related signaling, inflammatory and collagenolytic mediators may provide complementary information on periodontal and peri‐implant disease activity. A recent study reported increased GCF/PICF concentrations of IL‐1β, IL‐6, TNF‐α, and active matrix metalloproteinase‐8 (a‐MMP‐8) in diseased periodontal and peri‐implant sites, with IL‐1β and aMMP‐8 showing higher levels in peri‐implantitis than in periodontitis (Guarnieri et al. 2024). Accordingly, future longitudinal studies may explore whether CLEC11A‐related osteogenic/mechanosensitive markers, in combination with inflammatory and collagenolytic biomarkers, could serve as indicators of periodontal and peri‐implant disease progression. In addition, implant‐related prosthetic and functional factors should be considered in future peri‐implant studies, as implant survival and prosthetic complications may influence long‐term peri‐implant tissue conditions (Reda et al. 2022).

Experimental studies indicate that CLEC11A binding to ITGA11 can influence Wnt/β‐catenin signaling and osteoblast‐associated transcripts, such as RUNX2 (Logan and Nusse 2004; Shen et al. 2019). In the current human dataset, higher ITGA11, β‐catenin, and RUNX2 expression in some groups may be compatible with remodeling‐related responses under inflammatory conditions; however, because GSK‐3β did not differ significantly and CLEC11A tissue pairwise differences were not confirmed, the results do not demonstrate full pathway activation in vivo. Integrin α11β1 is a major collagen receptor and plays a critical role in extracellular matrix remodeling, myofibroblast differentiation, and osteogenic differentiation, processes highly relevant to periodontal tissue turnover and repair (Carracedo et al. 2010; Liang et al. 2025; Popova et al. 2007; Schulz et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2024). The increased ITGA11 expression observed in periodontitis, together with its positive correlations with GI, PD, BOP, and CAL, is consistent with an association between collagen‐remodeling processes and tissue inflammation (Rauwolf et al. 2017; Schulz et al. 2015).

The positive correlations of CLEC11A with ITGA11, GSK3‐β, RUNX2, and PIEZO1 at the protein level and with ITGA11, RUNX2, and PIEZO1 at the transcription level indicates that osteolectin‐related signaling interfaces with integrin‐α11–linked remodeling pathways and mechanotransduction in periodontal and peri‐implant tissues. The correlation of PIEZO1 with CLEC11A and ITGA11 (both q < 0.05) robustly supports the presence of a coupled mechanotransduction‐osteogenic axis in these tissues.

Tissue expression analyses showed significant intergroup differences in ITGA11, β‐catenin, and RUNX2, with higher expression in periodontitis than in periodontal health. These findings may reflect a host remodeling response to inflammation rather than a purely protective or destructive pathway, and should therefore be interpreted within the broader context of tissue turnover.

Interestingly, peri‐implant tissues showed a less consistent pattern than periodontal tissues. This may relate to differences in tissue architecture, vascularization, and biomechanical environment around implants (Ivanovski and Lee 2018; Zhang et al. 2024). This interpretation remains tentative because the peri‐implant tissue analysis was based on a small surgical subgroup.

Notably, β‐catenin and RUNX2 expressions were significantly higher in PIH compared to PH, suggesting that periodontal and peri‐implant tissues differ in baseline transcriptional state even under clinically healthy conditions. Given the established role of β‐catenin and RUNX2 in osteogenic differentiation and tissue homeostasis, this may reflect an adaptive or compensatory mechanism in peri‐implant tissues subjected to implant‐specific mechanical stress (Case et al. 2008; Wróbel et al. 2025; Yan et al. 2016). However, the present data cannot determine whether the observed differences are driven by loading, tissue composition, or unmeasured clinical variables.

In conclusion, CLEC11A (osteolectin) is differentially regulated in periodontal health, peri‐implant health, periodontitis, and peri‐implantitis, but the observed pattern was heterogeneous across fluid and tissue analyses. These findings should therefore be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating. Larger longitudinal studies with more balanced groups and broader nonsurgical sampling are needed to clarify these preliminary relationships at the intersection of inflammation, mechanotransduction, and bone/connective tissue remodeling.
