Accelerated brain aging in Veterans with posttraumatic stress symptoms and mild traumatic brain injury
Authors: Ruth Klaming, Andrea D Spadoni, Jessica Bomyea, Wesley K Thompson, Alan N Simmons
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExposure to trauma and mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI), which often co-occur and can both trigger acute and chronic pathophysiological processes, have been linked to brain changes indicative of accelerated neurological aging. However, few longitudinal studies have examined the impact of these factors over time or characterized how the chronicity and severity of associated symptoms affect aging trajectories. This study included individuals (N = 79) who were exposed to psychological trauma, with most of the cohort additionally having sustained a mTBI. Participants completed structural MRI scans and behavioral assessments during three study visits over 18 months at nine-month intervals. Utilizing a deep learning method (DeepBrainNet), brain age was estimated using a large, published, normative sample and compared against chronological age. Associations between resultant brain age and neurobehavioral symptoms over time were also examined. Findings revealed that predicted brain age was significantly older (i.e., 4.8 years) than chronological age at baseline (p < 0.0001). This brain age gap progressively widened over the course of evaluation, with a difference of 5.4 and 6.5 years at 9 and 18 months post-baseline, respectively. Participants reported mild to moderate posttraumatic stress disorder, depressive, neurobehavioral, and postconcussive symptoms, which remained stable and were not correlated with the accelerated rate of brain aging. The duration since trauma exposure did not significantly influence the rate of neurological aging. Results suggest that the experience of trauma, and the confluence of factors that frequently co-occur with trauma, have a profound impact on brain health.Keywords: Brain aging, Deep learning method, Posttraumatic stress disorder, Mild traumatic brain injury, Magnetic resonance imaging

Introduction

Post 9/11 Veterans, who were deployed to military combat zones, are frequently exposed to substantial psychological and head trauma during their deployment. Combat stress can lead to a number of debilitating psychological and neurological sequela, most commonly posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI). Symptoms associated with PTSD are heterogenous and clustered into intrusion, avoidance, cognition and mood symptoms, and arousal and reactivity symptoms (American Psychiatric Association and Association, 2013). Trauma (i.e., blast or blunt force) to the head, another signature war wound, can cause diffuse damage to the brain and acute symptoms of feeling disoriented and/or dizzy, headache, confusion, nausea, brief and posttraumatic amnesia (≤24 h) (Fehily and Fitzgerald, 2017). Head injuries are categorized as mTBI if loss of consciousness does not exceed 30 min, alteration of consciousness does not exceed 24 h, a Glascow Coma Scale score of 13-15, and no clinically relevant findings are detected on structural imaging (Esselman and Uomoto, 1995). Neurological symptoms following mTBI typically subside within a week, however, between 11% and 38% of mTBI patients experience chronic post-concussive somatic, neurocognitive, and neurobehavioral symptoms (Fehily and Fitzgerald, 2017; Cooksley et al., 2018; Dean et al., 2012). Among Veterans, mTBI is most commonly caused by blasts or explosions, objects hitting the head, falls, or vehicle accidents (Lindquist et al., 2017). While PTSD and mTBI can result from a single event, it is common for soldiers to experience multiple psychological traumas and head injuries throughout their deployment. Both conditions have been linked to substantial disability, suicidal ideation, as well as high rates of substance misuse (Gros et al., 2016; Merritt et al., 2019; Wisco et al., 2014).

Despite distinct primary etiologies, i.e., psychological versus physiological injury, PTSD and mTBI are characterized by many of the same long-term symptoms, including depression, anxiety, cognitive impairment, and irritability (Heath et al., 2023; Wolf et al., 2020). Both the high comorbidity and overlap in clinical presentation, point to shared underlying pathophysiological mechanisms and shared neural circuits affected by these conditions (Monsour et al., 2022; Kaplan et al., 2018). To gain a better understanding of the neurobiological substrates of mTBI and PTSD, it is important to consider primary and secondary pathophysiological processes that can take place following exposure to psychological trauma and physical injury to the head. Acute traumatic injury to the brain, particularly blast injuries, often create intracranial pressure and shearing of white matter tracts, causing microscopic lesions and diffuse axonal injury (Kaplan et al., 2018; Ray et al., 2002). Secondary injury processes include various inflammatory, cellular, molecular, and metabolic responses, which can result in neuronal dysfunction, neurodegeneration, and ultimately cell death (Fehily and Fitzgerald, 2017).

These secondary mechanisms following mTBI, including neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and glutamate excitotoxicity, have also been proposed to occur following exposure to severe psychological trauma (Fehily and Fitzgerald, 2017; Monsour et al., 2022; Kaplan et al., 2018). In particular, increased cytokine levels, indicative for neuroinflammation, as well as elevated markers of oxidative stress and glutamate excitotoxicity have been implicated in the pathophysiology of both mTBI (Vedantam et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2019; Miller et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2016) and PTSD (Kaplan et al., 2018; Miller et al., 2018; Waheed et al., 2018; Katrinli et al., 2022; Michopoulos et al., 2020; Rosso et al., 2017) and associated with accelerated biological aging in these conditions (Lohr et al., 2015; Schwab et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2023). At the macroscopic level, neuronal damage, degeneration, and cell death that result from these secondary processes, along with diffuse axonal injury, can lead to structural and functional changes in the brain that can be measured with neuroimaging techniques (Monsour et al., 2022; Kaplan et al., 2018; Weis et al., 2022). Prior research provides evidence for a greater burden of abnormalities in gray matter volume, cortical thinning, brain activation and functional connectivity, as well as compromised white matter integrity in dually diagnosed individuals compared to healthy controls or those with a single diagnosis (Weis et al., 2022; Spadoni et al., 2018; Brown et al., 2022; Mohamed et al., 2021). More specifically, alterations in prefrontal and limbic regions have been reported in mTBI and PTSD and associated with the neuropsychiatric and neuropsychological symptoms characteristic for both disorders, including executive deficits and emotional dysregulation (Kaplan et al., 2018; Siehl et al., 2018; Li et al., 2016; O'Doherty et al., 2018; Dieter and Engel, 2019; Vasterling et al., 2018; Stein and McAllister, 2009). However, the unique and combined effects of these disorders on brain abnormalities are still a matter of debate, especially with regard to symptom profiles and symptom etiology (Weis et al., 2022; Vasterling et al., 2018; Lepage et al., 2018; Martindale et al., 2020).

Posttraumatic stress and post-concussive symptoms can persist for many years and even decades despite treatment (Lee et al., 2020), causing a chronic and persistent state of stress. Both acute and prolonged stress play a critical role in the above-described pathophysiological processes and have been associated with accelerated cellular aging (Miller et al., 2018; Katrinli et al., 2020; Epel, 2009). Neuroanatomical changes due to normal cellular aging, such as reductions in gray matter volume and cortical thinning, are similar to those reported in PTSD and mTBI (Katrinli et al., 2020; Cole et al., 2015). Age-related neurological atrophy is linked to cognitive and functional decline, as well as decreased quality of life and increased susceptibility to age-related disease and death (Fletcher et al., 2018; Rabbitt et al., 2008; Gonzales et al., 2022). It is therefore vital to understand whether PTSD and mTBI can accelerate brain aging trajectories and lead to premature neurological aging. Early detection of individuals who show evidence of accelerated brain aging, would allow timely implementation of pharmacological, behavioral, or lifestyle interventions aimed at delaying further progression, or perhaps even reverse some neuroanatomical changes, as well as evaluation of treatment effectiveness (Baecker et al., 2021).

Recent advances in the development of machine learning methods that can compute brain age based on structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans allowing direct comparison of predicted age with chronological age (Cole et al., 2015; Cole and Franke, 2017; Franke and Gaser, 2019). Machine learning methods produce a normative model based on MRI scans of large cohorts of healthy control subjects, and estimate predicted brain age of an individual subject based on the degree to which this brain deviates from the norm (Baecker et al., 2021). The magnitude of neuroanatomical discrepancy between these two indices, i.e., the degree to which a brain has features older than it is, can serve as a biomarker for accelerated neurological aging. Thus, the gap between predicted age and chronological age can be used as a measure of brain health (Baecker et al., 2021). An advantage of machine learning methods is that brain age can be predicted at the individual level and with relatively minor preprocessing of MRI data utilizing automated processes, maximizing accessibility and clinical utility (Bashyam et al., 2020).

Initial work suggests that PTSD (Brown et al., 2022; Katrinli et al., 2020) and TBI (Cole et al., 2015; Dennis et al., 2024; Coetzee et al., 2025) are associated with neuroanatomical changes indicative of accelerated brain aging. However, since the majority of these studies, including those using brain age prediction methods, are cross-sectional, there is a need for longitudinal studies to understand the trajectory of this neuroanatomical change. For instance, it is unclear whether exposure to a traumatic event or biomechanical insult to the brain results in a one-time acceleration of brain age or whether this discrepancy continues to progress, possibly at varying rates. There is also a need to explore the potential impact of chronicity and severity of behavioral and neurocognitive symptoms on brain aging. In other words, time since trauma or injury, or severity of current symptoms, may exacerbate or temper effects on brain age. To address this gap, the current study uses a deep learning network (DeepBrainNet (Bashyam et al., 2020)) to obtain an estimate of brain age in Veterans at three points over a period of 18 months. Associations between longitudinal brain age data and posttraumatic stress and postconcussive symptoms are analyzed to gain a better understanding of psychological and head trauma in relation to symptom profiles and brain aging trajectories.

Methods

Subject cohort

The study included 79 Veterans, who were deployed to a military combat zone during Operation Enduring Freedom, Operation Iraqi Freedom, or Operation New Dawn (OEF/OIF/OND). Inclusion criteria were exposure to combat and exposure to psychological trauma experienced during military deployment (as defined by Criterion A on the Clinician Administered PTSD Scale; CAPS-5 ((Weathers et al., 2013))). A PTSD diagnosis was not a requirement for study inclusion. The majority of the cohort additionally sustained mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI) during their military deployment. However, mTBI was not a study inclusion criterion. Study eligibility was determined during a comprehensive phone screening and subsequent clinical interviews, conducted during the baseline study visit (T1), to assess PTSD symptoms as well as psychiatric comorbidities. Subjects were excluded if they had a current or past (30 days) alcohol or substance use disorder, bipolar disorder type I, psychotic disorder, neurological disorder, history of moderate or severe TBI, or if they were older than 60 years of age. Subjects participated in an initial study visit (T1) and returned for follow-up visits at 9 months (T2) and at 18 months (T3) post baseline. Neuroimaging scans, questionnaires, and neuropsychological assessments (Standardized Assessment of Concussion; SAC) were repeated at all study visits (T1-T3).

The study was approved by the VA San Diego Healthcare System (VASDHS) Institutional Review Board and the Research and Development Committee (Protocol #H130064). Subjects were recruited via printed or electronic advertisements posted at the VASDHS or local community colleges and provided written informed consent prior to participating in the study.

Clinical assessments

The CAPS-5 was administered at baseline to assess exposure to criterion A and whether study participants met diagnostic criteria for PTSD. The PTSD Checklist military version (PCL-M (Weathers et al., 1994; Tsai et al., 2016)) was administered at each study visit (T1, T2, T3) to measure change in PTSD symptom severity over time. The PCL-M includes 17 items, ranging from “1” (not at all”) to “5” (extremely) with a total score range from 17 to 85. The Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) was used to measure the presence and severity of depression based on a 9-item symptom checklist ranging from “0” (not at all) to “3” (nearly every day) (Kroenke et al., 2001). The Neurobehavioral Symptom Inventory (NSI) was used to measure 22 postconcussive symptoms (e.g., dizziness, fatigue, irritability, sensitivity to noise or light), commonly associated with mTBI, on a 5-point scale ranging from “0” (none) to “4” (very severe) (Cicerone and Kalmar, 1995). The Standardized Assessment of Concussion (SAC) was used to evaluate impairments in cognitive domains most sensitive to concussion, such as orientation to time, attention, concentration, as well as immediate and delayed memory recall with a total score of 30 (McCrea and Randolph, 1997). This brief screening test includes alternative item versions to reduce learning effects due to repeated testing. In the present study, subjects were randomly assigned to receive a different order of item versions of the SAC at each study visit. The presence of problems due to hazardous alcohol use were assessed with the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT (Saunders et al., 1993)). This measure was included because chronic heavy alcohol use has been linked to accelerated brain aging (Guggenmos et al., 2017; Battista et al., 2025; Zillich et al., 2024). Scores for this self-rating questionnaire range from 0 to 40, with a score of 8 or greater indicating harmful drinking behavior.

Neuroimaging and brain age computation

Subjects participated in a 3.0 T magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan (Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Germany) during all three study visits. High resolution T1-weighted scans were used to estimate brain age (160 sagittal 1.20 mm thick slices, repetition time = 2300 ms, echo time = 2.99 ms, inversion time = 1100 ms, FOV = 220 ∗ 220 mm2, matrix = 256 × 192 (interpolated to 256 × 256) flip angle = 9°; voxel size = 0.86 × 0.86 × 1.2 mm). Automated quality control of the raw T1-weighted images was completed using the MRI Quality Control tool (MRIQC (Esteban et al., 2017)), resulting in signal-to-noise ratio for gray matter (SNR GM), white matter (SNR WM), and total (SNR total), entropy focus criterion (EFC), and foreground-to-background energy ratio (FBER). Image data was preprocessed with Advanced Normalization Tools in R (ANTsR ((Avants et al., 2011))) including skull stripping, N4 field inhomogeneity correction, and registration to MNI space. ANTsRNet (https://github.com/ANTsX/ANTsRNet) was then used to access the DeepBrainNet model (Bashyam et al., 2020).

The DeepBrainNet model is a publicly available deep learning model (https://github.com/vishnubashyam/DeepBrainNet), which was used to generate an estimated brain age for each study participant in the present study. The DeepBrainNet model uses an inception-resnet-v2 framework as its network architecture and was trained on a large set of T1-weighted brain MRI scans, which were input following minimal and fully automated preprocessing (Bashyam et al., 2020; Szegedy et al., 2017). Following training and optimization, the model achieved a high prediction accuracy in computing a brain age delta (or brain age gap; BAG), which can be used as a biomarker for brain health. The DeepBrainNet was trained on a large and diverse dataset of MRI scans (N = 11,729) collected across multiple sites, acquisition protocols, and scanners, and included healthy control subjects between the ages of 3 to 95 years (i.e., across the lifespan). It therefore served as the normative dataset in the present study to compute predicted brain age for subjects based on their deviation from this norm (Bashyam et al., 2020). The model was also trained on sex differences ensuring that sex differences do not affect age prediction. In addition, the model was trained, tested, and validated using disease-specific cohorts (i.e., participants with Alzheimer's disease or mild cognitive impairment) (Bashyam et al., 2020). The mean absolute error (MAE) of the model was 3.7 and the correlation between chronological and predicted brain ages was r = 0.978 (Bashyam et al., 2020). BAG was computed by subtracting subjects' actual chronological age from predicted brain age (as derived by the DeepBrainNet algorithm), with a positive BAG indicating an older estimated than actual age and a negative BAG indicating a younger neurological age. For a more in-depth description of the training procedures and model architecture, see Bashyam et al., 2020 (Bashyam et al., 2020).

Statistical analyses

A paired sample t-test was conducted to compare the difference between predicted brain age and chronological brain age at baseline and at each time point. An Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) with Satterthwaite's method was used to explore symptom change over the course of the study. To examine associations between predicted brain age and chronological age and neurobehavioral symptoms over time (i.e., study visits T1-T3), a linear mixed effects (LME) model was conducted. Since brain age models have been reported to show systematic age-related bias of overestimation of age in younger and underestimation in older individuals (Liang et al., 2019), the present study applied a brain age bias correction. In particular, generalized additive model (GAM) residuals of predicted brain age in relation to chronological age were computed to derive a bias corrected BAG. Fixed effects in the LME predicting BAG included chronological age, time since trauma exposure (in years), mTBI (y/n), current symptom severity (i.e., PCL-M, PHQ-9, SAC, NSI, AUDIT), demographics (sex, years of education), and time since the baseline visit (in years). Subject was included as a random effect to account for repeated measures. Results of the LME model without symptom scores are shown in the supplements (Table S1). Additionally, an alternative model without age-bias correction and with the intercept set to 0 (as the analysis contrasts two measurements of age and to ensure beta value interpretations were not affected) is provided in the supplements (Table S2). Additionally, a Welch two sample t-test was conducted to compare age, BAG, and symptoms scores, and a Fisher's exact test was used to compare mTBI status between study completers versus dropouts (dropouts included subjects who missed one or both of the follow-up visits). An ANOVA was conducted to examine differences in scan image quality metrics across visits (i.e., SNR GM, SNR WM, SNR total, EFC, and FBER). Statistical analyses were performed with R software (version 4.2.2) (Team, 2014).

Results

Subject cohort

The study cohort included a total of 79 Veterans (97% male) with an average age of 34 years at baseline (Table 1). The majority of the cohort sustained mTBI (86%) and reported current postconcussive symptoms. Head injuries included concussions that resulted from a direct impact to the head as well as exposure to blasts, with subjects reporting a mean of 4 mTBIs, ranging from 0 to 36 (Table 1). A total of 52 study participants reported having sustained more than one mTBI. All subjects were exposed to at least one criterion A trauma during their military deployment and reported varying levels of PTSD symptom severity (48% met diagnostic criteria for PTSD based on the CAPS-5; a mean score of 44.85 (SD = 16.69) on the PCL-M in the present sample is above the suggested cutoff score of greater than or equal to 38 for OEF/OIF/OND Veterans (Tsai et al., 2016). On average, subjects experienced mild to moderate depression (PHQ-9 mean = 9.75; SD = 6.24 with a score of 5-9 indicative of mild depression and 10-14 of moderate depression (Kroenke et al., 2001), and reported generally mild postconcussive symptoms (NSI mean = 25.33; SD = 17.22 for the present sample falls into the 10th-24th percentile compared to normative data for a deployed mTBI group (Soble et al., 2014; Scimeca et al., 2024) and no cognitive deficits (mean of 26.5 out of 30 is considered in the normal range (McCrea et al., 1998)). Analyses show that neurobehavioral symptoms remained relatively stable across all visits and did not change significantly over time. Mean scores and score ranges at all three study visits are reported in Table 2.

The total study cohort (N = 79) included 50 subjects who completed all three study visits (T1, T2, T3 completers). There were 14 subjects who dropped after the baseline visit (T1 only), resulting in an attrition rate of 18%. Additionally, there were 5 subjects who completed T1 and T3 visits (and one subject was excluded at T2 due to missing data), resulting in 59 subjects at T2. Also including those who missed T2 but returned for T3 (N = 6), the attrition rate was 25%. There were 8 subjects who dropped after the first two study visits (and one subject was excluded at T3 due to missing data), resulting in 56 subjects at T3 (in addition to the 14 subjects who dropped after T1). There were no significant differences in baseline chronological age (t (67) = −1.60), p = 0.11), uncorrected BAG (t (58) = 0.97), p = 0.33), symptoms scores (PCL-M (t (59) = −0.08), p = 0.94), PHQ (t (55) = 0.56), p = 0.58), NSI (t (66) = −0.34), p = 0.73), AUDIT (t (58) = 0.13), p = 0.90), CAPS-5 (t (61) = −0.64), p = 0.53)), or mTBI status (p = 0.52, odds ratio = 1.52, 95% [0.33,6.69]) between study completers and dropouts, showing that there was no indication of bias with regards to study completion.

Brain age

The paired sample t-test showed significant differences between predicted and chronological brain age at each study time point (Table 3). At baseline, predicted brain age was 4.8 years older than chronological age, t (78) = 6.15; p < 0.0001. This discrepancy increased over time, with a BAG of 5.4 years at T2, t (58) = 5.90; p < 0.0001, and 6.5 years at T3, t (55) = 7.9; p < 0.0001. The T2 cohort had a slightly higher mean actual age than the T3 cohort as some subjects either missed a study visit or dropped out. However, as the LME analysis estimates within subject trajectories and included the time between study visits as a fixed effect, this difference in mean chronological age between T2 and T3 is unlikely to affect BAG trajectories over time. Distribution of BAG over the course of the study is shown in Fig. 1.

BAG was not significantly related to PTSD symptom severity, postconcussive or depressive symptoms, mTBI, or neurocognitive impairments. Time since trauma exposure (i.e., chronicity of PTSD symptoms) and alcohol related problems were also not significantly associated with accelerated brain aging. The lack of effect of alcohol use on brain aging in this sample was likely because alcohol use disorder was an exclusion criterion for the study and the sample therefore consisted of individuals who did not consume alcohol heavily.

Since the majority of the study cohort consisted of male subjects, it was not possible to explore sex-specific effects on brain age in this cohort. The model with only male subjects was a slightly better fit (BIC = 1191.50, AIC = 1149.43, log-likelihood = −561.71) than the model with the addition of female subjects (BIC = 1227.01, AIC = 1181.26, log-likelihood = −567.63), likely because the former was a more homogenous sample. However, sex was not a significant predictor in the model and excluding females from the analyses yielded the same primary findings as including them in the sample. Since the proportion of males and females in this study is representative of the Veteran population, females were not excluded from the cohort. Analyses showed a high degree of reliability on predicted brain age with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.880 (with a confidence interval ranging from 0.828 to 0.919, 5-95%).

Results from the ANOVA showed that there were no significant main effects of study visit on SNR GM (F (2, 194) = 0.52, p = 0.597), SNR WM (F (2, 194) = 1.65, p = 0.196), SNR total (F (2, 194) = 1.58, p = 0.209), EFC (F (2, 194) = 0.16, p = 0.854), or FBER (F (2, 194) = 2.46, p = 0.088), showing comparable MRI scan quality across timepoints.

Discussion

The aim of the present study was to measure brain aging over time in a group of individuals with trauma and mTBI using a deep learning method (DeepBrainNet) to generate an estimated brain age based on structural MRI. This estimated brain age was then compared to actual chronological age as a biomarker for accelerated neurological aging. The study included Veterans who were exposed to psychological trauma and mild traumatic brain injury during their OEF/OIF/OND military deployment, and who participated in neuroimaging scans and behavioral assessments at three, equidistant, timepoints over an 18-month period. Results showed that predicted brain age was significantly older than chronological age with a difference of 4.8 years at baseline. This discrepancy increased over time, with 5.4 years and 6.5 years between predicted and actual brain age at 9-month and 18-month follow-up visits, respectively. Overall, brain aging was found to occur at a faster rate than chronological aging in this cohort.

Subjects reported mild to moderate PTSD, depressive, neurobehavioral, and post-concussive symptoms. The severity of these symptoms remained stable throughout the study visits and was not associated with a faster rate of neurological aging. Moreover, time since psychological trauma exposure was not significantly associated with accelerated brain aging. This finding may suggest that the experience of a traumatic event, and not necessarily its clinical manifestation or the prolonged experience of symptoms resulting from the trauma, may have substantial and continuing effects on brain health. Alternatively, the absence of detectable associations between symptom severity and brain aging in this cohort may also reflect the restricted range and stability of symptoms over time. Moreover, as the present results demonstrate within-cohort longitudinal change based on model-predicted brain age, conclusions about the rate of change over time need to be drawn with caution.

The present study was unable to reliably disentangle whether the observed effect of accelerated brain aging was due to exposure to primarily psychological trauma, primarily mTBI, or the combination of both. While the presence of mTBI was not significantly related to accelerated brain aging, it is important to point out that the vast majority of the sample (86%) had sustained at least one mTBI, and all study participants were exposed to psychological trauma. Specifically, head injuries included both those resulting from exposure to a blast as well as direct impact to the head, with more than half of the cohort reporting exposure to more than one mTBI. Taking into consideration the small proportion of non-mTBI individuals, there is limited power to interpret this non-significance as evidence that mTBI did not contribute to accelerated neurological aging. It is therefore safest to interpret the present findings on brain aging as a potentially combined effect of exposure to psychological and neurological trauma. We will therefore discuss the present findings in the context of a sample that was comprised of combat-exposed Veterans who mostly experienced both head trauma and psychological trauma during their deployment.

The primary finding from this study indicates that exposure to psychological and physical head trauma may result in neuroanatomical changes in the brain that are consistent with those that typically occur due to aging (Katrinli et al., 2020; Cole et al., 2015). Time since trauma and severity of trauma-related psychiatric or neurobehavioral symptoms do not appear to significantly impact these processes in the current sample under the present measurement conditions, suggesting that chronicity and symptom profile may not further exacerbate brain aging. However, a matched longitudinal control group and broader symptom range are important to further probe this observation. Due to the high rate of head injuries in the present sample, we could not examine anatomical brain changes with regard to time since injury. Moreover, exposure to head injury and psychological trauma were not necessarily linked to the same event. Therefore, time since trauma was analyzed and interpreted with regards to the experience of criterion A. While we had hoped to examine brain age in relation to fluctuating course of symptom severity over time, as is often observed in PTSD (Bryant et al., 2013; Chopra et al., 2014; Bryant and Hadzi-Pavlovic, 2024), PTSD symptom severity remained relatively stable during the observed epochs, therefore we could not test whether exacerbations or remissions affect brain age. Interestingly, the lack of an association between severity of posttraumatic stress and accelerated aging, which is in line with a recent study (Clausen et al., 2022), suggests that the cascading effects of trauma exposure on the brain may not be dose dependent. When interpreting these findings, however, it is important to keep in mind that the present study did not include a sample with substantial variance in its features (i.e., Veterans without trauma exposure, or with trauma exposure but without substantial PTSD symptomology, or without mTBI), which could give insight into potential resiliency factors. One hypothesis is that accumulation of stress is not strictly linked to a specific severity or length of time since trauma but rather to a continued experience of life in a prolonged neurotoxic milieu that negatively affects brain health.

Present findings potentially indicate that neuropathological processes that lead to faster neurological aging can be triggered by a traumatic experience and can cascade over time resulting in progressively older brain age. It is plausible that chronic stress experienced in the wake of a criterion A trauma and mTBI symptoms, and associated pathophysiological processes, play a key role in continued accelerated aging beyond the acute impact. The two primary mechanisms that are likely relevant to this question are inflammation and oxidative stress, both of which have been implicated in the pathophysiology of PTSD and mTBI (Katrinli et al., 2020; Miller and Sadeh, 2014; Khatri et al., 2018; Kalra et al., 2022; Peruzzolo et al., 2022). Stress triggers a number of physiological processes, including the release of glucocorticoids via activation of the hypothalamo-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis (Miller et al., 2018; Garrido, 2011). Prolonged or repeated activation of the HPA axis and excessive release of glucocorticoids trigger the release of inflammatory cytokines, which in turn can lead to neuroinflammation (Tapp et al., 2019). In addition, elevated glucocorticoid levels due to chronic stress have been linked to increased oxidative stress and consequently oxidative damage (Miller and Sadeh, 2014; Costantini et al., 2011). Both neuroinflammation and oxidative stress have been proposed to lead to neurodegeneration and accelerated aging (Miller and Sadeh, 2014; Liguori et al., 2018).

There is a large body of neuroimaging research demonstrating structural and functional brain abnormalities in those with PTSD as well as in those with comorbid PTSD and mTBI, including reductions in gray matter volume, surface area, and cortical thickness in a variety of brain regions (Brown et al., 2022; Akiki et al., 2017; Harnett et al., 2020; O'Doherty et al., 2017). However, exploring potential effects of acute or chronic trauma on brain aging trajectories using neuroimaging-based machine learning algorithms is a relatively novel field of research (Liang et al., 2019; Clausen et al., 2022). This early work estimating predicted brain age contrasted with actual chronological age, provides initial evidence that PTSD and mTBI are associated with a significant brain age difference, suggesting reduced brain health and accelerated brain aging due to psychological and physical traumatic insult (Liang et al., 2019; Clausen et al., 2022). The present results are in line with this prior work, while showing higher predicted brain age differences than previous studies.

An important consideration in research employing machine learning approaches to estimate brain age is the chronological age of the subject cohort. Both a measurement bias (Liang et al., 2019) and a differential effect of trauma on brain aging in different age groups (Clausen et al., 2022) have been proposed to potentially obscure results. In particular, Liang et al. (2019) suggest that predicted brain age is commonly overestimated in younger individuals and underestimated in older individuals, but that this bias can be mitigated statistically (Liang et al., 2019). Similarly, Clausen et al. (2021) report a larger brain gap in younger men compared to healthy controls and older men with PTSD, and propose that young adulthood may present a critical window, during which the brain may be more vulnerable to the aging effects of PTSD (Clausen et al., 2022). As the first longitudinal study using machine learning to explore associations between trauma exposure and neuroimaging-based estimates of brain age over time, the present study contributes valuable information to this debate. Of note, study findings did not change after the model was age bias corrected. Although the mean age of the present cohort was 34, with a mean of 8 years since trauma exposure, analyses showed an increase in discrepancy between brain and chronological age over time, supporting the hypothesis that brains may be more susceptible to the effects of trauma during early compared to old adulthood. However, it is important to also point out that the brain age gap increased over time in the present sample (over the course of 18 months). It is also possible that the acceleration of a widening BAG over time observed in this study was in part due to variability in brain age estimates across repeated MRI scans. Future work aimed at assessing test-retest reliability and measurement variability in longitudinal MRI studies would provide valuable insight into this question.

The present study has the following limitations. First, the study did not contain separate control samples of non-trauma exposed Veterans, or substantial proportions of trauma-exposed Veterans without mTBI or PTSD symptoms. Instead, our sample was designed to recruit varying levels of PTSD and post-concussive symptoms which allowed evaluation of associations between symptom severity and brain aging trajectory. However, the relatively restricted age and symptom range of the current group may have contributed to the failure to replicate prior work in larger samples that showed accelerated aging with increased PTSD severity. Furthermore, it is important to point out that the present study did not include a non-trauma exposed, healthy control cohort. Results therefore cannot confirm that trauma exposure has a differential effect on brain age but provide strong support for this hypothesis since estimated brain age, which was compared to subjects’ actual chronological age, was computed based on a robust normative sample. Specifically, the DeepBrainNet model was trained on a large normative sample of healthy control brains (N = 11,729) and therefore provides more reliable estimates of control (non-Veteran, non-trauma, non-mTBI) features than a smaller, single-site sample could provide. In addition, the normative sample was collected across multiple sites, acquisition protocols, and scanners, reducing the likelihood that these factors may have confounded present findings. It also is important to point out that the present study did not include a matched longitudinal control comparator, limiting conclusions that can be drawn regarding the relative contribution of trauma exposure and mTBI on accelerated aging. As discussed above, a control cohort scanned with the same neuroimaging protocol would provide valuable insight into the potential effects of sample characteristics or artifacts from the MRI scanner or acquisition protocol. Finally, although this study has a longitudinal design, we do not have pre-deployment brain measures, allowing for the possibility of pre-existing risk factors driving accelerated brain age (Farmer et al., 2015). Present study findings should therefore be interpreted with caution with regards to the unique and combined effects of psychological and neurological trauma. If sample size allows, future study designs could be improved by including different study cohorts (i.e., trauma-exposed/non-trauma-exposed, with mTBI/without mTBI, military/civilian individuals, combat/non-combat trauma).

Other limitations include that the present analysis did not provide information on which brain changes were driving the model's estimation of older age (e.g., ventricular enlargement, atrophy, region-specific changes, etc.). Furthermore, while study enrollment was not fixed to a specific time since trauma exposure, our large range in time since trauma provided an opportunity to examine its relationship with brain age (we found none). It is also plausible that subjects who were more concerned about their psychological and neurological health were more likely to participate in research, introducing a bias in our cohort. The current study did not systematically assess whether subjects received pharmacological or psychotherapy over the course of the study. Although symptom scores were stable across the study visits, treatment status would provide important insights into the potential impact of symptom intervention on neurological aging. The current sample is of modest size; nevertheless, it does provide a unique sample of well characterized individuals with trauma who have been tracked with neuroimaging scans and behavioral assessments over time. The sample was largely male with combat exposure, which is representative of the Veteran population, but this limits the level to which these findings can be extended to other populations. While the analysis was robust to inclusion or exclusion of females, it was not powered to evaluate sex differences. Previous studies have reported sex-specific effects, with males with mTBI (Dennis et al., 2024) and PTSD (Clausen et al., 2022) showing more accelerated brain aging. We also did not include an analysis of the number of mTBI sustained, as our measure allowed for subjective estimated count and varied widely. Lastly, the lack of an association between accelerated brain aging and cognitive function (and lack of cognitive decline) is potentially due to the measurement tool used in this study. The SAC is a short screening tool and does not provide nuanced information about deficits in specific cognitive domains or more subtle impairments that can be detected with comprehensive neuropsychological assessments.

The current findings show an acceleration of neural aging in this cohort during the scope of the study. This should be interpreted with caution as there is insufficient evidence to assume that the trajectory would continue at this rate or would not show a reversion to the mean over an extended period. While three measurement time points provide an estimate of a slope, attempting to model quadratic or higher power models would likely lead to overfitting of the data. Evaluation of neurological, biological, lifestyle, and physiological, including epigenetic and metabolic factors, that may acutely affect the brain in a way to be interpreted as accelerated brain aging would be beneficial. Moreover, the lack of a longitudinal control comparator additionally restricts interpretations regarding the rate of aging observed in this study. Further work is therefore needed in larger well characterized samples with matched control cohorts and over longer time frames to find the most robust estimate of the effect of trauma on brain age over time.

In summary, this study aimed to understand if the previously observed effects of both the experience of a traumatic event defined as criterion A on the CAPS-5 and mTBI on brain aging are experienced longitudinally and what constructs impact these effects. While we replicated the previous cross-sectional findings that psychological and neurological trauma increase brain age, in the present sample, we found that the effects of these combined traumas continue long after the initial event. These do not appear to be obviously dependent on comorbid symptoms of depression, anxiety, and cognitive functioning. Findings suggest that the effect of trauma is not clearly conceptualized as a single hit to the brain but may represent a prolonged neurotoxic state that accelerates brain age.
