Role conflict and organizational cynicism in critical care nurses: evidence from a cross-sectional study
Authors: Zahra Karimi, Mahboobeh Shali, Paniz Rostami Varnosfaderani, Richard Mottershead, Matineh Sadat Tabatabaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCritical care nursing includes physically, emotionally, and ethically challenging jobs which can induce role conflict and organizational cynicism amongst other organizational attitudes. This research aimed to evaluate the association between role conflict and organizational cynicism among critical care nurses.MethodsThis cross-sectional study recruited 322 nurses working in intensive care units of hospitals under Tehran University of Medical Sciences who completed Role Conflict Questionnaire by Rizzo et al., and Organizational Cynicism Questionnaire by Dean et al. The data were analyzed through Spearman correlation test and multiple linear regression analysis.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 36.26 ± 8.61, and 73.6% were women. Role conflict had a mean score of 2.58 ± 0.83 and organizational cynicism of 2.73 ± 0.96, showing moderate level of both variables. A significant positive correlation was found between role conflict and organizational cynicism (r = 0.765, p < 0.01) as well as with its emotional, cognitive, and behavioral components. Multiple job holding and number of workplaces at one time were identified as the main factors for both variables.ConclusionThere exists a high association between role conflict and organizational cynicism among critical care nurses.Clinical trial numberNot applicableKeywords: Organizational cynicism, Role conflict, Nursing, Management, Healthcare

Introduction

Nurses are constantly faced with numerous psychological, emotional, and ethical pressures in their work environments, especially when they are responsible for caring for critically ill and life-threatening patients. These pressures can affect nurses’ mental health and lead to consequences such as decreased job satisfaction, decreased quality of care, and negative attitudes toward the profession and organization [1, 2]. Intensive care units are among the most stressful clinical environments due to their constant exposure to mortality, heavy workload, and the need for rapid decision-making in critical situations, and nurses in these units are exposed to high levels of burnout and organizational stress [3, 4]. Given the key role of nurses in the health system, it is of particular importance to identify organizational and occupational factors that affect their attitudes and professional performance. The nursing profession is inherently ethical, and adherence to ethical and humane values is an integral part of nurses’ professional role [5].

According to the Job Demands-Resources Model, high job demands, such as heavy workload and emotional strain, combined with limited resources, such as inadequate organizational support, lead to emotional exhaustion and burnout [6]. In these settings, nurses face, in addition to physical and work pressures, the emotional burden of compassionate care. Evidence suggests that prolonged exposure to patient suffering can lead to compassion fatigue, a phenomenon that depletes nurses’ emotional and moral capacity and affects the quality of their decision-making and professional interactions [7, 8]. In a recent study in hospitals affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences, moderate levels of compassion fatigue were reported in nurses and a significant inverse relationship was observed with moral intelligence, which emphasizes the close link between emotional-organizational conditions and the moral capacity of nurses in stressful environments [7].

In such stressful contexts, organizational factors play a decisive role in shaping nurses’ professional attitudes and responses. One of the most important of these factors is role conflict. Role conflict occurs when nurses face conflicting expectations, ambiguity in tasks, or conflict between organizational requirements and their professional and ethical values [9]. Studies have shown that role conflict is associated with increased job stress, decreased organizational satisfaction and commitment, and increased turnover among nurses, especially in critical care units [10, 11]. This situation can reinforce feelings of injustice and dissatisfaction in nurses and create a basis for negative attitudes towards the organization.

Organizational cynicism is defined as a negative and multidimensional attitude that includes cognitive, emotional and behavioral components towards the organization, managers and colleagues [12]. Organizational cynicism consists of three distinct dimensions: [1] the cognitive dimension, which reflects negative beliefs that the organization lacks integrity, honesty, and fairness; [2] the affective (emotional) dimension, which involves negative emotional responses such as anger, frustration, disappointment, and contempt toward the organization; and [3] the behavioral dimension, which refers to tendencies to engage in negative behaviors such as criticism, sarcasm, reduced effort, or withdrawal [13]. This attitude is usually formed as a result of psychological contract violation, lack of organizational support and perception of injustice and has consequences such as reduced organizational commitment, decreased job performance and increased desire to leave the job [14]. Recent research shows that organizational cynicism has a significant positive relationship with nurses’ burnout and can be exacerbated by stressors such as role conflict [15].

The relationship between role conflict and organizational cynicism has been investigated in some different studies; in such a way that role conflict can act as one of the most linked factors with organizational cynicism and exacerbate employees’ negative attitudes towards the organization [16]. In stressful environments such as intensive care units, where nurses are faced with the emotional burden of compassion fatigue and organizational pressures simultaneously, this relationship can be more intense [17, 18]. Hospitals affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences, as reference educational-therapeutic centers with a high volume of patients and complex clinical conditions, are a prime example of such environments. Although previous studies have reported associations between role conflict, workplace stressors, burnout, and organizational cynicism, limited evidence is available regarding these relationships among critical care nurses, particularly in the context of multiple job holding. To the best of our knowledge, no previous study in Iran has simultaneously examined the relationship between role conflict and organizational cynicism among critical care nurses while also considering concurrent employment. Therefore, this study provides context-specific evidence and contributes to a better understanding of the organizational experiences associated with cynicism in this important nursing population.

Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the relationship between role conflict and organizational cynicism in nurses working in intensive care units of hospitals affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences in 2025. The results of this study can help to better identify nurses’ organizational challenges, design effective management interventions, and ultimately promote nurses’ mental health and improve the quality and ethics of care in intensive care units.

Methods

Study setting

This study was conducted in the intensive care units of hospitals affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences, including Imam Khomeini Hospital Complex, Mahdi Clinic, Shariati, Valiasr, and Sina. These hospitals, which are educational and therapeutic centers located in different parts of Tehran, provide similar specialized and subspecialty services in the fields of internal medicine, surgery, and especially in intensive care units. Due to the presence of nurses with diverse socioeconomic backgrounds and exposure to stressful work environments and management of critical patients, these centers provide a suitable platform for examining issues related to role conflict and organizational cynicism in nurses. These hospitals also play a key role in managing critical patients and are considered a suitable environment for reflecting the realities of the stressful work environment of nurses [19].

Study design

This study is a descriptive-analytical cross-sectional study that aims to investigate the relationship between role conflict and organizational cynicism in nurses in intensive care units of hospitals affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences in 1404.

Sampling method

After approving the research plan and receiving a letter of introduction from the Faculty of Nursing and Midwifery of Tehran University of Medical Sciences and obtaining a code of ethics from the Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences, the researchers, with a letter of introduction from the Vice Chancellor for Research of the university, visited the hospitals under the supervision of Tehran University of Medical Sciences and, after coordinating with the respected officials, began sampling nurses in intensive care units. First, the objectives of the study were explained to the nurses and after obtaining written informed consent from them, they entered the study and then the questionnaires were provided to the nurses willing to participate.

Sampling in the present study was carried out using a stratified random method. First, hospitals affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences that have critical care units were considered, then the number of nurses in critical care units in each hospital was confirmed using statistical information, and then the required number was randomly selected from among the nurses. The selected nurses were informed about the objectives of the study, and written informed consent was obtained before they completed the questionnaires. Finally, 322 nurses from the intensive care units of Imam Khomeini, Mehdi Clinic, Shariati, Valiasr, and Sina hospitals completed the questionnaires.

Data collection tools

Demographic questionnaire including questions: age, gender (male and female), marital status (single, married, other), education (bachelor’s, master’s, PhD), work experience by year, Work experience in the Current organization, multiple job holding and the number of organizations they work for simultaneously.

Standard role conflict questionnaire by rizo et al

The Role Conflict Questionnaire was designed and developed by Rizo et al. (1970) to measure role conflict and was validated in Iran by Aghaei et al. (2011). This questionnaire has 8 questions and the answers are scored on a four-point Likert scale from “very true” to “not true at all” from 1 to 4. To calculate the score of each subscale, the score of each item related to that subscale is added together, and to calculate the overall score of the questionnaire, the score of all items in the questionnaire is added together. The range of scores for this questionnaire will be between 8 and 32. The higher the score obtained from this questionnaire, the greater the level of role conflict will be, and vice versa. Rizzo et al. (1970), who created and introduced this questionnaire for the first time, obtained the internal consistency of the questionnaire using Cronbach’s alpha of 0.81. In the Iranian version of this questionnaire, in the study of Aghaei et al. (2011), its value was obtained above 0.7, which indicates the appropriate reliability of the instrument [20, 21].

Dean et al. standard organizational cynicism questionnaire

The Dean et al. (1998), Brandes, et al., (1999) and Kalağan (2009) Standard Organizational Cynicism Questionnaire consists of 13 questions designed to measure organizational cynicism in three dimensions: emotional (4 questions), cognitive (5 questions), and behavioral (4 questions). The responses are scored on a five-point Likert scale from “strongly agree” to “strongly disagree” from 1 to 5. Based on this method of analysis, the scores obtained are summed and then each questionnaire is interpreted based on the scores. Score between 13 and 22: The level of organizational cynicism of individuals is low. Score between 22 and 44: The level of organizational cynicism of individuals is moderate. Score above 44: The level of organizational cynicism of individuals is high. Dean et al. obtained the internal consistency of the questionnaire using Cronbach’s alpha of 0.82, which indicates the appropriate reliability of the instrument [22, 23].

Ethical consideration

Ethical declaration and consent for participation

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (Approval number: IR.TUMS.FNM.REC.1404.097). The study was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national and institutional guidelines and regulations. All participants were thoroughly informed about the study objectives and procedures in person. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study and before completing the questionnaire.

Data implementation and analysis

Data for this study were collected using two standard questionnaires. Dean et al.‘s Organizational Cynicism Questionnaire (1998) was used to measure nurses’ negative attitude towards the organization, including three cognitive, emotional, and behavioral dimensions, and Rizzo et al.‘s Role Conflict Questionnaire (1970) was used to assess role conflict in nurses, including the dimensions of role ambiguity and conflict between job expectations. These questionnaires were distributed in person among nurses working in the intensive care units of Tehran Medical Sciences Hospitals. Before distributing the questionnaire, the objectives of the research and the method of answering was explained to the participants, and informed consent were obtained from them. The information collected was recorded anonymously, and participation in the research was completely voluntary. After collection, the data was checked for completeness and then the information was analyzed using SPSS software.

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26. Normality of variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and Q-Q plots. Due to non-normal distribution of the main variables, Spearman correlation coefficient was used to examine the relationship between role conflict and organizational cynicism. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify factor associated with. The significance level was set at p < 0.05. Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) was checked to assess multicollinearity.

Results

322 nurses working in critical care units were participated. The mean age of the study participants was 36.26 years which indicates a young population. The gender distribution showed 73.6% of females and in terms of marital status, 52.5% were married with a 43.8% of them being single. 72.7% of the participating nurses had bachelor’s degree, 24.2% master’s and 3.1 had a PhD degree. The mean work experience was 10.55 ± 7.09 years, with a mean of 6.50 years occupied in the current company. Also 26.1% of nurses work in more than one organization simultaneously (Table 1).

Although high correlations were observed between role conflict and dimensions of organizational cynicism (especially cognitive dimension, r = 0.945), variance inflation factors remained within acceptable limits (VIF < 5), indicating no serious multicollinearity issue. Nevertheless, such extremely high correlations may still indicate conceptual overlap between constructs or common method variance, and therefore should be interpreted cautiously.

In general, the current research showed that role conflict has a significant positive relationship with organizational cynicism and all of its dimensions (r = 0.765, p < 0.01) and can be considered as an important factor in shaping organizational cynicism among nurses working in critical care units. Further multiple regression model analysis indicated that work pressures such as multiple job holding, plays a much stronger role in role conflict and organizational cynicism than any personal characteristics.

Discussion

The findings of this study showed that among 322 Critical Care Units nurses, the level of role conflict was moderate and organizational cynicism was moderate to relatively high, so that the average total score of organizational cynicism was reported to be 2.73 and the behavioral dimension of cynicism had the highest level. The results also showed that there is a positive and significant relationship between role conflict and organizational cynicism and all three of its dimensions (emotional, cognitive, and behavioral), and the correlation coefficient for the overall organizational cynicism was very strong (r = 0.765). On the other hand, regression analysis showed that structural and occupational variables (particularly the number of concurrent workplaces) were stronger factor associated with of organizational cynicism than most demographic characteristics.

These patterns of findings are consistent with recent research in the field of occupational health and nursing, and are thematically compared and described with previous scientific findings.

Role conflict and organizational cynicism: compatibility with literature

The findings of this study, which indicate the existence of a strong and positive relationship between role conflict and organizational cynicism in nurses in special units, are consistent with the results of other studies. Correlation coefficients above 0.90 may indicate a degree of conceptual overlap between constructs. One possible explanation is that critical care nurses do not clearly separate experiences of role conflict from their negative emotional and cognitive evaluations of the organization. In highly stressful work environments, conflicting role expectations may rapidly develop into feelings of frustration, distrust, and perceptions of organizational unfairness. Therefore, these experiences may become closely intertwined in nurses’ perceptions. However, because role conflict and organizational cynicism are theoretically distinct constructs and were measured using separate validated instruments, these findings should be interpreted as evidence of a very strong association rather than proof that both variables represent the same phenomenon. Research has shown that job stress and stressors such as role conflict are strong drivers of organizational cynicism among healthcare workers, with increased negative attitudes and distrust of the organization observed when individuals perceive that the demands of different roles are incompatible [24]. Negative attitudes in the workplace, such as cynicism, can seriously damage the foundation of nursing practice: the therapeutic nurse-patient relationship. Nurses who become cynical often display emotional detachment, show reduced empathy, and engage less fully with patients. These behaviors ultimately weaken quality of care. For instance, research using the Nurse-Patient Therapeutic Interaction scale demonstrates how professional stressors that includes role conflict and cynicism can reduce therapeutic engagement, especially in demanding clinical environments [25].

This finding is also supported by larger systematic studies that show that job stress and role pressures are strongly associated with pessimistic attitudes and reduced organizational engagement [26]. The relationship may be particularly pronounced in critical care settings because nurses in these units frequently face urgent clinical decisions, high patient acuity, and competing professional expectations. Under such conditions, role conflict may be perceived as a persistent job demand that depletes emotional resources and increases feelings of frustration and inadequate organizational support. According to the Job Demands–Resources framework, prolonged exposure to high job demands without sufficient resources can contribute to negative work-related attitudes, including organizational cynicism [27]. The correlational results showed that the affective dimension of organizational cynicism is more strongly associated with role conflict than the cognitive and behavioral dimensions. This is consistent with contemporary burnout theories that suggest that negative emotional responses such as hopelessness and emotional exhaustion are the first reactions to role pressures that then translate into negative beliefs and behaviors. This pattern has also been reported in recent nursing studies, where emotional exhaustion and organizational avoidance precede the emergence of pessimistic behaviors [28].

In addition, recent studies have shown that stressful work environments, lack of structural support, and frequent role conflicts not only increase cynicism but are also associated with burnout [29].

Lack of significant role of demographic variables in role conflict

The lack of significant role of demographic variables in role conflict in this study suggests that role conflict is more a result of organizational and structural conditions of the work environment than individual characteristics. This pattern has been seen in other studies; for example, more extensive research in stressful health care environments has shown that age, gender, and education have little effect on role conflict mechanisms, and that structural and organizational factors such as role ambiguity, workload, and ineffective management are more decisive [30].

Other analytical studies have also shown that structural pressures, passive organizational culture, and lack of organizational support are more influential than individual variables in shaping role conflict and cynicism, which highlights the importance of designing support structures at the organizational level [31].

Concurrent employment and the prediction of organizational cynicism

One of the key findings of this study was that concurrent employment and an increase in the number of workplaces are significant factor associated with of organizational cynicism. This is consistent with research that has examined the role of job resources and external pressures in producing negative attitudes toward the organization. Studies have shown that when employees are forced to multi-job, work-life imbalance increases and feelings of organizational support decrease, which lead to cynicism and reduced organizational engagement [32]. This finding emphasizes that shared environmental pressures such as multi-jobs not only increase workload but also directly reinforce pessimistic attitudes toward the organization.

From the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) perspective, simultaneous employment in multiple organizations substantially increases job demands (time, emotional energy, and physical presence) while diminishing organization-specific resources and sense of belonging. This may result not only in emotional resource depletion but also in loyalty conflict between employers, thereby intensifying feelings of alienation and organizational cynicism [33].

Theoretical mechanisms and practical implications

In the organizational literature, contextual models such as the Job Demands–Resources model explain that when job demands (such as role conflict, structural instability, high workload) exceed the resources available to nurses, they will experience organizational cynicism and burnout [34]. This model is consistent with the findings of this study and recent research, and shows that creating organizational support resources (managerial support, organizational justice, open culture, and professional development) can reduce the negative consequences of role conflict [35].

From a practical perspective, these findings show health managers that focusing solely on the individual characteristics of nurses is not enough, but rather modifying work structures, improving organizational support conditions, and reducing role conflict can lead to reduced cynicism and improved performance [36]. In addition to organizational interventions, individual-level strategies are also beneficial. Emotion regulation training has been shown to reduce emotional exhaustion and help nurses manage negative emotions better [37, 38].

Compatibility with international and clinic-based studies

The findings of this study are also consistent with the results of research in various countries. For example, a study in Nigeria found that organizational cynicism was positively correlated with turnover intention and decreased organizational commitment, and this relationship was stronger when perceived organizational support decreased [17]. Another study found that organizational cynicism in nurses was associated with increased burnout and decreased job satisfaction, especially among more experienced workers [39].

Strengths, limitations, and suggestions for future research

The highlights of this study include the large sample size and the use of appropriate analyses and reliable instruments with high reliability, which helped to accurately measure the relationships between variables. However, this study has some limitations. The cross-sectional design precludes establishing definitive temporal or causal relationships between role conflict and organizational cynicism. Therefore, it is not possible to determine whether role conflict leads to cynicism or whether pre-existing cynical attitudes amplify the perception of role conflict, and it is recommended that future studies use longitudinal or experimental designs to better elucidate causal and causal processes. Also, future research could examine the role of mediating variables such as job strain, perceived organizational support, and organizational justice in the relationship between role conflict and organizational cynicism to determine the more complete mechanism of these relationships.

Another limitation of the study was that although a stratified random sampling approach was intended, participants were ultimately selected randomly from nurses who were willing to participate. This may introduce potential selection bias. This self-selection bias may have affected the reported levels of organizational cynicism in different ways. Nurses with higher levels of cynicism may have been less willing to participate because of distrust toward research activities or organizational processes. Conversely, highly cynical nurses may have been more motivated to participate as an opportunity to express their dissatisfaction and negative perceptions. Therefore, the observed level of organizational cynicism should be interpreted with caution, as the direction and magnitude of this potential bias cannot be determined in the present study. Additional limitations include the reliance on self-reported data, which may be subject to common method bias, and the fact that the study was conducted in only one university hospital system in Tehran, limiting generalizability to other regions or private hospitals in Iran.

Conclusion

Overall, the findings of this study indicate that organizational cynicism in critical care nurses is influenced by working conditions, role pressures, and organizational structures rather than being rooted in individual characteristics. Therefore, the attention of health system managers and policymakers to reducing role conflict, improving organizational support, and managing the consequences of multi-tasking can be an effective step in reducing organizational cynicism and improving the quality of nurses’ performance and job satisfaction. From a practical perspective, nursing managers should implement clear job descriptions to reduce role conflict and develop supportive policies for nurses with multiple jobs. Organizational efforts to optimize staffing and workload distribution are also recommended to decrease both role conflict and organizational cynicism.
