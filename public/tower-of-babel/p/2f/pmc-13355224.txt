Effect of wheat bran insoluble dietary fiber on the physicochemical, structural, and digestion characteristics of maize starches with different amylose contents
Authors: Wei Li, Yan Cui, Qinyue Gao, Huimin Liu, Mingzhu Zheng, Jingsheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The interaction between dietary fiber and starch is crucial for food quality control. This study explored the interaction of wheat bran insoluble dietary fiber (WBIDF) with normal, waxy, and high-amylose maize starches (NMS, WMS, HAMS) and its effects on composite system properties. The results showed that with 15% WBIDF supplementation, the water separation rates of NMS, WMS and HAMS decreased to 15.36%, 3.79% and 40.19%, respectively, and their pasting, rheological and textural properties were altered. WBIDF lowered rapidly digestible starch in all composite systems. Within the tested WBIDF addition range, NMS, WMS and HAMS composites achieved maximum resistant starch levels of 40.92%, 31.47% and 60.07%, respectively. WBIDF-starch interactions involved hydrogen bonds, physical entanglement, and water competition, forming compact structures and enhancing thermal stability and crystallinity. These findings confirm WBIDF effectively improves the physicochemical properties and nutritional value of maize starches, providing a theoretical basis for low-digestibility starch-based products.

Introduction

Maize starch possesses extensive applications in the food, pharmaceutical, and material science fields due to its cost-effectiveness and facile processability (Yu & Moon, 2021). However, native starches exhibit inherent functional limitations, such as susceptibility to a rapid viscosity decrease under elevated temperatures or mechanical shear (Mohamed et al., 2024), and proneness to syneresis following freeze-thaw cycles, thereby compromising product quality (Wang, Qin, et al., 2022). Crucially, the majority of native starches are rapidly digestible (RDS); their swift hydrolysis in the small intestine often results in a sharp postprandial glucose surge. Chronic excessive consumption of RDS is a significant contributor to metabolic syndromes, including obesity and type II diabetes (Guo et al., 2021). Conversely, slowly digestible starch (SDS) gradually releases glucose, thereby facilitating the maintenance of stable blood glucose levels (Lehmann & Robin, 2007). Resistant starch (RS), undigested in the small intestine, ferments in the large intestine to produce short-chain fatty acids, which in turn attenuate postprandial glucose elevation, modulate gut microbiota, and enhance intestinal barrier function (Niu et al., 2025a). The digestive behavior and physicochemical properties of starches are intimately linked to their molecular structure, with amylose content being a crucial determinant of their digestion resistance. Maize starches with varying amylose contents exhibit notably distinct digestion characteristics (Lv et al., 2021). Consequently, modifying starch to optimize its functional properties and regulate its digestibility has emerged as a central research focus within the food industry.

To overcome the inherent limitations of native starches, researchers have developed traditional modification techniques, including physical (Malumba et al., 2010), chemical (Ping et al., 2025), and enzymatic methods (Jia et al., 2024). However, chemical modification carries potential risks of chemical residues, while physical and enzymatic methods often face challenges such as high costs, low efficiency, stringent processing conditions, or potential damage to starch structure. These drawbacks make it difficult to meet the modern food industry's demand for green processing. Consequently, scholars have turned their attention to blending starch with natural non-starch polysaccharides (e.g., food hydrocolloids, dietary fibers) as a green and safe modification strategy (Guo, Tian, et al., 2024). Previous studies on fiber-starch composites mainly adopt two categories of preparation methods. The first category merely conducts simple physical mixing of dried fiber powder and starch without hydrothermal hydration treatment, which fails to trigger sufficient intermolecular contact and weakens the interactions between the two components. The second category employs hydrothermal co-processing to facilitate full hydration of starch granules and exposure of hydroxyl groups on fiber surfaces, thereby promoting the formation of hydrogen bonds and physical entanglement. This physical modification strategy via blending natural macromolecules requires no chemical reagents and endows products with extra nutritional functions, making it a research hotspot in the current field of starch science.

Studies have confirmed that various dietary fibers (e.g., oat β-glucan, inulin) significantly interact with starch (Zhao, Chen, et al., 2023; Zhuang et al., 2024), thereby altering the texture of starch-based foods and reducing their glycemic index. Zhang et al. (Zhang et al., 2022) reported that dietary fibers can form a physical barrier in the gastrointestinal tract, restricting the access of digestive enzymes to starch. This effectively reduces the starch digestion rate and glycemic response, which is crucial for controlling postprandial blood glucose levels. Wheat bran insoluble dietary fiber (WBIDF) is a high-yield by-product of wheat processing and also an excellent natural starch modifier. Compared with soluble dietary fiber, WBIDF possesses abundant, compact porous fibrous structures and abundant free hydroxyl groups. It exhibits stronger competitive water adsorption capacity and can more effectively hinder the contact between digestive enzymes and starch substrates, thus reducing starch digestibility and improving freeze-thaw stability to a greater extent. For the above reasons, WBIDF was selected as the fibrous material for this study. Existing studies have demonstrated the regulatory effects of insoluble fibers on starch; for example, (Li, Liu, et al., 2022) reported changes in the thermal and physicochemical properties of maize starch, while (He, Li, et al., 2023) found that these fibers slow starch digestion by inhibiting α-amylase. However, there is a lack of systematic comparative research on the differential effects of WBIDF on starches with different amylose contents and the underlying interaction modes.

Most previous relevant studies only adopted a single variety of maize starch or focused on soluble dietary fibers. Few publications systematically compared the differentiated interaction rules between wheat bran insoluble dietary fiber (WBIDF) and three types of maize starches (normal, waxy and high-amylose maize starches) with remarkably different amylose contents under identical hydrothermal preparation conditions. Although existing literature has verified that dietary fibers can inhibit starch hydration and digestion, it fails to interpret the internal mechanism why amylose content governs the binding intensity between fiber and starch. The present study innovatively quantified the correlations among amylose proportion, complexing index and resistant starch yield, and further proposed a synergistic interaction model specific to WBIDF-starch composite systems. This model categorizes the whole interaction process into three stages: water competition stage, hydrogen bonding combined with physical entanglement stage, and phase separation stage induced by excessive fiber addition. The findings of this work can provide theoretical references for the functional modification of starches with various amylose contents, develop new approaches for the high-value utilization of bulk agricultural by-products such as wheat bran, and lay a foundation for the exploitation of starch-based functional foods with favorable processing properties and specific health benefits.

Materials and methods

Materials

Wheat bran dietary fiber (purity ≥95%, based on fiber content) was purchased from Shaanxi Kairui Herbal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Xi'an, China). Normal maize starch (NMS, Cat. No.: S11149, purity ≥98%, amylose content 23.88%) was obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Waxy maize starch (WMS, purity ≥98%, amylose content 1.44%) and high-amylose maize starch (HAMS, purity ≥98%, amylose content 51.42%) were provided by the National Engineering Research Center for Wheat and Maize Deep Processing. All amylose contents were determined using the Megazyme Amylose/Amylopectin Assay Kit (K-AMYL). Porcine pancreatic α-amylase (A3176, 13 U/mg) ad amyloglucosidase (A7095, 300 U/mL) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). All other reagents were of analytical grade, and all solutions were prepared with distilled water.

Preparation of wheat bran insoluble dietary fiber (WBIDF)

Wheat bran dietary fiber was mixed with distilled water at a certain ratio. After thorough stirring, it was allowed to stand to dissolve the soluble components. The insoluble fraction was collected by filtration through filter paper and washed several times with clean water to remove residual soluble substances. The obtained wheat bran insoluble dietary fiber (WBIDF) was dried in an oven at 45 °C to constant weight and stored in a desiccator for later use.

Preparation of WBIDF-maize starch complexes

Weighed 10 g each of NMS, WMS, and HAMS (dry basis), mixed with 100 mL of water, and added WBIDF to the three starch suspensions at ratios of 0%, 3%, 6%, 9%, 12%, and 15% (w/w, dry basis). The mixtures were dispersed by magnetic stirring at room temperature for 10 min, and then subjected to hydrothermal treatment (95 °C, 30 min) following a method modified from (Zhang et al., 2025). After cooling the samples to room temperature, they were pre-frozen at −20 °C, freeze-dried using a freeze dryer for 48 h, ground, and passed through a 100-mesh sieve to obtain WBIDF-NMS, WBIDF-WMS, and WBIDF-HAMS complexes. Samples without WBIDF addition were used as controls and stored in a desiccator for later use.

Light transmittance

This experiment adopted the method described by (Song et al., 2024) with slight modifications. Accurately weighed 1 g of sample (dry basis) was dissolved in 100 mL of water. The suspension was pasted at 95 °C for 20 min and then cooled to room temperature. The light transmittance of the solution was measured at a wavelength of 620 nm using a microplate reader (Spectra Max 190, Molecular Devices, Inc., Silicon Valley, USA), with distilled water as the blank control.

Solubility and swelling power

This experiment adopted the method described by (Jia et al., 2025) with slight modifications. Weighed 2 g of sample (dry basis) and dissolved it in 100 mL of water, and the sample weight was recorded as W0. The suspensions were gelatinized at 95 °C for 30 min, cooled to room temperature, and then centrifuged at 6000 r/min for 15 min. The supernatant was dried to a constant weight (W1) in a pre-weighed petri dish in a drying oven at 45 °C, and the weight of the precipitate (W2) was recorded. The solubility (S) and swelling power (P) were calculated as follows:S%=W1/W0×100Pg/g=W2/W0×1−S×100

Freeze-thaw stability

The method of (Zhu et al., 2024) was adopted with slight modifications. Weighed 10 g of sample (dry basis), dissolved in 100 mL of water, stirred evenly, heated at 95 °C for 30 min, added the sample cooled to room temperature to a pre-weighed centrifuge tube (G1), and recorded the total weight (G2). The sample was frozen at −20 °C for 24 h, thawed in a 30 °C water bath for 2 h, and then centrifuged at 4000 r/min for 20 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the tube containing the remaining precipitate was weighed (G3). The syneresis rate was calculated as follows:Water separation rate%=G2–G3/G2−G1

Complexing index (CI)

The CI of WBIDF with the three maize starches was determined using the method of (Yu et al., 2024) with slight modifications. Weighed 0.8 g of sample (dry basis), dissolved in 10 mL of water, vortexed for 2 min, completely gelatinized the complex in a boiling water bath, cooled the sample to room temperature, added 50 mL of water and vortexed again, then centrifuged at 8000 r/min for 10 min. A 0.2 mL aliquot of the supernatant was mixed with 8 mL of distilled water and 1 mL of iodine solution (2% KI + 1.3% I₂, w/v), and the absorbance was measured at 690 nm using an enzyme-labeled instrument. The sample without WBIDF was used as a control, and the complexing index (CI) was calculated as follows:CI%=Ac−As/Ac×100

Ac is the absorbance of the control group, and As is the absorbance of the composite system.

Rapid visco analyzer (RVA)

The pasting properties of the samples were evaluated using a Rapid Visco Analyzer (RVA, Model 3JK-1, Perten Instruments, Stockholm, Sweden). A slurry was prepared by mixing 2.5 g of sample (dry basis) with 25 mL of water. The slurry was held at 50 °C for 1 min, heated to 95 °C at a rate of 6 °C/min and held for 2.5 min, then cooled at the same rate to 50 °C and held for 2 min. The sample was stirred at 960 rpm for the first 10 s and then at 160 rpm for the remainder of the test. The total test time was 13 min. The characteristic parameters, including peak viscosity, trough viscosity, breakdown, final viscosity, setback, and pasting temperature, were obtained using the analysis software of the RVA instrument.

Rheological properties

After equilibrating the samples obtained from RVA analysis at 25 °C for 5 min, their rheological properties were measured using a rheometer (MCR302, Anton Paar GmbH, Austria). The measurements were conducted at 25 °C using a parallel plate geometry (4.0 cm in diameter) with a 1.0 mm gap. For steady shear tests, the apparent viscosity was measured as a function of shear rate as it was increased from 0 to 300 s−1 and then decreased back to 0 s−1. For dynamic oscillatory tests, the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G′′) were recorded as a function of angular frequency over the range of 0.1–100 rad/s at a 1% strain.

Gel strength and hardness

Gel samples were prepared following the slurry preparation procedure and RVA pasting program described in Section 2.8. The samples obtained from RVA analysis were sealed with plastic film and stored at 4 °C for 12 h. A texture analyzer (TA-XT. Plus, Stable Micro Systems Co. Ltd., Surrey, UK) equipped with a cylindrical probe (P/0.5) was used to determine the gel strength and hardness. Gel strength was measured using a weak gel test mode with a compression speed of 1 mm/s, a trigger force of 5.0 g, and a compression depth of 10 mm; the maximum force was recorded. Hardness was tested using the Texture Profile Analysis (TPA) mode with the following parameters: pre-test, test, and post-test speeds of 1.00 mm/s, a target mode of strain, a compression strain of 50%, and a trigger force of 5.0 g.

In vitro digestion

The method of (Hu et al., 2023) was followed with slight modifications to determine the contents of rapidly digestible starch (RDS), slowly digestible starch (SDS), and resistant starch (RS). Prior to in vitro digestion measurement, all freeze-dried composite powders were ground and sieved through a 100-mesh sieve to achieve uniform particle size. No additional thermal, enzymatic or solvent pretreatment was performed on the samples. Preparation of the mixed enzyme solution (prepared immediately before use): A 12 g sample of porcine pancreatic α-amylase was dissolved in 80 mL of distilled water. After magnetic stirring, the solution was centrifuged at 4000 r/min for 30 min, and 69 mL of the supernatant was collected. A 9 mL aliquot of a solution prepared by mixing 6.3 mL of amyloglucosidase with 7.2 mL of distilled water was added to the collected supernatant and mixed thoroughly. For the digestion assay, 500 mg of the sample (dry basis) was weighed, mixed with 10 mL of sodium acetate buffer (0.1 M, pH 5.2) and glass beads, and equilibrated at 37 °C for 10 min. Then, 2.5 mL of the mixed enzyme solution was added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C with shaking (170 r/min). At 0, 20, and 120 min, 0.5 mL aliquots of the hydrolysate were collected and mixed with 20 mL of 66% ethanol to inactivate the enzymes. The resulting mixture was centrifuged at 5000 r/min for 5 min. Determination of glucose content: The glucose content was measured using a K-GLUC assay kit (Megazyme, County Wicklow, Ireland). A 0.1 mL aliquot of the supernatant was mixed with 3 mL of GOPOD reagent, incubated at 45 °C for 20 min, and the absorbance was measured at 510 nm using a microplate reader, with a 1 mg/mL glucose standard solution as the reference. The contents of RDS, SDS, and RS were calculated based on the glucose content as follows:RDS%=0.9G20−G0/TS×100SDS%=0.9G120−G20/TS×100RS%=100–RDS−SDSwhere G0, G20, and G120 represent the glucose content produced by hydrolysis at 0 min, 20 min, and 120 min, respectively. The glucose conversion factor was 0.9, and TS represents the total starch content (mg) in the sample.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The samples were uniformly dispersed on conductive gel, sputter-coated with a layer of gold‑palladium alloy, and their morphological features were observed using an ultra-high resolution field emission scanning electron microscope (JSM-IT800, JEOL Ltd., Japan) at 1000× magnification.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

The thermal properties of the samples were determined using a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC, Q2000, TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA), with reference to the method of (Cui et al., 2024). A 3 mg of the sample (dry basis) was mixed with 9 μL of water in an aluminum pan and equilibrated at room temperature for 24 h.The sample was scanned from 30 °C to 150 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under a nitrogen flow of 50 mL/min. The onset temperature (To), peak temperature (Tp), conclusion temperature (Tc), and enthalpy change (ΔH) were recorded.

Fourier transform-infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR)

The samples were analyzed using a FT-IR Spectrometer (Bruker Company, Karlsruhe, Germany). The sample was ground with dried potassium bromide powder at a mass ratio of 1:100 in an agate mortar and pressed into a pellet at 15 MPa for 60 s under vacuum. Spectra were collected by scanning in the range of 4000–400 cm−1. The peak intensity ratio R1047/1022 was calculated after deconvolution using OMNIC 8.0 software.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The analysis was conducted using an X-ray diffractometer (D/MAX2500, Rigaku Corporation, Japan) at a tube voltage of 40 kV and a tube current of 30 mA. Diffraction patterns were collected over a scanning range of 5°-50° (2θ) at a scanning rate of 4°/min with a step size of 0.01°. The relative crystallinity (RC) of the samples was calculated using the following formula:RC%=IC/IC+IA×100where IC and IA represent the cumulative diffraction intensities of the crystalline and amorphous regions in the diffraction pattern, respectively.

Data analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. All experiments were repeated three times. Results were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan's multiple range test in SPSS. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Figs and tables were generated using Origin 2018.

Results and discussion

Light transmittance analysis

The transparency of starch, characterized by its light transmittance, is a critical indicator for evaluating its visual quality and suitability for food processing, as it directly affects the appearance of the final product (Hornung et al., 2018). As shown in Fig. 1A, the light transmittance of maize starches with different amylose contents varied significantly (P < 0.05), following the order of WMS (88.01%) > NMS (76.97%) > HAMS (27.66%). This trend was negatively correlated with the amylose content, which is consistent with previous findings where cassava starch with a low amylose content exhibited higher light transmittance, whereas wheat starch with a higher amylose content showed lower transmittance (Figueroa-Lopez et al., 2024). This phenomenon can be attributed to the crystalline regions formed by amylose, which increase surface roughness and disrupt the uniformity of light reflection (Xu et al., 2024). The addition of WBIDF significantly reduced the light transmittance of NMS and HAMS, primarily due to the light-scattering effect introduced by the WBIDF particles (Srivastava et al., 2023). On the contrary, WBIDF exerts an insignificant effect on WMS. This is attributed to the fact that WMS consists almost entirely of highly branched amylopectin with short outer side chains, which forms loose and flexible gel networks after gelatinization. When blended with WBIDF, abundant branched binding sites on amylopectin readily form weak physical entanglements with WBIDF fibers, and its highly branched structure restrains excessive self-association and aggregation of starch molecules, thereby reducing the light scattering effect (Bof et al., 2021). Solubility and swelling power analysis

The solubility and swelling power of starch are critical parameters for evaluating its potential in processing applications (He, Sun, et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 1B and C, without the addition of WBIDF, WMS exhibited the highest solubility (44.49%) and swelling power (32.41%). This is attributed to its highly branched structure, which readily binds with water molecules to form a stable dispersion system (Lin et al., 2025). NMS showed intermediate values, while HAMS had the lowest. The low values for HAMS are primarily due to its high amylose content, which leads to the formation of tight double-helical structures with strong intermolecular forces, thereby hindering water molecule penetration (Lv et al., 2021). With the increasing addition of WBIDF, the solubility and swelling power of all three maize starches decreased significantly (P < 0.05). The extent of this reduction varied among the starch types and was negatively correlated with the amylose content, meaning that starches with lower amylose content were more significantly affected by WBIDF. On the one hand, starch molecules can form weak hydrogen bonds with the hydroxyl groups on the surface of WBIDF, endowing the starch structure with a certain degree of stability. On the other hand, WBIDF possesses a strong water-holding capacity, competing for water molecules and thus reducing starch hydration, which in turn inhibits solubility. In contrast, the HAMS system showed a smaller decrease in solubility, mainly because its dense crystalline structure makes it difficult for WBIDF to penetrate and interfere (Lv et al., 2021). Furthermore, the initially low solubility of HAMS limited the potential for further reduction.

Freeze-thaw stability analysis

Freeze-thaw stability is a key quality indicator for starch-based foods (Srichuwong et al., 2012) and is typically characterized by syneresis rate, where a lower rate indicates better stability. As shown in Fig. 1D, without the addition of wheat bran insoluble dietary fiber (WBIDF), the syneresis rate of HAMS was significantly higher than that of WMS and NMS, indicating its poorest freeze-thaw stability. This is primarily attributed to its high amylose content, which facilitates rapid and extensive recrystallization during freeze-thaw cycles, leading to the formation of a dense crystalline structure that causes severe gel network shrinkage and water expulsion (Soler et al., 2020). In contrast, WMS, which contains virtually no amylose, showed better freeze-thaw stability because its amylopectin structure is less prone to long-range ordered recrystallization. The stability of NMS was intermediate between the two. With the increasing addition of WBIDF, the syneresis rates of all three starch gels decreased to varying extents, demonstrating that WBIDF can effectively enhance the freeze-thaw stability of maize starch. WBIDF restricts water migration by constructing a network structure (Li et al., 2023). Furthermore, WBIDF molecules interfere with the recrystallization and ordered arrangement of starch molecules through physical entanglement and interactions, thus improving the water-retention capacity of the system (Scott & Awika, 2023). The improvement effect of WBIDF was most pronounced for the HAMS system, where all samples with added WBIDF showed significantly lower syneresis rates than the control group, greatly enhancing the freeze-thaw stability of HAMS.

Complexing index (CI) analysis

The CI is a crucial indicator for evaluating the degree of interaction between WBIDF and maize starch, measured based on the formation of starch‑iodine complexes; a higher CI value indicates a stronger interaction. As shown in Table 1, the CI values for all three starch systems increased significantly with the addition of WBIDF, yet the extent of interaction between different starch types and WBIDF varied significantly, primarily due to their distinct molecular structures. In the WMS system, the CI value first increased, then stabilized, and finally decreased slightly as the WBIDF concentration rose, reaching a maximum of 57.39% at a 12% addition level. This indicates the strongest interaction with WBIDF, which is mainly attributed to the highly branched structure and lower steric hindrance of WMS. At a high addition level (15%), excessive molecular crowding may have shielded some interaction sites, leading to a slight decrease in the CI value, although it remained at a high level overall. The NMS system exhibited the second-highest CI values, suggesting an effective interaction between WBIDF and NMS. This can be attributed to the formation of non-covalent bonds, such as hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, between WBIDF and starch molecules, which promotes complex formation (Li & Hu, 2021). The addition of WBIDF increased the peak, trough, and final viscosities of all three starch systems, indicating that it enhanced the stability and retrogradation tendency of the pastes by increasing the effective starch concentration and mechanical resistance within the system (Baye et al., 2017). Concurrently, WBIDF, acting as a solid dispersed phase, increases the mechanical resistance of the system, thus elevating the overall viscosity (He, Zhang, et al., 2023). Although the breakdown values for each system increased, the sharp rise in peak viscosity and the substantial increase in trough viscosity still suggest that WBIDF enhanced the shear-thinning resistance of the starch systems. The setback values for all three starch systems increased with the addition of WBIDF, indicating that WBIDF promoted the formation of a gel network upon cooling. Through physical filling and hydration, WBIDF provides a favorable environment for the rearrangement and crystallization of amylose molecules, facilitating the formation of a denser and stronger gel structure (Gong et al., 2024b). WBIDF exerted differential effects on the pasting temperatures of NMS and WMS systems. For the NMS system, supplementation with 3%–12% WBIDF significantly increased the pasting temperature, whereas the NMS sample with 15% WBIDF showed no significant difference from the blank control group. For the WMS system, only the group supplemented with 3% WBIDF exhibited an obvious elevation in pasting temperature. These results indicated that WBIDF could alter the energy required for initial water absorption and crystal disruption of starch granules within a specific dosage range. Due to its high amylose content, which forms a highly ordered and dense crystalline structure (Mao et al., 2023), HAMS did not fully gelatinize during the test; consequently, its pasting temperature could not be determined.

Rheological properties analysis

The rheological property curves are shown in Fig. 2. The steady-shear rheological tests revealed that all starch gels exhibited typical shear-thinning behavior. This indicates that the internal network structure of the gels was disrupted under shear force. Overall, the apparent viscosity of the three starch systems generally rose as WBIDF dosage increased across most shear rate ranges, which illustrated that WBIDF could strengthen the cross-linked network of starch gel. Nevertheless, a local reversal trend was observed in the WMS system at the shear rate of approximately 10 s−1, where the apparent viscosity of the 3% WBIDF-WMS sample exceeded that of the 6% WBIDF-WMS group. This enhancement is attributed to WBIDF being dispersed as solid particles within the starch network, which increased the solid-phase volume fraction and thereby enhanced the flow resistance (Regazzi et al., 2019). Its strong water-holding capacity limits the excessive swelling of starch granules, helping to maintain the integrity of the gel network (Culqui-Arce et al., 2025); And it physically entangles with starch molecular chains, forming a more complex and stable network structure (Li et al., 2024). The apparent viscosity of the WMS system was significantly lower than that of the NMS and HAMS systems, and the effect of WBIDF addition was comparatively smaller. This is primarily because WMS, being rich in amylopectin, struggles to form a robust and continuous three-dimensional network. This results in an inherently softer, less viscous gel, within which the reinforcing effect of WBIDF is less pronounced (Gong et al., 2024a). Dynamic frequency sweep tests further elucidated the effect of WBIDF on the viscoelastic properties of the starch gels, where the storage modulus (G') represents the elastic component and the loss modulus (G“) represents the viscous component. For both the NMS and HAMS systems, G' remained consistently higher than G" across the entire frequency range, indicating the formation of a predominantly elastic gel network. In contrast, the G' and G“ values of the WMS system were substantially lower than those of the other two starch systems. Notably, at low angular frequencies, G' was even lower than G", exhibiting characteristics of a viscous-like fluid. This once again confirms the soft nature of the WMS gel network structure. As the concentration of WBIDF increased, the G' and G“ values for all three systems rose, confirming that WBIDF effectively enhances the strength of the gel network. Among them, the G' and G" of the HAMS gel showed weaker frequency dependence, indicating a highly stable network structure (Hsi et al., 2025).

Gel strength and hardness analysis

Gel strength and hardness are key macroscopic indicators of a gel network's ability to resist external pressure and deformation (Vilgis, 2015). Table 1 presents the gel strength and hardness results for the composite systems. In the control groups without WBIDF, the NMS gel exhibited the highest gel strength and hardness. This is attributed to its moderate amylose content, which facilitates the formation of a dense and robust three-dimensional network structure during cooling (Mauro et al., 2023). In contrast, the WMS gel, which is nearly devoid of amylose, had the softest texture, with both gel strength and hardness at extremely low levels. Although HAMS is rich in amylose, its gel strength and hardness were relatively low, possibly because the amylose did not sufficiently leach out and participate in network formation under conventional gelatinization conditions (Han et al., 2023).

The addition of a moderate amount of WBIDF (3%–9%) enhanced the gel strength and hardness of NMS through physical entanglement and improved water binding, thereby increasing the cross-linking density and structural integrity of the starch gel network (Zhao, Li, et al., 2023). However, excessive addition (12%–15%) weakened the network strength due to phase separation. For WMS and HAMS, which form inherently weak gels, WBIDF exerted a consistent reinforcing effect. WBIDF enhances texture by constructing a supportive network and promoting the ordered arrangement of amylose molecules (Jang et al., 2018; Poulose et al., 2022). When the WBIDF addition level increased from 0 to 3%, the hardness of HAMS gel decreased slightly. At low supplementation levels, WBIDF acted as physical separators that disrupted the continuous cross-linking network constructed by amylose chains, weakened the hydrogen bonding forces between starch molecules, and reduced the compactness of the gel. As the WBIDF content further increased from 3% to 15%, rigid WBIDF fibers served as a supporting skeleton within the gel matrix. Abundant intermolecular hydrogen bonds formed between WBIDF and amylose, and the interlaced fibers constructed a dense composite three-dimensional network. Consequently, gel hardness rose continuously with increasing fiber dosage.

In the NMS and WMS systems, the addition of WBIDF did not show a significant or consistent effect on SDS content. However, in the HAMS system, a low dose (3%) of WBIDF significantly increased the SDS content. This may indicate that a moderate amount of WBIDF disrupts the integrity of the compact HAMS structure, promoting the conversion of a portion of its RS fraction into SDS (Zhang et al., 2022). In contrast to the decreasing trend observed for RDS, the addition of WBIDF significantly increased the RS content. In both the NMS and WMS systems, the RS content steadily rose with increasing concentrations of WBIDF. This composite material exhibits promising application prospects for developing low-glycemic staple foods targeted at hyperglycemic populations, and can be processed into functional noodles, whole-grain bread and other products. By contrast, the NMS-WBIDF blended system is suitable for instant sauces and puffed snack foods. From a nutritional perspective, high levels of resistant starch can alleviate sharp postprandial blood glucose spikes, regulate the balance of intestinal microflora, and reduce the risks of obesity and type 2 diabetes. Nevertheless, excessive WBIDF addition will impair the sensory texture and mouthfeel of final products. Therefore, the optimal dosage of dietary fiber should be determined according to specific food categories in practical production.

Morphological structure analysis

Even without the addition of WBIDF, the three starches exhibited different morphologies. Among them, NMS (Fig. 3A1) was characterized by large, sheet-like fragments with a relatively smooth surface and sharp angles, indicating a higher structural order and a lower degree of disruption. Compared to NMS, WMS (Fig. 3B1) consisted of smaller and more irregular fragments, but also had a relatively smooth surface. The structure of HAMS (Fig. 3C1) was agglomerated, porous, and irregular, suggesting that the native gel structure of HAMS is inherently weak. This indicates that there are inherent differences in the native granular structures of the three starches (Su et al., 2024), which is consistent with previous reports. The addition of WBIDF altered the original structure of the starch, making its surface rough and irregular. WBIDF molecules attached to and became embedded on the surface of the starch molecules, leading to a rearrangement and a more compact overall structure, which corroborates the results of the RVA analysis. This phenomenon became more pronounced as the WBIDF concentration increased. This indicates that clear interactions between WBIDF and starch molecules occurred during the gelatinization process, increasing the cross-linking between starch molecules and facilitating the formation of a more stable gel system post-gelatinization (Kou et al., 2022). This further explains the reason for the increased RS content and is consistent with the starch digestibility results. In summary, the SEM image analysis demonstrates that adding WBIDF significantly alters the morphology of the starch, with the extent of this effect depending on the starch type and the WBIDF concentration.

Thermal properties analysis

Table 4 shows the effect of different WBIDF concentrations on the thermal properties of maize starches with varying amylose contents. HAMS exhibited the highest To, Tp, Tc, and ΔH, which is attributed to its high amylose content requiring more energy for dissociation and gelatinization (Franca Lemos et al., 2018). WMS, composed almost entirely of amylopectin, had a less ordered crystalline structure, resulting in the lowest gelatinization temperatures and enthalpy value. The thermal parameters of NMS were intermediate between those of HAMS and WMS. The addition of WBIDF affected the thermal properties of the starches differently. For NMS and WMS, the addition of WBIDF decreased the ΔH. This suggests that the introduction of WBIDF disrupted the starch crystalline structure. By physically dispersing and adsorbing onto the surface of starch granules, WBIDF reduced the relative starch content and disrupted the order and continuity of the crystalline regions (Zhang et al., 2024). Furthermore, the strong water-holding capacity of WBIDF limited the free water available for gelatinization, leading to incomplete swelling of the starch granules and partial disintegration of the crystalline structure, which collectively lowered the energy required for gelatinization (Donmez et al., 2021). Despite the decrease in ΔH, at higher concentrations (15%), the To and Tp values slightly increased, confirming the existence of water competition. The reduced free water content lowered the efficiency of heat transfer to the starch crystalline regions, necessitating higher temperatures to melt the crystals and thus raising the gelatinization temperature (Chakraborty et al., 2022). This also indicates an interaction between WBIDF and starch molecules, which inhibits the gelatinization process and enhances the stability of the starch granules. For the HAMS system, the gelatinization characteristic temperatures (To, Tp, Tc) and gelatinization enthalpy (ΔH) exhibited identical unimodal variation trends with increasing WBIDF dosage. Within the 0%–9% WBIDF range, To, Tp, Tc and ΔH increased continuously and significantly, reaching maximum values at the 9% supplementation level. Further elevating WBIDF from 9% to 15% induced a remarkable and synchronous decline in all four thermal parameters. In the 0%–9% WBIDF range, abundant hydroxyl groups on WBIDF form hydrogen bonds with amylose chains, restricting free water and facilitating the generation of ordered double-helical and crystalline starch structures. The physical barrier effect of WBIDF also hinders starch granule swelling, so higher temperatures are required to complete gelatinization, accompanied by elevated ΔH for crystal dissociation (Chiodetti et al., 2024). When WBIDF dosage exceeds 9%, excessive insoluble fibers create severe steric hindrance that obstructs ordered alignment of amylose chains and suppresses crystal formation. Short-range ordered structure analysis

To investigate the binding mode between WBIDF and maize starch, as well as the structural changes of the complexes, their infrared spectra were measured. As shown in Fig. 4A-C, no significant shifts were observed in the positions of the major peaks for any of the samples. This indicates that the addition of WBIDF did not alter the fundamental chemical structure of the starch, and the interactions between them were predominantly non-covalent (Ek et al., 2021). A broad and strong absorption peak observed around 3500–3250 cm−1 is assigned to the stretching vibration of hydroxyl (O—H) groups, indicating that hydrogen bonding may act as the dominant non-covalent interaction between WBIDF and starch molecules (Zhiguang et al., 2025). The region at 1650–1640 cm−1 represents the H-O-H bending vibration of adsorbed water. The absorption peaks at 1047 cm−1 and 1022 cm−1 represent the ordered structures in the crystalline region and the amorphous structures in the non-crystalline region of starch, respectively. A higher R1047/1022 ratio indicates a greater proportion of ordered structures within the starch molecules (Sangwongchai et al., 2024).To investigate the short-range ordered structure of the composites, the spectral region between 1200 and 800 cm−1 was deconvoluted (Fig. 4D-F), and the R1047/1022 ratio was calculated to evaluate the degree of order (Table 4). The addition of WBIDF increased the R1047/1022 ratio for all three starch systems, indicating that it promoted a more ordered arrangement of the starch molecular chains. The extent to which the different starches responded to the addition of WBIDF varied (Chen et al., 2022).This increase in ordered structure implies that a higher temperature is required for its disruption, leading to an elevated gelatinization temperature. However, the WBIDF molecules simultaneously interfere with some of the hydrogen bonding between starch molecules, reducing the overall integrity of the starch structure and thereby causing a slight decrease in gelatinization enthalpy, which aligns with the previous conclusions. Consequently, this enhances the starch's resistance to enzymatic digestion, increasing the RS content and decreasing the RDS content, consistent with the findings from the digestibility studies.

Long-range ordered structure analysis

XRD is an effective method for characterizing the crystalline structure of starch. As reported in the literature (Su et al., 2024), native NMS and WMS exhibit a typical A-type crystalline structure, whereas HAMS displays a B-type pattern. As shown in Fig. 4G-I, after gelatinization, the characteristic diffraction peaks of the maize starches weakened significantly, and the patterns became predominantly amorphous, indicating that the hydrothermal treatment disrupted their ordered structures (Ma et al., 2024). The addition of WBIDF did not alter the positions of the diffraction peaks but did affect their intensity and sharpness. This suggests that interactions between WBIDF and starch molecules influenced the reorganization of the crystalline structure, which is consistent with the FTIR results. As the concentration of WBIDF increased, the intensity of the characteristic peaks gradually strengthened, indicating that WBIDF promoted the rearrangement of starch molecular chains into more ordered structures (Li, Zhao, et al., 2022). The characteristic peaks for NMS were observed at 17°, 20°, and 23°. In the control group, these peaks were weak and broad, whereas a high concentration of WBIDF enhanced the peak intensities at 17° and 23°. For WMS, the characteristic peaks were located at 17° and 23°. Being composed almost entirely of amylopectin, WMS readily forms regular double-helical structures after gelatinization, exhibiting a strong capacity for recrystallization (Wu et al., 2022). The HAMS control group showed the lowest crystallinity, with weak and broad peaks located at the same positions as those of NMS; the trend in its peak intensity changes was also similar to that of NMS. These results demonstrate that WBIDF significantly promotes the crystallization process of gelatinized starch, increasing the proportion and integrity of the ordered regions. However, the effectiveness of this action varies depending on the molecular structure of the starch.

The analysis of RC showed that WBIDF significantly increased the RC values of all three maize starches (P < 0.05) in a dose-dependent manner. This trend is consistent with the SEM observations and the changes in the R1047/1022 ratio, confirming that WBIDF promotes the ordered arrangement of starch molecules. Predominantly via hydrogen bonding, together with weak van der Waals forces and hydrophobic interactions, WBIDF modulates the rearrangement and crystallization process of starch, thereby stabilizing the short-range ordered structure (Kong et al., 2024). The initial crystallinity of the three starches followed the order WMS > NMS > HAMS, which positively correlated with their amylopectin content, indicating that amylopectin is more prone to forming double-helical structures (Guo, Zhang, & Mujumdar, 2024). In the WMS system, the optimal effect was observed at a 12% WBIDF concentration; higher concentrations may have reduced crystallinity due to excessive molecular aggregation. The abundant amylopectin in WMS formed a stable hydrogen bond network with WBIDF, which fixed the double-helical conformation (Zhong et al., 2025). Despite the more ordered structure, the diffraction peaks were weaker and broader, suggesting that WBIDF altered the mode of intermolecular interactions within the starch. This reduced the forces that needed to be overcome during gelatinization, leading to a lower gelatinization enthalpy, consistent with the DSC results. WBIDF significantly decreased the RDS content and increased the RS content, a phenomenon directly related to the increase in RC. An elevated RC results in a more compact starch structure, enhancing its resistance to amylolytic enzymes and thereby reducing its digestibility (Niu et al., 2025a). The crystalline structures corresponding to the characteristic peaks at 17° and 23°, particularly prominent in WMS, are known to be highly resistant to enzymatic hydrolysis (Wang, Wang, et al., 2022). WBIDF itself is indigestible and further limits enzyme access to starch through physical entanglement as well as dominant hydrogen bonding assisted by weak hydrophobic interactions and van der Waals forces, promoting ordered rearrangement and the formation of more enzyme-resistant crystalline regions.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the regulatory effects of WBIDF on the physicochemical, structural and digestive properties of three maize starches with different amylose contents. Rather than merely replicating the widely reported general interaction phenomena between dietary fiber and starch, this work systematically revealed the amylose-dependent differentiated interaction rules between wheat bran insoluble dietary fiber and the three maize starches. Combined with the quantitative correlation results between structural parameters and digestive characteristics, a three-stage synergistic interaction mechanism was further proposed, which enriches and perfects the theoretical framework of starch modification using insoluble dietary fibers. CI analysis indicated that the interaction was strongest between WMS and WBIDF (CI to 57.39%), primarily attributed to the lower steric hindrance of amylopectin, which facilitates hydrogen bond formation. FTIR and XRD results further confirmed that WBIDF altered starch properties and increased its relative crystallinity mainly via hydrogen bonding, with auxiliary contributions from van der Waals forces and hydrophobic interactions. The interaction strength showed a clear correlation with starch digestibility: a stronger interaction resulted in more pronounced resistance to digestion. Hydrogen bonding increased the gelatinization temperature of the composite system and formed a physical barrier that hindered enzyme-substrate contact, thereby reducing the digestion rate. Meanwhile, water competition by WBIDF limited the complete gelatinization of starch, decreasing enzyme accessibility. The changes in the physicochemical properties of the starch were closely linked to its digestive characteristics: the decrease in light transmittance, solubility, and swelling power reflected an increased compactness of the composite system, which was positively correlated with the reduction in RDS content. Changes in rheological properties, particularly the increase in the storage modulus, indicated an enhanced gel network, which provided a structural basis for the formation of resistant starch. Meanwhile, the freeze-thaw stability is improved. This study elucidates the mode by which WBIDF affects maize starches with different amylose contents, offering precise guidance for developing starch-based foods with low digestibility. However, control experiments with hydrogen bond disruptors (urea or sodium thiocyanate) were not performed in the present work. Relevant disruption tests can be carried out in future research to accurately quantify the relative strength of various intermolecular non-covalent interactions. Meanwhile, to facilitate its application in industrial food systems, follow-up experiments simulating real food formulations and processing technologies should be conducted to evaluate the stability of intermolecular interactions and the retention capacity of low-digestibility properties, thereby strengthening the practical application value of this research.
