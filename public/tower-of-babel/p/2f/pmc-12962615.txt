Preclinical assessment of GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T lipoplex product development for GNE myopathy
Authors: Fabienne Kerneis, Christopher M Jay, Donald Rao, Jeremy Winchester, Alexander Nemunaitis, Emily Nemunaitis, Ernest Bognar, Gladice Wallraven, Laura Stanbery, Adam Walter, Sep Sarshar, John Nemunaitis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsGNE myopathy is a heredity disease of unmet medical need associated with progressive skeletal muscle wasting, atrophy and weakness caused by mutations in the GNE gene. GNE plays a pivotal role in sialic acid production. Sialic acid is a critical part of glycoprotein, ganglioside and glycolipid cell-cell interaction which is necessary for normal skeletal muscle function. Previously we demonstrated safety and efficacy of the GNEwt gene lipoplex in one patient.MethodsWe engineered GNEM743T and dual function GNEwt/bi-shRNA plasmids to evaluate GNEM743T specific knockdown and GNEwt expression. Knockdown efficiency, protein expression, and functional rescue were assessed. A dose range study in mice quantified plasmid delivery and human GNE expression in muscle.ResultsWe demonstrate effective plasmid function via knockdown of the GNEM743T gene mutation and concurrent expression of GNEwt gene in a dose dependent manner. Similar in vitro increase in sialic acid production is shown between prior single function plasmid and GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T dual function plasmid. Moreover, we demonstrate murine in vivo muscle delivery and expression of GNEwt mRNA from the GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T plasmid delivered via DOTAP-Cholesterol lipoplex following intravenous injection.ConclusionThese results encourage future studies, potentially leading toward clinical testing of GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T lipoplex for GNE myopathy.

Introduction

GNE (UDP-N-acetylglucosamine 2-epimerase/N-acetylmannosamine kinase) myopathy, formerly known as Inclusion Body Myopathy 2, Distal Myopathy with Rimmed Vacuoles, Nonaka Myopathy, Quadriceps Sparing Myopathy or Hereditary Inclusion Body Myopathy is an ultra-rare autosomal recessive non-inflammatory muscle disease characterized by early adult onset and progressive debilitating muscle dysfunction inherited through a variety of mutations of the GNE gene [1,2]. GNEM743T is of higher incidence in people of Persian Jewish decent [2]. It is a well-accepted hypothesis that reestablishment of GNEwt function via delivery of expressive GNEwt gene will improve muscle sialic acid production and relieve muscle deterioration. Moreover, diminishing the adverse activity of GNEM743T by knockdown with bi-shRNA-GNEM743T we may further reduce muscle deterioration related to GNEM743T protein associated myopathy and adverse competition with GNEwt protein.

GNEwt protein is the rate limiting enzyme that catalyzes the first two steps of the biosynthesis of sialic acid [3]. Sialic acids are negatively charged monosaccharides typically found as the terminal sugars on glycoconjugates. They play pivotal roles in cellular processes such as cell signaling, cell adhesion, cell migration and inflammation events [4]. GNE Myopathy-associated GNE mutations have been shown to reduce sialic acid production which is essential for proper folding, stabilization, and function of skeletal muscle glycoproteins [5–8]. GNE mutations resulting in hyposialyation of muscle glycoproteins appear to contribute to myofibrillar degeneration and loss of normal muscle function [9]. Thus, most in the field conclude that impaired GNE function [10,11], and/or reduced expression [12] is a key pathogenic factor in GNE myopathy. Indeed, Penner et al. [13] characterized several different GNE mutations and demonstrated altered activity of GNE enzyme related to mutation correlated with varying degrees of severity, as assessed by downstream enzyme kinetics of ManNAc phosphorylation using a radiolabeled phosphate assay. More recently, using a patient-derived induced pluripotent stem-cell model, Schmitt et al implicated defective myogenesis as an additional contributing factor for GNE myopathy [14]. GNE deficiency impaired myogenesis was demonstrated in human muscle cell line C2C12 with GNE knockout [15]. These observations further implicate GNE deficiency not only resulting in reduction of GNE enzyme activity but also impaired muscle myogenesis.

Lack of sialic acid however is not the only fundamental component to GNE myopathy, rather the underlying mechanism also involves deficiency in the ability to properly synthesize and regulate sialic acid due to GNE gene mutations encoding crucial enzymes for sialic acid production [16]. Mutated GNE gene function also disrupts ability to restore normal sialic acid production in muscle cells.

Therapeutic opportunities for patients with GNE myopathy are limited. Treatment options remain limited, with aceneuramic acid being the only approved therapy in Japan as of 2020. As a result, palliative management of disease symptomology is required [17–27]. We thus initiated a preclinical exploration of the GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T lipoplex with the intent to improve the management of GNE myopathy.

The mutant GNE variant protein M743T has significantly reduced enzymatic activity when compared to GNEwt. This effect was related to a single point mutation leading to a substitution of threonine from methionine at position 743 and relates to a change in oligomeric state, and possibly altered protein folding of GNE [3,28]. GNE is known to form multimeric structures. The monomeric form is inactive, while the dimeric and hexameric forms exhibit different enzymatic activities, with the hexameric form showing proficiency in both epimerase and kinase activities [29]. Bennmann et al. [30] found that the M743T variant had 4.5 times increase in O-GlcNAcylation compared to GNEwt. Moreover, the half-life of the M743T variant was more than 2-fold the half-life of GNEwt protein. These factors provide competitive advantage of GNEM743T protein compared to GNEwt protein and support the “push and pull” strategy to lower GNEM743T activity while concurrently enhancing GNEwt protein expression. Weidemann et al. [28] also demonstrated a reduced solubility and lower isoelectric point of the GNEM743T protein in comparison to the GNEwt protein and further demonstrated that the phosphorylation of the GNEM743T protein influenced its isoelectric point. These observations likely relate to the results described by Bennmann. It has also been shown that O-GlcNAcylation extends the half-life of the GNEM743T compared to GNEwt protein activity thereby further contributing to the pathogenesis and severity of GNE myopathy. Removal of O-GlcNAc increases the GlcNAc 2-epimerase activity and the N-acetylmannosamine kinase activity of the M743T variant. O-GlcNAcylation plays a significant role in protein stability, enzyme activities and protein structure conformation. These alterations can also influence enzyme specificity [31–34]. Simultaneous replenishment of wildtype GNE and depletion of the competitive M743T mutant GNE, we hypothesize will more effectively restore functional GNE oligomer complex and muscle support function of GNE.

Previously we demonstrated evidence of improved transgene expression of GNEwt mRNA and protein in correlation with subsequent increased production of sialic acid in CHO-Lec3 cells (with GNE deletion) in vitro along with in vivo models and clinical human evidence of benefit [35–38]. We also complexed the GNE expression plasmid with a cationic liposome, composed of 1,2-dioleoyl-3-trimethylammonium-propane (DOTAP) and cholesterol (GNE-Lipoplex) and demonstrated dose related safety in BALB/c mice with IV injection [9,39]. These results demonstrated correlation of the GNEwt plasmid transfection with GNE transgene and protein expression at the desired target site, murine muscle, and provided sufficient evidence in murine model of safety at dose levels ≤40μg via IV delivery. We also demonstrated safety and improvement in local muscle strength correlating with enhancement of recombinant GNE (rGNE) muscle transgene expression and consequent increase of cell surface sialic acid following IM injection and later IV infusion of GNE-Lipoplex in a single patient with late stage GNE myopathy [37,38].

We now describe a novel upgraded GNE expression plasmid designed to achieve concurrent knockdown of GNEM743T mutation and expression of GNEwt, a dual function plasmid. The dual function plasmid has a bi-shRNAi expression unit engineered on the same plasmid with the GNEwt expression cassette. We also evaluated the final product lipoplex formulation (GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T lipoplex) in a pilot murine study to determine dose range, delivery and expression of the drug to muscle tissue. We hypothesized that concurrent reduction in GNEM743T protein will remove interference of disease related GNEM743T competitive action and will optimize functionality of elevated GNEwt protein expression [30].

Materials and methods

GNE clones and plasmid construction

Construction of the GNE clones and plasmid components describe in this assessment are compared to previous results as well as concurrent available murine models. A summary of performed experiments and planned studies including therapeutics, animal models, delivery and safety considerations are summarized in Supplemental Figure S1.

GNEM743T target plasmid

A GNEM743T target plasmid was created using pUMVC3 plasmid backbone (Aldevron, Fargo ND) by inserting the human GNE coding sequence, with the GNEM743T variant behind CMV promoter.

GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T dual function plasmids

Dual function plasmids express wild-type GNE and GNEM743T knockdown bi-shRNA. Four (4) GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T dual function plasmids were constructed to determine optimal functional knockdown of the GNEM743T mutant transcripts and expression of GNEwt. The pUMVC3 plasmid was the backbone for cloning. GNEwt coding sequence was inserted together with bi-shRNA sequence in the plasmid. Two different bi-shRNA target sequences against the GNEM743T variant were inserted at 2 possible locations, resulting in 4 different combinations of constructs (See Table 1 for bi-shRNA sequence and location). The GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T constructs were co-transfected with the GNEM743T target plasmid for GNEM743T specific knockdown activity assessment by Restriction Fragment Length Polymorphism (RFLP) assay (described below). The GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T plasmids and the GNEM743T target plasmid were constructed by Epoch Life Science (Missouri City, TX).

GNEwt plasmid

GNEwt plasmid was described and tested in prior publication [35]. The GNEwt plasmid is in the same pUMVC3 backbone as the GNEM743T target and GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T dual function plasmids.

Cell culture

HEK 293 (ATCC CRL-1573, Gaithersburg, MD) was maintained in a T225 culture flask with DMEM (ATCC, Manassas, VA) medium supplemented with 10% heat inactivated FBS and 2 mM L-Glutamine. After transfection, cells were cultured with 2% FBS.

K562 KO (with GNE knocked out, was generously provided by Natalia Gomez-Ospina of Stanford University) and K562 wildtype (ATCC, Manassas, VA) were cultured in RPMI (ATCC, Manassas, VA) medium supplemented with 10% heat inactivated FBS and 2 mM L-Glutamine.

GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T lipoplex

The Gradalis DOTAP:Chol Liposome formulation has been published [40]. Briefly, DOTAP and Cholesterol powders at 10:9 molar ratio are dissolved in ethanol and then injected into aqueous buffer (D5W) to create raw liposomes. The raw liposomes are buffer exchanged to remove the ethanol and volume adjusted to create the stock liposome with 10 mM DOTAP, 9.0 mM Cholesterol, in D5W buffer, with < 0.5% residual ethanol. The GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T is diluted to 0.8 mg DNA/mL and then mixed with stock liposomes (1:1 mix ratio) to create the final GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T lipoplex. The final product concentration is 5.0 mM DOTAP, 4.5 mM Cholesterol, 0.4 mg DNA/mL, in D5W buffer. QC testing of the GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T lipoplex final product includes: safety (sterility, endotoxin, and mycoplasma), lipid identity (thin layer chromatography), DNA identity (restriction digest), DNA concentration (Abs260) purity, and physical properties (OD400, Particle Size, Zeta Potential).

Cell transfection by electroporation

HEK 293 cells were grown to 80–90% confluence, harvested using trypsin-EDTA, centrifuged, and then resuspended in OptiMEM at a concentration of 2e6 cells/mL. 0.8e6 cells in 0.4 mL were transferred to a 4 mm gap electroporation cuvette and mixed with plasmids at a varied concentration. Cells were electroporated at 200 V, 1000 µF with infinite resistance and exponential decay, using the BioRad Gene Pulser Xcell (BioRad, Hercules, CA). After electroporation, cells were transferred to 6 wells plates at a concentration of 0.4e6 cells per well with 2.0 mL of cell-appropriate media and cultured for 24h or 48h before harvest.

Cell transfection by DNA-lipoplex

K562 KO cells were harvested, centrifuged, and resuspended in OptiMEM media. GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T lipoplex was mixed with cells at a varied concentration per 4e6 cells in 1 mL and incubated for 2 hours at 37 °C. After the incubation, GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T lipoplex cell mixture was seeded in 6-well plates at 2e6 cells per well with 2 mL media and grown 48 hours.

Restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) assay

Freshly harvested cells or frozen cells pellets were thawed on ice, and total RNA was extracted using Monarch® Total RNA Miniprep Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA). The concentration of extracted RNA was measured with Nanodrop (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA). 1–3ug of total RNA was reverse transcribed using LunaScript® RT SuperMix Kit and random primers (New England Biolabs). cDNA synthesis was done in 20 µL or 40 µL reaction based on manufacturer instructions. cDNA was PCR amplified at the GNEM743T region with designed primer set to create a BstXI restriction enzyme sequence for GNEwt, but not for the GNEM743T variant (Adaptor PCR sequence and BstXI digest is illustrated in Table 2). PCR reaction mixture (25 µL) consisted of Taq 2X Master Mix from NEB (NEB #M0270) with 3–5 µL of cDNA and 0.5 µL each of 1 µM PCR primers. PCR was performed with the Applied Biosystems SimpliAmp Thermal Cycler under standard PCR condition (briefly, 94 °C for 2 minutes, cycles at 94 °C for 15 seconds, 60 °C for 30 seconds and 72 °C for 1 minute for 25–30 cycles then incubated at 72 °C for 4 minutes and hold at 4 °C). RT-PCR amplified DNA was cut using BstXI enzyme (10 µL of PCR product were digested with 20 units of BstXI (NEB) for 45 minutes at 37 °C) and run on a 4% agarose gel (Invitrogen, Carlsbad CA).

Western blot

Cell pellets were lysed using M-PER reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) per manufacturer instructions. Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay kit (ThermoFisher Scientific) with the Epoch plate reader at 595 nm (Agilent BioTek, Santa Clara, CA). Cell lysate samples (approximately 20 to 40 µg) were run on a Mini-Protean Tetra Vertical Electrophoresis Cell and 4–20% Mini-PROTEAN TGX Stain-free Precast Gel (BioRad) and 1X TRIS/Glycine/SDS buffer for 90 min at 75 V/3A/300W. Protein transfer was carried out using the Trans-Blot Turbo System (BioRad) and TransBlot Turbo Mini 0.2 µm PVDF Transfer Pack (BioRad). Samples were transferred at 1.3 A/25V for 10 minutes. Following transfer, the membrane was blocked using EveryBlot Blocking Buffer for 5 min at room temperature. The membrane was incubated with anti-GNE polyclonal antibody (rabbit) (VWR, Radnor, PA) at 1:10,000 dilution and α/β-Tubulin antibody #2148 (rabbit) (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA) at 1:1,000 dilution. Secondary antibody was goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) HRP at a dilution of 1:10,000 (VWR). The membrane was washed and then ECL Western Blotting substrate (BioRad) was added for visualization using the ChemiDoc MP system (BioRad). Quantification of Western the blot bands was done using the ChemiDoc Image lab software, according to the BioRad instructions. The software calculated the intensity for each band, and the GNE to Tubulin ratio was calculated and normalized to the control group.

Sialic acid assay/statistics

Transfected K562 KO cell pellets were lysed using the freeze/thaw method with dry ice bath and dry heat block at 37 °C followed by mechanical shearing to lyse the cells. Lysates were analyzed using the Sialic Acid Assay Kit (Millipore Sigma, St. Louis, MO) as directed. Sialic acid samples were read at 549 nm in the Epoch plate reader. The concentration of the unknown samples was calculated against the kit standard curve and then sialic acid concentration normalized against protein concentration performed using the Bradford Kit Assay (ThermoFisher Scientific) [40]. Replicate samples were tested (n = 4) with the average and standard deviation calculated per treatment group. An ANOVA was conducted to determine whether the means of the three groups differed significantly. Upon identifying a statistically significant result from the ANOVA, pairwise t-tests were subsequently performed to assess specific differences between individual groups.

Murine study

A dose range mouse study was designed to evaluate GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T lipoplex maximum tolerated dose, drug delivery to the muscle, and GNE expression. The animal study was performed at Charles River (Durham, NC). Female BALB/c mice (n = 3 per group, approximately 20 g each) were treated with 25ug, 50 µg, or 100ug DNA-Lipoplex. Mice were injected through the tail vein with a slow IV push of 6–8 seconds. Animals were sacrificed at 24 hr, tissue was preserved in RNALater and shipped to Frontage Labs (Deerfield Beach, FL) for assay.

Preparation of DNA and RNA from murine study tissues for tissue delivery and expression analysis

Frontage Labs extracted total gDNA from mouse tissues using the Chemagic DNA 400 Kit on the Chemagic 360 machine (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA). Total RNA was extracted using Tri Reagent (Molecular Research Center, Cincinnati, OH) by manual method. Both gDNA and total RNA was quantified by Abs260. DNA quality was assessed by 0.8% gel electrophoresis and RNA quality was assessed via Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA).

End-point PCR

An aliquot of extracted gDNA was returned to Gradalis for end-point PCR. Custom designed pGNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T plasmid specific primers (forward: GAAGGCCCAGAGCATCCTAA and reverse: CGTTGTGTAGTCCAGAACCAT) were used with 2.5 ng template gDNA for end-point PCR (33 cycles using the SimpliAmp Thermal Cycler from Applied Biosystems). End-point PCR reactions were loaded on 4% agarose E-gel (Invitrogen) and the gel image was captured with ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad).

ddPCR for murine tissue DNA delivery analysis/statistics

The remaining gDNA was analyzed at Frontage Labs by ddPCR analysis. A custom designed primer/probe set was tested and demonstrated to specifically detect the GNE plasmid DNA, without cross-reacting with endogenous mouse GNE. gDNA samples were restriction digested with HindIII and BsrGI-HF to linearize the template DNA. ddPCR was performed using 10 ng restriction digested gDNA per reaction. The assay lower limit of quantitation (LLOQ) was 20 copies. Results were reported as copies GNE DNA per 10 ng total gDNA input. ddPCR results are reported as average of each treatment group (n = 3), with standard deviation, and t-test analysis against the control group for significant differences.

RT-ddPCR for murine tissue RNA expression analysis/statistics

Total RNA was extracted from tissue, DNase digested to remove residual DNA, and converted to cDNA. The commercially available TaqMan Human GNE (hGNE) kit (HS01103400_m1, Thermo Fisher) was used to detect GNE cDNA. The cDNA ddPCR assay was tested and demonstrated to specifically detect the plasmid expressed human GNE RNA, without cross-reacting with endogenous mouse GNE. The RT-ddPCR assay LLOQwas 10 copies. Results were reported as copies hGNE cDNA per 10 ng total RNA input. RT-ddPCR results are reported as average of each treatment group (n = 3), with standard deviation, and t-test analysis against the control group for significant differences.

Results

Construction of GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T dual function plasmids and GNEwt, GNEM743T expression plasmids

There is currently no GNEM743T expressing cell line available. In order to assess the effectiveness of GNEM743T specific knockdown, a GNEM743T target expression plasmid (Supplemental Figure S2A) was constructed for co-transfection with GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T dual function plasmids.

Four different GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T dual function plasmids were designed to express GNEwt and concurrently knockdown GNEM743T expression. The GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T plasmids were constructed with an expression cassette of GNEwt cDNA and a bifunctional shRNA unit in tandem. (Supplemental Figure S2B,C) The expression cassette was inserted behind the CMV promoter. The bifunctional shRNA unit is either in front of GNE coding sequence (Supplemental Figure S2B) or behind (Supplemental Figure S2C). Four GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T plasmids were designed to compare efficiency of GNEM743T knockdown and GNEwt expression. Two sets of bi-shRNA target sequences have slightly different seed region sequences shown in Table 1 (construct #1 and #2 vs. #3 and #4). The seed region sequence affects the efficiency of RNAi process, by locating the single nucleotide mutation sequence at slightly different locations of the seed sequence, the efficiency and selectivity could be different as we have observed with prior product construction to other molecular targets [40–43].

The location of bi-shRNA-GNEM743T relative to the CMV promoter directed transcription unit could also affect the efficiency of shRNA process and GNEwt expression. The bi-shRNA sequences either replaced the Intron A sequence in front of the GNE cDNA (construct #1 and 3) or placed behind GNE cDNA (construct #2 and #4). CMV Intron A contains a series of secondary structures that resemble the secondary structures of bi-shRNA. Placing the bi-shRNA at Intron A may improve the secondary structure processing which may affect activity. Each of the four bi-shRNA plasmid constructs were made with a RNAi target sequence match at the GNEM743T location to differentiate GNEwt from GNEM743T. The RNAi target sequences and their locations are summarized in Table 1.

Dose related expression of GNE and knockdown of GNEM743T assayed by restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP)

There is currently no antibody available to differentiate GNEwt from GNEM743T protein. We utilized the RFLP method to differentiate GNEwt vs. GNEM743T at the mRNA level. An adaptor PCR forward primer was designed to incorporate BstXI recognition sequence for PCR (See Table 2). The amplified PCR products for GNEwt contain the restriction enzyme BstXI recognition sequence while PCR products for GNEM743T do not. Consequently, GNEwt PCR products are digested by BstXI resulting in a smaller size (28 bp shorter) fragment than GNEM743T PCR product and can be visualized by gel electrophoresis.

The initial screen described above (Figure 1A) was with equal amount of GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T dual function plasmid and GNEM743T target plasmid. We next explored dose response of the GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T plasmid in terms of GNEM743T knockdown and GNEwt expression. We selected construct #3 based on results in Figure 1A to explore the dose response relationship in GNEM743T knockdown and GNEwt expression (Figure 1B). HEK293 cells were co-transfected with varied amounts of construct #3 (from 0x to 3x relative to GNEM743T target plasmid) together with a fixed amount (5 µg) GNEM743T expressing target plasmid. 24 hours post transfection, total cellular RNA were isolated and RT-PCR with BstXI adaptor primer set, PCR products were digested with BstXI and run on a 4% agarose gel. Increasing doses of construct #3 resulted in dose dependent decrease of GNEM743T transcripts in comparison to GNEM743T target plasmid only transfection (Figure 1B, Lanes 4-1 vs. Lane 5) indicate knockdown by GNEM743T targeting bi-shRNA component of dual function plasmid; concurrent to decrease in GNEM743T transcript, GNEwt transcripts were increased (GNEwt transcribed from GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T dual function plasmid) (Figure 1B lane 5, no GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T plasmid to lane 1 high GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T dose concentration). The mutant bands of each combination transfection (Figure 1B lanes 1–4) were compared to mutant expression plasmid only transfection (Figure 1B lane 5) to estimate knockdown efficiency using ImageJ software. The optimal knockdown was demonstrated at 1:2 and 1:3 ratio of GNEM743T target expression plasmid to dual function plasmid (79.2% knockdown Figure 1B lanes 1, 2). Within the analysis timeframe, knockdown was saturated at 1:2 ratio and dose dependent, therefore 1:2 was chosen as the optimal dose ratio for further analysis. GNEwt expression showed steady increase as increasing dose of GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T were applied at transfection. It appears the GNEwt transcript expression from the GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T plasmid was not adversely affected by the knockdown of GNEM743T transcripts, GNEwt mRNA expression from the dual function plasmid alone was not assessed in this study. Construct #4 showed similar dose response (Data not shown).

GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T dual function plasmid expresses GNE protein

We next tested the GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T dual function plasmid expression of GNE wt protein without interference from the GNEM743T knockdown component. GNE knockout K562 cells (K562 KO) present a platform to analyze the expression of GNE protein from the vector without endogenous GNE background. Human GNE protein has a known molecular weight range from 66 to 79 kDa, β-Tubulin with molecular size range around 50–55 kDa close to GNE size was selected as the endogenous expressed protein load control. K562 KO cells were transfected with either GNEwt or GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T construct #3 Lipoplex. Both GNEwt and GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T treated cells showed expression of GNE protein by western blot analysis (Figure 2, Lanes 2 and 3) while liposome only treatment did not result in appearance of GNE protein (Figure 2, Lane 1). The GNE band and β-Tubulin band intensities were quantified, quantified value of GNE from each treatment group was normalized with β-Tubulin load control of the empty liposome control for comparison. Quantitatively, the GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T construct #3 expressed a similar amount of GNE protein as the GNEwt plasmid (Figure 2) demonstrating comparable GNE expression without significant interference from the bi-shRNA unit. The same secondary antibody was used for both GNE and β-Tubulin which may affect the precise quantification of relative GNE protein level but should not be widely different.

Detection of GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T plasmid in muscle of in vivo systemic delivered GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T lipoplex

The next step was to evaluate a dose range of GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T lipoplex in vivo and determine if the plasmid lipoplex formulation was delivered to the quadriceps muscle using a murine model. Muscle is known to be difficult organ for systemic delivery of gene therapy agents [44]. A pilot study was designed to determine if formulated lipoplex was delivered to muscle. While GNE is characterizing by a sparing of the quadriceps muscle, the quadriceps was chosen to because it provides sufficient mass for downstream studies. A follow up study is planned to evaluate other muscle tissues. Normal BALB/c mice were chosen because the study scope was to determine maximum tolerated dose and drug delivery to the muscle, not drug efficacy. The animals (n = 3 per group) were treated with a single IV injection of GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T lipoplex at 25 µg, 50 µg, or 100 µg and then sacrificed 24 hours after treatment. Animals receiving 50ug dose showed lethargy and ruffled fur which was identified as the maximum tolerated dose. One animal in the 100 µg group was found dead the day after treatment, therefore 100 µg was identified as intolerable dose. Quadriceps tissues were harvested for nucleic acid extraction and PCR analysis. Mouse quadriceps from the 50 µg GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T lipoplex treatment group (n = 3) were extracted for total DNA and analyzed for drug delivery, as a first look before additional samples were tested. An end-point PCR assay with custom designed primers was designed to detect plasmid GNE without cross-reacting with the mouse gDNA. The plasmid DNA was detected in all (n = 3) 50 µg dose treated quadriceps samples, but not in the 0 µg untreated mice (Figure 4). Results indicate successful delivery of plasmid DNA into mouse quadriceps muscle at maximum tolerated dose.

Detection of GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T Dual Function Plasmids in Murine Muscle After Treatment. Normal laboratory strain mice were treated with GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T dual function lipoplex. Three mice per group were treated with 50 µg of GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T lipoplex via single tail vein injection and tissues collected 24 hours after treatment. PCR primers were specifically designed to amplify GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T. Total DNA harvested from quadriceps muscles of treated mice (lanes 4–6) demonstrated the presence of plasmid DNA compared to non-treated mice (lanes 1–3).

Quantification of GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T plasmid DNA and human GNEwt mRNA from muscle of in vivo treated mice further demonstrates delivery and expression with lipoplex formulation

Based on the initial end-point PCR results for the 50ug muscle samples, the delivery and expression of GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T lipoplex in each dose group was quantified using ddPCR analysis of quadriceps tissue. Custom DNA ddPCR primers were designed to detect human GNE portion of plasmids without cross reacting with the mouse genomic DNA. The ddPCR results demonstrate delivery of GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T plasmid to the quadriceps in the GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T lipoplex treated groups (Figure 5A). There was no human GNE expression plasmid detected in the 0 µg group. PCR results demonstrate a dose-response delivery of plasmid DNA to the muscle tissue following a single IV injection.

Discussion

Previously, in preclinical testing and with management of one patient with advanced GNE myopathy, we showed that plasmid delivery of GNEwt plasmid via DOTAP-cholesterol lipoplex is feasible (DNA delivery/RNA expression) to quadriceps muscle via IV infusion [38]. Treatment was reasonably well tolerated as measured beyond 1 year of IV dosing every 2 months. Furthermore, we demonstrated evidence of clinical benefit related to stabilization of muscle strength/function in association with systemic GNEwt lipoplex infusion [38]. However, in the majority of muscles tested over time, strength/function was only minimally or not improved at all. We postulated given the reported relationship of GNEM743T mutant protein inhibitory effect to GNEwt activity that a dual function GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T lipoplex product concurrently reduce the inhibitory mutant and replenish wt may be necessary to enhance GNEwt gene replacement activity. In this report, we demonstrated the designed dual function plasmid with successful activity of both components. Specifically concurrent knockdown of GNEM743T and expression of GNEwt protein and mRNA as well as related sialic acid production. We also verified in a murine model the successful delivery of GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T plasmid and expression of human GNE mRNA in muscle tissue. Based on these results as rationale for therapeutic opportunity for GNE myopathy patients with M743T mutation, we showed feasibility of concurrent GNEwt expression with knockdown of GNEM743T. Using this “push and pull” strategy to express GNEwt in combination with concurrent GNEM743T knockdown we believe we have shown proof of principle for additional testing, including further preclinical development of this strategy toward clinical testing of patients with GNEM743T myopathy.

The bi-shRNA based technology is an exquisitely specific knockdown technology, with capacity to target single nucleotide RNA alteration. We have previously published design and activity of several novel bi-shRNA lipoplexes, including constructs designed to knockdown KRAS, PDX1, STMN1, EWS/FLI1 and FURIN that have demonstrated specificity, safety and efficacy in oncogene knockdown in preclinical and clinical testing [40–43,45,46]. Preclinically, we have demonstrated target specificity and effective knockdown of KRAS G12 mutations and pancreatic and duodenal homeobox-1 (PDX1) using bi-shRNA constructs specific to each target. Knockdown was demonstrated in mutant specific cell lines and murine xenograft models [42]. Additionally, with bi-shRNA EWS/FLI LPX we demonstrated safety and survival benefit in a murine xenograft model of Ewing’s sarcoma. Gemogenovatucel-T (Vigil) is the most advanced bi-shRNAi technology. It is a triple function immunotherapy that is prepared using autologous tumor cells transfected with a dual function plasmid, which expresses GM-CSF and a bi-shRNA component to knockdown mRNA expression of FURIN [47]. Vigil has been studied in multiple clinical trials and has demonstrated remarkable safety and significant evidence of efficacy recently highlighted by RMAT designation in a unique subpopulation of advanced ovarian cancer patients [48–50]. Furthermore, Phase 1 clinical trial with bi-shRNA-STMN1 demonstrated proof of principle to mechanism of bi-shRNA inhibitory action by identifying the predicted target specific cleavage product via next generation sequence assessment testing [46].

For each bi-shRNA design, we routinely examine the potential off-target effects of bi-shRNA constructs via mRNA-Seq profiling. mRNA-Seq profiles from various doses of the bi-shRNA constructs transfected cells are compared against un-transfected or mock transfected cells. The bioinformatics analysis of mRNA-Seq profiles comparisons is focused on differentially expressed genes (DEG), particular emphasis on mRNAs with reduced expression. mRNA-Seq identified genes with reduced expression as the result of bi-shRNA transfection are further analyzed for homology with bi-shRNA target sequence. If numerous highly homologous gene sequences are identified through this process, alternative constructs are designed.

Several FDA approved products involving plasmid delivery have been proven to justify FDA recommendation for use in clinical management [38,51]. Plasmid construction can also be engineered to provide prolonged gene expression without integration in the genome or risk of replication [52,53]. Persistence elements (i.e., inverted terminal repeats [54,55]) can be incorporated into plasmid design to transiently prolong gene expression in vitro and in vivo thereby minimizing time of treatment timepoints and frequency of patient treatments. Inducible promoters can also be considered to promote expression in the presence of a positive regulator or in the absence of a negative regulator [56]. Taking promoter-enhancer sequence elements from a tissue specific gene to include in our expression plasmid, may for example, lead to as much as a 70-fold increased gene expression in vivo [57]. Such an approach will need to be explored as a possible future plasmid design if short-term GNE expression and GNEM743T knockdown demonstrate evidence of clinical muscle function enhancement. To evaluate the efficacy of the dual function “push–pull” approach, suitable in vitro and in vivo models of GNE myopathy are required, particularly for the M743T mutation. The mechanism and specificity of bi-shRNA requires an exact sequence match to the target sequence. While no ideal human M743T model currently exists, GNE knockout or mutant cell lines, including HEK293 and K562 systems, have been used to study GNE biology, with HEK293 cells providing a practical platform for transfection and monitoring sialylation changes under serum-depleted conditions [58–61]. Genetically edited muscle cell models also reveal altered F-actin and reduced epimerase activity [62]. However, patient-derived M743T muscle cells show inconsistent sialylation changes [63]. In vivo, Gne knock-in and transgenic mouse models demonstrate variable phenotypes, including renal failure, hyposialylation, and muscle involvement [63–68]. These models are based on the murine GNE sequence that is significantly divergent from human sequence around the M743T region and therefore would not be suitable for testing the human specific bi-shRNA sequence. Dual function constructs with murine GNE mRNA sequence and bi-shRNA tailored to the specific murine GNE sequence is necessary to validate for mutation-specific knockdown and rescue activity in vivo with GneM743T knock in mouse model, they could be utilized to test dual function capacity.

However, GNEM743T is only one of more than 376 genetic variations involving GNE myopathy syndrome [12,69]. RNAi targeted therapy is limited in that it only impacts the specific mutation, in this case GNEM743T, not all variants, and clinical evidence of efficacy in patients with GNEM743T myopathy will act as proof of principle for individualized plasmid constructs targeting other mutations.

Other therapeutic assessments involving non plasmid based products have thus far shown limited evidence of benefit to patients with GNE myopathy. Oral administration of n-acetylmannosamine (ManNAc) in advanced GNE myopathy patients was well tolerated and induced sustained increases in circulating levels of free n-acetlyneuraminic acid (Neu5Ac) [19]. Subsequently, a Phase 2 trial was conducted on 12 patients with GNE myopathy to further characterize ManNAc replacement therapy. Patients demonstrated a slower rate of decline and small transient improvement in extremity strength as well as muscle function enhancement via evaluation by “Adult Myopathy Assessment Tool” (AMAT [17]). However, these changes did not translate into statistically significant or durable functional benefit over longer follow up. Additionally, the biochemical assessment of treated patient samples did show intracellular biosynthesis of increased Neu5Ac following ManNAc [18,19] which support the conclusion that ManNac does not demonstrate significant durable functional muscle benefit.

Sialic acid (Neu5Ac/g) supplementation along with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) treatment also showed limited efficacy and demonstrated transient muscle strength improvement with no effect on muscle sialylation [20]. Transient adverse events including nausea, vomiting, headache, anorexia, and constipation were observed, thereby limiting further treatment.

Testing of an extended release formulation of aceneuramic acid (Ace-ER) initially demonstrated positive activity in Japanese variant GNE myopathy patients. A comparative single and multiple ascending dose Phase 1 trial of immediate release aceneuramic acid (Ace-IR) and Ace-ER showed that patients receiving Ace-ER had fewer adverse events [21]. Patients receiving Ace-ER achieved significantly higher serum levels of aceneuramic acid, compared to no increase for the Ace-IR group. A follow up study in Japan further evaluated Ace-ER vs. placebo in GNE myopathy patients showed statistically significant preservation of muscle strength [22,23]. However, a Phase 3 global trial of Ace-ER failed to demonstrate stabilization or improvement in muscle function extremity scores, knee extensor strength or improved GNE myopathy-Function Activity scale over placebo [24].

6’-Sialyllactose (6SL) is present in high concentrations in human milk and can be converted into sialic acid via an alternative pathway involving sialidases (also known as neuraminidases). Earlier preclinical studies showed that oral administration of 6SL in a GNE myopathy mouse model resulted in the recovery of muscle function and volume even during the symptomatic phase of the disease [70]. Based on these observations, two clinical trials were carried out to analyze the effects of oral doses of 6SL in patients with GNE myopathy. The first trial included 10 patients in a pharmacokinetic study and 20 patients in an efficacy and safety study over 96 weeks [25]. This trial lacked a placebo arm after the first 12 weeks but observed sustained improvement or stabilization in proximal muscle strength. The second trial followed 11 patients over 48 weeks (6 in the 6SL arm/5 in the placebo arm) [26]. This study did not demonstrate superior muscle strength compared to the placebo group, however biological activation through significant re-sialylation of cells showed a trend toward slowed muscle fat degeneration on MRI.

Several different GNE mutations beyond GNEM743T have demonstrated altered activity of GNE enzyme function as assessed by downstream enzyme kinetics [13]. All mutations reduce GNEwt enzymatic activity which may relate to gradual muscle function deterioration over many years rather than sudden myopathy. In support of this, Savelkoul et al. [11] suggested that GNE myopathy defects related to sialylation appear gradually in muscle tissue in relation to disruptions in GNE enzyme function.

It has also been reported that despite decreased biochemical activity of mutated GNE, the overall sialic acid levels in patients can be unaffected [71] or unrelated to clinical significance. Some mouse models carrying the GNE M743T alleles generated through homologous recombination in fact do not develop myopathy, which limits full evaluation of products designed to impact GNE myopathy [64,72]. Although the transgenic mouse model Gne(-/-)hGNED176V-Tg, which expresses the human GNE p.D176V variant shows functional myopathy, this is not the M743T variant [65].

Prevalence of GNE myopathy is suggested by Celeste et al. and is predicted to involve ∼40,000 patients worldwide. A recent publication attempting to determine the disease prevalence allele frequencies from population genetic databases estimated the prevalence to be 11–88 individuals per million [69]. However, only about 800 patients with clinical symptomatology have been reported, the reason for the discrepancy is unclear [12]. It could be due to under-diagnosis particularly outside USA, Japan and Europe and may be related to null mutation in GNE likely being associated with embryonic lethality.

In conclusion, evidence presented here supports further testing and preclinical evaluation of the dual function GNEwt/bi-shRNA-GNEM743T lipoplex as a potential future therapy for patients with GNE myopathy with the GNEM743T mutation.
