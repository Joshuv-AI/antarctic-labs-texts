Munc13 serves as a molecular filter for SNARE sorting and assembly in active zone condensates
Authors: Hong Zhang, Yu Zhang, Zhifei Zhao, Zelin Cao, Kexu Zhao, Mengshi Lei, Shen Wang, Cong Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceOur study reveals that the recruitment of Munc13-1 into RIM1/RIM-BP2 biomolecular condensates is not merely a localization event but a fundamental mechanism that confers transformative new functions. Within the condensate, Munc13-1 evolves from a soluble catalyst into an integrated organizational hub, capable of spatially sorting and concentrating client SNAREs and orchestrating their assembly with high fidelity. The condensate environment enables Munc13 to cluster Syb2 into nanodomains on vesicle membranes, thereby dramatically increasing local SNARE density and ensuring efficient SNARE complex assembly. Thus, biomolecular condensates provide the essential physical-chemical context that unlocks the full catalytic potential of Munc13-1, translating weak, transient interactions into rapid, precise, and spatially confined membrane fusion.

Neurotransmitter release mediated by synaptic exocytosis depends on the rapid fusion of synaptic vesicles (SVs) with the presynaptic membrane, a process tightly regulated by the release machinery at fusion sites (1, 2). The core components of the machinery are SNARE proteins, composed of synaptobrevin-2 (Syb2) on SVs, and syntaxin-1 (Syx1) and SNAP-25 (SN25) on the plasma membrane, which assemble into the four-helical bundle SNARE complex that provides the energy for membrane fusion (3–5). SVs carry approximately 70 copies of Syb2, and the presynaptic plasma membrane contains an estimated 500 ∼ 1,500 copies of Syx1 and SN25 per µm2 (6, 7). Although a single SNARE complex seems sufficient to drive fusion of small unilamellar vesicles (SUVs) in vitro (8), the number required per fusion event in vivo remains debated, with estimates ranging from 2 to over 10 (9–14). In addition, a constitutively open mutation in Syx1 accelerates synaptic vesicle fusion by increasing the number of engaged SNARE complexes, indicating that fusion kinetics depend on SNARE abundance (15). The high local density of SNAREs enables multiple complexes to cooperate, thereby achieving efficient membrane fusion. However, it remains unclear how SNAREs are efficiently targeted, clustered, and assembled at fusion sites to enable robust synaptic exocytosis.

The presynaptic active zone is a specialized region beneath the plasma membrane where SV fusion occurs. Ultrastructurally, it appears as an electron-dense projection and is composed of conserved scaffolding proteins, including Rab3-interacting molecule (RIM), RIM-binding protein (RIM-BP), Munc13, glutamine, leucine, lysine, and serine-rich protein (ELKS), and Liprin-α (16, 17). These components form nanoscale clusters essential for active zone integrity and function (18–21). Increasing evidence establishes that liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS), principally mediated by RIM and RIM-BP, drives active zone assembly (22–24). These biomolecular condensates recruit and cluster voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCCs) at fusion sites thereby facilitating Ca2+-triggered exocytosis. The functional importance of this organization is highlighted by genetic and pharmacological perturbations: double knockouts of RIM and RIM-BP, or RIM and ELKS, abolish neurotransmitter release and disrupt active zone ultrastructure (25, 26), while phase separation disruptors disassemble RIM clusters and impair evoked release (27). Thus, RIM/RIM-BP-mediated LLPS constitutes a fundamental structural and functional basis for synaptic exocytosis.

Munc13 proteins are scaffolding molecules highly enriched at the presynaptic active zone, where they play essential roles in multiple stages of synaptic exocytosis—including SV docking, priming, and fusion (28, 29). Munc13-1 is recruited to the active zone through a direct molecular interaction between its N-terminal m domain and the zinc finger (ZF) domain of RIM-1 (30, 31). The RIM-dependent localization positions Munc13-1 near VGCCs and fusion sites, and is important for vesicle tethering and docking. Beyond its role as a scaffolding protein essential for active zone assembly, Munc13-1 also regulates synaptic vesicle priming via direct, albeit weak, interactions with SNAREs. Its central MUN domain acts as a catalytic module that promotes the transition from the closed Munc18-1/Syx1 (M18-1/Syx1) complex to the fusion-competent SNARE complex (32–35). Key functional elements include i) the NF pocket, which binds M18-1/Syx1 to facilitate local conformation activation of Syx1 (32), and ii) subdomain D, which interacts with Syb2 to facilitate the association of Syb2 with the M18-1/Syx1 complex (36). Furthermore, it was implicated that Munc13-1 also binds and recruits SN25 via its MUN domain, thereby chaperoning the complete assembly of the SNARE complex (37). These interactions collectively underscore the central role of the Munc13-1 MUN domain in orchestrating SNARE complex formation and synaptic priming.

Based on the dual functionality of Munc13-1 in both active zone organization and SNARE assembly—mediated via its interactions with RIM-1 and SNAREs—we engineered a C2A-MUN chimera that preserves both molecular binding capabilities. Our results found that upon recruitment into RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates, C2A-MUN acquires enhanced activity, enabling it effectively to promote selective SNARE integration and catalyze SNARE complex assembly within this condensed environment. Furthermore, C2A-MUN promotes the coating of Syb2-bearing vesicles on condensate surfaces and induces Syb2 clustering on the membrane. Consistent with these findings, the introduction of RIM1/RIM-BP2 in PC12 cell induces the formation of synaptic active-zone-like condensates at the plasma membrane, which enhances dense-core vesicle (DCV) exocytosis via a Munc13-dependent mechanism. These results reveal a previously unrecognized function of Munc13-1 as a molecular filter and concentrator that selectively enriches SNAREs at privileged release sites within RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates.

Results

Munc13-1 Mediates SNAREs Recruitment into RIM1/RIM-BP2 Condensates.

RIM1/RIM-BP2 biomolecular condensates form specialized functional microdomains at presynaptic active zones, serving as privileged sites for SNARE complex assembly that drives synaptic vesicle fusion with the plasma membrane. The essential priming factor Munc13-1 is known to localize to these sites through a well-characterized interaction between its N-terminal C2A domain and RIM1’s zinc finger (ZF) domain, while its central MUN domain plays a catalytic role in SNARE complex formation essential for vesicle priming. To directly test the hypothesis that Munc13-1 actively orchestrates SNARE proteins enrichment within these condensates, we designed a minimal yet fully functional C2A-MUN chimera (residues 1 to 209 and 933 to 1531) that retains both the RIM1-binding C2A domain and the SNARE-catalyzing MUN domain (Fig. 1A). This strategic construct design enabled us to systematically investigate: i) the molecular requirements for Munc13-1 incorporation into RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates, and ii) its capacity to recruit and organize SNARE proteins within these phase-separated compartments.

To reconstitute RIM1/RIM-BP2-mediated phase separation in vitro, we utilized the SpyCatcher/SpyTag system to generate full-length RIM1 by ligating two fragments: RIM1-N (residues 1 to 474) and RIM1 PASB (residues 475 to 1334) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B). The purified RIM1 protein underwent robust LLPS at physiologically relevant concentrations (≤1 μM) upon addition of equimolar RIM-BP2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C), as previously reported (24). We then investigated C2A-MUN recruitment into these condensates. Fluorescence imaging revealed significant enrichment and colocalization of the C2A-MUN chimera with RIM-BP2 in phase-separated droplets (Fig. 1B). In stark contrast, the isolated MUN domain that lacks the C2A domain exhibited negligible partitioning into the condensates (Fig. 1B). Together, these data provide compelling evidence that the recruitment of Munc13-1 to RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates is principally governed by specific interactions between its C2A domain and RIM1.

We next investigated C2A-MUN’s capacity to mediate SNARE recruitment into RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates by introducing purified M18-1/Syx1, Syb2, and SN25 components. We found that C2A-MUN was absolutely required for recruiting any components into RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates, underscoring its role as a master organizer (Fig. 1C). Syb2 was the most efficiently recruited component, incorporating strongly even in the absence of M18-1/Syx1 and SN25 (Fig. 1C). In contrast, M18-1/Syx1 recruitment required Syb2, but not SN25 (Fig. 1C). Finally, SN25 incorporation depended on the prior recruitment of both Syb2 and M18-1/Syx1 (Fig. 1C). This hierarchy suggests a precisely ordered assembly pathway: Munc13-1 nucleates a core complex of Syb2 and M18-1/Syx1, which in turn templates the final incorporation of SN25.

To systematically dissect the mechanistic basis of C2A-MUN-dependent SNARE recruitment, we employed a structure-guided mutagenesis approach targeting four critical interaction interfaces: i) the NF pocket in MUN (N1128A/F1131A) required for M18-1/Syx1 binding (32), ii) the Syx1 linker region (R151A/I155A) necessary for NF pocket engagement (38), iii) the Syb2 C-terminus (R86A/K87A/W89A/W90A, termed 4M) that interacts with the MUN subdomain-D (36), and iv) the SN25 linker region (SN25 residues 83 to 142 replaced with SN23 residues 83 to 142, termed SN25SN23) that binds to the MUN domain (37). These mutations, carefully designed based on prior structural and biochemical evidence, specifically disrupted defined molecular interactions while preserving protein folding and condensate formation properties. As expected, these mutations significantly impaired C2A-MUN-mediated recruitment of SNAREs into RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates (Fig. 1D), demonstrating that precise MUN–SNARE recognition is essential for efficient recruitment.

To further investigate regulation, we tested Doc2B, a calcium sensor for spontaneous release that competes with M18-1/Syx1 for the MUN NF pocket (39). As expected, Doc2B localized robustly to condensates via C2A-MUN (Fig. 1E). Its recruitment, however, potently and specifically inhibited SN25 incorporation without affecting Syb2 (Fig. 1E), consistent with it blocking the M18-1/Syx1-dependent step in the assembly pathway (Fig. 1C). The interaction-deficient Doc2B I20A mutant served as an important negative control, showing neither significant condensate partitioning nor inhibitory effects on SNARE recruitment (Fig. 1E). Taken together, our findings suggest Munc13-1 as a central organizational hub and specificity filter that actively enriches and orders SNAREs within presynaptic RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates to ensure efficient vesicle priming.

Munc13-1 Promotes SV Coating on RIM1/RIM-BP2 Condensates via Specific Binding with Syb2.

SVs are densely packed with functional proteins, with Syb2 being the most abundant vesicular SNARE at approximately 70 copies per vesicle (6). Given our demonstration that Munc13-1 specifically recruits isolated Syb2 into RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates through MUN–Syb2 interaction, we investigated whether this mechanism mediates the association of reconstituted Syb2-bearing vesicles with RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates.

To characterize the intrinsic membrane-binding properties of RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates, we first examined their interaction with protein-free SUVs in the absence of Munc13-1 and Syb2. Using a reconstituted system with three distinct SUV preparations—DOPS-SUVs (79% POPC, 20% DOPS, 1% DiI), PIP2-SUVs [78% POPC, 20% DOPS, 1% PI(4,5)P2, 1% DiI], and neutral POPC-SUVs (99% POPC, 1% DiI), we observed that RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates preferentially associated with negatively charged DOPS-SUVs and PIP2-SUVs over neutral POPC-SUVs (Fig. 2 A and B), indicating their intrinsic electrostatic preference for negatively charged membranes as previously reported (22, 40). However, these findings require careful interpretation when considering native SVs, which exhibit more complex surface properties—while their membranes contain anionic phospholipids, they also bear abundant cationic proteins like Syb2 that significantly alter net surface charge at physiological context. In living cells, vesicle recruitment likely combines these weak charge interactions with stronger, specific protein binding (like MUN–Syb2) for precise docking.

To specifically isolate the contribution of MUN–Syb2 interaction from general electrostatic effects, we employed a charge-neutral experimental system by reconstituting Syb2 into neutral POPC-SUVs (designated POPC-Syb2). POPC-Syb2 showed robust, C2A-MUN-dependent coating on RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates, while control POPC-SUVs exhibited negligible droplet coating (Fig. 2C). This recruitment was also abolished by the Syb2 4M mutation disrupting MUN binding (Fig. 2C), indicating Munc13-1’s specific role in Syb2 recruitment into RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates via dual C2A–RIM1 and MUN–Syb2 interactions.

RIM1 and RIM-BP2 are concentrated at fusion sites through their association with VGCCs (41, 42). To determine whether RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates mediate Syb2-vesicles tethering at fusion sites via Munc13-1, we reconstituted active zone assembly using supported lipid bilayers (SLBs, 98% POPC, 2% DGS-NTA-Ni2+, 0.1% PEG5000-PE). Purified His6-VGCC was first reconstituted onto SLBs through His6-tag–NTA-Ni2+ interactions. Addition of RIM1 and RIM-BP2 induced the formation of RIM1/RIM-BP2/VGCC complexes that underwent LLPS, generating distinct membrane-associated clusters (Fig. 2 D and E). The C2A-MUN chimera was robustly recruited to these clusters (Fig. 2F) and, in turn, specifically tethered Syb2-containing vesicles (Fig. 2G). Recruitment was abolished both by the Syb2-4M mutation, which disrupts MUN binding, and by the isolated C2A domain, which lacks the MUN domain necessary for Syb2 interaction (Fig. 2 H and I and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–C). To determine whether Syb2 clustering strictly requires LLPS rather than multivalent binding alone, we employed LLPS-deficient RIM1 variants. The RIM1Δ10 mutant (lacking the essential region aa 871 to 880) and the RIM1-N fragment (lacking the C-terminal LLPS domain) both exhibited impaired phase separation with RIM-BP2 detected by a turbidity assay (43) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). When reconstituted on SLBs, these variants failed to promote normal VGCC and RIM-BP2 clustering, resulting in markedly reduced Syb2 vesicle recruitment (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 B–E). Thus, these results indicate that RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates establish a membrane-proximal platform in which Munc13-1 enables precise spatial coupling of SVs to active zone fusion sites via specific recognition of Syb2.

SVs contain multiple SNARE isoforms (e.g., Syb4, Syb7, VTI1A) involved in organelle trafficking and different types of release (44, 45). However, the mechanism ensuring selective sorting of Syb2-enriched SVs for evoked release remained unclear. Sequence analysis revealed that Syb2’s unique C-terminal motif (R86/K87/W89/W90), which mediates Munc13-1 binding, is poorly conserved in other Syb2 homologs (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). We hence reconstituted Syb2 and these homologs at equimolar ratios into POPC-SUVs to assess recruitment efficacy. As expected, RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates preferentially recruited vesicles containing reconstituted Syb2 over homologs in the presence of C2A-MUN (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). Taken together, these findings may elucidate the mechanistic basis for evoked release mediated by the canonical SNARE complex (Syb2/Syx1/SN25): RIM1/RIM-BP2 phase separation specifically enables Munc13-1-dependent sorting of Syb2-bearing SVs and assembly of canonical SNARE machinery.

Munc13-1 within RIM1/RIM-BP2 Condensates Reorganizes Syb2 Distribution on the Membrane.

We next investigated if Munc13-1, beyond vesicle recruitment, also controls the nanoscale organization of Syb2 on the membrane within RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates (Fig. 3A). Initial reconstitution of Syb2-His6 on SLBs revealed a homogeneous distribution pattern, and this uniform pattern was not altered by either C2A-MUN or RIM1/RIM-BP2 alone (Fig. 3 B and C). However, simultaneous application of C2A-MUN and RIM1/RIM-BP2 induced robust Syb2 clustering on SLBs (Fig. 3D). This clustering was abolished by either the Syb2 4M mutation or replacement of C2A-MUN with the isolated C2A domain (Fig. 3E and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 D and E), both of which disrupt MUN–Syb2 interactions. Thus, Munc13-1, upon its recruitment to RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates, directly clusters Syb2 on the membrane.

We further validated Munc13-1-mediated Syb2 clustering on the membrane using giant unilamellar vesicles (GUVs) (Fig. 3F). Reconstituted Syb2 was uniformly distributed on GUVs (Fig. 3G). Consistent with the SLB data, Syb2 underwent clustering on GUVs upon addition of RIM1/RIM-BP2 and C2A-MUN (Fig. 3 H and J). This effect was specific, as it was prevented by the Syb2 4M mutation (Fig. 3 I and J). Therefore, these data suggest that Munc13-1 enriched in RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates performs a dual function: recruiting Syb2-bearing vesicles and clustering Syb2 at the vesicle–condensate interface. We propose that this clustering increases the local concentration of Syb2 at fusion sites, which in turn promotes efficient SNARE complex assembly.

Munc13-1-Mediated SNARE Complex Assembly within RIM1/RIM-BP2 Condensates.

We next determined whether SNAREs recruited into RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates by Munc13-1 can be assembled into ternary SNARE complexes. To specifically detect fully assembled SNARE complexes, we employed Complexin (CPX), which selectively binds to assembled ternary SNARE complexes but not to individual SNAREs (46–48). We utilized two CPX variants: a superclamp mutant (scCPX), which binds stably to assembled complexes, and a nonclamp mutant (ncCPX), which is binding-deficient and served as a negative control (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). As anticipated, GST pull-down experiments confirmed that preassembled SNARE complexes bind scCPX but not ncCPX (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). C2A-MUN, when incorporated into RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates, efficiently catalyzed the assembly of complete SNARE complexes from M18-1/Syx1, Syb2, and SN25 in solution, as demonstrated by a robust enrichment of scCPX, but not ncCPX, within the condensates (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C and D).

We also determined SNARE complex assembly on SLBs. The addition of POPC-Syb2, M18-1/Syx1, and SN25 to a mixture of RIM1, RIM-BP2, VGCC, and C2A-MUN led to the specific recruitment of Syb2, M18-1/Syx1, and SN25 into the dense clusters (Fig. 4A). Consistent with results in solution, scCPX was significantly enriched in the dense clusters, while ncCPX showed negligible recruitment, indicating the formation of functional SNARE complexes on the membrane (Fig. 4 B–D).

The above findings prompted the hypothesis that Munc13-1-enriched RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensate could promote SNARE-mediated membrane fusion. To test this, we performed lipid-mixing assays using donor liposomes containing M18-1/Syx1 FL and acceptor liposomes bearing Syb2 FL. These assays were conducted in the presence of SN25, C2A-MUN, RIM1, and RIM-BP2 (Fig. 4E). Initial experiments with only SN25 and C2A-MUN showed minimal lipid mixing between the liposome populations (Fig. 4 F and G). Strikingly, adding 1 µM RIM1/RIM-BP2 significantly enhanced SNARE-mediated lipid fusion. This enhancement was critically dependent on functional C2A-MUN, as omitting it or using the catalytically inactive C2A-MUN NFAA mutant completely abolished lipid mixing (Fig. 4 F and G). Moreover, neither the Syb2-4M mutation (which disrupts MUN binding) nor the RIM1 PASB fragment (which forms condensates but cannot bind Munc13-1) enhance lipid mixing (Fig. 4 H and I and SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). To exclude nonspecific effects from any phase-separating scaffold, we employed a synthetic LLPS system, (SIM)10–(SUMO)10 (49, 50), which phase-separated at 2 µM (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B). Unlike the functional RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates (1 µM), even 10 µM of the synthetic condensates failed to enhance SNARE-mediated lipid fusion (Fig. 4 H and I). These data demonstrate that RIM1/RIM-BP2 phase separation promotes functional SNARE complex assembly via Munc13-1, ultimately driving membrane fusion.

RIM1/RIM-BP2 Condensates Enhance DCV Exocytosis through a Munc13-Dependent Mechanism in PC12 Cells.

To establish the physiological relevance of SNARE enrichment into condensates, we asked whether RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates enhance vesicle secretion in living cells. We used the PC12 cell model, where the machinery for DCV exocytosis is evolutionarily conserved with synaptic vesicle release (51–54).

We first explored if RIM1 and RIM-BP2 form liquid-like condensates at the plasma membrane in PC12 cells using TIRF microscopy. Upon overexpression, RIM1-mCherry formed distinct plasma membrane clusters that were rapidly dissolved by 1,6-hexanediol, indicating their formation through LLPS (Fig. 5 A–C). We next detect the functional role of LLPS on Syb2 recruitment and DCV exocytosis. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that Syb2 puncta exhibited significant spatial colocalization with RIM1/RIM-BP2 clusters (Fig. 5 D and E). This colocalization was significantly impaired by either 1,6-hexanediol treatment or by replacing RIM1 with a Munc13-binding deficient RIM1 mutant (RIM1 PASB, Fig. 5 D and E). Notably, the number of Syb2 puncta remained unchanged upon overexpression of either the wild-type or mutant complexes (Fig. 5 D and F). Consistent with the structural disruption of Syb2 clusters by 1,6-hexanediol, functional analysis using TIRF imaging with Syb2-pHluorin (Fig. 5A) revealed that this treatment led to a marked reduction in DCV exocytosis evoked by ionomycin (Fig. 5 G and H). Thus, these data collectively demonstrate that RIM1/RIM-BP2 phase separation is essential for efficient DCV recruitment and exocytosis.

We then explore whether RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates facilitate spatiotemporally precise DCV exocytosis. Following ionomycin-induced depolarization, 69.3% ± 2.9% of secretion events occurred within the RIM1 condensates (“in-phase”), significantly higher than the 30.7% ± 2.9% in noncondensate regions (“ex-phase”) of the same cells (Fig. 5I). This condensate-enhanced secretion was abolished in cells expressing RIM1 PASB, where secretion was evenly distributed (48.0% ± 2.4% in-phase vs. 52.0% ± 2.4% ex-phase; Fig. 5J). Moreover, western blot analysis confirmed that RIM1/RIM-BP2 and RIM1 PASB/RIM-BP2 were expressed at comparable levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). The number of Syb2 puncta and the cell area also showed no significant change (Fig. 5 D and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 B and C). Thus, these results indicate the reduction in in-phase exocytosis specifically results from the impaired recruitment of Munc13.

To further determine whether endogenous Munc13 is required, we used CRISPR-Cas9 system to generate a PC12 cell line with a complete deletion of both Munc13-1 and ubMunc13-2 (unc13 DKO). Consistent with previous observation (55), ubMunc13-2 is the predominant isoform in these cells, while Munc13-1 expression is barely detectable (SI Appendix, Fig. S7D). Given their functional similarity, we therefore performed rescue experiments in the DKO cells by expressing ubMunc13-2 WT or its mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S7E). As expected, deletion of both Munc13-1 and ubMunc13-2 markedly reduced DCV exocytosis evoked by ionomycin (Fig. 5 K and L). However, rescuing with ubMunc13-2 ΔC2A (impaired in RIM binding) or ubMunc13-2 NFAA (defective in SNARE assembly) also failed to restore DCV exocytosis (Fig. 5 K and L). Collectively, these results demonstrate that RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates spatially concentrate DCV exocytosis and that this enhancement requires a functional interaction with Munc13, likely by promoting the local enrichment of SNARE proteins at fusion sites.

Discussion

A fundamental question in neurotransmitter release is how the correct SNARE proteins are efficiently targeted to and concentrated at presynaptic release sites to ensure rapid and precise vesicle fusion. While LLPS of scaffold proteins like RIM1 and RIM-BP2 is known to organize the electron-dense active zone, the mechanism specifically governing the spatial recruitment and enrichment of the fusion machinery (SNAREs) within these condensates has remained unclear. Here, our study reveals a previously unrecognized function of Munc13-1 as a molecular filter and concentrator that selectively enriches SNAREs within RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates for efficient SNARE complex assembly and membrane fusion (Fig. 6). In particular, through enriching Munc13-1, RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates promote targeted recruitment of Syb2-bearing liposomes and further induce pronounced clustering of Syb2 on the vesicles. Furthermore, introducing RIM1/RIM-BP2 into PC12 cells leads to the formation of synaptic active-zone-like condensates at the plasma membrane, which significantly enhance DCV exocytosis through a Munc13-dependent mechanism.

Model of Munc13 as an organizational hub for SNARE sorting, concentrating, and assembly within RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates. LLPS of the scaffold proteins RIM1 and RIM-BP2 drives the formation of the electron-dense active zone. Within this condensate platform, Munc13-1 is specifically recruited via interaction with RIM1. This enables the subsequent incorporation of Syb2 and the M18-1/Syx1 complex, which in turn serves as a prerequisite for subsequent SN25 recruitment. Notably, by enriching Munc13-1, RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates drive the targeted docking of Syb2-bearing vesicles and further induce nanoscale clustering of Syb2 on the vesicular membrane. This spatial reorganization elevates the local SNARE density at fusion sites, facilitating efficient SNARE complex assembly and exocytosis.

In neuronal exocytosis, Munc13-1 orchestrates SNARE complex assembly through coordinated interactions with the M18-1/Syx1 complex, Syb2 and SN25 (28, 56). However, the specificity and sequential order of this assembly process remained unclear due to the weak and transient nature of the interactions between Munc13-1 and the other components. The specific recruitment of Munc13-1 into RIM/RIM-BP2 condensates—mediated by high-affinity interactions between its C2A domain and RIM’s ZF domain—confers two critical functional advantages: i) selective enrichment of canonical SNAREs (i.e., Syx1, Syb2, and SN25) at fusion sites, which significantly increases the local reaction probability for efficient complex assembly; and ii) elevation of local Munc13-1 concentration, which enhances its catalytic potency in facilitating Syx1 conformational opening and promoting subsequent binding with other SNAREs. By leveraging this condensate-based platform, we demonstrate that the incorporation of Syb2 and the M18-1/Syx1 complex is a prerequisite for subsequent recruitment of SN25 (Fig. 1). These findings support a model wherein RIM/RIM-BP2 condensates facilitate the formation of a quaternary intermediate complex comprising Munc13-1, M18-1, Syx1, and Syb2 (35, 36). This intermediate serves as a structural platform primed for SN25 incorporation, ultimately enabling the assembly of the fusogenic SNARE complex at release sites. This mechanism elegantly explains how weak, transient interactions can be harnessed within biomolecular condensates to achieve the specificity, efficiency, and regulatability required for precise neurotransmitter release.

A second major unresolved question is how synaptic vesicles are selectively captured by the active zone and delivered to fusion sites, particularly given the heterogeneous vesicle populations within the presynaptic terminal. Cryoelectron tomography (cryo-ET) has revealed that SVs associate with the plasma membrane via dense peripheral protein densities structurally resembling Munc13-1 (57), and growing evidence further indicates that Munc13-1 can assemble into nanoscale clusters (18, 58). Previous work has shown that Munc13-1 interacts with Syb2 to aid vesicle recruitment (36). In line with these observations, our findings reveal that by enriching Munc13-1, RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates facilitate the docking and membrane clustering of Syb2-bearing vesicles (Fig. 2). The function of Munc13-1 in this context likely arises from the coordinated action of its distinct domains. For example, whereas the C2C domain also contribute to SVs docking via phosphatidylserine binding (21, 58, 59), our study shows that the MUN–Syb2 interaction specifically recruits Syb2-enriched vesicles and induces Syb2 clustering on the membrane (Figs. 2 and 3), suggesting these domains act cooperatively to ensure precise Syb2 positioning. Furthermore, other lipid-binding domains of Munc13-1 (e.g., C1, C2B) can regulate its presynaptic positioning and conformation during neuronal activity (60, 61). Thus, Munc13-1 integrates multiple domain-specific functions to achieve efficient and precise control of synaptic vesicle recruitment. Moreover, synaptic vesicle recruitment is likely part of a synergistic network: the scaffold protein Piccolo has been reported to extract synaptic vesicles from the synapsin reservoir into RIM/RIM-BP condensates via LLPS (22, 40), while proper RIM and Munc13 localization—orchestrated by Liprin-α through another LLPS-dependent mechanism—is critical for vesicle docking (62–64). Importantly, Rab3–RIM interactions also contribute to vesicle docking (65, 66). Together, within RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates, RIM1, Munc13-1, and other scaffold proteins function synergistically to mediate the specific docking of Rab3- and Syb2-bearing synaptic vesicles, thereby ensuring spatiotemporally precise SV recruitment and positioning for subsequent synaptic vesicle priming.

SVs are densely packed with functional proteins, among which Syb2 stands out as the most abundant vesicular proteins, with approximately 70 copies per vesicle. The functional rationale for this high Syb2 copy number—significantly exceeding that of other integral vesicular proteins—has remained unclear. Here, we show that RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates preferentially enrich Syb2 over its homologs even under equimolar conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S4), suggesting that a high vesicular Syb2 copy number may support preferential vesicle sorting and targeting to fusion sites. This finding helps elucidate the central role of the canonical SNARE complex (Syb2/Syx1/SN25) in evoked release. Moreover, our results align with several independent observations: Syb4, Syb7, and Vti1a are primarily involved in spontaneous rather than evoked release (67–69); RIM-BP2 loss specifically disrupts evoked release without affecting spontaneous release (42, 70); and 1,6-hexanediol treatment selectively impairs evoked release (27). Beyond mediating the docking of Syb2-bearing synaptic vesicles, RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates drive a Munc13-1-dependent nanoscale spatial redistribution of Syb2 on the vesicular membrane, transforming its organization from a uniform distribution to high-density clusters (Fig. 3). We propose that this redistribution is functionally critical, as it dramatically increases the local concentration of Syb2 at prospective fusion sites, thereby facilitating rapid and efficient SNARE complex assembly and substantially enhancing the probability of synchronous vesicle fusion. Future studies using superresolution microscopy or cryo-ET will be essential to further elucidate the nanoscale spatial organization of Syb2 on primed SVs under native physiological conditions.

Directly imaging the coupling between phase separation and SV release in native synapses remains technically prohibitive. We therefore turned to the PC12 model system, where we found that RIM1/RIM-BP2 forms synaptic active-zone-like condensates that significantly enhance spatiotemporally precise DCV exocytosis in a Munc13-dependent manner (Fig. 5). Consistent with our model (Fig. 6), both in vitro membrane fusion assays and in vivo DCV exocytosis experiments demonstrate that RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates enhance the capacity of Munc13-1 to drive SNARE-mediated membrane fusion (Figs. 4 and 5). A key insight from this work is that within condensates, Munc13-1 undergoes a functional transformation from a diffusible cytosolic catalyst into a structured organizational hub, capable of spatially sorting and concentrating cognate SNAREs while directing their stepwise assembly with high fidelity. This mechanism not only accelerates the formation of fusion-competent SNARE complexes but also confines them to defined release sites, thereby substantially increasing the fidelity and synchrony of neurotransmitter release. More broadly, our findings illustrate how biomolecular condensates can regulate complex biochemical pathways by harnessing physicochemical principles such as local concentration enhancement and spatial compartmentalization—offering a generalizable framework for understanding the regulation of membrane trafficking and fusion in diverse cellular systems.

Materials and Methods

Escherichia coli Strain.

E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells were cultured in lysogeny broth medium at 37 °C (for bacterial growth) or 20 °C (for protein expression) as indicated at a shaking speed of 200 rpm.

PC12 Cells Culture.

PC12 cells (TCR8) were cultured in RPMI Medium 1640 basic (GIBCO) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (GIBCO) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (GIBCO) at 37 °C in a cell incubator (Thermo) with 5% CO2.

Antibodies.

The following antibodies were used: rabbit polyclonal anti-Syb2 (1:500; Proteintech, Cat# 10135-1-AP, RRID: AB_256918); rabbit polyclonal anti-mCherry (1:3,000; Proteintech, Cat# 26765-1-AP, RRID: AB_2876881); rabbit polyclonal anti-β-actin (1:3,000; Proteintech, Cat# 20536-1-AP, RRID: AB_10700003). Secondary antibodies were Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H+L) (1:1,000; Thermo Fisher, Cat# A-11001, RRID: AB_2534069); goat anti-mouse IgG (H+L) (1:10,000; Proteintech, Cat# SA00001-1, RRID: AB_2722565); and goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) (1:10,000; Proteintech, Cat# SA00001-2, RRID: AB_2722564).

Plasmids and Protein Purification.

The following protein constructs were cloned into the pETDuet-1 vector (Novagen): rat RIM-BP2 (residues 178 to 252 and 844 to 1040); mouse VGCC cytoplasmic domain (residues 2151 to 2327); rat Munc13-1 cytoplasmic domain C2A-MUN (residues 1 to 209 and 933 to 1531) and its mutant C2A-MUN NFAA (N1128A/F1131A); full-length rat Syb2 (residues 1 to 116, S61C), its cytoplasmic domain (residues 1 to 96, S61C), and the Syb2 4M mutant (R86A/K87A/W89A/W90A); full-length rat SN25 (residues 1 to 203, S187C); the rat Syx1 cytoplasmic domain (residues 1 to 261, C145S/S187C); rat CPX cytoplasmic domain constructs scCPX (residues 26 to 83, D27L/E34M/R37A) and ncCPX (residues 26 to 53); as well as full-length rat Syb4 (residues 1 to 140), Syb7 (residues 1 to 220), and Vti1A (residues 1 to 224). All recombinant proteins above were expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) strain grown in LB media at 37 °C to OD600 of 0.6 to 0.8, followed by induction with 0.4 mM IPTG at 20 °C for 20 h. Cells were then harvested and lysed using an AH-Nano Homogenizer (ATS Engineering Inc.) in Buffer A (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 1M NaCl) supplemented with 0.5% Triton X-100 and 1 mM PMSF. The lysates were centrifuged at 16,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C using a JA-25.50 rotor (Beckman Coulter). The resulting supernatant was incubated with Ni2+-NTA agarose (QIAGEN) at 4 °C for 3 h. The beads were washed with Buffer A containing 30 mM imidazole, and the bound proteins were eluted with 300 mM imidazole in Buffer B (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl) supplemented with 5 mM DTT, and 2 mM EDTA).

Separately, the following constructs were cloned into the pGEX-6p-1 vector (GE Healthcare) with an N-terminal PreScission protease-cleavable GST tag: rat Munc13-1 cytoplasmic domain C2A (residues 1 to 209); full-length rat Munc18-1 (residues 1 to 594); full-length rat SN25 (residues 1 to 203, S187C) and the SN25SN23 chimera (SN25 residues 83 to 142 replaced with SN23 residues 83 to 142); full-length mouse Doc2B (residues 1 to 412) and its Doc2B I20A mutant. For these GST-tagged proteins, cells were lysed in Buffer A supplemented with 0.5% Triton X-100, 5 mM EDTA, and 1 mM PMSF, followed by centrifugation at 16,000 rpm for 30 min. The supernatant was incubated with 2 mL of glutathione-Sepharose beads (Amersham-Pharmacia Biotech) at 4 °C for 3 h. After washing with buffer A, the bound proteins were cleaved from the GST tag using GST-tagged PreScission protease (10 U/mg protein) at 4 °C overnight in Buffer B. The eluted proteins were further purified by size-exclusion chromatography (GE Healthcare) in buffer B.

For monitoring DCV exocytosis in PC12 cells, rat RIM1 (residues 1 to 1334) and rat RIM-BP2 (residues 1 to 1069) cDNA sequences were fused with mCherry and iRFP, respectively, in Fig. 5. Additionally, a pH-sensitive EGFP fluorescent reporter was inserted into the C-terminal of Syb2.

The following constructs were generated in the pcDNA3.1 vector (Novagen) and C-terminally tagged with Flag and BFP: human ubMunc13-2 (residues 1 to 1610), ubMunc13-2 ΔC2A (residues 211 to 1610), and ubMunc13-2 NFAA (N1035A/F1038A).

SpyCatcher/SpyTag-Mediated RIM1 FL Ligation.

To generate full-length RIM1 via the SpyCatcher/SpyTag system, the rat RIM1 cytoplasmic domain RIM1-N (residues 1 to 474) was fused to SpyCatcher at its C-terminal and RIM1 PASB (residues 475 to 1334) was fused to SpyTag at its N-terminal. RIM1-N and RIM1 PASB were eluted with 300 mM imidazole in Buffer C (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 5 mM DTT, and 2 mM EDTA) and ligated at a 1:1 molar ratio at 4 °C overnight. The ligated product was further purified by size-exclusion chromatography in Buffer C.

Protein Labeling with Fluorophore.

The BODIPY FL-maleimide (BDPY, Molecular Probes), tetramethylrhodamine-5-maleimide, single isomer (TMR, Molecular Probes), Cy3 maleimide (GE Healthcare), and Cy5 maleimide (GE Healthcare) fluorophores were each dissolved in DMSO at 10 mg/mL. Purified proteins (5 to 10 mg/mL) in buffer B were labeled by adding fluorophore at a 5:1 molar ratio, followed by gentle rotation overnight at 4 °C. After addition of 10 mM DTT to stop the labeling process, labeled proteins were further purified using PD-10 desalting columns (GE Healthcare) with buffer B to remove excess dyes. Typically, the final labeling efficiency of each protein was adjusted to 1% by mixing labeled protein with unlabeled at a 1:99 ratio.

Imaging.

All protein aliquots were centrifuged at 16,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to remove potential precipitations prior to imaging. The working concentrations were typically 5 μM for RIM1/RIM-BP2 proteins and 10 μM for other proteins. In particular, the M18-1/Syx1 complex was prepared by incubating M18-1 and labeled Syx1 (residues 1 to 261) at a protein-to-protein ratio of 1.2:1 overnight at 4 °C. For SNARE recruitment into RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates via C2A-MUN in Fig. 1, 10 μM C2A-MUN, 10 μM M18-1/Syx1, 10 μM Syb2, and 10 μM SN25 were incorporated into a mixture of 5 μM RIM1 and 5 μM RIM-BP2. For CPX binding assay in SI Appendix, Fig. S4, 10 μM scCPX and ncCPX were supplemented in the reaction mixture. Unless otherwise specified, imaging was performed in buffer B (50 mM Tris pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl) supplemented with 5 mM DTT, and 2 mM EDTA.

For microscope-based assays, samples were injected into a homemade chamber consisting of a coverslip and glass slide separated by double-sided tape. For SLB and GUV imaging, samples were injected into chambered cover glass (Lab-tek). Images were acquired on a Nikon C2 confocal microscope with a 60× oil-immersion objective at room temperature, using identical settings for all samples within an experiment.

GST Pull-Down Experiment.

To monitor CPX binding to preassembled SNARE complexes, a GST pull-down assay was performed. To this end, ternary SNARE complexes were assembled by incubating 3 µM GST (or GST-Syb2) with 5 µM each of Syx1 and SN25 in 300 µL of Buffer B (with 0.01% Triton X-100 and 5 mM EDTA) for 3 h at room temperature, followed by immobilization on glutathione Sepharose 4B beads. Following extensive washing, the bead-bound complexes were incubated with 3 µM of either scCPX or ncCPX at 4 °C for 3 h. Finally, after a final wash, bound proteins were eluted competitively with 20 mM reduced glutathione in Buffer B, denatured, and analyzed by SDS-PAGE and CBB staining.

Immunofluorescence and Immunoblot.

To visualize the localization of Syb2 relative to RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates, PC12 cells were transfected with the indicated plasmid combinations (1 µg per plasmid) using Lipofectamine™ 3000 Transfection Reagent (L3000015, Invitrogen™). After 24 to 36 h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min. For experiments involving condensate disruption, cells were treated with 3% 1,6-hexanediol for 10 min prior to fixation. All samples were blocked with 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature to prevent nonspecific binding. Subsequently, cells were incubated with a primary antibody against Syb2 for 3 h at room temperature, washed three times with PBS, and then stained with an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Molecular Probes).

When PC12 cells reached 70 to 80% confluency in 6-cm dishes, they were transfected with the indicated plasmid combinations (3 µg of each plasmid). After 36 h, cells were harvested and lysed on ice for 30 min in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 5 mM DTT) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (TargetMol). Lysates were then centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C to remove insoluble debris. The resulting supernatants were resolved by SDS-PAGE and analyzed by immunoblotting with the specified antibodies.

SUVs Preparation.

Lipids (Avanti Polar Lipids) dissolved in chloroform and stored at −20 °C. For C2A-MUN promotes SUV coating on RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates, we first prepared three types of SUVs: DOPS-SUVs composed of 79% POPC, 20% DOPS, and 1% DiI; PIP2-SUVs containing 78% POPC, 20% DOPS and1% PI(4,5)P2, 1% DiI; and neutral POPC-SUVs with 99% POPC and 1% DiI. The lipid mixture was dried with nitrogen gas and applied with vacuum for at least 3 h. Subsequently, the mixture was slowly resuspended with buffer B supplied with 1% 3-[(3-cholamidopropyl) dimethylammonio] propanesulfonate (CHAPS) (w/v, Amersco), and then vortexed for 15 min. Finally, addition of 1 g/L Bio-Beads SM2 (Bio-Rad) to the dissolved lipid mixture to remove the detergent extensively at 4 °C overnight. For POPC-Syb2 preparation, Syb2 (residues 1 to 116) were added to the neutral POPC-SUVs mixture with a protein-to-lipid ratio of 1:250. The protein-liposome mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 30 min and then added with 1 g/L Bio-Beads SM2 (Bio-Rad) to remove the detergent extensively at 4 °C overnight.

GUV Preparation.

GUVs were prepared using a polyvinyl alcohol (PVA, MW 146,000 to 186,000, Sigma #363065)-assisted method as previously described (71). Briefly, 10% (m/v) PVA (20 µL) in ddH2O was spread on a glass slide to form a thin layer, and then the PVA-coated slide was dried at 40 to 50 °C for 30 min. A 20 µL lipid mixture (93% POPC, 5% DGS-NTA, 2% Cy5-PE), dissolved in chloroform at 1 mg/mL, was gently spread on the dried PVA layer using a glass pipette. Chloroform was evaporated immediately after spreading, the dried glass slide was placed in vacuum for at least another 1 h to remove residual chloroform. An O-ring was attached, and 200 µL of GUV preparation buffer (200 mM sucrose, 20 mM Tris pH 8.2, 50 mM NaCl) was added inside the ring, followed by 1 h incubation in the dark at room temperature. The GUV solution was collected and stored on ice for use within 2 d. For experiments, the GUV stock was diluted 20-fold in buffer B.

For validating Syb2 distribution on the membrane, 0.5 μM 2% labeled Syb2 (residues 1 to 96, S61C) was incubated with the GUV solution for 30 min at room temperature. 0.5 μM RIM1, RIM-BP2, and C2A-MUN were added to the Syb2-tethered GUV solution to assay the RIM1/RIM-BP2 droplet formation on GUVs.

SLB Preparation.

SLB were prepared as previously described with modifications (72). Briefly, we first prepared SUVs, comprising 98% POPC, 2% DGS-NTA, and 0.1% PEG5000 PE (all lipids were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids and dissolved in chloroform). SUVs was initially dried under a stream of nitrogen gas and applied with vacuum for at least 3 h. Subsequently, the mixture was slowly resuspended with buffer B supplied with 1% CHAPS and was further purified using PD-10 desalting columns (GE Healthcare) with buffer B to remove excess CHAPS. The SUV solution was centrifuged at 33,500 g for 45 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was kept for further use.

We next clean the chambered cover glass (Lab-tek) with Hellmanex III (Helma Analytics) overnight, followed by thorough rinsing with ddH2O. The cover glass was subsequently washed with 5 M NaOH for 1 h at 50 °C. This process was repeated three times and thoroughly rinsed with ddH2O. The cleaned cover glass was then equilibrated with buffer D (50 mM Tris pH 8.2, 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP), followed by addition of 150 μL SUVs and incubation at 42 °C for 1 h to generate SLB. SLB were washed with the buffer D for three times (sixfold dilution per time) and kept hydrated throughout to prevent oxidation. Then SLB were blocked with 1 mg/mL BSA in buffer D at room temperature for 30 min.

To test C2A-MUN mediated SV coating on RIM1/RIM-BP2 condensates or Syb2 distribution on the membrane, 0.5 μM 5% TMR labeled VGCC or 2% TMR labeled Syb2 (residues 1 to 96, S61C) was first incubated with SLB at room temperature for 1 h. Then the chamber was washed with 1 mg/mL BSA solution in buffer D to remove unbound proteins. 0.25 μM RIM1 and RIM-BP2, 0.5 μM C2A-MUN or C2A, 1 μM M18-1/Syx1, and SN25 were added to the SLB.

Lipid Mixing Experiment.

Lipid mixing experiment was performed as previously described (73). Donor liposomes, which were reconstituted with Syb2 (residues 1 to 116), contained 77% POPC, 10% POPE, 10% Cholesterol, 1.5% NBD-PE, 1.5% Rhodamine-PE. Acceptor liposomes, which were reconstituted with M18-1/Syx1 (residues 1 to 288), comprised 58% POPC, 10% POPE, 20% DOPS, 2% PIP2, and 10% Cholesterol. The lipid mixture (Avanti Polar Lipids) was dried with nitrogen gas and applied with vacuum for at least 3 h. The mixture was slowly resuspended with buffer B supplied with 1% CHAPS, and then vortexed for 15 min. M18-1/Syx1 (residues 1 to 288) and Syb2 (residues 1 to 116) were added to the mixture with a protein-to-lipid ratio of 1:250 and 1:250, respectively. The protein-liposome mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 30 min and then dialyzed with 1 g/L Bio-Beads SM2 (Bio-Rad) three times in buffer B to remove the detergent extensively.

For lipid mixing experiment, donor liposomes (0.25 mM total lipids) were mixed with acceptor liposomes (0.25 mM total lipids) in the presence of 5 µM SN25, 1 µM RIM1 and RIM-BP2, and 1.0 µM C2A-MUN in a total volume of 60 µL. Lipid mixing was monitored at 30 °C by measuring NBD fluorescence (excitation 460 nm, emission 538 nm) using a QM-40 spectrophotometer. Munc13-catalyzed lipid mixing was expressed as F1/F0, where F1 is fluorescence intensity over time and F0 is the initial intensity. Experiments were repeated at least three times, and data were analyzed using Prism 8.0.0.

During exocytosis, the fusion event was accompanied by an increase in Syb2-pHluorin fluorescence, and the fluorescence then gradually weakened, which was the result of fusion pore closure and vesicle reacidification. Therefore, the exocytosis events were observed by monitoring the changes of fluorescence intensity during membrane fusion and cargo release. Quantification and Statistical Analysis.

For SNARE recruitment in Fig. 1, a total of 3 images with 10 randomly selected droplets from each image (i.e., a total of 30 droplets) in each group were pooled together for quantification. A smaller circular area fitting the size of a droplet was used to measure the mean intensity of the droplet. For quantification of Syb2 clustering on SLBs, defined regions of interest from each image within a group were used to measure the mean intensity and analyzed. For the GUV assay (Fig. 3), protein distribution asymmetry across the GUV membrane was quantified as the ratio F1/F2, where F1 and F2 represent the peak fluorescence intensities on opposite sides of a line scan across the GUV. For fusion assay in Fig. 4, C2A-MUN-catalyzed lipid mixing was calculated by F1/F0 and each experiment was repeated at least three times. All the data above are presented as mean ± SEM. Significance was determined by one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test or Student’s t test (ns, not significant; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001).

For TIRF images, we first manually defined the phase-separated and exphase-separated regions. The probability of DCV exocytosis was then statistically analyzed in these respective regions. Data are presented as mean ± SEM; n = 17 biological replicates for the RIM1/RIM-BP2 group, n = 19 for the RIM1 PASB/RIM-BP2 group. Statistical significance and P values were determined by Student’s t test (**P < 0.01; ****P < 0.0001).

Statistical analysis was performed in Prism 8.0.0 software and is included in the relevant figure legends.
