G-protein activation of the dark-state conformation of the visual G protein-coupled receptor rhodopsin by releasing critical structural constraints
Authors: Eva Ramon, Kristina Kirchberg, Mireia Jiménez-Rosés, Jens Balke, Tai-Yang Kim, Arnau Cordomí, Ulrike Alexiev, Pere Garriga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
G protein–coupled receptors (GPCRs) operate through the binding and activation of heterotrimeric G proteins. Ligand interaction drives the receptor’s transition from an inactive to an active state, ultimately triggering G-protein activation and downstream signal transduction. The visual GPCR rhodopsin contains the chromophore 11-cis-retinal, which is covalently bound and functions as an inverse agonist, maintaining very low basal activity in the absence of light. Disruption of this basal receptor activity can lead to physiological consequences associated with retinal diseases such as congenital stationary night blindness and retinitis pigmentosa. Here, we describe a functional dark-state rhodopsin generated through engineered double and triple mutations at three well-defined structural microswitches that regulate the conformational stability of the dark ground-state: i) T942.61I, located near the protonated Schiff base linkage environment and linked to congenital stationary night blindness; ii) M2576.40Y positioned close to the tyrosine cluster (Y2235.58 and Y3067.53 of the NPxxY motif); and iii) E1343.49 within the conserved (D/E)RY motif, which participates in the so-called ionic lock involving R1353.50 and E2476.30. Characterization of these mutant rhodopsins provides additional insights into the structural basis of the inactive-to-active conformational transition in important functional domains and emphasizes rhodopsin conformational flexibility.

Introduction

Vision in vertebrates begins when light is absorbed by visual pigments present in retinal photoreceptor cells. In rod cells, rhodopsin enables sensitivity to low light levels, while the three types of cone opsins -red, green, and blue- operate in bright light. These visual pigments are G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) that consist of an apoprotein, opsin covalently bound to the chromophore 11-cis-retinal (11CR), a vitamin-A derivative that functions as an inverse agonist locking the receptor in its inactive state1. When exposed to light, chromophore 11CR is converted to all-trans-retinal (ATR), an isomerization reaction that shifts the β-ionone ring position out from its canonical chromophore binding site. This change corresponds to the initial step in vision and causes conformational rearrangements on the receptor, leading to its active state that subsequently launches downstream signaling pathways2. Rhodopsin is the photoreceptor protein found throughout the retinal rod cells and enables low-light vision.

Visual photoreception requires a very tight regulation of the receptor response to adjust to different light intensities, a process known as dark adaptation3. Visual pigments have developed an extremely efficient conformational structural arrangement that ensures a minimal basal activity of the receptor in the absence of a light stimulus. This tight conformation is achieved by covalent binding of a chromophore, the 11-cis-retinal, acting as an inverse agonist. Ligand binding locks the inactive conformation and ensures very low spontaneous thermal activation of the receptor, in the dark state what is referred to as dark noise4. During the photoactivation process, the inactive receptor undergoes a series of concerted conformational changes that lead to the active state, named Metarhodopsin II (Meta II).

Many GPCRs show constitutive activity that is defined as the ligand-independent G-protein activation. The discovery of GPCRs constitutive activity has had a profound impact both in the pharmacological study of these receptors and in an increased understanding of the molecular mechanisms of certain diseases with which it is associated. Although under usual conditions, rhodopsin has low constitutive activity, certain retinal disorders, including congenital stationary night blindness (CSNB) and retinitis pigmentosa (RP), can be caused by highly abnormal rhodopsin constitutive activity5. From the first report of constitutive activation in rhodopsin6, many mutations resulting in constitutively active rhodopsin mutants have been described, as well as their relevance to rhodopsin structural studies and their relationship to different retinopathies7,8. For instance, mutations at K2967.43 have been associated with RP, and G902.57D and A2927.39E mutations (superscripts refer to the general numbering system for GPCRs9) cause CSNB10. It is thought that in such mutants both the inactive and the active Meta II photointermediate states have a low interconversion energy barrier, implying that these mutations either destabilize the inactive state or stabilize the active state, or both11. Previous reports have shown that it is possible to have significant levels of dark activity in rhodopsin mutants, mainly in those mutants in which the Schiff base (SB) counterion has been mutated (E1133.28Q). In particular, the double mutant E1133.28Q/M2576.40Y was able to partially activate transducin in the dark with 11-cis-retinal bound12.

In this report, we strategically designed double, and triple, mutants that combine the individual substitutions T942.61I, E1343.49Q, and M2576.40Y known to lead to either dark and/or constitutive activity13–17. The CSNB mutant T942.61I mutant is constitutively active18,19 but does not show increased dark-state activity compared to the WT16, despite it results in extremely long-lived Meta II intermediate14,18 E1343.49Q exhibits 1.5-fold dark-state activity relative to WT and is constitutively active8,15. Substitutions at M2576.40 yield receptors with increased dark-state activity in the presence of retinal8 and also constitutive activity17.

Here, we analyze the effect of combined structural modifications at key microdomains on the receptor function. To this end, the mutant receptors have been constructed, immunopurified and spectroscopically characterized, and their functional properties have been determined. Our complementary UV-vis and fluorescence anisotropy spectroscopic analysis, together with molecular modeling, shows that the combination of the three studied mutations removes critical structural constraints at key microdomains, allowing the formation of an active-like conformation in the dark.

Results

Spectroscopic characterization of T942.61I/E1343.49Q, T942.61I/M2576.40Y, E1343.49Q/M2576.40Y and T942.61I/E1343.49Q/M2576.40Y mutants

We expressed the recombinant opsins corresponding to double and triple mutants combining individual point mutations T942.61I, E1343.49Q and M2576.40Y in COS-1 cells and immunopurified with the Rho-1D4 antibody in 2 mM Na2HPO4, pH 6.0, 0.05% DDM (see “Materials and Methods” section). T942.61I is located close to the retinal binding pocket, E1343.49Q lies close to the transducin binding site and M2576.40Y occupies an intermediate position (Fig. 1). All mutants were able to bind the native 11-cis-retinal chromophore to form a pigment. The T942.61I/E1343.49Q/M2576.40Y triple mutant, regenerated with 11-cis-retinal, and showed a main band at 378 nm with a minor contribution at 478 nm in the dark, compared to the characteristic visible absorbance band at 500 nm for the WT (Fig. 2A). The UV band at about 380 nm can be due to the chromophoric retinal moiety (either in the 11-cis or all-trans configurations) bound through an unprotonated Schiff base to opsin, or to free all-trans-retinal resulting from the hydrolysis of the Schiff base linkage with K2967.43. The band at 478 nm corresponds to the 11-cis-retinal chromophore bound to opsin via a protonated Schiff base linkage. Because the active (non-protonated) Meta II conformation absorbs at 378 nm, it appears that most of the chromophore in the triple mutant is bound via a non-protonated Schiff-based linkage. By contrast, double mutants T942.61I/E1343.49Q and T942.61I/M2576.40Y showed a blue-shift visible band (λmax = 478 nm, Fig. 2A). The spectroscopic properties of the double and triple mutants shed light on the effect of the single mutant combinations on the photobleaching and acidification behavior (Fig. 2B and Fig. S1). In this regard, the dark UV–vis spectrum of the T942.61I/E1343.49Q shows a visible absorbance maximum at 478 nm (Fig. S1A), whereas the T942.61I/M2576.40Y double mutant shows a main component at 478 nm and a smaller one at 378 nm, respectively, indicating the presence of protonated and unprotonated Schiff base-linked species (Fig. S1B). The E1343.49Q/M2576.40Y double mutant visible band was located at 500 nm like that for WT rhodopsin (Fig. S1C) indicating that the T942.61I substitution is the responsible for the blue-shift, from 500 nm to 478 nm, observed in the other double mutants and in the triple mutant. The behavior of the double mutants was analogous to the WT with the complete shift of the visible band to a 380-nm absorbing band. The T942.61I/E1343.49Q/M2576.40Y triple mutant is sensitive to illumination because a small amount of the Schiff base linked material is converted into the 380 nm absorbing species (Fig. 2B). All the studied mutants show the same spectroscopic pattern as the WT, with the formation of a 440-nm absorbing species upon acidification to pH 2 (the effect is known as “acid trapping” and represents trapping of all-trans- retinal chromophore bound to opsin by means of a protonated Schiff base linkage in the denatured protein). This indicates that the UV-absorbing species at 380 nm corresponded to the Meta II conformation that did not decay to opsin and free all-trans-retinal during the time course of the experiment. The photobleaching and acidification behavior which is found to be like that of WT rhodopsin, suggests that the combined mutations studied do not significantly perturb the cascade of photointermediates that should be analogous to those found for the native protein. We conducted experiments to determine the thermal stability of the dark-state conformation of the receptors by measuring the time decay of the visible band at 55 °C. We found that the mutants were very unstable in the dark compared to the WT, and complete decay of the visible band for the double and triple mutants occurred in the time frame of the spectral measurement (t1/2 for the process <1 min vs. 18min in the case of the WT) Table 1). This kinetic behavior is compatible with the functional data that suggests that the equilibrium between the active and inactive conformations in these mutants (particularly in the triple mutant) is markedly shifted towards the active conformation. Mutations at positions 1343.49 and 2576.40 (like in the E1343.49Q/M2576.40Y double mutant) should not affect the retinal binding capacity of the proteins but may cause differences in retinal isomers selectivity. The structure near the Schiff base is compact in the inactive conformation bound to 11-cis-retinal, based on the hydroxylamine reactivity for this double mutant (Fig. S1D). Hydroxylamine reactivity in the dark provides a measure of the chemical stability of the inactive conformation of rhodopsin and lack of reactivity indicates a compact tertiary structure around the Schiff base linkage in the retinal binding pocket at the extracellular domain of the receptor. In contrast, we have previously shown that the T942.61I single mutant alone confers high reactivity in its dark conformation, indicating a perturbation near the Schiff base linkage14. The functionality of the double and triple mutants was examined by means of a G-protein activation assay as described under “Materials and Methods”. The experiments were done in the dark and upon illumination of the samples on regenerated and immunopurified receptors (Table 2). The triple mutant showed full activation in the dark, and the same or even larger functionality than that of WT rhodopsin after illumination. Molecular basis of the T942.61I/E1343.49Q/M2576.40Y triple mutant

To provide the structural framework necessary to understand dark-state activity of T942.61I/E1343.49Q/M2576.40Y (3 M), we generated molecular models (see Materials and Methods) of 3 M and WT rhodopsin both in the dark (inactive) and in the active states (Fig. 4). The proposed mechanism for constitutive activity of T942.61I is through breakage of the SB- E1133.28 interaction21 (Fig. 4B). In the inactive state, T942.61 forms a hydrogen bond with the backbone carbonyl of C185 at the ECL2 and is close to E1133.28. E1133.28 interacts with the backbone nitrogen of S186. In the active conformation, T942.61 forms a hydrogen bond with E1133.28. M2576.40, near three key residues in GPCR activation: Y2235.58, Y3067.53 (of the NPxxY motif) and R1353.50 (34) (Fig. 4C). M2576.40 forms strong aromatic–sulfur interactions with Y3067.53 in the inactive state and with both Y2235.58 and Y3067.53 in the active state22,23. The cytoplasmic shift of TM6 opens a cavity that accommodates the C-terminus of Gα. The Tyr side-chain of the mutant would not be able to squeeze between TMs 6 and 7 in the WT inactive conformation. In fact, the crystal structure of M2576.40Y confirmed that the mutation triggered the opening of TM624. E1343.49 is part of the D(E)RY conserved motif at the intracellular side of TM3 and it is involved in the stabilization of the inactive conformation of most class A GPCRs (Fig. 4D). Constitutive activity of the E1343.49Q mutant comes from alterations in the hydrogen bond networks within the ionic lock22. We focused our analysis on how Glu/Gln1343.49 distinctly modulates the conformation of R1353.50. In the active structures, R1353.50 adopts an extended conformation, which is the most favorable for this residue. We ran molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the WT and 3 M, starting from the inactive state, with the objective of observing constitutive activation, and from the active state (in the absence of G protein) to observe spontaneous deactivation. To examine the contribution of T942.61I, we monitored the distance between residues T/I942.61 (Cα) and K2967.43/SB (Nζ), and of the salt bridge between K2967.43/SB (Nζ) and E1133.28 (Cδ) (Fig. 5A). The distribution of both distances, in the simulations of the inactive 3 M, was as in the active systems (both WT and 3 M). This indicates that 3 M spontaneously triggers the structural changes that start activation. To examine the contribution of M2576.40Y, we monitored the relative opening of TM6 relative to TM3 by measuring the distance between L1283.43 (Cα) and Y/M2576.40 (Cζ), and the χ2 dihedral angle of Y3067.53 of the NPxxY motif (Fig. 5B). We did not observe either opening of TM6 in the inactive systems or closing of TM6 in the active systems during the time course of the trajectories (500 ns each). However, the χ2 dihedral angle of Y3067.53 rotated by 90° in the simulations of the inactive 3 M, mimicking the values of the active WT and active 3 M simulations. In the simulations of the active WT, both the distance between E1343.49 and R1353.50 and the χ2 dihedral angle of R1353.50, rapidly evolved towards the values observed in the simulations of the inactive system. This did not occur in the simulation of the active 3 M, which maintained the active features despite the lack of G protein (Fig. 5C). Active structure at the receptor surface and binding of the Gtα C-terminus

To experimentally evaluate the active receptor conformation of the triple mutant in comparison to the single mutant E1343.49Q and WT rhodopsin, on the cytoplasmic side of the receptor to which the G protein binds, we performed fluorescence-based binding studies using a fluorescently labeled Gtα−C-terminal peptide (Gtα-F)25 and real-time dynamics measurements of helix 826–29 using time-resolved fluorescence anisotropy30,31.

Table 2 and Fig. 6 summarize changes in steady-state fluorescence intensity of Gtα-F in the presence of WT and mutant rhodopsins under dark and light-activated conditions. The fluorescence increase observed after light activation of WT rhodopsin is consistent with the formation of an active-like Meta II state and was set to 100% for comparative purposes. The respective dark-state value was ~6%, as expected. In the dark state, the E1343.49Q single mutant, and the triple mutant, exhibited fluorescence changes that are consistent with adoption of an active-like cytoplasmic surface. We note that these fluorescence measurements provide indirect evidence of conformational changes rather than quantitative binding, and the observed trends are interpreted accordingly. As one can clearly see, the anisotropy curves of the single and triple mutant (Fig. 7B, C, respectively) clearly differ from the WT anisotropy decay (Fig. 7A), already indicating different dynamics of helix 8 in these mutants. The real-time dynamics of helix 8 (ϕ2) become gradually faster when more mutations are introduced (Table 3 and Fig. 7D) and appear to display a fast dynamic behavior in the triple mutant as observed in opsin with about 1.5 ns compared to 3 ns in the WT26. Notably, upon light activation both in the single and in the triple mutant the nanosecond diffusional dynamics of helix 8 becomes faster, in contrast to WT (Table 3), indicating a different interaction network from the retinal Schiff base to the cytoplasmic surface that affect helix 8 dynamics. In addition to the dynamics of helix 8 also its conformational space (mobility, β2’) was shown to change upon formation of the active Meta II state. Also, inactive opsin differs from the active Meta II state with respect to helix 8 mobility: while upon light activation of WT rhodopsin the mobility of helix 8 decreases, it increases in the opsin state26,27. When comparing the single mutant E134Q with the triple mutant, the mobility of helix 8 upon light activation decreases by about 30% similar to WT, while this value is reduced to 12% in the triple mutant in accordance with the active state already formed in the dark for the triple mutant (Fig. 7E). Note, however, that the value of helix 8 mobility (in the dark state) is already reduced compared to WT (Table 3). This behavior is also reflected in the steric restriction of helix 8 mobility (Fig. 7F), that is increased upon light activation, but to a much lesser extent for the triple mutant.

Discussion

Constitutive activity of GPCRs refers to the potential activation of the G protein by the ligand-free conformation of the receptor. Constitutively active mutants can be good tools for the study of the structure of the active states and for elucidating the activation mechanism of GPCRs11. Several constitutive active mutants have been reported for rhodopsin that show activity in the opsin state without 11CR bound13–19. In fact, 11CR is an inverse agonist that locks the dark conformation of the receptor in its inactive state and ensures a very low level of dark activity (i.e., spontaneous thermal activation of rhodopsin without illumination). In spite of this, some reports have shown that it is possible to have significant levels of dark activity in mutants in which the Schiff base counterion has been mutated (e.g., E1133.28Q mutant)12. A previous study showed that the double mutant E1133.38Q/M2576.40Y was able to activate transducin in the dark with 11CR bound12,17. In this regard, mutations that cause activity of rhodopsin in the dark state (without illumination) provide very useful information for analyzing the nature of the conformational stability of the dark inactive state, and the structural, and derived functional, features of naturally occurring mutations associated with retinal diseases.

We have used highly pure 11CR for rhodopsin regeneration of the purified rhodopsins and great care was taken to avoid isomerization by handling all the samples on ice. However, it is a concern that there might be ATR present in the samples coming from an unknown isomerization mechanism. Although the presence of ATR in the samples cannot be completely ruled out, the observed fluorescence trends are interpreted as consistent with active-like conformations induced by the mutations rather than resulting from unknown isomerization mechanisms.

We have previously shown that the T942.61I mutant folds properly and is able to bind 11CR to form chromophore with a blue-shifted visible band at 478 nm when compared to WT rhodopsin (500 nm). This mutant showed low thermal stability and a long-lived active Meta II conformation12. Its high chemical reactivity towards hydroxylamine in the dark, when compared to WT rhodopsin, suggested a more open structure of the dark conformation of the protein around the Schiff base linkage14. E1343.49Q exhibits 1.5-fold dark-state activity relative to WT and is constitutively active8,15. Any substitution at M2576.40 (except Leu) yields receptors with increased dark-state activity in the presence of retinal and also constitutive activity8,17. In particular, M2576.40Y exhibits 2-fold dark-state activity relative to WT rhodopsin. Furthermore, the double mutants M2576.40Y/E1133.28Q and M2576.40Y/E1343.49Q showed higher level of constitutive activity than the corresponding single mutants indicating a synergistic effect caused by the mutations at two different sites8.

We propose that the mutations, mainly T942.61I, introduced in the triple mutant result in significant lowering of the pKa of the protonated SB linkage. This would allow deprotonation of the SB nitrogen yielding a conformation which may be closer to the active conformation than that induced by direct disruption of the SB by mutation of the E1133.28 counterion (like in the E1133.28Q mutant). The photobleaching and acidification results of the mutants studied suggest that the signaling mechanism should be the same to that of the native receptor.

Some of the transducin activation differences, in the studied mutants, are difficult to explain but may be due to a specific role of T942.61 in modulating the conformation changes that occur near the protonated Schiff base nitrogen. T942.61I is a mutation associated with the retinal dysfunction CSNB. This mutation causes a mild visual phenotype but the physiological effects may be somehow connected with the increased functionality that may exert subtle disturbances in the visual phototransduction connected to dark adaptation. It is possible that mild night blindness mutations are associated with the increased activity of the mutant rhodopsin (with retinal bound) rather than to increased activity of the mutant opsins (in the absence of retinal). Although constitutive activity of opsin mutants has been proposed as the main molecular mechanism for CSNB32, there is also evidence that the night blindness mutant G902.57D bound to its chromophore 11-cis-retinal can stimulate the visual cascade spontaneously at a rate large enough to produce visual dysfunction in transgenic mice33.

Also at the molecular level, it has been reported that the covalent bond between retinal and Lys-2967.43 is necessary to transfer structural changes from the photoisomerized agonist to the receptor and that the covalent bond firmly retains the low-affinity agonist within the receptor, promoting more efficient G protein activation34.

In our case, the engineered triple mutant shows full capacity for activating rhodopsin both in darkness and upon illumination. The fluorescence changes observed with the LY-labeled Gtα C-terminal peptide are interpreted as indirect evidence that the cytoplasmic surface adopts an active-like conformation in the dark, complementing the anisotropy and MD data. Retinal remains bound to opsin, potentially mimicking the active conformation better than direct elimination of the E1133.28 salt bridge.

Our results confirm that the combination of the three studied mutations at T942.61, E1343.49, and M2576.40 unleashes structural constraints allowing the formation of an active-like conformation in the dark. These three residues belong to key microswitches that maintain critical intramolecular interactions and keep the receptor in its inactive state.

Furthermore, the fluorescence anisotropy experiments indicate substantial structural changes at the cytoplasmic surface, particularly in the diffusional dynamics and conformational space of helix 8, which connects via the NPxxY motif to the retinal binding pocket. In combination with the Gtα C-terminal fluorescence data, we suggest that E1343.49Q suffices to form an active cytoplasmic surface for potential G protein interaction, but additional conformational changes via M2576.40 and T942.6I are required for full activation. Previous studies combining MD simulations with time-resolved anisotropy have provided a detailed atomistic picture of receptor activation35,36.

Our presented MD simulations reveal many analogies between 3 M dark-state rhodopsin and the active WT rhodopsin. Clearly mutations at positions E1343.49 and M2576.40 cause constitutive activity13,15 without breaking the salt bridge between K2967.43 and E1133.28 in contrast to T942.61I or G902.57D mutation18,19. It should be noted that the binding of the G protein transducin in the functional analysis can definitely shift the triple mutant conformation towards a fully active conformation.

Conclusions

Mutations that cause activity of rhodopsin in the dark state (without illumination) can be utilized for dark state stability analysis and the study of the structural features of naturally occurring mutations associated with retinal diseases, such as retinitis pigmentosa. Herein, we report a rhodopsin triple mutant (3 M) combining T942.61, E1343.49 and M2576.40, three residues belonging to key microswitches responsible for maintaining the receptor in its inactive state and able to unleash critical structural constraints.

Our spectroscopic studies demonstrated features consistent with the WT Meta II state in the 3 M dark-state conformation. The engineered 3 M displayed full capacity for G-protein activation both in darkness and upon illumination. Fluorescence anisotropy experiments indicated that substantial structural changes, in particular in the conformational space of helix 8 that connects, via the NPxxY motif, the cytoplasmic surface to the retinal ligand binding pocket. The fluorescence changes observed with the LY-labeled Gtα C-terminal peptide are interpreted as supportive, indirect evidence that the cytoplasmic surface adopts an active-like conformation. Molecular dynamics simulations confirmed the analogies between 3 M dark-state rhodopsin and the active WT rhodopsin. Overall, this work highlights the importance of the key microswitches responsible for maintaining the dark state configuration. Our results unravel the structural details and subtleties of the inactive/active conformational transition, controlled by only few crucial residues (out of more than 200), and revealing allosteric communications pathways within GPCRs.

Materials and methods

Materials

11-cis-retinal was supplied by the National Eye Institute, National Institutes of Health. Purified rho-1D4 monoclonal antibody was obtained from the National Culture Center, Minneapolis and was coupled to CNBr-activated Sepharose 4B Fast Flow (Amersham Pharmacia Biotech). n-Dodecyl-β-D-maltoside (DDM) was provided by Anatrace (Maumee, OH). CompleteTM protease inhibitor mixture was from Roche Molecular Biochemicals and was used at a concentration of 1 tablet/75 ml of buffer37. The fluorescence probes iodoacetamide fluorescein (5-IAF) and Lucifer yellow (LY) were purchased from Molecular Probes. Sephadex G25 for purifying the labeled protein was purchased from GE Healthcare.

Cell culture materials

COS-1 cells (ATCC No. CRL-1650) were provided by American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA). DEMEM medium (catalog number 12491015) and antibiotics (100U/ml Penicillin/Streptomycin (catalog number 15140122) were from Thermofisher (Spain). DEAE-Dextran transfection reagent was provided by Sigma (Spain).

Buffers

The solutions used are defined as follows: Buffer A (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 1.5 mM KH2PO4, and 8 mM Na2HPO4, pH 7.2); buffer C (0.05% DDM in Buffer A, pH 7.2); buffer D (0.05% DDM in 2 mM Na2Pi, pH 6); buffer E (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl)38.

Construction, expression, and purification of the mutant receptors

Opsin mutants were constructed using the QuikChange site-directed mutagenesis kit (Stratagene, La Jolla, CA) on the synthetic bovine opsin gene cloned in PMT4 vector38,39. 5′-gatctcttcatggtcttggtggcttcatcaccaccctctacacctctctc-3′, and 3’- gagagaggtgtagagggtggtgatgaagccaccaagaccatgaagagatc-5’, are the forward and reverse oligomer sequence, respectively, for T94I, 5’-ggtagtactggcgatccagcggtacgtggtggtgtgc-3’ and 3’- gcacaccaccacgtaccgctggatcgccagtactacc-5’ for 134 and 5’-cgtatggttatcatctacgtcatcgctttcctaatctgc-3’ and 3’- gcagattaggaaagcgatgacgtagatgataaccatacg-5’for 274 positions. The expression and purification of the visual receptors was performed as previously described38. Briefly, plasmids encoding the WT or mutant rhodopsin were transfected in five COS-1 cell plates, by using DEAE-dextran with 15 μg plasmid DNA per 150 cm plate; and cells were harvested between 48 and 60 h after transfection38. Then, the pigments were reconstituted with 10 μM 11-cis-retinal and solubilized with 1% (wt/vol) DDM in Buffer A. The pigments were immunopurified using rho 1D4- Sepharose 4B in Buffer C or Buffer D. Receptor binding to the Rho-1D4 sepharose was carried out at 4 °C, and washes and protein elution were performed at room temperature. Sepharose was washed three times with Buffer C, and then three times with Buffer D. Finally, the receptors were eluted either in Buffers C or D containing 100 μM 1D4 9-mer peptide (TETSQVAPA). Elution in Buffer C was used for determining the A280/A500 ratio corresponding to the overall eluted protein (folded + misfolded fractions)38. On the other hand, the characterization assays were performed using rhodopsin eluted in Buffer D, which corresponds to the correctly folded protein fractions39.

UV–vis absorption spectra of WT and rhodopsin mutants

All measurements were performed as previously described38. A Cary 100Bio spectrophotometer (Varian, Australia), incorporating a water-jacketed cuvette holder connected to a circulating water bath, was used. Temperature was regulated via a Peltier accessory integrated with the spectrophotometer. Spectra were recorded over the 250–650 nm range, with a 2 nm bandwidth, 0.1 s response time, and a scan rate of 300 nm/min38. The molar extinction coefficient (ε) of each mutant was determined by an acid denaturation method40. Each ε value was calculated from the formula, ε = (A/ARh)(A440Rh/A440)εRh, where A was the absorbance at the λmax value, A440 was the absorbance at 440 nm after acid denaturation, and εRh was the molar extinction of rhodopsin (42.7 × 103 M−1 cm−1). The spectral ratio, which is used as a measure of pigment yield and stability, is defined as A280 divided by A at the visible λmax value after correction for any difference in the mutant pigment ε value as described above38.

Photobleaching and acidification of purified rhodopsins

Samples were exposed to a 150 W light source coupled with an optical fiber and a 495 nm cut-off filter. Dark-adapted pigments were illuminated for 10 s to achieve complete photo-conversion to the 380 nm-absorbing species. Immediately following photobleaching, the samples were acidified by adding 2 N H₂SO₄ to reach a final pH of 1.9. After 2 min, upon stabilization, an absorption spectrum was recorded38.

Thermal bleaching assay

Rhodopsin thermal bleaching rates in the dark were determined by recording the reduction of the Aλmax absorbance band together with the increase of A380 over time, at either 37 °C or 55 °C38. Transducin activation assays

The pigment’s ability to activate transducin was assessed by the radionucleotide filter binding assay38. The experiments were performed using 1–5 nM WT opsin or mutant receptor in DDM detergent solution in a buffer containing 10 mM Tris·HCl (pH 6.7), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM CaCl2, 0.5 mM EDTA, 1 mM dithiothreitol, 1 μM transducin purified from retina, and 3 μM [35S]GTPγS (5 Ci/mmol). The final concentration of detergent in the samples was 0.015% DDM. Reactions were initiated by addition of [35S]GTPγS in the dark, and samples were filtrated after 1 h incubation either in the dark or after illumination to determine the uptake [35S]GTPγS by the G protein38.

Fluorescence-based Gtα C-terminus binding assay

Steady-state fluorescence intensity data were collected with a SPEX Fluoromax 3 spectrophotometer (HORIBA Jobin Yvon, Kyoto, Japan) using the DataMax Software Version 2.20. Measurements of the fluorescence intensity of a variant of a high-affinity analog (VLEDLKSCGLF) from the C-terminal segment of the transducin α-subunit labeled with LY, Gtα-F25 in the presence of WT and mutants in 50 mM TRIS pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl were performed at 20 °C in 3 × 3 mm quartz cuvettes from Hellma. The measurements were performed as steady-state ensemble fluorescence recordings, providing indirect evidence of conformational changes consistent with active-like receptor states, rather than directly quantifying binding affinity or specificity. The measurements were performed in the dark and light-activated state of the WT and the mutants. Light activation of rhodopsin was obtained by illuminating the sample with light of (150 W) for 1 min with λ > 475 nm. Fluorescence excitation of LY was at 428 nm. Fluorescence emission was recorded from 480 nm to 650 nm and the maximal intensity was compared. Labeling of GTα-F was carried out essential as previously described26. The 0% baseline corresponds to the fluorescence intensity of Gtα-F in buffer alone. Observed increases in fluorescence following light activation are interpreted as consistent with the formation of an active-like cytoplasmic receptor surface, without implying quantitative binding measurements.

Molecular dynamics

Dark-state and active forms of rhodopsin were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank entries 1GZM and 3DQB, respectively (dark-state WT, active WT)42,43. Dark state and active forms of T942.61I/E1343.49Q/M2576.40Y triple mutant were modeled by mutating the residues with PyMol v1.7 (dark-state 3 M, active 3 M), respectively44. Acidic and basic amino acids were considered charged with the exception of D832.50 (both states of WT- and 3M-rhodopsin), E1133.37 (active WT-rhodopsin and both 3M-rhodopsin) and E1223.28 (all systems), which were protonated37. The Schiff base has been modeled deprotonated, except in the dark-state 3M. The inactive and active models contain 20 and 44 internal water molecules, respectively, introduced using HomolWat (https://alf06.uab.es/homolwat/)45.

All models included a covalently bound retinal molecule, using the retinal parameters that were taken from a previous study and for every system, three replicas were run46. Simulations were run with the GROMACS 5.1.4 simulation package24 employing the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field as implemented in GROMACS and Berger parameters for POPC lipids, which have been previously validated47. The simulation conditions were as in previous studies24,37. All systems were embedded in a pre-equilibrated cubic box containing a 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn- glycero-3-phosphatidylcholine (POPC) lipid bilayer. The final systems had ≈190 POPC molecules, ≈14,000 water molecules, and 0.15 M concentration of Na+ and Cl− ions. All systems were energy-minimized and subsequently subjected to a 27 ns MD simulation for equilibration with decreasing positional restraints on protein coordinates. These restraints were released, and 500 ns of MD trajectory were produced at constant pressure and temperature37.
