Sharpness matters: Higher image resolution improves generalization and explainability in chest X-ray classification
Authors: Aditya V. Kulkarni, John R. Zech, Paul H. Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deep learning (DL) chest radiograph (CXR) models are often trained on downsampled images to reduce computational overhead, despite clinical workflows operating at high resolution. Previous studies have investigated the impact of input resolution on CXR classification accuracy, yet two fundamental pillars of safe and trustworthy AI, explainability and generalizability, remain underexplored. In this retrospective study, we evaluated how training image resolution affects CXR classification performance and explanation quality in internal versus external testing. We trained Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) for disease classification on the SIIM-ACR Pneumothorax and RSNA Pneumonia datasets at six resolutions (ranging from 64\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}64 to 1024\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}1024) using five-fold cross-validation and evaluated models on internal and external test sets. Internal performance was high across resolutions (AUROC >0.85), but external testing showed substantially worse generalizability at lower training resolutions, with internal-to-external drops >20% versus 4.2%-10.7% at higher resolutions (512\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}512 to 1024\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}1024). Higher resolutions also produced more concise explanations, with the tightest saliency-map coverage at 1024\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}1024 (<4%) across models and datasets, and improved explanation quality on external data (peak precision plateauing at 768\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}768 for pneumothorax). Overall, training at higher CXR resolutions improved both generalizability and explainability, providing practical guidance for radiology AI design beyond internal test performance.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has been rapidly adopted in radiology with a focus on using deep learning (DL) algorithms for the diagnosis of diseases1–3. Although medical images are routinely acquired at high resolutions to capture fine pathological details, DL models commonly down-sample medical images during training4–6 to reduce computational costs, which may limit their ability to identify subtle diseases. This practice contrasts sharply with clinical workflows, where radiologists rely on high-resolution images to make accurate diagnoses.

Previous studies7,8 have shown that DL models trained using lower resolution medical images can still achieve high performance for certain disease diagnoses in medical imaging, despite the common assumption that fine-grained details are essential for accurate predictions. A seminal study published in 2020 by Sabottke et al.7 suggested that when training DL classification models for chest radiograph (CXR) diagnosis, model performance plateaued for most diseases at resolutions of 256\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}256 and 320\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}320 pixels, although some had peak performance with higher image resolutions, i.e., 512\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}512 or 600\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}600; note that Sabottke et al. did not evaluate higher resolutions, despite the fact that native CXR resolution is typically 1024\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}1024 or above). Subsequent work in CXR DL models8 demonstrated similarly variable conclusions with peak performance for some diseases on low resolutions, e.g., 256\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}256, and others at higher resolutions, e.g., 1024\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}1024. These variable conclusions leave an unanswered question: what is the optimal image resolution for training deep learning CXR classifiers?

Discrepancies in reported optimal resolutions for DL classification models in radiology may stem from two key factors. First, previous studies on the topic have not evaluated generalizability of models on an external testing set (i.e. out-of-distribution [OOD] testing), despite the well-known potential overestimation of model performance when evaluated on internal testing images only5. Second, prior studies have not evaluated image resolution’s impact on DL model explainability as determined by saliency map localization of imaging abnormalities9–12. DL model generalizability and explainability are both important for rigorously assessing the impact and clinical implications of using images of different resolutions when training DL classification models for radiology, especially as computational capabilities have improved in recent years that can accommodate high resolution images.

In this study, we revisit the impact of imaging resolution on medical imaging classification by addressing previously overlooked aspects of model generalizability and explanability. Specifically, we evaluated the following questions: Does increasing image resolution for training DL CXR classifiers improve not only in-distribution accuracy, but also model generalizability to external testing sets and explainability? We hypothesized that training models with higher resolution images will result in models that have higher external testing performance (even if internal performance is similar between models trained with low vs. high resolution images) and better explainability performance in localizing radiographic abnormalities.

The inclusion criteria for dataset selection in our study for both model training and testing included strong ground truth labels, defined as radiologist-defined labels and annotations (as opposed to natural language processing [NLP]-defined labels), to ensure face validity of our evaluations. We chose this inclusion criteria to improve on prior study designs on the topic of image resolution and CXR DL classification7,8, which previously used datasets with weak labels provided by NLP15,16), which are known to have high error rates15,17, and which could confound model evaluations.

We used two primary datasets for model training and internal testing Table 1: the SIIM-ACR Pneumothorax dataset18 (n=10,675) and the RSNA Pneumonia dataset19 (n=26,684), both of which have radiologist-performed pixel-level annotations for the disease of interest (pneumothorax segmentation masks for SIIM-ACR and pneumonia bounding boxes for RSNA). We used 90% of data for model development (training and tuning) and reserved 10% of both datasets as a hold out internal test set. The split was made at the patient level to avoid data leakage between splits.

For external (OOD) testing, we used the VinBigData20 and PTX-49821 datasets for pneumothorax and the SIIM-FISABIO-RSNA COVID-1922 for pneumonia. For pneumothorax external testing, we considered using the popular CANDID-PTX dataset (n= 19,237); however, at the time of this study (November 2024), this dataset was no longer available for research use23 due to new restrictions from the New Zealand government where the dataset originates from. An external pneumothorax test set was thus constructed by combining the PTX-498 dataset (n = 498 positive cases) with a VinBigData subset consisting of all positive pneumothorax cases (n = 96) and 594 randomly sampled negative cases from other disease labels; the negative sample size was chosen to match the total number of positive cases (498 + 96 = 594), yielding a balanced dataset of 1,188 images (594 positive, 594 negative). Saliency-map performance was assessed exclusively on PTX-498, as VinBigData lacks pixel-level pneumothorax annotations. Annotations for pneumonia in the SIIM-FISABIO-RSNA dataset were provided as radiologist-performed bounding boxes.

Image preprocessing comprised Z-normalization, followed by min-max scaling to adjust pixel values between 0 and 1. The normalization code was implemented in Python=3.8.10 using NumPy. The images were then resized using bilinear interpolation, the default method in the torchvision library. All resizing operations were performed with the torchvision framework to ensure consistency across different resolutions.

Since our main objective was observing trends across image resolutions and not optimizing models for a given dataset, we chose to use a five-fold cross-validation training strategy (as shown in Fig. 2A) to mitigate sensitivity of model performance to the training/validation split. All models were initialized with ImageNet-pretrained weights, and finetuned using a fixed learning rate of 0.0001 and standard hyperparameters (Supplemental Section 2). The decision to use these hyperparameters was based on empirical evaluation of the potential benefits from resolution-specific hyperparameter tuning, which proved to be inconsistent across different train-validation splits when evaluated using a five-fold cross-validation (Supplemental Fig. 1). Code used for all model development and evaluation is available at https://github.com/YiLab-SJ/sharpness-matters.git.

Evaluation strategy

We aggregated the outputs of the five models from the cross-validation strategy into an ensemble for final evaluation (as shown in Fig. 2A), using unweighted averaging as our aggregation strategy for both classification logits and saliency maps. For evaluating classification performance, we used the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUROC) Curve as our metric. We also evaluated computational tradeoff of using higher resolutions. We report peak memory (MB) usage, inference time (milliseconds), and throughput (Images/second) (Supplemental Section 3).

Characterizing good explanations

We identified two features that make for good AI explanations in CXR diagnosis: Conciseness and Accuracy. Previous literature24 has criticized saliency maps for their inability to provide useful explanations, noting that these maps can be inaccurate, as well as nonspecific (i.e., too large to be informative about where a disease is), thus failing to improve AI explainability. Therefore, it is important to choose a metric that captures both conciseness and accuracy to faithfully evaluate the usefulness of these explanations.

Unlike standard segmentation tasks (where false negatives are just as crucial as false positives), false positives are detrimental for explainability because highlighting the wrong regions can lead to immediate mistrust. Previous work has explored metrics that focus on the importance of precision in AI explanations, including sparsity24, hit rate25, in which only one point is used as an explanation and a metric \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{gt}$$\end{document} which focuses on the overlap of the most salient pixels with the ground truth mask26. The latter metric can be reformulated as precision, a common metric that evaluates positive predictive power. In this study, we chose to use precision as our primary metric for evaluating saliency maps. Precision quantifies the proportion of predicted salient pixels that overlap with the ground truth annotation. It is defined as the ratio of true positives (TP) to all predicted positives, i.e the sum of TP and false positives (FP):\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {{Precision}} = \frac{| M^{GT} \cap M^{pred} |}{| M^{pred} |} = = \frac{TP}{TP+FP} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M^{GT}$$\end{document} is the ground truth mask and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M^{pred}$$\end{document} is the predicted explanation mask.

By focusing on precision, we account for both conciseness and accuracy, offering insights into specificity and reliability of salient regions. This is particularly important in radiology, where consistent and trustworthy saliency maps can be a critical component of AI-assisted clinical workflows.

In addition to precision, we also report coverage, which represents the average size of explanation masks for evaluating conciseness independent of accuracy. Coverage is simply defined as ratio of the size of the explanation mask to the size of the image.

Saliency map generation and evaluation

We used Grad-CAM27 to generate saliency maps. Since quantitative evaluation metrics typically compare binary masks, we thresholded the normalized Grad-CAM saliency maps to binary explanation masks. Since the choice of optimal thresholds can vary for different datasets and models, we chose to evaluate both precision and coverage on three different thresholds (t=0.25, t=0.5, t=0.75) and reported the average performance as shown in Fig. 2B (see Supplemental Section 4 for details and reasoning behind thresholding method). Since the ground truth annotations for pneumonia datasets are bounding boxes, we extracted bounding boxes (Supplemental Section 5) from the thresholded saliency maps for the pneumonia dataset for a fair comparison.

For our saliency map localization evaluations, saliency map localization precision is set to zero for all false predictions. This reflects clinical practice, where a saliency map would only be presented following a positive disease prediction. Consequently, no map would be shown for false negative results (i.e., the disease that is present is not localized), and for false positive results, no disease is present for the map to localize (i.e., the saliency map localization precision is zero).

Statistical analysis

For each model and dataset, AUROC and Precision for every resolution was compared against every other resolution, while inter-model and inter-dataset comparisons were excluded. Results that were considered a statistically significant improvement needed to be significantly better than all lower resolutions. Results with a P-values lower than 0.05 were defined as statistically significant. Given the large number of comparisons, we applied the Benjamini/Hochberg False Discovery Rate procedure28 on a per-dataset basis to reduce the likelihood of false positives. For classification performance, we used Delong’s test29 to compare AUROC values. We employed the Mann–Whitney U test30 for saliency localization metrics, since our data was unpaired and not normally distributed. Since models trained at different resolutions produce distinct sets of false positives and false negatives, each resolution yields a unique evaluation subset, making the data unpaired across resolutions.

Results

Classification performance

Although training models with higher-resolution images offered marginal gains in classification performance on internal test datasets, it led to a substantial improvement in performance on external (OOD) data. This enhanced external performance reached a plateau when models were trained with images at 512 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 512 pixels. Computational tradeoff

Supplemental Tables 1 and 2 show trends indicating a non-linear relationship between image resolution and all computational benchmarking metrics. As image resolution increases, both ResNet and DenseNet exhibit a consistent upward trend in peak memory usage and inference time per batch, with minimal changes at lower resolutions (64–224) and sharp increases beyond 512\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}512, indicating non-linear computational scaling. Throughput, in contrast, shows a steady decline with increasing resolution, with the most significant drop occurring between 224 and 512, marking a critical threshold for performance degradation. These results suggest that while low-resolution inputs maintain efficient runtime characteristics, higher resolutions impose substantial computational and memory demands.

Discussion

In this study, we revisit the question of how image resolution impacts DL performance for CXR classification. The widespread use of 224 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 224 inputs in medical image classification is partly inherited from transfer learning practices established in natural image analysis, where ImageNet-pretrained models commonly use this resolution. While this convention has practical advantages, including compatibility with standard pretrained architectures, it has also encouraged the routine use of relatively low-resolution inputs in medical imaging. While prior work on this topic7,8 suggested little or modest gains on increasing resolution for many classification tasks, these studies did not perform external testing or explainability analysis, both of which are critical for clinical evaluations of AI in medical imaging. In this study, we evaluate these aspects of DL model performance and found that models trained with lower resolution images were more susceptible to poor generalizability and had less concise and accurate explanations. Although these trends were slightly more pronounced in pneumothorax, both diseases evaluated showed consistent improvements with increasing resolution, despite the varying levels of visual granularity required for their detection.

We found that training with low resolution can lead to poor generalizability to external data. While increasing training image resolution above 512\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}512 did not lead to major gains in classification performance in internal or external testing, resolutions below this threshold resulted in a sharp performance decline, particularly for pneumothorax. Our findings align with prior observations31 that DL models often show a performance gap between internal and external datasets. However, the role of image resolution in modulating this gap has received limited attention. We show that training at lower resolutions amplifies this generalization gap, suggesting that lower-resolution models fail to capture features essential for robustness to external data and revealing an added vulnerability of low-resolution training to distributional shifts. Strong in-distribution performance at low resolutions can be misleading, a limitation that may have gone unnoticed in prior work7,8 due to the lack of external testing. Although the exact optimal resolution may vary by pathology, our findings underscore the importance of maintaining at least 512\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}512 resolution for reliable deployment, which offers a practical balance between generalizability and computational cost, particularly as computational capacity becomes less of a limiting factor over time.

We observed that increasing image resolution increased conciseness (lower coverage) in explanations, aligning with qualitative findings from our work and prior studies8, which attribute this effect to a reduced effective receptive field in CNNs at higher resolutions. However, while previous work primarily focused on qualitative observations, our study also provides quantitative evidence supporting this trend. In our experiments, higher resolutions produced more concise localizations of disease. These findings suggest that when conciseness of explanation is important, higher resolutions should be considered, as they consistently yield more focused saliency maps regardless of pathology type or model architecture.

In our experiments, quality of explanations, as defined by precision, improved as image resolution increased. Previous work24–26 emphasizes the importance of focused explanations to increase trust and adoptability of AI algorithms. Motivated by these insights, we use precision as an indicator of explanation quality in our evaluation. For both crisp-appearing imaging findings (pneumothorax, i.e., a line representing the pleural edge) and coarser-appearing pathology (pneumonia, which frequently presents as relatively ill-defined opacities compared to a pleural edge), higher resolution training data yielded better out-of-distribution explanations. This reinforces the notion that higher resolutions are more robust to external data, not only when it comes to classification performance, but also for the quality of explanations. To our knowledge, this is the first study to quantitatively examine how image resolution affects the quality of saliency map explanations. Given our findings, researchers should consider the importance of precise explanations and improved generalizability based on the level of granularity required for their application against other factors like computational trade-offs when choosing image resolutions for their AI pipelines.

The effect size of improvement in quality of explanations and classification with higher resolution training data was, however, higher for pneumothorax compared to pneumonia. One explanation is that pneumothorax is more challenging to localize than pneumonia for radiologists due to its subtle nature, and AI-models may benefit from the fine-details captured at higher resolutions for diseases that require more granularity. This is also consistent with clinical practice, where pneumothorax diagnosis often relies on careful inspection of the pleural line and other subtle radiographic details; in this sense, higher input resolution may function analogously to magnified visual inspection. Secondly, disease manifestation can influence the behavior of explanation metrics. Pneumonia has a more heterogeneous radiographic presentation, ranging from large dense consolidations to smaller and subtler infiltrative opacities, which may partly explain why improvements with increasing resolution are less pronounced than for pneumothorax. For example, pneumonia can span large portions of the lungs, making concise localization less critical. As a result, bounding boxes are commonly used for annotation. However, this annotation style can affect the interpretation of localization metrics. Specifically, large ground-truth boxes can inflate metric scores if the saliency maps have high coverage–even if they are imprecise, as often seen in low-resolution models. This may blur the gap between poor and meaningful explanations, inflating quantitative performance of low-resolution explanations for pneumonia.

This study has limitations. While we evaluated multiple CXR datasets and diseases, our findings may not generalize to other imaging modalities and pathologies. In addition, our experiments focused on CNN-based architectures, and do not explore transformer-based models. Evaluating whether the trends observed here extend across model families is an important direction for future work. In addition, our external pneumonia evaluation used a COVID-focused dataset, which may not fully capture the broader radiographic and clinical heterogeneity of non-COVID pneumonia. As a result, the observed external generalization trends for pneumonia should be interpreted with some caution and may not directly extend to all pneumonia subtypes or clinical settings. The scale of evaluation, particularly for pneumothorax, was also influenced by the limited availability of expert-annotated datasets; for example, resources like CANDID-PTX32 exist but are no longer accessible for research use per updated governmental restrictions23. From a technical standpoint, evaluating the quality of model explanations remains inherently challenging. In this work, we used quantitative metrics such as precision and coverage, focusing on conciseness, inspired by prior work24–26. While these metrics offer a reproducible and interpretable way to assess explanation quality, it remains unclear whether they are optimally aligned with clinical expectations, which is a topic for future research. Future user studies involving clinicians could help validate the relevance of these metrics or inform the development of new ones better suited to clinical decision-making. Finally, while this study isolates the effect of input resolution, performance in medical image AI may also be improved through architectural innovation or ROI-based preprocessing. Exploring how these strategies interact is an important direction for future work.

In conclusion, we have revisited the question of whether higher image resolutions improve DL model classification performance in medical imaging. By expanding our evaluation to include external test performance using strong ground truth standards and disease localization, we present a rigorous evaluation of the benefits of increasing image resolution. We found that higher image resolution significantly improves both generalizability and explainability of CXR DL classification models. Based on our findings, we recommend starting with 512x512 image resolution as a reasonable balance between computational cost and performance.
