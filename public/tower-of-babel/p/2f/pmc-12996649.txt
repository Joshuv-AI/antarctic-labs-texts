Persistent histone H3K27 acetylation contributes to excessive scar formation after spinal cord injury through the regulation of microglial cholesterol accumulation
Authors: Qiujiang Qiao, Qing Gong, Ning Li, Haowei Sun, Xufang Ru, Ning Mu, Beike Chen, Wenyan Li, Tunan Chen, Hua Feng, Yujie Chen, Huilin Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractScar formation is a critical determinant of neurological recovery following spinal cord injury (SCI) because scars act as a physical support and barrier that influences axonal regeneration and remyelination. However, the regulatory mechanisms governing scar formation remain incompletely understood. Integrated multiomics analysis of publicly available single-cell RNA-seq and ATAC-seq data (GSE230765) from adult mice during the subacute/chronic phases of SCI revealed epigenetic mechanisms underlying microglial activation. Cell communication analysis revealed a connection between persistent microglial activation and excessive scar formation after SCI. Pharmacological inhibition of histone acetylation using L002 was employed to validate the H3K27ac-mediated regulation of the microglial phenotype, scar formation, and functional recovery. Persistent microglial activation post-SCI resulted in characteristic cholesterol metabolic reprogramming, with intracellular cholesterol accumulation correlated with sustained microglial activation. Single-cell ATAC-seq revealed chromatin accessibility-mediated epigenetic control of cholesterol metabolism gene expression, identifying H3K27ac as a pivotal regulator. L002-mediated H3K27ac inhibition attenuated cholesterol accumulation, mitigated neuroinflammation, and reduced scar formation through the disruption of microglia–astrocyte–fibroblast communication. Mechanistically, SPP1 secretion from activated microglia drove excessive scar formation, and inhibition of H3K27ac reduced the level of SPP1. Our study suggests that H3K27ac mediates the epigenetic regulation of myeloid cell activation in SCI pathogenesis. Targeted modulation of this histone modification site attenuates chronic microglial activation and subsequent scar formation, suggesting an innovative therapeutic strategy for neural repair. These findings establish chromatin remodeling as a promising target for improving functional recovery post-SCI.Keywords: Spinal cord injury, H3k27ac, Microglia, Cholesterol metabolism, Scar formation

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) patients frequently develop neurological impairments involving sensory, motor, and autonomic functions following acute trauma [1,2]. The regenerative capacity of the central nervous system (CNS) is limited after injury. Although various neuroregenerative strategies have shown promise [3,4], persistent challenges remain because of the complex pathological microenvironment and physical barriers formed by scar tissue. Scar tissue, which is an incomplete and nonfunctional tissue following repair, plays dual roles during different phases of postinjury recovery, and two distinct scar types emerge: astrocyte-derived glial scars and fibroblast-mediated fibrous scars. Glial scars establish a physiological barrier that isolates damaged areas, preventing inflammatory spread and stimulating axon regeneration. However, glial scars concurrently release inhibitory molecules [5,6]. Similarly, fibrous scars provide structural support by filling the lesion cavity while sustaining an inhibitory microenvironment characterized by chronic inflammation and fibrotic deposition [7,8]. Overall, excessive scar formation during the chronic phase may be detrimental to the repair of the injured spinal cord. Understanding the key initiating factors that govern scar formation mechanisms remains pivotal for developing targeted therapeutic interventions to enhance neural regeneration post-SCI.

Neonatal mice and zebrafish demonstrate scarless healing with superior neural functional recovery following SCI [9,10]. This regenerative capacity is correlated with the transient microglia/macrophage activation observed in these models. In contrast, adult mouse SCI models exhibit sustained activation of myeloid cells [11]. Spinal scar tissue in adult mammals primarily consists of persistently activated microglia/macrophages, astrocytes, fibroblasts, and the extracellular matrix components secreted by these cell types. The presence of sustained myeloid cell activation in adult murine SCI models strongly suggests that abnormally prolonged activation of these cells constitutes a critical factor in scar formation [7,12]. The important pathological role of dysregulated myeloid cell activation extends across multiple disease states [13,14]. Therefore, identifying key regulators of persistent microglial/macrophage activation is essential for understanding the mechanistic contributions of these regulators to scar tissue development.

Cholesterol, a vital biomolecule that is abundant in the CNS, plays critical roles in maintaining cellular homeostasis. Aberrant cholesterol accumulation in microglia has been implicated in the pathogenesis of multiple CNS disorders [15,16]. Previous studies have shown that cholesterol overload is a key contributor to the pathological persistence of microglial/macrophage activation [17]. Emerging evidence has demonstrated that cholesterol accumulation in spinal myeloid cells exacerbates tissue damage and impedes neurological recovery after SCI [18] and that the underlying mechanism involves extensive phagocytosis of cellular and myelin debris by microglia/macrophages. Phagocytosis-derived cholesterol deposition in these myeloid cells has been recognized as a pivotal driver of scar formation. However, the regulatory mechanisms that govern the shifts in cholesterol metabolism in these cells remain poorly understood.

Epigenetic regulation serves as a critical mechanism that governs innate immune cell responses under diverse stimuli. Among epigenetic modifications, histone modifications, particularly acetylation, have been extensively investigated. The distal enhancer H3K27ac facilitates transcriptional activation by increasing chromatin accessibility during cellular stress responses, and sustained H3K27ac enrichment is associated with chronic inflammation in microglia [19,20]. However, the functional role of H3K27ac in the pathogenesis of traumatic SCI remains undefined. In this study, we identified a strong correlation between excessive scar regeneration and persistent microglial activation. Single-cell RNA sequencing in adult mice revealed a progressive increase in the proportion of microglia from the acute phase to the chronic phase of injury, accompanied by pronounced activation signatures. This activated state was strongly linked to dysregulated cholesterol metabolism, a connection further supported by single-cell ATAC-seq data, which demonstrated chromatin accessibility-mediated control of myeloid cholesterol metabolism. With a focus on the pivotal role of H3K27ac in chromatin remodeling, this study systematically investigated the following: 1. The effects of H3K27ac levels on cholesterol metabolism and the activation of microglia during the chronic phase and 2. The relationship between H3K27ac and scar formation. Our findings provide novel insights into the epigenetic regulation of myeloid cell activation during neural repair processes.

Materials and methods

Animals

A total of 260 adult male C57BL/6J mice (8–16 weeks of age, weighing 20–30 g) were used to establish traumatic SCI models. The animals were maintained under comfortable, controlled environmental conditions (21 ± 3 °C, 50 ± 5 % humidity) with a 12/12-h light/dark cycle and provided ad libitum access to food and water. All the experimental procedures were approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Third Military Medical University (No. AMUWEC20223823). Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups following a computer-generated randomization protocol. This study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines for research reporting.

SCI model and drug treatment

A crush spinal cord injury model (SCI group) was established as previously described [21]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (3 % induction, 1.5 % maintenance) and positioned under a surgical microscope. After sequential incisions of the skin and paraspinal muscles were made, laminectomy was performed at the T9–T10 level to expose the spinal cord. No. 5 Dumont forceps (Fine Science Tools) were then applied vertically at the T10 level for 5 s to induce standardized SCI. A gap of 0.5 mm created by the forceps when closed was defined as a moderate crush injury as previously described [21]. The sham-operated mice (uninjured group) underwent the same laminectomy procedure but the spinal cord was not clipped. Postoperatively, the animals received daily injections of antibiotics for infection prophylaxis. Bladder function was monitored twice daily, with manual bladder expression performed until spontaneous voiding resumed. Specifically, all the above surgeries were performed by the same operator. The BMS scores of the SCI mice at 0.5, 1, and 3 days postinjury were used to verify reproducibility at the behavioral level. Mice with an abnormal BMS score were excluded from the study, as described in Supplementary Material 1. The histone acetylation inhibitor L002 was administered intraperitoneally at a dosage of 10 mg/kg once every 3 days for a total of 5 injections beginning on the 7th day after injury (SCI + L002 group). Mice in the vehicle group were injected with the same vehicle without L002 (SCI + Veh group).

Bioinformatics analysis

Multiomics (scRNA-seq, scATAC-seq and spatial transcriptomics) data acquisition and quality control

Publicly available scRNA-seq, scATAC-seq and spatial transcriptomics data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (accession number GSE230765) and an interactive website (http://tabulaeparalytica.com). The data were downloaded as a gene expression matrix. The scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq data were subjected to quality control using Seurat (version 5.3.0) according to previously published methods [21].

Identification of marker genes and cell type annotation

Cell type-specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the “FindAllMarkers” function in Seurat (version 4.3.0). Cell type annotation was conducted by cross-referencing established lineage-specific marker genes from the CellMarker 2.0 database (http://bio-bigdata.hrbmu.edu.cn/CellMarker) with our transcriptional profiles. For data visualization, we implemented dimensionality reduction through uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) algorithms using Seurat's standard pipeline.

Gene set score analysis

The gene sets utilized were acquired from the GSEA portal (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org). Gene set enrichment scores were computed using the gene set variation analysis (GSVA) package (v2.0.7) with default parameters. For comparative analysis, we implemented the AddModuleScore algorithm in Seurat (v5.3.0) to calculate module activity scores. Differential pathway activation between experimental groups was assessed through nonparametric Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, with Benjamini‒Hochberg correction applied for multiple hypothesis testing (P value adjusted <0.05).

CellChat analysis

To investigate intercellular interactions among distinct cell types, we performed cell‒cell communication analysis using the CellChat package (version 2.1.2). Single-cell transcriptomic datasets were preprocessed and normalized using standard workflows. CellChat algorithms were applied to identify significant ligand‒receptor interactions and quantify the probabilities of communication between heterogeneous cell populations. Finally, we visualized the interaction networks to map cellular communication patterns, employing graph-based layouts to highlight key signaling pathways.

Analysis of scATAC-seq data

scATAC-seq data were processed using the Signac package (v1.7.0) following established chromatin accessibility workflows. The data were first read, and a Seurat object was created. Cell type annotations were transferred from matched single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets, leveraging our previously published annotation framework. Following quality control, we performed term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) normalization and dimensionality reduction using latent semantic indexing (LSI). For multimodal integration, we employed weighted nearest neighbor analysis to align the scATAC-seq profiles with their scRNA-seq counterparts. Differential chromatin accessibility was assessed through the Benjamini‒Hochberg method (with candidate peaks requiring a |average log2FC|≥1 and an FDR <0.05). Significant peaks were annotated using the mm10 genome build.

Immunofluorescence staining

After anesthesia, the mice were transcardially perfused with precooled saline through the left ventricle until hepatic blanching was observed. This was immediately followed by secondary perfusion with ice-cold 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Approximately 5 mm long perilesional spinal cord tissues were carefully excised and postfixed in fresh 4 % PFA at 4 °C for 24 h. After fixation, the tissues underwent graded dehydration in 10 %, 20 %, and 30 % sucrose solutions (24 h at each concentration). Cryosections (20 μm thick) were prepared using a cryostat (CM3050S-3–1-1; Leica, Shanghai, China) and stored at −80 °C until further processing. The frozen sections of the spinal cord included peripheral axial or longitudinal sections.

Tissue permeabilization was performed using Enhanced Immunostaining Permeabilization Buffer (P0097; Beyotime) for 20 min at RT. The sections were then blocked with QuickBlock™ Blocking Buffer for Immunol Staining (P0260; Beyotime) for 20 min at RT. The following primary antibodies diluted in QuickBlock™ Primary Antibody Dilution Buffer for Immunol Staining (P0262; Beyotime) were applied to the sections and incubated at 4 °C overnight: anti-IBA1 (1:400, chicken, Abcam, ab318302), anti-H3K27ac (1:7000, rabbit, Abcam, ab177178), anti-TNFα (1:200, mouse, Proteintech, 60291-1-Ig), anti-GFAP (1:200, mouse, Boster, BM0055), anti-NeuN (1:400, mouse, Abcam, ab104224), anti-OLIG2 (1:100, rabbit, Abcam, ab109186), anti-APC (CC1) (1:100, mouse, Sigma‒Aldrich, OP80), anti-MBP (1:500, mouse, Thermo, MA5-15922), anti-NF200 (1:200, rabbit, Sigma‒Aldrich, N0142), and anti-laminin (1:1000, Rabbit, Thermo, PA1-16730). The following day, the tissue sections were rinsed three times with PBS. Species-matched fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies were then applied for incubation at room temperature for 1.5 h in the dark. After three additional PBS washes, the slides were mounted with Antifade Mounting Medium containing DAPI (P0131; Beyotime). High-resolution confocal imaging was performed using an LSM 880 microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The observation field under the microscope was random with an appropriate magnification level. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence was conducted using ImageJ V1.53 (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD).

ORO staining

Oil Red O staining was performed using a modified Lipid Staining Kit (C0158; Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The samples were fixed and dehydrated through a graded series of sucrose solutions. The processed specimens were cryosectioned in the coronal plane using a freezing microtome at −20 °C. Following equilibration at room temperature for 10 min, the tissue sections were briefly rinsed with washing solution for 20 s. Excess solution was carefully removed by aspiration, and the sections were incubated with freshly prepared Oil Red O working solution for 20 min at room temperature. After incubation, the sections were rinsed with washing solution for 30 s. This step was followed by a brief wash in distilled water under gentle agitation for 30 s. Finally, the sections were mounted for microscopic examination.

Western blotting

Spinal cord tissues were homogenized in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (P0013C; Beyotime Biotechnology) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. The lysates were sonicated on ice (3 cycles of 10-sec pulses) followed by centrifugation at 12,000×g for 25 min at 4 °C. The protein concentrations in the supernatants were determined using a BCA Assay Kit (P0010; Beyotime), after which the samples were adjusted to equal concentrations. After 5 × SDS‒PAGE protein loading buffer (Boster, AR1112) was added, the protein samples were denatured by boiling at 100 °C for 10 min in a metal bath. The proteins were separated on 10 % SDS‒PAGE gels under constant voltage (80 V for the stacking gel and 120 V for the separating gel) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes using the wet transfer method (220 mA, 90 min). The membranes were blocked with 5 % nonfat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, followed by three 10-min TBST washes. Primary antibodies against the following proteins were diluted in antibody dilution buffer: H3k27ac (1:10,000, rabbit, Abcam, ab177178), MMP3 (1:10,000, mouse, Proteintech, 66338-1-Ig), TGFβ (1:10,000, rabbit, Proteintech, 81746-2-RR), INOS (1:8000, rabbit, Proteintech, 18985-1-AP), MBP (1:100, mouse, Thermo, MA5-15922), and β-actin (1:50,000, mouse, Proteintech, 66009-1-Ig). The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After three 10-min TBST washes the next day, the membranes were probed with the corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-rabbi, 1:5000; anti-mouse, 1:5000) for 1.5 h at room temperature. Following the final TBST washes (3 × 10 min), protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and visualized with a Fusion FX Edge Spectra multifunctional imaging system (Vilber Bio Imaging, Beijing, China). Band intensity was quantified with ImageJ software (v1.53).

ChIP‒qPCR

The ChIP assay was performed using a BeyoChIP™ Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP) Assay Kit and a Protein A/G Magnetic Beads Kit (P2080; Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, spinal cord tissues were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and homogenized using a prechilled mortar and pestle. The pulverized tissue was resuspended in ice-cold lysis buffer and centrifuged at 12,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Crosslinking was achieved by incubating the cell pellets with 1 % formaldehyde at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by quenching with glycine for 5 min. After three washes with PBS, the chromatin was fragmented using a sonicator to obtain 200–1000 bp DNA fragments. The sheared chromatin was precleared with protein A/G magnetic beads for 1 h at 4 °C before immunoprecipitation with a rabbit anti-H3K27ac antibody (1:50) or a normal rabbit IgG control overnight. Antibody‒chromatin complexes were captured by incubation with 80 μl of protein A/G magnetic beads for 1 h at 4 °C. Beads were magnetically separated (10 s) and sequentially washed with low-salt buffer (0.1 % SDS, 1 % Triton X-100, 2 mM EDTA, 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl), high-salt buffer (500 mM NaCl), LiCl buffer (0.25 M LiCl, 1 % NP-40, 1 % sodium deoxycholate, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0)), and TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM EDTA)). Crosslinking was reversed by incubation with 200 mM NaCl and 10 μg of proteinase K at 65 °C for 6 h. Purified DNA was analyzed by qPCR using SYBR Green Master Mix with specific primers.

Flow cytometry

Mice were humanely euthanized via intraperitoneal administration of sodium pentobarbital. Cardiac perfusion was immediately performed using ice-cold saline. Spinal cord tissues were rapidly excised and transferred to cell staining buffer (abs9475, Absin) to maintain cell viability. Tissue processing involved the following three sequential steps. Mechanical dissociation: the tissues were minced into 1 mm3 fragments using sterile scalpels; enzymatic digestion: the fragments were incubated with tissue dissociation solution (abs9482, Absin) at 37 °C for 30 min with gentle agitation (200 rpm); and filtration: the digested suspension was passed through a 40-μm nylon mesh after enzymatic activity was quenched with 5 mL of cell staining buffer. Cell isolation was achieved through differential centrifugation (500×g, 5 min) followed by myelin removal using a discontinuous Percoll gradient (30 %/70 %). The cells were subjected to immunostaining with the following antibody panel in the dark (4 °C, 30 min): V450-conjugated anti-CD45, APC-conjugated anti-CD11b, and BODIPY493/503 for lipid droplets. After two washes with staining buffer, the cellular profiles were analyzed on a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer. FlowJo software (v10.10.0) was used for compensation control and population gating.

Behavioral testing

Functional recovery was evaluated using four standardized behavioral tests. For Basso Mouse Scale (BMS) scoring, two trained observers who were binded to the experimental groups independently assessed hindlimb motor function on a scale of 0–9. The mice were placed in an open-field arena. Three consecutive trials were conducted per session, with final scores representing a consensus between observers. Rotarod performance was measured using an accelerating rotating rod as previously described. After acclimation training, the mice underwent three test trials. We recorded the latency to fall (seconds) and calculated the mean values from three consecutive trials per animal. Hindlimb strength was quantified through inclined plane testing. The mice were positioned on an adjustable platform starting at 0° inclination. The angle was increased in 5° increments until the animal could not maintain its position for ≥5 s. The maximum sustainable angle was recorded from five trials, with 2-min rest periods between attempts. Locomotor coordination was assessed using a horizontal ladder apparatus. We randomized the rung patterns before each trial by removing 25 % of the rungs. The mice performed five crossings per session, with error rates calculated as (missteps/total steps) × 100 %.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism version 8.0 was used for statistical analysis. The experimental data are shown as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). For statistical comparisons, an independent two-sample Student's t-test (between two groups) and one-way ANOVA (between multiple groups) with Tukey's test were employed for normally distributed data with equal variances. A p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Microglia exhibit persistent alterations in cholesterol metabolism and cholesterol accumulation after SCI

Single-cell sequencing analysis of spinal cord tissues from the sham group and the SCI group at 7 days and 2 months post-SCI revealed eight distinct cell populations: oligodendrocytes, microglia, neurons, astrocytes, fibroblasts, lymphocytes, ependyma, and endothelial cells (Fig. 1A and B). Cell type identification was validated through characteristic marker expression (Fig. 1C). Quantitative analysis revealed that among these cell types, microglia increased in population most significantly from the subacute phase to the chronic injury phase (Fig. 1D). To examine the microglia at higher resolution, we reclustered microglia from the uninjured, pSCI7d and pSCI2m groups, resulting in the identification of five distinct clusters (Fig. 1E and F). Specific gene expression profiles of these microglial subpopulations revealed their functional states: Cluster 0 was characterized by increased expression of genes involved in endosomal transport, vesicle organization and macroautophagy (Flt1, Colec12, Gas7, Prkg1 and Dkk2). Cluster 1 was associated with homeostatic processes and expressed high levels of genes such as Siglech, Atp8a2 and Csmd3. Clusters 3 displayed upregulation expression of genes involved in cytoplasmic translation, phagocytosis and lipid transport (Gpnmb, Apoe, Actg1, Ctsb and Spp1), with cluster 4 showing upregulation of genes involved in RNA splicing, regulation of the innate immune response and viral processes (Irf7, Ifi35, Ccl12, and Ifit2). Cluster 5 expressed genes involved in cytoplasmic translation, small GTPase-mediated signal transduction and cellular component disassembly (Plp1, Sparc and Pcdh9) (Fig. S1A and B). Functional enrichment analysis of microglia revealed persistent alterations in cholesterol metabolism in response to SCI, accompanied by alterations in lysosomal function and autophagy. Among metabolic pathways, cholesterol metabolism was most prominently affected in microglia (Fig. 1G and H). Gene set variation analysis confirmed the sustained dysregulation of cholesterol-related pathways at both 7 days and 2 months post-SCI compared with the sham control treatment (Fig. 1I). Importantly, ORO staining revealed persistent cholesterol accumulation in spinal cord tissues even at 2 months post-injury (Fig. 1J).

Chromatin accessibility regulates changes in persistent cholesterol metabolism in microglia after SCI

Epigenetic modifications are common regulatory mechanisms in acute cellular responses. Building on the cell annotations from the single-cell RNA sequencing data, the single-cell ATAC-seq results were similarly used to classify the cells into eight major cell types: oligodendrocytes, microglia, neurons, astrocytes, fibroblasts, lymphocytes, ependymal cells, and endothelial cells (Fig. 2A). Further analysis of the microglial ATAC-seq data revealed temporal dynamics (|average log2FC|≥1, FDR <0.05) through volcano plots comparing chromatin accessibility at 7 days post-injury, at 2 months post-injury, and in the sham group (Fig. 2B and C). This accessibility modulates transcriptional activity by regulating the epigenetic control of gene expression, with increased chromatin accessibility typically enhancing transcription. Intersection analysis between regions showing enhanced chromatin accessibility and upregulated genes in the microglial transcriptomes revealed functional enrichment specifically in cholesterol metabolism pathways (Fig. 2D and E). In support of these findings, coordinated patterns emerged from violin plots illustrating cholesterol-related gene expression and corresponding heatmaps of differences in chromatin accessibility, collectively validating the epigenetic regulation of cholesterol metabolism (Fig. 2F and G).

The level of H3K27ac in microglia continuously increases after SCI and regulates the expression of cholesterol metabolism-related genes

The regulation of chromatin accessibility primarily involves histone modifications and DNA methylation. Among these modifications, histone modifications, including acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and lactylation, serve as predominant epigenetic mechanisms during acute stress responses. Single-cell gene set scoring revealed a sustained increase in epigenetic regulatory activity from the subacute phase to the chronic phase following spinal cord injury, with histone acetylation demonstrating the most pronounced increase (Fig. 3A). Further analysis revealed that microglia, as the primary cellular component, exhibited increased histone acetyltransferase activity (Fig. 3B). Heatmap visualization revealed the expression patterns of histone acetyltransferase- and deacetylase-associated genes specifically within microglial populations (Fig. 3C), and H3K27ac emerged as the most prevalent type of histone regulation. Western blot analysis revealed persistently elevated H3K27ac levels in spinal cord tissues at both 7 days and 2 months post-injury compared with those in the sham group (Fig. 3D, WB: pSCI7d vs. uninjured, p < 0.0001; pSCI2m vs. uninjured, p < 0.0001, n = 6). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed the sustained increase in H3K27ac in microglia following injury (Fig. 3E–G, IF: microglia, pSCI7d vs. uninjured, p < 0.0001; pSCI2m vs. uninjured, p < 0.0001; astrocytes, pSCI7d vs. uninjured, p = 0.0011; pSCI2m vs. uninjured, p = 0.0006; neurons, pSCI7d vs. uninjured, p = 0.9903; pSCI2m vs. uninjured, p = 0.0154; n = 5). Specifically, the levels of H3K27ac in microglia (labeled with IBA1) significantly increased from the sham group to the pSCI7d and pSCI2m groups. The levels of H3K27ac in astrocytes (labeled with GFAP) also increased from the sham group to the groups 7 days and 2 months post-SCI. The H3K27ac level in neurons did not significantly increase from the uninjured group to the group at 7 days after SCI but increased from the groups at 7 days to 2 months post-SCI. These results suggested that H3K27ac-mediated epigenetic regulation may be widely involved in the functional remodeling of multiple neural cell types after SCI, but the results of sc-RNA/sc-ATAC-seq and IF staining revealed that most of the main cells whose H3K27ac levels increased after SCI were microglia. ChIP‒qPCR analysis revealed direct transcriptional regulation of cholesterol metabolism-related genes, including SOAT1, ABCA1, ABCG1, and NCEH1, by H3K27ac (Fig. 3H).

Pharmacological inhibition of H3K27ac reduces cholesterol accumulation in microglia and improves the microglial inflammatory activation phenotype

To investigate the functional role of H3K27ac activity in microglial cholesterol metabolism and inflammatory responses, we used the acetyltransferase inhibitor L002 to decrease H3K27ac levels (Fig. 4A). Western blot analysis and immunofluorescence staining confirmed the ability of L002 to reduce H3K27ac levels in microglia (Fig. 4B and C; WB: SCI + L002 vs. SCI + Veh; p < 0.0001; n = 6; IF: microglia; SCI + L002 vs. SCI + Veh; p < 0.0001; n = 5). After L002 treatment, the level of H3K27ac in astrocytes decreased slightly, while the level of H3K27ac in neurons did not change significantly (Fig. S2A and B). Quantitative assessments using cholesterol assays and flow cytometry demonstrated that H3K27ac inhibition significantly decreased cholesterol accumulation in microglia postinjury (Fig. 4D and E; total cholesterol: SCI + L002 vs. SCI + Veh, p = 0.0204, n = 4; FCM: MFI of BODIPY493, SCI + L002 vs. SCI + Veh, p = 0.0405). ORO staining also revealed reduced cholesterol accumulation in spinal cord tissues in the SCI + L002 group (Fig. 4F). Consistent with these findings, immunofluorescence staining revealed a marked reduction in TNFα expression in L002-treated microglia (Fig. 4G–IF: microglia, SCI + L002 vs. SCI + Veh, p = 0.0011; n = 5). Furthermore, Western blot analysis corroborated these observations, revealing slightly decreased protein levels of the inflammatory mediators TGFβ and iNOS following H3K27ac suppression (Fig. 4H and I, WB: iNOS, SCI + L002 vs. SCI + Veh, p = 0.0151; TGFβ, SCI + L002 vs. SCI + Veh, p = 0.083; n = 6). Interestingly, the decreases in the levels of these inflammatory factors were small, so other functional proteins may work in concert to cause excessive scar formation.

Pharmacological inhibition of H3K27ac regulates scar formation during the chronic phase after SCI via SPP1

Compared with adult mice, neonatal mice exhibit superior functional recovery following SCI, with scarless repair representing a key distinguishing feature. Our cell communication analysis revealed that microglia interact most robustly with fibroblasts and astrocytes postinjury (Fig. 5A). Intriguingly, these two cell types are critically involved in scar formation. SPP1 emerged as a key signaling mediator in these cellular communications (Fig. 5B and C). Spatial transcriptomic profiling further demonstrated the colocalization of the scar complex with astrocyte and fibroblast populations (Fig. 5D and E). Single-cell sequencing demonstrated that the expression of SPP1 remained elevated after SCI and was closely associated with the subpopulation of microglia characterized by altered cholesterol metabolism (Fig. 5F). Inhibition of histone acetylation significantly reduced SPP1 expression levels (Fig. 5G, WB: SCI vs. uninjured, p < 0.0001; SCI + L002 vs. SCI + Veh, p = 0.0300; n = 6). This intervention also effectively attenuated scar formation in the lesion area, whereas exogenous recombinant SPP1 protein administration reversed this antiscarring effect (Fig. 5H and IF: SCI + L002 vs. SCI + Veh, p = 0.0005; SCI + L002+rSPP1 vs. SCI + L002, p = 0.0109, n = 4). Furthermore, the density of microglia changed in a similar manner after treatment (Fig. 5I, IF: SCI + L002 vs. SCI + Veh, p = 0.0068; SCI + L002+rSPP1 vs. SCI + L002, p = 0.0208; n = 4). These findings collectively establish SPP1 as a pivotal mediator through which cholesterol-accumulating microglia drive scar formation that works synergistically with other inflammatory factors to promote chronic-phase scarring after SCI.

Delayed inhibition of histone H3K27ac improves SCI-induced neurological outcomes

Spinal scar formation plays a dual role in postinjury neural recovery. Although scar tissue provides essential structural support for axonal regrowth and remyelination through tissue reconstruction, excessive and persistent scarring creates physical barriers that impede neural regeneration. The complex pathological microenvironment within scar tissue promotes neuronal apoptosis and inhibits remyelination. Our study demonstrated that delayed pharmacological inhibition of histone H3K27ac significantly enhanced chronic phase functional recovery after SCI, improving postural stability and hindlimb motor function (Fig. 6A–E). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed increased neuronal survival (Fig. 6F–IF: SCI + L002 vs. SCI + Veh, p = 0.0072; n = 6). Furthermore, this intervention promoted oligodendrocyte maturation and increased myelination capacity (Fig. 6G, H, IF: SCI + L002 vs. SCI + Veh, p = 0.0420; n = 6). These findings collectively suggest that targeted H3K27ac inhibition during the subacute phase represents a promising therapeutic strategy for optimizing neural repair processes after SCI.

Discussion

In contrast to the regenerative capacity of peripheral nerves, the endogenous repair mechanisms of the CNS following traumatic SCI support significantly impaired functionality [22]. Current limitations in repair of the injured spinal cord involve primarily two interrelated factors: restricted axonal regeneration in sensorimotor pathways and the formation of inhibitory microenvironments dominated by scar tissue [12,23]. Scar tissue secretes multiple regeneration-suppressing factors and physically obstructs functional neural reconnection. Scar formation has dual temporal effects on SCI recovery. Early scar formation aids structural restoration by bridging lesion sites, but excessive scarring in the nonacute phase creates a formidable barrier to axonal regrowth and remyelination, ultimately compromising functional recovery [5,24]. Two distinct scar types contribute to this process [25]: glial scars demonstrate paradoxical functions: establishing protective barriers against inflammatory spread and stimulating axonal regeneration while simultaneously releasing potent inhibitors [26]. Fibrotic scars provide mechanical stabilization and fill the lesion cavity, but persistent inflammation and fibrotic remodeling in these scars create additional regenerative barriers [27,28]. Notably, compared with mammals, zebrafish exhibit superior neural regeneration after SCI(10 [29]). This divergence stems from the formation of permissive bridges by zebrafish glial cells for regrowing axons, in contrast to mammalian scars, which impede repair. These findings suggest that strategic modulation of excessive scar deposition, particularly by controlling the timing of scar formation and scar composition, represents a crucial therapeutic target for enhancing neural regeneration and functional recovery following SCI. In this study, a crush injury model was established to explore the abovementioned mechanisms. The Dumont forceps crush model was chosen for the following reasons: 1. This model allows the quantification of injury severity by precisely adjusting the forceps gap to meet the experimental requirements. Previous studies have confirmed that this model induces homogeneous functional deficits, which reduces experimental errors and is consistent with the requirement for model stability in our study. 2. Compared with that in transection models, the injury mechanism in this model is more consistent with clinical closed spinal cord injury, making the results more clinically translatable [5,11,21].

Following SCI, the myeloid cell population in the lesion area primarily comprises macrophages and especially microglia during the nonacute period. Persistent activation of these microglia/macrophages strongly regulates scar formation. Like zebrafish, neonatal mice demonstrate scarless healing post-SCI, enabling axonal regrowth across lesion sites [9,30]. This remarkable regenerative capacity stems from unique neonatal microglial properties. Experimental evidence has revealed that microglial depletion disrupts healing processes and impedes axonal regeneration in neonates. In adult mice, nonspecific depletion of microglia also increases the volume of injury lesions [31]. When transiently activated, neonatal microglia facilitate spinal cord reconnection by secreting functional proteins such as fibronectin and organizing the extracellular matrix. Notably, these cells subsequently exhibit accelerated inflammation resolution. These two essential features constitute the basis of scar-free repair. Unlike their adult counterparts, which remain perpetually activated, neonatal microglia rapidly revert to homeostasis after initial activation. This precise temporal control of microglial activity has been proven to be critical for scarless healing [32,33]. The CTGF paradigm illustrates this principle: transient CTGF expression mediates bridging repair in zebrafish, whereas sustained CTGF expression in adult rats promotes fibrotic scarring [34]. We also discovered that nonacute-phase myeloid cells maintain persistent activation through cholesterol accumulation and extensive crosstalk with fibroblasts/astrocytes, ultimately driving excessive scar formation.

When the CNS sustains trauma, microglia rapidly initiate a multiphase response characterized by transcriptional reprogramming [35,36]. This activation drives significant functional alterations in factors including phagocytic capacity, lysosomal activity, oxidative stress responses, and metabolic shifts in glucose or lipid processing. Epigenetic modifications serve as crucial regulatory mechanisms for gene expression adaptation, particularly in immune cells responding to microbial challenges, acute injury, and various stimuli. Histone modifications are essential for regulating transcriptional programs during microglial/macrophage activation [20,37]. Notably, Vini et al. demonstrated that altered histone acetylation patterns specifically modulate cholesterol metabolism-related genes in immune cells [19]. This epigenetic modulation directly affects phagocytic capacity, thereby impairing remyelination in aging mice with demyelinating injuries. This activated subpopulation, characterized by the downregulation of homeostatic genes, has also been termed disease-associated microglia (DAMs). Notably, dysregulated cholesterol metabolism has emerged as a hallmark feature of DAMs [38,39]. This study also demonstrated that significant alterations in cholesterol metabolism occur within microglia following spinal cord injury and can persist into the chronic phase.

Notably, DAMs exhibit remarkable heterogeneity across individuals, disease types, and disease stages. To investigate DAM dynamics, Lan et al. developed an Spp1 fate-mapping model to track activated DAM populations [40]. Their findings revealed striking age-dependent differences: neonatal mice demonstrated rapid DAM plasticity, with SPP1+ cells reverting to a homeostatic state postinjury, whereas their adult counterparts showed persistent SPP1+ DAM populations that could be eliminated only through gradual cell death during recovery. SPP1, a highly phosphorylated secreted glycoprotein, mediates cellular processes through interactions with integrins and CD44 receptors. Experimental evidence has demonstrated that SPP1 drives fibroblast activation, directly contributing to fibrosis in multiple diseases [41,42,43]. In SCI pathophysiology, the expression of myeloid-derived SPP1 is sustained, peaking acutely postinjury and remaining elevated throughout the chronic phase. This sustained expression pattern contrasts sharply with the transient increase in SPP1 expression observed in neonatal SCI models.

Previous studies have demonstrated that abnormal cholesterol accumulation drives excessive scar tissue formation. Our findings further revealed that histone acetylation at H3K27 regulates alterations in cholesterol metabolism in microglia/macrophages. Such chronic myeloid activation ultimately promotes pathological scar formation and impedes neurological recovery. However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, although microglia/macrophages exhibit the most pronounced increase in H3K27ac levels post-SCI, other cell types also exhibit slight changes in histone acetylation. Future investigations should identify key regulators that specifically control H3K27ac dynamics in myeloid cells and use transgenic models to selectively manipulate myeloid H3K27ac levels, thereby strengthening the causal evidence between myeloid-specific epigenetic changes and scar pathogenesis. Second, the functional distinction between microglia and macrophages in scar formation remains unclear. This gap in knowledge could be resolved using lineage-tracing transgenic models or pharmacological depletion approaches to comparatively analyze their respective contributions. Third, while both glial and fibrotic scars constitute an inhibitory barrier for neural regeneration, emerging evidence suggests that glial scars may paradoxically provide structural support for neural repair. Therefore, the differential effects of persistent myeloid activation on these scar subtypes require investigation, along with the development of distinct therapeutic strategies targeting each scar type.

In conclusion, this study involved comparative transcriptomic analysis of spinal cord tissues following SCI. Our findings demonstrated that the sustained activation of myeloid cells correlates with scar formation. Through integrated single-cell RNA sequencing, single-cell ATAC sequencing and ChIP‒qPCR analyses, we identified the epigenetic regulation of alterations in cholesterol metabolism in microglia. Specifically, histone H3K27 acetylation significantly increases after SCI and persists into the chronic phase. Pharmacological inhibition of H3K27ac using L002 effectively reduces cholesterol accumulation, attenuates proinflammatory responses in myeloid cells, downregulates SPP1 expression, and modulates scar formation. While the epigenetic landscape following SCI requires further investigation, our findings establish H3K27ac modulation as a promising therapeutic strategy to mitigate excessive scarring and improve neurological outcomes after SCI.
