Health and economic gains from bariatric surgery in Portugal: an in-depth analysis using a Markov model
Authors: Pedro Cardoso, Patrícia Redondo, Pedro Castro, Joana Sousa, Joana Oliveira Fagundes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity is a chronic condition with significant health and economic impact worldwide. While conventional treatments, including dietary changes, lifestyle modifications, and non-targeted pharmacotherapy, are widely used, their long-term effectiveness in sustaining weight loss remains limited. Metabolic and bariatric surgery has shown superior clinical benefits, including comorbidity remission and increased life expectancy, but its initial cost raises concerns about its economic viability compared to non-surgical treatments. Therefore, this study assesses the long-term cost-effectiveness of bariatric surgery versus conventional treatment, considering health outcomes and economic impact within the Portuguese healthcare.MethodsThe patient journey was mapped based on clinical guidelines and adapted to the Portuguese context via stakeholder interviews. Obesity prevalence and associated comorbidities were estimated using epidemiological data. A Markov model was created to simulate obesity progression over ten, twenty years, and lifetime horizons, using monthly cycles. The model accounts for weight regain, complications, and different health states, including obesity with diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, stroke, cancer, and death. It was implemented in R Studio using heemod package. Direct healthcare costs and health outcomes were calculated. A probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA), with 500 iterations, was conducted to assess uncertainty.ResultsThe results demonstrate that bariatric surgery, compared to conventional treatment, enhances life expectancy, quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), and reduces obesity-related comorbidities. Over 10 years, surgery yields 6 QALYs to each patient costing 9243€, compared to 4.9 QALYs and a cost of 6328€ for the non-surgical arm. The cost-effectiveness analysis shows that, despite higher initial costs, bariatric surgery is cost-effective over time. The incremental cost per QALY gained is estimated at 2756€, positioning bariatric surgery as an economically advantageous intervention at commonly accepted willingness-to-pat thresholds (WTP). After 20 years, this strategy emerges as dominant, improving health outcomes while reducing overall costs. On the lifetime horizon, surgical patients gain 7 additional QALYs and cost 4534€ less than non-surgical patients. PSA shows that the cost-effectiveness of this strategy is maintained.ConclusionThe analysis suggests that surgery is an effective strategy to improve outcomes and managing expenses. Future research should refine the model exploring upcoming treatment strategies as additional evidence on its outcomes becomes available.Keywords: Metabolic and bariatric surgery, Cost-effectiveness, Markov model, Economics modelling

Background

Obesity is a chronic complex disease caused by an excess of body fat leading to health impairment [1]. In the last years, obesity has rapidly evolved from a minor public health concern into a global pandemic. An estimated 890 million people worldwide are living with obesity, and, in 2019, an estimated 5 million deaths were attributed to noncommunicable diseases caused by overweight and obesity [1, 2]. Obesity and overweight are associated with several diseases, including cardiovascular disease (CVD), type 2 diabetes, cancer, osteoarthritis, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), chronic kidney disease (CKD) and sleep apnea. The economic impact of obesity encompasses not only the direct costs associated with prevention, treatment, and management but also significant indirect costs. These include productivity losses due to absenteeism, premature retirement, and early mortality. According to the World Obesity Federation (WOF), the total cost of obesity is projected to reach US$ 4 trillion annually by 2035 [3]. The financial burden of obesity is considerable; according to the WOF [4], the economic impact of overweight and obesity in Portugal was estimated at 4.03€ billion in 2019. This figure includes 1.50€ billion in direct costs and 2.54€ billion in indirect costs, translating to 394€ per capita and accounting for 1.84% of the nation’s GDP [4]. Recognizing the economic burden of obesity has led to an increased focus on the cost-effectiveness of the various interventions available. As such, a Portuguese study, conducted in 2013, performed a cost-effectiveness analysis comparing two types of bariatric surgery—gastric banding and gastric bypass—alongside a non-surgical treatment option (best medical management) [5]. Utilizing a Markov model, the analysis evaluated the impact of obesity-related comorbidities on the outcomes of these treatments, and included a representative population of severe obese patients. Subgroup evaluations were based on the presence of comorbidities, diabetes, age, and body mass index. The results showed that gastric bypass emerged as the most effective strategy, demonstrating a dominant position with significant savings in lifetime costs and an increase in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). Specifically, gastric bypass provided an additional 1.9 QALYs and saved an average of 13,244€ per patient, over a lifetime, compared to best medical management for individuals with a body mass index over 35 [5]. The greatest benefits were observed in younger patients, those with a body mass index (BMI) between 40 and 50, and patients without obesity-related diseases [5]. In 2017, two other European studies analyzed the cost-effectiveness of bariatric surgery compared to medical management. An Italian study [6] revealed that bariatric surgery resulted in an increase in costs of 2661€ while generating an additional 1.1 QALYs over 10 years, leading to an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of 2412€ per QALY [6]. Over a lifetime, bariatric surgery led to savings of 8649€, an increase of 0.5 life years, and 3.2 additional QALYs, meaning it was found to be dominant over conservative management in the long term [6]. Similarly, a Spanish study [7] compared bariatric surgery with conservative management for treating severe obesity. The Markov model analysis revealed that, over a 10-year period, bariatric surgery resulted in a cost increase of 9386€ while providing an additional 1.6 QALY, resulting in an ICER of 5966€ per QALY [7]. However, over a lifetime, bariatric surgery led to an increase of 0.6 life years and 4.4 QALYs, with an average cost savings of 300€ per patient [7].

Given the increasing burden of obesity, both in terms of healthcare costs and complications, it is essential to evaluate treatment options not only in terms of clinical effectiveness but also economic sustainability. While bariatric surgery has demonstrated superior clinical outcomes compared to conventional treatment, its initial costs raise concerns about economic viability. Therefore, it is essential to assess whether the long-term benefits of surgery—such as improved quality of life and reduced healthcare utilization—justify its higher upfront investment. Despite the already existing corpus of evidence, data applicable to Portugal is still limited and now significantly outdated. Furthermore, existing models often fail to account for weight regain mechanisms, which are becoming increasingly relevant in the management of post-surgical patients [8]. This study evaluates the cost-effectiveness of bariatric surgery versus conventional treatment in Portugal, from a healthcare system perspective, by analyzing direct healthcare costs and QALYs over different time horizons by using a Markov model.

Methods

To evaluate the economic impact of obesity management in Portugal, the patient journey based on established clinical guidelines and real-world practice was mapped. This process involved conducting semi-structured in-depth interviews with seven medical experts, including surgeons, endocrinologists, and general practitioners, all with extensive experience in obesity management. Experts were selected to ensure representation of different treatment strategies across the full continuum of care. After individual interviews, responses were consolidated, and a consensus was achieved through iterative discussion rounds, where discrepancies were resolved by majority agreement among the experts. The journey was structured to include different stages, from initial diagnosis to long-term disease management.

Following the patient journey mapping, direct cost data related to obesity management was gathered, including medical and non-medical appointments, hospitalizations, exams, and specialized treatments. These costs were estimated using national health expenditure data and a micro-costing approach, which considers the utilization and unit costs of each healthcare resource. Hospitalization costs were specific to bariatric surgery, while medical consultations covered general practitioners, specialists, and nutritionists. Emergency visits were excluded from the analysis due to the lack of available information about the underlying diagnosis, making it impossible to directly associate the visits with obesity.

A cost-effectiveness analysis was conducted to allow the comparison of different treatment strategies in terms of both costs and benefits, providing a comprehensive view that informs both clinical and policy decisions. This type of analysis is particularly relevant when considering the high upfront cost of options such as bariatric surgery, which may be offset by long-term health benefits. To thoroughly assess these costs and health outcomes, a Markov model was employed utilizing the gathered cost data. The model allows for the projection of costs and health outcomes over extended time horizons, which is essential to determine whether the long-term benefits of interventions such as bariatric surgery outweigh their initial costs, accounting for factors such as comorbidity remission and increased life expectancy.

In this study, the Markov model was developed with specific health states, namely obesity without comorbidities, obesity with diabetes, surgical complications, stroke, cardiovascular diseases, cancer and death. Transitions between these states were based on probabilities obtained from epidemiological data and clinical studies, and time cycles were defined as monthly periods to simulate disease progression over a decade. Two different strategies were included: the first is bariatric surgery, which encompasses both gastric sleeve and gastric bypass procedures, along with the diagnosis process and a follow-up period of 3 years post-surgery, as this is the recommended follow-up according to obesity treatment national guidelines. The second strategy is standard non-surgical treatment, which includes the diagnosis process, behavioral approaches, diet, and drugs (excluding novel GLP-1 agonists, except for diabetic patients as these drugs are not reimbursed for obese patients in Portugal, only for diabetic), followed by a 3-year follow-up. This approach enabled the evaluation of the health impacts of obesity over time and provided estimates of future costs associated with different disease progression trajectories.

Model type/structure

The time-varying cohort multistate Markov model was designed to simulate disease progression over a forty-year period for a cohort of 1000 patients (Fig. 1). The model incorporates key health states associated with obesity and employs transition probabilities derived from national health surveys and longitudinal studies. This approach facilitates a comprehensive analysis of how obesity and its related conditions evolve over time, including the potential effects of an increase in the number of surgeries compared to standard multidisciplinary and behavioral approaches.

Transition probabilities

The evidence supporting this analysis is built on a robust foundation of 55 unique references, of which 45% consist of systematic reviews or meta-analyses. A significant portion of the evidence is of high quality, with 55% of studies receiving a Grade A classification, and 70% falling under Level I of recommendation. The GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations) system is a framework used to assess the quality of scientific evidence and the strength of recommendations in clinical guidelines [9]. Moreover, it includes 11 studies from Portugal, adding for local relevance. The credibility of the corpus of evidence used is supported by the fact that the authors of the included studies have an average H-index of 38, indicating a strong academic impact. All transition probabilities were adjusted to monthly periods.

Country and perspective

Model inputs were collected to reflect the Portuguese context, including transition probabilities, utilities, and costs. Prevalence data was gathered to build the base cohort, and expert input was used when necessary. This model assumes the perspective of the national healthcare system (payer).

Cycle length and time horizon

The model simulation was built using monthly cycles to better represent acute events such as complications and stroke. The base scenario uses a 10-year time horizon (120 cycles), with two additional scenarios: one spanning 20 years (240 cycles) and a lifetime scenario of 40 years (480 cycles). The 40-year scenario approximates life expectancy from age 45 in Portugal, which is currently about 38 additional years [45].

Pre-defined scenarios and subgroup analysis

Pre-defined scenarios and subgroup analysis are crucial for gaining a comprehensive understanding of how different variables influence the cost-effectiveness of interventions across diverse populations. Utilizing pre-defined scenarios allows for a systematic exploration of a range of plausible conditions, such as varying population demographics or model base parameters, which helps to assess the robustness of the results. Subgroup analysis further refines these findings by identifying how specific groups might benefit differently, thereby supporting more targeted and equitable decision-making in healthcare resource allocation.

In this study, three different scenarios were created for different time horizons: 10 years, 20 years, and a lifetime (up to 40 years). Additionally, two scenarios were developed for the subgroup of patients with BMI ≥ 40 kg/m², considering both 10-year and lifetime time horizons.

Assumptions for effects and utilities

For the cost effectiveness-analysis, a thorough desk research was performed, focusing on the base utilities for the Portuguese obese population. The model primarily focuses on the reduction in absolute BMI units from baseline, beginning after the first cycle (1 month) and continuing to the lowest recorded level, which occurs after 1 year. After that point, a monthly weight regain mechanism is incorporated for surgical interventions, using both the absolute amount of weight regained and the percentage of patients who regain weight post-surgery to define a monthly disutility impact associated with weight regain. This mechanism is active for a period of 10 years. Quality of life (QoL) impacts are incorporated through disutility values obtained from the United Kingdom (UK) utility catalog [46], while base utilities are adjusted for age, sex, and diabetes within the Portuguese population. Additionally, age-related disutility is applied monthly. Post-surgical recovery is considered as a 30-day disutility period. This comprehensive approach aims to account for both the health benefits and the recovery phases of weight-loss interventions. A discount rate of 4% was applied to utilities, as defined in the national economic evaluation guidelines [47]. The reference values used for effects are listed on Tables 5 and 6.

Software

The collected corpus of evidence and all the input parameters were structured using Microsoft Excel. Model was developed and deployed using R (R Foundation, V 4.4.0) and the health economics modelling package Heemod (Kevin Zarca, V 1.0.1.9). The compilation and coding were performed using Microsoft VSCode.

Sensitivity analysis

To account for uncertainties in the model parameters and to assess the robustness of the results, a comprehensive probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) was incorporated into the study, with 500 iterations. This analysis is crucial because it allows for the exploration of how variability in key parameters—such as transition probabilities, costs, and utility values—can influence the overall outcomes of the model. Transition probabilities were modelled using beta and log normal distributions, utilities using beta distributions, base parameters using log normal and normal distributions and cost drivers using gamma distribution. The minimum and maximum values derived for utilities were estimated by varying the considered value by 10%, and the expert inputs defined the range for cost drivers, allowing the calculation of distribution parameters. By systematically varying these parameters across plausible ranges based on their respective distributions, the PSA generates a spectrum of potential scenarios rather than relying on a single deterministic outcome. This approach not only highlights the most influential parameters but also provides confidence intervals around the model’s predictions, offering a clearer understanding of the uncertainty inherent in the model. Consequently, the results derived from the PSA enhance the credibility and robustness of the findings, ensuring that they are not overly sensitive to any single assumption or estimate. This deeper level of analysis supports better decision-making by illustrating the range of possible outcomes and the likelihood of different scenarios under varying conditions.

Cost-effectiveness analysis results

Bariatric surgery demonstrates clear cost-effectiveness and dominance over non-surgical therapy in both 20-year and lifetime timeframes for the general population. Over 20 years, bariatric surgery emerges as the dominant strategy, delivering better health outcomes while costing less. Specifically, surgery costs 13,559€ compared to 13,800€ for non-surgical management, resulting in a cost saving of 240€ while providing an additional 3.076 QALYs. This is even more pronounced over a lifetime (40 years), as for the general population, bariatric surgery not only saves 4534€ compared to non-surgical therapy, but also provides a significant gain of 7.458 QALYs. For individuals with BMI ≥ 40 kg/m², bariatric surgery remains dominant over a lifetime, yielding 6.821 additional QALYs while saving 4534€ compared to non-surgical therapy. These results indicate that bariatric surgery offers both cost savings and substantial long-term health benefits, solidifying its role as the superior intervention over time. Even in shorter-term timeframes, where bariatric surgery is not dominant, such as the 10-year horizon, it remains highly cost-effective (compared to common WTP thresholds), as it costs 2914€ more than medical management but delivers an additional 1.057 QALYs, resulting in an ICER of 2756€ per QALY. Similarly, for the BMI ≥ 40 kg/m² group, over 10 years, surgery provides 0.96 more QALYs at an ICER of 3049€ per QALY, highlighting its value even when it doesn’t lead to immediate cost savings. In addition to cost savings, bariatric surgery leads to significant health improvements.

The state occupancy distribution (Fig. 2) for the base scenario clearly demonstrates the impact of bariatric surgery on both comorbidities and health outcomes. The expected burden, measured by the time spent in a given health state and the number of entries into that state, is significantly reduced with bariatric surgery compared to a non-surgical approach. Specifically, comparing the bariatric surgery strategy with a non-surgical approach shows an expected burden reduction of 8% in stroke, 69% in diabetes, 59% in cardiovascular disease, and 25% in cancer, underscoring the positive effects beyond the ICER value. Given the relatively young age of the obese population, with an average life expectancy of 36 years, the long-term benefits of bariatric surgery make it a highly viable and life-enhancing option for managing obesity.

State occupancy - base scenario

The probabilistic sensitivity analysis included 91 parameters and was conducted with 500 iterations (Fig. 3). The final values after the PSA further enhance the cost-effectiveness of bariatric surgery, showing an average incremental cost of 845€, a benefit of 2.73 QALYs, and an ICER per QALY of just 309€. Additionally, 96% of all iterations remain either cost-effective or dominant, reinforcing the conclusions established in the base scenario.

PSA - 52 parameters (500 iterations)

The parameter-specific analysis of PSA results shows that most included parameters have little impact on QALY outcomes (Fig. 4). The main exception is the transition probability between ‘Without Comorbidity’ and ‘Diabetes’ in the non-surgical arm, which accounts for a 22% variance in total utilities. This is due to uncertainty caused by limited evidence in studies on non-diabetic patients undergoing bariatric surgery. Additionally, the utility value associated with each unit of BMI reduction has a high impact, with a 12% variance. As with the previous case, evidence linking specific utility values to weight reduction is scarce and often relies on limited cost-effectiveness studies or single-center retrospective studies, leading to high variability.

Conclusion

The Markov model used in this study provides valuable insights into the progression of obesity and its comorbidities, enabling accurate predictions of future costs and assessments of the cost-effectiveness of treatment strategies. Metabolic and bariatric surgery, compared to non-surgical therapy, is a dominant strategy from a 20-year horizon to a lifetime. Surgery patients gained 7.458 additional QALYs and saved 4534€ in costs over a 40-year period, with total costs amounting to 19,994€, providing 36 years of life and 18 QALYs. While bariatric surgery is not dominant in the 10-year horizon, it remains cost-effective, with an ICER of 2756€ per QALY for the overall population and 3049€ per QALY for patients with a BMI ≥ 40 kg/m². Metabolic and bariatric surgery has also shown to significantly reduce the burden of obesity associated conditions such as stroke, diabetes, cancer, and cardiovascular disease. These findings reinforce that, despite its higher initial cost, bariatric surgery rapidly becomes cost-effective and ultimately leads to long-term savings.

Given the rising prevalence of obesity and its substantial economic burden, integrating bariatric surgery as part of a broader obesity management strategy could improve the sustainability of the healthcare system. By reducing the incidence of obesity-related diseases such as stroke, diabetes, cancer, and cardiovascular disease, this intervention has the potential to alleviate pressures on healthcare resources while enhancing patient outcomes. However, access to bariatric surgery in Portugal remains limited due to long waiting lists in the public sector and insufficient funding in both public and private healthcare sectors. Addressing these barriers is crucial to ensuring that cost-effective interventions reach those who need them, ultimately supporting a more sustainable and efficient healthcare system.

The Markov model, while robust, has inherent limitations. Transition probabilities derived from desk research are not BMI-adjusted, potentially underestimating benefits for patients with severe obesity. The model does not account for treatment failures, reinterventions due to treatment failure, or strategy crossovers, and simplifying the acute and chronic dynamics of comorbidities, such as cardiovascular diseases, poses challenges. Additionally, a societal perspective accounting for absenteeism costs, years lost due to disability, and productivity losses is lacking, which would likely further support the cost-effectiveness of the surgical strategy. This is because surgery typically yields faster and larger health improvements, potentially reducing work absence and disability sooner and more substantially. It employs a ten-year time horizon with monthly cycles and a 4% discount rate, which may not fully capture long-term changes in health policies, therapeutic adherence, or therapeutic strategy after weight regain. While scenario and probabilistic sensitivity analyses address parameter uncertainty, data variability may still affect result precision. Despite these limitations, the model remains a valuable tool for informing health policy and resource allocation decisions.
