Chemical Exposure Assessment in Traditional Korean Pottery Workplaces
Authors: Jongsu Kim, Sungyo Jung, Taehong Kwon, Gitaek Oh, Boowook Kim, Chungsik Yoon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study assessed occupational exposure to airborne hazardous substances—including total particulate matter (TPM), respirable suspended particles (RSPs), respirable crystalline silica (RCS), carbon monoxide (CO), and total volatile organic compounds (TVOCs)—in traditional Korean pottery workplaces.MethodsMeasurements were conducted in three representative settings: a small studio, a college facility, and an outdoor climbing kiln. Task-based sampling was performed for TPM, RSP, and RCS, while real-time monitoring captured temporal variations in nanoparticles, CO, and TVOCs.ResultsTPM and RSP levels did not exceed the American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists (ACGIH) threshold-limit values (TLVs), but RCS exceeded the TLV of 25 μg/m3 in 5 out of 96 samples (5.2%), all during shaping or trimming in the pottery wheel room of the college workplace. Nanoparticles accounted for 78.9 ± 10.2% of the total particle number concentrations (PNCs), with high emissions during active kiln operation. CO and TVOC concentrations peaked during bisque and glaze firings, especially in the climbing kiln, although CO did not exceed the TLV of 25 ppm. Kiln type and task type significantly influenced particulate exposure levels, with pottery wheeling and climbing kiln use showing the highest concentrations. Peephole closure during firing and the use of exhaust ventilation in spray booths notably reduced emissions.ConclusionThese findings highlight the need for targeted control strategies, particularly in indoor studios and shaping/trimming areas, to reduce exposure to fine particles and gases in artisanal pottery production.

Introduction

Pottery is a decorative craft involving the creation of items such as tableware, bricks, and ornamental wares by shaping nonmetallic, inorganic materials like clay and glaze, and then firing them at high temperatures in a kiln. According to Republic of Korea 's 2023 Ceramic Census, there were 1,683 pottery businesses or studios employing 3,815 workers. Over 95% of these were small-scale operations with fewer than 10 employees (49% had 1 worker, 31% had 2, 13.2% had 3–4, 4.5% had 5–9, and only 2.3% had more than 10) [1].

The pottery production process typically includes raw clay preparation (grinding, mixing, and wedging), shaping and trimming, drying, glazing, firing, post-treatment, and packaging. Once shaped and trimmed, the clay is dried to remove moisture and strengthen the form. The dried clay, or greenware, undergoes bisque firing at around 750–850°C to reduce water absorption and prepare it for glazing. After cooling, the bisque ware is glazed and then glaze-fired at 1,200–1,400°C. The glaze—a mix of materials like kaolin, feldspar, zinc oxides, and glycerin—adds luster, color, and durability. Glazing helps apply a thin layer on ceramic surfaces to express luster and patterns and increase the ceramics' strength.

Despite its artistic appeal, pottery work poses several health risks. Workers are exposed to hazardous substances such as respirable crystalline silica (RCS), heavy metal dust, nanoparticles, carbon monoxide (CO), sulfur dioxide (SO2), and nitrogen dioxide (NO2), which can cause serious occupational diseases [[2], [3], [4]]. Occupational diseases, including pneumoconiosis and silicosis, which frequently occur in ceramic manufacturing workers, are closely related to RCS [5,6]. RCS exposure is strongly linked to pneumoconiosis and silicosis and is classified as a group 1 human carcinogen due to its association with lung cancer [7].

Although there are published guidelines and educational materials on ceramic art and its hazards [8], scientific studies assessing actual exposure levels remain limited. This study aimed to evaluate the emission of particulate matter—including nanoparticles and RCS—as well as CO and total volatile organic compounds (TVOCs) from different kiln types in traditional Korean pottery workplaces.

Materials and methods

Workplace description

Measurements were conducted at three pottery workplaces in the Republic of Korea: a small studio (A), college pottery studios (B), and an outdoor climbing kiln (C). Workplaces A and B covered the full pottery-making process, while Workplace C focused only on glaze firing of bisque ware previously fired in electric kilns. Details of each site are provided in Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Fig. S1.

Workplace A is a 19 m2 studio in a downtown area. It includes a pottery wheel and glaze dipping in the main room, separated by sliding doors from a kiln room containing an electric oxidation kiln.

Workplace B is part of a fine arts college in Seoul. Measurements were taken in: (1) a wheel studio with many pottery wheels, (2) a glazing room for preparing and applying glaze, (3) a kiln room with various electric kilns, and (4) a private studio for graduate students.

In both Workplaces A and B, general ventilation was available through windows and doors; however, these were mostly kept closed during winter, potentially allowing minor leakage, which was not thoroughly assessed. In Workplace B, local exhaust ventilation in the glaze spraying room consisted of four spray booths connected to a single duct. In the private studio, a ceiling blower provided a gentle downward airflow to facilitate the rapid settling of ceramic dust generated during trimming. Workplace C is an outdoor climbing kiln built with fire bricks and refractory clay, inclined at 15°, and consisting of seven arch-shaped chambers and a chimney. During the early to mid-firing phase (9–10 hours), firewood was fed into the bottom chamber to preheat it to around 900°C. Additional wood was added through stoke holes in each chamber wall until temperatures reached 1,200–1,400°C.

Sampling and evaluating methods

Sampling design

The evaluation of airborne hazardous substances was conducted from late December 2021 to early April 2022, during which most windows remained closed and the entrance doors connected to the outside were generally kept shut except during entry or exit. The target hazardous substances included particulate matter and gaseous compounds such as CO and TVOCs. Particulate matter was collected using sample pumps with filters to measure total particulate matter (TPM), respirable particulates (RSP), and RCS, through both personal and area samples. In addition, a real-time aerosol monitor was used to assess temporal variations in particulate matter concentrations. Gaseous substances were also measured using a real-time aerosol monitor.

Pottery manufacturing, as an artistic activity, is characterized by irregularities in the number of workers, work patterns, and working hours compared to conventional manufacturing workplaces. Accordingly, we first identified the work patterns through a preliminary survey and then focused measurements on periods when specific tasks were being performed. As a result, measurements were conducted over multiple days with varying durations. For example, in workshop A, measurements were conducted over a total of six days. Four personal samples were collected across three days. Each sample was collected during glazing, shaping, trimming, or during the continuous sequence of shaping and trimming. Pottery wheeling samples were collected over three days (four samples); glaze dipping samples were collected over two days (three samples); and bisque firing and glaze firing were each measured over two days.

In workshop B, a total of eleven personal samples were collected over four days from three workers who agreed to participate in the study and whose tasks mainly involved shaping or trimming with a knife in the pottery wheel room. Area samples were collected over five days, mainly during periods when work was actively being carried out in the respective locations. Although the kiln room in Workplace B was large (187 m2), no personal samples were collected because the worker only briefly entered the room occasionally to check the kiln's operation, resulting in negligible exposure during the investigation period. In Workshop C, all measurements were completed within a single day. Personal samples were collected when four workers were loading pine firewood into the main chamber of the climbing kiln.

TPM, RSP, and RCS samples measured with filters were analyzed individually without time-weighted averaging, as interpreting time-weighted averages was challenging due to irregular working hours and significant deviations in sampling durations from the standard 8-hour work shift. Area samples were measured from 0.8 m to 1.5 m from the floor depending on the working posture (standing or sitting). Except for Workplace C's main chamber, all area samples were measured within a 1 m radius of the working place or emission source. In Workplace C's main chamber, area samples were measured about 1.5 m away from the stoke hole because there was a risk of hitting a pump, and firewood had to be piled up.

While the filter samples were collected exclusively during the periods when workers were actively performing tasks, the real-time instruments measured over the entire duration of each operation. Specifically, for the kiln, measurements were conducted continuously from the start to the end of its operation, irrespective of the worker's presence in the area. In Workplace C, which has an inclined kiln, CO and TVOC were measured by sequentially relocating the real-time instruments to different levels—referred to as the first, second, and third floors—corresponding to multiple observation ports along the long kiln. Although these are not actual building floors, the continuous slope was divided into floors for convenience in measurement and description (Fig. S1). To assess particle size distribution during pottery processes, a Scanning Mobility Particle Sizer (NanoScan SMPS 3910, TSI, USA; 10–420 nm) was used alongside an Optical Particle Sizer (OPS 3330, TSI, USA; 0.3–10 μm). SMPS and OPS operated at 0.8 L/min and 1 L/min flow rates, respectively, with 1-minute sampling intervals. A diffusion drier was connected to the SMPS to control humidity. Particle number concentrations (PNCs) were calculated by midpoint diameter (Dp) using Aerosol Instrument Manager (AIM 9.0) and Multi-Instrument Manager (MIM 3.0, TSI, USA).

CO and TVOC emitted during kiln firing were measured using an Indoor Air Quality Meter (IAQ-Calc 7545, TSI, USA) with a resolution of 0.1 ppm and an accuracy of ±3% of the reading, and a photoionization detector (ppbRAE 3000, RAE Systems, Denmark) with a resolution of 1 ppb and an accuracy of ±3%. Each instrument was zero-calibrated prior to use according to the respective manual instructions, and the ppbRAE 3000 was additionally accuracy-calibrated using 10 ppm isobutylene. Devices were placed at the same locations as the filter samplers, with 1-minute sampling intervals. In the climbing kiln, measurements began on the first floor and continued intermittently, moving the instruments upward floor by floor—from the second-floor stoke hole to near the top-floor stoke hole (within 1 m)—following worker activity.

The number of personal and area samples, as well as their sampling durations, are summarized in the results presented in Table 1. The Seoul National University Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved our research ethics, as it includes personal sampling data from the potters (IRB No. 2202/001-009).

Evaluating for TPM, RSP, and RCS

TPM, RSP, and RCS measurements were conducted following the National Institute of Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) Manual of Analytical Methods (NMAM 0500, 0600, and 7602) [[9], [10], [11]]. For TPM, a 37 mm polyvinyl chloride (PVC) filter (5 μm pore size, SKC Inc., USA) was placed in a 3-piece cassette (SKC 225-2LF, SKC Inc., USA) and connected to an air sampling pump (KMS-5100, KM Specialty Pumps & Systems, Inc. USA or Escort Elf, Zefon International) with a flowrate of 2 L/min. RSP was measured using an aluminum cyclone (SKC 225-2-1, SKC Inc., USA) with a 37 mm PVC filter and a GilAir Plus pump (Sensidyne LP, USA) set at 2.5 L/min. After sampling, filters were conditioned in a temperature- and humidity-controlled room for over 24 hours, then weighed three times using an electronic balance (Mettler XP6, Mettler-Toledo, USA) with a readability of 0.001 mg and a repeatability of 0.0008 mg; the measurements were then averaged. Pump flow rates were calibrated with a flow calibrator (Defender 520-M, Mesa Labs., USA) before and after use.

For RCS analysis, RSP-loaded filters were ashed in porcelain crucibles at 650°C for 2 hours. Each sample was mixed with 200 mg of KBr, ground, and pressed into 13 mm pellets using a lab press. Pellets were analyzed with Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR CARY630, Agilent Technologies, USA) at 800 cm−1. A calibration curve was prepared using certified NIST-SRM1878a samples (2.5–50 μg) with a correlation coefficient (r) of 0.998. The limit of detection (LOD), based on three times the standard deviation of seven blank replicates, was 0.133 μg/sample. This value was divided by the average air sampling volume (0.47 m3), which was calculated by multiplying the mean sampling time (204 ± 52 minutes) and the mean flow rate (2.3 ± 0.2 L/min) of both personal and area samples, to determine the air concentration LOD of 0.28 μg/m3. Values below this LOD were substituted with LOD/√2, corresponding to 0.20 μg/m3 [12].

Background TPM, RSP, and RCS samples were collected concurrently with area sampling, with details on sampling durations and numbers provided in Table 1. To minimize external influences, background samples were taken at least 3 m from the workplace, on sides without windows or doors, and at a height of 1.5 m. In Workplace A (urban), vehicular impact was minimized; Workplace B (university campus) had minimal external sources; and in Workplace C (mountainside), background samples were collected 30 m down-slope from the kiln, considering wind direction to avoid smoke plume interference.

Statistical methods

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the data distribution. Concentration values of TPM, RSP, and RCS were categorized by measurement location (Workplaces A, B, and C) and summarized using the geometric mean (GM), geometric standard deviation (GSD), and range (min–max) when the data followed a log-normal distribution. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare the GM concentrations between personal and area samples that did not follow a log-normal distribution.

To compare exposure levels across the five different pottery processes (bisque firing, glaze firing, pottery wheeling, glaze dipping, and glaze spraying) and kiln types (oxidation kiln, reduction kiln, and climbing kiln), we first conducted a Kruskal–Wallis H test, a nonparametric alternative to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Upon finding a statistically significant result (p < 0.05), we performed nonparametric post-hoc pairwise comparisons using Dunn's test with Bonferroni correction to control for multiple comparisons. Statistical tests were conducted at a significance level of p < 0.05 (two-tailed) using R software version 4.2.0 (R Development Core Team, Vienna, Austria). R and Microsoft Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corporation, USA) were used for data organization and figure generation.

Results

TPM, RSP, and RCS concentrations between study subjects

Table 1 summarizes the airborne concentrations of TPM, RSP, and RCS across three pottery workplaces. The GM(GSD) of TPM, RSP, and RCS , except for background concentration, was 146.46 μg/m3 (2.28), 49.10 μg/m3 (2.50), and 2.21 μg/m3 (4.65), respectively. The GMs of TPM and RSP were highest in personal samples from Workplace C, which involved outdoor climbing kiln use. In contrast, RCS concentrations were most elevated in Workplace B, particularly in personal samples collected during shaping and trimming tasks in porter wheel room, with some exceeding the ACGIH TLV of 25 μg/m3. Although background levels were generally low across all sites, a considerable number of RCS samples—especially in Workplaces B and C—were below the LOD.

Fig. 1 presents the distributions of TPM, RSP, and RCS concentrations in area and personal samples across the three workplaces (A–C). While TPM and RSP concentrations were below their respective ACGIH TLVs of 10 mg/m3 and 3 mg/m3, personal exposure levels tended to be higher than area measurements, particularly in Workplaces B and C. However, these differences were not statistically significant. RCS concentrations in personal samples from Workplace B were relatively high, with some measurements exceeding the ACGIH TLV of 25 μg/m3, as indicated by the outliers and elevated medians in the RCS plot. In contrast, RCS levels in Workplaces A and C were substantially lower and remained well below the TLV in both personal and area samples. Despite these observed patterns, statistical comparisons did not reveal significant differences among the workplaces.

Fig. 2 illustrates TPM, RSP, and RCS by pottery process (top row) and kiln type (bottom row). Across processes, pottery wheeling exhibited the highest median concentrations for all three metrics (TPM, RSP, and RCS), with statistically significant differences compared to other processes (p < 0.05). Glaze firing also showed relatively high TPM and RSP concentrations, whereas glaze spraying consistently exhibited the lowest values across all metrics.

In terms of kiln type, the climbing kiln, typically used outdoors, was associated with significantly higher TPM and RSP concentrations compared to electric kilns. In contrast, RCS concentrations were highest in the electric oxidation kiln, although the difference was not statistically significant.

These findings suggest that both task type (e.g., shaping/trimming during pottery wheeling) and kiln type influence airborne particulate levels, with implications for targeted exposure control strategies.

Number concentration and size distribution of nanoparticles

Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S2 present the evolution of particle size distributions during different firing processes in various kiln types. The data, obtained by merging PNCs from SMPS and OPS using MIM software, show dynamic changes in PNCs (dN/dlogDp) over time. Among the total PNC, nano-sized particles (<100 nm) accounted for 78.9 ± 10.2% (range: 63.3–90.6%), and most of the particles generated during traditional pottery manufacturing are nanoparticles (Table S2).

During bisque and glaze firings in electric oxidation kilns (a, b), particle concentrations increased notably in the submicron range (10–100 nm), particularly during the active firing phases. In the electric reduction kiln (c), glaze firing produced elevated particle concentrations across a broader size range, with prominent spikes between 100–1000 nm. The climbing kiln (d) showed a more variable pattern with intermittent emissions, possibly due to manual stoking or uneven combustion. Overall, particle generation varied significantly by kiln type and firing stage (Fig. 3).

Table S2 shows PNCs excluding background concentrations (values measured when no work was being performed or the kiln was not operating). The PNC's with 10–10,000 nm particle size distribution of each kiln showed high averages in the order of glaze firing in electric oxidation kiln (1.14 × 105 ea/cm3), glaze firing in cilmbing kiln (2.59 × 104 ea/cm3), bisque firing in electric oxidation kiln (8.34 × 104 ea/cm3), and glaze firing in electric reduction kiln (7.14 × 104 ea/cm3). The reason for dichotomizing the same kiln into two rows is to compare the concentration of the two different conditions, as shown in Supplementary Figure S2: when the peephole plugs were closed from 200 minutes to 286 minutes from the time the kiln was activated (upper row), and when they were kept open during the same period (bottom row). Both experiments were carried out on the same kiln firing schedule on different days.

Concentrations of CO and TVOCs

Table 2 displays the CO and TVOC concentrations near three kilns. Both gaseous substances were measured when the kilns were operating, and their concentrations were sorted by kiln type's sampling time, average, and range. The CO concentration was the highest in the electric oxidation kiln (when oxidation firing), with both average and maximum concentrations of 3.55 ppm and 23.7 ppm, respectively. Conversely, the TVOC concentration was highest in the climbing kiln (2nd-top floor), with average and maximum concentrations of 5,732 ppb and 12,034 ppb, respectively.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to evaluate major hazardous agents—TPM, RSP, RCS, CO, and TVOC—in the traditional Korean pottery manufacturing industry. To achieve this, exposure assessments were conducted across three workplaces, stratified by task type.

The main findings showed that among the TPM, RSP, and RCS samples collected using filters, TPM and RSP did not exceed the ACGIH TLVs of 10 mg/m3 and 3 mg/m3, respectively. However, RCS exceeded the ACGIH TLV of 25 μg/min 5 out of 96 samples (5.2%)3 [13], all of which were related to shaping or trimming processes in Workplace B. Specifically, 4 of these 5 samples were from personal samples collected from 11 workers performing these tasks (exceeding the TLV in 36.3% of personal samples), and 1 sample was from an area sample collected at the corresponding location.

As shown in Table 1, RCS concentrations in both personal and area samples from the pottery wheel room in workshop B were relatively high, and samples exceeding the ACGIH-TLV during working hours were observed only in this workshop. Notably, the elevated personal exposures coincided with high RCS concentrations in the corresponding area samples. This pattern is reflected in the outlier shown in the RCS box plot for pottery wheeling in the upper part of Fig. 2. In particular, four personal samples (ranging from 26.2 to 65.7 μg/m3) and one area sample (46.0 μg/m3), all collected during shaping and trimming tasks in the pottery wheel room, showed elevated levels. Although the exact reason is unclear, it may be attributed to the fact that all workers in this room were university students with less than two years of experience, unlike the more experienced workers in workshops A and C, making them less proficient in handling clay. During sampling, substantial amounts of clay were observed on the floor and on their clothing. Additionally, although only three students consented to repeated measurements, many other student workers were present, potentially increasing overall concentrations. The closed windows and doors, which were opened only during entry and exit, also resulted in inadequate ventilation.

As mentioned in the Methods section, the results in Table 1 are not 8-hour time-weighted averages (TWAs) but reflect only the actual task duration, which suggests that directly applying the ACGIH TLVs may have limitations. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted considering that artistic work differs from traditional industrial work patterns. While there are established adjustment methods for cases where the working time exceeds eight hours, caution should be exercised when applying occupational exposure limits to nonroutine tasks with shorter working durations.

It is also noteworthy that when particulate matter was measured using a real-time instrument in terms of number concentration, the proportion of nanoparticles was very high, at 78.9 ± 10.2%. Given their large surface area, nanoparticles are known to pose greater health risks [14,15]. CO and TVOC concentrations were measured using real-time instruments; although CO did not exceed the ACGIH TLV of 25 ppm, it reached up to 23.7 ppm momentarily at Workplace C. While no TLV has been established for TVOC, a maximum concentration of 12 ppm was measured at Workplace C, which is an outdoor site. Despite being outdoors, Workplace C exhibited higher levels of TPM, RSP, CO, and TVOC compared to Workplaces A and B (as shown in Table 1, Fig. 2, and Table 2), likely due to the frequent addition of firewood as fuel.

When comparing concentration differences by the kiln type, the TPM and RSP concentrations in the climbing kiln were relatively high; however, they were not statistically significant. There was a statistically significant difference in the electric oxidation kiln RCS GM concentration than those of the other two kiln types; however, no sample exceeded 25 μg/m3. According to a study that analyzed 11 high school ceramic classrooms, RSP's and RCS's GM concentrations were 0.029 mg/m3 and 0.006 mg/m3, respectively, with GM values were 0.036 mg/m3 and 0.002 mg/m3, respectively [16].

Several interesting facts were discovered by measuring PNC results by particle size. When firing in an electric oxidation kiln, the PNCs ranging from 10 nm to 420 nm were approximately 7.5 times higher when the peepholes were left open (1.62 × 105 ea/cm3) than when they were closed (2.16 × 104 ea/cm3) (Fig. 3, Table S2). This result was observed only in the electric oxidation kiln. The electric kiln used in Workplace A has a sealed structure when the plugs are in place. Once the kiln is closed, there is no pathway for nanoparticles to be released into the air, resulting in a low concentration. It was confirmed that during firing in an electric kiln, the PNC concentrations increased rapidly in mid-firing and then gradually decreased, consistent with the previous study [17]. However, unlike the electric oxidation kiln in Workplace A, the two kiln types installed in the other two workplaces used fire bricks as plugs but were not completely enclosed. This is because the electric reduction kiln brick had a small circular hole in the center. In the climbing kiln, smoke escaping from the gap could be seen while firewood was added, even when the bricks were inserted into the chambers' peephole.

Weyant et al (2016) noted that fluctuations in emissions were observed when fuel was added to a continuous kiln (a connected kiln where the fire is always burning and potteries are being fired and cooled simultaneously in different parts) [18]. Our study also shows that the PNCs fluctuated every time firewood was added at an interval of about 5 min in the climbing kiln (Fig. 3-d).

PNCs from 10 nm to 420 nm in the glaze dipping (5.61 × 104 ea/cm3), where the glaze powder was blended with water and mixed with a drill before the unglazed bisque ware was dipped, were 8.3 times higher than those in the glaze spray booth (6.73 × 103 ea/cm3) in Workplace B (Table S2). Installing an exhaust fan in the glaze spray booth effectively reduces the glaze concentration sprayed in the mist form. The glazing procedure, including mixing the glaze, crushing it with a drill, and sieving it, was still undertaken on the floor or on ordinary tables in the glaze room. Although lead-containing glaze was not used in this study's workplaces, there have been many studies on health risks due to lead or other heavy metals [19].

According to the CO and TVOCs measurement results presented in Table 2, the average and maximum CO and TVOCs concentrations were highest in the bisque firing process of the electric oxidation kiln and the climbing kiln, respectively. Regarding electric kilns, the potter would often come close to the door to check the temperature inside the kiln or open and close peepholes. In addition, in the climbing kiln, the location where the potter puts firewood into the chamber was closer to the kiln than where the measuring devices were fixed. In practice, the CO and TVOC concentrations were occasionally greater than the maximum values shown in Table 2 when the measuring devices were brought close to the electric kiln peepholes or the smoke generated in the climbing kiln chamber. Therefore, it is likely that the exposure level was underestimated. Furthermore, unlike climbing kilns, electric kilns do not require the potter to be near the electric kiln at all times. However, if the electric kiln is installed in a small indoor space, the potter there is at a high risk of being chronically exposed to the particles and toxic gases emitted from the kiln during daily working hours.

Ceramic companies in the Korea reported an average of 56.4 times at electric kiln and 3.0 times at firewood kiln annually [1]. Potters using climbing kilns worked on bisque ware in their private workplaces, including those in Workplaces A and B, directly handling clay and using an electric kiln to perform bisque firing. The most time-consuming task in the entire process was shaping and trimming. In addition, as a consequence of this study, some samples exceeded the RCS ACGIH TLV level only in the shaping and trimming, implying that exposure to RCS may pose a health concern for potters [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]. Therefore, for artisanal potters, reducing exposure to particulate matter or gaseous agents in indoor private workplace environment, where they spend most of their time, is an important strategy to reduce occupational exposure [25]. A previous study reported that high CO concentrations occur during the forklift operation and that RCS exposure may arise when moving bags of raw materials [3]. These two tasks were not included in this study.

From a methodological perspective, this study is limited by the fact that only three workplaces were measured due to difficulties in obtaining cooperation. However, we attempted to overcome this limitation by collecting extensive data through real-time and task-based measurements. Nevertheless, this study is valuable as the first to evaluate hazardous agents in the Korean pottery industry and to investigate the effects of open or closed peephole plugs and spray booths with exhaust fans on PNCs, including changes in the concentration of particulate and gaseous substances generated in kilns. In the future, a comprehensive study should be conducted on the variables affecting the concentration of various harmful substances—such as heavy metals, SO2, and NO2—across a wider range of kiln types and ceramic manufacturing processes.

Conclusion

This study identified significant exposure to airborne hazards, especially RCS and nanoparticles, in traditional pottery settings. While TPM and RSP levels remained below ACGIH TLVs, RCS exceeded the limit in shaping and trimming processes, indicating a potential health risk. Kiln type and task strongly influenced particulate and gas emissions, with climbing kilns and pottery wheeling contributing to higher exposure levels. The findings emphasize the need for improved ventilation and process controls, especially in enclosed workspaces. Despite the limited number of workplaces studied, this is the first comprehensive assessment in Korea to combine real-time monitoring with task-based sampling in pottery environments. Future research should expand the scope to include additional kiln types, heavy metals, and gaseous pollutants.
