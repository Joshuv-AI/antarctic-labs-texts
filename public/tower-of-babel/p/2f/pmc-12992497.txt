A multi-magnification digital colposcopy image datasets for cervical cancer screening in Nigeria
Authors: Ayotunde Olumuyiwa Ogunlaja, Olumide Thomas Adeleke, Placid Chukwudumebi Ughonu, Moses Olufemi Oyelami, Gbemi Ano-Edward, Yinka Tola Bakare, Israel Temidayo Bobo, Gbenga Oladayo Atanda, Isaac Adedoyin Oyebade, Ayoade Olaoluwa Afolabi, Opeyemi David Adeniji, Grace Oluwatosin Bello, Obadare Obafemi Okunoye, Oladipo Olutade Ladoye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This dataset comprises 3356 high-resolution digital colposcopy images collected from 332 women who attended cervical cancer screening at Bowen University Teaching Hospital, Ogbomoso, Nigeria, between June and July 2025. Each participant underwent comprehensive colposcopy imaging at four magnification levels (1 ×, 3 ×, 5 ×, and 7 ×) both before and after the application of 5% acetic acid solution. The acetic acid test, administered with a 45-second waiting period, enhances visualization of cervical epithelial abnormalities through characteristic acetowhite changes. All images were captured at 2048 × 1536 pixel resolution in JPEG format using medical-grade digital colposcopy equipment with integrated LED illumination. The dataset spans two diagnostic categories based on expert colposcopic interpretation: Normal (210 participants, 2134 images) and Abnormal (122 participants, 1222 images). The Abnormal category encompasses cervical lesions identified by established colposcopic features including acetowhite changes, punctation, and mosaicism. Images are organized in a patient-centric folder structure with standardized filenames encoding acquisition parameters, facilitating machine learning model development. Expert gynecologists classified and annotated the images using the Computer Vision Annotation Tool (CVAT) platform. This annotated collection enables the development and benchmarking of artificial intelligence models for automated cervical cancer detection, with particular relevance for deployment in resource-constrained healthcare environments where specialist expertise remains scarce.

Background

Cervical cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women in low- and middle-income countries, including Nigeria [2,3]. Despite being largely preventable through early detection and treatment, screening coverage in these regions remains critically low due to cost constraints, limited infrastructure, and a shortage of trained cytologists [4,5]. Traditional screening methods such as Pap smears and visual inspection with acetic acid are hindered by subjectivity and resource dependency, while digital colposcopy offers improved visualization of cervical lesions but requires expert interpretation [6]. This dataset was compiled to support the development of an artificial intelligence-powered cervical cancer screening model tailored to the Nigerian population. The collection comprises digital colposcopy images obtained at varying magnifications before and after acetic acid application, with expert annotation provided by gynecologists at Bowen University Teaching Hospital, Ogbomoso. The goal is to enhance diagnostic accuracy, reduce dependence on specialist interpretation, and enable scalable, cost-effective screening solutions in settings where such resources are scarce.

Data Description

Overview

The dataset comprises 3356 digital colposcopy images collected from 332 women who attended cervical cancer screening at Bowen University Teaching Hospital between June and July 2025. Images were captured at multiple magnification levels (1 ×, 3 ×, 5 ×, and 7 ×) before and after application of 5 % acetic acid solution. Diagnostic classification was based on expert colposcopic interpretation by experienced gynecologists. Pap smear cytology was additionally performed for a subset of participants where clinically indicated; however, it was not uniformly available and was not used for dataset labelling. The dataset represents two diagnostic categories: Normal (no cervical abnormalities) and Abnormal (cervical lesions identified by colposcopic features including acetowhite changes, punctation, and mosaicism).

Demographic and clinical characteristics of study participants.

The study population was predominantly married with high levels of formal education (78.4 % tertiary or postgraduate). All participants reported no history of tobacco use, and 91.0 % were HIV seronegative. Notably, 82.6 % had not undergone a previous Pap smear, underscoring the limited cervical cancer screening coverage in this population.

Expert gynecologists classified cervical findings as abnormal based on the presence of one or more of the following established colposcopic features after acetic acid application: acetowhite epithelial changes, punctation patterns, and mosaicism. It is important to note that colposcopic impression alone cannot provide definitive histopathological sub-classification; such distinction would require tissue biopsy, which was beyond the scope of this screening-focused data collection. The dataset therefore maintains a binary classification scheme (Normal vs. Abnormal) that accurately reflects the level of diagnostic certainty achievable through colposcopy and is clinically meaningful as a screening-level decision in resource-limited settings.

Folder structure and file organization

The repository follows a hierarchical directory structure with two primary folders: `01_Normal' and `02_Abnormal'. Within each category, subfolders named by participant identifier (buth_001 through buth_345) contain all images for that individual. This patient-centric organization facilitates proper train-validation-test splitting in machine learning applications. The binary folder structure reflects the colposcopic classification and supports screening-level classification experiments. Researchers interested in more granular severity analysis can leverage the CVAT polygon annotations provided with the dataset, which mark lesion boundaries, acetowhite regions, and anatomical landmarks, enabling lesion morphometry without requiring the dataset to impose sub-classifications that exceed the diagnostic certainty of colposcopy.

Image filenames encode acquisition parameters using the convention: [PatientID]_[p/po]_[magnification].jpg. For instance, `buth_042_p_5x.jpg' denotes Patient 042, pre-acetic acid, 5 × magnification. When multiple images were captured at the same settings to ensure quality, variant suffixes (a, b, c) are appended. All images are stored in JPEG format at 2048 × 1536 pixel resolution with 90–95 % compression quality, using RGB (24-bit) color space.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Ethical approval and participant recruitment

This prospective cross-sectional data collection effort was conducted at the Gynecology Clinic of Bowen University Teaching Hospital, Ogbomoso, Oyo State, Nigeria. Ethical approval was obtained from the Bowen University Teaching Hospital Research Ethics Committee (Protocol Number: BUTH/). Women aged 21 years and above attending the clinic for cervical cancer screening or routine gynecological examination were invited to participate. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, active menstruation, active vaginal infections, and history of total hysterectomy. All participants provided written informed consent after receiving detailed explanation of the procedures and intended data usage. The collection adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with Nigeria Data Protection Regulation guidelines.

Materials and equipment

Digital colposcopy examinations were performed using a high-resolution colposcope capable of capturing images at 2048 × 1536 pixels with adjustable magnification (1 ×, 3 ×, 5 ×, 7 ×) and integrated medical-grade LED illumination. Additional materials included sterile disposable vaginal specula (Cusco type), 5 % acetic acid solution, sterile cotton wool and swabs, normal saline (0.9 % sodium chloride), Ayres spatula and cytobrush for cytological sampling, glass slides, and Papanicolaou staining reagents (Hematoxylin, OG6, EA-50). Images were stored on a desktop computer connected to the colposcope and transferred to encrypted external storage following each session.

Digital colposcopy imaging protocol

Trained gynecologists performed all examinations in a dedicated room with standardized lighting. After positioning the participant in lithotomy position and inserting a saline-moistened speculum, excess mucus was gently removed to ensure clear visualization. The imaging protocol consisted of two phases. In the pre-acetic acid phase, images were captured at 1 × (overview), 3 × (intermediate), 5 × (detailed), and 7 × (close-up) magnifications. Following initial capture, 5 % acetic acid was liberally applied to the entire cervix using sterile cotton swabs. After a 45-second waiting period to allow acetowhite changes to develop in areas of abnormal epithelium, post-acetic acid images were captured at the same four magnification levels. When necessary to ensure optimal quality, multiple images were captured at each setting. This protocol yielded an average of approximately 10 images per participant (median: 10; range: 8–17), totaling 3356 images across 332 participants.

Image quality control

All captured images underwent automated quality assessment using Laplacian variance as a sharpness metric. Laplacian variance quantifies edge definition by computing the variance of the second-order spatial derivative of the grayscale image; higher values indicate sharper images with well-defined tissue boundaries, while lower values indicate blur. Magnification-specific thresholds were empirically calibrated to account for the inherent reduction in macroscopic texture contrast at higher magnification levels (1 ×: 80, 3 ×: 100, 5 ×: 120, 7 ×: 130). Additional automated checks for extreme brightness (mean pixel intensity >235), underexposure (<20), and insufficient contrast (standard deviation <10) were applied but did not flag any images, confirming consistent imaging conditions throughout the data collection period.

Of the 3356 images in the dataset, 82 (2.4 %) were flagged for suboptimal sharpness. The distribution was predominantly at higher magnifications: 7 × magnification accounted for 72 flagged images (87.8 % of all flags) and 5 × magnification accounted for 10 (12.2 %), with no images flagged at 1 × or 3 × magnification. This pattern is consistent with the reduced depth of field and increased motion sensitivity inherent to higher-magnification colposcopy. All flagged images were retained in the dataset with quality indicators provided in a supplementary metadata file (quality_flags.csv), which lists each flagged image alongside its patient identifier, magnification level, sharpness score, and applied threshold. This approach enables researchers to apply their own quality-based inclusion criteria and reflects authentic clinical imaging conditions that support research on robust algorithm development for real-world deployment.

Annotation methodology

Four experienced gynecologists with colposcopy expertise received standardized training on the annotation protocol before classifying images into two categories: Normal and Abnormal. Abnormal classification was based on the presence of acetowhite changes, mosaicism, punctation, or atypical vessels identified during colposcopic assessment. Annotators also marked anatomical landmarks (cervical os, transformation zone) and delineated lesion boundaries using polygon tools where applicable. Each image was independently reviewed by at least two gynecologists, with disagreements resolved through consensus discussion. Annotations were exported in JSON format compatible with standard machine learning frameworks.

Dataset preparation for machine learning

The dataset was prepared for machine learning model development using patient-level splitting to prevent data leakage. All images from a single participant appear in only one subset, ensuring that multiple views from the same patient do not distribute across training and evaluation sets. The recommended split allocates 70 % of patients (232 participants, ∼2349 images) for training, 15 % (50 participants, ∼504 images) for validation, and 15 % (50 participants, ∼503 images) for testing. This approach yields performance estimates that reflect true generalization to previously unseen patients.

To facilitate reproducible benchmarking, a reference baseline configuration is defined for use with the recommended patient-level data split, in which participants are allocated to training, validation, and test sets in a 70 %, 15 %, and 15 % ratio, respectively. A DenseNet-121 model pretrained on ImageNet is selected as the baseline architecture, reflecting its widespread adoption in medical image classification and prior cervical imaging studies. The model will be fine-tuned using a binary classification task distinguishing Normal and Abnormal cases. Model evaluation will be based on commonly reported clinical and machine learning metrics, including classification accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC AUC). These metrics are intended to provide a consistent reference framework against which future studies can compare alternative models, training strategies, or evaluation protocols developed using this dataset.

Limitations

Pap smear cytology results were not uniformly available for all participants; diagnostic labels are based on colposcopic impression rather than histopathological or cytological confirmation, which is a recognised limitation of this dataset. Colposcopic classification into Normal and Abnormal categories reflects expert visual assessment but does not constitute definitive histopathological diagnosis. Researchers should implement appropriate strategies such as weighted loss functions or synthetic oversampling when training classification models to address class imbalance. The collection represents a single institution in southwestern Nigeria, which may limit generalizability to other geographic populations or clinical settings. Additionally, while the 45-second acetic acid application interval follows standard clinical practice, variations in timing could affect acetowhite visualization consistency across cases.

Ethics Statement

This work involved human subjects. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to colposcopy examination and sample collection. The research protocol was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was granted by the Bowen University Teaching Hospital Research Ethics Committee (Protocol Number: BUTH/REC-2640). All participant identifiers were anonymized using a secure coding system, with the mapping file stored separately under restricted access in compliance with Nigeria Data Protection Regulation requirements.
