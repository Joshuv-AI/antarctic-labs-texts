A comparison of partner resemblance trends across putative same-sex and opposite-sex couples in the UK biobank
Authors: Tanya B Horwitz, Kristen M Kelly, Matthew C Keller
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study of partner resemblance has widespread implications in the social and natural sciences. To date, the vast majority of such research has focused on opposite-sex couples. This study compared patterns of partner correlations (“assortment”) across 66 traits in the UK Biobank (UKB) in 89,628 female-male (FM), 236 female-female (FF), and 216 male-male (MM) putative couples. The average FF, MM, and FM correlations were positive (indicating similarity rather than dissimilarity), though both same-sex partner types yielded significant correlations for only a minority of traits at the Bonferroni-corrected level. FM pairs showed more positive assortment (punadjusted<2e-16) than did MM or FF pairs for year of birth and number of children. Much existing same-sex partner correlation research has reported less similarity for age in MM pairs compared to opposite-sex pairs, while some (though not all) of the literature has reported lower similarity for age in FF pairs than in FM pairs. Expanding knowledge about same-sex assortment can contribute to family-based genetic designs and relationship psychology research in addition to diversifying couple-centered treatment modalities. Finally, this research can help to identify how couple dynamics and shared systemic factors potentially relate to ageing-related outcomes, behavior change, and healthcare needs in the LGBT community.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-12966-9.Subject terms: Human behaviour, Psychology

Introduction

Background

There has been extensive research on trait resemblance in human romantic, sexual, and marital partners. This phenomenon is sometimes referred to as “nonrandom mating,” “homogamy,” or “assortative mating,” though it has not been studied exclusively in pairs with offspring. Partner resemblance studies frequently find evidence for positive correlations in partners1–21. Meanwhile, very few traits show evidence of negative correlations (“disassortative mating”)3,7,19,22–30.

Unsurprisingly, the vast majority of partner resemblance research to date has focused on opposite-sex (female-male) pairs. A small number of studies on this topic, however, have compared rates of similarity across gender/sex compositions (female-female (FF), male-male (MM), female-male (FM)) for demographic, economic, behavioral, health-related, and physical traits. Because the sample that we use for this study (described in the Methods) does not include a gender variable, we will mostly refer to partnerships as “same−sex” and “opposite−sex” going forward, though the studies we discuss use a combination of the designations “sex” and “gender” to describe individuals and relationships. There is evidence that, like FM pairs, MM and FF pairs are more similar than dissimilar to one another with respect to several traits31–35, although some research points to different magnitudes of similarity across partner types. Some studies have shown evidence that MM partners are less homogamous (less similar) than opposite-sex partners with respect to age and education; meanwhile, FF similarity generally either mirrors that of FM pairs or lies in between FM and MM similarity rates31,34–36. United States census data have revealed that same-sex couples are more likely to be interracial/interethnic when compared to opposite-sex couples31, while a paper focused on health outcomes found more homogamy in U.S.-based same-sex partners (stratifying across males and females) for presence of chronic medical conditions, some health-related behavior, and body mass index when compared to opposite-sex partners33. Other research has investigated patterns across opposite-sex and same-sex partners with respect to labor market, economic, and household variables such as occupation, income, and division of finances/household tasks32,35,37. Both opposite-sex and same-sex couples in the U.S. have demonstrated positive assortment for labor market variables and investment income according to census data32 (though a small study found no relationships between same-sex partners with respect to several (socio)economic factors that were positively correlated in the study’s opposite-sex partners35). A study drawing from same-sex couples and heterosexually-coupled siblings/sibling-in-laws of participants showed some evidence that same-sex couples—particularly lesbian couples—divided their finances more equally than did opposite-sex couples. Additionally, the same study reported that divisions of household tasks in same-sex couples often differed from those demonstrated in opposite-sex couples, the latter of whom were more likely to divide chores along traditional, gendered lines37. Finally, while a handful of studies have investigated homogamy and relationship satisfaction/duration/dissolution in opposite-sex couples1,23,38–43, the literature on same-sex assortment trends for psychological and relationship quality variables is likely too sparse to be generalizable35.

Human partner resemblance is pertinent to many disciplines, including genetics17,44–49, sociology, economics, epidemiology, anthropology, and psychology50–52, informing research on health and health behaviors4,5,11–15,28,33,53–63, relationship psychology1,23,40,64–67, and variation in dating and marriage norms. In addition to advancing knowledge about social stratification and personal preferences, research on “convergence,” wherein partners become more similar for a trait over time, could inform relationship-centered interventions focused on health-related behavior changes around habits like exercise, diet, substance use, and other “plastic” traits7,11,53,54. Finally, existing evidence for non-random matching on some (if not all) psychiatric phenotypes16–21,68 suggests that further investigating these trends could be similarly valuable for understanding epidemiological patterns and improving current treatments in the mental health/illness sphere.

Study of same-sex partner resemblance is an important topic for further investigation, with relevance to multiple scientific and social science disciplines, particularly those exploring relationships, marriage, sexuality, and dating practices. Examining similarities between same-sex partners provides insights into how cultural and gender-related factors influence relationship formation. For example, comparing rates of within-pair similarity across MM and FF couples may reveal whether certain traits are valued differently based on sex/gender—information that can be difficult to discern from studying opposite-sex couples alone. Additionally, if different types of couples tend to meet in different environments, different forms of social stratification may be partially responsible for assortment pattern differences across pair types. At the same time, it is useful to compare MM, FF, and FM pair resemblance because it is possible that individuals in same-sex pairs have different preferences than their sex-matched, heterosexually-coupled counterparts.

Current gaps in knowledge and overview of current research

Increased recognition and acceptance of same-sex unions in many countries have allowed researchers to leverage a few large register- and census-based databases for the purpose of assortment research, with same-sex sample sizes in this research generally ranging from hundreds to thousands of pairs, and at least one same-sex sample31 exceeding 10,000 pairs. At the same time, while numerous papers have examined opposite-sex assortative mating trends, we know of only a handful of studies that examine these patterns in long-term same-sex partnerships. While certain general trends—for example, that partners correlate positively for a large majority of studied traits and that certain traits such as political and religious values and educational attainment tend to show particularly high degrees of assortment21—are well-established in the opposite-sex assortment literature, little is known about differences in assortment patterns across FM, MM, and FF partnerships. Current evidence suggests lower similarity in age and education among MM partnerships compared to FM partnerships, but additional research is needed to determine if this pattern extends to other traits and other populations. Furthermore, the literature on assortative mating in same-sex couples is notably lacking for traits related to substance use, health behaviors, relationship dynamics, biological characteristics, and daily habits. Indeed, many of these traits have not yet been examined at all in studies of same-sex partner resemblance.

In the current report, we aim to address these gaps through the assessment of putative same-sex and opposite-sex cohabitating/married partner pairs taken from the UK Biobank (UKB), a large database that has collected data on over 500,000 middle-aged and elderly individuals living in the United Kingdom69. While a few projects have used UKB data to study characteristics of women who have sex with women and men who have sex with men70–73 and others have deduced putative opposite-sex, cohabitating partners within the sample for the purpose of studying assortment6,21,48,49,74–76, we do not know of any published assortative mating study that investigates same-sex assortment in the UKB. Thus, we leverage data from the UKB to calculate and compare partner correlations for several dozen traits in putative FF, MM, and FM partner pairs. In addition to reporting within-group correlations, we test whether the magnitude of concordance for each trait varies across pair types.

Within-group correlations

The opposite-sex pairs yielded a mean correlation of 0.19 across the final list of 66 traits, which were filtered from an initial list of 132 traits (see Analysis of within-group correlations in the Methods). Across these 66 traits, the mean correlation was 0.15 for FF pairs and 0.15 for MM pairs. The median correlations across partner types were nearly identical to one another: for the FM, FF, and MM pairs, they were 0.14, 0.14, and 0.15, respectively. Of the 66 correlations, 10.61% were significantly different from zero in FF pairs, and 25.76% were significantly different from zero in MM pairs at the Bonferroni-corrected level. For FM pairs, 93.94% of correlations were significant at the Bonferroni-corrected level. Though two negative correlations were significant at the Bonferroni-corrected level in FM pairs, no negative correlations were significantly different from zero for either of the same-sex groups. The above differences in significance were likely mostly due to differences in statistical power rather than differences in effect sizes across correlations.

In the MM group, time spent watching television per day, alcohol intake frequency, and year of birth showed the highest rates of concordance (rs =.56-0.57, p < 2e-16 for all). In the FF group, we found the highest rates of concordance for the same three traits (rs = 0.54-0.59, p < 2e-16 for all). In the FM group, pairs showed the highest rate of concordance for year of birth (r =.86, p < 2e-16), followed by number of children (r =.70, p < 2e-16) and alcohol intake frequency (r =.57, p < 2e-16).

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed partner resemblance trends (“assortment”) in putative opposite-sex and same-sex cohabitating couples drawn from the UKB with respect to 66 traits. Similar to what Horwitz et al. (2023)21 reported, the vast majority of point estimates for opposite-sex partners were significantly positive after Bonferroni correction. A minority of correlations across all 66 traits reached significance for same-sex pairs, although this was not entirely surprising given the much smaller sample sizes of same-sex partners. For the vast majority of the traits examined, there were no significant differences in assortment across any of the pair types. Like in the case of opposite-sex partners, the mean correlations for FF and MM pairs were both positive. Median correlations were also positive across the board.

Paired tests indicated slightly less assortment overall for FF pairs than for FM pairs but yielded p-values of > 0.05 for both the FM vs. MM and FF vs. MM comparisons. A more detailed examination of the results revealed a complex picture. Some traits showed higher point estimates of similarity among FF pairs compared to FM pairs, as well as compared to MM pairs. Nevertheless, after applying Bonferroni correction, we found no significant differences in assortment across any groups for 64 of the 66 traits we ultimately examined. Owing to large standard errors in the same-sex pairs, this was true even for some corresponding point estimates with seemingly large discrepancies (i.e., − 0.318 and 0.014 for Nervous Feelings in FF and MM pairs, respectively). When considering the median correlation alone as an indicator of general partner similarity, there was virtually no difference in overall trends across pair types. At the same time, a Wilcoxon signed-rank test (which is more robust to outliers than the t-test) found an overall tendency for higher correlations in FM pairs compared to FF pairs. This could indicate that, while women in both FF and FM pairs tend to couple with similar partners overall, women in opposite-sex couples have a slightly stronger tendency towards assortment when the cumulative effect of many traits is considered.

Contrary to findings from several previous studies, we did not observe significant group differences in concordance for educational attainment. However, our correlational estimates for educational attainment in the MM (r =.38) and FM (r =.49) samples closely align with those reported by Verbakel and Kalmijn’s 2014 study34 (r =.35 for MM and r =.47 for FM), which examined larger samples and, due to greater statistical power, did find a significant difference in educational concordance between MM and FM pairs.

Like opposite-sex partners, both MM and FF partners showed high correlations for year of birth. While none of the differences between MM and FF correlations were statistically significant, FM partners were significantly more correlated than were FF and MM partners on both year of birth and number of children. That FM pairs had the highest correlation for number of children was to be expected, although, more surprisingly, we found no association for number of children within the MM sample. The direction of our findings regarding group differences in year of birth reflect previous findings comparing MM and FM couples, as well as some of the findings comparing FF and FM couples, though the latter have been more mixed (see below).

Past research has suggested same-sex partners, though they often show trends of assortment31–35, may be less correlated for some factors (i.e., demographic factors), when compared to opposite-sex couples. For example, in multiple samples, MM couples have been less similar for educational attainment and age when compared to FM couples, while FF pairs generally correlate for these traits neither less than MM couples nor more than FM couples31,34–36. There is also a small amount of evidence to suggest more assortment in same-sex couples for some anthropometric and health-related traits when compared to opposite-sex couples33. While our results did reproduce some of these findings, they did not support all of them.

Our finding that opposite-sex partners were significantly more likely to assort on year of birth than were either of the same-sex groups is consistent with some of the findings that have been reported on this topic; across the literature, there is particularly strong support for this finding with respect to MM vs. FM pairs. There are numerous theories and potential explanations that align with trends of decreased assortment for age (and possibly other traits) in same-sex or MM relationships. First, it is possible that men seeking gay relationships prefer larger age differences in their relationships34. A study that drew from personal advertisements in newspapers found that, while the age of men seeking women and women seeking men correlated at r =.83 and r =.94, respectively, with the age of their preferred partner, the age of men seeking men correlated at only r =.54 with the age of their desired partner80. One theory posits that same-sex dyads’ decreased adherence to the general matching “rule” for some traits is linked to their relationships already subverting another social norm: namely, opposite-sex coupling in romantic and sexual partnerships31,34,81. Other potentially influential factors concern the settings in and circumstances under which partners tend to meet each other. For example, if the opposite-sex partners in our sample were more likely to meet in age-stratified educational settings, we might expect to see a corresponding increased rate of assortative mating for year of birth. Additionally, differences in number of potential mates, as well as in geopolitical contexts and rates of geographic mobility across groups, may contribute to differences in matching trends31,34,81.

There were several measures related to health or biology—for example, number of medications, number of illnesses, several anthropometric traits, and certain smoking-related traits—that were positively correlated in MM pairs at the Bonferroni-corrected significance level. Though most such traits were not correlated in FF pairs at the same significance threshold, degree of FF resemblance trended toward significance in the positive direction for many of these traits (see Supplementary Tables S2A, S2C, and S2D). Though it has not been studied extensively in same-sex couples, one study of U.S. couples found more partner similarity for certain health-related indicators in MM and FF pairs when compared to FM pairs33.

Prior research has explored how gendered dynamics in (largely FM) couples may relate to manifestations of health behaviors such as doctor visits and substance use over the course of a relationship. For example, there is evidence to suggest that female partners in particular exert influence on male partners’ health behaviors11. Further, there are complex implications regarding the relationships between divorce and substance use behaviors54,82,83. For example, Power et al. (1999)82, who used different thresholds for heavy alcohol consumption across British men and women, found that, when adjusting for drinking levels at age 23, risk of heavy drinking at age 33 was higher in divorced participants than in those who had been continuously married and that this pattern held for both men and women. Reczek et al. (2016)54, who used equivalent thresholds for heavy alcohol consumption across men and women, found evidence that divorce increases men’s but decreases some women’s heavy drinking, with variation in trends across the lifespan. One paper showed that, when considering men and women in both same-sex and opposite-sex relationships, women in FM relationships attempted to directly regulate their spouses’ health habits the most and men in FM relationships attempted to directly regulate their spouses’ health habits the least such that those in same-sex couples lay somewhere in the middle. Exertion of other social control dynamics (facilitation and support) was associated with somewhat different patterns across groups. The same paper observed that health behavior trait values and within-couple disparities in trait values for health behaviors predicted social control dynamics in relationships yet did not entirely account for gendered patterns of social control tactics84. Further research on same-sex partners in this arena could be instrumental to determining to what extent unidirectional and/or bidirectional mechanisms within romantic and sexual relationships fuel health behavior changes over time and to what extent these relationship mechanisms are gender-/sex-specific.

Another key related issue concerns group differences in health-related behaviors across sex-by-pair type combinations. For example, one paper that examined participants aged 50 and older in the Health and Retirement Study found that those in same-sex relationships had higher odds of cognitive impairment compared to those in opposite-sex relationships, even after controlling for sociodemographic covariates; interestingly, the discrepancy was no longer significant once marital status was added as a covariate85. This highlights a potentially important intersection between ageing, same-sex partnerships, and other aspects of relationships that can affect outcomes in individuals. For example, it is possible that changes in laws around gay sex and marriage could have an impact on the later-life health outcomes of older individuals who are attracted to the same sex or who are in same-sex relationships.

Although our same-sex samples were not small by most standards, our opposite-sex sample, which included up to 89,628 pairs per trait, far outnumbered that of our FF sample (up to 236 pairs per trait) and that of our MM sample (up to 216 pairs per trait). These same-sex sample sizes in combination with our stringent significance threshold meant that the MM and FF analyses were underpowered to detect the statistical significance of modest within-group correlations: assuming no missing pairs in the MM sample, the least extreme correlation value that we had 80% power to detect for this sample was r 0.299, while the corresponding threshold in the full set of FF pairs was r 0.287. There was insufficient data to warrant meta-analysis of past same-sex assortment studies, making it difficult to evaluate how our findings fit into a broader landscape of geographic, generational, and/or cultural trends.

Next, the UKB is not fully representative of the UK population or of the global population. Participants were mostly between the ages of 40 and 69 when they were initially recruited, and so findings from this cohort may not translate across all generations or ages. While its very high proportion of white participants broadly reflects the UK’s population, the UKB clearly does not represent the global population on a racial or ethnic level. In addition, there are ways in which the UKB is not reflective of similarly aged UK residents. Though they did not perform formal statistical comparisons, Fry et al. (2017)86, comparing similarly aged, matched subsets of the UKB to national English and Welsh census data, reported that UKB participants were more likely to own their property outright (56.7% vs. 40.6%). Additionally, when compared to those in the general English population, UKB participants were less likely to be current smokers, though some age-by-sex groups in the UKB had a slightly higher rate of heavy smoking when compared to age-matched groups in the general English population. There were also notable differences in disease-related data: UKB men between the ages of 45 and 54 were less than half as likely to self-report cardiovascular disease when compared to age-matched men in the general English population, while UKB women between the ages of 45 and 54 were less that one quarter as likely to report cardiovascular disease than were English women in the same age range; UKB participants also had a lower mortality rate than those in the general population at age 70–74 years.

As mentioned in the Methods and as demonstrated in Supplementary Tables S2A, S2B, and S2D, trait means and prevalences are not all consistent across couple type in the UKB. Further, UKB participants who are members of putative couples should not be considered representative of the UKB as a whole, and couples with two UKB-participating members may not be representative of all UKB participants with partners. The present study is, to our knowledge, the first to examine same-sex assortment in individuals residing in the UK, and we are unaware of any studies on this topic that sample from outside of the United States and a handful of Northern and Western European countries. Minimal sample diversity in same-sex assortment research thus continues to hinder the generalizability of the findings on this topic to the global population.

The UKB does not directly gather information on the couple status of pairs, which has presented limitations for all studies that have sought to use the cohort to assess assortment in putative opposite-sex couples. That being said, several studies have evaluated putative couples in the UKB6,21,48,49,74–76, and Kelly et al.’s93 criteria build on past criteria and the results of several tests demonstrating which variables are most likely to introduce bias and which are likely to identify true couples. Because the current paper also sought to analyze same-sex pairs, we faced some additional challenges in attempting to infer couple status. As a consequence of fluctuating recognition, legal status, and social acceptance around same-sex sex and same-sex relationships in the UK, we suspect that some members of cohabitating same-sex couples did not report same-sex intercourse and/or did not report living with a spouse or partner because of privacy concerns. With these concerns in mind, we only included pairs with certain combinations of responses to questions relating to sexual history as putative same-sex couples, as described in the Methods. Doing so probably eliminated some true same-sex pairs for other reasons as well. For example, two members of a same-sex couple with a history of opposite-sex penetrative sex but not of same-sex penetrative sex may have reported having had sexual intercourse but not same-sex intercourse if they interpreted the latter as referring only to penetrative sex. This possibly explains why members of potential FF pairs were more likely than members of potential MM pairs to say they had never had sexual intercourse. It is also possible that some of the pairs in which someone responded in the negative to this question were asexual same-sex romantic unions that involved one person who had never had any type of sexual encounter. While it is likely that we did not identify all true same-sex couples, we believe that the matching criteria we used allowed for accurate detection of true pairs and conferred a low rate of false positives (see the Partner detection section of the Methods).

Another limitation of our methodology is that it only accounted for resemblance in dyadic units. While several studies have examined assortment in re-married participants, further development and application of methods that account for multiple sexual or romantic relationships may be particularly relevant to the study of male-male assortment given evidence that consensual non-monogamy is much more normative in male-male partnerships than in other types of partnerships37. Finally, because the UKB does not record gender identity, it is possible that we included participants whose gender did not align with their reported sex, which would have affected how pairs were classified.

In conclusion, we evaluated resemblance in cohabitating putative partners in the UKB, including both opposite-sex and same-sex dyads in our sample. To our knowledge, this was the first study to explore trends in putative same-sex partners drawn from the UKB and many of the traits that we examined have not previously been analyzed in same-sex partner resemblance research. The vast majority of traits yielded non-zero partner correlations in opposite-sex pairs, while far fewer traits were associated with significant non-zero correlations in same-sex pairs; at the same time, only two traits (both continuous) were associated with differences in assortment across any of the pair types at the Bonferroni-corrected level. This combination of findings was, in many cases, likely the result of the opposite-sex pairs comprising a much larger sample. More insights from this line of research would further contribute to social psychological, epidemiological, economic, genetic, and relationship satisfaction research and could help inform substance-related and other psychiatric treatment models, as well as best practices in ageing care for same-sex couples.

Methods

Description of the sample

The present study drew from the UK Biobank (UKB), which obtained ethical approval from the Northwest Multi-Center Research Ethics Committee. All research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Participants provided written informed consent. The present study was conducted using data received under Application Number 16651.

The UKB is a large database of biological, physical, imaging, and phenotypic data taken from approximately half a million people who resided in Scotland, England, and Wales at the time of their recruitment and who lived relatively close to one of the designated assessment centers. Individuals who met the eligibility requirements and were registered with the UK’s National Health Service received postal invitations to participate in the UKB87. Due to its emphasis on health and ageing variables, the UKB gathered data on participants who were mostly between the ages of 40 and 69 when they were recruited between 2006 and 201088.

Between 2012 and 2013, 20,000 individuals participated in repeat assessments87; the UKB also began collecting imaging data in 2014, with repeat imaging assessments beginning in 201989. In addition to the 2012–2013 repeat assessment, phenotypic data were also collected during the initial and repeat imaging visits. However, we used baseline data only for the partner detection criteria and for correlational analyses.

Partner detection

All analysis was performed in RStudio using R versions 4.4.1 and 4.5.0 (https://www.r-project.org/)90. Because cohabitating and married partners are not explicitly reported in the UKB, we drew on Kelly et al. (2025)’s partner detection criteria to identify putative opposite-sex spouse/partner pairs93. To detect putative same-sex pairs, we made modifications to the opposite-sex criteria, as described below. Kelly et al. created two versions of their criteria, one of which uses a colocation file that the UKB created based on participant addresses and which was only provided to a small number of researchers, and the other of which colocates pairs using other geographically-derived variables but not the UKB colocation file. The current paper uses the UKB colocation file version of the criteria. Figure 1 shows the original Kelly et al. opposite-sex partner detection criteria, accompanied by modifications we made in the current paper to accommodate detection of same-sex pairs.

Our same-sex criteria yielded a final sample of 236 FF pairs and 216 MM pairs. We analyzed the two same-sex partner types (FF and MM) independently in order to examine FF and MM resemblance separately rather than looking at same-sex pairs as a whole. We had a final FM sample of 89,628 pairs. The participants in same-sex couples (Mean FF year of birth = 1956, SD = 6.86; Mean MM year of birth = 1955, SD = 7.87) were younger than the participants of the corresponding sex in opposite-sex couples (Mean F = 1951, SD = 7.18; Mean M = 1949, SD = 7.21). The mean birth year of the overall UKB (Mean F = 1952, SD = 8.03; Mean M = 1951, SD = 8.21) more closely reflected the opposite-sex sample. The same-sex couples were much more formally educated than the opposite-sex couples. Among those with non-missing responses to the education question, 60.9% of women in FF couples and 52.8% of men in MM couples had a college or university degree, while only 28.3% of women in FM couples and 34% of men in FM couples had a college or university degree. In the full sample, 37.2% of male participants and 32.9% of female participants had a college or university degree. Among those with non-missing responses for ethnic background, the percentage of white participants, for both sexes and in all pair types (97.5% for FF; 95.1% for MM; 97% for FM F; 97.3% for FM M), was similar to that of white participants in the entire UKB (94.6% for both sexes).

The probability that two randomly-drawn opposite-sex individuals from the same assessment center would match on all stages of partner detection criteria (but not including the requirement that pairs be co-located in the UKB co-location file and other non-applicable steps and not including an optional Step 10 included in Kelly et al.) was 0.0303, while the probability of two randomly-drawn female participants from the same assessment center matching was 1.1e-5 and the probability of two randomly-drawn male participants from the same assessment center matching was 3.2e-5 (the latter two lower probabilities are driven by the additional requirement that at least one member of same-sex pairs report same-sex intercourse). The actual probability of a false match for each group using the full set of criteria would be even lower because a false match in our samples would additionally require that the pairs be incorrectly matched in UKB’s colocation file, which is itself an unlikely event. Supplementary Table S1B summarizes the probabilities of two randomly-drawn participants of each dyadic sex combination from the same assessment center matching on each progressive step (in addition to the prior steps), excluding the requirement that pairs be co-located in the UKB colocation file and other non-applicable steps.

Analysis of within-group correlations

We began by compiling baseline responses from all the participants included in our final putative couple sample for 132 continuous, binary, and ordinal traits. The traits that we initially considered were similar to the list of traits examined in Horwitz et al. (2023)21, but we made slight changes to how some of the variables were coded, largely for reasons related to accuracy, interpretability, or low frequency of certain responses. The summary and correlational results for continuously-coded traits are in Supplementary Table S2A. The summary data for dichotomously-coded traits are in Supplementary Table S2B and were computed after eliminating traits not associated with at least 200 pairs per group but before eliminating traits based on actual or expected cell frequencies. The correlational results for dichotomously-coded traits are in Supplementary Table S2C, and the summary and correlational results for ordinally-coded traits are presented in Supplementary Table S2D. Supplementary Tables S2A, S2C, and S2D also include corresponding UKB Field IDs, trait descriptions, and trait codings.

We selected a final list of continuous traits with at least 200 pairs per pair type (FF, FM, MM). These sample sizes were calculated after excluding pairs with at least one outlier (defined as someone with a value of ≥ 4 standard deviations from the trait mean). This outlier exclusion was based on means and standard deviations specific to sex and pair type (females in FF pairs, males in MM pairs, females in FM pairs, males in FM pairs), where means and standard deviations for same-sex partners were pooled across partner 1 and partner 2 (separately for males and females).

For dichotomous and ordinal traits, we began by also narrowing down our list of traits to those for which there were at least 200 pairs with complete information in each pair type group. For dichotomous traits, we further required that the contingency tables of trait values corresponding to each pair type all have expected cell frequencies of ≥ 5, as well as observed cell frequencies of > 0. For opposite-sex pairs, discordant cells were differentiated by sex (pairs with 0-coded females and 1-coded males versus pairs with 0-coded males and 1-coded females). For same-sex pairs, to account for the exchangeability of partner 1 and partner 2, observed cell sizes for discordant cells were calculated as half the total number of discordant pairs (separately for each sex). However, we used a different method for calculating correlations for dichotomously-coded traits in FF and MM pairs (see below).

Of our initial 132 traits, 66 (26 continuous, 26 dichotomous, and 14 ordinal) met requirements for analysis across all groups. We eliminated 29 continuous traits, 10 ordinal traits, and 12 binary traits for which there were not 200 or more complete pairs of responses across all three couple types. We eliminated an additional 15 binary traits for not having adequate observed or expected cell frequency counts (see above).

Typically, intraclass correlations are used for correlating continuous variables within dyadic units when there is no distinction between members of a dyad (i.e., partner 1 and partner 2 in a same-sex pair)91. However, given our aim of comparing MM and FF concordance to FM concordance and in the interest of making the present results comparable to those in Horwitz et al. (2023)21, we instead used Pearson’s correlation for continuous traits for all partner types. Since which partner in the MM and FF pairs was assigned “partner 1” and which was assigned “partner 2” could have an influence on the resulting correlation, we created 500 versions of each same-sex sample (separately for each sex) such that each version had a randomly-assigned order for each pair. We calculated 500 correlations between partner 1 and partner 2 for each continuous trait based on these 500 sets of divided samples and selected the median correlation as our result. We used a similar method for our final list of dichotomous and ordinal traits but instead used tetrachoric partner correlations for dichotomous traits and polychoric correlations—the latter of which can accommodate more than two levels per variable—for ordinal traits. The minimum and maximum correlation observed for each trait across all 500 versions in both pair types can be found in Supplementary Tables S4A (continuous traits), S4B (dichotomous traits), and S4C (ordinal traits).

Comparison of concordance across FF, MM, and FM partners

To assess differences in correlations across partner pair types for each continuous trait, we performed three difference tests comparing Fisher Z transformed correlations for each pairwise combination of groups (FF vs. FM, MM vs. FM, and FF vs. MM). To do this, we first calculated the Fisher-transformed partner correlation for the n1 pairs with complete data in group 1 (one pair type) and the Fisher-transformed partner correlation for the n2 pairs with complete data in group 2 (another pair type); we then divided the difference between the fisher-transformed correlations bythe square-root of the sum of their estimated variances. Finally, we tested whether the resulting value was different from zero. This method performed better overall in a Monte Carlo simulation than did structural equation modeling (SEM) or several different linear regression methods. In addition to avoiding Type I errors, the difference test method was able to detect correlational differences between pair types at a conservative, Bonferroni-corrected p-value threshold in some cases when the other methods were not. Note that, for both binary (see below) and continuous trait simulations, the Bonferroni-corrected p-value threshold was 0.05/(3*n traits) to account for three comparisons between groups (FM vs. FF, FM vs. MM, FF vs. MM), regardless of the number of equations used for the method in question.

For binary traits, we used the R package lavaan92 to compare tetrachoric correlations across pair types with SEM based on threshold models. We performed three model comparisons per trait, where each of the three tests again corresponded to each possible pairwise comparison across the three groups. To evaluate differences for each comparison, we determined whether a model that constrained the correlations to be equal across the two groups in question fit significantly worse than one where the correlation for each group was freely estimated. To perform these tests in same-sex pairs, we used the partner ordering that yielded the median point estimate for the corresponding trait and sex in the original correlation tests (see Analysis of within-group correlations). The approach above performed better than most alternative approaches, including logistic regression, in a Monte Carlo simulation in terms of avoiding type I errors, although difference tests comparing the Fisher Z transformations of the tetrachoric correlations also performed well. For ordinal traits, we used SEM to test differences in polychoric correlations.

When considering all 66 traits, Shapiro-Wilk normality tests revealed that the differences between the FM Fisher-transformed correlations and the FF Fisher-transformed correlations were not normally distributed (W = 0.81, p = 8.223e-08), nor were the differences between the FM Fisher-transformed correlations and the MM Fisher-transformed correlations (W = 0.80, p = 5.384e-08). However, the differences between the Fisher-transformed FF and Fisher-transformed MM correlations were (W = 0.99, p =.7725). When the two most extreme FM within-pair correlation (for year of birth and number of children) were dropped, the Shapiro-Wilk normality tests were no longer significant for the differences involving FM pairs (W = 0.97, p =.1921 for the differences between the Fisher-transformed FF and Fisher-transformed FM correlations; W = 0.98, p =.5464 for the differences between the Fisher-transformed MM and Fisher-transformed FM correlations).

Figure 8a–c display Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for comparisons involving FM pairs and a Paired samples t-test for the comparison between the same-sex groups. However, in the Results (see Comparison across partner types), we report results for both types of tests for the FF vs. MM comparison for comparability; in addition to Wilcoxon signed-rank tests comparing all 66 traits, we also report results for Paired samples t-tests for comparisons involving FM pairs with year of birth and number of children omitted across groups.
