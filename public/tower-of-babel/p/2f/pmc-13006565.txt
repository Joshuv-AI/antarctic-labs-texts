Gastrointestinal Tolerance and Refeeding Syndrome in Severely Malnourished Children Treated with Oral Nutrition Supplements Compared to F-75/F-100: A Hospital-Based Randomized Controlled Pilot Trial
Authors: Klara Yuliarti, Lanny Christine Gultom, Cut Nurul Hafifah, Ina Susianti Timan, Merci Monica Boru Pasaribu, Ferry Liwang, Valensia Vivian The, Damayanti Rusli Sjarif
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeCurrent World Health Organization guidelines recommend F-75/F-100 for inpatients and ready-to-use therapeutic foods (RUTF) for outpatients with severe acute malnutrition (SAM). In Indonesia, F-100 continues to be administered after discharge because of limited evidence of RUTF’s acceptability and effectiveness in a local setting. Home-prepared F-100 carried the risk of improper mixing and bacterial contamination. Therefore, ready-to-drink oral nutritional supplements (RTD ONS) may serve as alternatives to F-75/F-100. This study aimed to investigate the safety and effectiveness of ONS in hospitalized children with SAM.MethodsThis study was a pilot randomized controlled trial comparing the gastrointestinal tolerance of F-75/F-100, high-energy ONS, and standard-energy ONS in 108 hospitalized subjects aged 12–60 months old with complicated SAM. Subjects were monitored for gastrointestinal symptoms and refeeding syndrome.ResultsThe incidence of vomiting due to the formula was not significantly different among the three groups (p=0.362), as was the incidence of diarrhea, which was 13.9% (F-75/F-100), 16.7% (high-energy ONS), and 11.1% (standard-energy ONS) (p=0.939). The incidences of refeeding syndrome were 13.9%, 5.6%, and 2.8% in the F-75/F-100, standard-energy ONS, and high-energy ONS groups, respectively (p=0.169). The high-energy ONS showed the highest weight increment (8.5±3.2 g/kg/day) among the three groups.ConclusionReady-to-drink ONS is a safe and effective therapeutic nutritional option for children with complicated SAM who live in areas with poor sanitation. This study provides preliminary results and requires further investigation to address the limitations of a small sample size and short study timeframe.

INTRODUCTION

Severe acute malnutrition (SAM) is a pervasive cause of child mortality, particularly in emerging countries, because it increases the risk of mortality by up to 11 times in children under five years old [1234]. A Joint Statement by several organizations reported that approximately 20 million children under the age of five suffer from SAM annually, with a 5 percent mortality [5]. Indonesia’s Ministry of Health reported that up to 2.8% of children under five years of age had SAM in 2017 [6].

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends the use of formulas 75 (F-75) and 100 (F-100) in hospitals, followed by ready-to-use therapeutic food (RUTF) at home, as the standard treatment for SAM. However, RUTF are not widely available in some countries, including Indonesia. Few studies have reported that Indonesian RUTF demonstrated poor acceptability. Therefore, in Indonesia, we continue to administer F-100 at home and teach parents to prepare for F-100. Nevertheless, there are challenges that can lead to suboptimal results after treatment [78]. The exact composition of the formula is difficult to accurately follow because parents use household serving sizes to measure the amount of milk powder, sugar, and oil. There is also a high risk of microorganism contamination due to non-hygienic formula preparation, especially in areas with limited clean water resources, which is common in Indonesia [910].

Ready-to-drink (RTD) oral nutrition supplement (ONS) is a liquid formula categorized as a formula for special medical purposes that should be used only for medical indications under prescription by pediatricians, as written in the Regulation of the Indonesian National Agency of Drug and Food Control no.1 of 2018. ONS is prescribed for children with faltering growth or mild to moderate malnutrition and has demonstrated promising efficacy in improving nutritional status [1112]. Currently, two types of RTD ONS are available in Indonesia: high-energy ONS (1.5 kcal/mL) and standard-energy ONS (1 kcal/mL). High-energy ONS is preferable in patients with inadequate intake and volume restrictions, such as congenital heart disease and large intra-abdominal masses [1314]. High-energy RTD ONS has an osmolarity of 440 mOsm/L, which is slightly higher than that of F-100 (419 mOsm/L) and much higher than that of standard-energy ONS (285 mOsm/L), which may increase the risk of gastrointestinal (GI) intolerance, that is, osmotic diarrhea [15].

Hospital-made F-75 contains a relatively low amount of phosphorus; therefore, the daily phosphorus consumption in patients receiving hospital-made F-75 was less than that recommended by the WHO (60 mg/kg/day) [16]. High carbohydrate and low phosphorus content may increase the risk of refeeding syndrome [17]. Current WHO guidelines recommend the use of premixed products following the new United Nations (UN) procurement specifications for F-75 [1819]. Extrinsic phosphorus, in the form of phosphorus salt, has been added to the premix commercial product (Nutriset®; Nutriset) at an amount of 560 mg phosphorus/L of F-75 (i.e., a child fed F-75 at 130 mL/kg/day receives approximately 73 mg/kg/day of phosphorus). Premixed F-75 is not available in Indonesia; therefore, hospital-made F-75 with low phosphorus content is still used. The ONS formulas had higher protein-energy ratios (8.9% for high-energy ONS and 9.6% for standard-energy ONS) than F-75. The different nutritional profiles of ONS and F-75 also require further investigation regarding any increased risk of refeeding syndrome during the stabilization phase.

Refeeding syndrome (RS) is a potentially life-threatening condition that occurs in malnourished individuals who suddenly resume feeding [20]. It is characterized by electrolyte imbalances, particularly hypophosphatemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, and/or thiamine deficiency [2021]. The clinical symptoms of RS arise from a deficiency of these minerals/vitamins and rapid changes in their serum concentrations, leading to metabolic and electrolyte disturbances. A gradual increase in caloric intake and close daily monitoring of electrolyte levels are essential for preventing RS [20].

Two studies reported the use of ONS in mild-to-moderate undernutrition; however, no studies have reported the use of ONS in children [1112]. This is the first trial investigating GI tolerance and the efficacy of ONS given to children under five with complicated SAM. Given the slightly higher osmolarity of high-energy ONS than that of F-100, there is concern about increased GI intolerance, particularly osmotic diarrhea, which has not been previously evaluated in this vulnerable population. Additionally, the difference in protein and phosphorus contents between ONS and F-75 may influence the risk of refeeding syndrome. Because the hospital-made F-75 used in Indonesia has a lower phosphorus content than the WHO-recommended premixed formulations, there is an urgent need to assess whether ONS provides better phosphorus repletion and minimizes the risk of RS.

This study aimed to compare gastrointestinal tolerance and the risk of refeeding syndrome in patients with complicated SAM treated with RTD-ONS and F-75/F-100. These findings provide essential evidence for alternative nutritional strategies in the management of SAM under five conditions, potentially improving treatment outcomes and addressing current challenges in implementing WHO-recommended feeding protocols in poor sanitation settings. This article is presented in accordance with the CONSORT Reporting Checklist.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

This open-label, randomized controlled trial was conducted between September 2019 and April 2021. All children aged 12–60 months with complicated SAM according to the WHO Health Organization criteria who visited Cipto Mangunkusumo National General Hospital and Fatmawati Hospital, Jakarta, and who had not received nutritional treatment for at least one month before recruitment were eligible for this study. Children aged 6–12 months old were excluded from this study because ONS is not indicated for children under one year old. Children diagnosed with malignancy were also excluded ad interim with protocol amendment, since these children had a high dropout rate due to poor general conditions, such as sepsis, gastrointestinal bleeding, diarrhea due to chemotherapy, and emergency surgical procedures.

The three intervention arms were F-75/F-100 (group A), high-energy RTD ONS (group B), and standard-energy RTD ONS (group C), along with the standard SAM treatment protocol. Since there was no reference study to determine the sample size, we conducted this pilot study and recruited 30 subjects per arm as the generally accepted minimal sample size for interventional studies, and added six additional subjects to anticipate a 20% dropout rate; thus, the sample size was 36 subjects for each arm. Randomization was performed by the Clinical Research Supporting Unit (of) Faculty of Medicine, University of Indonesia. Subjects from Dr. Cipto Mangunkusumo Hospital were randomized using block randomization of four with three intervention arms (2:1:1), while subjects from Fatmawati Hospital were randomized using block randomization of four with two intervention arms (2:2), as Fatmawati Hospital could not provide F-75/F-100. The randomization code for each participant was placed in a sealed envelope. When a subject was eligible for this study, the researchers opened the sealed envelope consecutively and assigned the subject to the given group.

Informed consent statement

Informed consent was obtained from all subjects involved in the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients for the publication of this paper.

Ethical statement

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Indonesia (KET-369/UN2.F1/ETIK/PPM.00.00/2019).

Study variables, data collection, and data quality assurance

Informed consent was obtained from all eligible subjects. A nasogastric tube was inserted in 99 of 109 (91.2%) subjects due to poor appetite, since subjects with complicated SAM had moderate to severe underlying disease. According to the WHO Guidelines for SAM management, children with an SAM intake of less than 80% should be given enteral nutrition [16]. Caregivers were trained to properly handle the home enteral nutrition, store and prepare the formula, and correctly position the child while feeding. In addition, complications due to improper use of the nasogastric tube have been explained. The subjects were hospitalized for various lengths of stay for training in home enteral nutrition and treatment of underlying medical conditions. After discharge, a home visit was conducted to ensure that the parents or caregivers were regularly administering the formula and to identify any problems related to home enteral nutrition. However, home visits were only conducted for participants enrolled before March 2020 because of the coronavirus pandemic. Participants enrolled after March 2020 were monitored using daily calls and/or text messages. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Indonesia.

On the first day of enrolment, trained physicians collected the subjects’ data using a structured research data form. Age; sex; socioeconomic status; parental education; ethnicity; number of siblings; nutritional intake before the intervention; length of stay; and complete physical examination, including height, weight, arm circumference, and head circumference, were also recorded. Weight and height were measured using a Seca® balance (GmbH & Co. KG) and a length board or a standing board. All measurements were performed twice if they showed exact results or three times if the results varied. If the latter occurred, the average of three measurements was used. WHO Anthro® software (www.who.int/childgrowth/software) was utilized to calculate the z-scores of weight-to-age, height-to-age, and weight-to-height for each subject. Blood samples were collected to measure initial complete blood counts and baseline electrolyte levels (sodium, potassium, chloride, ionized calcium, phosphate, and magnesium).

We calculated the recommended daily allowance (RDA) based on ideal weight (weight for height using the WHO growth chart). Feeding advancement was performed in accordance with the WHO Guideline on SAM [16]. During the stabilization-initial phase (first day), subjects were given 80–100 kcal/kg actual BW/day, which is equal to 50–75% of the RDA. Then, for the stabilization-transition phase (in this study, second day), 100–150 kcal/kg actual BW/day, which corresponds to 75–100% of the RDA; for the rehabilitation phase (in this study, third day onwards), 150-220 kcal/kg actual BW/day, which is equal to 100% of the RDA [8]. The actual daily intake was recorded by calculating the total volume of formula administered in a day and subtracting the volume of vomiting in a day, if any. The frequency of defecation was recorded daily, and stool consistency was evaluated using the Bristol stool chart. Diarrhea due to GI intolerance was defined as a change in consistency and an increase in the frequency of defecation at least three times per day, 48 hours after formula administration. Follow-ups were performed daily to ensure that the forms were completed by parents or guardians.

On the third day of the intervention, blood electrolytes were retested to evaluate the risk of refeeding syndrome (RS) as the secondary outcome of the study. Refeeding syndrome is defined as a minimum 10% reduction in serum potassium, magnesium, and/or phosphate levels below the lowest reference range, with or without organ dysfunction, and occurs within five days after energy reinitiation [19]. Based on the ASPEN consensus recommendation, RS was categorized as mild (10–20% reduction), moderate (20–30% reduction), or severe (>30% reduction and/or organ dysfunction due to a decrease in electrolyte and/or thiamine deficiency) [21]. After 14 days of intervention, physicians measured the subjects’ anthropometric status and completed the GI tolerance questionnaire in an outpatient clinic.

The Clinical Research Supporting Unit Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Indonesia, monitored the study to ensure quality. The progress in this trial, including protocol deviations and adverse events, has been reported. Routine verification of the completed trial master file, data safety monitoring board, and recruitment tracking logs was also performed.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted on using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 20.0 (IBM Co.) for Windows with the appropriate test. The results of the statistical tests were considered significant at p-value <0.05. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to analyze differences in vomiting frequency and diarrhea duration among the three groups. The incidence of RS was analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. The difference between weight increments among groups was analyzed using ANOVA, followed by a post-hoc analysis was performed for significant results using the Mann-Whitney test. Total intake analysis was performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by post-hoc analysis using the Mann-Whitney test.

One hundred and eight subjects were recruited for this study (36 children in each arm). After 14 days of the intervention, 30 patients in the F-75/F-100 group, 33 in the high-energy ONS group, and 29 in the standard-energy ONS group completed the study. Most patients had a low socioeconomic status, whereas a few had a middle-low socioeconomic status. The length of stay varied from one to eight days, depending on the subjects’ medical conditions and comorbidities. The baseline anthropometric indices of the subjects, including the weight-for-age z-score (WAZ), height-for-age z-score (HAZ), weight-for-height z-score (WHZ), and mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC), were similar in all groups.

Gastrointestinal tolerance

The gastrointestinal side effects were minimal and did not differ significantly among the three groups (Table 3). The Bristol stool scores for the F-75/F-100, standard-, and high-energy ONS groups were not significantly different (p=0.322). We found no significant differences in the frequency of diarrhea among the three groups. The duration of diarrhea in the 15 subjects is presented in Fig. 2, which also shows no statistical difference among the three groups (p=0.733). The severity of vomiting, classified based on the frequency and volume of vomiting, was recorded and showed that there was no difference in gastrointestinal tolerance among all groups (p=0.362) (Table 3).

Refeeding syndrome

Refeeding syndrome was detected in eight of the 108 subjects, all of whom were asymptomatic. The incidence of RS was higher in the F-75/F-100 group (5 of 36 subjects) than in the standard-energy ONS group (2 of 36 subjects) and high-energy ONS groups (1 of 36 subjects). One case of severe RS developed in the F-75/F-100 group, in addition to three cases of mild RS and one case of moderate RS. In the high-energy and standard-energy ONS groups, only one case of mild RS and two cases of moderate RS were detected. However, the incidence of RS was not significantly different among the three groups (p=0.169).

The patient who experienced severe refeeding syndrome manifested severe hypophosphatemia and mild hypokalemia without any clinical symptoms. The baseline phosphate level was 2.4 mEq/L, and the potassium was 3.9 mEq/L when she started F-75 at 72% of the RDA. The baseline blood glucose level was normal (110 mg/dL). On day 2, the intake was increased to F-75 at 90% of the RDA, and the patient showed good gastrointestinal tolerance. On day 3, the electrolyte test showed a decrease in phosphate to 0.9 mEq/L and potassium to 2.8 mEq/L. The vital signs and electrocardiographic findings were normal. Hypophosphatemia and hypokalemia were corrected with intravenous phosphate and potassium. On day 4, the phosphate electrolyte level increased to 3.2 mEq/L, and the potassium level was normal, i.e., 3.7 mEq/L. An oral phosphate preparation of 1 mmoL/kg/day was then administered as maintenance, and F-100 was administered with 100% RDA. The patients were discharged on day 6 in good condition. Home enteral nutrition was continued, and the subjects showed a good weight gain of 5.9 g/kg/day after 2 weeks of intervention.

Nutritional status

Data on weight, length/height, and MUAC were collected before and 14 days after the intervention. The baseline WAZ, HAZ, and WHZ/MUAC showed no differences among the three groups. After 14 days of intervention, there was a significant difference in weight gain (p=0.001). The high energy ONS group showed the highest weight increment (8.5±3.2 g/kg/day), while the standard energy ONS and F-75/F-100 group achieved 7.8±2.7 g/kg/day and 5.2±4.4 g/kg/day, respectively, with p=0.001. A significant difference in the post-intervention WHZ was also observed among the three groups (p=0.001). The detailed analysis for nutritional status could be found in the Supplementary Table 1.

Comparison of total intake

Calorie intake was significantly higher in the high-energy and standard-energy ONS groups than in the F-75/F-100 group during hospitalization. After discharge, the subjects continued enteral nutrition administration at home until 14 days after the intervention, and their daily intake was recorded. The intake during home treatment and total intake did not differ among the three groups. Actual intake was measured by reducing the total intake (mL/day) by the volume of vomit (mL/day), and the result was compared to the prescribed amount (mL/day) and expressed as a percentage. There was no significant difference in the actual intake (%) of the three groups, which was 95.35 (87.38–99.10), 98.00 (93.40–100), and 96.50 (91.95–99.25) in F-75/F-100, high-energy ONS, and standard-energy ONS groups, respectively (p=0.147). The detailed report for actual intake could be found in the Supplementary Table 2.

DISCUSSION

The current WHO SAM treatment recommendation is to use F-75/F-100 in the stabilization phase and RUTF in the rehabilitation phase [8]. However, RUTF is not widely available in Indonesia, and studies on its acceptability and efficacy are limited. A large trial in Indonesia, published in 2023, reported low compliance (70.9%) and a high dropout rate (40%) within the first week of SAM treatment using RUTF [22]. Another study in Uganda reported a similarly low success rate (65%) with a structured transition from F-75 to RUTF [23]. Consequently, F-100 is still used for home-based rehabilitation in Indonesia despite concerns regarding microbial contamination and improper mixing due to poor sanitation and low education levels among caregivers.

This study found no statistically significant differences in the incidence of RS among the three groups. However, the incidence of RS was higher in the F-75/F-100 group, likely due to the low phosphorus content in the hospital-made F-75. The highest weight increment was observed in the high-energy ONS group (8.5±3.2 g/kg/day), followed by the standard-energy ONS group (7.8±2.7 g/kg/day) and the F-75/F-100 group (5.2±4.4 g/kg/day, p=0.001). Post-hoc analysis confirmed significant differences in weight gain between the F-75/F-100 and ONS groups.

This study found a higher incidence of RS in the F-75/F-100 group than in the ONS group, although the difference was not statistically significant, probably due to the relatively small sample size. The reintroduction of calories must be gradually increased to prevent the refeeding syndrome [16]. The higher incidence of RS in the F-75/F-100 group was most likely due to the low phosphorus content (175 mg/L) in the hospital-made F-75 compared to 560 mg phosphorus/L of premixed F-75, as recommended by the WHO and UN specifications [161819]. This combination of a high proportion of energy from carbohydrates and low phosphorus content may be problematic [17]. Studies in children with SAM have reported low blood phosphate levels at admission and a further decline during the stabilization phase, which is associated with increased mortality [24252627]. A study in Uganda showed that F-75/F-100 with the new UN specifications (with added extrinsic phosphorus) yielded a more stable phosphate profile on day 3 than rice porridge, which contains low phosphate [28]. This finding suggests that the ingredients of hospital-made F-75 require the addition of extrinsic phosphorus to meet the phosphate requirements of SAM.

Contrary to concerns regarding higher protein content in ONS, this study showed that ONS did not increase the risk of refeeding hypophosphatemia. This contrasts with findings in preterm infants receiving aggressive parenteral nutrition, where hypophosphatemia was linked to high protein intake without sufficient phosphorus [29]. The much higher phosphate content in ONS (223.5 mg/100 mL in high-energy ONS and 153 mg/100 mL in standard-energy ONS) compared to that in the hospital-made F-75 (17.5 mg/100 mL) likely contributed to this result [16].

Diarrhea is a common issue in SAM due to villous atrophy and carbohydrate intolerance. Villous atrophy, resulting in carbohydrate intolerance and diarrhea, has been reported in patients with SAM. Water and additional glucose supplied from formula intake may increase fluid loss. This complication can be closely monitored and immediately treated by inpatients [8]. This study found no significant differences in stool consistency or incidence of diarrhea among the three groups. This is likely due to the comparable osmolarity levels in formulas F-75 (413 mOsm/L), F-100 (419 mOsm/L), high-energy ONS (440 mOsm/L), and standard-energy ONS (285 mOsm/L).

Iron supplementation during the stabilization phase is generally avoided because of the risk of bacterial overgrowth. However, the iron content in ONS follows the Codex Alimentarius standards (approximately 1 mg/100 kcal), making it safe to administer in the formula for SAM treatment. No signs of infection were observed following ONS administration.

Weight gain was the highest in the high-energy ONS group, likely due to better intake during hospitalization. The highest intake was observed in the high-energy ONS group, followed by the standard-energy ONS and F-75/F-100 groups. The caloric density of high-energy ONS (1.5 kcal/mL) allows for lower-volume administration, reducing the risk of vomiting. The use of high-energy ONS provides the advantage of a lower volume for achieving isocaloric feeding. In addition to slightly poorer feeding tolerance, home administration of F-75/F-100 carries the risk of an inconsistent composition, resulting in a lower calorie intake.

A current randomized trial in Indonesia comparing five RUTF types reported weight gain of only 1.3–1.6 g/kg/day, much lower than the WHO target of 5.0 g/kg/day [23]. Poor compliance with RUTF, especially in the standard and soy-based RUTF groups, contributed to low weight gain. The greater weight gain observed in this study suggests that ONS may be a viable alternative for managing SAM in outpatient settings.

This study highlights the need for safer and more practical alternatives to hospital-made F-75/F-100. The higher phosphate content in ONS supports its potential to reduce refeeding hypophosphatemia, which is a critical issue in SAM treatment. These findings suggest that hospital-made F-75 should be fortified with extrinsic phosphorus to meet the WHO recommendations to prevent refeeding syndrome.

Cost analysis

Cost is an important aspect in decision-making for nutritional management in SAM. We attempted to calculate the costs based on our study findings. Taking a case of a child’s ideal weight of 10 kgs and caloric requirement of 1,000 kcal/day for the rehabilitation phase, the estimated 14-day cost of treatment (USD) was 24.78 for F-100, 81.06 for RTD ONS 1.5 kcal/mL, and 121.52 for RTD ONS 1 kcal/mL (Table 4). When calculated based on WHZ gain over 14 days, the cost per 1 SD increment (USD) was 30.97, 60.49, and 95.69 for F-100, RTD ONS 1.5 kcal/mL, and RTD ONS 1 kcal/mL, respectively. Compared to F-100, the cost for RTD ONS 1.5 kcal/mL was twice as high, while RTD ONS 1 kcal/mL was three times as high. These findings suggest that while all products improved nutritional status as required by WHO, which is 5–10 g/kg/day for SAM, F-100 is the most affordable option. However, the use of RTD ONS might be considered in poor sanitation areas or immunocompromised patients. In addition to that, the faster recovery of SAM achieved by RTD ONS might be needed in cases of SAM with moderate-severe underlying diseases. Currently, WHO still recommend F-75/F-100 as the gold standard for inpatient SAM management.

Strengths and limitations

The strength of this study lies in its rigorous methodology, which was conducted at a National Central Public Hospital with a high standard of clinical care and research. Unlike other studies on nutritional interventions for SAM in community settings, more than 90% of the subjects in this study received nasogastric tube feeding in keeping with the WHO guidelines, which recommend enteral administration in SAM cases with less than 80% formula intake. The enteral nutrition route ensured an accurate calculation of intake, thereby minimizing variability and bias in intake recording. This study also describes a simple cost analysis based on the results, which helps provide insights into the comparison of costs among the three nutritional interventions.

Since this was a pilot study, a key limitation was the relatively small sample size (36 subjects per group), which may have resulted in low power for detecting smaller effects. We did not perform a formal sample size calculation because there were no references to previous studies. Based on the results of this pilot study, a precise sample size calculation should be performed in future studies. Another limitation was that the study population consisted of children with complicated SAM and moderate-to-severe underlying diseases. This limits the generalizability of our findings to children with uncomplicated SAM. Future research should replicate this study in children with SAM without significant comorbidities. Furthermore, the open-label design, short study time frame, and lack of biochemical workup might have influenced the results of our study and must be addressed in future studies.

Conclusions

The use of ONS for SAM treatment is safe, as demonstrated by the similar incidence of refeeding syndrome and gastrointestinal tolerance profile compared with F-75/F-100. High-energy ONS may be beneficial because of its higher caloric density, which allows a lower volume to achieve isocaloric intake. Lower osmolarity ONS (<300 mOsm/L) also led to better gastrointestinal tolerance than the higher osmolarity F-100 (419 mOsm/L). This pilot study has several limitations, including a small sample size, a specific population of SAM that limits generalizability, an open-label design, a short study time frame, and a lack of biochemical workup. Therefore, larger studies addressing these issues are required to confirm the results of this study. Cost analysis showed that RTD ONS 1.5 kcal/mL is twice as expensive as F-100 to increase 1 SD of WHZ for 2 weeks of therapy, but provided faster WHZ gain. Given the significantly higher price of RTD ONS, their routine use in low-resource settings should be carefully evaluated against locally available and affordable alternatives, such as F-75/F-100, which remains the gold standard for SAM treatment. This study provides preliminary data to inform policymakers in providing therapeutic food for children with complicated SAM who have moderate to severe underlying diseases and live in poor sanitation environments.
