Clinical outcomes of thoracic radiotherapy in combination with chemoimmunotherapy in elderly patients with extensive‐stage small cell lung cancer
Authors: Jinmin Han, Wencai Zheng, Jie Jiang, Huan Shi, Chengxin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeWe evaluated the effect of thoracic radiotherapy (TRT) on extensive‐stage small‐cell lung cancer (ES‐SCLC) in elderly patients (aged ≥ 60 years) in the era of immunotherapy (IO) and analyzed the prognostic factors to guide personalized treatment.MethodsThe clinical data of 178 elderly patients were reviewed. Kaplan‐Meier curves were generated to estimate the survival rates. Multivariate prognostic analyses were performed using Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsThe mOS in the CRT‐IO group was superior to that in the CT‐IO group (22.0 vs. 14.7 months, p = 0.014). In addition, the mPFS in the CRT‐IO group was significantly longer than that in the CT‐IO group (11.2 vs. 7.0 months, p = 0.023). The mOS in the CRT‐IO group was better than that in the CRT group; however, the difference was not statistically significant (22.0 vs. 17.3 months, p = 0.103). Overall survival was the longest in the CRT‐IO group, followed by the CRT and CT‐IO groups.Patients in the low‐dose group had longer survival times than those in the high‐dose group; however, this difference was not statistically significant (all p > 0.05).Multivariate analysis suggested that a KPS (Karnofsky performance status) score ≥ 80 (p = 0.003) was a good predictor of longer OS, whereas liver metastasis (p = 0.004) was associated with shorter OS.ConclusionsCRT‐IO should be prioritized as a treatment for elderly patients with ES‐SCLC with good performance status and liver metastasis. However, CRT could be a viable option for patients with a compromised performance status or for whom economic factors are a concern.

INTRODUCTION

Small‐cell lung cancer (SCLC) accounts for approximately 15% of all lung cancers and is characterized by its aggressive nature, rapid growth, and early metastasis, which contribute to its poor prognosis. 1 , 2 Approximately 65% of patients have extensive‐stage disease at the initial diagnosis. 3 With improvements in living standards and population aging, the proportion of elderly patients with SCLC has gradually increased, representing a substantial challenge in geriatric oncology. 4

Currently, chemoimmunotherapy (CT‐IO) is the first‐line treatment for extensive‐stage small‐cell lung cancer (ES‐SCLC) based on the IMpower133 and CASPIAN randomized trials. 5 , 6 In addition, the CAPSTONE‐1 and ASTRUM‐005 studies have confirmed that the introduction of immunotherapy (IO) could benefit patients. 7 , 8 Recently, multiple prospective studies and retrospective analyses have demonstrated that thoracic radiotherapy (TRT) can significantly decrease disease progression and increase survival in patients with ES‐SCLC; however, it has not been uniformly adopted worldwide. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 The ASTRO 2020 guideline strongly recommends TRT for patients with ES‐SCLC who achieve a response to chemotherapy (CHT). 18 Currently, no consensus exists on whether elderly patients will benefit from TRT in this era of IO.

In clinical practice, elderly patients with ES‐SCLC are less likely to receive antineoplastic therapy owing to age, comorbidities, fear of toxicity, and uncertainty regarding the benefits. TRT is commonly used as a palliative treatment to relieve symptoms at local or distant metastatic sites. Two published studies reported that elderly patients are likely to have worse performance status and more treatment‐related toxicities. 19 , 20 Large‐scale clinical studies targeting elderly patients are urgently needed to determine optimal treatment modalities. Therefore, we assessed the impact of TRT on the prognosis of ES‐SCLC in elderly patients in the era of IO and identified potential subgroups of patients who would benefit from TRT by analyzing predictive factors in elderly patients to develop a reasonable and optimized comprehensive treatment strategy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient characteristics and study design

Our retrospective study included patients aged 60 years or older with ES‐SCLC, according to the Veterans Lung Cancer Association (VALG) classification system, who were treated at Shandong Cancer Hospital and Institute from January 2017 to February 2020. Based on the administration of individualized treatment, patients were categorized into two groups, namely, the IO and CRT groups. The IO group was further divided into the chemoradioimmunotherapy (CRT‐IO) and CT‐IO groups. Patients who received only CHT were not included in our study because several studies have confirmed that their outcomes were worse than those of patients who received CRT. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with extensive‐stage disease confirmed by cytological or pathological diagnosis and imaging examinations at the time of diagnosis, (2) those with a Karnofsky performance status (KPS) score ≥ 70, (3) no history of surgery, CHT, radiotherapy, or IO, and (4) patients with complete clinical data. In addition, patients who received fewer than two cycles of treatment or whose data were incomplete were excluded. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong Cancer Hospital. Written informed consent was not required owing to the retrospective nature of our study.

Treatment strategy

All patients who received TRT underwent three‐dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3D‐CRT) or intensity‐modulated radiotherapy (IMRT). The gross tumor volume (GTV) was delineated based on contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) images and was defined as the primary lung tumor and positive lymph nodes. The clinical target volume (CTV) was defined as the GTV uniformly expanded by 5–8 mm with the addition of high‐risk lymph node drainage areas. The planning target volume (PTV) was defined as the GTV uniformly expanded by 5–10 mm in all directions. All patients received a total radiation dose of 30–60 Gy. The dose was determined by radiation oncologists, as no unified dose is recommended in any of the guidelines. However, the selection of immunotherapeutic agents has been inconsistent. The programmed death‐1 (PD‐1) and programmed death‐ligand‐1 (PD‐L1) inhibitors used in this study were pembrolizumab, nivolumab, and atezolizumab. The chemotherapy regimens were primarily platinum‐based, with etoposide combined with platinum being the most common regimen. The dosage of the CHT drugs was appropriately reduced based on the patient's physical condition. Other regimens included irinotecan and platinum. The prophylactic cranial irradiation (PCI) doses were 25 Gy/10 fractions or 30 Gy/10 fractions. After disease progression, second‐line treatments were administered at the discretion of the clinician.

Statistical analysis

Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from the date of diagnosis to death from any cause or the date of the last follow‐up. Progression‐free survival (PFS) was defined as the period from the date of diagnosis to the date of disease progression or death due to any cause. The primary endpoint of the study was OS, and the secondary endpoints were PFS and adverse reactions. Pearson's chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test was used to determine differences in baseline characteristics. Survival analyses were conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method, and comparisons between groups were made using log‐rank tests. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed using Cox proportional hazard models. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Efficacy evaluation and adverse reaction evaluation

Tumor response was assessed using the Response Evaluation Criteria for Solid Tumors (RECIST) criteria. During treatment, the efficacy was evaluated every two cycles when feasible. Adverse events were recorded and graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events Version 4.0 (CTCAE v4.0).

RESULTS

Clinical characteristics of enrolled patients

The study included patients with ES‐SCLC, who were divided into two groups based on the treatment they received. In total, 178 patients were included, of whom 51 (28.7%) underwent IO, and 127 (71.3%) underwent CRT. The median age was 65 years (range 60 to 79), with 10 (5.6%) patients aged ≥ 75 years. Most were male (n = 137, 77%), and 41 were female (23%). The baseline characteristics of the patients were well balanced between the two groups, with no significant differences in age, sex, or KPS score, et al. (p > 0.05). Survival analysis

The median follow‐up duration for the entire group was 45.3 months. The median OS (mOS) was longer in the IO group than in the CRT group; however, the difference was not significant (20.2 vs. 17.3 months, p = 0.850). Survival analysis revealed that patients who received IO had a similar median PFS (mPFS) to patients who received CRT in the first‐line setting (8.93 vs. 9.00 months, p = 0.207), indicating that patients who received IO tended to have longer survival than did those who received CRT; however, the difference was not statistically significant, as shown in Figure 1.

We subsequently analyzed the patients in the IO group in greater detail. The mOS in the CRT‐IO group was higher than that in the CT‐IO group (22.0 vs. 14.7 months, p = 0.014). Furthermore, mPFS in the CRT‐IO group was significantly longer than that in the CT‐IO group (11.2 vs. 7.0 months, p = 0.023). The addition of TRT in the context of IO was beneficial for both PFS and OS, suggesting that the combination of CRT and IO may confer a survival advantage in elderly patients with ES‐SCLC, as shown in Figure 2. In addition, the mOS in the CRT‐IO group was better than that in the CRT group; however, the difference was not statistically significant (22.0 vs. 17.3 months, p = 0.103). Notably, the mPFS of the CRT‐IO group was longer than in the CRT group (11.2 vs. 9.0 months, p = 0.027) (Figure 3).

Furthermore, 24 patients received maintenance immunotherapy (IO maintenance), whereas 27 patients did not. Encouragingly, patients receiving maintenance IO had longer mPFS (11.2 vs. 7.1 months, p = 0.004) and mOS (26.2 vs. 13.9 months, P < 0.001) than their counterparts did, as shown in Figure 6. In addition, we analyzed the impact of sex and weight change on survival. Compared with men, women had longer mOS (24.1 vs. 16.4 months, p = 0.033) and mPFS (10.0 vs. 8.70 months, p = 0.192). Univariate and multivariate Cox analyses

The prognostic factors associated with favorable OS were analyzed in this study. Univariate analysis revealed that male sex, a KPS score < 80, a smoking index >400, ≥ 2 metastases, and the presence of liver metastasis and bone metastasis were associated with shorter OS (P < 0.05), and that the CRT‐IO group had longer survival than the CT‐IO group. Factors with statistical significance were included in the multivariate analysis model, and potential confounders were adjusted for, revealing that a KPS score ≥ 80 (p = 0.003) was a good predictor of longer OS, whereas liver metastasis (p = 0.004) was associated with shorter OS. This analysis confirmed that the CRT‐IO was an independent predictor of OS (p = 0.049). This finding indicates that, in addition to clinical factors, CRT‐IO plays a crucial role in determining survival outcomes in elderly patients with ES‐SCLC. The details are presented in Table 3.

Response to treatment and adverse reactions

The ORRs in the three groups were 66.7%, 59.3%, and 59.1% (p = 0.780), respectively. Grade ≥ 3 myelosuppression occurred in 15 (29.4%) patients and 31 (24.4%) patients in the IO group and the CRT group, respectively (p = 0.491). Further analysis revealed that grade 3 myelosuppression occurred in 7 (29.6%) and 8 (29.2%) patients in the CRT‐IO and CT‐IO groups, respectively. No significant difference was noted between the two groups (p = 0.971).

With regard to gastrointestinal reactions, grade ≥ 2 reactions occurred in 13 (25.5%) patients in the IO group and 26 (20.5%) patients (p = 0.464) in the CRT group. In the IO group, more patients in the CRT‐IO group than in the CT‐IO group had reactions greater than grade 2 (29.2% vs. 22.2%; p = 0.570).

Given the limited number of patients in our study and the fact that both radiation‐ and immune‐related pneumonitis share certain common risk factors and clinical manifestations, 23 we combined these patients with those with pneumonitis in our analysis. Three patients developed grade ≥ 2 pneumonitis in each group (12.5% vs. 11.1%), whereas in the CRT group, there were 15 patients (11.8%). Overall, the CRT‐IO group experienced the most adverse events; however, this difference was not statistically significant. Most of these side effects were manageable, and no significant differences in treatment‐related mortality were observed across the groups.

DISCUSSION

Historically, the outcomes for patients with ES‐SCLC have been poor, with a 5‐year overall survival rate of less than 7%. 24 However, recent advances in first‐line treatment, particularly the introduction of IO in combination with CHT, have significantly improved survival outcomes. This new standard of care has led to a longer survival with an acceptable safety profile. 2 Recent studies have demonstrated that the addition of IO to the standard CHT can improve the OS of patients with ES‐SCLC. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 However, the impact of these treatment modalities, particularly in older patients (≥ 60 years), remains an area of active investigation. Treatment options may be limited owing to comorbidities and reduced tolerance to aggressive therapies. Previous studies have demonstrated that TRT can improve the survival of patients with ES‐SCLC; however, there is insufficient evidence to prove whether TRT can improve the survival of elderly patients with ES‐SCLC in the era of IO.

We summarized the clinical data of patients aged ≥ 60 years who were initially diagnosed with ES‐SCLC. The results demonstrated that adding TRT to IO administered to elderly patients with ES‐SCLC improved the mOS (22.0 vs. 14.7 months, p = 0.014) and mPFS (11.2 vs. 7.0 months, p = 0.023), with no increase in adverse reactions. A systematic review and meta‐analysis of clinical trials involving older patients (≥ 65 years) with ES‐SCLC revealed that the combination of CHT and IO significantly improved OS, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 0.80 (95% CI: 0.72–0.90), indicating a 20% reduction in mortality. 25 This benefit was consistent with the findings from pivotal phase III trials such as the IMpower133 and CASPIAN study, 5 , 6 which demonstrated similar improvements in OS for the entire patient population. A real‐world study by Lamy et al. revealed that TRT was significantly associated with a better PFS and OS, suggesting that TRT may also be beneficial when combined with CT‐IO. 26 Furthermore, patients treated with CRT‐IO had superior mPFS than those who received CRT. Compared with the CRT group, the CRT‐IO group demonstrated longer survival, although the difference was not statistically significant. Moreover, no significant difference was observed in mOS or mPFS between the IO and CRT groups. Overall, survival was the longest in the CRT‐IO group, followed by the CRT and CT‐IO groups. In terms of efficacy, although no significant difference was present, the ORR was also the highest in the CRT‐IO group, whereas the ORR in the CT‐IO group was similar to that in the CRT group (66.7% vs. 59.3% vs. 59.1%). A comparison of the survival rates and ORRs among the three groups indicated that TRT is a viable option. Moreover, patients who received maintenance IO had longer mOS and mPFS than nonrecipients did, and the differences were statistically significant. In summary, CRT‐IO could be selected for elderly individuals if the patient's physical status permits it, whereas CRT could be considered if the patient has economic constraints. These findings support the use of multimodal treatment approaches in elderly patients with ES‐SCLC, who often have poor outcomes due to the aggressive nature of the disease.

We explored the appropriate TRT timing and optimal dose/fraction for the population that received CRT‐IO. Patients who received early radiotherapy had significantly longer mPFS than those who received late radiotherapy, and this difference was statistically significant. In addition, a trend toward a longer mOS was present in the early radiotherapy group. These findings suggest that incorporating radiotherapy earlier in the treatment course may be beneficial for patient survival. This finding was supported by a study published by Zhao et al. in patients with LS‐SCLC, 27 and similar conclusions were reached in a study involving ES‐SCLC patients; 22 however, further validation is needed in the future. When combined with immunotherapy, early radiotherapy may enhance therapeutic efficacy. Early radiotherapy can potentially induce a stronger antitumor immune response by releasing tumor antigens and modulating the tumor microenvironment, which may synergize with immunotherapy to improve patient outcomes. However, the optimal timing for radiotherapy in combination with immunotherapy remains to be fully elucidated and may vary depending on the specific cancer type and treatment regimen. Our findings should be interpreted with caution because of the limited number of patients included. Patients in the low‐dose group had longer survival times than those in the high‐dose group; however, this difference was not statistically significant. Several possible explanations exist for this observation. First, some patients in the low‐dose group underwent hyperfractionated radiotherapy. These findings suggest that hyperfractionated radiotherapy is the preferred treatment option for patients with ES‐SCLC. Studies have demonstrated that hyperfractionated radiotherapy can potentially enhance the therapeutic effects on certain cancer types.22, 28 Secondly, when radiotherapy is combined with IO, high‐dose radiotherapy may increase the risk of toxic side effects, which may be detrimental to patient survival. In addition, higher radiation doses can lead to increased immune‐related toxicity. 29 This may negatively affect the survival benefit conferred by the combined treatment. Finally, the results should be interpreted with caution due to the limited number of patients included in our study. Future studies with larger cohorts are needed to confirm these findings and better understand the optimal dosing and scheduling of radiotherapy in combination with IO for patients with ES‐SCLC.

In addition, we conducted univariate and multivariate analyses of data from elderly patients with ES‐SCLC. The results revealed that the KPS score and liver metastasis status were independent prognostic factors affecting patient survival. Several studies have reported that age is an independent prognostic factor for SCLC. 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 However, age was not an independent prognostic factor in our study, which is inconsistent with the results of the four articles mentioned above. This discrepancy may be related to the relatively small number of patients included in the study. In addition, 5.6% of patients were aged ≥ 75 years. In general, patients over 75 years of age are less likely to receive high‐intensity combined radiotherapy and CHT. Treatment may be abandoned because of factors such as low bone marrow, liver, and kidney reserve capacity, poor tolerance to treatment, and poor ability to clear or metabolize drugs. Notably, our study revealed that the KPS was an independent prognostic factor in elderly patients with ES‐SCLC. We assumed that the KPS score better reflected the general state of elderly individuals and their tolerance to treatment. Therefore, for elderly patients with ES‐SCLC, we should not limit our treatment plan based on age alone but rather guide treatment based on KPS scores. If elderly patients are in good physical condition and have relatively good organ function, they should undergo standard combined CHT. If tolerated, TRT alone or in combination with IO can be administered.

Moreover, previous studies 14 , 34 have confirmed that the location and number of metastases in patients with ES‐SCLC are related to the prognosis. Our results revealed that among all elderly patients with ES‐SCLC, those without liver metastasis had better outcomes, which was consistent with the findings of the study published by Lu et al. 33 Ren et al. concluded that liver metastasis was the worst prognostic factor for ES‐SCLC. According to the RTOG 0937 study, 11 patients without liver metastasis had better survival rates, similar to the results of our study. A single‐center study that included 394 patients with ES‐SCLC 35 reached a similar conclusion. The improvement in the survival rate of patients with brain metastases may be partly attributed to the widespread application of cranial MRI in clinical practice and early local treatment of patients with brain metastases. Therefore, active treatment may be necessary for elderly patients with ES‐SCLC with better KPS scores and liver metastasis. Currently, the prognostic impact of sex in elderly patients is unclear. Certain studies have reported that women have better outcomes than men. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 36 Our study confirmed that women had a longer mOS than men in the univariate analyses; however, no significant difference was present in the multivariate analyses (p = 0.344). The impact of the smoking index on the survival of elderly patients is still controversial. Our study revealed that 52.8% of the elderly patients had a long‐term and heavy smoking history. Although these data demonstrated that the smoking index had no impact on patient prognosis in multivariate analyses, it is still necessary to guide patients to quit smoking based on the findings of previous studies. 37 , 38 In addition, patients who did not experience weight loss tended to have a longer mOS than those who experienced weight loss; however, this difference was not statistically significant. This may be because the sample size of the included patients was small and did not adequately reflect the survival time.

In conclusion, the combined treatment regimen of CHT, TRT, and IO offers clinical benefits to elderly patients with ES‐SCLC. These findings suggest that integrating TRT with CHT and IO can effectively reduce the risk of treatment failure and improve the survival of elderly patients with ES‐SCLC. In addition, these findings support the notion that elderly patients with good performance status may benefit significantly from CRT‐IO, whereas those with a compromised status may find less‐intensive regimens valuable. Overall, this study provides crucial evidence to guide clinical decision‐making and optimize treatment strategies for elderly patients, emphasizing the need for tailored approaches to maximize treatment efficacy and quality of life. However, this study has several limitations. First, the use of multivariate analysis could not eliminate the risk of selection bias inherent to retrospective studies (e.g., unbalanced basic cardiopulmonary function and treatment plans). Second, the small sample size may not fully represent all elderly patients with ES‐SCLC. Third, the doses and regimens of TRT used in this study differed, and the impact of the radiotherapy dose on efficacy was not analyzed. This study did not analyze the comorbidities that may have affected survival. Therefore, the role of TRT in elderly patients with ES‐SCLC in the era of IO needs to be confirmed in large‐sample prospective randomized controlled studies. Future research should focus on optimizing treatment protocols and addressing the unique needs of elderly patients to further advance clinical treatment strategies. In addition, exploring the optimal sequencing and dose of treatments, as well as identifying biomarkers that predict the response to therapy, will help refine treatment protocols and improve outcomes.

CRT‐IO should be prioritized as a treatment strategy in elderly patients with ES‐SCLC with good performance status and liver metastasis owing to its potential benefits. However, for those with compromised performance status or for whom economic factors are a concern, CRT could be a viable option. Future research on comprehensive treatment plans for elderly patients with ES‐SCLC should be strengthened to maximize patient benefits. Further studies are needed to better understand the long‐term outcomes and quality of life in older patients receiving these therapies.
