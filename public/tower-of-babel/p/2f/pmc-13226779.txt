Engineering the probiotic Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 as a host organism for coliphage production
Authors: Hailin Zhang, Ru Zhu, Ruoting He, Yuran Zhang, Ji Luan, Yuqing Liu, Youming Zhang, Hailong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacteriophage therapy emerges as a pivotal alternative in the ongoing battle against antibiotic-resistant bacteria. Employing probiotic bacteria as hosts for bacteriophage production bestows several notable advantages compared to traditional pathogenic hosts. These advantages encompass an enhanced safety profile, streamlined downstream processing, and greater regulatory acceptance. In this study, we engineered Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN), a non-pathogenic probiotic strain, to serve as a safe chassis host for efficient coliphage production. EcN is resistant to T1, T3, T5, and T7 phages and the T4 phage can only infect it at high titers. By eliminating all known phage defense systems in the genome, we constructed the EcN-9 strain that is susceptible to T3 and T7 phages. The infection efficiency of the T4 phage on the EcN-9 strain was 1000 times higher than that on the EcN-WT strain. Introduction of the appropriate FhuA receptor in EcN-9 rendered it susceptible to T1 and T5 phages. Optimizing the expression levels of FhuA led to a remarkable enhancement in the efficiency of plating, with increases of 3872-fold and 2593-fold for T1 and T5, respectively. Building upon this foundation, we further refined the phage production process by developing a fed-batch fermentation strategy, which not only substantially reduced the multiplicity of infection for inoculation to 0.1, but also resulted in high phage titers, reaching 4.47 × 1010 PFU mL−1 for T1, 9.21 × 1010 PFU mL−1 for T4, and 7.22 × 1010 PFU mL−1 for T5. This study provides a strategic framework for adapting preferred bacterial host systems to produce target phages.Keywords: Phage therapy, Phage production, Phage defense system, Phage receptor, Probiotic bacteria

Introduction

The resurgence of interest in bacteriophage (phage) therapy has emerged as a promising solution for combating multidrug-resistant bacterial infections, as global antibiotic resistance is projected to cause up to 10 million annual deaths by 2050 if no effective measures are taken [1,2]. However, the clinical translation of phage therapy faces significant manufacturing challenges, particularly regarding the safety profile of production systems. Due to the limited host range, current phage production predominantly utilizes pathogenic bacterial strains identical to clinical isolates targeted for therapy [[3], [4], [5]]. While this approach ensures phage virulence against specific pathogens, it introduces substantial biosafety risks throughout the manufacturing pipeline [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Therefore, using safe hosts to produce phage products will promote the development of phage therapy.

Non-pathogenic model bacteria are well-characterized and lack virulence factors, eliminating the risks of unintended infections during large-scale phage production. Moreover, these bacteria often have well-established genetic tools and metabolic pathways, enabling precise engineering to optimize phage yields [[10], [11], [12]]. However, the use of non-pathogenic hosts for phage production presents several limitations and challenges: (i) Non-pathogenic hosts may retain phage defense systems (such as CRISPR-Cas, restriction-modification (RM) systems, abortive infection mechanisms) that impair phage infection [[13], [14], [15]]. (ii) Low bacterial susceptibility to phages, often allowing infection by only a few phages. To address these limitations, strategies involve engineering host organisms, such as by deleting defense mechanisms or optimizing amplification-related genes, and selecting non-pathogenic hosts phylogenetically proximal to the phage natural host. Although recent advances in bioinformatics have facilitated the systematic identification of bacterial defense systems [[14], [15], [16]], strategies for efficient phage production via the systematic deletion of such defense systems remain largely unexplored.

Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) is a well-established probiotic strain that does not produce any enterotoxins or cytotoxins associated with pathogenic E. coli strains [17,18], and has been approved to be used in Europe for nearly 100 years to treat various gastrointestinal disorders [19]. Among all the strains of E. coli, only EcN has been identified to be non-pathogenic [20]. Here, we selected EcN as the chassis host to construct a phage-susceptible cell factory via the deletion of phage defense systems and introduction of heterologous receptors. Knockout of nine known phage defense systems in EcN resulted in a substantial improvement in the infection efficiency of phages T1, T4, and T5. Optimization of the expression of the heterologous FhuA receptor significantly enhanced the efficiency of plating (EOP) of phages T1 and T5. Ultimately, the infection efficiencies of T1, T4, and T5 in the recombinant EcN strains were comparable to those observed in their native host, E. coli DH10B. This study demonstrates that reprogramming bacterial susceptibility to phages by modulating phage defense systems and receptor expression can establish a biosafety-enhanced platform for the production of therapeutic phages.

Methods

Bacteria culture conditions

E. coli strains (Table S1) were cultured in LB medium at 30 °C or 37 °C supplemented with the appropriate antibiotics (15 μg mL−1 chloramphenicol, 100 μg mL−1 ampicillin, 15 μg mL−1 kanamycin, or 5 μg mL−1 tetracycline).

Measurement of phage titers

The titers of phages were determined using the double layer agar assay. The phage lysate, which was filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane (Millipore, cat. no. SLGPR33RB), was subjected to serial dilution. One hundred μL of diluted phage solutions was combined with 100 μL of the overnight culture of E. coli (OD600 = 3.0–4.0) and incubated at 37 °C for 5 min. The mixtures were blended with 8 mL of molten LB soft agar (0.7% wt/vol) and then plated onto LB agar plates. Following incubation at 37 °C for 4–8 h, the plaques were counted. The phage titers were calculated as plaque-forming units per mL (PFU mL−1).

Phage spot dilution assays

Two hundred μL of the overnight culture of E. coli was added to 12 mL of LB soft agar (0.7% agar, wt/vol) warmed at 52 °C, and poured onto an LB plate. Phages, at an initial concentration of 5 × 109 PFU mL−1, were subjected to serial dilution in a 10-fold gradient. Two μL of each dilution was added onto the solidified bacterial lawn. After incubation at 37 °C for 4–8 h, the plates were pictured using the Bio-Rad gel imaging system.

Construction of plasmids

The plasmid backbones and target genes were respectively amplified via PCR or overlap extension PCR using ApexHF HS DNA Polymerase (Accurate Biotechnology Co., Ltd., cat. no. AG12207) and the oligonucleotides listed in Table S2. The PCR products were purified with the TIANgel Purification Kit (TIANGEN, cat. no. DP219) after the agarose gel electrophoresis according to the manufacturer's instructions. The DNA on the column was eluted with ddH2O. Target gene fragments were subsequently cloned into BAC, p15A or pBR322 vectors via RecET-mediated linear‒linear homologous recombination in E. coli GB05-dir [21,22]. Recombinant plasmid-containing colonies were selected on LB agar plates supplemented with chloramphenicol or ampicillin. Correct recombinant plasmids were confirmed by restriction analysis and Sanger sequencing.

Knockout of endogenous plasmids pMUT1 and pMUT2 in EcN

The workflow for knockout of pMUT1 and pMUT2 in EcN via plasmid incompatibility and counter-selection was illustrated in Fig. S1. The kanR-sacB cassette was amplified via PCR using the p15A-kanR-sacB plasmid [23] as the template, the ApexHF HS DNA Polymerase (Accurate Biotechnology Co., Ltd., cat. no. AG12207) and the oligonucleotides listed in Table S3. The kanR-sacB cassette was seamlessly inserted downstream of the mobA gene in plasmid pMUT1 through recombineering in E. coli GB05-red cells [22], generating the pMUT1-sacB recombinant plasmid. Subsequently, the pMUT1-sacB plasmid was electroporated into EcN-WT and selected on LB agar plates containing kanamycin. Positive clones underwent serial passaging (5–12 generations) in antibiotic-supplemented LB liquid medium with 2-h intervals. Elimination of the pMUT1 plasmid was monitored by colony PCR analysis. Individual clones lacking pMUT1 were further cultured in LB medium containing 10% (wt/vol) sucrose (5–10 passages). Successful removal of the pMUT1-sacB plasmid was confirmed through colony PCR screening.

For pMUT2 elimination, a kanR-sacB cassette was integrated downstream of the relE gene in the pMUT2 plasmid via recombineering to construct the pMUT2-sacB recombinant plasmid. The same plasmid elimination procedure was performed as for elimination of pMUT1, and the EcN-1 strain lacking both endogenous plasmids was ultimately obtained.

Knockout of the phage defense systems in the EcN genome

The workflow for knockout of the phage defense systems in the EcN genome using recombineering and the Cre-lox site-specific recombination was illustrated in Fig. S4. The pR6K-lox71-cmR-lox66 plasmid was used as template to amplify the lox71-cmR-lox66 DNA fragment by PCR using ApexHF HS DNA Polymerase (Accurate Biotechnology Co., Ltd., cat. no. AG12207) and the oligonucleotides listed in Table S4. The lox71-cmR-lox66 fragment containing the 50–500 bp homology arms for recombineering at both ends. The pSC101-BAD-gbaA-ampR plasmid [24] was transformed into EcN mutant cells, and successful transformants were selected on LB plate containing ampicillin. The lox71-cmR-lox66 fragment was then integrated into the EcN chromosome through recombineering [22,25,26]. Putative recombinant clones were verified by colony PCR and DNA sequencing.

For Cre recombination, the pSC101-BAD-cre-tetR plasmid [27] was transformed into EcN recombinant cells carrying the lox71-cmR-lox66 fragment on the chromosome. A 40 μL of overnight culture was inoculated into 1.4 mL fresh LB medium with tetracycline and incubated at 30 °C with 950 rpm shaking for 2 h. Cre recombinase expression was induced by adding 35 μL 10% (wt/vol) l-arabinose solution (Sigma, cat. no. E003256-25G). After 5 h, the cultures were diluted 105-fold and plated on LB plate. After incubation at 30 °C for 16 h, the chloramphenicol-sensitive colonies were screened using double streaking. Successful excision of the chloramphenicol resistance gene was confirmed by PCR.

Deletion of the fhuANissle gene in EcN

The lox71-cmR-lox66 DNA fragment was used as template to amplify lox71-cmR-lox66 fragment carrying (∼500 bp) homology arms flanking fhuANissle by overlap extension PCR using ApexHF HS DNA Polymerase (Accurate Biotechnology Co., Ltd., cat. no. AG12207) and the oligonucleotides listed in Table S5. Deletion of the fhuANissle gene in EcN-9 was carried out as the procedure for knockout of the phage defense systems. Successful knockout of fhuANissle was confirmed by PCR.

Measurement of E. coli growth curves

The overnight culture of the EcN-1–9 strains was diluted to an OD600 of 3.0, and 1.5 mL was inoculated into 50 mL of LB medium containing appropriate antibiotics. The cultures were grown at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. The OD600 was measured each hour.

Growth curve analysis of the EcN-9-ΔfhuANissle strain was performed in 96-well microplates as follows: an appropriate volume of the overnight culture was diluted in 10 ml of LB medium containing appropriate antibiotics to an OD600 of 0.2 and dispensed into 96-well plates. The 96-well plates were placed into a TECAN Infinite 200 microplate reader, and the OD600 was measured every 15 min at 37 °C with agitation.

Measurement of phage adsorption efficiency

A 1.5 mL aliquot of an overnight E. coli culture (OD600 = 3.0–4.0) was inoculated into 50 mL LB broth supplemented with l-arabinose. Cells were cultured at 37 °C or 30 °C until reaching OD600 = 0.6–0.8. Phages at an MOI of 0.001were added into the culture. After 5 min incubation, a 1-mL aliquot was centrifuged at 8500×g for 2 min to pellet cell-phage complexes. The titers of phages in the supernatant were quantified. Adsorption efficiency was calculated as: [1 − (supernatant phage titer/initial phage titer)] × 100%.

Measurement of phage EOP values

The E. coli DH10B strain was used as the reference host. The phage EOP values were calculated as the ratio of phage titers obtained from other test hosts to those measured on the E. coli DH10B strain.

Fed-batch process for phage production

A 1.5 mL aliquot of an overnight E. coli culture (OD600 = 3.0–4.0) was inoculated into 50 mL LB medium supplemented with appropriate antibiotics, followed by incubation at 37 °C until reaching the log phase (OD600 = 0.6–0.8). Subsequently, phages at an MOI of 0.1 were added into the culture and the mixture was maintained at 37 °C for 4–5 h until peak phage titers were achieved. Cells and cellular debris were removed via centrifugation at 9500×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant of the phage lysate was added into the cell pellet of 50 mL E. coli culture at log phase and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 5 h (1st feeding). Then, cells and cellular debris were removed again via centrifugation and the supernatant of the phage lysate was added into the cell pellet of 50 mL E. coli culture at log phase to incubate at 37 °C for 5 h (2nd feeding). The feeding procedure was iteratively performed 4 rounds. The phage titers were quantitatively assessed during each feeding round.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis comparing two groups was performed using a two-sided Student's t-test. ∗P < 0.05; ∗∗P < 0.01; ∗∗∗P < 0.001; ns, not significant. Error bars indicate the standard deviations.

Results

Elimination of phage defense systems increased the susceptibility of EcN to phages

We initially assessed the susceptibility of E. coli DH10B and the wild-type EcN (EcN-WT) to the T-series coliphages, namely T1, T3, T4, T5, and T7. Spot dilution assays revealed that all the aforementioned T phages were capable of infecting DH10B; However, none of them could infect EcN-WT except T4 (Fig. 1A). The T4 phage could form plaques on DH10B at a 105 dilution; In contrast, it failed to form plaques on EcN-WT at dilutions exceeding 100. We speculated that the phage defense systems present in EcN-WT impede the propagation of the T4 phage within the EcN-WT cell.

Elimination of phage defense systems increased the susceptibility of EcN to phages. (A) Spot dilution assays of the T1, T3, T4, T5, and T7 phages on E. coli DH10B and the EcN-WT. (B) The locations of the identified phage defense systems in EcN-WT. (C) Genotypes of EcN recombinant strains in which the identified phage defense systems were knocked out. (D) Spot dilution assays of the T3, T7, and T4 phages on EcN recombinant strains in which the identified phage defense systems were knocked out.

To promote the propagation of the T4 phage within the EcN-WT cell, we attempted to eliminate its phage defense systems. Using the DefenseFinder (https://defensefinder.mdmlab.fr) and PADLOC (https://padloc.otago.ac.nz) bioinformatics tools, we successfully identified 8 phage defense systems located on the chromosome and the Kiwa defense system situated in the endogenous plasmid pMUT2 (Fig. 1B). Besides pMUT2, EcN-WT harbors another endogenous plasmid pMUT1. To facilitate introduction of exogenous plasmids, both pMUT1 and pMUT2 were eliminated using the strategy illustrated in Fig. S1. The cassette containing the kanamycin resistance gene (kanR) as a selection marker and the sacB gene as a counterselection marker was inserted into pMUT1 to generate the recombinant plasmid pMUT1-sacB through recombineering in E. coli GB05-red [26]. The pMUT1-sacB plasmid was electroporated into EcN-WT. After 12 passages in the LB medium supplemented with kanamycin, the wild-type pMUT1 plasmid was successfully eliminated due to plasmid incompatibility (Fig. S2). Then, the EcN strain harboring the pMUT1-sacB plasmid was grown on the LB plate containing 10% (wt/vol) sucrose. The sacB gene on the pMUT1-sacB plasmid encodes levansucrase, an enzyme that converts sucrose into levan that is toxic to host cells [28]. Under the selection pressure exerted by the sucrose-containing LB plate, colonies of the EcN strain devoid of the pMUT1-sacB plasmid were successfully obtained. Subsequently, employing the identical strategy used for eliminating the pMUT1 plasmid, the pMUT2 plasmid was removed from the EcN-WT (ΔpMUT1) strain (Fig. S1 and S2). Ultimately, the EcN-1 strain, which lacks both endogenous plasmids, was successfully obtained (Fig. 1C). Growth curves of the EcN-WT strain and the EcN-1 strain indicated that the elimination of the pMUT1 and pMUT2 plasmids does not compromise the growth of EcN (Fig. S3).

The phage defense systems situated on the chromosome of the EcN-1 strain were deleted through recombineering followed by Cre-lox site-specific recombination (Fig. S4). To delete the RM type I defense system, the recombineering plasmid pSC101-BAD-gbaA-tetR [26] was electroporated into the EcN-1 strain. The cassette encompassing the lox71 site, the chloramphenicol resistance gene (cmR), and the lox66 site, and flanked by 50-bp for recombineering was electroporated into the EcN-1 strain that was expressing the Redαβ recombinases. The EcN-1-lox-cmR recombinant strain was obtained by selecting on LB plates supplemented with chloramphenicol and then was confirmed through PCR and Sanger sequencing. Then, the temperature sensitive plasmid pSC101-BAD-gbaA-tetR was cured from the EcN-1-lox-cmR strain by cultivating at 37 °C. The Cre recombinase expressing plasmid pSC101-BAD-cre-tetR [27] was electroporated into the EcN-1-lox-cmR strain. After inducing the expression of the Cre recombinase, the cmR selection marker was removed through Cre-lox site-specific recombination and the EcN-2 recombinant strain was obtained. The EcN-2 recombinant strain, in which both the Kiwa defense system and Type I RM system were deleted, was validated by PCR (Fig. S5). The remaining seven identified phage defense systems situated on the chromosome were sequentially deleted using the same strategy, yielding the cumulative knockout recombinant strains EcN-3, EcN-4, EcN-5, EcN-6, EcN-7, EcN-8, and EcN-9 (Fig. 1C and Fig. S5). In the EcN-9 strain, all nine identified defense systems were knocked out. Growth curves of the EcN-WT strain and the EcN-2∼9 strains indicated that elimination of the phage defense systems situated on the chromosome does not compromise the growth of EcN (Fig. S3).

Spot dilution assays revealed that the T4 phage failed to form plaques on EcN-WT at dilutions exceeding 100 (Fig. 1A); However, it could form plaques on the EcN-1∼9 strains at a 105 dilution (Fig. 1D). The susceptibility of the EcN-1 to 9 strains to the T4 phage is comparable to that of the DH10B strain (Fig. 1A and D). The T3 and T7 phages could infect the EcN-7, EcN-8, and EcN-9 strains; However, they were unable to infect the EcN-WT, and EcN-1 to 6 strains. The T3 phage was able to form plaques on the EcN-7∼9 strains at dilutions less than 1000. The T7 phage could form plaques on the EcN-8 and EcN-9 strains at dilutions less than 1000; In contrast, it could form plaques on the EcN-7 strains at dilutions less than 100. Consequently, elimination of phage defense systems increased the susceptibility of EcN to phages. The T1 and T5 phages still could not infect EcN-1 to 9 strains. It is likely that there is an absence of receptors for the T1 and T5 phages on the EcN cell.

Expression of the FhuA receptor protein renders EcN susceptible to infection by the T1 and T5 phages

FhuA, an energy-coupled outer membrane transporter protein in E. coli that mediates ferrichrome transport [29], has been shown to serve as a receptor for phages T1 and T5 [30]. Because the T1 and T5 phages can infect the DH10B strain but not the Nissle strain (Fig. 1A), we compared the amino-acid sequences of FhuA proteins from DH10B and Nissle strains. Sequence alignments revealed an 89.1% similarity between the FhuADH10B (WP_000124442.1) and the FhuANissle (AID77215.1) proteins, and 82 out of 753 amino acids were different (Fig. 2A).

The E. coli strain GB2005 [26], a derivative of the DH10B strain in which the fhuA gene was disrupted through the insertion of the IS2 transposon, was employed to assess the impact of fhuA expression on infection by T1 and T5 phages. Initial efforts to clone fhuADH10B under the regulation of the J23119 promoter, a constitutive strong promoter in E. coli, in the medium-copy pBR322 (15–20 copies per cell) and low-copy p15A (10–12 copies per cell) plasmids, were unsuccessful. It is likely that the high expression of the FhuADH10B membrane protein is toxic to the E. coli GB2005 cell. Successful cloning of fhuADH10B in E. coli GB2005 was achieved by placing it under the control of an arabinose-inducible PBAD promoter in a single-copy BAC (bacterial artificial chromosome) vector. Spot dilution assays showed that the expression of FhuADH10B in E. coli GB2005 reinstated its susceptibility to T1 and T5 phages when 3 g L−1 of the inducer l-arabinose was added into the medium (Fig. 2B–E). However, T1 and T5 phages were unable to infect E. coli GB2005 expressing FhuANissle (Fig. 2B–E). Moreover, the expression of FhuADH10B enhanced the adsorption efficiency of the T1 and T5 phages on E. coli GB2005 to (50.4 ± 0.7) % and (60.3 ± 1.2) %, respectively (Fig. 2C–F).

Subsequently, we introduced the BAC carrying the PBAD-fhuADH10B cassette into both EcN-WT and EcN-9 cells. Spot dilution assays showed that while the EcN-WT strain expressing FhuADH10B remained resistant to T1 phage infection, the EcN-9 strain expressing FhuADH10B became susceptible to T1 phage infection, indicating that both the receptors and the phage defense systems exert an influence on the infection process of the T1 phage. Both EcN-WT and EcN-9 strains expressing FhuADH10B were susceptible to T5 phage infection (Fig. 2D–G). Despite the fact that the titers of the T1 and T5 phages exhibited low levels on EcN recombinant strains, these findings indicate that the introduction of a particular receptor can alter a bacterial strain's susceptibility to phages.

Enhancement of the adsorption efficiency and the EOP values by optimizing the expression of the FhuA receptor protein

To enhance the EOP values of the T1 and T5 phages on EcN-9 (pBAC-PBAD-fhuADH10B), we optimized the expression of the FhuADH10B receptor protein. The PBAD-fhuADH10B cassette was cloned into the low-copy p15A and medium-copy pBR322 plasmids. When 3 g L−1 of the inducer l-arabinose was added to the medium, the adsorption efficiency of the T1 phage on EcN-9 expressing FhuADH10B from the pBR322 plasmid was 1.3 times higher than that from the p15A plasmid, which was 2.2 times higher than that from BAC (Fig. 3A). Similarly, the adsorption efficiency of the T5 phage on EcN-9 expressing FhuADH10B from the pBR322 plasmid was 1.5 times higher than that from the p15A plasmid which was 1.6 times higher than that from BAC (Fig. 3B). Regarding the EOP values, the T1 phage on EcN-9 expressing FhuADH10B from the pBR322 plasmid was 1.8 times higher than that from the p15A plasmid which was 2.8 times higher than that from BAC (Fig. 3C). For the T5 phage, the EOP value on EcN-9 expressing FhuADH10B from the pBR322 plasmid was 3.2 times higher than that from the p15A plasmid, which was 2.2 times higher than that from BAC (Fig. 3D).

(C) The EOP values of the T1 phage on EcN strains. (D) The EOP values of the T5 phage on EcN strains. n = 3 independent experiments. Data are presented as the mean ± S.D. Statistical analysis comparing two groups was performed using a two-sided Student's t-test. ∗P < 0.05; ∗∗P < 0.01; ∗∗∗P < 0.001; ns, not significant. EcN-9 was used as the control (CK). Subsequently, we further fine-tuned the expression of FhuADH10B from the pBR322 plasmid by titrating the concentration of the inducer l-arabinose added to the medium. The results suggested that the adsorption efficiency of the T1 phage on EcN-9 (pBR322-PBAD-fhuADH10B) grown on LB medium supplemented with 4.5 g L−1 of l-arabinose was 1.4 times higher than that supplemented with 3 g L−1 of l-arabinose, which was 1.6 times higher than that supplemented with 1.5 g L−1 of l-arabinose (Fig. 3A). For the T5 phage, the adsorption efficiency on EcN-9 (pBR322-PBAD-fhuADH10B) grown on LB medium supplemented with 4.5 g L−1 of l-arabinose was 1.2 times higher than that supplemented with 3 g L−1 of l-arabinose, which was 1.2 times higher than that supplemented with 1.5 g L−1 of l-arabinose (Fig. 3B). The EOP value of T1 phage on EcN-9 (pBR322-PBAD-fhuADH10B) grown on LB plates supplemented with 4.5 g L−1 of l-arabinose was 1.8 times higher than that supplemented with 3 g L−1 of l-arabinose, which was 3.6 times higher than that supplemented with 1.5 g L−1 of l-arabinose (Fig. 3C). The EOP value of T5 phage on EcN-9 (pBR322-PBAD-fhuADH10B) grown on LB plates supplemented with 4.5 g L−1 of l-arabinose was 2.1 times higher than that supplemented with 3 g L−1 of l-arabinose, which was 4.1 times higher than that supplemented with 1.5 g L−1 of l-arabinose (Fig. 3D). When the l-arabinose concentration in the medium exceeded 4.5 g L−1, no further increase was observed in either the adsorption efficiencies or the EOP values (Fig. 3A–D).

The inducible PBAD promoter within the pBR322-PBAD-fhuADH10B plasmid was replaced with the native promoter (PfhuA) of the fhuA gene from E. coli DH10B (Table S6), resulting in the construction of the pBR322-PfhuA-fhuADH10B plasmid. The adsorption efficiencies of the T1 and T5 phages on EcN-9 (pBR322-PfhuA-fhuADH10B) grown on LB medium were 2.4-fold and 2.6-fold higher, respectively, compared to those on EcN-9 (pBR322-PBAD-fhuADH10B) grown on LB medium supplemented with 4.5 g L−1 of l-arabinose (Fig. 3A and B). Similarly, the EOP values of the T1 and T5 phages on EcN-9 (pBR322-PfhuA-fhuADH10B) cultured on LB plates were 119.3-fold and 172.1-fold higher, respectively, compared to those on EcN-9 (pBR322-PBAD-fhuADH10B) grown on LB plates supplemented with 4.5 g L−1 of l-arabinose (Fig. 3C and D).

The FhuANissle protein, encoded by the EcN-9 genome, cannot be recognized by the T1 and T5 phages (Fig. 2C–F). Within EcN-9 cell, the FhuADH10B protein, expressed from the pBR322 plasmid, and the FhuANissle protein, expressed from the genome, compete for localization on the cell membrane. When the fhuANissle gene in the genome was deleted (Fig. S6), the growth of EcN-9 was not compromised (Fig. S7). The adsorption efficiencies of the T1 and T5 phages on EcN-9-ΔfhuANissle (pBR322-PfhuA-fhuADH10B) were 1.2-fold and 1.1-fold higher, respectively, compared to those on EcN-9 (pBR322-PfhuA-fhuADH10B) (Fig. 3A and B). The EOP values of the T1 and T5 phages on EcN-9-ΔfhuANissle (pBR322-PfhuA-fhuADH10B) were 3.5-fold and 1.1-fold higher, respectively, compared to those on EcN-9 (pBR322-PfhuA-fhuADH10B) (Fig. 3C and D). Finally, by optimizing the expression of the FhuADH10B protein and deleting the fhuANissle gene from the EcN genome, the adsorption efficiencies of the T1 and T5 phages on EcN-9-ΔfhuANissle (pBR322-PfhuA-fhuADH10B) reached (52.7 ± 1.5) % and (74.6 ± 0.9) %, showing improvements of 11.7-fold and 7.4-fold, respectively, compared to those on EcN-9 (pBAC-PBAD-fhuADH10B) (Fig. 3A and B). The EOP values of the T1 and T5 phages on EcN-9-ΔfhuANissle (pBR322-PfhuA-fhuADH10B) reached (0.67 ± 0.01) and (0.93 ± 0.02), which were enhanced by 3871.6 and 2592.8 times, respectively, in comparison with those on EcN-9 (pBAC-PBAD-fhuADH10B) (Fig. 3C and D).

Spot dilution tests demonstrated that even at low titers (10−5 and 10−6 dilution), the T1 and T5 phages can form distinct lysis zones on EcN-9-ΔfhuANissle (pBR322-PfhuA-fhuADH10B), confirming that it has become highly susceptible to T1 and T5 phages. The susceptibility of the EcN-9-ΔfhuANissle (pBR322-PfhuA-fhuADH10B) strain to T1 and T5 phages is comparable to that of the DH10B strain to these two phages (Fig. 3E and F).

Enhancing phage titers via the fed-batch process

The production of the T4 phage on the EcN-9 strain, as well as the production of the T1 and T5 phages on the EcN-9-ΔfhuANissle (pBR322-PfhuA-fhuADH10B) strain, was initially evaluated in a 50 mL culture within 250-mL flasks, using a range of multiplicity of infection (MOI) values (0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10). The results indicated that phage production rose in tandem with an increase in MOI values. The highest titer was achieved at an MOI of 10, while the lowest titer was obtained at an MOI of 0.001. At the MOI of 10, the titer of the T4 phage on the EcN-9 strain was 1.22 × 1010 PFU mL−1, and the titers of the T1 and T5 phages on the EcN-9-ΔfhuANissle (pBR322-PfhuA-fhuADH10B) strain were 1.91 × 1010 PFU mL−1 and 2.21 × 1010 PFU mL−1, respectively (Fig. 4A–C).

In the large-scale phage production, the practical implementation at an MOI of 10 poses significant challenges. To lower the initial MOI and further boost phage titers, we attempted to adopt a fed-batch process for phage production. At the initial infection stage, phages at an MOI of 0.1 were utilized to infect EcN recombinant strains. Once phage titers reached their maximum values, the lysate supernatant was harvested through centrifugation, and then added into the fresh culture of EcN recombinant strains at log phase (Fig. 5A). This enabled phages to re-infect the EcN recombinant cells at a higher MOI, ultimately leading to high-titer phage production.

In the fed-batch operation, the peak titers of T1, T4 and T5 were achieved after the second feeding, reaching (4.47 ± 0.05) × 1010 PFU mL−1, (9.21 ± 0.24) × 1010 PFU mL−1 and (7.22 ± 0.21) × 1010 PFU mL−1, respectively (Fig. 5B–D). These values represent 5.3-, 10.1-, and 7.9-fold increases, compared to that at MOI = 0.1. Nevertheless, subsequent feedings resulted in a gradual decrease in phage titers for all three phages (Fig. 5B–D). This decline might be attributed to nutrient depletion and accumulation of inhibitory metabolites. Overall, these results demonstrate that the fed-batch strategy enables efficient production of T1, T4, and T5 phages.

Discussion

The prevailing dependence on clinical isolates of pathogenic bacteria as the hosts for phage production in phage therapy has raised concerns regarding biological safety [7,8]. Utilizing non-pathogenic strains for phage production not only substantially diminishes the biological safety risks but also streamlines the purification procedure. Here, we explored the feasibility of engineering the probiotic EcN as the host for producing phages that cannot naturally infect it. The specificity of phages, which has been shaped by the long-term co-evolution between phages and their host bacteria, is evident in their capacity to exclusively recognize and infect specific bacterial types or strains [31,32]. In this study, we focused on two critical factors that influence phage infection efficiency: bacterial phage defense systems and receptor proteins. Through the systematic deletion of nine phage defense systems within the EcN-WT genome, we successfully engineered a recombinant strain that exhibited susceptibility to T3, T4, and T7 phages. Elimination of known phage defense systems within EcN-WT increased its susceptibility to the T4 phage to the levels comparable with the susceptibility of the E. coli DH10B strain to the T4 phage. Furthermore, the heterologous expression of the FhuADH10B receptor protein in EcN rendered it susceptible to T1 and T5 phages. By optimizing the expression levels of the FhuADH10B receptor protein, the EOP values of T1 and T5 phages on EcN were enhanced by 3871.6 and 2592.8 times, respectively. We achieved a susceptibility of EcN to the T1 and T5 phages that was comparable to that of the E. coli DH10B, the natural host for these two phages. Ultimately, we devised a fed-batch strategy that enabled the production of T1, T4, and T5 phages with titers exceeding 4 × 1010 PFU mL−1 at a low inoculation MOI of 0.1.

To evaluate the contribution of cumulative defense system knockout to the elevated EOP values of phages T1 and T5 in EcN, we constructed a series of recombinant EcN strains. In both wild-type EcN and the cumulative defense-system knockout strains EcN-1 through EcN-9, we deleted the fhuANissle gene and introduced the pBR322-PfhuA-fhuADH10B plasmid for heterologous expression of FhuADH10B (Fig. 6). Deletion of fhuANissle and heterologous expression of FhuADH10B rendered both wild-type EcN and all cumulative knockout strains susceptible to infection by phages T1 and T5. Specifically, the EOP values of phages T1 and T5 rose from undetectable levels in wild-type EcN to 3.28 × 10−3 and 0.47, respectively, in EcN-ΔfhuANissle (pBR322-PfhuA-fhuADH10B), which retains all native defense systems. Compared with this strain, the EOP of phage T1 was further elevated by 7-, 56-, 177-, and 198-fold in strains carrying cumulative knockout of four, five, six, and nine defense systems, respectively (Fig. 6A and B). Similarly, relative to the strain with three defense systems knocked out, the EOP of phage T5 was increased by 1.7-, 1.8-, and 1.9-fold in strains with cumulative deletion of four, five, and six defense systems, respectively (Fig. 6C and D). These results demonstrate that removal of the defense systems in EcN enhanced the infectivity of phages T1 and T5, in addition to phage T4.

The T3 and T7 phages cannot infect the EcN-WT strain, but they can infect the EcN-7, EcN-8, and EcN-9 strains (Fig. 1D). However, the susceptibilities of the EcN-7, EcN-8, and EcN-9 strains to these two phages were >1000 times lower than the susceptibility of the E. coli DH10B strain to these two phages. Sequencing of tail fiber genes (gp11, gp12, and gp17) from T3 and T7 phages propagated on EcN-7, EcN-8, and EcN-9 detected no mutations. Meanwhile, spot dilution assays confirmed comparable infectivity of the re-isolated phages (Fig. S8). Thus, the susceptibility of EcN-7, EcN-8, and EcN-9 to T3/T7 phages arises from the inactivation of bacterial defense mechanisms, not from phage genomic mutations. Both the T3 and T7 phages employ lipopolysaccharide (LPS) as the receptor to infect E. coli cells. The molecular structure of the LPS of EcN is different from all other LPS types of E. coli strains [17,33]. The low infection efficiency of the T3 and T7 phages on the recombinant EcN strains may be caused by the low affinity between the EcN LPS receptors and the phage receptor binding proteins. Additionally, unidentified phage defense systems may exist within the EcN genome to hamper efficient propagation of the T3 and T7 phages in EcN. Based on current understanding of the interaction between phages and their hosts [13,16], we are still unable to predict unknown phage defense systems.

In addition to well-characterized phage defense systems such as CRISPR-Cas and restriction-modification mechanisms, bacterial hosts have developed multi-dimensional and multi-level strategies to combat phage predation through prolonged co-evolution arms races with phages. These anti-phage strategies encompass: (i) structural modifications of surface receptors and extracellular polysaccharide secretion to physically impede phage adsorption [[34], [35], [36]], (ii) metabolic downregulation to limit resources essential for phage replication [37], (iii) production of soluble receptor decoys, such as outer membrane vesicles, that neutralize phage binding capacity [38,39], and (iv) quorum sensing-mediated coordination of collective defense responses through signaling molecules like acyl-homoserine lactones, enabling preemptive activation of protective measures [40,41]. This multilayered and non-uniform defense architectures complicate host engineering efforts. Furthermore, as strict obligate parasites, the key stages of phage life cycle, including genome replication, capsid assembly, and release of progeny particles, must depend on the metabolic substrates and biosynthetic precursors provided by the host [42,43]. This dependence directly results in phages exhibiting a highly specific host infection spectrum. Such specificity further restricts the production of corresponding phages to only closely related model bacterial strains.

Although significant hurdles persist in engineering bacterial hosts for enhanced phage production, particularly due to the phage-specific adaptation requirements necessitating case-by-case modification strategies, our results have demonstrated the feasibility of this approach. We anticipate that the increasing understanding of phage-host interaction mechanisms will facilitate metabolic engineering of non-pathogenic model bacteria as chassis hosts for production of a diverse range of phages.

Conclusions

Nissle 1917 is the sole non-pathogenic probiotic strain of E. coli that has been identified to date. To exploit it as the chassis host for coliphage production, we systematically eliminated all of known phage defense systems and optimized expression of the suitable heterologous FhuA receptor protein. The infection efficiencies of T1, T4, and T5 phages on the recombinant EcN strains were comparable to that on the E. coli DH10B, the natural host for these three phages. Additionally, we developed a fed-batch fermentation strategy to achieve the production of T1, T4, and T5 phages using the recombinant EcN strain as the chassis host with titers exceeding 4 × 1010 PFU mL−1 at a low inoculation MOI of 0.1. This work presents a feasible strategy for the safe and efficient phage production for phage therapy.
