Early-onset fetal growth restriction with normal Doppler findings associated with prenatal diagnosis of an intrahepatic portosystemic shunt: A case report
Authors: Erkan Sağlam, Mustafa Raşit Özler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Congenital portosystemic shunts are rare vascular anomalies of the fetal venous system caused by abnormal connections between the portal and systemic circulation. Although prenatal diagnosis is challenging, it is essential for appropriate counseling and management.A 34-year-old pregnant woman was referred at 26 weeks of gestation with suspected early-onset fetal growth restriction. Ultrasonography revealed severe fetal growth restriction, with all biometric parameters below the 1st percentile, while Doppler findings remained normal. Targeted evaluation demonstrated an abnormal vascular connection between the left portal vein and the left hepatic vein, consistent with an intrahepatic portosystemic shunt, without additional structural anomalies.The pregnancy was closely monitored, and a female infant weighing 1410 g was delivered at 37 weeks of gestation. The neonatal course was uneventful except for low birth weight.Intrahepatic portosystemic shunts should be considered in the differential diagnosis of unexplained early-onset fetal growth restriction, even when conventional Doppler findings are normal.

Introduction

Congenital portosystemic shunts are rare vascular anomalies of the fetal venous system, defined by abnormal connections between the portal venous system and the systemic circulation [1]. They are estimated to occur in approximately 1 in 30,000–50,000 live births [2]. These anomalies are thought to result from disturbances in the development of the vitelline veins during embryogenesis, leading to abnormal communications between the portal and systemic venous systems, and may be associated with conditions such as intrauterine growth restriction, cardiac overload, and chromosomal abnormalities [3].

During fetal life, the umbilical vein, portal venous system, and ductus venosus function together as an integrated system that transports oxygenated blood from the placenta to the fetal circulation. Developmental abnormalities in any component of this system may result in abnormal shunting of blood into the systemic circulation, referred to as umbilical–portal–systemic venous shunt (UPSVS). Since the umbilical vein and ductus venosus close after birth, postnatal classifications do not fully reflect the complex venous structure present during fetal life.

Therefore, Achiron et al. [4] proposed an in-utero classification system that categorizes shunts according to their anatomical origin into umbilical-systemic, ductus venosus-systemic, and portal-systemic types, with the latter further divided into intrahepatic and extrahepatic subgroups. This classification has been shown to be useful in guiding prenatal diagnosis and assessing prognosis.

Despite an increasing number of prenatally diagnosed cases in recent years, data on the prenatal characteristics and clinical course of fetal portosystemic shunts are still limited. In particular, intrahepatic portosystemic shunts associated with intrauterine growth restriction are rarely reported.

This report describes a prenatally diagnosed intrahepatic portosystemic shunt associated with early-onset fetal growth restriction and discusses the case in light of the literature.

Case Presentation

A 34-year-old pregnant woman, gravida 4, para 2, was referred to a tertiary perinatology clinic at 26 weeks of gestation with suspected early-onset fetal growth restriction (FGR). Her obstetric history included two uncomplicated term vaginal deliveries and one abortion. She reported smoking 3–4 cigarettes per day; otherwise, her medical and family history was unremarkable.

Gestational age determined by first-trimester crown–rump length (CRL) was consistent with the last menstrual period. A first-trimester ultrasound examination performed at 12 weeks of gestation, including routine assessment of the ductus venosus, demonstrated a normal ductus venosus waveform without evidence of venous abnormalities. The mean uterine artery pulsatility index (UtA-PI) was 1.80 (approximately the 60th percentile), with no evidence of unilateral or bilateral early diastolic notching. A detailed second-trimester anatomical survey performed at 20–22 weeks of gestation revealed no structural fetal abnormalities. Routine antenatal laboratory tests, including complete blood count, biochemical parameters, infection screening, and glucose tolerance testing, were within normal limits.

Ultrasonographic evaluation at 26 weeks of gestation revealed a live fetus with significant growth restriction. Biometric measurements were as follows: biparietal diameter corresponding to 22 weeks +3 days (<1st percentile), head circumference < 1st percentile, abdominal circumference corresponding to 23 weeks +1 day (<1st percentile), and femur length corresponding to 23 weeks +2 days (<1st percentile). The estimated fetal weight was 578 g (<1st percentile), consistent with severe growth restriction. Amniotic fluid volume was normal. The mean uterine artery pulsatility index measured at presentation was 0.97 (approximately the 69th percentile), with no evidence of early diastolic notching. Umbilical artery Doppler indices were within normal limits (PI: 1.32; RI: 0.74), middle cerebral artery Doppler was also within normal limits (PI: 1.89; approximately the 35th percentile), and the ductus venosus waveform was normal.

A detailed fetal anatomical examination demonstrated an abnormal vascular connection between the medial branch of the left portal vein (LPVm) and the left hepatic vein in the transverse abdominal plane (Fig. 1). The ductus venosus was normal. No additional structural anomalies were identified. Amniocentesis for fetal karyotyping was recommended because of early-onset severe fetal growth restriction and the presence of a fetal vascular anomaly. However, after detailed genetic counseling, the patient declined invasive prenatal testing and provided informed refusal.

The patient was followed weekly with amniotic fluid assessment, Doppler studies, and biophysical profile evaluation. Throughout the follow-up period, amniotic fluid volume remained normal; umbilical artery, middle cerebral artery, and ductus venosus Doppler findings remained within normal limits; and biophysical profile scores were consistently 10.

At 37 weeks of gestation, a cesarean section was performed because of breech presentation. A live female infant weighing 1410 g was delivered, with Apgar scores of 8 and 9 at 1 and 5 min, respectively.

The neonate was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit because of low birth weight. No advanced resuscitation was required. The infant did not require respiratory support, surfactant therapy, or mechanical ventilation. Enteral feeding was initiated with breast milk and formula, and short-term total parenteral nutrition was administered.

Neonatal echocardiography was normal. No complications such as intraventricular hemorrhage, necrotizing enterocolitis, retinopathy of prematurity, or chronic lung disease were observed. No infection was detected.

Postnatal abdominal ultrasonography was suboptimal because of bowel gas but showed normal hepatic and portal venous structures.

The neonate was discharged on day 20 of life in good clinical condition.

Discussion

Congenital portosystemic shunts are rare anomalies of the fetal venous system that arise due to disruption of the normal connections between the umbilical vein, portal venous system, and ductus venosus [5]. Prenatal recognition of these anomalies is critical for predicting prognosis and planning appropriate perinatal management. Ultrasonography is the primary diagnostic tool that allows noninvasive evaluation during both prenatal and postnatal periods. Direct visualization of abnormal vascular connections is diagnostic, while indirect findings such as dilated hepatic veins, prominent umbilical vein, and abnormal tubular vascular structures may also aid in diagnosis [6], [7].

According to the in-utero classification proposed by Achiron et al., fetal portosystemic shunts are categorized into umbilical-systemic, ductus venosus-systemic, and portal-systemic shunts, with portal-systemic shunts further divided into intrahepatic and extrahepatic subtypes [4]. The presence of an intact intrahepatic portal venous system (IHPVS) is emphasized as an important prognostic factor. In the present case, the abnormal communication involved the medial branch of the left portal vein and the left hepatic vein and was consistent with a Type 3a intrahepatic portosystemic shunt. Notably, the ductus venosus remained anatomically intact and demonstrated a normal flow pattern. This combination is of particular interest because fetal portosystemic shunts may coexist with ductus venosus abnormalities, whereas the present case demonstrated an intrahepatic portal-systemic communication despite preservation of normal ductus venosus flow. The preserved IHPVS and absence of prenatal signs of cardiac overload were consistent with the clinically stable perinatal course [4], [8].

Intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) is one of the most common clinical presentations of intrahepatic portosystemic shunts [9], [10], [11]. Achiron et al. reported an IUGR rate of approximately 58% in the intrahepatic shunt group [4]. This condition is likely related to reduced hepatic perfusion resulting from insufficient portal blood flow to the liver parenchyma, which may lead to decreased levels of insulin-like growth factors (IGF-I and IGF-II) and consequently impaired fetal growth [12], [13].

In the present case, early-onset severe fetal growth restriction developed despite the absence of Doppler evidence suggestive of impaired placentation. First-trimester ductus venosus assessment showed a normal waveform, and the mean uterine artery pulsatility index was within the normal range, without early diastolic notching. At 26 weeks of gestation, when severe fetal growth restriction was diagnosed, the mean uterine artery pulsatility index remained within the normal range and no early diastolic notching was observed. Umbilical artery, middle cerebral artery, and ductus venosus Doppler findings were also normal. This longitudinal Doppler profile argues against overt placental insufficiency as the sole explanation for the severity of fetal growth restriction and supports consideration of alternative mechanisms, including abnormal fetal venous shunting.

Maternal smoking is an established risk factor for fetal growth restriction and placental dysfunction and therefore represents a potential contributing factor in this case. However, the reported exposure was limited to 3–4 cigarettes per day, and neither maternal uterine artery Doppler assessment nor serial fetal Doppler surveillance demonstrated the pattern typically associated with clinically evident placental insufficiency. Accordingly, a contribution from smoking cannot be excluded, but smoking alone may not fully explain the severe early-onset growth restriction observed. The intrahepatic portosystemic shunt may therefore have contributed to impaired fetal growth through altered hepatic perfusion, although a causal relationship cannot be established from a single case.

Recent studies have demonstrated that intrahepatic portosystemic shunts are associated with a lower incidence of severe comorbidities and generally have better outcomes compared with other shunt types [8], [14]. In these cases, a conservative “wait-and-see” approach is often sufficient, which is consistent with the clinical course observed in the present case.

A considerable proportion of intrahepatic portosystemic shunts may close spontaneously after birth [15], [16]. This is thought to be related to postnatal hemodynamic changes that reduce shunt flow, eventually leading to closure of the abnormal venous connection. However, long-term follow-up remains important, as some patients may later develop complications such as hyperammonemia, liver dysfunction, or hepatic lesions.

In the present case, no signs of cardiac overload were observed prenatally, and the postnatal course was clinically stable. The absence of major complications is consistent with preservation of the intrahepatic portal venous system.

This report has two important limitations. First, invasive prenatal genetic testing was declined despite counseling. Although no additional structural abnormalities were identified on the detailed second-trimester anatomical survey or subsequent targeted examination, an underlying chromosomal abnormality cannot be definitively excluded. Therefore, the apparently isolated nature of the vascular anomaly should be interpreted with caution. Second, the prenatally detected shunt could not be definitively confirmed on postnatal imaging because abdominal ultrasonography was technically limited by bowel gas. Nevertheless, intrahepatic portosystemic shunts may close spontaneously after birth, and small-caliber shunts may not always be detectable on postnatal ultrasonography [17]. These limitations underscore the importance of careful prenatal characterization and appropriate postnatal follow-up.

In conclusion, intrahepatic portosystemic shunts should be considered in the differential diagnosis of unexplained early-onset fetal growth restriction, particularly when conventional maternal and fetal Doppler findings remain normal. Detailed evaluation of the fetal hepatic and portal venous systems may reveal uncommon vascular causes of impaired fetal growth that would otherwise remain unrecognized. Prenatal anatomical characterization, assessment for associated abnormalities, and appropriate postnatal follow-up are important for counseling and management.
