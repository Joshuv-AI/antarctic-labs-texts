ZBP1 mediated restriction of Toxoplasma gondii in both the early acute and chronic brain stages of infection
Authors: Lydia Anne Sibley, Isaac William Babcock, Naa Dedee Amadi, Anne Elise Marchildon, Abigail Grace Kelly, Sydney Ann Labuzan, John Robert Lukens, Tajie Heartsong Harris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractZBP1 is an innate sensor of nucleic acids in the Z-conformation and regulates immune responses via cell death pathways and nuclear factor κB signaling. Previous work has demonstrated an essential role for ZBP1 in viral restriction; however, the role ZBP1 plays in protection against other pathogens is in early stages of exploration. Toxoplasma gondii is an obligate intracellular parasite that can infect all nucleated cells and establishes a chronic infection of the brain requiring a robust innate and adaptive immune response and multiple cell death pathways for host survival. Importantly, innate sensors of T. gondii infection of the central nervous system are still being defined. Prior work has implicated caspase-1 and caspase-8 in the control of T. gondii in the brain. Because ZBP1 can activate both caspase-1 and caspase-8, we sought to explore the role of ZBP1 during T. gondii infection. During early infection, we found an increase in parasite burden; a decrease in NK1.1+ cell, neutrophil, and monocyte recruitment; and impaired NK1.1+ cell cytokine production in the peritoneum of Zbp1−/− mice. Interestingly, during the early adaptive immune response, the immune cell recruitment defects and impaired parasite control were ameliorated. During the brain stage of infection, ZBP1 regulated parasite restriction and numerous inflammatory responses to infection. Our findings establish ZBP1 as a critical regulator of host defense to T. gondii and highlight the importance of ZBP1 in development of inflammation during both the acute and chronic infection.Keywords: neuroinflammation, Toxoplasma gondii, ZBP1

Introduction

The innate immune system is one of the first lines of defense against invading pathogens. During infection, sensing of pathogen-associated molecular patterns and damage-associated molecular patterns by pattern recognition receptors is crucial to initiate inflammatory responses. One important pattern recognition receptor is ZBP1.1 Initially classified as an innate immune sensor of cytosolic DNA, recent work has demonstrated that ZBP1 can induce inflammation and regulate numerous cell death pathways.2–5 ZBP1 contains left-handed helical Z-conformation nucleic acid (Z-NA) Zα domains which sense nucleic acids in the Z-conformation. Additionally, ZBP1 contains RIP homotypic interaction motif domains allowing for interaction with RIPK1 and RIPK3. ZBP1, RIPK1, and RIPK3 form the core scaffold of the PANoptosome, which can (1) drive nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) signaling, (2) interact with FADD and caspase-8 to induce apoptosis, (3) phosphorylate MLKL to induce necroptosis, and (4) induce activation of the inflammasome and drive caspase-1-mediated pyroptosis.6,7 Because ZBP1 is implicated in each of these various cell death pathways, it has been classified as a central regulator of PANoptosis. While each pathway plays important roles in defense against pathogens, the specific roles of ZBP1 during infection in nonviral systems remains largely undefined.

The obligate intracellular parasite, Toxoplasma gondii, causes the highly pervasive infection, toxoplasmosis.8 T. gondii is naturally acquired by oral ingestion and invades cells of the gut epithelium. From there, the rapidly replicative and highly lytic form of the parasite, tachyzoites, can infect hematopoietic cells or enter the bloodstream directly and disseminate throughout the entire body.9 In mice, innate immune detection of T. gondii occurs through sensing of parasite profilin by Toll-like receptors (TLRs) 11/12.10–13 Early work has also suggested a role for NLRP1 in parasite sensing and inflammatory responses.14,15 TLR11/12 activates MyD88 signaling and stimulates CD8α+ dendritic cells (DCs) to produce IL-12, a critical cytokine for controlling the acute phase of infection.16,17 IL-12 recruits and activates natural killer (NK) cells and type 1 innate lymphoid cells (ILC1) to produce IFNγ, the main mediator of parasite killing.18,19 However, there is some evidence of MyD88-independent IL-12 production and subsequent IFNγ responses.17,20 Moreover, humans lack functional Tlr11 and Tlr12 genes, suggesting an alternative mechanism of parasite sensing.21 Importantly, this suggests additional unidentified mechanisms of parasite sensing and immune activation aside from TLRs. Additional innate immune responses to T. gondii infection include secretion of chemokines (e.g. CCL2, CXCL1, and CXCL2), to attract CCR2+Ly6Chi inflammatory monocytes, Ly6G+ neutrophils, granulocytes, and DCs to the site of infection.22,23 Later in the acute infection, DCs and macrophages produce IL-12 and IL-18, respectively, to support T cell differentiation, IFNγ production, and parasite killing.16,24,25 The acute phase of infection spans about two weeks, and by the end of this period the parasite is cleared from most tissues.

T. gondii takes up chronic residence in immune privileged tissues (i.e. the brain) and maintains infection throughout the lifetime of the host. Around a week after infection, T. gondii crosses the blood-brain barrier and in response to parasite invasion, brain-resident glial cells shift into an inflamed state.26 Glia mount numerous antiparasitic responses including cytokine and chemokine secretion to stimulate infiltration of immune cells (e.g. T cells and monocytes) into the brain.27–29 The main mechanism of T. gondii control in the brain is through T cell production of IFNγ.30–33 IFNγ activates a variety of genes involved in immune cell activation and parasite killing, including Zbp1.34–36 One important IFNγ-mediated parasite killing pathway is production of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) by infiltrating inflammatory monocytes and macrophages.37–41 Together, T cell, myeloid, and glial immune responses are crucial to host survival.

Cell death is an important defense mechanism against pathogen infection, and multiple cell death pathways have been implicated in defense against T. gondii during both the acute and chronic phases of infection.25,42–44 During the acute stage of infection, caspase-1 was shown to drive neutrophil and monocyte recruitment to the peritoneum.25 Additionally, Casp1−/− as well as Casp1/11−/− mice had impaired CD4+ T cell IFNγ responses driven by impaired IL-18 during the acute phase of infection.25,45 In the brain, caspase-1/11 contributes to immune cell recruitment and parasite control.42 Also in the brain phase of infection, caspase-8, but not RIPK3, is critical for parasite control and host survival despite robust immune responses.44,46 In addition to cell death, NF-κB signaling is highly protective against T. gondii infection and plays a key role in driving T cell-IFNγ production.47–52 Because ZBP1 is an innate sensor that has a dual role in both cell death and NF-κB signaling, we sought to explore the role of ZBP1 throughout the course of T. gondii infection. We identified a role for ZBP1 in cytokine production and immune cell response in the peritoneum during the early acute phase of infection. Interestingly, once the adaptive immune system was activated, Zbp1 deficiency did not impact parasite restriction in the peritoneum. Zbp1 is minimally expressed in the brain at baseline, but several reports have shown Zbp1 is highly upregulated in the brain in response to T. gondii infection.53–56 Here, we identified cells that highly express Zbp1 in the brain in response to infection and found that ZBP1 played a role in T cell cytokine production, monocyte infiltration, and parasite restriction during the chronic stage of infection. Overall, we demonstrate that ZBP1 plays an important role in stimulating immune responses to T. gondii at several stages of the infection.

Methods

Animals

Wild-type (WT) (C57BL/6J) (#000664), Casp1−/− (#32662),57 and CBA/J (#000656) strains were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. Swiss Webster strains were purchased from Taconic. Zbp1−/− (C57BL/6JCya-Zbp1em1/Cya mice [Catalog S-KO-11088]) mice were provided by John R. Lukens purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (strain number KOCMP-58203-Zbp1-B6J-VA). Casp8−/−Ripk3−/− and Casp8+/−Ripk3−/− mice were provided by John R. Lukens.58,59 All mice were maintained within our animal facility. All animal care and use procedures were approved by and conducted in accordance with the University of Virginia’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol number 3968).

Parasite and infection

To maintain the type II strain Me49 T. gondii line, Swiss Webster were chronically infected (2–6 mo) and the parasite was passaged through CBA/J mice for experimental infections. Brains of CBA/J mice (infected 3–8 wk) were collected in 3 mL of 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Gibco; Cat#14190144) and homogenized through an 18G needle and then a 22G needle (BD; Cat#305195, BD; Cat#305155). Tissue cysts were enumerated by mounting 30 μL of brain homogenate on a microscope slide and counting cysts visually on a DM 2000 LED brightfield microscope (Leica). Brain homogenate was diluted to a concentration of 10 tissue cysts of Me49 in 200 μL of 1× PBS. Experimental mice were inoculated by intraperitoneal injection.

Tissue processing

Mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation at specified days postinfection. Peritoneal exudate cells and fluid were collected by injecting 5 mL of cold 1× PBS through the intact peritoneal membrane with a 27G needle (BD; Cat#305109), massaging the abdomen, and removing the 1× PBS through the intact peritoneal membrane with a 27G needle. Peritoneal lavage fluid was centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 5 min to pellet cells. Cells were then resuspended in complete RPMI media (cRPMI) (10% fetal bovine serum [Gibco; Cat#10082-147], 1% penicillin/streptomycin [Gibco; Cat#15140-122], 1% sodium pyruvate [Gibco; Cat#11360-070], 1% nonessential amino acids [Gibco; Cat#11140-050], and 0.1% 2-mercaptoethanol [Gibco; Cat#21985-023]). Remaining peritoneal lavage fluid was stored at −20 °C. Cardiac puncture using a 27G needle was used for blood collection. Blood was stored overnight at 4 °C then centrifuged at 20,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C to separate serum (Eppendorf 5425 R). Serum was stored at −20 °C. For collection of brain and/or spleen, transcardiac perfusion with 20 mL cold 1× PBS was performed. Brains and spleens collected for flow cytometry were put into 3 mL cold cRPMI. Brains were homogenized with an 18G needle and 500 μL of homogenate was stored at −20 °C for RNA and DNA extraction as well as cyst counting. Remaining brain homogenate was enzymatically digested with 0.227 mg/mL collagenase/dispase (Sigma-Aldrich; Cat#10269638001) and 50 U/mL DNase (Sigma-Aldrich; Cat#11284932001) at 37 °C for 45 min with inversion of the tubes every 15 min. Postdigestion, brain homogenate was passed through a 70 μm filter (Corning; Cat#352350), washed with cRPMI, and centrifuged at 1,800 rpm for 10 min. Filtered brain homogenate was then resuspended with 20 mL of 40% Percoll (Cytiva; Cat#17-0891-02) and centrifuged at 650 g for 25 min. Myelin was removed via aspiration of upper layer. Brain samples were then resuspended in cRPMI. Spleens were mechanically homogenized and passed through a 40 μm filter (Corning; Cat#352340), then washed with cRPMI and centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 5 min. Samples were resuspended in 1 mL of RBC lysis buffer (0.16 M NH4Cl) for 2 min. Cells were washed with cRPMI and centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 5 min and resuspended in cRPMI. For intracellular cytokine staining, cells were resuspended in 200 µL cRPMI with 20 µg/mL brefeldin A (BFA) (Selleckchem; Cat#S7046) or a combination of 20 µg/mL BFA, 200 ng/mL PMA (Sigma-Aldrich; Cat#P1585), and 1 µg/mL ionomycin (Sigma-Aldrich; Cat#I0634) for 5 h at 37 °C. Cells suspensions were enumerated in a 1:10 dilution of 0.4% trypan blue solution (Sigma-Aldrich; Cat#T8154) and manually counted on a hemocytometer (Hausser Scientific; Cat#3110) using a DM 2000 LED brightfield microscope. Brains collected for cell lysate cytokine quantification by ELISA were placed into digestion buffer (HBSS with calcium and magnesium [Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat# 14025092], 50 U/mL DNase, 4 U/mL Papain [Worthington Biochemical; Cat#LS003124]). Brains were passed through an 18G needle and incubated for 15 min at 37 °C; this was repeated 3 times over a 45 min incubation period. Postdigestion, brain homogenate was passed through a 70 μm filter and washed with microglia growth media (MGM) (DMEM/F12 [Gibco; Cat#11320-033], 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin), and centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 5 min. Myelin was removed via Percoll gradient as previously described. Cells were washed with 5 mL of MGM and centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 5 min. Cells were resuspended in MGM and enumerated as previously described. 250,000 cells were plated per well into a 96-well plate in a total volume of 200 μL of MGM. Samples were incubated overnight at 37 °C. To measure maximal cytokine levels, cells were treated with Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich; Cat#028SK001) (final concentration 0.1%) 30 min before collection. To collect supernatant, the plate was centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 2 min and supernatant was transferred to a new 96-well plate and stored at −20 °C.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions were plated into a 96 well plate and centrifuged at 2,000 rpm for 2 min. Cells were resuspended in 50 µL Fc Block: FACS buffer (1× PBS, 0.2% bovine serum albumin [BSA] [Bioworld; Cat#9048-46-8], and 2 mM EDTA [Invitrogen; Cat#AM9260G]) with 0.1 μg/mL 2.4G2 antibody (BioXCell; Cat#CUS-HB-197) and 0.1% rat gamma globulin (Jackson Immunoresearch; Cat#012-000-002) and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. Without washing off the Fc block, 50 µL FACS buffer containing antibodies for surface markers and fixable live/dead viability dye were added to the cells. Cells were incubated for 30 min at 4 °C and then washed twice with 50 µL FACS buffer. Cells were fixed with fixation/permeabilization solution (eBioscience; Cat#00-5123-43 and Cat#00-5223-56) for 20 min at room temperature or at 4 °C overnight. For intracellular staining, cells were then washed twice with 50 µL 1× permeabilization buffer (eBioscience; Cat#00-8333-56) and stained for intracellular markers in 1× permeabilization buffer for 30 min at 4 °C. Finally, cells were resuspended in 200 µL FACS buffer and acquired using a 3- or 5-laser Cytek Aurora Flow Cytometry System. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software V10.10.0 (TreeStar). The Fixable Viability Dye eFluor 780 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#50-112-9035) or eFluor 506 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#65-0866-18) was used at a dilution of 1:800. The following antibodies were used at a dilution of 1:200: CD45-AF700 (BioLegend; Cat#103128), CD11b-PerCP Cy5.5 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#45-0112-80), CD11b-APC-eFluor 780 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#47-0112-82), Ly6G-BV711 (BioLegend; Cat#127643), iNOS-APC (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#17-5920-82), MHCII-Super Bright 780 (Fisher Scientific; Cat#78-5321-82), MHCII-PE Cy5 (Fisher Scientific; Cat#15-5322-81), CD11c-eFluor 506 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#69-0114-80), TCRβ-APC (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#17-5961-81), CD3e-FITC (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#11-0031-85), CD3e-PE Cy7 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#25-0031-81), CD4-BV650 (Fisher Scientific; Cat#563232), CD8-BV421 (Fisher Scientific; Cat#563898), Foxp3-eFlour 450 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#48-5773-82), CD44-PE-eFlour 610 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#61-0441-82), CD62L-NovaFluor Blue 660-40S (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat# M006T02B07-A), IFNγ-PerCP Cy5.5 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#45-7311-82), TNFα-PE (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#12-7321-81), TNFα-AF488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#53-7321-82), NK1.1-FITC (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#11-0031-85), CD19-FITC (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#11-0193-82), CD19-BV711 (Fisher Scientific; Cat#563157), B220-FITC (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#53-0452-82), B220-PE Cy5 (Fisher Scientific; Cat#553091), IL-12p40-APC (BioLegend; Cat#505205), CD64-PE Cy7 (BioLegend; Cat#139314), NK1.1-Super Bright 780 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#78-5941-82), Tbet-PE Cy7 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#25-5825-80), and Eomes-eFluor 450 (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#48-4875-82). Ly6C-PE (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#12-5932-82) was used at a dilution of 1:400.

Flow cytometry gating strategy

For peritoneum and spleen, the following gates were used: total IL-12+ cells (size, singlets, live, IL-12+), IL-12+ DCs (size, singlets, live, CD3/B220/CD19/NK1.1−, CD64−, CD11c+, MHCII+, IL-12+), NK cells (size, singlets, live, NK1.1+), NK cell cytokine (size, singlets, live, NK1.1+, IFNγ or TNFα+), CD4 cells (size, singlets, live, TCRβ+, CD4+), CD4 cell cytokine (size, singlets, live, TCRβ+, CD4+, IFNγ or TNFα+), CD8 cells (size, singlets, live, TCRβ+, CD8+), CD8 cell cytokine (size, singlets, live, TCRβ+, CD8+, IFNγ or TNFα+), ILC1s (size, singlets, live, NK1.1+, CD127+, Tbet+), ILC1 cytokine (size, singlets, live, NK1.1+, CD127+, Tbet+, IFNγ or TNFα+), myeloid TNFα (size, singlets, live, CD45+, CD11b+, TNFα+), neutrophils (size, singlets, live, CD45+, CD11b+, Ly6G+), infiltrating monocytes (size, singlets, live, CD45+, CD11b+, Ly6G−, Ly6Chi, MHCII+), iNOS-producing inflammatory monocytes (size, singlets, live, CD45+, CD11b+, Ly6G−, Ly6Chi, iNOS+), NKT cells (size, singlets, live, NK1.1+, TCRβ+), and NKT cell cytokine (size, singlets, live, NK1.1+, TCRβ+, IFNγ or TNFα+).

For brain, the following gating strategies were used: neutrophils (size, singlets, live, CD45hi, CD11b+, Ly6G+), infiltrating monocytes (size, singlets, live, CD45hi, CD11b+, Ly6G−, Ly6Chi, MHCII+), iNOS producing inflammatory monocytes (size, singlets, live, CD45hi, CD11b+, Ly6G−, Ly6Chi, iNOS+), CD4 cells (size, singlets, live, TCRβ+, CD4+), CD4 cell cytokine (size, singlets, live, TCRβ+, CD4+, IFNγ or TNFα+), CD8 cells (size, singlets, live, TCRβ+, CD8+), and CD8 cell cytokine (size, singlets, live, TCRβ+, CD8+, IFNγ or TNFα+).

Elisa

Serum, peritoneal exudate fluid, and cell lysate cytokine levels were detected according to the manufacturer’s instructions using Quantikine ELISA (Mouse IL-12 p40 allele specific [R&D Systems; Cat#M1240]) and DuoSet ELISA (Mouse IFN-gamma [R&D Systems; Cat# DY485], Mouse TNF-alpha [R&D Systems; Cat#DY410-05], Mouse IL-1 alpha/IL-1F1 [R&D Systems; Cat #DY400-05], Mouse IL-18 [R&D Systems; Cat# DY7625-05]) with the DuoSet Ancillary Reagent Kit 2 (R&D Systems; Cat# DY008B).

Parasite burden assessments

Parasite burden was measured by real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) of genomic DNA (gDNA). gDNA was isolated from mouse peritoneum exudate cells (PECs) and brain, with brain tissue first being homogenized in PBS using the Omni TH tissue homogenizer (Omni International), using the Isolate II Genomic DNA Kit (Bioline; Cat#BIO-52067). The T. gondii 529 bp repeat region was amplified using the SensiFAST Probe No-ROX Kit (Bioline; Cat#BIO-86005) and CFX384 Real-Time System (Bio-Rad) as previously described.60 A total of 500 ng of gDNA was loaded into each reaction. The number of T. gondii genomes per µg of tissue DNA was quantified by standard curve ranging from 3 to 300,000 genome copies. To measure brain parasite burden by cyst counts, whole brains were passed through an 18G then 22G needle to mechanically homogenize the tissue. Cysts were enumerated as described in Parasite and Infection.

Real-time quantitative PCR

To measure gene expression, PECs were homogenized directly in TRIzol (Fisher Scientific; Cat#15-596-026), while brain samples were loaded into tubes (Sarstedt; Cat# 72.693.005) containing TRIzol reagent and zirconia/silica beads (Biospec; Cat#11079110z) and homogenized for 30 s with a Mini-bead beater machine (Biospec; Cat# 607). RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The High-Capacity Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems; Cat# 4374967) was used to generate cDNA. Quantitative PCR was performed using 2× Taq-based Master Mix (Bioline; Cat#21105) and TaqMan gene expression assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#4331182). Samples were run on a CFX384 Real-Time System thermocycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Genes were normalized to murine Hprt and relative expression was calculated using the 2(−ΔΔCT) method.61 The following Thermo Fisher Scientific mouse gene probes were used: Hprt (Mm00446968_m1), Cxcl1 (Mm00445235_m1), Cxcl2 (Mm00436450_m1), Ccl2 (Mm00441242_m1), Ccr2 (Mm04207877_m1), Zbp1 (Mm01247052_m1) Nos2 (Mm00440502_m1), Ifng (Mm01168134_m1), Tnf (Mm00443258_m1), Il1a (Mm00439620_m1), Il1b (Mm00434228_m1), Icam1 (Mm00516023_m1), and Vcam1 (Mm01320970_m1).

MERFISH (Multiplexed Error-Robust Fluorescence in Situ Hybridization)

To measure spatially resolved RNA transcripts in individually segmented nuclei in whole brain slices, a 342-gene panel was created including housekeeping genes, genes to differentiate cell types, and genes related to inflammation and cell death. C57BL/6 mice were injected intraperitoneally with saline or Me49 and sacrificed 28 d postinjection (dpi). Dissected brains were fresh frozen (embedded in OCT and frozen in isopentane chilled with liquid nitrogen) then sectioned at 10 µm thickness (Leica CM1950) directly onto MERSCOPE slides (Vizgen). MERSCOPE User Guide for Fresh and Fixed Frozen Tissue Sample Preparation Rev D with amendments made based on MERSCOPE Quick Guide Modified Fixation for Fresh & Fixed Frozen Sample Preparation Rev A and MERSCOPE Technical Note Improving Tissue Adherence Rev A were used to prepare slides. The Vizgen Post-Processing Tool (v1.1.2) was used for cell segmentation and configured to use the Cellpose cyto2 model. DAPI+ nuclear signal from the third z-plane was used for cylindrical segmentation. Segmentation output files generated by VPT were imported into a Python 3 environment using Squidpy (v1.2.2) to construct an AnnData (v0.10.4) cell-by-gene expression matrix. To filter out low-quality or false cell profiles, nuclei were only retained if they contained more than 20 total transcripts. Additionally, nuclei total transcript counts, number of detected genes, and cell volume had to fall within the following thresholds: total transcript counts and detected genes greater than 20 and below the 99th percentile, nuclear volume within the 1st to 99th percentiles. Samples were integrated into a shared latent space using scVI-tools (v1.0.4). High-quality cells were then annotated through Leiden clustering and differential expression of canonical cell type marker genes analysis using the Wilcoxon rank sum test in Scanpy (v1.9.6). From the saline injected (“naïve”) group, 55,399 cells were segmented and 32,247 transcripts per field of view were detected; for the Me49 injected section (“28 dpi”) 58,479 cells with 29,758 transcripts per field of view were detected. Vizgen MERSCOPE Visualizer software was used to produce figures.

Statistical analysis

Experiments with only 1 experimental replicate were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software) unpaired t test or 1-way analysis of variance. Experiments with multiple replicates were analyzed in R using a randomized block analysis of variance and least-squares means paired with a Tukey post hoc analysis to adjust P values for multiple comparisons. This analysis accounts for the biological variability between infections with experimental group modeled as a fixed effect and experimental day modeled as a random effect.62 All data were graphed in GraphPad Prism 9. Outliers were identified and removed using ROUTs method with a Q value of 1. P values are indicated as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.

Results

Zbp1 deficiency impaired parasite restriction, immune cell recruitment, and cytokine production at the site of infection 5 dpi

To explore the role of Zbp1 during acute T. gondii infection, we infected C57BL/6 (WT) mice and Zbp1−/− mice by intraperitoneal injection with 10 cysts of the type II T. gondii strain, Me49. Zbp1 expression increases in the PECs throughout the acute stage of infection (Fig. 1A). At 5 dpi, we found a 2-fold increase in parasite burden in the PECs of Zbp1−/− mice compared with WT mice (Fig. 1B). Increased parasite burden is typically due to an impaired immune response, including IL-12 production, which is critical for driving early immune responses against T. gondii.18,32,38,39,63 We measured IL-12p40 levels by ELISA in the PEC fluid and serum and found a ∼40% decrease in IL-12p40 levels in the PEC fluid, and conversely, a ∼35% increase in the serum of Zbp1−/− mice compared with WT mice (Fig. 1C, D). To measure IL-12 production via flow cytometry, we collected PECs at 5 dpi and incubated the cells with BFA for 5 h at 37 °C to inhibit protein secretion. We quantified the total number of IL-12+ within the PECs by flow cytometry and surprisingly, we found no difference in the total IL-12+ cell populations between WT and Zbp1−/− mice (Fig. 1E, F). Dendritic cells have previously been shown to be the main producers of IL-12 during T. gondii infection.16 To identify the cell populations producing IL-12 we subgated the total IL-12 population to explore immune cell populations (Fig. 1E–H). Indeed, we found that DCs (Lineage− [CD3/B220/CD19/NK1.1], CD64−CD11c+MHCII+) were the main producers of IL-12 within the PECs (Fig. 1G, H). Importantly, there was no difference in either number of percentage of total IL-12+ cells within any cell population between our WT and Zbp1−/− mice. While confounding, this data demonstrate a deficit in IL-12 cytokine at the site of infection that could contribute to the increase in parasite burden.

DC production of IL-12 induces NK cell and T cell production of IFNγ, a main driver of T. gondii restriction. We next assessed IFNγ production by ELISA and found Zbp1−/− mice had a ∼57% reduction of IFNγ levels in the PEC fluid compared with WT, although this reduction was not observed in the serum of the Zbp1−/− mice (Fig. 2A, B). Due to the minimal adaptive immune response at 5 dpi, we hypothesized that NK cells would be the critical cell population for IFNγ production. To measure PEC NK and T cell capacity to produce cytokines, we collected PECs at 5 dpi and treated the cells with a combination of BFA, PMA, and ionomycin for 5 h at 37 °C and then quantified cell populations by spectral flow cytometry. Indeed, in the peritoneum we found a ∼40% decrease in the overall number of NK1.1+ cells which resulted in a ∼55% decrease in the number of NK1.1+IFNγ+ cells (Fig. 2D–G). We found no difference in the number of CD4+ T cells but an increase in the number and frequency of CD4+IFNγ+ cells (Fig. 2H–K). We found no difference in the number of CD8+ T cells or the number or frequency of CD8+IFNγ+ cells (Fig. 2L–O). Graphs are represented as paired groups based on experimental day to account for experimental variability (Fig. 2F, G, J, K, N, O). Exploring group 1 innate lymphoid cells (ILC1s) (NK1.1+CD127+Tbet+), we found a significant increase in the number of ILC1s in the PECs of Zbp1−/− mice compared with WT (Fig. 2P). Interestingly, we found a 53-fold increase in the number of IFNγ+ ILC1s and a 15-fold increase in frequency of IFNγ+ cells within the ILC1 population of Zbp1−/− mice (Fig. 2Q–S). Importantly, the average number of NK1.1+IFNγ+ cells in WT mice was about 10-fold higher than the average CD4+IFNγ+ and CD8+IFNγ+ cell number, and 1,000-fold higher than the average WT ILC1 IFNγ+ number. While there was a drastic increase in the number of IFNγ+ ILC1s in the Zbp1−/− mice, the number of NK1.1+IFNγ+ cells was 100-fold larger than the number of IFNγ+ ILC1s. Thus, NK1.1-expressing cells are the main producer of IFNγ in the peritoneum at this time point.

TNFα is also a critical cytokine for restricting T. gondii throughout the course of infection, and early work found that TNFα, in conjunction with IL-12, is required for maximal production of IFNγ by NK cells during T. gondii infection.63–65 We assessed TNFα production at 5 dpi in the PECs and found Zbp1−/− mice had a ∼45% reduction of TNFα cytokine in the PEC fluid compared with WT, but no difference in the serum (Fig. 3A, B). Numerous cells can produce TNFα including NK cells, T cells and myeloid cells.66 Within the PEC population, we found a decrease in the number and frequency of NK1.1+TNFα+ (∼75% decrease) and CD45+CD11b+TNFα+ (∼50% decrease) cells but no difference in the number and frequency of CD4+TNFα+ or CD8+ TNFα+ cells (Fig. 3C–N). Of note, CD45+CD11b+ generally includes myeloid cells as well as NK cells and ILCs. We found an increase in the number and frequency of TNFα+ ILC1s in the PECs of Zbp1−/− mice compared with WT mice; however, this was a minor population of TNFα-producing cells (Fig. 3O–Q). From this we conclude that impaired NK1.1+ cell and CD45+CD11b+ cell production of TNFα in the peritoneum contributes to the reduction in TNFα seen in the PEC fluid at 5 dpi.

During the acute phase of infection, Ly6G+ neutrophils and Ly6Chi inflammatory monocytes migrate to the peritoneum.22,23,25 We examined immune cell populations in the peritoneum by flow cytometry at 5 dpi and found a 3-fold decrease in the number of Ly6G+ neutrophils in Zbp1−/− mice compared with WT (Fig. 4A, B). Neutrophil recruitment is driven by CXCL1 and CXCL2.22 Interestingly, we found an increase in the expression of Cxcl1 in Zbp1−/−PECs and no difference in Cxcl2 (Fig. 4C). Additionally, we found a ∼2-fold decrease in the number of Ly6ChiMHCII+ inflammatory monocytes within the Zbp1−/− PECs (Fig. 4D, E). CCR2-expressing monocyte recruitment is driven by CCL2.22 Interestingly, we found a decrease in the expression of both Ccl2 and Ccr2 in the PECs from Zbp1−/− mice at 5 dpi (Fig. 4F).

Based on the cytokine and immune cell population analysis, we hypothesized that the increased parasite burden observed in Zbp1−/− mice is primarily due to a localized impaired immune response. Moreover, a reduction in immune cell recruitment to the site of infection could implicate an overall impairment in the immune system. We assessed the spleens of WT and Zbp1−/− mice to identify potential intrinsic immune deficits at 5 dpi. We found no differences in overall numbers of NK cells, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, or NKT cells including subpopulations that are IFNγ+ or TNFα+ in the spleens of Zbp1−/− mice compared with WT controls at this time point (Fig. S1A–L). Additionally, we found no difference in the number of IL-12+ DCs in the spleens of Zbp1−/− mice compared with WT control mice (Fig. S1M). We also found no difference in the number of Ly6G+ neutrophils but an increase in the number of CD11b+Ly6Chi monocytes between Zbp1−/− and WT mice (Fig. S1N, O). Additionally, to assess if Zbp1−/− mice had impairments in immune populations at baseline, we quantified immune populations present in the PECs in mice that were not infected with T. gondii. We found no differences in overall percentage of B cells, myeloid cells, T cells, NKT cells, or NK cells between WT and Zbp1−/− mice at baseline (Fig. S1P). The lack of immune differences seen in the spleen and the elevated/equivalent levels of IL-12p40, IFNγ, TNFα in the serum of Zbp1−/− compared with WT mice suggests no general impairment in the immune system of Zbp1−/− mice. Overall, our data demonstrate a deficit in parasite control at 5 dpi that is driven by an impairment in cytokine production and immune cell recruitment to the site of infection in Zbp1−/− mice.

Caspase-1, but not RIPK3 or caspase-8, plays a role in the immune response at 5 dpi

ZBP1 is a regulator of PANoptosis.67 Here, we explored components of pyroptosis, necroptosis, and apoptosis utilizing Casp1−/−, Casp8+/−Ripk3−/−, and Casp8−/−Ripk3−/− mice to explore the immune response within the peritoneum at 5 dpi. Previous studies have shown that caspase-1 plays a role in neutrophil and monocyte recruitment at 8 d post–T. gondii infection.25,68 As expected, we found a dramatic decrease in the number of neutrophils (∼85% decrease) and monocytes (∼45% decrease) in the peritoneum of Casp1−/− mice compared with WT mice at 5 dpi but no difference in neutrophils or monocytes between WT and Casp8+/−Ripk3−/− or Casp8−/−Ripk3−/− mice (Fig. S2A–D). Additionally, we found a 65% decrease in the number of NK1.1+IFNγ+ cells in the peritoneum of the Casp1−/− mice compared with WT mice (Fig. S2E). We also measured a large decrease in both IFNγ and IL-12p40 levels in the PEC fluid and serum of the Casp1−/− mice (Fig. S2F, G). Surprisingly, we found a decrease in parasite burden in the PECs of Casp1−/− mice compared with WT mice (Fig. S2H). In the Casp8+/−Ripk3−/− mice we found no difference in NK1.1+IFNγ+ cell number, IFNγ levels, or PEC parasite levels (Fig. S2I–K). In the Casp8−/−Ripk3−/− mice, we found a decrease in NK1.1+IFNγ+ cell number but an increase in PEC fluid IFNγ levels, and no difference in the serum (Fig. S2I, J). The differences seen in IFNγ+ cell number and cytokine levels did not impact parasite burden in the PECs of Casp8−/−Ripk3−/− mice (Fig. S2K). Here, we identified immune deficits in the Casp1−/− mice that have phenotypic similarities to the immune deficits seen in the Zbp1−/− mice at 5 dpi. However, we found antithetical changes in parasite levels between the Zbp1−/− and WT mice compared with the Casp1−/− and WT mice in the PECs at this time point. We did not observe any overlapping immune deficits in the Zbp1−/− mice and Casp8+/−Ripk3−/− or Casp8−/−Ripk3−/− mice, suggesting that ZBP1 does not activate necroptosis or apoptosis during T. gondii infection.

In Zbp1−/− mice, parasite burden and immune cell recruitment deficiency is resolved by 8 dpi

NK cell production of IFNγ is crucial for early restriction of T. gondii, but the adaptive T cell response becomes critical for parasite control and cytokine production as infection progresses.24 To explore the impact of the adaptive immune response in Zbp1−/− mice during the later phase of acute T. gondii infection, we measured parasite burden and immune populations in the PECs at 8 dpi. Because of the increase in parasite burden in the PECs of Zbp1−/− mice compared with WT at 5 dpi, we expected an increase in parasite burden in the PECs of Zbp1−/− mice at 8 dpi. Surprisingly, we found no difference in parasite burden in the PECs at this time point (Fig. 5A). Thus, we conclude that the impaired parasite restriction seen at 5 dpi has been resolved by 8 dpi.

To understand the resolution of parasite control, we quantified immune cell populations and cytokine production in the PECs. We found no difference in the number of Ly6G+ neutrophils, CD11b+Ly6ChiMHCII+ inflammatory monocytes, or CD11b+Ly6ChiiNOS+ cells in the peritoneum between the two groups (Fig. 5B–D). Measuring cytokine levels by ELISA we found no difference in IFNγ or IL-12p40 levels in peritoneum exudate fluid or serum between WT and Zbp1−/− mice (Fig. 5E, F). In addition to IL-12, IL-18 has also been shown to play a role in reinforcing IFNγ production at 8 dpi.25,69 We found no difference in IL-18 levels in peritoneum exudate fluid; however, we did find an increase in IL-18 serum levels in the Zbp1−/− mice compared with WT (Fig. 5G). At this time point (8 dpi) the adaptive immune system is activated and T cells play an important role in IFNγ production.70 Exploring immune cell populations in the peritoneum that drive IFNγ production, we found no difference in overall number of NK cells, NKT cells, or CD4+ T cells; however, we did find a decrease in the number of CD8+ T cells between Zbp1−/− and WT mice (Fig. 5H–K). To measure cytokine production, we stimulated cells with BFA/PMA/ionomycin and found about 50% fewer CD3−NK1.1+IFNγ+ cells but no difference in frequency between WT and Zbp1−/− mice (Fig. 5L, M). A reduction in IFNγ was also seen in the NKT cell population, with about 65% fewer CD3+NK1.1+IFNγ+ cells and no difference in frequency within the CD3+NK1.1+ population (Fig. 5N, O). We found no significant difference in CD4+IFNγ+ cell number with a slight but significant decrease in frequency of CD4+IFNγ+ cells between the 2 groups (Fig. 5P, Q). Finally, we found a 40% decrease in CD8+IFNγ+ cell number driven by the overall decrease in CD8+ T cell number in the Zbp1−/− mice (Fig. 5R, S). Importantly, the population of CD4+IFNγ+ cells (∼270,000 cells in WT mice) was the largest population of IFNγ+ cells by cell type; compared with CD8+IFNγ+ cells (∼150,000 cells in WT mice), CD3−NK1.1+IFNγ+ cells (∼40,000 cells in WT mice), and CD3+NK1.1+IFNγ+ cells (∼40,000 cells in WT mice) (Fig. 5I, K, M, O). We conclude that the increase in parasite burden in the peritoneum seen in the Zbp1−/− mice at 5 dpi is ameliorated at 8 dpi likely due to IFNγ production by CD4+ T cells. Additionally, the recruitment deficit of neutrophils and monocytes seen in the Zbp1−/− mice at 5 dpi was also resolved.

Zbp1 deficiency leads to increased parasite burden and impaired inflammatory responses in the brain during chronic T. gondii infection

Around 1 wk postinfection, the parasite crosses the blood-brain barrier, invades the brain, and takes up chronic residence.26 Impairment in restricting T. gondii during early stages of infection can result in increased parasite seeding into the brain and long-term increases in parasite burden. At 8 dpi, we found no difference in brain parasite levels measured by quantitative PCR between WT and Zbp1−/− mice, suggesting no difference in the seeding of the parasite into the brain (Fig. 6A). However, by 12 dpi we did see an increase in brain parasite levels in the Zbp1−/− mice (Fig. 6B). The parasite burden was also elevated at 28 and 42 dpi in Zbp1−/− mice compared with WT, suggesting a brain-intrinsic deficit in T. gondii control (Fig. 6C, D). Zbp1 is one of the most highly upregulated genes in the brain in response to T. gondii infection.53–56 To understand the dynamics of Zbp1 upregulation we measured Zbp1 expression levels in the brain at 8, 12, 28, and 42 dpi (Fig. 6E). We found Zbp1 to be highly upregulated at 28 and 42 dpi. To gain an understanding of which cell types express Zbp1 and other PANoptosis/NF-κB related genes in the brain we utilized a reference MERFISH (Multiplexed Error-Robust Fluorescence in Situ Hybridization) dataset. We found that Zbp1 is very lowly expressed in the uninfected mouse brain (Fig. 6F–H). However, at 28 dpi Zbp1 is highly expressed in numerous cell populations including T cells, macrophages, microglia, endothelial cells, and oligodendrocytes (Fig. 6I–K).

Measuring critical immune populations in the brain at 28 dpi by flow cytometry, we found no difference in the number of infiltrating Ly6G+ neutrophils (Fig. 7A); however, we did find ∼20% decrease in the number of CD11b+Ly6Chi and CD11b+Ly6ChiiNOS+ inflammatory monocytes in Zbp1−/− mice compared with WT (Fig. 7B, C). Concertedly, we found a robust decrease in expression of Ccl2, Ccr2, and Nos2 in brains of Zbp1−/− mice (Fig. 7D). Parasite control is regulated by IFNγ and TNFα production. Monocyte recruitment into the brain is driven, in part, by TNFα signaling.23,71 Exploring T and NK cell populations in the brain, we found no difference in the overall number of CD4+ or CD8+ T cells but a decrease in NK and NKT cells between WT and Zbp1−/− mice (Fig. 7E–H). To explore immune cell populations in the brain that can produce IFNγ and TNFα, we stimulated cells with BFA/PMA/ionomycin. Interestingly, we observed a significant decrease (∼33%) in the number of CD4+IFNγ+ and CD8+IFNγ+ cells (Fig. 7I, J). We found no difference in CD3−NK1.1+IFNγ+ cells but a 2-fold decrease in CD3+NK1.1+IFNγ+ cells (Fig. 7K, L). Exploring TNFα production, we found significant decrease (∼33%) in the number of CD4+TNFα+, and CD8+TNFα+ T cells in the Zbp1−/− mice compared with WT mice (Fig. 7M, N). We found about a 3- and 3-fold decrease in CD3−NK1.1+TNFα+ cells and CD3+NK1.1+TNFα+ cells, respectively, in the Zbp1−/− mice compared with WT mice (Fig. 7O, P). Further, expression levels of Ifng and Tnf were decreased (∼50%) in the Zbp1−/− mice. IFN-regulated genes (Gbp2, Igra6, and Irgb6) were also downregulated in the Zbp1−/− mice (Fig. 7Q). An impairment in cytokine production in the Zbp1−/− mice suggests that ZBP1 either directly or indirectly impacts T cell and NK cell IFNγ and TNFα production.

Pyroptosis is an inflammatory form of cell death that leads to the release of IL-1 cytokines.72 Previous research exploring the pyroptosis pathway during chronic T. gondii infection found a role for IL-1α, but not IL-1β, in mediating parasite control and immune cell recruitment. IL-1 receptor signaling was shown to regulate the recruitment of iNOS-producing inflammatory monocytes and the expression of vascular adhesion molecules important for immune cell infiltration into the brain (i.e. Icam1, Vcam1, and Ccl2).42 Due to the role of ZBP1 in regulating pyroptosis and the decrease in the number of CD11b+Ly6ChiiNOS+ inflammatory monocytes seen in the Zbp1−/− mice, we measured IL-1α from lysate of brain-purified cells at 28 dpi. We found a decrease in total IL-1α present in the Zbp1−/− mice compared with WT, suggesting that ZBP1 impacts basal inflammatory tone of the infected brain (Fig. 7R). Additionally, we found a ∼50% decrease in expression levels of numerous inflammatory markers and vascular adhesion molecules (Il1a, Il1b, Il1r1, Icam1, and Vcam1) in the brains of Zbp1−/− mice (Fig. 7S). In sum, during chronic T. gondii infection, we find that ZBP1 plays a role in control of the parasite in the brain. Zbp1 deficiency resulted in impairment of key inflammatory pathways involved in cytokine production and immune cell recruitment.

Discussion

ZBP1 has primarily been characterized as an innate sensor and a critical modulator of viral infections.67 In multiple in vivo and in vitro models of viral infections, ZBP1 has been shown to be an important regulator of inflammation to limit viral replication and pathology.67 In several respiratory viral infections, ZBP1 contributes to immune cell recruitment and lung inflammation.73,74 In central nervous system viral infections, ZBP1 confers antiviral effects within neurons and contributes to host survival.75,76 In studies using bone marrow-derived macrophages, lipopolysaccharide, Aspergilus fumigaus, or Candida albicans induce ZBP1-mediated PANoptosis.67 However, the specific role for ZBP1 in defense against bacterial and fungal infections has not been extensively studied in vivo. Far fewer studies have focused on the role of ZBP1 in parasitic infections. In this study, we identified a role for ZBP1 in eliciting immune responses against the intracellular parasite T. gondii during both the systemic acute and chronic brain stages of infection. During the early stage of infection, Zbp1 deficiency impacted parasite control, cytokine production, and immune cell recruitment to the peritoneal cavity. During the chronic stage of infection, we confirmed a role for ZBP1 in parasite control in the brain and identified deficits in cytokine responses.77 Our findings are consistent with prior reports showing that ZBP1 drives inflammatory responses and plays a role in controlling pathogen levels, similar to the roles described for ZBP1 in multiple viral infection models.4,67,73,75,76 Our work and that of others have demonstrated that ZBP1 is an important regulator of both innate and adaptive immune responses.

While we found that ZBP1 plays an important role in restricting T. gondii, it remains unclear what activates ZBP1 during parasite infection. ZBP1 detects Z-NAs in response to certain viral infections, in response to cellular stress, and during transcription and translation.67 At baseline, Zbp1 is highly expressed in immune cells.53 We, and others, found Zbp1 to be highly upregulated in the brain during chronic T. gondii infection, and we identified Zbp1 expression in brain-resident cells as well as in brain-infiltrating immune cells.55,56 Importantly, how T. gondii is sensed during infection, especially in the brain, has yet to be completely elucidated.42 The high level of Zbp1 expression throughout the brain and immune compartment would suggest that various cells are primed for ZBP1-mediated sensing during T. gondii infection. As an intracellular parasite, T. gondii could potentially induce Z-NAs in infected cells through release of parasite DNA upon vacuole disruption or host cell DNA damage induced by parasite secreted proteins.78–81 However, given a limited number of cells are directly infected by the parasite, ZBP1 is likely responding to broader sources of Z-NAs generated in response to the overall infection.82,83 One such source of Z-NAs could be mitochondrial DNA release in response to oxidative stress.84–86 T. gondii infection results in increased levels of reactive oxygen species, both in vitro as well as in the brains of infected animals, and reactive oxygen species–induced cellular stress can induce ZBP1 activation.23,38,67,87 Z-DNA may also be induced by enhanced transcription during infection, particularly in immune cells. During T. gondii infection, there is robust expansion and differentiation of immune populations.23 A proliferative/activated state increases levels of RNA splicing and exposure of short interspersed nuclear elements, both sources of Z-NA.67,88 In this study, we found a reduction in key regulators of parasite control: immune cell recruitment and cytokine production in both the peritoneum and brain of Zbp1−/− mice. As immune cells highly express Zbp1, it is likely that ZBP1 activation is a crucial regulator of inflammation within the immune compartment.89 While there are many potential sources of Z-NAs, more work will be needed to identify the cell type specific mechanism of ZBP1 activation.

ZBP1 is a well-established regulator of cell death pathways and inflammation in response to viral infection. One mechanism by which ZBP1 could aid in pathogen control is through its involvement in cell death pathways. Here we explored a role for components of pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis, (caspase-1, caspase-8, and RIPK3, respectively) at 5 dpi. One of the striking immune deficits that we observed in the Zbp1−/− mice at 5 dpi was a defect in neutrophil and monocyte recruitment into the peritoneum. Previous studies from our lab demonstrated a role for caspase-1 in neutrophil and monocyte recruitment to the site of infection at 8 dpi, but a sensor upstream of caspase-1 has not been identified.25 Caspase-1–mediated pyroptosis leads to the release of inflammatory mediators that are known to initiate immune responses during acute T. gondii infection.72,90 Mice deficient in components of the IL1 receptor family (Il1r1−/−, Il1rap−/−, and Il1rl1−/−) exhibit a decrease in neutrophil recruitment during acute T. gondii infection.25,42,91 Additionally, the alarmin S100A11 was shown to recruit monocytes during T. gondii infection and can be released via caspase-1/11.68 Thus, we hypothesized that ZBP1 regulates caspase-1–mediated pyroptosis for neutrophil and monocyte recruitment into the peritoneum. As expected, we found Casp1−/− mice had reduced neutrophil and monocyte numbers at the site of infection while Casp8+/−Ripk3−/− and Casp8−/−Ripk3−/− mice had no difference in neutrophil and monocyte recruitment. Thus, we propose that ZBP1 is one potential driver of caspase-1 during the early stage of infection. Interestingly, Casp1−/− mice exhibit an increase in PEC parasite burden at 8 dpi due to a deficit in IL-18 and CD4+ T cell IFNγ production.25 We did not see a similar phenotype in the Zbp1−/− mice at 8 dpi, suggesting that ZBP1 is not responsible for pyroptosis and IL-18 release at 8 dpi. However, there could be multiple sensors, likely varying by cell type and timing, that induce activation of pyroptosis throughout the course of infection.

The other major phenotype we found at 5 dpi in the Zbp1−/− mice was a deficit in IL-12p40 levels in the peritoneal exudate fluid, which was associated with impaired NK1.1-expressing cell number and decreased IFNγ levels in the peritoneum. We question whether ZBP1 directly or indirectly contributes to IL-12 production. While we did see a stark decrease in IL-12p40 levels by ELISA, surprisingly we found no difference in the number of IL-12+ cells by flow cytometry. The lack of a difference in IL-12+ cells but a decrease in overall IL-12 levels may suggest a cell-intrinsic role for ZBP1 in the release of IL-12. We hypothesize that the cells are capable of producing IL-12, as we found in our flow cytometry quantification, but there is an impairment in cytokine release, resulting in a decrease in IL-12 in the PEC fluid. Additionally, IFNγ can regulate IL-12.92,93 Because we found a decrease in IFNγ present in the PEC fluid, this could also contribute to the decrease in IL-12 in the PEC fluid. Curiously, there was an increase in serum IL-12p40 levels in Zbp1−/− mice compared with WT mice at 5 dpi. This could potentially be a result of an overall increase in parasite burden in the PECs. Importantly, we found no difference in immune cells in the spleens of Zbp1−/− mice at 5 dpi, suggesting that systemic immune activation is unimpaired.

Another important phenotype that we found at 5 dpi was a decrease in NK1.1+TNFα+ cells and decreased TNFα cytokine levels in the Zbp1−/− mice. Similar results of NK cell-TNFα impairment was seen in a model of Angiostrongylus cantonensis infection in Zbp1-deficient mice.94 TNF production can be driven by NF-κB signaling and ZBP1 has been shown to regulate NF-κB.67,95 Taken together, we hypothesize a cell-intrinsic mechanism of ZBP1/NF-κB–mediated cytokine production. Overall, our work highlights a role for ZBP1 in various immune responses during the early acute stage of infection and supports a role for caspase-1 functioning downstream of ZBP1.

During the chronic stage of T. gondii infection, we found that Zbp1−/− mice had increased parasite burden, reduced iNOS+ infiltrating inflammatory monocytes, reduced T cell cytokine production, and reduced IL-1α present in the brains of the Zbp1−/− mice. Previous studies have explored components of cell death pathways during the chronic stage of T. gondii infection and demonstrated changes in immune responses and parasite control that are not fully recapitulated in the Zbp1−/− mice. During the chronic stage of infection, Casp1/11−/− mice had increased brain parasite burden and a decrease in iNOS+ infiltrating inflammatory monocytes, Casp8+/−Ripk3−/− mice had no difference in parasite burden but an increase in T cells in the brain, and Casp8−/−Ripk3−/− mice had an 8-fold increase in parasite burden and amplified immune responses.42,46 In the brains of Zbp1−/− mice, we found a decrease in cytokine production that was not seen in the Casp1/11−/−, Casp8+/−Ripk3−/−, or Casp8−/−Ripk3−/− mice, suggesting that ZBP1 could be regulating cytokine production in a cell death–independent manner during chronic T. gondii infection. During Zika virus infection of the central nervous system, ZBP1-RIPK1-RIPK3 signaling restricts viral replication in neurons in a cell death–independent manner.75 ZBP1 playing a role outside of cell death in the brain during Zika infection could implicate an important role for ZBP1 in regulating inflammation during T. gondii brain infection in a cell death–independent manner. The spatial transcriptomics data in this study demonstrated that during chronic T. gondii infection, various cells including infiltrating immune cells, microglia, oligodendrocytes, pericytes, and endothelial cells express Zbp1. Recent work exploring the role of ZBP1 during Angiostrongylus cantonensis brain infection found that Zbp1 deficiency resulted in impaired lymphocyte infiltration into the brain as well as a decrease in the frequency of CD4+TNFα+, NK+TNFα+, and NK+IFNγ+ cells in the brain.94 Defects in T cell cytokine levels mirror the impairment in T cell cytokine production in the brain of Zbp1−/− mice that we found during T. gondii infection, suggesting a potential cell-intrinsic role for ZBP1 in lymphocyte cytokine production. Importantly, T cell production of IFNγ and TNFα can impact monocyte recruitment, glial activation, and endothelium responses during T. gondii infection.65,71,96–98 This could suggest that the impairment seen in T cell cytokine production in Zbp1−/− mice could have implications for other immune deficits seen. Additionally, in the Zbp1−/− mice we found a decrease in the number of iNOS+ inflammatory monocytes and in IL-1α levels during the chronic stage of infection. NF-κB can drive iNOS and IL-1α production, which may suggest a role for ZBP1 in regulating inflammatory molecules in both microglia and monocytes, two cell types that highly express Zbp1.42,95,99,100 One surprising cell type that highly expresses Zbp1 in the brain during infection was oligodendrocytes. Interestingly, previous work from our lab showed that oligodendrocytes highly express IL-33 during T. gondii infection, and IL-33 signaling on astrocytes plays a role in parasite restriction and T cell production of IFNγ.29 IL-33 is an alarmin that is released by cell death/damage and the high level of expression of Zbp1 in oligodendrocytes might suggest a role in IL-33 release.101 ZBP1 activity can be induced by multiple sources of Z-NAs and can regulate numerous cell death pathways as well as NF-κB signaling.67 Thus, it is highly likely that ZBP1 can induce cell type–specific activity. Our work begins to explore the role of ZBP1 during chronic T. gondii infection; however, further work will be necessary to parse the ZBP1-regulated pathways within individual cell populations.

While the primary focus of ZBP1 research in recent years has concentrated on its role in regulating cell death pathways during viral infection, our study highlights the importance of ZBP1 in contributing to inflammatory responses potentially through NF-κB signaling.102 In this study we find a general role for ZBP1 in promoting inflammatory responses in the brain. As an interferon-induced gene, Zbp1 expression can be upregulated in numerous neuroinflammatory states. Recent work has begun to explore a role for ZBP1 in the pathogenesis of Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease, stroke, and schizophrenia and suggests a role for ZBP1 in production of inflammation in the brain.103–107 In a mouse model of AD, ZBP1 regulated transcription of various proinflammatory genes. In both AD and Parkinson’s disease models, Zbp1 disruption was protective against disease pathology and progression.103–105 In models of stroke and schizophrenia, Zbp1 was expressed in microglia and associated with inflammation.106,107 Parsing apart the context-dependent, cell type–specific role for ZBP1 in various neuroinflammatory systems will be beneficial for creating highly targeted therapeutics relevant to multiple neurodegenerative diseases. Overall, our work highlights versatile roles for ZBP1 in immune responses to T. gondii and is an important step in understanding how ZBP1 mediates inflammation in the brain.
