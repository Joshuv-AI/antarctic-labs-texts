Construction of lactylation (LA) risk signature in prostate cancer based on 4D fast DIA L-lactated quantitative genomics
Authors: Fan Zou, Yongchen Jin, Ziteng Zhang, Yishan Zhang, Mingdong Wang, Fangge Zhu, Jinming Qiu, Haoyuan Ye, Yi Fu, Hao Ping
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLactylation (LA) plays a crucial role in regulating protein stability, angiogenesis, and immune modulation. Global lactylation of proteins in prostate cancer cells is a key event in tumor progression. This study aimed to explore the characteristics of LA in patients with prostate cancer (PRAD) and construct a LA-related risk model to predict prognosis.MethodsLA-related genes in prostate cancer were screened through quantitative lactylation proteomics of human tissues from Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University. Based on the TCGA and GEO databases, patients were divided into two LA-related gene clusters. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to identify the heterogeneity of the grouping, and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the clusters were identified. A LA risk model was constructed using Lasso-Cox regression analysis, and its efficacy was verified in the TCGA, GSE116918, and GSE70769 cohorts through K-M curves, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and nomograms. The most representative gene, KCNMA1, was selected for in vitro and animal experiments to verify its association with prostate cancer.ResultsBased on quantitative lactylation proteomics, two LA clusters were identified in prostate cancer and were significantly associated with prognosis. A total of 122 DEGs were screened to construct a gene risk model. The K-M curves verified the differences between the high - and low - risk groups of the model in the test group and the training cohort (test group: P = 0.025; training group: P < 0.001). The ROC curve verified that the prognostic model had good accuracy. The nomogram integrating staging and LA risk factors showed high accuracy and reliability in predicting the prognosis of prostate cancer. The expression of KCNMA1 in PCa was significantly lower than that in NATs, and its expression level decreased with the increase in grading. In cell experiments, overexpression of KCNMA1 promoted the infiltration of M1 macrophages by inhibiting the RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting the proliferation, migration, and invasion of prostate cancer cells. Animal experiments demonstrated that overexpression of KCNMA1 inhibited the growth rate of tumors.ConclusionThe LA risk model constructed in this study can effectively predict the prognosis of prostate cancer and is expected to become a new type of test scoring criterion. KCNMA1 is expected to become a novel target for prostate cancer.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-06990-6.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the second most common malignancy and the fifth leading cause of cancer-related death among men globally, with over 1.4 million new cases and 375,000 deaths annually [1]. Incidence varies widely, being highest in high-income countries due to aging populations, widespread PSA screening, and lifestyle factors, while mortality remains elevated in low-resource regions due to late-stage diagnoses. Risk factors include age (> 65 years), African ancestry, family history, and genetic mutations [2]. Current therapies for localized PCa (surgery, radiotherapy) yield high cure rates, but advanced/metastatic disease remains challenging. Androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) is the cornerstone for metastatic hormone-sensitive PCa (mHSPC), yet most patients progress to castration-resistant PCa (CRPC) within 2–3 years. CRPC treatments (e.g., novel anti-androgens, chemotherapy, PARP inhibitors, radioligands) extend survival but face limitations: drug resistance, severe toxicity, and heterogeneous responses driven by tumor genomics and microenvironmental adaptations [3, 4]. Metastatic CRPC, often involving bone and visceral sites, has a dismal 5-year survival rate (< 30%). Emerging challenges include optimizing early detection, addressing health disparities in access to advanced therapies, and developing biomarkers for personalized treatment. Future directions focus on targeting resistance mechanisms, immunotherapies, and combinatorial strategies to improve outcomes for high-risk and refractory cases.

Lactylation, a recently discovered post-translational modification (PTM), involves the covalent attachment of lactate to specific lysine residues on proteins, regulating their function and cellular processes. Lactylation first identified in 2019 by Zhao Yingming’s team, histone lactylation (e.g., H3K18la, histone H3 lysine 18 lactylation) was shown to modulate gene transcription by altering chromatin structure, linking cellular metabolism to epigenetic regulation. This modification challenges the traditional view of lactate as a metabolic byproduct, highlighting its role as a signaling molecule. Mechanistically, lactonization occurs through the formation of an ester bond between the carboxyl group of lactic acid and the ε-amino group of lysine, and it has been reported that E1A Binding Protein P300 (EP300) [5], Alanyl-TRNA Synthetase (AARS) can be involved in the read/write process of lactonization [6], and lysine ε-amino groups, with histone deacetylases (HDACs) implicated in its removal [7, 8]. Biologically, lactylation drives pro-inflammatory gene expression in macrophages, influences tumor microenvironments by promoting angiogenesis and immune evasion, and may regulate metabolic enzymes and transcription factors. To date, histone and non-histone lactylation have been demonstrated to participate in the progression and metastasis of multiple cancers. Among these, H3K18la has been most extensively studied. Glycolysis promotes pancreatic cancer progression by enhancing H3K18 lactylation at this site [9]. Lactate-mediated H3K18la drives the expression of RUBCNL, thereby promoting resistance to bevacizumab treatment in colorectal cancer [10]. Additionally, H3K18 lactylation enhances immune evasion in non-small cell lung cancer [11]. Research on non-histone proteins is also burgeoning. Elevated NBS1_K388 lactylation and reduced P53_K120/P53_K139 lactylation have been corroborated to slow tumorigenesis [12, 13]. However, research on non-histone lactylation in prostate cancer remains scarce.

In this study, we employed 4D Fast DIA L-lactylation quantitative proteomics to screen genes regulating pan-lactylation in prostate cancer. Based on these genes, patients from the TCGA and GEO databases were classified into lactylation cluster and non-lactylation cluster. Differentially expressed genes between the subtypes were identified, and a prognostic model was constructed to predict the prognosis of prostate cancer patients. Additionally, we selected Potassium Calcium-Activated Channel Subfamily M Alpha 1 (KCNMA1) from the prognostic model for validation. KCNMA1 encodes the α subunit of the calcium-activated BK channel, a protein involved in diverse physiological processes, including smooth muscle contraction, neurotransmitter release, and neuronal excitability [14, 15]. Through in vivo and in vitro experiments, we have for the first time confirmed that KCNMA1 acts as a critical tumor suppressor gene in prostate cancer. Previous studies have shown that KCNMA1 can regulate BKCa channels, and it has also been indicated that BKCa and TRPV4 interact with the MAPK signaling pathway [16, 17]. This provides a theoretical basis for our finding in this paper that KCNMA1 regulates the MAPK signaling pathway [18, 19]. This study provides a promising predictive tool for prostate cancer patients and identifies a potential therapeutic target, offering novel insights into the diagnosis and treatment of prostate cancer.

Materials and methods

Sample Preparation and LC-MS/MS analysis

Specimens were obtained from patients admitted to the Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University between January 2019 and March 2024. For PTM experiments, inhibitors were added to the lysis buffer (1% protease inhibitor cocktail, 1% phosphatase inhibitor for phosphorylation, 50µM PR-619, 3µM TSA and 50mM NAM for acetylation). The remaining debris was removed by centrifugation at 12,000 g at 4 °C for 10 min. Finally, the supernatant was collected and the protein concentration was determined with BCA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Tryptic peptides dissolved in solvent A (0.1% FA) were directly loaded onto an Evotip following the protocol by the manufacturer. Peptides were separated with the preset Whisper 40-SPD (Samples per Day) method on the EvoSep One liquid chromatography system system (EvoSep), with the mobile phase consisted of solvent A and solvent B (0.1% FA in ACN). The peptides were subjected to capillary source followed by the timsTOF Pro2 mass spectrometry. The electrospray voltage applied was 1.5 kV. Precursors and fragments were analyzed with TOF detector. The timsTOF Pro was operated in data independent parallel accumulation serial fragmentation (dia-PASEF) mode. The full MS scan range was set as 300–1700 m/z and 8 PASEF scans were acquired per cycle. The MS/MS isolation window was set as 25 m/z in the range of 398–1023 m/z. Subsequent bioinformatics analysis was supported by PTM Bio (Hangzhou, China). The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE [20] partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD066054.

Collection and preprocessing of data

To obtain RNA-seq data in FPKM format and prognostic data (follow-up time over 30 days) for 495 TCGA prostate cancer (TCGA-PRAD) samples, the UCSC Xena browser (GDC hub) at https://gdc.xenahubs.net was utilized. The log2 (TPM + 1) conversion method was used to convert normalized FPKM values to transcripts per million (TPM). Furthermore, we acquired normalized expression and clinical data for 340 PRAD samples from the GEO database: GSE116918 and GSE70769. If a gene matched multiple probes, only the highest value was retained.

Consensus clustering analysis

The R software package “ConsensusClusterPlus” was used to perform consensus unsupervised clustering analysis to categorize patients into distinct molecular clusters based on LA-related genes expression. The clustering was done using the following criteria. To begin, the cumulative distribution function (CDF) curve gently and smoothly increased. Second, there were no small sample size groups. Finally, clustering increased intra-group correlation while decreasing inter-group correlation. To study changes in LA-related genes during immune infiltration, we employed the ESTIMATE method to assess each patient’s immunological and stromal scores. The CIBERSORT method was also used to compute the fractions of 22 human immune cell subgroups in each PRAD sample [21]. Metabolic signature was characterized using the “IOBR” R package. IOBR is made up of four distinct functional modules [22].

Prognostic model construction

The TCGA-PRAD cohort was chosen for building the LA risk model, and it was divided into two groups: TRAIN and TEST. Univariate Cox regression modeling was used to identify prognostic matrix La center genes impacting overall survival (OS); the genes with p < 0.05 were then entered into the LASSO Cox regression analysis by the glmnet R software package. Based on the median LA risk score, PRAD patients were divided into high and low LA risk groups, and the difference in OS between the two groups was calculated using Kaplan-Meier curves and the log-rank test. The LA risk model was also validated in the test group. The patients were then classified as high-risk or low-risk based on the highest reflection point of the best ROC curve. The Kaplan-Meier technique was used to compare differences in overall survival (OS).

Development and evaluation of nomogram

To quantify the prognostic potential of our LA risk score model in clinical practice, we built a nomogram in R using the “Survival” and “RMS” packages to predict 1, 3, or 5-year OS. The nomogram included a risk score as well as additional clinicopathological characteristics that the Cox regression analysis revealed as independent risk factors. Furthermore, using the “coxph” function of the R package “survival,” we used Schoenfield’s residual test to determine if these risk variables matched the equally proportionate risk hypothesis. Finally, the calibration curves and time-dependent ROC curves were used to assess the robustness of the nomogram.

Analysis of clinical correlations

To investigate the clinical significance of our risk score model, we compared the clinicopathological characteristics of the two groups with distinct risks using the chi-square test. The outcome was displayed as a heat map using the R libraries ‘pheatmap’ and ‘ggpubr’. Clinicopathological characteristics include age, sex, tumor grade, tumor site, and TNM status. Furthermore, a stratification analysis was performed to compare the disparity in risk scores among various clinical groups, such as Age (≤ 65 and > 65), Gleason Score (6–7 and 8–10), T stage (T1-T2 and T3-T4), N stage (N0 and N1), and PSA level (< 10 and > 10).

Gene sets enrichment analysis

Gene ontology sets (c5.go.v7.5.1.symbols.gmt) and curated gene sets (c2.cp.kegg.v7.5.1.symbols.gmt) were downloaded from the GSEA website (https:///www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/downloads.jsp). To compute and visualize the results, the R packages ‘limma,’ ‘org.Hs.eg.db,’ ‘DOSE,’ ‘clusterProfiler,’ and ‘enrichplot’ were utilized.

Immunohistochemistry

Specimens were obtained from patients admitted to the Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University between January 2019 and March 2024. The pathology department supplied the specimen with wax slabs. All patients signed forms of informed assent. This study was granted ethical approval by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University. Detailed clinical information can be found in Table 1. The prepared tissue sections were treated for 10 min with 3% H2O2 according to standard protocol. After rinsing with PBS thrice, the specimens were blocked with a mixture of 3% BSA and 0.3% Triton X-100 for 1 h before being incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody. Furthermore, the samples were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with the appropriate fluorescent secondary antibody. DAPI was used to counterstain the nuclei. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, multiplex immunofluorescence staining was conducted using TSA fluorescence kits from Servicebio, China. Finally, the fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX51) was used to obtain the images.

Cell culture

RPMI-1640 medium (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sijiqing Biological Engineering Materials Company, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Life Technology) was used for growing the human PCa-derived PC3 and DU145 cell lines. At 37 °C, the cells were incubated in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The total RNA from the clinical samples was extracted using TRIzol (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) following the instruction manual and then measured by NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher, USA). cDNA was synthesized by employing SweScript All-in-One First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) following the protocol.2×Universal Blue SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix was used to amplify the cDNAs using the Lightcycler 4800II (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). A reference gene (GAPDH) was used to normalize the average CT value of target genes. We calculated the relative gene expression as 2 − ΔΔCt. The sequences of primers (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China):

GAPDH forward : caggaggcattgctgatgat,

GAPDH reverse : gaaggctggggctcattt,

KCNMA1 forward: cctggcctcctccatggt,

KCNMA1 reverse: ttctgggcctccttcgtct.

Lentivirus infection

The lentivirus was purchased from Genechem (Shanghai). The lentiviral expression vector designated as KCNMA1 (pSLenti-EF1-P2A-Puro-CMV-KCNMA1-3xFlag-WPRE) was used for KCNMA1 gene (NM_001014797.3) delivery and stable overexpression. The empty vector designated Vector (pSLenti-EF1-P2A-Puro-CMV-MCS-3xFlag-WPRE) was used as a negative control. The MOI (multiplicity of infection) was 20 in DU145 and 50 in PC3. According to the manufacturer's protocol, cell transfection was performed using six-well plates. The number of cells was controlled at 8×105 during transfection. The medium was changed 1 day after transfection. Three days after transfection, puromycin at a concentration of 2 µg/ml was used for screening. After continuous screening and passage for 3 generations, the stable expression cell line was cryopreserved.

Western blot analysis

Cellular samples were collected and digested for 30 min on ice in RIPA solution (Beyotime, China) along with 0.1 mM PMSF (Beyotime, China) and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, China). The lysate was separated by centrifugation, and the resulting supernatant was retrieved. The protein concentration was determined using the PRAD method originated from Beyotime, China. According to their molecular weight, after transfer to PVDF membranes, the protein samples underwent sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis with either 12 or 15% concentration. After blocking PVDF membranes with 5% nonfat milk at room temperature for 1 h, they were rinsed thrice with TBS-T. The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the respective primary antibodies and rinsed thrice with TBS-T. The membranes were subsequently incubated with goat anti-mouse IgG (SA00001-1, Proteintech) at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, the bands were scanned, and the ChemiDoc™ Touch Imaging System (BIO-RAD) was used to acquire images. ImageJ software evaluated the results. On three separate occasions, the trial was conducted. The antibodies and their usage details are presented in Table 1.

Co-Immunoprecipitation

For co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), the supernatant was first incubated with IgG (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) or anti-KCNMA1 antibody (Santa Cruz, USA) overnight at 4 °C on a rotary shaker. Protein A + G beads (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) were then added and incubated with the mixture. Following incubation, the beads were collected and washed. The immunoprecipitated complexes bound to the beads were mixed with 1X Sample Loading Buffer and denatured by heating at 95 °C for 5 min. The denatured protein samples were then collected for Western blotting analysis.

CCK8 assay

Cell viability was assessed via the CCK8 assay protocol with the utilization of the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Biosharp). The cells were seeded into 96-well Corning plates (USA) at a seeding density of 2 × 10³ cells per well in a volume of 100 µl of culture medium. Subsequently, after 1, 2, and 3 days of culture, 10 µl of CCK-8 was added to the wells containing the cells, followed by an incubation period of 1 h at 37 °C. The absorbance of each well at a wavelength of 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader from Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc. Each experimental group was subjected to three independent experiments.

Colony-Formation assay

For a 14-day incubation period, PC3 and DU145 cells were seeded in six-well plates with 2 × 103 cells per well. The colony formation rate was measured as methanol fixation and 0.5% crystal violet staining.

Immunofluorescence

Cell cultures were established on glass coverslips. The supernatant was then carefully aspirated, followed by three washes with phosphate-buffered saline. Fixation was performed using 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 15 min. After permeabilization by incubating in 0.5% Triton-X-100 solution for 15 min, non-specific binding sites were blocked with goat serum for 30 min at 4 °C. Cells were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After thorough washing, fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at ambient temperature. Nuclear staining was achieved using DAPI. Microscopic imaging was conducted with a fluorescence microscope (BX53, Olympus, Japan). The antibodies and their usage details are presented in Table 1.

Assessment of transwell migration

In Corning, New York, USA, the traditional cell invasion experiment method utilized a transwell chamber coated with Matrigel. For migration experiments, 600 ml of complete media were added to the lower chamber, while the upper chamber contained 200 ml of medium consisting of 1 × 104 PC3 and DU145 cells in serum-free RPMI-1640 (with an 8-mm pore size, supplied by Corning). After 48 h of incubation, the chamber was cleaned thrice with PBS, then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. A 1:8 mixture of matrigel and serum-free medium was prepared before conducting the invasion experiment. After 80µL of the mixed solution was poured into the upper chamber, the subsequent stages of the experiment were identical to the migration experiment.

scRNA-seq analysis

We leveraged the TISCH2 website (http://tisch.comp-genomics.org/home/), a scRNA-seq database centered around the tumor microenvironment (TME), to examine the relationship between the expression of XCL2 and different cancer cell types [23].

Coculture assay for the migration of M1 macrophage

Cell migration assays were performed using a 24-well Transwell plate system. For this experiment, 5 × 104 cells from KCNMA1-overexpressing (KCNMA1) and negative control (Vector) groups of PC3 and DU145 cell lines were seeded into the lower chambers. Concurrently, 5 × 104 M1 macrophages were added to the upper chambers. After a 48-hour co-culture period under standard conditions, non-migrated cells remaining in the upper compartments were carefully removed by gentle washing. Migrated cells were then fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 15 min and visualized by staining with 0.5% crystal violet solution. Digital images of stained cell populations were acquired using a phase-contrast inverted microscope (Olympus) for quantitative analysis.

Animal experiments

The Experimental Animal Center of Capital Medical University (Beijing, China) provided 4-week-old female Balb/c nude mice. A total of 5×106 DU145 cells stably transduced with recombinant lentiviral vectors expressing KCNMA1 (KCNMA1) or negative control (Vector) were subcutaneously inoculated into the right flank of each mouse. Tumor growth was monitored biweekly using a vernier caliper to measure the longest (L) and shortest (W) diameters, with tumor volume calculated by the formula V = (L×W2)/2. At the endpoint, excised tumors were collected for subsequent analyses.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses employed SPSS Statistics 26. The mean ± SD is provided for each data point. Student’s t-test was utilized to compare multiple groups. Using the X2 test, we evaluated the relationship between protein level and clinical characteristics. Spearman’s correlation analysis determined the relationship between target genes. p < 0.05 was used to establish statistical significance.

Results

Proteomics reveals differences between PCa and normal adjacent tissue

This study collected paired prostate cancer (PCa) and normal adjacent tissue (NAT) specimens from 5 patients at Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University. Bar charts and violin plots demonstrated the distribution and inter-sample variability of protein intensity values (Fig. 1A and B). Pearson correlation analysis revealed higher homogeneity among NAT samples compared to the substantial heterogeneity observed in PCa samples (Fig. 1C). Through systematic data processing and threshold selection, we identified 335 PCa/NAT upregulated proteins (e.g., HPN, EIF4EBP3, FABP5) and 476 downregulated proteins (e.g., S100P, SLC2A5, LTF), visualized through circos plots (Fig. 1D), volcano plots (Fig. 1E), and heatmaps (Fig. 1F). Subcellular localization analysis indicated that 54.25% of differentially expressed proteins were localized to the cytoplasm and nucleus (Fig. 1G) [24]. COG/KOG classification demonstrated their predominant involvement in cellular processes and signaling and metabolism [25]. The process of cellular processes and signaling mainly involves Signal transduction mechanisms and Intracellular trafficking, secretion, and vesicular transport, while metabolism mainly involves lipid/amino acid/carbohydrate transport and metabolism (Fig. 1H). KEGG enrichment analysis showed that the differential proteins were mainly focused on activities such as cellular processes as well as metabolism (Fig. 1I).

Cluster analysis between differential proteins

To further clarify the cell biological functions of PCa different from NAT, we performed functional enrichment and pathway analysis on the up and down regulated datasets from proteomic analysis. The results showed that the functional enrichment of the proteome mainly included cellular metabolic functions, such as acetyl-CoA biosynthetic process from pyruvate, acetyl-CoA biosynthetic process, regulation of unsaturated fatty acid biosynthetic process (Fig. 2A). Meanwhile, KEGG-enriched analyzed species mainly also focused on cellular metabolism, such as Lipoic acid metabolism, Fatty acid elongation, Metabolism of xenobiotics by cytochrome P450, Citrate cycle (TCA cycle) (Fig. 2B). To determine the functional differences between proteins with different folding changes, these proteins were categorized into four groups, referred to as Q1 to Q4, based on folding changes (Fig. 2C). Gene ontology (GO) and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed for each group of proteins (Fig. 2D and E). The results showed that in addition to affecting metabolic activities, these proteins also influence mRNA activity, protein localization, signal transduction, and other processes. Given the aberrant metabolic activity in PCa, we next performed lactylome sequencing. In terms of pathway enrichment, differences were seen between proteomics and lactomics. In contrast to the enrichment results of proteomics, lactylome enrichment showe d an increase in the methylation level of histone H3K27, which is involved in the regulation of collagen fibril organization, the tol-like receptor signaling pathway, and the IL-17 signaling pathway, and protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum (Fig. 2F and G). Cluster analysis of the emulsification modification results was then performed, which showed a significant increase in modified proteins in groups Q1 and Q4 compared to groups Q2 and Q3 (Fig. 2H). Enrichment analysis also highlighted tumor-related functions and pathways (Fig. 2I and J). This suggests that increased lactation may play a key regulatory role in these pathways during the onset and progression of PCa. plays a key regulatory role in the onset and progression of PCa.

Characterization of lactylated clusters in PRAD

Using proteome as well as lactylome, we identified a number of genes that are potentially associated with Lactylation. In this regard, we intersected the above results with a Venn diagram and identified the 19 genes most associated with Lactylation (Fig. 3A). We utilized a consensus clustering method to identify patients with PRAD based on the expression patterns of the 19 LA-related genes (Fig. 3B) to further investigate the expression features of LA-related genes in PRAD. According to our findings, k = 2 proved to be an ideal choice for separating the complete cohort into clusters A (n = 499) and B (n = 336) (Fig. 3C and D). The Kaplan-Meier curves revealed that patients in cluster A had a longer OS than patients in cluster B (log-rank test, p = 0.001; Fig. 3E). PCA analysis indicated substantial changes in the transcription patterns of fibroblasts between the two groups (Fig. 3F). Cluster B was significantly enriched in cell adhesion, immunity-related, and carcinogenesis pathways, including cell adhesion, T cell receptor signaling, B cell receptor signaling, TGF-β signaling pathway, chemokine signaling, and cytokine receptor interaction, according to GSVA enrichment analysis (Fig. 3G). To explore the involvement of LA-related genes in the TME of PRAD, we used the CIBERSORT method to analyze the correlations between the two clusters and 22 human immune cell subsets in each PRAD sample. The infiltration of most immune cells differed significantly between the two clusters (Fig. 3H).

Differences in metabolic functions between lactylation sub-clusters

Protein lactylation modification in cells is inseparable from the cellular metabolic process. Lactic acid produced by glycolysis has become one of the indispensable processes. We used the IOBR package to explore the differences in metabolic functions between cluster A and cluster B. The Metabolism set enrichment analysis showed that pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation, alpha-linoleic acid metabolism, and linoleic acid metabolism were more significantly enriched in cluster A (Fig. 4A, S1A). There were also significant differences in cholesterol metabolism between cluster A and cluster B, represented by processes such as cholesterol biosynthesis and steroid biosynthesis (Fig. 4B, S1B). The performance of fatty acid metabolism was mainly distributed in Fatty Acid Degradation and Fatty Acid Elongation (Fig. 4C, S1C). In Hypoxia, the processes of Glycolysis and Hu_hypoxia signature were also mainly enriched in cluster A (Fig. 4D, S1D). Finally, we explored the key cellular processes in tumor characteristics. Establishment of a prognostic model related to lactylation and validation of its feasibility

First of all, 650 DEGs in cluster A and cluster B were selected for the next analysis. The shared differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the training cohort were subjected to univariate Cox regression analysis, with overall survival (OS) and survival time as the dependent variables. A total of 122 prognosis-related genes (P < 0.05) were identified, and the top 30 genes were visualized according to their P values (Fig. 5A). Meanwhile, we conducted a correlation analysis on these 30 genes, among which ALDH2, CENPF, and ANPEP were identified as the key genes (Fig. 5B). Cox regression analysis using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) was then used to minimize the number of genes. Finally, nine genes (KCNMA1, C1QTNF1, SFRP1, PRKG1, DACT1, BVES, CTGF, NCAPD3 and F5) were utilized to create a risk score prediction model (Fig. 5C and D). The risk score for each sample was calculated as follows: Risk score = (−0.31833551780769) * KCNMA1 + (−0.575787190528879) * C1QTNF1 + (−0.457224737511225) * SFRP1 + (0.716459274436944) * PRKG1 + (0.705241867292724) * DACT1 + (−0.465185836225777) * BVES + (0.322867470831585) * CTGF + (−0.162573797928321) * NCAPD3 + (0.219749044466104) * F5. We employed a risk assessment system to appropriately identify low risk and high risk prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD) samples. The cutoff value chosen was the median of the risk assessment in the training set. The samples were sorted according to their risk scores and divided into two groups based on the aforementioned cutoff value: a low risk group (n = 417) and a high risk group (n = 418). Meanwhile, we used another classification method to divide the samples into a test group (n = 417) and a train group (n = 418), Risk score plots and heatmaps for the all, test, and train groups are presented in Figure S2A-C. To evaluate the accuracy of the predicted risk score model, we presented the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves of the test group and the training group at the 2-year, 3-year, and 4-year time points (Fig. 5E and F). The K-M curve demonstrated that samples with high prostate cancer (PRAD) risk scores had shorter progression-free survival compared to those with low risk scores (p < 0.001; Fig. 5G). Subsequent analysis of high/low risk groups in both test and training cohorts revealed significant prognostic differences between the groups (Fig. 5H and I).

KCNMA1 is downregulated in PCa and correlated with patient prognosis

To further validate the feasibility of the prognostic model, we conducted bioinformatics analysis on the 9 genes in the LA signature model and ultimately selected KCNMA1 for further investigation, due to its significant association with both survival outcomes and diagnosis of prostate cancer. We first evaluated the expression level of KCNMA1 in pan-cancer using the TIMER 2.0 database. Results showed that KCNMA1 was downregulated in cancer cells (Fig. 6A). Further focused analysis revealed that KCNMA1 expression was significantly lower in bladder cancer tissues compared to normal tissues (both unpaired and paired samples) through TCGA database analysis (Fig. 6B and C). TCGA survival analysis identified KCNMA1 as a significant tumor suppressor gene in prostate cancer with the highest prognostic relevance among pan-cancers (Fig. 6D). Kaplan-Meier curves indicated that prostate cancer patients with high KCNMA1 expression had longer survival times compared to those with low expression (Fig. 6E). ROC curve analysis demonstrated good diagnostic value for KCNMA1 (AUC: 0.835) (Fig. 6F). Next, we collected 8 prostate cancer (PCa) tissue samples and adjacent normal tissues (NAT) from Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University. RT-qPCR (real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction) confirmed lower KCNMA1 mRNA levels in PCa tissues compared to NAT (Fig. 6G). Immunohistochemistry staining showed the highest KCNMA1 expression in NAT, followed by Gleason Score 6/7 tissues, with the lowest expression in Gleason Score 8/9/10 tissues (Fig. 6H and I).

Meanwhile, to enhance the significance of this study, we inhibited key enzymes in the glycolytic pathway using Oxamate and DCA in in vitro experiments to suppress lactate production (Figure S5A-S5F). The results showed that after inhibiting lactate generation, the proliferative and migratory capacities of PCa significantly decreased.

KCNMA1 overexpression inhibits proliferation, migration, and invasion of PCa

To further evaluate the function of KCNMA1 in PCa, we transfected plasmid DNA into PC3 and DU145 cell lines to successfully establish two stable cell lines. qRT-PCR (quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction) analysis (Fig. 7A and B) verified the transfection efficiency of the plasmid DNA and confirmed its use as the interference construct for subsequent experiments. Western blot analysis further demonstrated significantly downregulated KCNMA1 protein levels in both PC3 and DU145 prostate cancer cells (Fig. 7C and D). CCK-8 cell proliferation assays showed that KCNMA1 overexpression significantly inhibited the proliferation of PC3 and DU145 cells (Fig. 7E and F). Colony formation assays assessing single-cell proliferation revealed that increased KCNMA1 expression significantly reduced colony formation numbers in prostate cancer cells (Fig. 7G and H). As KI67 is a key marker of cell proliferation and serves as a prognostic and predictive biomarker, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated significantly lower KI67 expression in PCa cells with KCNMA1 overexpression compared to negative controls [26] (Fig. 7I and J). Transwell migration and invasion assays further evaluated tumor cell motility. Results showed that KCNMA1 overexpression significantly suppressed the migration and invasion of PC3 and DU145 cells (Fig. 7K-M).

KCNMA1 modulates M1 macrophage chemotaxis via MAPK signaling pathway

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis via STRING identified 50 candidate proteins interacting with KCNMA1, including CSN3, KCNN1, KCNN2, KCNN3, PRKACA, and PRKACB (Fig. 8A). Functional annotation using GO terms revealed that KCNMA1 is primarily involved in ion transport across cellular membranes as part of the transporter complex (Fig. 8B) [27]. To further explore its tumor-suppressive mechanisms, Wikipathways enrichment analysis highlighted potential roles of KCNMA1 in regulating MAPK signaling, lipid metabolism, and dopamine biosynthesis pathways (Fig. 8C). Focusing on the MAPK pathway, western blot analysis demonstrated significant downregulation of key pathway components [28, 29] RAS, RAF, phosphorylated MEK (P-MEK), and phosphorylated ERK (P-ERK) in KCNMA1-overexpressing cells, while total MEK and ERK levels remained unchanged (Fig. 8D and E). Immunofluorescence microscopy further showed inhibited nuclear translocation of phosphorylated ERK in response to KCNMA1 overexpression (Fig. 8F). We verified the direct or indirect interaction between KCNMA1 and RAF proteins using CO-IP assay (Fig. 8G). To characterize KCNMA1’s role in shaping the tumor microenvironment, ESTIMATE algorithm analysis revealed significant positive correlations between KCNMA1 expression and stromal scores (R = 0.633), ESTIMATE scores (R = 0.501), and immune scores (R = 0.305) (Figure S6A). Subsequent immune infiltration profiling identified NK cells, neutrophils, Th1 cells, mast cells, and macrophages as the most prominent immune cell types associated with KCNMA1-regulated tumor immunity (Figure S6B). Single-cell RNA sequencing further demonstrated KCNMA1 co-expression in both prostate cancer cells and macrophages (Fig. 8H, S6C, S6D). In vitro experiments were conducted to validate macrophage recruitment. THP-1 monocytes were differentiated into M1 macrophages (Fig. 8J), which were then cocultured with PC3/DU145 prostate cancer cells. Transwell migration assays showed that KCNMA1-overexpressing cells exhibited significantly enhanced chemotactic activity toward M1 macrophages compared to controls (Fig. 8K).

Discussion

Prostate cancer (PCa) treatment has long been centered on endocrine agents due to its unique endocrine therapy-sensitive nature. While PCa generally exhibits favorable prognosis, a subset of patients develops short-term resistance to endocrine therapy, for whom effective treatment strategies to prolong survival remain lacking. As a newly identified post-translational modification, lactylation has emerged as a critical regulatory mechanism in various diseases, with particular emphasis on cancer research. Lactate production in the tumor microenvironment serves as the cornerstone of this modification. Lactate, the end product of glycolysis, functions as a key energy source for mitochondrial respiration, a gluconeogenic precursor, and a signaling molecule. Under conditions of high glycolysis and low OXPHOS (Warburg effect), this metabolic reprogramming confers significant advantages to proliferating cells, including tumor cells [30, 31]. Approximately 85% of glucose uptake in cancer cells is converted to lactate, while only 5% of glycolytic pyruvate undergoes mitochondrial OXPHOS, enabling rapid cytoplasmic ATP synthesis. The presence of monocarboxylate transporters allows tumor cells to freely acquire abundant lactate, driving pan-lactylation of both histone and non-histone proteins. Recent advances in histone and non-histone lactylation research have uncovered intriguing mechanisms: H3K18la regulates RUBCNL/pacer transcription to promote colorectal cancer chemoresistance [10]; H3K9la impairs cisplatin responsiveness in ovarian cancer [32]; HIF1α lactylation enhances KIAA1199 transcription to facilitate prostate cancer angiogenesis [33]. Notably, previous studies have rarely addressed the prognostic implications of lactylation. Our research focuses on the prognostic impact of protein lactylation in prostate cancer (PCa) cells. We constructed a novel lactate-related prognostic model that serves as a powerful tool for predicting survival outcomes in PCa patients, filling a critical gap in this emerging field. To explore lactylation-associated genes in PCa, we employed 4D fast data-independent acquisition (DIA) L-lactylation quantitative proteomics sequencing on human prostate cancer tissue samples [34]. This approach identified 305 lactylated proteins and 479 modification sites. The encoding genes of these proteins underwent prognostic re-screening, yielding 19 key lactylation-modified genes significantly associated with patient outcomes. These genes were further analyzed using integrated datasets from TCGA, GSE116918, and GSE70769.

Using 19 lactylation signature genes, PCa patients from TCGA, GSE116918, and GSE70769 datasets were classified into two distinct molecular clusters (Cluster A and Cluster B). Cluster B patients exhibited more advanced clinicopathological features (stage, grade, Gleason Score) and shorter survival durations compared to Cluster (A) Significant tumor microenvironment (TME) heterogeneity was observed between clusters: Cluster B demonstrated substantially increased infiltration of immune cells, including T cells, NK cells, dendritic cells, and neutrophils. Elevated lactylation levels of histones and non-histone proteins in cancer cells regulate protein stability [35, 36], participate in cellular inflammatory responses [37], and modulate gene transcription processes [33], thereby reshaping the tumor immune microenvironment. Notably, Cluster B was enriched in biological processes associated with tumor progression, including chemokine signaling pathway, TGF-β signaling pathway, T cell receptor signaling, and B cell receptor signaling. We constructed a prognostic model using differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between Cluster A and Cluster (B) This model predicts prostate cancer (PCa) patient survival outcomes based on high/low risk scores derived from nine key genes: KCNMA1, C1QTNF1, SFBP1, PRKG1, DACT1, BVES, CTGF, NCAPD3, and F5. The model’s predictive capacity was validated in independent cohorts from TCGA and GEO online databases, providing a robust foundation for PCa prognosis prediction.

Prognostic Model-Driven Identification of KCNMA1 as a Novel Therapeutic Target in Prostate Cancer. The establishment of a prognostic model provides critical insights into predicting prostate cancer (PCa) outcomes and guiding precision therapy. To identify effective therapeutic strategies, we selected KCNMA1, a promising target from this prognostic model. Historically characterized as a membrane transporter, KCNMA1 plays essential roles in calcium/potassium ion homeostasis and cellular signaling. Dysfunction of its potassium channel activity is linked to multiple vascular, neurological, endocrine, and renal disorders, including hypertension, obesity, epilepsy, autism, incontinence, and stress-related diseases [38, 39]. For example, GWAS studies have implicated KCNMA1 in obesity-related hypertension pathogenesis. Notably, KCNMA1 research in cancer remains limited, with only a single study demonstrating its tumor-suppressive role in colorectal cancer through reduced expression [15]. However, this study lacked mechanistic insights. Our research focuses on KCNMA1’s role in cancer progression and its underlying molecular mechanisms. Expression analysis of 16 PCa tissues across different pathological stages revealed significant downregulation of KCNMA1 compared to 8 NATs, with expression levels inversely correlating with tumor progression. Functional validation demonstrated that KCNMA1 overexpression in PCa cells reduced proliferation, migration and invasion, establishing its tumor-suppressive role in PCa progression. The reduced expression of proliferation-related protein KI67 further supported this observation.

Subsequently, we investigated the underlying mechanisms of KCNMA1 by analyzing RNA sequencing data from the TCGA database. The top 50 interacting proteins with the highest co-expression scores were identified, and functional enrichment analysis (GO, KEGG, WikiPathways) revealed significant associations with the MAPK signaling pathway [40]. Subsequent Western blot validation in PCa cells demonstrated that KCNMA1 overexpression potently suppressed the RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK signaling cascade, thereby inhibiting oncogenic processes. Notably, deregulation of the RAS-MAPK signaling axis is among the most common events in tumorigenesis, exerting profound effects on cancer cell proliferation [41, 42]. This pathway represents a promising yet challenging target in precision oncology, with mutations frequently observed in pancreatic, colorectal, and lung cancers. Emerging evidence increasingly implicates RAS-MAPK dysregulation in PCa progression as well [43, 44]. Our study revealed that KCNMA1 suppresses prostate cancer (PCa) progression through dual mechanisms: inhibiting the MAPK signaling pathway and modulating the tumor immune microenvironment. While KCNMA1 overexpression did not significantly affect macrophage polarization, single-cell sequencing data identified its expression in PCa cells and macrophages. Functional assays demonstrated that KCNMA1 potently induced chemotaxis of pre-differentiated M1 macrophages, which are critical for anti-tumor immunity. Mechanistically, KCNMA1-mediated MAPK pathway inhibition promoted recruitment of tumoricidal M1 macrophages to the tumor microenvironment, thereby augmenting immunosurveillance. These findings establish KCNMA1 as a novel therapeutic target with dual roles in oncogenic signaling suppression and immune activation. The clinical implementation of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), such as PD-1/PD-L1 monoclonal antibodies, has revolutionized cancer treatment over the past two decades by offering curative opportunities for previously incurable solid tumors. However, prostate cancer (PCa) remains resistant to ICIs due to its immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) characterized by limited T-cell infiltration [45, 46]. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), the most abundant immune cells in the TME, exhibit functional plasticity by mediating both direct cytotoxicity through phagocytosis/cytokine secretion and antigen presentation via MHC class II molecules, thereby playing critical roles in immunosurveillance [47, 48]. This dual functionality positions TAMs as promising therapeutic targets, particularly in “cold” tumors like PCa where they could potentially bridge innate and adaptive immunity to overcome treatment resistance.

However, there is a minor contradiction between this study and previous research. M Bloch et al. found that KCNMA1 amplification can promote the proliferation of prostate cancer cells [49]. Nevertheless, our study has validated the tumor-suppressive effect of KCNMA1 from multiple dimensions including tissues and cells, and we believe the results are solid and reliable. Regarding the aforementioned paradox, we hypothesize that KCNMA1 may act as a molecule with dual effects: it exerts a cancer-promoting effect in advanced prostate cancer (PCa) due to differences in sample types, whereas it exhibits a tumor-suppressive effect in early-stage PCa. Meanwhile, this study has certain limitations in the number of clinical specimens collected from patients. In future research, we aim to increase the number of clinical samples analyzed, screen for therapeutic targets of lactylation modifications in prostate cancer (PCa) through sequencing result analysis, and validate through multi-center studies that blocking these targets to reduce lactylation levels can inhibit or promote the expression of targeted molecules. This approach will enhance the generalizability of the research and potentially transform the therapeutic landscape for PCa.

The lactylation-related (LA) prognostic model developed in this study provides a sensitive tool for predicting prostate cancer (PCa) outcomes, offering actionable guidance for clinical decision-making. Patients identified with high-risk scores at initial diagnosis could benefit from intensified therapeutic strategies. Additionally, the identification of KCNMA1 as a novel functional gene provides a potential therapeutic target for PCa treatment, addressing the unmet need for post-endocrine resistance therapies. This discovery expands precision oncology options by leveraging KCNMA1’s dual roles in suppressing oncogenic signaling and modulating macrophage-mediated immunosurveillance.
