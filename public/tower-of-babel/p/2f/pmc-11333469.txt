Exploring the effects of urban network topologies on entropy trajectories of segregation
Authors: Alexander V Mantzaris, Yuh-Haur Chen, George-Rafael Domenikos, Lakshminarayan Choudur
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSegregation is a threat to the aspirations of producing a cohesive society and modelling its dynamics can be done in order to help design preventative measures. This work explores the question of whether the network topologies of urban spaces can affect the pace at which populations can become segregated. The simulation dynamics employed augment the canonical Schelling model in such a way that it also captures the affinity for agents to prefer denser regions which also offer sufficient local homogeneity. It is shown that different networks synthetically generated and from real city maps can alter the rate of segregation. The results also show that using the entropy trace on the distribution of agent edge degree across all agents correlates with the segregation reinforcing the relevance of physics inspired modeling of social systems. This investigation shows that it is possible to explore and select network arrangements which can be less conducive towards segregation movements during the stages of urban planning so that the rate is reduced. An additional finding is that the entropic measure is closely associated with the common statistic for such modeling efforts.Keywords: Entropy, Segregation, Schelling, Urbanization, City planningSubject terms: Computational science, Information technology

Introduction

Social segregation1,2 is a pressing issue which can threaten and undermine the cohesion of a society if not addressed. This has phenomena has been noted throughout history3 and can be attributed to a wide variety of reasons such as prioritization based on race4, wealth5, religion6, and even athletic team support7,8. From the perspective of computational social science9 (and more recently social physics10) this process can be studied using models such as the Schelling model of social segregation11,12. The model encapsulates the dynamic of residential movements where each agent seeks to satisfy a homogeneity constraint of an aggregate number of immediate neighbors having the same identity variable. Simulations of this agent based system show how even slight preferences can cause the system to quickly converge toward a completely segregated society. Such a society would then possibly loose the ability to function peacefully13,14. Many factors are involved when residents decide to move from one area to another15,16 and the original Schelling model captures only a single aspect. Recent developments encapsulate more of the complex decision making process while still maintaining the fundamental approach taken in the Schelling model (eg17 which incorporates income constraints with identity variables). Figure 1 demonstrates the states from two time points of a Schelling simulation, times 1 and 41, where the initial state shows limited segregation and the later time a greater amount of segregation. This is a result of applying the Schelling dynamics at an agent individual basis.

On the left plot an example of an initial state of the Schelling model can be seen with the number of agents found to have satisfied the homogeneity constraint (R=30). On the right plot at iteration 41 it can be seen how the Schelling dynamics have relocated agents to homogeneous regions producing a larger homogeneity satisfaction across all agents (R=139).

This paper builds upon the Schelling model to investigate how the layout of a city (the urban network) can affect the of rate segregation18–20. It explores the hypothesis that certain network arrangements may be more or less conducive for segregation and that this can be explored using the topology. This is important since city planning should take this aspect of safety into account as it may be too costly to change fundamental aspects of the connectivity after the city develops and matures. According to the UN report on population and urbanization21,22 the relative number of occupants in cities is expected to increase across the world while increasing the number of mega cities23. This produces a risk of segregation within the urban environment which is a challenge since distances between regions in large cities can offer spaces for different types of isolation24 (explored with empirical evidence in17).

The canonical Schelling model defined in11 is a good basis for the general set of models which can be defined to explore residential segregation dynamics. This is largely due to the direct representation of the locality homogeneity constraint and exploration of new positions it offers. There are are wide variety of modifications and extensions which have been made to the Schelling model to tailor it for different investigations as will be explored in “Related work” section. For the study conducted the canonical model will incorporate a dynamic to capture the tendency for residents to prefer dense locations given that there is a level of similar neighbors which satisfies the homogeneity constraint. This dynamic of the model proposed seeks to capture the macroscopic pattern observed in urbanization studies by the UN and others21,22 where the new residencies in higher dense regions are sought. This introduction is key for mimicking the reduction of rural areas being occupied in favor of larger denser cities and why the number/size of mega cities are growing25. Another aspect of the classic model which is modified but not uncommon in research employing the Schelling model is to use a ratio threshold for the local homogeneity rather than the original count as this will permit a broader range of graph topologies to be explored where the number of local neighbors has a broader range.

Common to Schelling model analysis is the simulation trajectory of the proportion of agents which are found to be in sufficiently homogeneous localities. This can also be considered as the number of agents which do not seek to relocate, whether feasible or not, due to the satisfaction of the constraint on the homogeneity of their locality. With a random allocation of agents on the grid, along with empty positions, typically a ratio of less than 1.0 is found for the remain status of satisfied agents. As agents relocate to new positions where the constraint is satisfied this trend generally increases and can be plotted to examine the degree to which the simulated society has reached complete segregation. This work will also utilize an entropy metric on the topology and on the distribution of the number of neighbors agents have (survey on information entropy calculations on complex networks26). As the distribution of the number of neighbors agents have (edges) changes along simulation time, this entropy will also change and be presented in the results. The association between the remain ratio (satisfaction) trajectory will be presented along with the agent edge entropy in the “Results” section. The different networks explored, both synthetic and real, show an association between the entropy of the agent connectivity distribution and with the remain ratio during the phase of segregation increase where agents are changing positions. Different network topologies will be explored based on the generation algorithm and the real datasets from different cities.

The entropy metric on the distribution of the local agent association number (number of other agents in locality is the location density), is chosen not only because of the correlation demonstrated in the results. The entropic approach allows for a non-parametric measure which is expected to be more robust across a wide range of situations. The assumptions made a minimal and there is a direct interpretation in terms of information for making comparisons27. Another aspect is that it reinforces the goals of social physics10 which aims at bringing techniques and theories from physics into the area of social science. Associations discovered between physics based metrics can direct further research to establish principles on modelling the complex social phenomena observed.

The Results show the levels of segregation between independent simulations and whether they correlate with the network entropy in both synthetic and real topology datasets. The results show that for certain topology entropy comparisons this association measured through correlation is significant for the majority of the simulation. This motivates the necessity of using such simulations when exploring different urban planning projects.

Related work

The growing field of Social Physics (reviewed in10) has the aim of using techniques, methodologies and paradigms from the field of physics in order to more deeply understand sociological phenomena. This effort was envisioned decades ago as its early development is described in28 where notable researchers are documented as being supportive of the idea of an “Empiric Social Science”29. It is noteworthy that even James C. Maxwell is found to have alluded to social physics from the work done on the statistical properties of gases30.

Agent based systems (ABMs) model the dynamics of the social participants from the ‘bottom up’31 and the Schelling model can be considered an ABM as well. The modeling paradigm of ABMs can be considered as an analogue of finite element modeling for sociology and there is work which does use this overlap to model different systems with the perspective with organic tissues as well32,33. What sets the study of physical systems with ABMs apart from classical sociological research is that there are some core principles of physics which can relied upon in order to produce consistent simulated behaviors10. Example of key principles are the four laws of thermodynamics34 where the first is that of the conservation of energy (energy cannot be created or destroyed) and the second that entropy can never decrease over time.

The Schelling model can be parameterized and augmented in a variety of fashions to suit different scenarios more accurately. The work of35 presents a unified Schelling model for a wide variety of scenarios while maintaining the key Schelling dynamics. In17 the model is augmented with a monetary dynamic which acts as a type of barrier energy for agent movements which can produce homogeneity satisfaction. The motivation for introducing the monetary dynamic is based on empirical observations of segregation trends where financial resources limited or facilitated the preference for the homogeneous regions (as shown from an empirical study36). This concept is explored within the social physics lens in37 where the entropy trace from a Schelling simulation is produced showing that it decreases as the homogeneity increases. The decreasing entropy is not consistent with the laws of physics and does not align with the aspirations of social physics. The work of38 shows how a dual dynamic, incorporating a monetary dynamic, can address the paradox39 of the decreasing entropy trend by producing the overall increasing system entropy. Entropic modeling for lattice based systems, not only for the Schelling model, have been explored in40 and in41 shows that a system based on ideological exchanges of political discourse can be designed to maintain the entropy throughout the simulation. In a more general case the study of42 looks at how the Game of Life can be studied using Thermodynamics where the natural variables such as the Temperature is produced.

There is general interest in using the Schelling model within the context of social physics such as the work of43 that studies the phase transition properties of the model as it move from unsegregated to segregated. Connections with the Ising model of ferromagnetism is used to find a physical analogue within the Schelling model44,45. This analogue is based on the locality of common overlap of similar spins which is then aggregated. The states across the grid can then be found to produce a macrostate at each time point of the simulation trajectory.

Considering a more general connectivity topology than the lattice grid used in Schelling modeling allows for investigations that can more closely align with the intricacies of actual cities. As cities develop they can produce very non-uniform network structures although they can still be members of various network types studied in network science46. Urban planning has the aim at directing development in a manner which will be beneficial for residents in the long run for many issues such as waste management47, traffic congestion, commerce, and here we explore whether the arrangements and connectivity can actually alter the rate of social segregation. There are notable modeling explorations that cover hazardous social developments such as civil unrest (forecasting efforts48), political polarization (broad introduction to its dynamics49), economic panics50,51 and many other disruptions to the productive means of civil discourse. Given the large population centers of modern cities it is possible for whole areas to become detached socially within a large integrated network which would detriment society52. Therefore urban planners can take into consideration how certain topological arrangements can be avoided to slow the pace of segregation once the preference emerges is of value53. The work of54 explores how an urban network of Washington D.C. can be used with the Schelling model to study the phenomena of ghettos.

Methodology

The methodology developed here is based on the canonical Schelling model11 and the approach of estimating the entropy in a network outlined in26. The purpose is to explore simulation traces of segregation among agent population with their corresponding entropy traces. The Schelling model is augmented in order to promote agents to remain in sparsely populated areas with a lesser probability than higher density regions. This tendency for residents to relocated to regions of higher density is a driver for the established observation of continuous urbanization55,56 (dense regions offer improved occupational mobility, opportunity, access to government services etc). In order to represent this preference within the Schelling model inspiration from the work of35 is drawn which shows how the dynamics can be generalized to capture different behaviors. This further strengthens the need for an exploration of urban topologies with the trajectory of entropy traces as it can be expected that residents may move according to a segregation pattern providing a diverse distribution of regions for where homophilic satisfactions can be found.

Discussion

The work presented here explores the value of exploring segregation trajectories in different urban topologies with trajectories of the entropy over homophilic connections between residents belonging to different identities. This exploration resides within the scope of the field of social physics which aims to augment sociological research using techniques established from physics. The model for simulating segregation is the Schelling model with the modification that allows agents to prefer denser populated regions according to documented findings of urban trends across the world. The entropy trace defined for the simulation is based on the distribution the number of homophilic connections (edges) agents have. This definition for the entropy helps see how diverse are the sizes of the amount of homophily experienced by agents as the state becomes more segregated. Synthetic and real world networks are explored showing that for certain changes significant statistical correlations are to be found between segregation traces and the entropy traces. This can provide more associated information than the homogeneity satisfaction value across the agents which is typically the only value used to summarize the state of the Schelling simulation. The results of the homophilic satisfaction (segregation) traces in relation to the entropy traces based on the distribution of the number of homophilic neighbors can produce significant associations. A key result is that more grid like arrangements produce lower entropy (uncertainty) reducing the risk for hubs to emerge within groups of the same identity.

Overall this study shows how social physics measures can assist in studying segregation patterns and provide insight when exploring urban planning designs which aim to be more resilient against segregation. The usage of the entropic metrics on the network space and the distribution of homophily among agents/residents is not directed as competing against other metrics of segregation but to introduce an entropic modeling paradigm which can be another useful metric in addition to others.

Possible extensions include exploring how barriers of movement and agent adjacencies are impacted by highways and the building environment, as explored in57 and60. Another potential avenue for improvement is to handle graphs of different sizes since the entropy values can change due to a larger more diverse distribution being produced because of a larger population. Notable limitation of this work is that although the entropic metric describes the distribution uncertainty for the homophilic edges but the density of the occurrences on the map are not taken into account.
