Towards a shuttle-based workflow for daily online magnetic resonance imaging-guided particle therapy: Anatomical robustness and organ variability in the female pelvis
Authors: Friderike K Longarino, Danae Droutsas, Lars Wessel, Cedric Beyer, Rita Pestana, Julia Bauer, Katharina Seidensaal, Jürgen Debus, Sebastian Klüter, Nathalie Arians
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Female pelvic external outline was stable in shuttle-based MRI-guided workflow.•Mean distance to agreement of the external remained < 1 mm during shuttle transports.•Physiological organ motion was the main source of variability.•Bladder filling significantly correlated with gynecological structure displacement.•Shuttle-based MRI-guided workflow is feasible for gynecological sites.Keywords: MRI-guided particle therapy, Daily online adaptive radiotherapy, Shuttle transfer, Female pelvis, Cervical cancer

Introduction

Particle therapy provides highly conformal dose distributions and reduced ionizing radiation exposure to surrounding healthy tissues compared to photon radiotherapy [1]. However, interfractional changes can compromise the delivery of the planned target coverage and sparing of normal structures [2]. To mitigate such dose distortions, online adaptive particle therapy accounts for anatomical changes occurring during treatment [3], [4], [5]. Currently, image guidance in particle therapy mainly relies on 2D orthogonal X-ray imaging, in-room computed tomography (CT) imaging, on-board cone beam CT (CBCT) imaging, and surface guidance [6], [7]. Integrating magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for image guidance could offer daily target localization with improved soft tissue contrast, without the additional ionizing radiation exposure associated with X-ray-based imaging techniques [5], [6], [8].

Previous studies demonstrated the feasibility of treating gynecological cancer patients with proton and carbon ion therapy [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]. MRI-guided particle therapy may be particularly beneficial for uterine cervical and endometrial cancers [5], as these tumors exhibit substantial interfractional motion due to fluctuations in bladder and rectal filling, despite bladder and bowel preparation protocols [14]. Also, cervical cancers often show pronounced tumor regression during treatment, with reported tumor volume reductions of 48–96% by the end of radiotherapy [15]. Such interfractional anatomical changes can alter tissue density as well as shape and position of organs within the beam path, leading to range uncertainties that may compromise tumor coverage and sparing of organs-at-risk. MRI guidance may therefore be particularly valuable for these patients in regard to tumor control and reducing toxicity, which would improve quality of life.

One potential approach for realizing daily online MRI-guided workflows is a shuttle-based strategy, in which patients remain immobilized in the treatment position during transfer between the MRI room and the particle therapy treatment room [16]. Shuttle-based systems, mainly applied in photon-based radiotherapy, have been proposed and investigated in previous studies [17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]. The feasibility of maintaining positional stability using a shuttle-based approach in particle therapy has been demonstrated for liver carcinoma patients [23], suggesting that such a set-up could also be suitable for developing MRI-guided particle therapy workflows for other indications, including the female pelvis. Nevertheless, systematic investigations of the anatomical robustness of radiotherapy set-ups in shuttle-based workflows for the female pelvis are lacking.

This study quantified positional stability and organ variability of seven structures in the female pelvis over time during different shuttle transport scenarios with our current uterine cervical and endometrial cancer immobilization set-up in a group of healthy volunteers, with the goal of applying the workflow for MRI-guided particle therapy for gynecological malignancies.

Material and Methods

Study design

An overview of the study design is shown in Fig. 1. Healthy female volunteers underwent four MRI scans at the Heidelberg Ion Beam Therapy Center (HIT, Germany), interspersed with a waiting period and intra-hospital transport simulations. To control for physiological effects that can cause cervical and uterine motion (i.e., bladder and rectal filling), and to simulate clinical conditions with a full bladder, participants followed a standardized and clinically used drinking and fasting protocol prior to MRI acquisition. The subjects were instructed to fast for at least one hour and to void their bladder 30 min before the study and then drink 350 ml of water. During the study, participants were asked to remain still in accordance with the clinical protocol for female pelvic patient treatments. Each volunteer was positioned with our current uterine cervical and endometrial cancer immobilization set-up (requiring approximately 7 min) in supine position on an indexed knee and foot support (IT-V, Innsbruck, Austria) fixed to a transfer table (Slate AirShuttle, CQ Medical, Avondale, USA) which can be mounted on a trolley (AirDrive Trolley, CQ Medical). The transfer system employs a low-friction air-bearing mechanism that enables patient transfer from the MRI scanner table to the treatment table without requiring active movement by the patient [21]. Details of this approach have already been reported in a study focusing on the stability of liver position [23].

Overview of the study design. The subjects underwent a series of four pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans interspersed with a waiting period and intra-hospital transport simulations using a patient-transfer shuttle system. The study comprised the following: volunteer positioning on an indexed knee and foot support fixed to a transfer table, MRI 1, 10-minute waiting period as a time control phase, MRI 2, short transport of 7 min, MRI 3, long transport of 10 min, and MRI 4.

First, a baseline MRI dataset (MRI 1) was acquired. Participants then remained stationary in the MRI scanner for 10 min to account for time-dependent physiological changes that can cause cervical/uterine motion (time control). A second MRI dataset (MRI 2) was acquired to quantify displacements due to time-dependent physiological effects. Next, participants were transferred from the MRI table onto the trolley and transported along a short route over a smooth floor surface, beginning and ending in the MRI room (requiring approximately 7 min). This route simulated the transfer of patients from the MRI room to a treatment room at HIT. A third MRI dataset (MRI 3) was acquired to evaluate displacements during this short transport. Then, participants underwent a second transfer and transport along a longer route that included uneven tiled flooring to replicate more challenging transport conditions (requiring approximately 10 min). Finally, a fourth MRI dataset (MRI 4) was acquired to assess potential displacements under these more demanding transport conditions. The mean total duration of the study was 73 min (range: 67–77 min).

Study participants

Approval from the ethics committee of the Medical Faculty of Heidelberg University (S-964/2020) and written informed consent was obtained from each participant. Twenty healthy women were enrolled, covering a range of ages and body mass indices (BMIs) (median age: 25 years, range: 18–55 years; median height: 1.68 m, range: 1.58–1.80 m; median weight: 64.5 kg, range: 50–103 kg; median BMI: 22.9 kg/m2, range: 18.9–31.8 kg/m2). In subject 17, the cervix (with vaginal apex) and uterus were absent due to prior radical hysterectomy.

MR image acquisition

MR imaging was performed using a 1.5 T MAGNETOM Sola MRI scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany), which is located approximately 15 m from the HIT treatment rooms [24]. For each set of scans, two MR pulse sequences were performed: a 3D T1 GRE (VIBE, TR: 6.9 ms, TE: 2.33/4.39 ms, spatial resolution: (1 × 1 × 3) mm3, bandwidth: 450 Hz/pixel, acquisition time: 3 min and 25 s), and a 3D T2 TSE (SPACE, TR: 1500.0 ms, TE: 222.0 ms, spatial resolution: (1 × 1 × 3) mm3, bandwidth: 521 Hz/pixel, acquisition time: 4 min and 6 s). The scan area completely covered the abdominal and pelvic cavity, including the relevant gynecological organs and their movement spaces.

Analysis of the pelvic MR images

In each MRI set, the external, cervix, cervix and vaginal apex, uterus, bladder, peritoneal cavity with the intestine, and rectum were contoured on T1-weighted images using RayStation version 2024B (RaySearch Laboratories, Stockholm, Sweden). The cervix and vaginal apex were delineated as a separate contour, as the vaginal apex is frequently included in the radiation field in cases of cervical cancer. The external body contour extended from the sacral promontory to the symphysis pubis. All structures were manually contoured by one physicist to minimize interobserver variability and verified by a radiation oncologist. Rigid registrations were performed between MRI scans.

Two primary sets of comparison were conducted for each subject: (i) comparing each scan with the initial scan to assess the accumulative shift throughout the study (accumulative shifts: MRI 1 – MRI 2, MRI 1 – MRI 3, MRI 1 – MRI 4); and (ii) comparing each scan with the subsequent scan to assess the consecutive shift between image sets (consecutive shifts: MRI 1 – MRI 2, MRI 2 – MRI 3, MRI 3 – MRI 4). To quantify organ displacement, Dice similarity coefficients (DSC) [25] and mean distances to agreement (MDA) [26] were calculated for each contour using built-in RayStation functions [27], [28]. The DSC is a measure of the volumetric overlap between two contours, with values ranging from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (complete overlap), while the MDA is the mean surface distance between two contours, expressed in units of length, where a value of 0 indicates complete agreement between the contours [29]. Given the anatomical relationships of female pelvic organs, Pearson correlation coefficients between changes in bladder volume and the DSC and MDA values for consecutive shifts of the cervix, cervix and vaginal apex, and uterus were calculated, with p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Comparison of consecutive shifts showed that the median DSC (MDA) of the external contour was 0.99 (0.5 mm) for MRI 1 – MRI 2, 0.99 (0.9 mm) for MRI 2 – MRI 3, and 0.99 (0.9 mm) for MRI 3 – MRI 4 (Fig. 2(b) and (d)), suggesting high stability of patient positioning. For both consecutive and accumulative shifts, DSC values remained > 0.97 and MDA values were < 1.9 mm for all subjects (Supplementary Fig. 1).

For the cervix, the DSC was higher and the MDA was lower for MRI 1 – MRI 2 compared to MRI 2 – MRI 3 (Supplementary Fig. 2). In contrast, the DSC was lower and the MDA was higher for MRI 1 – MRI 2 compared to MRI 3 – MRI 4. A similar trend was observed for the cervix and vaginal apex contour (Supplementary Fig. 3). Median MDA values observed for MRI 1 – MRI 2/MRI 2 – MRI 3/MRI 3 – MRI 4 were 2.2/2.8/1.8 mm (ranges: 1.1–6.2/0.8–7.8/0.9–5.3 mm) for the cervix, and 2.5/2.6/2.0 mm for the cervix with vaginal apex. These findings suggest that the impact of the time control phase was greater than that of the long transport, yet smaller than that of the short transport. Representative axial slices from three subjects with the minimum, median, and maximum cervix MDA values are shown in Supplementary Fig. 8 to illustrate contour differences between MRI 2 and MRI 3.

For the uterus, an overall upward (downward) trend in mean DSC (MDA) was observed across consecutive study stages, accompanied by a reduction in standard deviations, indicating greater positional variability during the time control phase compared with the transport phases (Supplementary Fig. 4). Median MDA values observed for MRI 1 – MRI 2/MRI 2 – MRI 3/MRI 3 – MRI 4 were 3.4/3.7/3.6 mm, respectively.

For the bladder, mean DSC (MDA) values were higher (lower) for MRI 1 – MRI 2 than for MRI 2 – MRI 3; however, mean DSC (MDA) values were lower (higher) for MRI 1 – MRI 2 compared with MRI 3 – MRI 4 (Supplementary Fig. 5). Median MDA values for MRI 1 – MRI 2/MRI 2 – MRI 3/MRI 3 – MRI 4 were 3.6/4.2/3.7 mm, respectively. Changes in bladder volume over the course of the study are depicted in Fig. 3; similar patterns between the mean bladder volume differences and mean bladder MDA values across consecutive study stages were observed. The largest range of bladder volume increase was observed between MRI 2 – MRI 3, followed by MRI 1 – MRI 2, while the smallest range occurred between MRI 3 – MRI 4 (ranges within 1.5 × the interquartile range (IQR) for MRI 1 – MRI 2/MRI 2 – MRI 3/MRI 3 – MRI 4: 3.8–209.4/11.6–233.8/2.2–179.9 cm3) (Fig. 3(a)). The bladder filling rate increased from MRI 1 – MRI 2 (mean: 4.4 cm3/min) to MRI 2 – MRI 3 (mean: 5.7 cm3/min), then decreased during MRI 3 – MRI 4 (mean: 4.2 cm3/min) (Fig. 3(b)). The Pearson correlations are visualized as scatter plots between changes in bladder volume and DSC or MDA for the cervix (Fig. 4), the cervix and vaginal apex (Supplementary Fig. 9), and the uterus (Supplementary Fig. 10). Discussion

Prior to implementing a shuttle-based workflow for MRI-guided particle therapy in patients with gynecological cancer, it is essential to evaluate organ variability in the female pelvis to ensure that organ motion is not influenced by the shuttle transport process. The study demonstrated that high stability in the external outline of the female pelvis can be maintained during shuttle transfers. The external contour exhibited minimal displacement, with a median MDA of 0.9 mm for both short and long shuttle transports (Fig. 2). All MDA values remained < 0.9 mm for the time control, < 1.4 mm for the short transport, and < 1.7 mm for the long transport (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Physiological organ motion was the main source of organ variability and was only marginally influenced by the shuttle transport. For the cervix, the median (maximum) MDA values across all volunteers were 2.2 mm (6.2 mm) for the time control, 2.8 mm (7.8 mm) for the short transport, and 1.8 mm (5.3 mm) for the long transport (Fig. 2). Mean consecutive shifts of the cervix, cervix and vaginal apex, and bladder were greatest during the short transfer, followed by the time control phase, with the smallest displacements observed during the long transfer (Table 1). Counterintuitively, the long transport exhibited the lowest shifts among the three scenarios, suggesting that transport duration and conditions do not substantially affect the robustness of the shuttle workflow. Instead, our data indicates that intrinsic physiological changes—primarily bladder filling—are the principal drivers of organ displacement in the female pelvis. The short transfer coincided with the period of greatest bladder volume increase (Fig. 3), inducing larger organ displacements. In contrast, the long transfer occurred during a subsequent phase characterized by a reduced filling rate, resulting in greater organ stability. Accordingly, variability in organ position across the different transfer scenarios is primarily attributable to the bladder filling rate at the time of transfer rather than to the transport itself. This conclusion is supported by statistically significant correlations between bladder volume changes and consecutive shifts of the cervix (and vaginal apex) and uterus (Table 2). Notably, the bladder filling rate was highly heterogeneous across volunteers despite a standardized drinking protocol, indicating that further optimization of bladder preparation protocols could enhance reproducibility. To decrease organ motion, it may be beneficial to increase the interval between fluid intake and imaging and irradiation, thereby aligning image acquisition and treatment delivery with a phase of reduced bladder filling. The observation of rapid temporal bladder filling variability suggests that sufficiently large planning target volume (PTV) margins remain necessary, even in the context of daily MRI-guided particle therapy. However, the use of MRI guidance in combination with optimized bladder filling strategies may facilitate a reduction in required PTV margins. Moreover, the observed reduced rate in later bladder filling stages presents an opportunity for predictive modeling. Quantifying cervix displacement per standardized unit change in bladder volume could enable improved prediction of target position between MRI acquisition and irradiation.

The dosimetric impact of organ displacements depends on the selection of ion beam angles. At our institution, treatment planning is based on two CT scans (full and empty bladder) in accordance with the existing consensus guidelines [30], [31] and two posterior oblique fields [12], chosen to avoid beam trajectories that pass through the rectum and to reduce interfractional uncertainties related to rectal filling. During the planning process, the full-bladder and empty-bladder CT datasets are fused, and the clinical target volume (CTV) is initially delineated on the full-bladder CT scan, as described in [12]. Subsequently, the CTV is adapted and expanded on the empty-bladder CT scan to ensure consistent coverage of the target structures even under empty-bladder conditions. In this study, physiological organ motion was the main source of organ variability, whereas shuttle transports had only a marginal influence. The observed median MDA values remained below the PTV margins of 5 mm (7 mm in the beam direction) applied at our institution for this indication.

For the intestine and rectum, the observed changes were similar across the three consecutive comparisons (Fig. 2), suggesting that the transport interventions did not result in additional displacement compared with the time control period.

The median DSC/MDA values for the external, cervix, cervix and vaginal apex, intestine, and rectum observed in this study were within the tolerances published by the American Association of Physicists in Medicine (AAPM) Task Group No. 132, which recommends DSC values greater than 0.8 and MDA values below 3 mm for the evaluation of image registrations in photon therapy [29]. At the time of writing, no corresponding guidelines values for DSC and MDA have been established for particle therapy.

While fully integrated MRI-guided radiotherapy systems are not commercially available for particle therapy, a shuttle system integrated with an MRI scanner located outside the treatment room offers a flexible and cost-effective alternative. A shuttle-based system could also reduce uncertainty between the planning CT and planning MRI datasets, because it can be used to transport patients between CT and MRI scanners. Our study has indicated the robustness of the immobilization set-up across different transport scenarios, thus providing a promising starting point for such integration. In a clinical treatment workflow, MRI acquisition would require approximately 10 min, followed by shuttle transport from the MRI room to the treatment room (approximately 7 min). Planar X-ray positioning verification would then be performed (approximately 5 min), followed by pelvic ion beam treatment delivery (approximately 20 min, depending on the number of treatment fields). The duration of treatment adaptation, including quality assurance and clinical plan assessment, is workflow-dependent and should be minimized. For example, approximately 7 min have been reported for an online adaptive proton therapy workflow [4], whereas about 25 min have been reported for an online adaptive photon therapy workflow [21]. Thus, the total duration of the present study for participants undergoing a series of four MRI scans was likely slightly longer than the time required for patients during a treatment session.

Our results are comparable to those of a study using a shuttle-based MRI-guided photon radiotherapy workflow for pelvic malignancies, which reported median displacements of ≤ 2 mm in all spatial directions between MR and CBCT imaging [19]. Our results are also consistent with those of a study that quantified intrafractional anatomical changes during photon radiotherapy using pre- and post-fraction CBCT scans [32]. In that study, the group-mean intrafractional displacements averaged over the cervix-uterus were 0.1 ± 1.4, 1.8 ± 1.5, and –2.8 ± 1.8 mm in the left–right, cranio–caudal, and anterior–posterior directions, respectively. Similarly, another study reported differences below 2.8 mm (standard deviation) between pre- and post-treatment CT images in the abdominal/pelvic region [17]. A significant correlation between bladder inflow rate and cervix-uterus motion was observed (r = 0.6, p < 0.01) [32], in agreement with our findings.

A notable strength of this study was its systematic investigation, which quantified displacements and deformations in the female pelvis related to both time and the transport scenarios. The inclusion of twenty healthy volunteers, representing a range of ages and BMIs, strengthens the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the incorporation of both short and long shuttle transport scenarios demonstrated the system’s potential adaptability, suggesting that these findings could be applied to hospital settings with similar transport conditions. However, the investigation was conducted on a cohort of healthy volunteers with a mean age of 28 years, which is lower than the mean age at diagnosis of cervical cancer globally (53 years) [33]. The organ variability observed in this study cohort may not fully reflect that seen in cervical cancer patients, where factors such as tumor presence, post-surgical changes, and tissue response could influence variability. Moreover, this analysis assessed the variability of the entire cervix and uterus, whereas smaller radiotherapy target volumes, e.g., in the postoperative setting, may be more susceptible to displacement. However, in the subject with a prior hysterectomy, no larger organ displacements were observed compared with the other participants (Supplementary Figs. 5–7). Previous studies have reported interfractional vaginal cuff displacements during adjuvant pelvic radiotherapy with mean values of 3.0 mm (medio–lateral), 2.9 mm (superior–inferior), and 7.0 mm (anterior–posterior) [34], and 1.5 mm (medio–lateral), 3.6 mm (superior–inferior), and 3.7 mm (anterior–posterior) [35]; thus, bladder filling protocols and the use of rectal balloons may be useful to minimize positional variations.

Future research should involve larger patient populations and other treatment sites to confirm these findings and the clinical applicability of the shuttle-based workflow. Additionally, different bladder preparation protocols could be evaluated and compared to assess their impact on organ variability in the pelvis.

In summary, the study demonstrated that high stability in the external outline of the female pelvis can be maintained while using a shuttle-based workflow for MRI-guided adaptive particle therapy. Physiological organ motion remained the main source of variability and was only marginally influenced by the shuttle transport. These findings support the feasibility of implementing a shuttle-based MRI-guided workflow for gynecological sites and suggest that optimizing bladder preparation protocols may further enhance reproducibility. This approach could be a further step towards reducing toxicity and improving quality of life in gynecological cancer patients.
