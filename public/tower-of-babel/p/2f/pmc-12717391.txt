Predicting major surgical complications in intensive care units and in-hospital mortality of patients from the emergency room: A retrospective single-center study
Authors: Ting-Lung Lin, I-Ling Chen, Wei-Hung Lai, Ying-Ju Chen, Kuan-Han Wu, Hui-Chuan Chang, Yu-Cheng Lin, Yu-Hung Lin, Wei-Feng Li, Chih-Chi Wang, Po-Hsun Chang, Shih-Feng Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMajor surgical complications (MSCs) are a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in patients requiring surgical intensive care, especially those admitted from the emergency room (ER). The characteristics and predictors of MSCs in this high-risk population remain poorly defined. The objective is to identify the incidence and independent predictors of MSCs and in-hospital mortality among adult patients admitted to the surgical intensive care unit (SICU) from the ER after emergency surgery.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, observational cohort study of 322 adult patients admitted to the SICU from the ER after emergency surgery at a tertiary hospital in Taiwan between January 2022 and February 2023. Postoperative complications were graded using the Clavien–Dindo classification. Patients were divided into minor (Grades I–IIIa) and major (Grades IIIb–V) complication groups. Multivariate logistic regression identified predictors of MSCs and in-hospital mortality.ResultsMSCs occurred in 20.8% of patients. In-hospital mortality was 14.0%. Independent predictors of MSCs included higher Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II scores, presence of shock at intensive care unit (ICU) admission, biliary tree and soft tissue surgery, an American Society of Anesthesiologists score ≥4, and prolonged mechanical ventilation. These factors also independently predicted mortality. Patients with MSCs had higher in-hospital mortality (47.8% vs. 5.1%, p < 0.01), longer ICU stays (15.3 vs. 5.8 days), and higher hospital costs ($21,599 vs. $9607; p < 0.001).ConclusionsEmergency surgery patients admitted to SICU from the ER face a high risk of major complications and death. Physiological severity, surgical complexity, and pre-ICU instability are key predictors. Early identification of these factors can guide risk mitigation strategies.Keywords: Major surgical complication, surgical intensive care unit, emergency surgery, risk prediction, Clavien–Dindo classification

Introduction

Despite ongoing advances in surgery, anesthesiology, and critical care, major surgical complications (MSCs) continue to pose a significant challenge, particularly in the setting of emergency procedures. While minor surgical complications (mSCs) may be manageable, MSCs—including anastomotic leaks, hemorrhage, sepsis, and multiorgan failure—contribute disproportionately to patient morbidity, mortality, and healthcare burden.1–4 Patients admitted to the surgical intensive care unit (SICU) from the emergency room (ER) represent a unique and vulnerable cohort. These individuals often present with acute pathology, limited opportunity for preoperative optimization, and systemic instability—all of which predispose them to adverse outcomes.5–7

Prior studies have explored predictors of postoperative complications in elective surgical populations or generalized intensive care unit (ICU) cohorts.8–13 However, relatively few investigations have specifically addressed ER-to-SICU surgical patients—a group that embodies a convergence of surgical urgency, physiologic derangement, and comorbid burden. The clinical trajectory of these patients is complex, and failure to identify high-risk features early can result in delayed intervention and poor outcomes.

Moreover, while the Clavien–Dindo classification (CDC) has become a validated and standardized tool for classifying surgical complications, 14 it has not been widely applied to stratify postoperative risk among SICU patients admitted from the ER. In this context, quantifying both the incidence and the predictors of MSCs, along with their impact on ICU and hospital outcomes, can support more accurate triage, guide surgical decision-making, and help allocate critical care resources efficiently.

This study aims to address this knowledge gap by characterizing MSCs and in-hospital mortality in patients who undergo emergency surgery and are subsequently admitted to the SICU from the ER. We also identify the independent clinical predictors of adverse outcomes using multivariate modeling. Ultimately, this study seeks to inform better risk stratification and perioperative management strategies in this high-risk patient population.

Methods

Study setting and participants

We conducted a retrospective, observational cohort study at Kaohsiung Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, a major tertiary referral center in Taiwan. This study received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Kaohsiung Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, Taiwan (March 13th, 2023, Number: 202300280B0). It was carried out in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975 as revised in 2024. Because all the patients’ details were de-identified, and informed consent was waived. The reporting of this study conforms to STROBE guidelines. 15

All adult patients (≥20 years) who underwent emergency surgery and were admitted directly from the ER to the SICU between January 2022 and February 2023 were consecutively included. The SICU is a 23-bed unit caring for critically ill surgical patients across various specialties.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients were eligible if they underwent an emergency surgical procedure and had a recorded SICU admission directly from the ER. Exclusion criteria included age <20 years, missing surgical or ICU admission data, multiple ICU admissions during the same hospitalization (only the first was included), or transfers from wards or outpatient settings.

Data collection and variables

We extracted data from the Chang Gung Research Database, a comprehensive electronic health record system. 16 Variables collected included demographics, Charlson comorbidity index, 17 Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II), 18 Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), 19 American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, 20 reasons for ICU admission, surgical site and type, operative duration, wound class, 21 timing from ER to operation, use of minimally invasive techniques, and prior surgery within 30 days before the index surgery. The APACHE II score and SOFA score were assessed at SICU admission, while the ASA score was collected during the operation. Biliary tree surgery involves the intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts.

Intervention data included mechanical ventilation (duration and >48 h), total parenteral nutrition (TPN), blood transfusion, dialysis, central line placement, and postoperative medication use (including antibiotics, steroids, antifungals). Key lab parameters included albumin, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), creatinine, hemoglobin, platelet count, and bilirubin. The laboratory variables were collected at the time of SICU admission.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the occurrence of MSCs, defined as CDC Grades IIIb–V.22,23 Secondary outcomes included ICU stay, hospital stay, 30-day mortality, in-hospital mortality, and hospital costs.

Statistical analysis

Patients were divided into two groups based on complication severity: mSCs (Grades I–IIIa) and MSCs (Grades IIIb–V). Continuous variables were summarized as means ± standard deviations and compared using Student's t-test (unpaired) or Mann–Whitney U test as appropriate. Categorical data were analyzed using chi-square or Fisher's exact test. For continuous variables with missing data, imputation was performed using the median values computed from all available cases in the cohort. Variables with p values < 0.05 were selected for multivariate logistic regression using the enter method to identify independent predictors of MSCs and in-hospital mortality. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were used to assess multicollinearity among variables. Kaplan–Meier curves were generated for survival analysis. All analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise Guide version 5.1, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Two independent investigators double-checked all data.

Results

A total of 322 adult patients admitted to the SICU from the ER after emergency surgery were included in the analysis. Among them, 67 patients (20.8%) developed MSCs, while 255 (79.2%) experienced mSCs. In-hospital mortality was 14.0% (n = 45), with significantly higher mortality in the MSCs group (47.8%) compared to the mSCs group (5.1%) (Figure 1).

Univariate analysis

Patients with MSCs demonstrated significantly higher disease severity at SICU admission, as evidenced by elevated SOFA scores (median 7.0 (interquartile range (IQR), 5.0–10.0) vs. median 9.0 (IQR, 6.0–13.0), p < 0.001) and APACHE II (median 20.0 (IQR, 14.0–25.0) vs. median 24.0 (IQR, 20.0–30.0), p < 0.001). Shock on admission was more prevalent in the MSCs group (35.8% vs. 13.3%, p < 0.001). Preoperative ASA scores ≥4 were also significantly more common in the MSC cohort (68.7% vs. 42.8%, p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Patients in the major MSCs group were more likely to have undergone prior surgery within 30 days before the index operation (1.57% vs. 7.46%, p = 0.021). Surgical complexity influenced outcomes, with biliary tree surgery (7.5% vs. 1.2%, p = 0.003) and soft tissue surgery (16.4% vs. 7.8%, p = 0.034) more frequently observed in the MSCs group. Prolonged mechanical ventilation (>48 h) was significantly associated with MSCs (85.1% vs. 58.0%, p < 0.001), as was increased duration of ventilator use (median 2.0 (IQR, 1.0–3.0) vs. median 8.0 (IQR, 2.0–16.0) days, p < 0.001).

Several interventions and laboratory parameters were also significantly different between groups: higher rates of TPN, blood transfusions, central venous catheter use, hemodialysis, double lumen use, antibiotics use, albumin level, elevated ALT and creatinine levels, and lower platelet count were all associated with MSCs.

Outcomes

Patients with MSCs had markedly prolonged ICU stays (median 4.0 (IQR, 3.0–7.0) days vs. median 10.0 (IQR, 5.0–19.0) days, p < 0.001) and significantly higher hospital costs (US$6390.7 vs. US$14,224.1, p < 0.001) (Table 2). Although the hospital stays did not reach statistical significance (median 18.0 (IQR, 12.0–31.0) vs. median 27.0 (IQR, 10.0–45.0) days, p = 0.055), there was a trend toward a longer hospital stay in the MSCs group.

Discussion

This study highlights the clinical burden and predictors of MSCs and in-hospital mortality in patients admitted to the SICU following emergency surgery from the ER. Our findings show that nearly one in five patients developed MSCs, with these complications strongly associated with increased mortality, prolonged ICU stay, and significantly elevated healthcare costs.

The incidence of MSCs in our study (20%) was comparable to that reported by Dharap et al. in their 2022 prospective observational study. 24 However, important methodological and population differences exist. Their cohort included patients undergoing either elective or emergency general surgery, and MSCs were defined as Clavien–Dindo grade III or higher. Moreover, their patient population did not consist exclusively of ICU patients.

Our study is also consistent with the literature regarding the impact of surgical complications on hospital outcomes. In a retrospective study from American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program, patients who underwent emergency surgery and had severe complications such as pneumonia, stroke, and major bleeding were strongly associated with death. 25 Similarly, a retrospective observational study utilizing a Canadian national database demonstrated that patients who experienced postoperative complications following elective or emergency general surgery incurred substantially higher hospital costs. 26 These findings collectively emphasize that MSCs not only increase the risk of mortality but also impose a significant economic burden on healthcare systems.

Several critical risk factors were identified. Elevated APACHE II scores and the presence of bleeding at SICU admission consistently emerged as strong predictors of both MSCs and mortality. Similarly, elevated ALT was also referred to as acute liver dysfunction, which represents acute illness and hence was associated with higher mortality. These findings underscore the role of acute physiological derangement and hemodynamic instability in the early postoperative period as a harbinger of poor outcomes, reinforcing prior reports in critically ill surgical populations.27,28

The ASA physical status score, which reflects preoperative comorbidity burden, was another key determinant. Patients classified as ASA ≥4 had more than a twofold increased risk of MSCs and over a sixfold higher risk of death, aligning with previous literature that demonstrates the predictive value of ASA status in surgical risk models.29,30

Notably, soft-tissue surgeries were independently associated with increased complication and mortality risks. These procedures are often associated with higher infection risk, consistent with prior studies linking contaminated soft-tissue operations with adverse outcomes.31,32 However, given the low incidence of soft-tissue surgery, this finding should be cautiously interpreted to avoid overestimating its influence on outcomes.

Based on our findings, patients who received quinolone therapy were more likely to experience MSCs. The use of quinolones may reflect the presence of preexisting infections or more severe underlying conditions, both of which can predispose patients to postoperative morbidity.33,34 In addition, the use of immunosuppressants or corticosteroids before surgery was associated with a higher risk of postoperative mortality. These medications can impair wound healing, increase susceptibility to infection, and attenuate the physiological response to surgical stress, thereby contributing to poorer outcomes.35,36 Together, these observations highlight the importance of careful preoperative evaluation and optimization in patients receiving antibiotics or immunosuppressive therapy.

The survival impact of MSCs was profound. Patients who experienced major complications had nearly 40% 30-day mortality, and nearly half died before discharge. This highlights the importance of early identification and aggressive management of complications when they occur, as well as the potential role of failure-to-rescue as a quality-of-care metric.

From a resource utilization perspective, MSCs were associated with a threefold increase in ICU days and more than double the hospital costs. These findings mirror those of previous economic studies, emphasizing that MSCs are not only clinically significant but also carry a substantial financial burden.37,38

Clinical implications

The identification of reliable early predictors provides an opportunity to implement targeted interventions. Patients presenting with high APACHE II or ASA scores, shock, or undergoing complex surgeries should be flagged for enhanced monitoring, proactive complication prevention, and possibly prioritized access to senior surgical or critical care teams. Incorporating these predictors into risk stratification tools or electronic health record alerts may facilitate timely escalation of care.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. It was retrospective and single-centered, which may limit generalizability. Some unmeasured confounders, such as intraoperative variables (intraoperative blood loss and surgeon experience) and postoperative care process indicators, were not available. Moreover, given the study's retrospective design, the predictors were only associated with the outcomes, and causality cannot be inferred. Nonetheless, the use of a well-defined cohort, validated complication classification, and robust statistical modeling strengthens our conclusions.

Future directions

Further research should focus on validating these predictors across multicenter cohorts and examining the impact of intervention bundles in high-risk subgroups. Real-time predictive analytics using machine learning models integrated into SICU monitoring systems may also improve early detection and response.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort study of SICU patients admitted from the ER following surgery, we identified several independent predictors of MSCs and in-hospital mortality. Key risk factors included elevated APACHE II scores, shock on admission, ASA classification ≥4, complex surgical procedures (especially involving the biliary tree or soft tissue), and prolonged mechanical ventilation.

Our findings highlight the multifactorial nature of postoperative risk in critically ill surgical patients and underscore the urgent need for early recognition of high-risk individuals. Incorporating these predictors into perioperative management protocols may improve outcomes, reduce complications, and optimize resource utilization in the SICU.

Future studies should focus on multicenter validation and the integration of predictive models into real-time clinical decision support tools.
