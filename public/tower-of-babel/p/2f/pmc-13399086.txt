Early diagnosis of gestational diabetes mellitus evidence from Asia countries: A meta‐analysis and systematic review
Authors: Ke Thi Lan Anh, Nguyen Ngoc Anh, Pham Minh Nguyet, Dang Phuong Linh, Tran Thi Hai Yen, Vo Duy Quan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a common metabolic complication of pregnancy associated with substantial maternal and neonatal morbidity. Asian populations demonstrate increased susceptibility to glucose intolerance and may develop gestational diabetes earlier in pregnancy. However, the prevalence and outcomes of early‐diagnosed gestational diabetes mellitus (E‐GDM) in Asian populations remain incompletely understood.MethodsA systematic review and meta‐analysis was conducted following PRISMA guidelines. PubMed, ScienceDirect, and Web of Science were searched from database inception to February 2026 to identify studies evaluating gestational diabetes diagnosed before 24 weeks of gestation in Asian populations.ResultsA total of 2329 records were identified, and 17 studies were included, with 16 eligible studies for meta‐analysis. E‐GDM prevalence ranged from 0.75% to 22.81%, accounting for 9.43%–78.78% of total GDM cases. Compared with conventional GDM, E‐GDM was associated with increased risks of C‐section or instrumental delivery (OR = 1.13, 95% CI 1.01–1.26), preterm birth (OR = 1.38, 95% CI 1.07–1.78), and insulin therapy requirement (OR = 1.60, 95% CI 1.09–2.36). Most neonatal outcomes were similar between groups. Women with E‐GDM showed greater metabolic vulnerability and possible postpartum metabolic dysfunction.ConclusionsE‐GDM in Asian populations identifies women with greater metabolic vulnerability and increased obstetric risk. E‐GDM was associated with higher insulin requirements, increased risks of preterm birth and operative delivery, and possible long‐term metabolic dysfunction. Harmonized diagnostic criteria and region‐specific screening strategies are needed.Keywords: Asian populations, Early gestational diabetes, systematic review

INTRODUCTION

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a common metabolic disorder of pregnancy and a growing global public health concern. 1 , 2 Defined as glucose intolerance with onset or first recognition during pregnancy, GDM is associated with substantial short‐ and long‐term adverse outcomes for both mothers and their offspring. These include hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, C‐section delivery, fetal overgrowth, neonatal metabolic complications, and an increased lifetime risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease. 3 The rising prevalence of GDM reflects broader global trends in maternal age, overweight and obesity, and metabolic dysfunction, and poses increasing challenges for maternal healthcare systems. 4

Emerging evidence indicates that a substantial proportion of women who ultimately meet diagnostic criteria for GDM already demonstrate abnormal glucose metabolism in the first trimester or early second trimester. 5 , 6 , 7 Early pregnancy hyperglycemia has been independently associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, including fetal overgrowth and preeclampsia, even when glucose levels do not meet the threshold for overt diabetes. 8 , 9 Consequently, early diagnosis of GDM (E‐GDM) has been proposed to identify women at increased metabolic risk and enable earlier lifestyle or pharmacological intervention; however, whether early diagnosis improves pregnancy outcomes remains uncertain.

However, the clinical value of E‐GDM diagnosis in Asian populations remains uncertain despite the increasing burden of hyperglycemia in pregnancy across the region. 10 Asian populations exhibit distinct GDM characteristics compared with Western populations. Asian women demonstrate greater susceptibility to GDM despite lower BMI, with South Asians showing particularly elevated risk. 11 Pregnancy outcomes also differ, with Asian populations generally reporting fewer macrosomic or large‐for‐gestational‐age (LGA) infants and relatively more small‐for‐gestational‐age births. 12 , 13 These differences likely reflect ethnic‐specific metabolic characteristics, including greater visceral adiposity at lower BMI, reduced β‐cell reserve, and altered insulin secretion patterns. Western evidence further suggests that E‐GDM may represent a more severe metabolic phenotype associated with adverse perinatal and long‐term maternal outcomes; however, whether these findings can be directly extrapolated to Asian populations remains unclear. 14

Importantly, Asian populations should not be considered a homogeneous group. South Asian populations generally demonstrate a higher GDM burden and postpartum diabetes risk, largely driven by greater insulin resistance and impaired β‐cell compensation, whereas East Asian populations more commonly exhibit β‐cell dysfunction despite a lower BMI. Southeast Asian populations demonstrate additional variability related to nutritional transition, urbanization, and healthcare systems. 15 , 16 Differences in screening strategies and diagnostic criteria further contribute to regional variation. Nevertheless, studies from Asia have reported inconsistent findings regarding prevalence, screening approaches, diagnostic thresholds, and the effectiveness of early intervention, limiting comparability and preventing consensus. 17 Furthermore, most current recommendations are derived largely from Western populations and may not adequately reflect the metabolic characteristics of Asian women.

Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta‐analysis to clarify the prevalence and clinical implications of E‐GDM in Asian populations and inform region‐specific screening strategies.

MATERIAL AND METHOD

Meta‐analysis framework

This systematic review and meta‐analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. 18

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, ScienceDirect, and Web of Science from database inception to February 2026. The search combined terms related to “gestational diabetes mellitus” or “pregnancy hyperglycemia” with keywords for early diagnosis (e.g., “early screening,” “first trimester,” “early pregnancy”) and terms identifying Asian populations, including specific country names when applicable. After removing duplicates, two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Reference lists of included studies were also manually screened to identify additional relevant publications.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they involved pregnant women in Asian countries, evaluated E‐GDM, defined as GDM diagnosed before 24 weeks of gestation, used observational or randomized study designs, and reported extractable data on GDM prevalence, diagnostic performance, maternal outcomes, or neonatal outcomes.

Studies were excluded if they focused on pregestational or overt diabetes, assessed GDM only at the standard 24–28 week screening window, were non‐original articles, lacked sufficient data, or were not published in English.

Outcome definitions

Primary outcomes included the prevalence of E‐GDM and its proportion among total GDM cases in Asian populations. Secondary outcomes included maternal outcomes (gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, C‐section, or instrumental delivery, preterm birth, gestational weight gain, and insulin therapy requirement), neonatal outcomes (macrosomia, LGA infants, small‐for‐gestational‐age infants, neonatal hypoglycemia, neonatal intensive care unit admission, respiratory complications, neonatal jaundice, neonatal death, and shoulder dystocia), postpartum metabolic outcomes, and maternal risk factors associated with E‐GDM.

Data extraction

Data extraction was independently performed by two reviewers using a standardized, predesigned extraction form. Outcome data were obtained from text, tables, or figures. Corresponding authors were contacted when clarification or additional data were required. If outcome measures were not explicitly reported but could be derived mathematically from available data, they were calculated accordingly.

Quality assessment and risk of bias

The methodological quality of included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers. Studies were assessed using the modified Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. 19 Each study was classified as having low, moderate, or high risk of bias based on selection, comparability, and outcome assessment domains. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or adjudication by a third reviewer.

Statistical analysis

Meta‐analyses were performed using a random‐effects model. Effect sizes were pooled with 95% confidence intervals using odds ratios for dichotomous outcomes and weighted or standardized mean differences for continuous outcomes. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I 2 statistic, with values > 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity. Prespecified subgroup analyses according to screening strategy (universal vs selective screening) were performed. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots for outcomes including at least ten studies. A P‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using Review Manager 5.4 (Cochrane Collaboration).

RESULT

Search results

Study selection and the characteristics of included studies

A total of 2,329 records were identified through database searching. After removal of duplicates and non‐English articles, 2172 records were screened, and 1023 were excluded based on title and abstract screening. Full‐text reports of 1149 records were sought for retrieval, of which 322 could not be retrieved. Subsequently, 827 full‐text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 810 reports were excluded mainly because of the absence of early GDM diagnostic data or insufficient quantitative information.

Finally, 17 studies were included in the systematic review. The study by Bin Liu et al. was excluded from the meta‐analysis because its definition of E‐GDM differed from other included studies. Specifically, only women diagnosed during the early oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) were classified as E‐GDM and did not receive treatment, whereas women diagnosed at both early and standard OGTTs were classified as conventional GDM and received treatment. 20 This inconsistency in E‐GDM classification and management could affect comparability across studies. Therefore, 16 studies were eligible for meta‐analysis (Figure 1).

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram.

Included studies were conducted across several Asian and Middle Eastern countries, predominantly India and Japan, with additional studies from Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Thailand, Iran, China, and Israel. Most studies were cohort studies (10 retrospective and five prospective), with two cross‐sectional studies. Study periods ranged from 2003 to 2024. Detailed study characteristics are summarized in Table S1.

Early GDM prevalence in Asian populations and screening strategies

Substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies in screening strategies, diagnostic criteria, and study populations. Among universal screening cohorts, fasting plasma glucose (FPG)‐based approaches reported GDM prevalence ranging from 7.9% to 22.8%, with early‐diagnosed cases accounting for 22.1%–78.8% of total GDM cases. 6 , 21 , 22 , 23 Universal OGTT reported prevalence between 14.8% and 21.6% 24 , whereas hybrid approaches showed wider variation, with prevalence ranging from 0.8% to 5.8% and E‐GDM proportions ranging from 25.9% to 34.1%. 25 Universal HbA1c‐based screening identified a prevalence of 9.7%, with E‐GDM accounting for 35.3% of total GDM cases. 8

In high‐risk populations, universal FPG‐ or OGTT‐based screening strategies reported early diagnosis proportions of 30.4%–40.5%. 26 , 27 Selective screening or OGTT‐based approaches showed lower early detection rates, ranging from 9.4% to 34.3%. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 Combined universal FPG with selective OGTT screening yielded intermediate detection rates (34.1%). 32

Diagnostic criteria also varied across studies. While most studies applied IADPSG/WHO 2013 criteria, others used Carpenter–Coustan 23 , 29 , 30 , Japanese Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology 26 , NICE or DIPSI criteria 33 , or locally adapted thresholds. These differences in screening approaches and diagnostic definitions likely contributed to variability in reported prevalence and early diagnosis rates. Further details are summarized in Table S2.

Maternal and neonatal adverse outcomes associated with E‐GDM

Maternal adverse outcomes

Early‐diagnosed GDM was associated with a statistically significant increase in the risk of C‐section or instrumental delivery (OR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.01–1.26, P = 0.03; I 2 = 37%) (Figure 2). The risk of preterm labor was also significantly elevated in the E‐GDM group (OR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.07–1.78, P = 0.01; I 2 = 63%) (Figure 3).

Insulin treatment requirement of E‐GDM

Women with E‐GDM had a significantly higher likelihood of requiring insulin therapy compared with U‐GDM (OR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.09–2.36, P = 0.02). Subgroup analyses demonstrated a statistically significant association in studies implementing universal screening strategies (OR = 1.89; 95% CI: 1.25–2.86, P = 0.003) 8 , 22 , 31 , 32 , 34 , whereas no statistically significant association was observed in studies employing selective screening approaches (OR = 1.19; 95% CI: 0.56–2.50, P = 0.66) 24 , 28 , 30 . No statistically significant subgroup difference was identified (P = 0.28) (Figure 7).

Neonatal adverse outcomes

Compared with U‐GDM, no statistically significant differences were observed across most neonatal outcomes among infants born to women with E‐GDM. No significant associations were observed for neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 0.98–1.62), neonatal death (OR = 1.25, 95% CI: 0.71–2.21), respiratory distress (OR = 1.21, 95% CI: 0.78–1.86), or shoulder dystocia (OR = 1.71, 95% CI: 0.27–10.74).

Regarding fetal growth outcomes, no statistically significant differences were observed for small for gestational age (OR = 1.23, 95% CI: 0.98–1.53), LGA (OR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.78–1.24), neonatal jaundice (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 0.82–1.26), or macrosomia (OR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.54–1.26). Mean birthweight also did not differ significantly between groups (SMD = −0.06, 95% CI: −0.15 to 0.02; P = 0.16). Substantial heterogeneity was observed across several pooled estimates (Table 1; Figures S1–S9).

Subgroup analysis according to screening approach

Maternal outcomes

For maternal outcomes, stratified analyses by screening strategy showed that E‐GDM was not significantly associated with C‐section or instrumental delivery in studies using universal screening (OR = 1.11, 95% CI: 0.94–1.30, P = 0.22), whereas a statistically significant association was observed in selective screening studies (OR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.01–1.40, P = 0.04). No statistically significant subgroup difference was observed (I 2 = 0%, P = 0.52). 8 , 21 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 34

For preterm delivery, a significant association was identified in the universal screening subgroup (OR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.10–2.09, P = 0.01), but not in the selective screening subgroup (OR = 1.11, 95% CI: 0.80–1.53, P = 0.53). No statistically significant subgroup difference was observed between screening strategies (Figures S10, S11). 8 , 21 , 24 , 25 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 34

Neonatal outcomes

For neonatal outcomes, stratified analyses according to screening strategy showed no statistically significant association between E‐GDM and NICU admission. In studies employing universal screening, no significant increase in NICU admission was observed among neonates born to mothers with E‐GDM (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 0.91–1.76, P = 0.17). Studies using selective screening also showed no statistically significant association (OR = 1.31, 95% CI: 0.98–1.75, P = 0.07).

E‐GDM was also not significantly associated with macrosomia or LGA infants under either screening strategy. For macrosomia, pooled estimates remained non‐significant in both universal screening studies (OR = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.41–1.25, P = 0.23) and selective screening studies (OR = 1.22, 95% CI: 0.83–1.80, P = 0.31). Similarly, no statistically significant associations were observed for LGA in universal screening (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.73–1.27, P = 0.77) or selective screening studies (OR = 1.05, 95% CI: 0.64–1.73, P = 0.84).

Assessment of subgroup heterogeneity indicated no significant between‐group heterogeneity for NICU admission and LGA outcomes (I 2 = 0%). In contrast, moderate subgroup heterogeneity was observed for macrosomia across screening strategies (I 2 = 58.5%) (Figures S12–S14).

Long‐term maternal metabolic outcome of E‐GDM

Three studies assessed progression to type 2 diabetes mellitus and long‐term metabolic dysfunction following E‐GDM compared with U‐GDM. An Indian cohort reported a higher prevalence of postpartum diabetes in the E‐GDM group compared with usual diagnosis (22.2% vs. 12.6%) at 32 months. 22 Similarly, a Saudi Arabian study observed increased rates of impaired glucose tolerance and overt diabetes among women with E‐GDM, although the differences were not statistically significant. 28 In contrast, a large Israeli cohort demonstrated that second‐trimester GDM was associated with the highest subsequent risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus, with an adjusted hazard ratio nearly threefold higher than that observed for early GDM. Early GDM increased type 2 diabetes mellitus risk primarily among women without obesity. 23

Regarding metabolic function, E‐GDM was associated with greater postpartum insulin resistance and impaired β‐cell compensation. Higher HOMA‐IR values and a lower disposition index were reported in the E‐GDM group, indicating reduced β‐cell function compared with classical GDM. 22 Early GDM was also associated with a tendency toward higher postpartum BMI across studies. 23 , 28 Although several cardiometabolic parameters appeared less favorable in E‐GDM, most differences did not reach statistical significance (Table 2).

Risk factors for develop of E‐GDM

Pooled analyses indicated that women with E‐GDM were older (SMD = 0.17; 95% CI: 0.04–0.30; P = 0.009) and had higher pre‐pregnancy BMI (SMD = 0.25; 95% CI: 0.19–0.30; P < 0.001) compared with women diagnosed after 24 weeks of gestation. However, these associations were accompanied by moderate to substantial heterogeneity across studies (I 2 > 50%).

In contrast, family history and previous obstetric history factors were not significantly associated with early diagnosis. Neither a family history of gestational diabetes or diabetes mellitus (OR = 1.30; 95% CI: 0.88–1.93; P = 0.18; I 2 = 84%) nor a previous history of GDM (OR = 1.37; 95% CI: 0.94–2.00; P = 0.10; I 2 = 60%) differed significantly between women with E‐GDM and those diagnosed later in pregnancy.

Glycemic parameters showed differential patterns between groups. FPG concentrations were higher among women with E‐GDM (SMD = 0.77; 95% CI: 0.33–1.20; p = < 0.001), although considerable heterogeneity was observed (I 2 = 99%). In contrast, both the 1‐h glucose value (SMD = −0.04; 95% CI: −0.21 to 0.12; P = 0.60; I 2 = 90%) and the 2‐h measurement (SMD = −0.24; 95% CI: −0.50 to 0.03; P = 0.08; I 2 = 96%) were comparable between early and standard diagnostic groups (Table 3; Figures S15–S21).

Heterogeneity

Substantial between‐study heterogeneity was observed across pooled analyses, likely reflecting differences in screening strategies, diagnostic criteria, study populations, and study designs. Among maternal outcomes, heterogeneity was generally low to moderate for C‐section or instrumental delivery and preeclampsia, whereas gestational hypertension, preterm labor, gestational weight gain, and insulin treatment requirement demonstrated substantial variability across studies. Heterogeneity for insulin therapy remained considerable despite stratification according to screening approach, suggesting that factors beyond screening strategy may contribute to differences between studies.

For neonatal outcomes, moderate heterogeneity was observed for macrosomia, LGA infants, NICU admission, and respiratory distress, whereas neonatal jaundice showed relatively low variability. Shoulder dystocia demonstrated substantial heterogeneity, likely reflecting differences in obstetric management and event frequency across populations. In contrast, small‐for‐gestational‐age infants and neonatal death showed consistent findings with minimal heterogeneity across studies. Overall, the observed heterogeneity highlights the underlying clinical and methodological diversity among included studies and should be considered when interpreting pooled estimates.

Risk of bias assessment

Methodological quality was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS). Overall study quality was moderate, with most included studies demonstrating acceptable methodological rigor (Table S3). Most studies clearly defined study objectives and enrolled reasonably representative populations, although incomplete reporting of participant selection procedures and non‐response assessment was observed in several studies.

Adjustment for potential confounding factors and comparability between study groups was less consistently addressed across studies, representing the most common sources of methodological limitation. In contrast, outcome assessment methods and statistical analyses were generally appropriate and adequately reported. Overall, the predominance of moderate‐quality observational evidence suggests that pooled findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Publication bias

Publication bias assessment was performed for outcomes with at least ten included studies, namely C‐section or instrumental delivery and gestational hypertension. Visual inspection of the funnel plots did not demonstrate marked asymmetry for either outcome. For C‐section or instrumental delivery, studies appeared relatively symmetrically distributed around the pooled effect estimate, suggesting a low likelihood of substantial publication bias.

For gestational hypertension, mild asymmetry was observed, with several smaller studies showing relatively larger effect estimates; however, the overall distribution remained broadly balanced and no clear evidence of severe small‐study effects was identified. Given the limited number of studies and the observational nature of included cohorts, publication bias could not be completely excluded (Figures S22, S23).

DISCUSSION

This meta‐analysis demonstrates that E‐GDM in Asian populations identifies a subgroup of women with greater underlying cardiometabolic susceptibility rather than merely earlier detection of conventional GDM. 9 , 35 Women with E‐GDM showed higher fasting glucose concentrations, greater insulin treatment requirements, and increased risks of operative delivery and preterm birth. In contrast, most neonatal outcomes remained comparable between early‐ and conventionally diagnosed GDM groups. These findings suggest that E‐GDM in Asian populations may represent a distinct clinical phenotype with implications extending beyond pregnancy.

Distinct metabolic characteristics of E‐GDM in Asian populations

Women diagnosed early demonstrated higher fasting glucose despite similar post‐load glucose values compared with women diagnosed later in pregnancy. 6 , 24 , 27 , 33 This pattern may reflect predominant abnormalities in basal glucose regulation, potentially related to hepatic insulin resistance and impaired basal insulin secretion. Elevated fasting glucose early in pregnancy may reflect underlying dysglycemia predating pregnancy in at least a subset of women. 36 , 37 The substantially greater requirement for insulin treatment further supports that women identified early represent a subgroup with more pronounced metabolic disturbance. 28 , 32 , 34

Evidence outside Asia has suggested that E‐GDM is associated with greater neonatal morbidity, fetal overgrowth, and long‐term cardiometabolic risk. Previous studies reported higher insulin resistance, elevated HbA1c and C‐peptide concentrations, increased treatment requirements despite earlier intervention, and greater risks of LGA infants, neonatal hyperinsulinemia, and postpartum metabolic abnormalities. 38 , 39 In contrast, our findings in Asian populations demonstrated more consistent associations with insulin treatment requirement, operative delivery, and preterm birth, whereas neonatal outcomes were largely similar between groups. These differences may reflect population‐specific metabolic characteristics, as glucose dysregulation in Asian women frequently develops at lower BMI thresholds and may involve greater β‐cell dysfunction relative to obesity‐driven insulin resistance. 11 , 40 Consequently, early pregnancy hyperglycemia in Asian populations may indicate greater maternal susceptibility without necessarily producing the degree of fetal overgrowth more commonly reported in non‐Asian cohorts.

Maternal and neonatal implications of E‐GDM

E‐GDM was associated with increased risks of operative delivery and preterm birth. 8 , 26 , 31 , 32 , 34 Several mechanisms may contribute to these findings, including endothelial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, and impaired placental adaptation associated with maternal hyperglycemia. 14 , 41 , 42 In addition, greater clinical surveillance following early diagnosis may influence obstetric management decisions and lower thresholds for intervention, particularly in healthcare settings with high baseline operative delivery rates. 43 , 44 The observed relationship with operative delivery may therefore reflect both biological risk and clinical practice patterns.

In contrast, neonatal outcomes were generally comparable between E‐GDM and conventionally diagnosed GDM. No clear associations were observed for macrosomia, LGA infants, neonatal intensive care admission, respiratory complications, neonatal jaundice, neonatal death, or shoulder dystocia. 26 , 28 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 34 These findings suggest that although E‐GDM identifies women at greater maternal risk, neonatal implications remain less certain and may be influenced by differences in diagnostic criteria, treatment intensity, screening strategies, and baseline population characteristics across studies.

Long‐term cardiometabolic implications of E‐GDM

Beyond pregnancy outcomes, E‐GDM may represent an early marker of future cardiometabolic vulnerability. 22 , 23 , 28 In Asian populations, glucose dysregulation often develops at younger ages and lower adiposity levels, potentially allowing pregnancy to reveal underlying susceptibility earlier in the disease trajectory. 45 , 46 Women diagnosed early demonstrated higher fasting glucose and greater insulin treatment requirements, supporting the possibility that some cases reflect impaired glucose regulation preceding pregnancy rather than purely gestational metabolic adaptation. 22 , 32 , 47

Long‐term follow‐up studies further suggest increased risks of postpartum metabolic dysfunction among women with E‐GDM, including greater insulin resistance, impaired β‐cell compensation, and progression toward type 2 diabetes. 22 , 23 , 48 Given the rapidly increasing burden of diabetes across Asia, identifying women with E‐GDM may provide opportunities for earlier risk stratification, postpartum monitoring, and long‐term prevention strategies.

Evidence from the TOBOGM study further highlights the complexity of dysglycemia detected early in pregnancy. Women diagnosed before 20 weeks demonstrated higher risks of adverse composite perinatal outcomes despite treatment initiation during conventional screening windows. 49 Together with our findings, these observations suggest that early pregnancy dysglycemia identifies women with greater underlying susceptibility; however, substantial heterogeneity across studies indicates that early screening strategies cannot yet be universally applied. Instead, optimal approaches must balance feasibility, diagnostic accuracy, and overall clinical benefit. 9 , 35

Implications for screening strategies in Asian populations

Early FPG testing may be attractive in many Asian settings and may offer practical advantages in many Asian settings because of lower cost and easier implementation, particularly in resource‐constrained healthcare systems. 50 However, transient early hyperglycemia, pre‐existing glucose intolerance, and persistent gestational dysglycemia may not always be clearly distinguishable, potentially increasing overdiagnosis and unnecessary intervention. 51 Conversely, selective screening approaches may fail to identify clinically important dysglycemia, particularly in Asian populations where metabolic risk frequently occurs in women without overt obesity. 52

These considerations support individualized screening approaches adapted to local healthcare systems and disease burden. Earlier evaluation may be particularly relevant among women with advanced maternal age, elevated pre‐pregnancy BMI, or higher fasting glucose levels. In settings with substantial dysglycemia burden and sufficient clinical resources, broader screening approaches may also be appropriate. Ultimately, screening strategies across Asia should balance feasibility, diagnostic accuracy, and clinical benefit while recognizing the considerable biological and healthcare heterogeneity across the region. 53

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, substantial heterogeneity was observed across maternal and neonatal outcomes, likely reflecting differences in diagnostic criteria, screening strategies, timing of testing, maternal characteristics, and clinical management across studies. Second, women classified as E‐GDM represent a heterogeneous group that may include individuals with previously unrecognized dysglycemia before pregnancy as well as transient early hyperglycemia that does not persist later in gestation, limiting the ability to distinguish true early GDM from pre‐existing metabolic abnormalities. Third, considerable variability in screening implementation and diagnostic thresholds across Asian populations may have influenced pooled estimates and reduced comparability between studies. Finally, the overall methodological quality of included studies was moderate, and residual confounding inherent to observational designs cannot be excluded. Therefore, findings should be interpreted with caution.

CONCLUSION

Our findings suggest that E‐GDM in Asian populations may identify women with greater cardiometabolic vulnerability rather than solely representing earlier detection of conventional gestational diabetes. Early diagnosis was associated with higher risks of operative delivery, preterm birth, and insulin therapy requirement, whereas most neonatal outcomes remained comparable between early‐ and conventionally diagnosed GDM. Given the rising burden of dysglycemia at lower BMI levels across Asia, early pregnancy hyperglycemia may identify women at increased long‐term metabolic risk. Future strategies should emphasize harmonized diagnostic criteria, region‐specific screening approaches, and integration of antenatal care with postpartum metabolic surveillance and diabetes prevention.
