Background

Scrub typhus is an acute febrile illness caused by Orientia tsutsugamushi, an obligate intracellular bacterium transmitted through the bite of infected larval trombiculid mites (chiggers). It has traditionally been considered endemic to the Asia–Pacific “tsutsugamushi triangle,” encompassing South and Southeast Asia, the Western Pacific, and northern Australia [1]. In these regions, over a million new infections are estimated to occur annually, and if left untreated, the case fatality rate can exceed 30% [2, 3].

A recent systematic review reported a wide global seroprevalence of scrub typhus, highlighting its substantial but underrecognized burden in endemic and newly affected regions [4]. Despite growing recognition as a neglected tropical disease, scrub typhus continues to cause significant morbidity and mortality in Asia due to diagnostic delays and limited awareness in non-endemic regions.

Clinically, scrub typhus presents as an undifferentiated febrile illness that often mimics malaria, dengue, or leptospirosis, leading to diagnostic uncertainty in the absence of the characteristic eschar. The disease spectrum ranges from mild fever to multiorgan dysfunction, with complications including interstitial pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), hepatitis, acute kidney injury, myocarditis, and meningoencephalitis [5, 6]. The hallmark pathological process is a disseminated vasculitis and perivasculitis caused by endothelial invasion by O. tsutsugamushi, leading to microvascular thrombosis and tissue ischemia [7]. While hepatic dysfunction is the most common intra-abdominal manifestation, ischemic injury to solid organs such as the spleen or kidney is exceedingly rare.

We describe a previously healthy 35-year-old woman with scrub typhus who developed simultaneous splenic and renal infarction—an association not reported previously. This case highlights the potential for O. tsutsugamushi–induced vasculitis to cause visceral ischemia and underscores the importance of early imaging and targeted antibiotic therapy in endemic settings.

Case presentation

A 35-year-old woman from a rural area in an endemic region presented during the post-monsoon season with a 5-day history of fever, cough, shortness of breath, and anorexia, followed by left upper-quadrant abdominal pain for 3 days. She had no known comorbidities, chronic illnesses, prior thrombotic events, or history of recurrent pregnancy loss.

On examination, she was alert and oriented (GCS E4V5M6). Her vital signs were: blood pressure 133/68 mmHg, pulse 120 beats/min, respiratory rate 28/min, temperature 38.5 °C, and oxygen saturation(SpO2) 91% on room air. Arterial blood gas analysis revealed mild hypoxemia with a PaO₂/FiO₂ (P/F) ratio of approximately 230, along with respiratory alkalosis. Chest auscultation revealed bilateral coarse crepitations. Abdominal examination showed tenderness in the left upper quadrant. No rash or eschar was identified.

Laboratory evaluation demonstrated neutrophilic leukocytosis (total leukocyte count 13 × 10⁵/L), hemoglobin 12.7 g/dL, and mild thrombocytopenia (1.1 × 10⁵/µL). Liver function tests were deranged with aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 257 IU/L, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 86 IU/L, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) 520 IU/L, and total bilirubin 2.11 mg/dL (direct 1.10 mg/dL). Renal parameters were within normal limits (urea 17 mg/dL, creatinine 0.67 mg/dL) and urine routine microscopy was normal, with no hematuria or proteinuria.

Inflammatory markers were elevated, including C-reactive protein (CRP) 31.2 mg/L and D-dimer 1.98 µg/mL. Peripheral smear showed microcytic hypochromic red cells, neutrophilic leukocytosis, and thrombocytopenia.

Scrub typhus IgM ELISA was positive using a validated commercial kit i.e. InBios Scrub Typhus DetectTM with a regional optical density cut-off of 0.5; testing was performed after five days of symptom onset, consistent with expected seroconversion. Investigations for other tropical infections were negative, including dengue IgM ELISA, malaria peripheral smear/rapid antigen testing, and blood cultures for enteric fever.

Chest X-ray demonstrated bilateral infiltrates suggestive of pneumonia. Two-dimensional echocardiography showed preserved left ventricular systolic function (ejection fraction ≈ 60%), with no valvular vegetation or intracardiac thrombus. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed hepatomegaly with gallbladder wall edema. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) of the abdomen demonstrated hepatomegaly with peripheral wedge-shaped, non-enhancing hypodense areas involving the superior pole of the spleen and the lower pole of the right kidney, consistent with splenic and renal infarctions (Fig. 1A and B).

(A) Axial image showing a peripheral, wedge-shaped, non-enhancing hypodense area in the superior pole of the spleen (*), consistent with splenic infarction. (B) Axial image showing a similar wedge-shaped, non-enhancing hypodense lesion in the lower pole of the right kidney (*), consistent with renal infarction

Given the patient’s age and visceral infarcts, an extensive evaluation for autoimmune and vasculitic etiologies was undertaken. Antinuclear antibody (ANA), anti–double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), antiphospholipid antibodies (APLA), and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) were all negative. Coagulation profile was normal.

Empirical treatment was initiated with intravenous doxycycline (100 mg twice daily) and azithromycin (500 mg once daily), along with supportive care including low-flow oxygen and hepatoprotective agents. Over the next 72 h, the patient became afebrile, respiratory distress resolved, and liver enzymes and inflammatory markers showed steady improvement. By Day 4, she maintained SpO2 of 98–99% on room air. She was discharged on Day 8 in stable condition and remained asymptomatic on follow-up. A concise clinical timeline is provided in Table 1.

Discussion

Scrub typhus is an emerging infectious disease in the Indian subcontinent, characterized by Orientia tsutsugamushi induced systemic vasculitis and endothelial injury. The organism primarily targets vascular endothelial cells, resulting in widespread perivasculitis, microvascular thrombosis, and ischemic injury across multiple organs. Commonly involved sites include the lungs, liver, kidneys, and central nervous system, whereas splenic involvement is distinctly rare [8, 9].

The principal pathogenic mechanism in scrub typhus is focal or disseminated vasculitis resulting from endothelial invasion by Orientia tsutsugamushi and subsequent immune-mediated inflammation. Infection of endothelial cells and macrophages leads to endothelial injury, vascular leakage, cytokine release, and formation of microthrombi. This vasculitic process accounts for hemorrhage, ischemia, and infarction reported in organs such as the liver, spleen, kidneys, and brain [10, 11].

Splenic infarction has been reported sporadically and is usually described as an isolated complication. Across imaging based studies, the incidence of splenic infarction ranges from approximately 3.8% to 16%, reflecting heterogeneity in disease severity and imaging practices [12–14]. Several case reports have documented solitary or multiple splenic infarcts in scrub typhus, typically in the absence of endocarditis, arterial obstruction, or underlying coagulopathy, supporting a vasculitic etiology [8–10, 15, 16].

In contrast, our patient exhibited simultaneous splenic and renal infarctions, a combination not previously reported in scrub typhus. While splenic infarction has been attributed to localized endothelial injury secondary to vasculitis, concurrent renal involvement suggests a shared pathogenic mechanism. The most plausible explanation involves Orientia tsutsugamushi induced endothelial invasion and apoptosis, leading to cytokine-mediated endothelial activation, vascular inflammation, and intravascular microthrombi formation within small arterioles. A transient hypercoagulable state driven by systemic inflammation may further amplify this ischemic cascade. The coexistence of splenic and renal infarction in our patient therefore reflects a disseminated vasculitic and microangiopathic phenomenon, expanding the recognized vascular spectrum of scrub typhus.

From a clinical standpoint, abdominal pain in scrub typhus should not be dismissed as nonspecific. Persistent focal abdominal tenderness, particularly in the setting of hepatic dysfunction or elevated inflammatory markers, should prompt consideration of vascular complications. Although D-dimer elevation is non-specific and frequently observed in severe infections, contrast-enhanced computed tomography remains the diagnostic modality of choice for identifying visceral infarction. In our case, normal echocardiography, a normal coagulation profile, negative autoimmune and antiphospholipid testing, and absence of prior thrombotic history effectively excluded embolic and hypercoagulable causes, strengthening the causal association with scrub typhus–related vasculitis [9].

Early initiation of anti-rickettsial therapy most commonly doxycycline results in rapid clinical and biochemical recovery in the majority of cases [9, 15]. Recent randomized evidence has demonstrated that combination therapy with doxycycline and azithromycin may be beneficial in severe scrub typhus, leading to faster defervescence and fewer treatment failures; however, this benefit should not be extrapolated to all cases [17]. In our patient, early initiation of appropriate antimicrobial therapy led to marked clinical improvement within 72 h, underscoring the reversibility of O. tsutsugamushi–induced endothelial injury when promptly treated.

In summary, this case expands the vascular phenotype of scrub typhus by documenting concurrent splenic and renal infarction. Recognition of such atypical ischemic manifestations is essential in endemic regions, as early imaging and timely anti-rickettsial therapy can prevent further organ injury and lead to favorable outcomes.

Conclusion

Concurrent splenic and renal infarction represents an expanded vascular phenotype of scrub typhus. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for visceral ischemia in patients presenting with persistent abdominal pain, particularly in the setting of systemic inflammation. Prompt recognition and early initiation of appropriate anti-rickettsial therapy, most commonly doxycycline, can result in favorable outcomes. This case highlights scrub typhus as a systemic vasculitic illness capable of causing ischemic complications beyond the commonly involved organs.
