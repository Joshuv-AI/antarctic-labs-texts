In Pursuit of the Optimal Antimicrobial Stewardship Practice Model: A Multihospital Analysis of Direct Patient Care Interventions to Characterize the Role of Infectious Diseases Pharmacists
Authors: Harrison Phan, Jefferson Cua, Alice Margulis Landayan, Lourdes R Menendez Alvarado, Lee Amaya, Josh Caraccio, Timothy P Gauthier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis retrospective analysis across 3 hospitals evaluates antimicrobial stewardship interventions by clinical pharmacists, specialist pharmacists, and infectious diseases specialist pharmacists. Findings highlight differences in intervention types, urgency, and complexity, underscoring the value of infectious diseases specialist pharmacists in optimizing patient care and antimicrobial use within a multitiered pharmacy practice model.

Antimicrobial stewardship programs (ASP) are required by regulatory bodies and the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Core Elements of Antibiotic Stewardship serve as a key guiding point for ASP development [1, 2]. Within these Core Elements of Hospital Antibiotic Stewardship, “pharmacy expertise” is listed, which recognizes the importance of pharmacist engagement for leading and implementing efforts to improve antimicrobial use. Standardization of how pharmacy expertise is functionally integrated into ASP has been outlined by leading pharmacy organizations but cannot be fully standardized because pharmacy practice models, organizational workflows, and institutional needs can be highly variable [3–5]. Publications such as the description from Harmon et al. about what ASP pharmacists do globally within a large integrated healthcare system and the pharmacist staffing calculator from Echevarria et al. developed in the Veterans Affairs Health Administration are of tremendous value for contextualizing potential program structures [6, 7].

As the role of ASPs evolves, it is essential that healthcare facilities leverage their pharmacists’ roles in direct patient care to maximize the impact of their work, while empowering all pharmacists to take responsibility for medication use decisions [3]. As this is done today and, in the future, infectious diseases (ID) pharmacists (many of whom also function as ASP pharmacists) will be frequently tasked with leading initiatives for improving antimicrobial use through patient-specific antimicrobial interventions. There are limited published data about interventions made by pharmacists with advanced ID pharmacist training. Jantarathaneewat et al. found that, over an 8-month period, an ID pharmacist's interventions had an 82% acceptance rate and were more likely to be guideline adherent compared to non-ID pharmacist interventions [8]. Babiarz et al. found that, over a 5-year period, ASP recommendations from ID pharmacists had a 92% acceptance rate, which they reported to be associated with significant cost avoidance [9]. Although the notion that ID pharmacists can be successful in making impactful direct patient care intervention appears relatively established, what type of interventions they should make and what practice model supports optimal use of their skillset remains less clear.

To explore how ID pharmacists may provide the greatest direct patient care value, we aimed to evaluate antibiotic interventions by comparing clinical pharmacists (CP) versus specialist pharmacists (SP, not including ID specialists) versus ID specialist pharmacists (IDSP).

METHODS

This was a retrospective analysis that included 3 academic community hospitals that operate within an 11-hospital healthcare system. The ASP was established systemwide starting in 2019, which overlapped with the implementation of a new pharmacy practice model. Under the new model, hospital pharmacists were designated as CP-I, CP-II, SP-I, or SP-II, which corresponded to increased complexity of expected interventions. The IDSP fell into specialist pharmacist I or II buckets depending on years of experience, but all IDSPs were board-certified ID specialists who completed postgraduate year 2 pharmacy residencies in ID. The hospital-level IDSPs were incorporated into a team led by an enterprise-level ASP pharmacist manager that primarily focused on nondirect patient care functions (eg, protocols, policies, workflows, inventory management). This model enabled the entity-level IDSPs to have more of a focus on direct patient care tasks, such as making interventions. At a 950-bed hospital during the study period, there were 3 IDSPs (2 generalist and 1 oncology-focused), whereas 1 IDSP covered a 450-bed and a 30-bed hospital. Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 provide further detail on the staffing model and career ladder.

Intervention data were collected for April and May 2024 from an electronic clinical surveillance tool (VigiLanz Pharmacy Surveillance Pro, Inovalon, Bowie, MD). Using rule-based artificial intelligence, this program creates alerts to evaluate potential interventions such as renal dosing, bug-drug mismatch, and new vancomycin levels. Alerts may also be manually created. The extracted dataset was deidentified and then filtered to exclude nonantimicrobial interventions. Fields evaluated included hospital name, key drug, pharmacist name, pharmacist type, intervention detail, alert name, intervention outcome, alert source, and alert urgency label.

When acknowledging alerts, pharmacists select “per-protocol” or “accepted” to designate an intervention was made, whereas no action necessary indicates no intervention was made (ie, an alert prompted chart review but no change). Per-protocol refers to interventions delegated to pharmacists by medical providers through a policy. Intravenous to oral conversion, renal dose adjustments, and pharmacokinetic consults are all examples of pharmacist interventions managed per-protocol. Interventions without an associated policy required the pharmacist to communicate with the provider to ultimately have the recommendation accepted or rejected. CP, SP, and IDSP were evaluated by volume of alert outcome, alert type, alert urgency, alert source, and intervention type.

For statistical analysis, categorical variables were summarized using frequency and proportion, whereas continuous variables were described using median and interquartile range. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to assess differences in the distribution of continuous data. For categorical data, differences in proportions were evaluated using either the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. A P value <.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. This project was designated as nonhuman subject research by the local institutional review board.

RESULTS

The initial report identified 43 408 interventions across the 2-month period, of which 31 440 (75%) were excluded for not being related to antimicrobial drugs. Of the remaining 11 968 interventions for antimicrobials, 7165 (60%) were managed by a CP, 2623 (22%) were managed by a SP, and 2180 (18%) were managed by an IDSP. The data included 124 total pharmacists, with 97 CP, 23 SP, and 4 IDSP.

Table 2 provides details for intervention types by pharmacist type category as well as median interventions by pharmacist type. There were 32 CP with less than 10 interventions, 15 with 10–20 interventions, and 50 with more than 20 interventions. There were 6 SP with less than 10 interventions, 3 with 10–20 interventions, 8 with 20–100 interventions, and 6 with >100 interventions. Of the 6 SP with >100 interventions, 4 were critical care specialists and 2 were pediatrics specialists.

DISCUSSION

A clinical practice model leveraging pharmacists with different operational and clinical responsibilities led to antimicrobial stewardship intervention patterns from CP, SP, and IDSP that coincided to their assigned duties and had an overall low rejection rate across all groups. IDSPs had a high rate of interventions that required medical provider interaction, managed a large percentage of high-urgency alerts, and had a median number of ID-related interventions that was ∼18 times higher than CPs and ∼5 times higher than SPs. The greater volume of per-pharmacist interventions by IDSPs was anticipated given IDSPs are allowed dedicated time to focus on these types of interventions, whereas CPs and SPs had to also address non-ID interventions.

The CP group documented and implemented the greatest quantity of interventions, which was largely supported by alerts automatically generated by the electronic pharmacy surveillance software. The CP group had a large proportion of medium-urgency and no action necessary alerts, while also completing the bulk of interventions with lower complexity and routine protocols (eg, intravenous to oral, renal dose adjustment, pharmacokinetic monitoring). The SP group managed many per-protocol interventions, particularly pharmacokinetic dosing and renal dose adjustments. Interventions from the SP group were predominately system-generated alerts, yet they had the lowest proportion of IV to PO conversions, suggesting differences in role emphasis. With CPs taking on the more routine antimicrobial stewardship intervention workflows, the SP and IDSP groups were empowered to focus elsewhere.

Evaluating the intervention types, differences were observed across the pharmacist groupings, IDSPs completed significantly more antimicrobial duration of therapy, drug allergy avoidance/clarification, and antimicrobial change interventions. These findings and the high number of manual interventions suggest that IDSPs are working beyond the capacity of automated systems, spending more time on higher-urgency and more complex antimicrobial interventions. We feel these findings are a call to action about what ID pharmacist practice should look like and raise concern about the supply of ID pharmacists with the skillset to practice at this level. There are only 144 infectious diseases postgraduate year 2 training programs available through the American Society of Health-System Pharmacy, and it is no secret that many individuals with this training do not stay in the field [10, 11]. To battle the progression of antimicrobial resistance, institutions not only need to have a pharmacy practice model that supports high-level IDSP work, they also need to have adequately trained pharmacists to employ [12].

This analysis is limited by the exclusion of clinical or financial outcomes attached to the interventions. The burden to do this is beyond our capacity and assigning a value to each pharmacist intervention is accompanied by a slew of confounders that are complicated by the substantial variety of clinical details that present with each intervention. Even in the absence identifying what the impact is, demonstrating where the impact remains relevant to practice and novel. External validity may be impacted by practice model variability.

CONCLUSION

A pharmacy practice model incorporating leadership support and pharmacists with varying operational and clinical responsibilities enables hospital-based IDSPs to identify and implement interventions that leverage their unique skillset, which supports a positive impact on patient care, antibiotic resistance, and healthcare costs.
