A novel use of temporary ICL during DMEK to prevent gas-induced opacification of a scleral-fixated Carlevale IOL
Authors: Martina Aguilera Zúñiga, José Luis Güell, Óscar Gris, Alberto Salmoral, Carlos Martín, Nuno Moura-Coelho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report a novel surgical strategy using a temporary implantable collamer lens (ICL) during Descemet membrane endothelial keratoplasty (DMEK) to prevent gas-induced opacification of a scleral-fixated Carlevale intraocular lens (IOL) in a vitrectomized eye.ObservationsA 68-year-old male with a history of blunt trauma, pars plana vitrectomy, and Carlevale IOL implantation developed irreversible corneal edema. DMEK was performed with the intraoperative placement of an inverted ICL in the anterior chamber, serving as a protective barrier between the gas tamponade and the IOL. The ICL was explanted two weeks later, without complications. The endothelial graft remained well attached throughout the follow-up period, with no intraoperative or postoperative adverse events. The optical clarity of the Carlevale IOL was preserved, and no ICL-related complications were observed. Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) improved from logMAR 1.00 preoperatively to logMAR 0.22 by 3 months, with stability maintained through the 6-month follow-up period.Conclusions and importanceTemporary ICL implantation during DMEK may be an effective strategy to prevent gas-induced opacification in eyes with hydrophilic IOLs. Unlike IOL exchange, which adds surgical trauma, or gas-minimizing strategies, which risk detachment, this approach preserves full tamponade while safeguarding IOL clarity.Keywords: Descemet Membrane Endothelial Keratoplasty (DMEK), Intraocular lens opacification, Hydrophilic intraocular lens, Carlevale intraocular lens, Temporary Implantable Collamer Lens (ICL)

Introduction

Descemet membrane endothelial keratoplasty (DMEK) has become the leading surgical technique for the treatment of endothelial dysfunction, consistently demonstrating superior visual outcomes, faster recovery, low rejection rates and sustained graft survival over time.1,2 Despite its advantages, DMEK presents greater technical difficulty in certain ocular conditions, particularly in eyes with a history of pars plana vitrectomy or aphakia, where anterior chamber (AC) stability is often compromised.3

An additional concern arises in pseudophakic eyes with acrylic hydrophilic intraocular lenses (IOLs), which are particularly susceptible to opacification following exposure to intraocular gas. Gas tamponade during DMEK, which is essential for graft adherence, has been implicated in triggering hydrophilic IOLs opacification due to dehydration and subsequent deposition of calcium and phosphate crystals on or within the optic.4,5 Recent evidence has reported IOL opacification in approximately 9 % of pseudophakic eyes after DMEK, with onset ranging from 2 weeks to 10 months postoperatively. Hydrophilic IOL material was identified as the strongest risk factor, conferring a 65-fold higher risk than hydrophobic lenses. Rebubbling is an independent risk factor because it entails repeated IOL exposure to intracameral gas. Notably, up to one-third of opacified IOLs eventually require explantation, a procedure that carries substantial surgical risk.6

The Carlevale IOL (Soleko, Rome, Italy) is a sutureless scleral-fixated IOL (SSF-IOL) designed for aphakic eyes without capsular support.7 Its haptics are anchored within scleral tunnels, with long-term stability achieved through fibrotic integration, similar to other SSF-IOL techniques.8 However, the lens is made of hydrophilic acrylic, which is prone to irreversible opacification when exposed to intracameral gas during DMEK.

Hydrophilic IOL opacification remains a significant concern in DMEK. However, no physical barrier or intraocular device has been described to prevent this complication. To this end, we report the temporary implantation of an inverted implantable collamer lens (ICL; Visian ICL V4c, STAAR Surgical, Monrovia, CA, USA) in the anterior chamber, serving as a protective barrier against gas–IOL contact while preserving complete tamponade for graft adherence.

Case report

A 68-year-old male with a history of blunt ocular trauma to the right eye developed a traumatic cataract associated with iris damage. Management included pars plana lensectomy-vitrectomy, iris suturing, and secondary implantation of a Carlevale scleral-fixated IOL. Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) improved from logMAR 0.8 to 0.30.

Six months later, the patient experienced progressive visual decline due to irreversible corneal edema. At that time, BCVA had deteriorated to logMAR 1.00, with a manifest refraction of +0.75 D sphere and −3.25 D cylinder × 85°. Given the ongoing endothelial dysfunction and visual deterioration, an endothelial keratoplasty was indicated. However, the hydrophilic acrylic composition of the Carlevale IOL introduced an additional challenge due to the well-documented risk of gas-induced opacification.

At 6 months after implantation, the Carlevale IOL was stable and well-positioned, and primary exchange was dismissed as a first-line strategy. To mitigate the risk of hydrophilic IOL opacification, a temporary inverted ICL was implanted as a protective barrier and retained during the early postoperative period.

The surgery was performed in February 2025 by an experienced corneal surgeon (J.L.G.). Informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication. Surgical video recordings were collected for documentation. Intraoperative and postoperative anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) was used to evaluate graft attachment, ICL position, and AC depth, while slit-lamp photographs were obtained during follow-up.

Surgical technique

Surgery was performed under sedation and local anesthesia. Two paracenteses were created at the 3- and 10-o'clock positions, and a balanced salt solution (BSS) maintainer was introduced through the temporal paracentesis to stabilize the AC. A peripheral iridectomy was performed at the 6-o'clock position using Vannas scissors under infusion.

An ICL (−0.5 D, 12.1 mm) was inserted into the AC through a 2.4-mm superior clear corneal incision using McPherson forceps, without an ophthalmic viscosurgical device.

The ICL was implanted in an inverted position with posterior convexity and aligned horizontally to avoid covering the inferior iridectomy. Descematorhexis was performed under air following fluid–gas exchange, and DMEK was carried out using the “small bubble technique”.3 After graft insertion, a small air bubble was placed beneath the Descemet roll, over the ICL. One edge of the graft was gently grasped with specialized forceps, and controlled tapping on the corneal surface facilitated gradual unfolding. Once centered, a 20 % sulfur hexafluoride (SF6) tamponade was applied and adjusted to an 80 % anterior chamber fill, sparing inferior iridectomy. Intraoperative AS-OCT confirmed full graft attachment and an adequate ICL vault.

Results

The postoperative course was uneventful. No ICL-related complications were observed. The Descemet graft remained fully attached throughout the follow-up period, with no signs of detachment or interface fluid, as confirmed by postoperative AS-OCT (Fig. 1).

Postoperative anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) depicting a fully attached Descemet graft. The inverted temporary ICL (arrow) is visible in the anterior chamber, and the Carlevale IOL (asterisk) is positioned behind the iris.

The ICL was uneventfully explanted two weeks later, once complete reabsorption of gas had occurred, and no rebubbling was required. The procedure was performed under viscoelastic conditions to ensure endothelial safety (Video 2). In this case, viscoelastic protection alone was sufficient during ICL removal, owing to the adequate AC depth. However, in eyes with a shallow anterior chamber, the use of a spatula placed between the ICL and endothelium throughout the maneuver may provide additional protection.

Discussion

Although uncommon, opacification of hydrophilic IOLs is a clinically meaningful complication of endothelial keratoplasty, well documented after both DSAEK and DMEK.6,9, 10, 11 This concern is particularly relevant for the Carlevale IOL, whose hydrophilic acrylic composition predisposes to gas-related calcification, while fibrotic scleral fixation makes exchange technically complex once stable.7,8

Primary IOL exchange was considered but not chosen. Explantation of a fibrotically anchored Carlevale requires reopening of the scleral tunnels and is often accompanied by posterior vitrectomy, considerably increasing surgical morbidity in an already traumatized, vitrectomized eye.

To avoid this, strategies aimed at limiting gas exposure have been proposed, such as reducing the tamponade bubble to the pupillar margin or using air instead of SF6.12,13 However, gas-minimizing approaches have consistently been associated with higher rates of graft detachment and rebubbling compared with fuller, longer-acting tamponades.14, 15, 16 Thus, reducing the tamponade to protect the IOL may compromise graft adhesion. Importantly, no barrier technique has been previously described to prevent hydrophilic IOL opacification during DMEK.

While prior reports of temporary ICL use in aphakic or aniridic eyes focused on facilitating graft handling,17,18 in our case, the ICL was repurposed primarily as a barrier to shield the Carlevale IOL from gas exposure. The inverted orientation was essential, as placing the convex surface posteriorly maximized clearance from the corneal endothelium, whereas a standard orientation would have positioned the optic closer to the cornea and increased the risk of contact once the gas had dissipated. Regarding the retention period, the ICL was maintained for two weeks to cover the interval of greatest risk for rebubbling, even though 20 % SF6 typically persists for only one week.

This approach, however, is not exempt from limitations. The main drawbacks are the added cost of the ICL and the need for a second procedure for explantation. Endothelial safety remains a theoretical concern; although intraoperative AS-OCT confirmed adequate vault and uneventful explantation under viscoelastic protection, long-term data are required to establish safety. Reliable endothelial cell count data were not available; however, pachymetry and AS-OCT served as surrogate indicators of corneal stability. Moreover, the follow-up period in this case was limited to 6 months, sufficient to demonstrate short-term stability but inadequate to assess long-term outcomes. Confirmation in larger studies is necessary to validate its reproducibility, safety, and long-term efficacy.

Although the risk of opacification could simply be accepted,19 temporary inverted ICL implantation may represent a practical barrier-based strategy to preserve both graft adhesion and IOL clarity, which is particularly relevant in eyes where IOL exchange carries a higher risk. Importantly, recent clinical evidence has confirmed that Carlevale IOL opacification after gas tamponade is a real and vision-threatening complication,20 further reinforcing the rationale for preventive strategies such as the one described here.

Conclusion

Temporary implantation of an inverted ICL during DMEK may represent a promising strategy to prevent the gas-induced opacification of hydrophilic IOLs in complex eyes. In our case, this approach preserved IOL clarity without compromising graft adhesion. Further studies with larger cohorts are required to confirm its safety and efficacy.
