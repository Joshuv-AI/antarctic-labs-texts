Correlation between clinical features of acute episode of major depressive disorder and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (HPA) function: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Liangsheng Liu, Mingwan Zhou, Xing Yang, Duoduo Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesFocusing on the acute phase of major depressive disorder(MDD), this study aimed to systematically explore the specific correlations between core functional markers of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and the seven factor dimensions of the 24-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-24), moving beyond the conventional coarse-grained analytical framework that only associates HPA axis activity with the overall diagnostic status of depression to provide refined clinical evidence for elucidating the pathophysiological role of the HPA axis at the symptomatic dimensional level.MethodsNinety-nine outpatients with acute episodes of MDD and Eighty-seven healthy controls were selected. Serum samples were collected once in the morning from both groups to measure the serum cortisol levels. Multiple dimensions of clinical characteristics of the depressive group was assessed using the HAMD-24.ResultsSerum cortisol levels during acute depression episodes were significantly higher than those in the healthy control group. In the MDD group, serum cortisol levels were significantly positively correlated with the anxiety, cognitive impairment, diurnal variation and retardation factors and the total HAMD-24 score (ρ > 0, P < 0.05). It tended to be positively not significantly correlated with the despair factor (ρ > 0, P > 0.05), and tended to be negatively but not significantly correlated with the weight and sleep disturbance factors (ρ < 0, P > 0.05).ConclusionThese findings refine HPA axis pathophysiology understanding in acute MDD, emphasizing symptom-specific associations over generalized depressive status links. Aberrant HPA axis activity emerges as a potential biological marker for acute MDD and a promising novel therapeutic target for antidepressant development.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-07926-0.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD), also known as unipolar depression (UD) or major depression, is a severe illness characterized by persistent sadness, hopelessness, and loss of interest in activities [1]. Its global prevalence ranges from 3% to 10% [2] with a lifetime prevalence of approximately 20% [3]. MDD is characterized by a high relapse rate, significantly impacting mental health and severely impairing patient functioning [4, 5]. Patients with MDD have twice the mortality risk compared to the general population and a reduced life expectancy of 7–14 years [6, 7], imposing a heavy burden on society [8]. According to the World Health Organization, by 2030, MDD will be the leading cause of the global disease burden [9].

Among the various pathophysiological mechanisms, the hypothalamic-pituitary.-adrenal (HPA) axis plays a crucial role [10]. The hypothalamus secretes corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), which acts via the hypothalamic-pituitary pathway to stimulate the pituitary gland to release adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH). ACTH enters the bloodstream and stimulates the adrenal glands to release cortisol, which is the primary effector hormone of the HPA axis. Under normal conditions, cortisol regulates HPA function through a feedback mechanism depending on the body’s stress status, forming a closed-loop system [11–14].

Studies have shown that HPA axis dysfunction occurs in MDD, most notably with elevated serum cortisol levels compared to healthy individuals [15]. Dysregulation includes abnormal cortisol circadian rhythms and failure to suppress cortisol release in dexamethasone suppression tests (DST) [16]. At a deeper level, abnormal methylation of HPA axis-related genes, such as NR3C1, CRH, CRHR1, and CRHR2 CpG sites, is significantly associated with MDD risk, indicating that epigenomic variations can prospectively predict MDD onset independently of nearby genetic variants [17–21]. These findings suggest that changes in the methylome of the HPA axis can predict MDD development. Both blood and genetic abnormalities demonstrate that HPA dysfunction correlates with MDD severity. Moreover, HPA function tends to normalize with clinical improvements [22].

MDD symptoms are primarily assessed clinically using rating scales, including clinician-administered scales such as the HAMD-17, HAMD-21, HAMD-24, and Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS), as well as self-report scales such as the Zung Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) and Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) [23]. These scales have long-standing clinical use and high reliability and validity. The HAMD-24 can be categorized into seven factor structures: anxiety, weight, cognitive impairment, circadian rhythm, retardation, sleep disturbance, and despair, providing a concise and clear reflection of the patients’ clinical features [24]. Factor analysis not only reflects the psychopathological characteristics of patients with MDD but also the clinical outcomes of targeted symptom groups after treatment.

Although the correlation between MDD and the HPA axis has been recognized for some time, research continues actively because the exact pathological mechanism of MDD remains unclear, and no definitive biological markers or breakthrough treatments have been identified [22]. Further study of the HPA-MDD relationship may deepen our understanding of the HPA’s role in MDD and potentially reveal new mechanisms for antidepressant drug development [25].

This study focused on exploring specific correlations between core functional markers of the HPA axis and the seven factor dimensions of the HAMD-24 during acute episodes of MDD, compared with previous studies that focused on the relationship between the overall symptoms of MDD and the HPA axis, the content of this study is more detailed and the target symptoms are clearer.This can more precisely analyze the link between MDD symptoms and HPA function, providing accurate assessment and targeted treatment to predict which symptoms require acute intervention, reduce suffering, lower risks of self-harm and suicide, shorten disease course, and promote social function recovery—urgent clinical needs with high feasibility and significance.

Subjects

The MDD group consisted of outpatients who met the DSM-V diagnostic criteria for depression. The control group comprised healthy individuals who underwent physical examinations at our hospital. The study period is from July 1, 2023, to June 30, 2025.

Methods

For both groups, 5 ml of fasting venous blood was drawn at 8 a.m. the next morning to measure the serum cortisol concentration. Cortisol detection was performed using the Roche cobas e402/e801 fully automatic electrochemiluminescence immunoassay system with original matched reagents, based on the competitive immunoassay principle, with a total assay duration of 18 min. Immune complexes were formed through two incubation steps, and results were derived by measuring chemiluminescence intensity combined with a 2-point calibration curve. The assay had a measurement range of 1.5–1750 nmol/L, a limit of detection of 1.5 nmol/L, and a precision with coefficient of variation (CV%) ≤ 7.3%.Reference interval: 166–507 nmol/L from 6:00 to 10:00 AM.

All patients with MDD were assessed using the HAMD-24, recording the total and factor scores.All raters conducting the HAMD-24 assessments are full-time psychological evaluators from the Psychological Assessment Center of our hospital. They have received rigorous training, and the inter-rater reliability of the assessment scale meets the required standards.

Statistical analysis

Data were processed using the IBM SPSS Statistics 23 software. The normality of continuous variables in the MDD group was tested. Variables meeting normality were analyzed using Pearson’s correlation, and those with skewed distributions were analyzed using Spearman’s correlation. To better visualize correlations, cortisol levels, and HAMD-24 total and factor scores were standardized using z-scores for linear and nonlinear correlation analyses.

Normality test

With a sample size of N > 50, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests were used at α = 0.05. p ≥ 0.05 indicates normal distribution; p < 0.05 indicates significant deviation. The results showed K-S p-values < 0.05, indicating a skewed distribution. The S-W test showed cortisol p = 0.098 (marginally acceptable), others p < 0.05, confirming overall skewness.

Z-score standardization of variable

As cortisol levels and HAMD-24 7-factor scores differ substantially in magnitude, Z-score standardization facilitates direct comparison and joint modeling of these variables. All variables (Zscore(cortisol), Zscore(Anxiety), Zscore(Weight), Zscore(Cognitive), Zscore(Diurnal), Zscore(Retardation), Zscore(Sleep), Zscore(Despair), Zscore(HAMD)) were standardized via Z-score transformation: Z = (X − M)/SD, with X = raw variable value, M = sample mean, and SD = sample standard deviation. Post-standardization, each variable had a mean of 0 and SD of 1.

Correlation analysis

Based on the normality results, all continuous variables, except cortisol, were non-normally distributed; thus, the nonparametric Spearman correlation was used. Spearman coefficient ρ with two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered significant. FDR methods were used in multiple comparison between HAMD-24 factors(https://www.sdmproject.com/utilities/?show=FDR).

Linear and nonlinear correlation analysis

Linear analysis was performed using SPSS, and residuals were examined to assess linearity. If the linear R² was high and close to the nonlinear models, linear was accepted. If the linear R² was the lowest and the quadratic/cubic R² was significantly higher, nonlinearity was chosen. For nonlinear fits, curve estimation was used to select the best model based on the maximum R² and significant F-test. In this study, the cubic R² was highest with ΔR²≥0.05 and p < 0.05; therefore, cubic models were selected (Supplementary Table 1).

Serum cortisol and anxiety factor

Significant positive correlation (ρ = 0.337, P < 0.05). The relationship showed a three-phase “U-shaped + rising” curve rather than simple linear or quadratic, suggesting mild anxiety corresponds to lower cortisol (low stress), moderate to high anxiety may trigger significant HPA activation and cortisol rise, and very high anxiety may show a “plateau effect” indicating axis desensitization (Fig. A).

Serum cortisol and weight factor

Negative but nonsignificant correlation (ρ=–0.028, P > 0.05), indicating that cortisol is not a major driver of weight changes (Fig. B).

Serum cortisol and cognitive impairment factor

Significant positive correlation (ρ = 0.233, P < 0.05) with an inverted “N”-shaped cubic curve, indicating a non-monotonic three-phase relationship. Mild cognitive impairment may be associated with low cortisol depletion, and near-normal cognition shows cortisol rebound, suggesting reactivation mechanisms (Fig. C).

Serum cortisol and diurnal variation factor

Significant positive correlation (ρ = 0.347, P < 0.05) characterized by inverted “S”-shaped cubic curve, indicating that the transition from moderate diurnal variation disturbance to normal rhythm is a key phase of rapid cortisol decline. (Fig. D).

Serum cortisol and retardation factor

An inverted “S”-shaped cubic curve suggested a significant positive correlation (ρ = 0.440, P < 0.05), showing a non-monotonic three-phase relationship, implying mild to moderate retardation may be a sensitive window for rapid cortisol decline (Fig. E).

Serum cortisol and sleep disturbance factor

Negative but nonsignificant correlation (ρ=–0.056, P > 0.05) with an “N”-shaped cubic curve. Mild sleep problems may accompany low cortisol (low HPA activation), whereas moderate to severe sleep disturbances show cortisol rebound, suggesting secondary activation (Fig. F).

Serum cortisol and despair factor

Positive but nonsignificant correlation (ρ = 0.126, P > 0.05) with an inverted “S”-shaped cubic curve, suggesting that mild despair may activate the HPA axis, which then gradually fatigues; Severe despair shows cortisol decline, indicating HPA exhaustion(Fig. G).

Serum cortisol and total HAMD score

An “S”-shaped curve indicates significant positive correlation (ρ = 0.447, P < 0.05),suggesting mild depression corresponds to low cortisol (low HPA activation), while moderate to severe depression shows high cortisol secretion (Fig. H).

Correlations among HAMD total and factor scores

Anxiety, weight, diurnal variation, despair, and total HAMD scores showed significant positive correlations (ρ positive, P < 0.05). Cognitive impairment was significantly correlated with the total HAMD score (ρ positive, P < 0.05). Diurnal variation significantly correlated with sleep disturbance, despair, and total HAMD scores (ρ positive, P < 0.05). Retardation and sleep disturbance were significantly correlated with despair and total HAMD scores (ρ positive, P < 0.05) (Table 3). After FDR correction, the significant correlations remained statistically significant (Supplementary Table 2) (All scatter plots are supplemented as Fig.I-VIII).

Discussion

The exact pathophysiology of MDD remains elusive. Accumulating evidence suggests that HPA axis abnormalities may play a pivotal role in its pathogenesis [26]. Hypercortisolemia is associated with neurotransmitter system dysregulation and dysfunction of neural circuits involved in mood regulation, thereby correlating with depressive symptomatology [27]. Under physiological stress, serum cortisol levels can surge more than tenfold [28]. MDD itself represents a state of chronic stress, with affected individuals exhibiting higher stress burden and cortisol concentrations compared to healthy controls. The positive correlation between stress severity and depressive symptomology in MDD further supports the presence of HPA axis dysfunction [29]. Repeated failure of cortisol suppression mechanisms ultimately leads to sustained hypercortisolemia in patients [30–32].

However, conflicting findings have been reported, with some studies failing to demonstrate a significant association between depressive symptoms and cortisol levels [33–34]. Both investigations highlight that the relationship between cortisol and depressive symptomatology is contingent upon study population characteristics and the specific dimensions of HPA axis assessment employed. Analyses limited to narrow cohorts or relying on unidimensional cortisol measurements are unable to capture the nuanced interplay between HPA axis activity and depressive symptoms, a key contributor to the well-documented heterogeneity in this research domain. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Sahu et al. confirmed significantly elevated serum cortisol levels in depressed patients relative to controls, but noted substantial heterogeneity (I²=85%) across studies [35]. This high heterogeneity is likely attributable to factors including ethnic and geographic diversity of study populations, mixed enrollment of patients with acute and remitted MDD, and inadequate control of confounding variables such as somatic comorbidities and psychotropic medication use—all of which may interfere with accurate cortisol quantification.

Other studies have reported HPA axis dysfunction, including hypercortisolemia or circadian rhythm disturbances, in 40–60% of adult MDD patients [36–38]. These discrepancies are not inherently contradictory but stem from several key methodological differences. First, distinct indicators were used to assess HPA axis function: prior studies focused on glucocorticoid receptors, salivary cortisol, and the cortisol awakening response, whereas the present study quantified serum cortisol concentrations. Second, heterogeneous inclusion criteria contributed to divergent results—earlier research enrolled patients across various depressive subtypes and disease severities without rigorous confounding variable control, while our study strictly included participants with acute severe unipolar depression and excluded all identified confounders. Third, variable diagnostic criteria for HPA axis dysfunction were employed; the present study adopted a dual criterion combining reference range thresholds with statistical significance to enhance detection accuracy. Finally, this study restricted enrollment to DSM-5-diagnosed patients with severe unipolar depression, and these population-specific characteristics further account for the inconsistent findings relative to prior research.

Consistent with relevant literature, the present study found elevated serum cortisol levels in MDD patients compared to controls. Regarding circadian rhythm disturbances, mild HPA activation was observed even in the context of severe rhythm disruption, with no significant correlation between activation degree and disturbance severity. This finding may reflect that circadian rhythm abnormalities in MDD are primarily mediated by the suprachiasmatic nucleus and melatonin system, rather than direct HPA axis dysregulation [39]. Hypercortisolemia is a well-recognized feature of melancholic depression [40], which aligns with the present study’s observation of a significant correlation between elevated cortisol levels and HAMD-24 retardation factor scores—where depressive mood constitutes a core indicator [41]. Menke et al. identified HPA axis dysfunction in MDD subtypes characterized by prominent anxiety symptoms, with high serum cortisol as a key distinguishing feature; these subtypes are typically associated with more severe symptomatology and poorer treatment outcomes [42]. Similarly, the present study found that anxiety symptoms in MDD were strongly linked to HPA axis hyperactivation. The HAMD-24 anxiety factor encompasses psychic anxiety, somatic anxiety, gastrointestinal symptoms, hypochondriasis, and impaired insight. Both psychic and somatic anxiety are associated with greater subjective distress than depressive mood alone, with somatic anxiety demonstrating robust predictive value for suicide risk [43, 44]. Psychotic features such as hypochondriasis and impaired insight further complicate treatment trajectories.

Excessive HPA axis activation and sustained cortisol elevation are associated with hippocampal metabolic dysfunction and volumetric reduction [45]. The hippocampus plays a critical role in memory processing and decision-making within the limbic system [46, 47], and its dysfunction and atrophy correlate with cognitive impairment- a common comorbidity in MDD [48]. Consistent with previous research, the present study found a positive but nonlinear correlation between serum cortisol levels and HAMD-24 cognitive impairment factor scores.

Symptoms including emotional and physical fatigue, negative work attitudes, and hopelessness overlap with the HAMD-24 despair factor, which encompasses feelings of incompetence, despair, and low self-esteem [49, 50]. These symptoms have been associated with blunted HPA axis activation and nonsignificant cortisol elevation [51], aligning with the present study’s finding of a positive but nonsignificant correlation between despair factor scores and cortisol levels. This pattern may reflect the “activation-fatigue-exhaustion” continuum of HPA axis function in MDD: early stages are characterized by predominance of anxiety, depressed mood, stress reactivity, and suicidal ideation, while later phases are marked by helplessness, despair, and motivational anhedonia. Clinically, this suggests that prioritizing anxiety and depressive symptom reduction to prevent excessive HPA activation and adverse outcomes, followed by targeted interventions for fatigue and helplessness, may help mitigate disease progression risks. Lindqvist et al. demonstrated a positive correlation between suicidal ideation and serum cortisol levels, noting that cortisol reduction may attenuate suicidal intent [52].

The present study also found nonsignificant negative correlations between cortisol levels and HAMD-24 weight and sleep disturbance factor scores. However, MDD and weight gain (obesity) share common HPA axis-related pathological pathways [53]. The enzymes 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase types 1 and 2 regulate active cortisol conversion, with dysregulation reported in MDD [54] and obesity [55], respectively. This discrepancy may be attributed to the cross-sectional design of the present study, which focused on acute MDD episodes, whereas weight gain is a gradual process that typically develops over prolonged illness duration. Other studies have documented significant associations between sleep disturbance—including disrupted sleep rhythms, insomnia, and hypersomnia—and HPA axis activation [56]. This inconsistency likely reflects the complex multifactorial etiology of sleep disturbances in MDD, involving interactions between multiple pathophysiological pathways such as the microbiota-gut-brain axis [57].

The total HAMD-24 score serves as a validated measure of MDD severity. Zhang et al. reported a significant positive correlation between HAMD total scores and cortisol levels [58], a finding replicated in the present study which demonstrated a near-linear positive correlation. This further confirms that HPA axis hyperactivation and sustained maladaptive cortisol release are among the most consistent biological findings in MDD [59].

As shown in Table 3, HAMD-24 factor scores collectively contribute to the total score, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptomatology. Correlations among these factors reflect the symptom diversity and heterogeneity inherent to MDD. Anxiety and despair factors were positively correlated, suggesting that acute-phase anxiety symptoms may precipitate despair-related negative cognitions or behaviors. Except for the weight factor, despair correlated positively with all other HAMD-24 factors, indicating that negative thoughts and behaviors may persist until full symptomatic remission and require targeted clinical attention. Sleep disturbance and diurnal variation factors were positively correlated, consistent with their frequent clinical co-occurrence in MDD.

Conclusion

In summary, the diagnostic criteria for MDD have achieved fundamental unification between the DSM-V and the eleventh version of the international Classification of.

Diseases (ICD-11) systems, with global applicability [60].This study demonstrates that during acute episodes of MDD, the HPA axis is overactivated, leading to an abnormal elevation of serum cortisol levels. This preliminarily confirms that MDD is a complex psychiatric disorder with a highly heterogeneous pathophysiology [61, 62]. Through an exploration of correlations between serum cortisol and the multiple dimensions of HAMD-24, we found that not all symptoms correlated positively with serum cortisol, but core symptom clusters showed significant associations with HPA hyperactivation. This suggests that HPA axis dysfunction is one of the pathophysiological mechanisms of MDD and can serve as a biological marker for acute episodes.

Against the backdrop of unmet needs for breakthrough treatments in major depressive disorder (MDD), these findings offer novel perspectives for antidepressant drug development and clinical treatment selection, and support the HPA axis as a potential novel therapeutic target for MDD.

Limitations

While the present study identified several clinically distinctive findings by analysing correlations between serum cortisol levels—the core effector of the HPA —and multidimensional scores of the HAMD-24 in patients with MDD, it is subject to several methodological limitations that require further refinement. These include a cross-sectional study design, a relatively modest sample size, sole reliance on a single assessment scale and a single time point for blood sampling, incomplete collection of demographic characteristics, and potential biases in the statistical methods applied. Collectively, these limitations may restrict the generalizability of the study’s findings and conclusions to clinical practice. In subsequent investigations, we will address these shortcomings through targeted optimizations, including the adoption of multi-modal assessment scales(includ observer-rated and self-reported scales), dynamic blood sampling combined with longitudinal follow-up evaluations, comprehensive collection of demographic variables that may influence study outcomes, and the refinement of statistical analytical approaches.
