Epigenetic age acceleration is associated with occupational exposures, sex, and survival in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis
Authors: Yue Zhao, Xiayan Li, Kai Wang, Gayatri Iyer, Stacey A Sakowski, Lili Zhao, Samuel Teener, Kelly M Bakulski, John F Dou, Bryan J Traynor, Alla Karnovsky, Stuart A Batterman, Eva L Feldman, Maureen A Sartor, Stephen A Goutman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAmyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is linked to ageing and genetic and environmental risk factors, yet underlying mechanisms are incompletely understood. We aimed to evaluate epigenetic age acceleration (EAA), i.e., DNA methylation (DNAm) age acceleration, and its association with ALS case status and survival.MethodsIn this study, we included 428 ALS and 288 control samples collected between 2011 and 2021. We calculated EAA using the GrimAge residual method from ALS and control blood samples and grouped participants with ALS into three ageing groups (fast, normal, slow). We associated EAA with ALS case status and survival, stratified by sex, and correlated it with environmental and biological factors through occupational exposure assessments, immune cell proportions, and transcriptome changes.FindingsParticipants with ALS had higher average EAA by 1.80 ± 0.30 years (p < 0.0001) versus controls. Participants with ALS in the fast ageing group had a hazard ratio of 1.52 (95% confidence interval 1.16–2.00, p = 0.0028) referenced to the normal ageing group. In males, this hazard ratio was 1.55 (95% confidence interval 1.11–2.17, p = 0.010), and EAA was positively correlated with high-risk occupational exposures including particulate matter (adj.p < 0.0001) and metals (adj.p = 0.0087). Also, in male participants with ALS, EAA was positively correlated with neutrophil proportions and was negatively correlated with CD4+ T cell proportions. Pathways dysregulated in participants with ALS with fast ageing included spliceosome, nucleocytoplasmic transport, axon guidance, and interferons.InterpretationEAA was associated with ALS case status and, at least in males, with shorter survival after diagnosis. The effect of EAA on ALS was partially explained by occupational exposures and immune cell proportions in a sex-dependent manner. These findings highlight the complex interactions of ageing and exposures in ALS.FundingNIH, CDC/National ALS Registry, ALS Association, Dr. Randall Whitcomb Fund for ALS Genetics, Peter Clark Fund for ALS Research, Sinai Medical Staff Foundation, Scott L. Research in context

Evidence before this study

Altered DNA methylation (DNAm) age, DNAm levels, and differentially methylated positions (DMPs) across various tissues, such as blood and brain, have been described in ALS in the past decade. To date, some previously published studies demonstrate that increased DNAm age (i.e., epigenetic age acceleration; EAA) associates with ALS status or survival from birth. The epigenome serves as a surrogate of environmental influences, and, in ALS, may help explain the association of the exposome and environmental exposures with ALS risk and phenotype. However, studies have not examined these associations in ALS. Therefore, the association of DNAm age with ALS characteristics linked to environmental exposures remains incompletely understood.

Added value of this study

Our data link EAA to ALS survival from time of diagnosis and occupational environmental exposures. First, we show that both male and female participants with ALS have accelerated ageing versus controls. Second, we report faster EAA is associated with poorer ALS survival, especially in males, indicating a sex-specific link between EAA with ALS survival from diagnosis. Third, we found that EAA was correlated with higher occupational exposures. Importantly, associations of EAA with survival, exposures, and immune metrics (i.e., elevated neutrophil proportions and reduced CD4+ T cell proportions) were highly sex-dependent, and more pronounced in male participants. Finally, we integrated EAA with transcriptomics, characterizing the molecular contributions of DNAm ageing to ALS case status and survival, which revealed dysregulated pathways in spliceosome, nucleocytoplasmic transport, axon guidance, interferons, and neutrophil extracellular trap observable in blood.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our current study demonstrates that EAA is correlated with ALS case status and, in males, with survival. DNAm age is also associated with environmental exposures and immune phenotypes, highlighting the complex interactions of ageing and exposures in ALS. These findings could potentially have clinical utility for prognostication using EAA as a surrogate of disease progression, although validation studies are warranted. Furthermore, if EAA is shown to increase in individuals prior to the onset of ALS, it could facilitate risk stratification in a subpopulation at elevated genetic risk.

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a fatal neurodegenerative disease causing progressive motor impairment.1 The complex disease mechanisms,2,3 including dysregulated RNA processing (specifically splicing) and nucleocytoplasmic transport, underly heterogeneous clinical presentations. ALS results from a combination of genetic4 and environmental1,5 factors, and only a minority of individuals harbour a monogenic mutation.6,7 Exposures, like pesticides, metals, and persistent organic pollutants, associate with ALS onset8, 9, 10 and progression.10, 11, 12 Epigenetics represents a potential convergence between genetic predisposition and environmental exposures.13,14 The epigenome coordinates neural development, plasticity, and ageing by responding to stimuli beyond the inherited genetic code.15 DNA methylation (DNAm) is an epigenetic modification that adds a methyl group to the DNA cytosine ring at the C5 position, often affecting gene transcription.16 Small7,17 and large18 studies have demonstrated alterations in the ALS methylome.

Recently, epigenetic age calculation methods using high-throughput DNAm data from blood have improved.19 While first-generation epigenetic age estimators20,21 correlate with chronological age, second-generation epigenetic age estimators22, 23, 24 predict biological age and better reflect organ and tissue function. For instance, second-generation epigenetic age estimators capture immunosenescence, i.e., changes in blood immune cell composition and function with increasing age, that is associated with a decrease in CD8+ and CD4+ T cells and B cells and an increase in neutrophils.22,23 Notably, individuals with higher-than-expected epigenetic age, or epigenetic age acceleration (EAA), tend to have shorter lifespans versus those with normal epigenetic age. Moreover, EAA is linked to socio-environmental factors, including smoking, BMI, and socio-economic status, and to age-related diseases, including cancer, depression, coronary heart disease,24 Parkinson's disease,25 and dementia,24 although mechanisms remain elusive.26

Here, we show that EAA, calculated from blood DNAm profiles, is higher in ALS versus control participants, especially in males. We further show that EAA is associated with sex-specific ALS survival, self-reported occupational risk and exposures, and ALS-related cellular pathway alterations.

Methods

Study design and participants

The data presented here are derived from: 1) several cross-sectional studies to test the associations between EAA and disease status, transcriptome changes, and immune cell proportions; 2) a case–control study of participants with ALS compared to controls; and 3) a cohort of the same participants with ALS followed longitudinally until death or censoring (Fig. 1). Our study includes patients seen at the University of Michigan (UM) Pranger ALS clinic from 2011 to 2021 with available DNA and RNA biospecimens; all patients meeting Gold Coast ALS diagnosis were invited to participate. Control participants from Michigan without a personal or first- or second-degree ALS or neurodegenerative disease family history (e.g., ALS, Alzheimer's disease and Alzheimer's related dementias, Parkinson's and related diseases, Huntington's disease) were identified via a UM research interest database, random address mailing, and Facebook advertisements.9, 10, 11 Specifically, controls were enrolled using three strategies: 1) The Michigan Institute for Clinical & Health Research (MICHR) hosts a listing of individuals interested in volunteering for research studies. Via this listing, individuals can be notified of our study and indicate if they would like a study team member to contact them for further details; 2) The Survey Research Organization in the UM Institute for Social Research purchased addresses from a marketing database to target households where an individual was likely to have an age >45 years and lived within 1 h driving distance to our blood collection sites (Michigan cities of Ann Arbor, Northville, Brighton, Midland, Grand Rapids). These letters were initially mailed in early 2020. Recruitment of participants via these letters was impacted by two events outside our control. First, the COVID-19 pandemic placed restrictions on human subject research. Second, due to collapse of a dam, 2500 homes in Midland, Michigan were destroyed along with the evacuation of 10,000 people. As a result of these events, 3) we turned to Facebook advertisements coordinated by MICHR, beginning in September 2020. MICHR was able to specify age, sex, and geographic categories for advertisement targets. Participants were greater than 18 years old. Consented participants provided blood samples and completed an exposure assessment survey.9 Available demographics and ALS features included self-reported sex, disease onset segment, onset age, age at diagnosis, and diagnostic El Escorial Criteria and revised ALS functional rating scale (ALSFRS-R) score. The sample size of 600 participants (400 ALS and 200 control) was estimated based on the primary analysis of comparing EAA between ALS and controls. With this sample size, we can detect an effect size (i.e., the mean difference divided by a pooled standard deviation) as small as 0.25 with 80% power and 5% type I error using a two-sided two-sample t test.

DNA extraction and EPIC BeadChip protocol

DNA was extracted from participant blood samples using QIAamp DNA Blood Maxi Kit (cat# 51194; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Following isolation, DNA was sent to the UM Biomedical Research Epigenomics Core for profiling using the Infinium Human MethylationEPIC BeadChip. Genomic DNA was quantitated using the Qubit BR dsDNA kit (cat# Q32853; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and assessed on the TapeStation 2200 Genomic DNA screenTape. For each blood sample, ∼250 ng genomic DNA was bisulfite converted using the EZ DNA Methylation Kit (cat# D5002; Zymo Research, Irvine, CA). Cleaned samples were then hybridized to the genome wide Infinium Human MethylationEPIC BeadChip array covering ∼850,000 CpGs, followed by washing and scanning by the UM Advanced Genomics Core.

DNA methylation data processing and quality control

Raw red/green IDAT files were read into R using the minfi Bioconductor package (v1.32.0).27 Initial quality control based on detection p-values and signal intensity was performed using the ENmix Bioconductor package (v1.22.4) (Supplementary Figure S1a).28 Specifically, 16,782 out of 865,859 probes were removed due to more than 5% of samples having missing values. An additional 59,390 probes were removed either for being within 2bp of a SNP, a known cross-hybridizing probe, or both. After filtering, probe intensities were background- and dye-corrected using the NOOB background correction,29 followed by stratified quantile normalization. β-values were calculated as the ratio of the methylated probe intensity to the sum of methylated and unmethylated probe intensities and a constant number of 100.

The participant sex for each DNAm sample was determined by Principal Component Analysis (PCA) using the β-values of the top 10,000 most variable CpG probes, and the resulting sex was compared to the recorded sex (Supplementary Figure S1b). Four samples did not cluster with either male or female. Three samples were recorded as female but clustered with male, and three samples were recorded as male but clustered with female. These 10 sex-mismatched samples (4 ALS, 6 control) were removed from downstream analysis (Supplementary Figure S1c). Additionally, two ALS samples were removed due to more than 5% of the probes having missing values. This led to a final study population comprising 716 samples, with 428 from the ALS group and 288 from the control group (Supplementary Figure S1d). Out of these, 669 samples (400 ALS and 269 controls) were also successfully profiled using bulk RNA-seq.

Epigenetic age acceleration

We reasoned that the GrimAge method23 is most appropriate for our study, because we were studying associations with a disease rather than predicting chronological age. Additionally, the older age of our cohort, which differs significantly from the relatively younger age range (26–38 yrs) used for training DunedinPoAm,30 aligns more closely with the age range (28–62 yrs) used for training GrimAge. Therefore, epigenetic age was calculated via the GrimAge method for our main analysis; however, the main findings were verified by DunedinPoAm. Of note, a standalone GrimAge python program was used with participants' chronological age (age at blood draw), sex, and β-values of 30,084 probes as input. Of the 30,084 probes, 4020 probes had missing β-values among all the participants, and these missing β-values were replaced by the values in the datMiniAnnotation3_gold file, which was provided by the GrimAge program. EAA, representing the residual from a linear regression model, was calculated as the difference between the measured and expected epigenetic age based on control samples. Using control samples for the linear model ensured the distribution was centered to a control population. A positive EAA indicated the person's epigenetic age was higher than the expected epigenetic age of a healthy person with the same chronological age. The use of the residual method contrasts with another EAA calculation method that uses simple subtraction of chronological age from DNAm age, which results in EAA values dependent on chronological age (Supplementary Figure S2).

Cell type deconvolution

DNAm data was deconvoluted using the FlowSorted.Blood.EPIC package (v 1.4.1) into constituent cell types (CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, Natural Killer cells, B cells, Monocytes, and Neutrophils) proportions using the Identifying Optimal Libraries (IDOL) algorithm.31

Occupational exposures

Standard occupational classification (SOC) codes and occupational exposure scores, derived from a subset of participants using published methods,9 were used to evaluate the association of occupational exposures and EAA. Briefly, participants completed an assessment that queried self-reported exposures in the occupational setting. Occupations in production, installation, maintenance, repair, construction, extraction, farming, fishing, forestry, and military were denoted as high-risk; other occupations were denoted as low-risk. Exposure scores to exposure types (i.e., metals, pesticides) were derived from the questions that captured the likelihood of being exposed to each exposure type. These exposure scores were quantified as per calculations by an exposure scientist (SAB) and presented in Supplementary Table S1 in Goutman et al.9

Genome-wide genotyping and C9orf72 expansion status

Genome-wide genotypes at 1,748,250 positions were measured for 465 samples in our cohort as previously published.4 DNA samples were also analyzed for the presence of the C9orf72 repeat expansion per published protocols.32 Alleles with 30 or more repeats were defined as positive. Genetic principal components (PCs) were calculated as previously described,4 and the top PCs were used as covariates in the differential expression analysis. Briefly, genetic PCs were computed after merging with the 1000 Genomes reference panel.33 The top 4 PCs were selected for inclusion based on the elbow plot (Supplementary Figure S3). For instances where genetic PC data were unavailable, imputation was performed by the k-Nearest Neighbours algorithm with k set to 6.

RNA extraction and sequencing

The same batch of blood samples was used for DNA methylation EPIC bead chip and RNA sequencing simultaneously. Blood samples for RNA were collected in PAXgene tubes (cat# 762165; Qiagen) following manufacturer specifications and remained frozen until extraction. RNA was extracted from these PAXgene tubes using the PAXgene Blood miRNA Kit (cat# 763134; Qiagen). RNA was assessed using the TapeStation (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) and Qubit RNA broad-range assay (cat# Q10211; Thermo Fisher Scientific).

rRNA-depletion libraries

Samples with RNA integrity number (RIN) < 5.5 were prepared by the NEBNext Globin & rRNA Depletion Kit (Human/Mouse/Rat) (cat# E7750X; New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA), xGen Broad-range RNA Library Prep (cat# 10010145; Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA), and xGen Normalase UDI Primers (cat# 10009795, 10009800, 10009811, 10009812; Integrated DNA Technologies). An input of 90 ng of total RNA was ribosomal depleted using the rRNA Depletion module. The rRNA-depleted RNA was then fragmented 5–10 min as determined by RIN of input RNA as per protocol, then copied into first strand cDNA using reverse transcriptase and random primers. The 3 prime ends of the cDNA were then adenylated and adapters were ligated. The products were purified and enriched by 16 cycles PCR to create the final (total) cDNA library.

PolyA-selection libraries

Samples with RIN ≥5.5 were prepared using the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (cat# E7490L; New England Biolabs), xGen Broad-range RNA Library Prep (cat# 10010145; Integrated DNA Technolgoies), and xGen Normalase UDI Primers (cat# 10009795, 10009800, 10009811, 10009812; Integrated DNA Technologies), where 90 ng of total RNA was converted to mRNA using polyA purification. The mRNA was fragmented and copied into first strand cDNA using reverse transcriptase and random primers. The 3 prime ends of the cDNA were then adenylated and adapters were ligated. The products were purified and enriched by 16 cycles PCR to create the final (poly-A) cDNA library.

The quality of final libraries was assessed by Qubit hsDNA (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and LabChip (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA). The samples were pooled and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq S4 for 150bp paired end reads. BCL Convert Conversion Software v3.9.3 (Illumina, San Diego, CA) was used to generate demultiplexed Fastq files. RNA-seq was performed by the UM Advanced Genomics Core.

RNA-seq data processing and quality control

Raw RNA-seq reads (fastq files) were trimmed with Cutadapt, aligned to the human reference genome build hg38 using STAR 2.5.3a, and quantified with featureCounts v2.0.3, using “-s 2” for reversely stranded reads. Samples were subjected to extensive RNA-seq quality control utilizing FastQC, RSeQC, and Fastq Screen and results were collated with MultiQC. Additional sequencing was performed as needed for mRNA samples having fewer than 10 million reads aligned to non-globin genes. The final median read depth was 87 (range 41–256) million reads per sample.

PCA and unsupervised clustering were used to assess batch effects involving RNA-seq library preparation methods and sequencing runs between samples (Supplementary Figure S4). Gene counts were normalized for library size and log2 transformed using the variance stabilizing transformation function in DESeq2. PCs were calculated based on the top 500 genes with the largest variance by the plotPCA function. Samples clustered into two groups based on library preparation (poly-adenylated selection or total rRNA depletion). Batch correction was performed by the ComBat_seq function in the sva Bioconductor package, with library preparation and batch runs as a combined new factor for batch correction, while controlling for variables of biological interest: case/control status, age, sex, and C9orf72 status. Sex was confirmed by examining the expression of the X chromosome gene XIST and 49 male specific Y chromosome genes (Supplementary Data 1),34 and comparing to their recorded sex. To remove technical variation due to polyA-selection versus rRNA-depletion cDNA library preparation, 13 globin genes (Supplementary Data 2) were filtered. Additionally, lowly expressed genes (raw read counts less than 5 in more than 50% samples) were excluded. This yielded 22,530 genes and 669 samples.

The UM Institutional Review Board (IRBMED HUM28826) approved the study. All participants provided written informed consent.

Statistics

EAA groups

Participants with ALS were categorized into three EAA groups by first quartile (−0.8 years or lower), middle half (−0.8 to 3.4 years), and last quartile (3.4 years or higher). These EAA quartiles were applied for survival analyses and differential gene expression analyses in participants with ALS. Similarly, healthy control EAA grouping used cutoffs based on healthy control quartiles: first quartile as slow (−2.2 years or lower), middle half as normal (−2.2 years to 1.6 years), and last quartile as fast ageing (1.6 years or higher). These quartiles were applied for differential gene expression analyses in controls. Differences in baseline characteristics between groups were calculated using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Pearson's Chi-squared test for categorical variables. Differences in DNAm age and EAA between groups were calculated using linear models adjusting for age.

EAA associations with ALS case status, disease progression rate, and survival

The associations of EAA and neutrophil cell proportion with case status were tested by logistic regression adjusted for chronological age and sex. The proportions of different immune cells sum up to a constant, near 1, indicating linear dependence among them. To address this, blood cell proportions that exhibited a variance-inflation factor (VIF) greater than 5 were removed. Additionally, any cell proportions that were not statistically significant (p > 0.05) were excluded from the model. Disease progression rate at the time of initial assessment of ALSFRS-R was calculated as DFS’35 and its correlation with EAA was studied by linear regression. Kaplan–Meier curves were generated for ALS survival post diagnosis. A Cox proportional hazards (CoxPH) model was adjusted for EAA (both continuous and categorical), symptom onset age, sex, diagnostic delay (time from symptom onset to diagnosis), diagnostic El Escorial criteria, ALSFRS-R, and onset segment. Model non-linearity, outliers, and PH assumptions were checked using martingale, deviance, and Schoenfeld residuals. Additionally, CoxPH models stratified by sex were implemented using the same covariates.

EAA variance partitioning

Incremental R2 estimates from linear regression were employed to assess if the introduction of additional variables contributed to an enhanced explanation of variance, compared to the base model which considered EAA as the response variable and included case–control status and age as independent variables. This method was instrumental in determining the extent to which the added variable accounted for variance in EAA, beyond what was explained by the null model. Similar to the logistic regression model discussed in the previous subsection, blood cell proportions with a VIF greater than 5 and those not statistically significant (p > 0.05) were removed from the linear model.

Differential gene expression

We tested for differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using linear models and moderated t-statistics on normalized batch-corrected gene counts. DEGs between fast versus slow ageing in participants with ALS and separately in controls were identified with the same model testing for DEGs between case versus control, except case/control group was replaced with fast/slow EAA group. DEGs that were associated with every one-unit change in EAA were identified with the model above, but using a continuous EAA variable. Genes with an FDR adjusted p-value ≤0.05 were significant. Differential expression testing was performed using linear models and moderated t-statistics in the limma R package (v3.48.3) (functions: voom, lmFit and eBayes). Batch-corrected gene counts were first normalized for library size and transformed on a log2 scale, and then normalized between samples by the trimmed mean of M-values normalization method in edgeR. The DEGs between ALS case versus control groups were identified with a model design controlling for technical variation due to library preparation and batch, and for variation due to cell type proportions, genetic heterogeneity (PC1 ∼ PC4), and C9orf72 status.

Functional analysis

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using fgsea R package (v3.18) on Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways and MSigDB Hallmark gene sets.

Over-representation analysis was performed using a one-sided Fisher's exact test from the functions enrichKEGG and enrichGO in the clusterProfiler R package (v4.8.3) on KEGG pathways and Gene Ontology (GO) terms. DEGs with unadjusted p-value <0.05 were used for fast versus slow ageing comparisons in ALS, as well as for fast versus slow ageing comparisons in controls. DEGs with Benjamini and Hochberg corrected p-value <0.05 were used for ALS case versus control comparison. This decision was made because the quality of enrichment analysis is optimized for the number of genes in this range and the test is robust to false positive individual genes. KEGG pathways, Hallmark gene sets, and GO terms with a Benjamini and Hochberg-corrected p-value <0.05 were selected for visualization and interpretation.

MR analysis

Two-sample Mendelian randomisation (MR) analysis was performed using the TwoSampleMR R package.36 Summary statistics were used from a GWAS of GrimAge EAA37 and from a GWAS of ALS.38 The top SNPs from the GrimAge EAA GWAS were obtained and then clumping to prune SNPs in linkage disequilibrium was applied. Next, those SNPs or proxy SNPs from the ALS GWAS were used for two-sample MR analysis.

Next, one-sample MR using the MendelianRandomization R package39 was performed. As a genetic instrument, a polygenic score for EAA was created. Two polygenic scores were created using different EAA GWAS p-value threshold for inclusion: 5 × 10−8 (what was considered significant in parent GWAS), or 1.0 (including all available SNPs). Polygenic scores were created using PRSice-2,40 with clumping and pruning using default parameters. First, whether polygenic score was associated with EAA was evaluated. Next, MR with inverse variance weighted method was performed in models adjusted for chronological age, sex, and three genetic PCs.

All statistical analyses were performed using R v4.2 and v4.3 (https://www.R-project.org/).

Results

We investigated ALS associated changes in blood methylome and transcriptome, with DNAm profiles from 428 ALS and 288 controls, of which 400 ALS and 269 controls also had RNA-seq profiles (Fig. 1, Table 1). The average DNAm age of participants with ALS was 1.8 years higher than controls after adjusting for chronological age (pcase_status<0.0001, page < 0.0001, [linear regression], Fig. 2a). The average EAA of participants with ALS was also 1.8 years higher than controls, and was independent of chronological age, as expected from the method used (pcase_status<0.0001, page = 0.56, [linear regression], Fig. 2b). A sensitivity analysis excluding participants with ALS and a family history of ALS did not change these associations (Table 1). Thus, EAA is associated with higher ALS odds.

Among the important clinicodemographic variables, EAA was correlated with diagnostic ALSFRS-R (r = −0.16, p = 0.0013, [Pearson's correlation]), i.e., participants with higher EAA tended to have more severe disease (Supplementary Figure S5a). EAA was statistically significantly correlated with symptom onset age only in females (Supplementary Figure S5b). EAA did not significantly correlate with diagnostic delay. EAA was higher in males than females but was not significantly associated with onset segment or El Escorial criteria (Supplementary Figure S5c & d). Thus, EAA was associated with more severe disease at diagnosis.

Fast ageing participants with ALS had a shorter post-diagnosis survival (median survival 1.81 years) versus normal ageing (2.44 years) and slow ageing (2.44 years) groups (p = 0.033, [log rank]) by Kaplan–Meier analysis (Fig. 2c). Adjusting for clinicodemographic variables (Table 2, Supplementary Table S1) confirmed that fast EAA participants with ALS had poorer survival (hazard ratio [HR] 1.52, p = 0.0028, [CoxPH]) versus the normal EAA group (Fig. 2d), which held with EAA modelled as a continuous variable (HR = 1.04 for every one year increase in EAA, p = 0.0087, [CoxPH]; Supplementary Figure S6). As a sensitivity analysis, including C9orf72 repeat expansion did not change the association between EAA and ALS survival (Supplementary Figure S7).

SOC code data were available from 465 of the ALS and control and self-reported occupational exposures were available from 504 ALS and control participants (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Figure S10).9 As noted previously,9 males were more likely to have worked in a high-risk occupation than females, and ALS males were more likely to have worked in a high-risk occupation than controls (Fig. 4b). EAA was higher in males who worked in high-versus low-risk occupations (Fig. 4c). Stratifying participants by sex and case status, EAA was associated with occupational risk, but only in male participants with ALS as too few females had high-risk occupations for a similar comparison. Correlating EAA to self-reported occupational exposure scores, which are highly correlated (Supplementary Figure S11), revealed positive correlations with exposure scores for particulate matter (PM), volatile organic compounds, pesticides, metals, and combustion and diesel exhaust, but not electromagnetic fields, biologicals, radiation, or corrosives (Fig. 4d). Neutrophils, the most abundant immune cell type in blood, play key roles in ALS. On average, participants with ALS had 6.1% higher neutrophil proportions than controls (means of 60.7% ALS versus 54.6% controls; p < 0.0001, [t-test]; Supplementary Figure S13). EAA generally correlated positively with neutrophil proportions and negatively with CD4+ T cell proportions, except in female participants with ALS (Supplementary Figure S14, Supplementary Table S3). Survival was not statistically significantly associated with neutrophil proportions using a CoxPH model with the same covariates (Supplementary Figure S15). Hence, in males, EAA is correlated with immune cell proportions that contribute to ALS case status.

Next, we determined the percent of EAA variability explained by occupational risk and immune cell proportions in males only since we had limited females in high-risk occupations. Neutrophil proportion (p < 0.0001, [linear regression]) and occupational risk group (p = 0.015, [linear regression]) were associated with EAA in males, explaining 7% and 2% of the variability, respectively, and 9% jointly, consistent with their lack of association (Supplementary Figure S16).

To investigate EAA mechanisms potentially underlying ALS risk, we identified common DEGs and dysregulated pathways in ALS versus controls (ALS case status-related) and fast versus slow EAA groups in controls (EAA-related) from peripheral blood samples (Fig. 5a–b, Supplementary Figures S17–18a, Supplementary Data 3). Common DEGs included overexpressed NRG1 and SASH1 (Supplementary Results). Common pathways included up-regulated oxidative phosphorylation (normalized enrichment score [NES] 1.99, FDR<0.0001) and MYC targets (NES = 1.93, FDR<0.0001) and down-regulated B cell receptor signalling (NES = −2.11, FDR<0.0001), Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation (NES = −2.11, FDR<0.0001), Th17 cell differentiation (NES = −2.01, FDR<0.0001), and antigen processing and presentation (NES = −1.98; FDR = 0.00026), indicating weakened immune function. Functional overrepresentation analysis of the same groups unveiled additional common up-regulated disease pathways, such as ALS, Parkinson's disease, and prion disease (Supplementary Figure S17d, Supplementary Data 4). Several specific genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation were common between the comparisons, including NDUFA4, COX6C, and COX7A2L (Supplementary Figures S19–S21). This overlap underscores the potential shared molecular mechanisms between ALS risk and general EAA.

To explore how EAA mechanisms influence ALS survival, DEGs and pathways were identified between fast versus slow ageing participants with ALS (Fig. 5c&d). In fast ageing participants with ALS, seven of 31 up-regulated genes were associated with axonal and synaptic functions, including axon development and synapse assembly. The top down-regulated pathway in fast ALS ageing was the spliceosome pathway (NES = −2.52, FDR<0.0001, Fig. 5d&e, Supplementary Figures S17d & S18b, Supplementary Data 3–4), pointing to dysfunctional cryptic splicing as a fundamental ALS mechanism related to ageing. This is further supported by overrepresentation of down-regulated genes in the GO term RNA splicing (Supplementary Figure S22, Supplementary Data 5). Other down-regulated pathways included MYC targets (NES = −2.15, FDR<0.0001) and nucleocytoplasmic transport (NES = −1.85, FDR = 0.0025, Fig. 5e). Up-regulated pathways included interferon α response (NES = 1.75, FDR = 0.0032), TNFα signalling via NFκB (NES = 1.61, FDR = 0.0015), neutrophil extracellular trap formation (NES = 1.64, FDR = 0.027, Fig. 5e), and axon guidance (NES = 1.55, FDR = 0.042, Fig. 5e). Sex-stratified analysis identified common and unique pathways altered in males and females with ALS in fast versus slow EAA groups (Supplementary Figure S23).

Lastly, to gain insight into whether EAA is causative for ALS, MR was performed but found that the currently available EAA genetic predictors are weak genetic instruments in this study population (Supplementary Results).

Discussion

There is a tremendous need to identify factors that influence ALS risk and progression, monitor at-risk individuals, and prognosticate. Given strong links between ALS, ageing, and environmental exposures, identifying epigenetic mechanisms that mediate biological ageing and exposure effects may yield important insights. Indeed, patients with ALS exhibit differentially methylated positions (DMPs) across tissues (e.g., blood, brain) versus controls.43, 44, 45, 46 Herein, we examined EAA to capture premature ageing in ALS, reflecting the cumulative effect of time, environmental exposures, and genetic factors, all relevant to disease pathology. EAA differs from global DMP analysis by focusing on CpG sites that strongly correlate with ageing, providing a targeted approach to understand ageing-related diseases. We found that EAA, measured by DNAm, was significantly associated with ALS case status and, in males, with survival. Further, the impact of EAA was sex and neutrophil dependent and linked to occupational exposures and important disease pathways, highlighting the relevance of EAA to ALS.

Our analysis indicated participants with ALS had 1.8 years higher EAA versus controls, suggesting faster epigenetic ageing is linked to ALS case status. EAA in ALS has been reported.47 A small study comprising three monozygotic twin pairs and one set of triplets discordant for ALS showed advanced EAA, based on an epigenetic clock of 353 CpG sites, in the person with ALS versus their unaffected twin or triplet.48 Higher DNAm age in affected twins was also noted in monozygotic twins harbouring SOD1 and ARHGEF28 mutations and C9orf72 repeat expansions that were discordant for ALS.49 A large study of several cohorts observed a significant association of ALS case status with EAA18 using the clock by Zhang et al.,50 but interpretation was limited due to heterogeneity in the association across cohorts.18 Conversely, no significant differences in DNAm age were seen between 61 ALS cases and 61 controls, possibly due to limited probe overlap between the 450K methylation array used and GrimAge.51 However, in sum, the body of evidence indicates accelerated biological ageing in ALS. EAA may also be a characteristic of neurodegenerative diseases more broadly, with reports in Alzheimer's disease and isolated rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder, the strongest prodromal marker for α-synucleinopathies like Parkinson's disease.52,53

Our analysis further underscored that EAA is correlated with post-diagnosis survival in male participants with ALS, an association which persisted even after adjusting for age at symptom onset. In our approach, we calculated EAA using residuals rather than subtraction and accounted for chronological age because it is critical to measuring EAA.54 Our study design additionally leveraged a control population to accurately estimate expected DNAm age. Our main outcome was survival post-diagnosis, as opposed to survival from birth, to focus on ALS progression as the relevant disease metric. While Zhang et al.55 identified an association of onset age with survival from birth, they used an ALS case-only cohort and their methods resulted in spurious findings related to chronological age.54 Nevertheless, Zhang et al.56 did show significant correlation between EAA and disease duration in a small cohort of patients with C9orf72 positive after adjusting for age, which may have held using the residual GrimAge method, as we have demonstrated herein. Hop et al.18 observed that associations of DNAm age (estimated by a first-generation DNAm age estimator, i.e., chronological age predictor50) with ALS survival vanished after accounting for chronological age, as opposed to our analysis which employed the lifespan predictor GrimAge. Overall, our study using a rigorous analytical method demonstrated that fast EAA was associated with poorer ALS survival, especially in males, substantiating the influential role of EAA on ALS progression.

The pronounced EAA effects our study found in males, especially in survival, are consistent with observed sex-specific ALS differences.41,42,57,58 In one registry, male-to-female prevalence ratio was 2.5 in the 25-34-year age group, which narrowed to only 1.2 in the 65-74-year age group,59 indicating sex differences are more pronounced earlier in life and prior to menopause in females. Sex hormones (e.g., estrogen, progesterone) represent a possible reason for ALS sex differences, and, indeed, regarding DNAm specifically, females have slower epigenetic ageing up to menopause.60 However, it is possible that the associations between EAA and ALS survival in males could be related to hormones alone, or to other unique factors.

Other possible reasons for ALS sex differences include distinct environmental and occupational exposure patterns and unique biological responses to toxin exposure. Supporting this possibility, we saw in males that EAA was associated with occupational exposures, including particulate matter, volatile organic compounds, pesticides, metals, and combustion and diesel exhaust.9 Previously, occupational exposures,8,9 pesticides,61 metals,62, 63, 64 and diesel exhaust65 were shown to be associated with increased ALS risk, especially in males, which we now posit may be linked to EAA. Similar to our results, a study of healthy adults found sex differences in age acceleration based on clinical biomarkers were associated with PM2.5.66 Moreover, one study linked DMPs in ALS to self-reported exposures and occupational histories.67 Therefore, our identified association of DNAm age to environmental exposures in ALS fills a critical knowledge gap.

Given immune cell correlations with ALS risk and survival,41,42,68,69 we also correlated EAA with immune profiles. We found EAA correlated positively with neutrophils and negatively with CD4+ T cells, except in females with ALS. This sex variation may be due to reduced statistical power or sex-specific immune responses to ageing, disease risk, or environmental stress. Our results broadly align with ageing studies that found EAA is correlated with decreased CD8+ and CD4+ T cells and B cells and increased neutrophils.22,23

Finally, our examination of whether EAA underlies ageing- and ALS disease-related pathways by investigating correlation of EAA to gene expression showed that general EAA was associated with several neurodegenerative and prion-risk pathways. ALS risk-related pathways highlighted weakened immune function and increased oxidative stress. Additionally, down-regulated WNT, estrogen, NFκB, and TNFα signalling, which were reported as four out of seven down-regulated pathways in motor neurons derived from induced pluripotent stem cells of patients with ALS,70 were correlated with both ageing and ALS risk. Collectively, deficient immune function and excessive oxidative stress are indicative of molecular mechanisms underpinning ageing and ALS onset.

Examination of ALS survival-related pathways by comparing fast to slow ageing participants with ALS highlighted disrupted splicing and nucleocytoplasmic transport, along with pathways reflecting advanced ALS stages characterised by extensive neuroinflammation and diminished oxidative phosphorylation.71 We also noted pathways dysregulated in opposite directions in fast versus slow ageing in ALS compared to fast versus slow ageing in control (e.g., oxidative phosphorylation, MYC signalling). Further study of these opposite trending pathways may help decipher which drive disease versus which are compensatory attempts to slow disease progression. Despite the small sample size, we identified 43 DEGs in fast versus slow ageing patients, with two, NRG1 and SASH1, also up in ALS versus controls. Overexpressing NRG1 isoform I in skeletal muscle and inhibiting NRG1 isoform III in spinal cord effectively preserved motor neurons in ALS mice.72,73 SASH1, a tumor suppressor involved in cell proliferation and apoptosis, is unexplored in ALS but may warrant further investigation.

Our analyses reinforce the feasibility of using peripheral blood to identify epigenetic, immune, and transcriptomic profiles linked to ageing and ALS characteristics. Indeed, we detected dysregulated inflammation, nucleocytoplasmic transport, and impaired splicing with ALS and ageing in blood that were previously reported in neuronal studies.74 We previously demonstrated changes in peripheral neutrophil and CD4+ T cell numbers linked to rapid ALS progression.69 Similarly, blood chromatin accessibility changes were associated with neuronal functions and earlier ALS onset in one study,75 while another study identified correlations between pathological processes in the brain to transcripts in blood cells in participants with ALS.76 Cumulatively, our study and the literature support the viability of leverageing blood analyses to uncover pathways linked to ALS characteristics.

Our study has several strengths. First, our large sample size facilitated sex-stratified analyses of moderate power. Second, the study design allowed using the control group to calculate age acceleration with high accuracy in a manner independent of chronological age. Using this rigorous method, we showed that EAA is correlated with occupational environmental exposures and immunosenescence in ALS. Finally, our integration of EAA with transcriptomic changes separately in ALS and healthy control participants identified molecular mechanisms underlying links between the epigenetic clock and ALS pathology. As for limitations, potential controls were excluded if they had a personal or family history of ALS or a neurodegenerative disease, a restriction not placed on the ALS population; although a sensitivity analysis excluding participants with ALS and a family history of ALS showed no significant changes in the epigenetic age, further studies including controls with fewer exclusionary criteria are warranted. Next, survivorship bias is always a possibility in these types of studies where only “healthier” patients survive to the time of a first or second clinic visit and contribute samples. In addition, the occupational exposure data sample size was limited, with only three females in the high-risk group. Additionally, our study is correlative, and it remains unclear whether epigenetic factors are a cause or consequence of premature ageing in ALS. The Mendelian randomisation analysis did not show evidence that GrimAge acceleration was causal for ALS. Mendelian randomisation analysis requires strong assumptions, such as genetic instruments impacting outcome only through the exposure. GrimAge acceleration may be a biomarker or a mediator of health factors that are related to ALS. We recommend follow up studies with longitudinal study designs to assess the temporality of our findings. We also could not determine whether faster EAA modifies disease risk and phenotype or rather is a consequence of neurodegeneration and case status. Further, it remains unknown whether EAA mediates the effects of occupational exposure on ALS. More exposure data is essential to better understand the mediating role of EAA on ALS risk and survival. Lastly, exposures in our study were restricted to occupational codes and self-reported occupational exposures. Future studies should include household exposures, air pollution, metals, organic pollutants, and other environmental exposures.

Collectively, our study unveiled EAA in both male and female participants with ALS versus controls, which was also linked to shorter survival from diagnosis in males. EAA in males was associated with occupational exposures, potentially explaining part of the sex-specific association with survival. These findings highlight the complex interaction of ageing and exposures on ALS case status and survival. DNAm age could serve as a monitoring tool of ALS risk in susceptible individuals and as a biomarker of progression, especially in males. Future interventions77,78 that slow EAA may lower ALS risk and increase ALS survival, particularly among males in high-risk occupations. Further prospective longitudinal studies could corroborate our findings and expand investigation of EAA to additional exposures and risk factors in the ALS exposome.
