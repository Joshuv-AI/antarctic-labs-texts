Impact of home-based preoperative physical activity on postoperative complications in patients undergoing oncologic surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Takuya Yanagisawa, Noriatsu Tatematsu, Kazuhiro Hayashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to determine whether home-based preoperative physical activity (PA) influenced the incidence of postoperative complications (PCs) in patients who underwent oncologic surgery.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted using five electronic databases. Full articles with observational designs that compared by preoperative PA or PCs were included. The primary outcome was some type of PC, and a meta-analysis was performed to determine the impact of preoperative low PA on the incidence of PCs. Sub-analysis was performed to identify subgroups based on the severity of the PCs and the cancer site.ResultsEight studies were included in the analysis. Low preoperative PA was associated with all PCs (odds ratio [OR] = 2.42, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.44 to 4.07) but not with severe PCs (OR = 1.93, 95% CI = 0.51 to 7.37). In a subgroup of patients with gastrointestinal cancers, low preoperative PA was associated with all PCs (OR = 3.44, 95% CI = 1.63 to 7.26) and severe PCs (OR = 2.93, 95% CI = 1.30 to 6.58).ConclusionsThis systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that patients with low preoperative PA undergoing oncologic surgery, particularly those with gastrointestinal cancers, are more likely to develop PCs. A definitive conclusion could not be reached, however, due to the limited number of studies exclusively examining this relationship in cancer patients.

Introduction

In 2022, approximately 20 million new cases of cancer were diagnosed worldwide [1], and it is estimated that 45 million surgical procedures will be required annually worldwide by 2030 [2]. Approximately 60% of postoperative patients have minor complications, classified as Clavien–Dindo (CD) grades 1 and 2, while approximately 25% of patients that undergo elective surgery have severe complications, classified as CD grades 3 and 4 [3]. Among patients with breast, colorectal, and gastric cancers, which comprise a significant number of cancer patients worldwide, approximately 36–47% develop postoperative complications (PCs), with approximately 6–14% developing severe complications [4]. As PCs negatively impact postoperative length of stay (LOS) and survival [5, 6], prioritizing their prevention is critical.

Although numerous risk factors for complications following oncologic surgery have been identified, preoperative physical activity (PA) is reported to be a potentially modifiable factor [7–10]. Some reports, however, refute the relationship between preoperative PA and the incidence of PCs [11–13]. A systematic review published in 2019 that included a meta-analysis of the relationship between preoperative PA and PCs in patients undergoing oncologic surgery revealed no significant relationship [14], but the meta-analysis was performed on only four articles from the earliest records to 2017 [14]. In the previous systematic review [14], the number of articles included in the meta-analysis was limited, and most of the included studies had small sample sizes. Additionally, the fact that additional studies have been performed since the systematic review was published, an updated systematic review examining the relationship between preoperative PA and PCs is needed. The importance of increasing PA in preoperative rehabilitation (prehabilitation) for patients with cancer was recently demonstrated in a systematic review of prehabilitation; many of the studies included, however, reported on unsupervised prehabilitation [15]. To optimize the benefits of prehabilitation, it is important to clarify the relationship between home-based preoperative PA and PCs in patients undergoing oncologic surgery for cancer. Hospitalized patients often experience reduced PA due to a variety of factors [16], suggesting that the amount of PA differs between home and hospital settings. The objective of this systematic review was to investigate the relationship between home-based preoperative PA and PCs in patients who underwent oncologic surgery.

Materials and methods

The protocol for this systematic review with meta-analysis was registered in the PROSPERO (No. CRD42024586951). This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [17].

Search strategy

To identify relevant literature, we conducted electronic searches through September 9, 2024, in the following databases: MEDLINE/PubMed, Web of Science, CINAHL, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library. The following terms related to “participants”, “exposures”, and “outcomes” were searched: “cancer”, “preoperative physical activity”, and “postoperative complications”, respectively. The detailed search strategy is presented in Appendix 1 (Online Resource 1). To ensure the comprehensiveness of this systematic review, no restrictions were placed on the language, date of publication, cancer site, age, or type of PCs included in the analysis. Asterisks were used to truncate words, ensuring that every possible ending after the asterisks was included in the search. References of prospective and retrospective observational studies were manually searched for additional studies.

Study selection

Publications were included if (1) they were full articles with a prospective or retrospective observational design and compared by preoperative low PA vs. high PA (other than those determined to be low PA in the judgement of each study) or presence of PCs vs. absence of PCs; (2) all study participants were patients undergoing oncologic surgery; (3) preoperative PA was measured by questionnaire (including single-item measure) or activity meter (including pedometer) before admission (home-based), and judged as low PA or high PA; and (4) PCs were assessed using some kind of evaluation battery, and judged as the presence of PCs or absence of PCs. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) not a prospective or retrospective observational design (nor secondary analysis studies); (2) participants included non-cancer patients or patients undergoing palliative surgery; (3) PCs were not assessed using an evaluation battery or uncertain; and (4) other reasons (e.g. PA is treated as continuous variables, PA is not measured preoperatively, PA is not measured).

The searched articles were uploaded to Rayyan (Qatar Computing Research Institute, Ar Rayyan, Qatar) [18], and duplicate articles were removed. After removing duplicate articles, the identification of articles that met the inclusion criteria was independently conducted by two reviewers (T.Y. and K.H.). Articles were screened for title, abstract, and full text. The reviewers independently performed a full-text assessment using a standardized eligibility sheet to identify relevant articles to include in the review. Any discrepancies were resolved within each step by a third reviewer (N.T.) through discussion.

Quality assessment

The quality of the studies was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [19], a tool designed to assess the risk of bias in observational studies. This assessment was conducted by two trained independent reviewers (T.Y. and K.H.), and differences were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer (N.T.) through discussion. The NOS includes the eight domains, scoring 0 or 1 point or 0 to 2 points in each domain. The total scores were calculated, with higher scores indicating higher quality studies.

Data extraction

The following data were extracted from the included studies by one reviewer (T.Y.): features of studies including (1) author’s last name and publication year; (2) study design; (3) country; (4) sample size; (5) sex distribution; (6) mean or median age; (7) cancer site; (8) preoperative treatment; (9) postoperative treatment; (10) surgical approach; (11) assessment of PA; (12) type and assessment of PCs; and (13) mean or median postoperative length of stay (LOS). A second reviewer (K.H.) cross-checked the extracted data. The exposure was preoperative low PA judged by questionnaire (including single-measure item) or activity meter (including pedometer). The primary and secondary outcomes were some kind of PC and postoperative LOS, respectively.

Data synthesis and analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using the Review Manager software version 5.4 (The Cochrane Collaboration, London, UK). The effect of home-based preoperative PA on the incidence of PCs in patients undergoing oncologic surgery was estimated using a forest plot of odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI). Additionally, the effects of home-based preoperative PA on postoperative LOS, secondary outcome, in patients who underwent oncologic surgery was estimated using a forest plot of the standardized mean difference (SMD) with 95% CI. In the estimation of SMD, when LOS was reported as the median and interquartile range in each study, it was converted to estimates of the mean and standard deviation using previously described methods [20]. SMD values of < 0.2, 0.2 to 0.5, and > 0.5 were defined as small, moderate, and large effect sizes, respectively [21]. If the preoperative PA was divided into three or more groups, two groups were included in the analysis: the group with the lowest PA and a group that was the sum of the other groups. Meta-analysis was performed using a random effects model. We also assessed statistical heterogeneity using the I2 statistic and Cochrane chi-squared test (Q test). Furthermore, we adopted the I2 levels suggested by the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (0%, 25%, 50%, and 75% representing no, low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively). Also, Cochrane chi-squared test (Q test) was used for the I2 statistic, and a p-value of < 0.10 was considered statistically significant [22]. A stratified meta-analysis was planned for assessing PA (questionnaires [including single-item measure] or activity meter [including pedometer]), the severity of the PCs (all PCs or severe PCs), and the cancer site. Severe PCs were defined as the CD classification grade ≥ 3 [23, 24]. We assessed the possibility of publication bias using funnel plots.

Results

Study selection

The total number of records identified through the database was 4437 (Fig. 1). After removing duplicate articles, a total of 2792 articles were included and screened. After title and abstract screening, 111 articles were selected for full-text screening. Of the 111 articles, there were 10 studies with ineligible designs, 28 with ineligible participants, 22 with ineligible outcomes, and 43 with other eligible reasons for exclusion (e.g., preoperative PA was not evaluated, the number of patients with exposure and outcomes was not clear); therefore, 103 articles were excluded. Finally, 8 articles [25–32] met the inclusion criteria in the present systematic review.

Study characteristics

The characteristics of the included articles (4 prospective and 4 retrospective studies), involving a total of 3907 patients, are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. Preoperative PA was assessed by questionnaire in seven studies [25, 26, 28–32] and by pedometer in one study [27]. The questionnaire used to assess preoperative PA consisted of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire short form (IPAQ-SF) [33] in three studies [25, 28, 29], Short QUestionnaire to ASsess Health enhancing physical activity (SQUASH) [34] in one study [30], Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG-PS) [35] in one study [31], Golden Leisure-Time Exercise Questionnaire (GLTEQ) [36] in one study [32], and single-measure item about exercise in one study [26]. The PCs set by the included articles were some type of complication in seven studies [25–30, 32] and delirium in one study [31]. Of the eight studies that used some type of PC as an outcome, six studies [26–30, 32] used the CD classification [23, 24] and one study [25] used the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events v4.0 [37] to evaluate the PCs. Delirium was assessed by the Confusion Assessment Method for the Intensive Care Unit in the study that had delirium as an outcome [38].

Effect of home-based preoperative PA on all PCs

Eight studies [25–32] were included in a random-effects meta-analysis. A total of 3907 patients were compared to assess the presence of any PCs between the preoperative low PA and high PA groups. The meta-analysis revealed a significant difference in the presence of all PCs between the two groups (OR = 2.42, 95% CI = 1.44 to 4.07), with high heterogeneity (Q test, p < 0.01; I2 = 76%) (Fig. 2 A). In a subgroup analysis of patients with gastrointestinal cancers, six studies [25, 27–31] were included in a random-effects meta-analysis. A total of 2186 patients were compared to assess the presence of any PCs between preoperative low PA and high PA groups. The meta-analysis indicated a significant difference in the presence of any PCs between the two groups (OR = 3.44, 95% CI = 1.63 to 7.26), with high heterogeneity (Q test, p < 0.01; I2 = 79%) (Fig. 2B). The study by Kim et al. [31] evaluated PCs in terms of delirium only, differing from the other studies. Due to the small number of studies using an activity meter, it was not possible to perform a sub-analysis based on the type of preoperative PA measurement.

Effect of home-based preoperative PA on severe PCs

Three studies [27, 28, 32] were included in a random-effects meta-analysis. A total of 1327 patients were compared to evaluate the presence of severe PCs between the preoperative low PA and high PA groups. The meta-analysis revealed no significant difference in the presence of severe PCs between the two groups (OR = 1.93, 95% CI = 0.51 to 7.37), with probability of moderate heterogeneity (Q test, p = 0.10; I2 = 56%) (Fig. 3 A). In a subgroup analysis of the patients with gastrointestinal cancers, two studies [28, 29] were included in a random-effects meta-analysis. The presence of severe PCs was compared between preoperative low PA and high PA groups among 263 patients with gastrointestinal cancers. The meta-analysis indicated a significant difference in the presence of severe PCs between the two groups (OR = 2.93, 95% CI = 1.30 to 6.58), with not heterogeneity (Q test, p = 0.48; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 3B). Impact of preoperative PA on postoperative LOS

Two studies [27, 29] reporting postoperative LOS for each low PA and high PA group were included in a random-effects meta-analysis. Postoperative LOS was compared between low PA and high PA groups comprising a total of 197 patients. The meta-analysis indicated no significant difference in the postoperative LOS between the two groups (SMD = 0.95, 95% CI = −0.38 to 2.28), with high heterogeneity (Q test, p < 0.01; I2 = 93%) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The present systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated the impact of home-based preoperative PA on the incidence of PCs in patients who underwent oncologic surgery. Patients with preoperative low PA had a significantly higher risk of some type of PC than those without preoperative low PA. Furthermore, this systematic review included a sub-analysis of the relationship between preoperative PA and the presence of PCs by the severity of the PCs and the anatomic location of the participants’ cancer. This systematic review is the first to investigate the relationship between preoperative PA and PCs in such detail in patients undergoing oncologic surgery. However, because of the high heterogeneity of the results integrated in this review, it may be necessary to unify the study design and research methods.

Impact of home-based preoperative PA on PCs

Eight studies [25–32] examined the relationship between preoperative PA and PCs, and our meta-analysis showed that preoperative PA was associated with all PCs. Moreover, three articles investigated the relationship between preoperative PA and severe PCs. Patients with low preoperative PA did not have a significantly higher risk of severe complications than high PA patients. Previous studies reported that patients with poor PA may have impaired immune cell functions and lower levels of inflammatory responses [39, 40]. Furthermore, experimental studies have demonstrated that prolonged sitting for 6 h significantly impairs lower extremity micro- and macrovascular dilator function in young healthy males [41]. Additionally, 5-day bed rest has been shown to increase insulin resistance, serum glucose, total cholesterol, and systolic blood pressure while reducing calf blood flow in healthy volunteers [42]. Therefore, preoperative daily inactivity may have a negative effect on systemic physiology, increasing the risk of developing some kind of PC due to impaired physiologic function. Preoperative exercise is reported to affect postoperative pain, function, complications, and quality of life [43]. Consequently, patients with preoperative low PA should be targeted for interventions to prevent PCs, although it may not prevent serious PCs.

On the other hand, a meta-analysis restricted to patients with gastrointestinal cancers showed a significant association of preoperative low PA with all PCs and severe PCs. Because exercise-based prehabilitation for cancer patients is reported most frequently for gastrointestinal cancers [15], we consider that the results of the sub-analysis of this systematic review provide important insights into the subject of prehabilitation for patients with gastrointestinal cancers. Only one study [27] of patients with gastrointestinal cancer, however, objectively assessed preoperative PA using a pedometer. Although, the number of steps used to define low PA in this study was 2500 steps/day, the definition of low PA assessed by the number of steps needs to be examined in detail. A meta-analysis showed that a behavioral intervention using motivational interviewing increased the average number of steps taken per day by 1323 [44]. Therefore, it may be important to assess the risk of PCs with preoperative step counts and to encourage patients to increase preoperative PA through interventions using motivational interviewing. However, the results for patients with only gastrointestinal cancer were derived from a smaller number of studies, and more studies are needed to draw conclusions in the future.

Differences in preoperative PA assessment methods

This systematic review planned to perform a sub-analysis based on the assessment method of preoperative PA and the severity of PCs. This sub-analysis could not be performed, however, due to the small number of studies that objectively assessed preoperative PA using pedometers or activity meters. Most of the studies included in this systematic review assessed preoperative PA with questionnaires or single items, but the methods varied. The studies included in the meta-analysis used the IPAQ-SF [33], SQUASH [34], and GLTEQ [36] to assess preoperative PA and differed as to whether PA was assessed for all time or leisure time. Also, one of the studies included in the meta-analysis evaluated PA with the ECOG-PS [35], but the ECOG-PS evaluates PA as a percentage. It is assumed that these differences in evaluation methods led to variations in the results of each study and affected the results, including the heterogeneity, of the meta-analysis. In terms of heterogeneity, one study evaluated only delirium as a PC. Furthermore, it is reported that questionnaire assessments of PA overestimate PA [45, 46] and the assessment of PA by questionnaire has a measurement error due to recall bias [47]. The influence of the limitations of the questionnaire on the results can also be inferred. Therefore, the use of activity meters, including pedometers, may be more appropriate than questionnaires for PA assessment to predict PCs in patients undergoing oncologic surgery. Few articles evaluating preoperative PA using activity monitors or pedometers are available; the use of activity monitors and pedometers should be recommended, however, for the evaluation of PA.

Impact of preoperative PA on postoperative LOS

Because few studies reported postoperative LOS in low PA and high PA groups, only two studies [27, 29] were included in the meta-analysis. Meta-analysis of these two studies [27, 29] did not reveal a significant association between preoperative low PA and prolonged postoperative LOS. In general, PCs prolong postoperative LOS [5] and preoperative low PA also prolongs postoperative LOS [48]. Therefore, it is likely that PCs also increase the time required for treatment and prolong postoperative LOS in oncologic surgery patients, but the results of this meta-analysis do not support the results of previous studies. Both studies included patients with colorectal cancer in this meta-analysis, but the median postoperative LOS was reported as 7 days in the study by Richards et al. [27] and 14.5 days in the study by Yanagisawa et al. [29]. The difference in the postoperative LOS between studies may be influenced by differences in medical systems between countries, which could affect the results of the meta-analysis.

Study limitations

This systematic review has several limitations. First, we used the MEDLINE/PubMed, Web of Science, CINAHL, Scopus, and Cochrane Library databases for the search. Although this appears to have yielded a sufficient number of articles, the use of additional databases might have allowed us to extract more articles. Second, the number of studies included in this systematic review was small, because many papers treated preoperative PA as a continuous variable in screening and also included non-cancer patients. Nevertheless, this systematic review included more studies than previous systematic reviews [14] and provides detailed evidence of the relationship between preoperative PA and PCs, especially for patients undergoing surgery for gastrointestinal cancers. Third, many of the articles included in this systematic review did not specify the details of postoperative treatment. Because postoperative rehabilitation reduces the risk of developing some kind of PC [49], interpretation of the results should take into account the effect of postoperative treatment on PCs. Fourth, according to the Cochrane Handbook, at least 10 papers must be included in a meta-analysis to evaluate publication bias using Egger’s test [22], and the number of papers included in this study was eight, making it difficult to evaluate publication bias. Fifth, with respect to low PA as defined in each of the studies included in this review, the degree of low PA varies and some caution is needed in interpreting the association between low PA and PCs. Finally, most of the studies included in this systematic review classified PCs only by severity, and the relationship between preoperative PA and PCs could not be examined separately according to the type of PC. Future studies should examine the relationship between preoperative PA and the type of PC.

Conclusion

Home-based preoperative PA may impact the incidence of PCs in patients undergoing oncologic surgery, particularly in those with gastrointestinal cancers. Nevertheless, it is currently difficult to draw definitive conclusions, due to the paucity of studies that have examined this relationship in patients with cancer. Additional studies are needed to verify the relationship between preoperative PA and PCs according to the cancer site and to examine in detail the type of PCs associated with preoperative PA based on a comprehensive evaluation of preoperative PA using activity meters or pedometers.
