Factors driving implementation success of mental health gap action programme (mhGAP) in Colombia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Felipe Agudelo-Hernández, Helena Vélez-Botero, Laura Inés Plata-Casas, Andrés Camilo Delgado-Reyes
Published: 2025

Background

In low- and middle-income countries, it is estimated that nearly one-third of the population experienced a mental health disorder during the COVID-19 pandemic [1]. Despite the high prevalence of mental disorders, access to mental health care remains limited in these countries due to a range of factors such as stigma, shortage of mental health providers, and limited funding [2]. Similarly, in these contexts, between 76% and 85% of individuals with mental, neurological, and substance use disorders do not receive the care they need [3].

In response to these challenges, community-based programs and primary mental health care approaches have been proposed to improve access to care and reduce the burden of mental disorders in the region [4]. Low-complexity mental health programs aim to provide comprehensive, integrated, and culturally sensitive mental health care [4, 5]. These programs often include a range of interventions such as psychoeducation, psychosocial support, mutual support groups, and medication management, and are led by a multidisciplinary team of mental health providers [3, 5]. The implementation of primary and community-level strategies has been associated with improved quality of life [6], educed symptomatology [7] and decreased healthcare costs through fewer hospitalizations [8]. impacting individuals’ functioning and mortality indicators [8, 9].

One such program is the Mental Health Gap Action Programme (mhGAP) by the World Health Organization (WHO) [3]. Although proposed since 2008 to address the deficit in access to quality mental health services worldwide, mhGAP has faced implementation challenges [4, 10]. The mhGAP includes two main implementation guides: the mhGAP Intervention Guide (mhGAP-IG), which provides clinical decision-making tools for the management of priority mental, neurological, and substance use disorders in non-specialized health settings by health personnel, and the community intervention guide, which targets mental health awareness, stigma reduction, and understanding in the community through community leaders and decision-makers. It comprises key components such as policy development, plans, and strategies for mental health care, capacity-building of healthcare providers, improved mental disorder care in non-specialized health settings, and community awareness generation about mental disorders [3]. It has both clinical and community components and consists of five lines of action: sectoral and coordinated management for implementation, preparation for implementation, training (education), supervision, and operationalization [3].

Moreover, it has been described that evidence-based practices take 17–20 years to implement, and fewer than half of these practices are successfully integrated into health systems [11]. Compounding this situation, the evaluation of evidence-based interventions (programs, guidelines, treatments, practices) does not always reflect the complexity of the contexts [4, 11].

In the case of mhGAP, despite the potential benefits of community mental health programs, their successful implementation and evaluation can be challenging, particularly in low- and middle-income countries like those in Latin America [4]. One of the main challenges in program implementation is ensuring that interventions consider implementation variables for their development [12, 13]. However, ensuring these variables can be particularly difficult in Latin American countries, where resources may be limited, and mental health providers may have limited training in evidence-based practices [14].

The use of implementation measures has been proposed as a strategy to ensure that community mental health programs are delivered faithfully and effectively to improve mental health outcomes [12]. Implementation measures are tools used to assess the quality and effectiveness of the implementation process and can be used to identify areas for improvement and monitor progress over time. By using these measures, mental health providers can ensure that the intervention is being delivered as intended and can make adjustments to the intervention as needed to improve outcomes [13, 14]. By identifying these factors, providers can develop strategies to address barriers and enhance facilitators to improve implementation success [15].

The present study

In this context, prior to 2023, the mhGAP program in Colombia underwent extensive deployment in one of its five action lines, namely training, specifically focusing on training participants using both the mhGAP Intervention Guide (mhGAP-IG) for health personnel and the community intervention guide for community leaders and decision-makers. For this purpose, a cascading training process, progressively implemented over time, known as training of trainers, was carried out initially under the auspices of the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) for the Ministry of Health and subsequently across different regions. This was conducted following the methodology outlined by the strategy itself, with adaptations to the specific context. This methodology delineates two phases: a training phase and a follow-up phase, during which reference professionals accompany the strategy for approximately four months on a weekly basis [3].

With the implementation of the mhGAP guides in Colombia, it is expected to improve the timeliness of access to mental health services and comprehensive care, foster continuity of care, and strengthen primary care, thereby promoting human rights and dignity for those suffering from these disorders, advancing towards equity. However, implementation problems have been described in Colombia regarding the mhGAP and mental health strategies in community settings and at the primary level of health care [6, 16]. Based on the above, the objective of this research is to determine implementation variables for mhGAP in Colombia, including system facilitators for implementation, strategy accessibility, adaptability and acceptability, and capacity for training and supervision.

Methods

Participants and procedure

A quantitative, cross-sectional, correlational study was conducted within the framework of the mhGAP implementation in Colombia, between the years 2021 and 2023. The study employed convenience sampling and included all territories where official mhGAP training was conducted by the health authority during this period. The six territories included in the study represent all Colombian departments where official mhGAP training was implemented by the health authority between 2021 and 2023.

A total of 447 individuals trained in mhGAP participated in the study. Among these participants, 54.8% were male, and the rest of the population identified as female. The group comprised 349 healthcare professionals trained in mhGAP, 72 territorial mental health leaders responsible for planning mental health strategies in Colombian departments or municipalities, and 63 individuals identified as community mental health managers.

The instrument was administered to individuals trained in mhGAP, under the supervision of authorized lead trainers appointed by the Colombian Ministry of Health and PAHO. There were no exclusion criteria. The objectives of instrument administration were explained in person as part of the training. However, the instrument was administered one month after the training phase to assess guides performance post-training.

The instrument administration was conducted online. A self-administered virtual questionnaire was employed, implemented through the KoboToolbox platform, which was part of the protocol designed for this study and included informed consent. All participants agreed to participate and signed the informed consent form.

Information was kept confidential through anonymization of all participant data, ensuring no individual identification was possible. Aggregated findings were agreed to be shared with the mhGAP management committee, which is comprised of the Colombian Ministry of Health and PAHO/WHO-Colombia. Given that the Ministry of Health oversees mhGAP training actions in Colombia, the committee was informed of the study and facilitated access to the target population.

Measures

A questionnaire was composed of sociodemographic data, particularly related to origin, role in mental health, age, and gender. Additionally, the Implementation Drivers Scale (IDS) was applied, which was validated with the same population [17]. The complete questionnaire is provided in Supplement 1.

IDS comprises Likert-type questions designed for clinical and administrative mental health professionals, as well as community mental health leaders. It consists of 18 items distributed across four dimensions: system enablers for implementation, accessibility of the strategy, adaptability and acceptability, and strategy training and supervision. These dimensions exhibited Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.914, 0.868, 0.927, and 0.725, respectively, indicating high internal consistency [17].

The scale is based on the Active Implementation Framework (AIF) [18, 19]. The AIF incorporates drivers that provide a framework for understanding the essential elements necessary to ensure that evidence-based practices are faithfully implemented and sustained over time. These drivers include staff selection, training, supervision, intervention adaptation, and organizational support [18]. These align with those proposed by mhGAP [3, 5].

Statistical analysis

Data obtained were analyzed using SPSS 26. Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistical methods: mean (x̄) and standard deviation (SD). Bivariate correlation analysis was conducted among the study variables.

Ethical considerations

All participants agreed to complete the instrument by signing an informed consent form, which emphasized the confidentiality of identifiable and occupational data. This research was approved by [Masked]. This work adheres to the standards for research involving human subjects as provided in the Declaration of Helsinki of 2000. It represents a minimal-risk investigation.

Discussion

The implementation of the mhGAP guides training is essential for feasible interventions aimed at improving timely access to mental health services and comprehensive care, promoting care continuity, strengthening primary care, and contributing to the enjoyment of the right to health and the well-being of individuals, their families and caregivers [5, 6, 10, 16]. Despite the increasing prevalence of mental disorders in low- and middle-income countries, programs addressing care gaps face implementation challenges [4]. Therefore, this study determined, in a Latin American context, the system facilitators for implementation, strategy accessibility, adaptability and acceptability, and capacity for training and supervision, as key variables in implementing evidence-based practices.

The negative correlation found in our results regarding the lack of guides training follow-up and the total score suggests that follow-up is associated with greater effectiveness in the guides implementation process, which aligns with the proposed implementation framework in the study’s evaluation instruments [18, 19]. The significant positive correlations among the different subscales suggest interdependence between them in terms of implementation and effectiveness.

In this regard, the implementation of the mhGAP guides and the program, requires adequate support, coordination and supervision from the specialized care level, for which monitoring and supervision of the staff involved are fundamental and essential components. This is necessary for the transfer of skills and knowledge from mhGAP training to clinical practice, fostering respectful and non-prejudicial attitudes, ethical treatment, and the promotion and protection of the human rights of individuals with mental health conditions [9].

Strategies that strengthen primary care and community settings improve people’s quality of life when implemented correctly [20, 21]. These highlights barriers related to the mhGAP implementation processes: while the WHO reports that mhGAP is being used in over 100 countries, implementation in many contexts has largely stopped at the training phase rather than continuing through the essential follow-up component [5, 6, 16]. This could be due to lack of financial resources, lack of institutional leadership support, and resistance from some implementing organization members [14]. Additionally, proper contextual adaptation should be a priority in the implementation process, as inadequate adaptation constitutes a significant barrier. While mhGAP includes a comprehensive adaptation guide that outlines steps for adapting the guides and training materials, this guidance is rarely followed systematically [14, 17, 22–24].

The results also suggest that both leaders of support groups or community strategies and mental health directors demonstrated significantly different implementation perceptions compared to clinically trained individuals, with both groups showing more positive scores across implementation domains. This result is highlighted as relevant, as clinical professionals are responsible for delivering the training, and their perceptions could influence the program’s dissemination.

In this regard, other studies [25] investigating the implementation of public mental health programs in Colombian contexts have indicated a disconnect between political rhetoric and territorial realities, reflected in insufficient investment, a lack of adequately trained personnel and a low prioritization of mental health at the local level. The applicability of programs such as mhGAP requires a critical reinterpretation that prioritizes structural inequalities, cultural barriers and the co-creation of knowledge with communities. Harmonizing these actions among clinical, community, and administrative staff is a priority for bridging the mental health gap and the implementation gap. Future studies could address mechanisms for building models that strengthen the continuum of care and where each actor in mental health systems plays a clearer and more active role.

Therefore, actions performed in the territory by different stakeholders need to be coordinated, and actions agreed upon must be monitored, emphasizing the governance territories should have. Hence, there is an insistence on the need for articulation of provider and service actions within and outside the healthcare system [16, 24]. This coordination of action plans and their monitoring, which promotes the standardization of minimum mental health competencies at the primary care level, encourages engagement in health service provision goals and ensures their future projection [10].

These collaborative actions will help build processes that, accompanied by health authority leadership and active and decisive participation of community actors/sectors (community platform), lead to the consolidation of an integrated and barrier-free system that allows access to mental health services for all [14, 26]. The participatory implementation of actions requires planning, preparation, and provision, as well as generating the political-institutional conditions for mental health to be a priority in public policy, so that resources are allocated, sectoral, intersectoral, and community work are encouraged, contributing to the efficient use of cost-effective care pathways [14, 24–26].

Furthermore, it is evident mhGAP trainers and territorial mental health coordinators (individuals responsible for mental health program coordination at the departmental or municipal level) share similar implementation perceptions, particularly regarding the lack of adequate information systems for mhGAP-IG 2.0. However, these groups differ in their perceptions of territorial resource allocation for the guides implementation. Although there is not a significant proportion of trainers per territory, as most are in central regions of the country.

Iversen et al. [19] mention that a solution to this could be pre-service training, referring to teaching programs (e.g., in medical, public health, and nursing schools) provided to future healthcare workers before entering service. Therefore, it is essential to involve the productive sector, the education sector, and other sectors that allow for positive mobilization of social determinants in mental health. Thus, global strategies have been designed that guide academic health programs to include programs such as mhGAP in their training curricula. This includes raising awareness and understanding among teaching staff, training educational leaders on mhGAP-IG, forming a team and developing an action plan, reviewing the current curriculum, adapting mhGAP-IG to the educational institution’s circumstances, implementing a pilot curriculum, and evaluating and revising the curriculum [27]. This could help overcome implementation barriers noted in the present study, such as staff turnover and lack of familiarity with tools for use at the first levels of health care.

According to the present study, it has been described that implementation is more successful when those involved are carefully selected and receive training, mentoring, and monitoring during implementation; organizations and institutions provide the necessary infrastructure for implementation, training, monitoring, and result evaluation. The community is involved in program and practice selection and evaluation, there is a political context that facilitates resources and openness to necessary learning and adjustments in the implementation process [6].

Although this study did not address economic analyses, other research has related service continuity to quality of life [7] and symptom reduction [8], leading to reduced healthcare system costs through fewer hospitalizations, ultimately impacting higher individual functioning, apart from reduced mortality [26], in which the mhGAP 2.0 strategy could contribute. Therefore, it is recommended to involve territorial entities and levels of care, medication supply, and medical supplies, among others [3, 19].

As limitations of this study, the lack of measures in mental health care continuity in other territories where the strategy is being implemented could be considered, as well as leaders of implementing organizations. This study provides the most comprehensive measurement to date in Colombia of a program that constitutes an integral axis of public policy in mental health and could therefore contribute to the design of future implementation strategies in Colombia and similar contexts. Future studies could investigate the financial indicators of these strategies, the impact on other variables related to quality of life, and professionals’ perceptions of the difficulties in implementing the program using mixed-method studies.

Conclusions

The mhGAP program is a tool to improve the response of the first levels of care in the Colombian context, facing an urgent need that intensified in recent years due to the pandemic. However, it guides presents significant implementation challenges at the national level, especially established by multilevel governance, promoting multisectorality and the implementation of national public policy strategies.

It is necessary to promote and drive collective knowledge management processes in mental health at the local level, continue incorporating departments and districts into guides and program implementation, increase national and departmental intersectoral coordination to achieve greater reduction in mental health care gaps. Furthermore, continuing with the strengthening of human talent as a continuous process close to the humanization of services, working on aspects directly related to social protection and improvement in living conditions, as well as research and implementation processes related to contextual adaptation.
