Do Genetic Variants Directly Shape Population-Level Schizophrenia Burden? A Global Genomic Analysis
Authors: Junxian Tao, Haiyan Chen, Siyu Wei, Yuping Zou, Chen Sun, Jing Xu, Lian Duan, Yuanbo Zhan, Ruilin Li, Jiacheng Wang, Linna Yuan, Wei She, Songlin Lu, Ning Wang, Qinduo Ren, Yan Guo, Yuan Xu, Chang Wang, Hongsheng Tian, Chen Zhang, Yu Dong, Guoping Tang, Zhenwei Shang, Wenhua Lv, Mingming Zhang, Hongchao Lv, Yongshuai Jiang

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSchizophrenia imposes a substantial global health burden, with marked geographic variation in incidence, prevalence, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). Although genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have identified numerous individual-level risk variants, it remains unclear whether cross-population differences in allele frequencies are associated with variation in population-level disease burden.MethodsWe integrated allele frequencies from the 1000 Genomes Project with age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR), and age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR) across 20 countries (1990–2021) using the Global Burden of Disease 2021 data. Associations between allele frequencies and burden metrics were evaluated using linear mixed-effects models. Independent signals were defined through linkage disequilibrium–based clumping and annotated via multi-omic gene prioritization. Polygenic risk score (PRS) analyses were conducted in the UK Biobank for validation.ResultsWe identified 20, 20, and 14 independent single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) significantly associated with ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR, respectively. Two loci overlapped with Psychiatric Genomics Consortium GWAS findings, including variants within ADGRV1 and upstream of STAG1. These loci collectively explained a meaningful proportion of cross-country variation (partial R2 = 0.13–0.22). Implicated genes (e.g., LRRC4C, GPC5, PVALB) are involved in neurodevelopmental and synaptic processes. In UK Biobank analyses, schizophrenia cases exhibited higher PRSs derived from ASIR- and ASPR-associated SNPs (p = .0024 and .0095).ConclusionsThese findings suggest that population-level allele frequency variation is associated with global differences in schizophrenia burden and accounts for a meaningful proportion of cross-population variation. This transferable population-genomic epidemiological framework may inform future research on complex diseases and mental health disparities.Keywords: Epidemiology, Genetic variation, Global Burden of Disease (GBD), Population genomics, Schizophrenia

Schizophrenia is a chronic and severe psychiatric disorder affecting an estimated 23.2 million individuals worldwide and accounting for 14.8 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) (95% uncertainty interval [UI], 10.9–19.1) in 2021 (1). DALYs quantify the loss of healthy life years due to premature mortality and disability attributable to a disease. Despite its substantial health and social impact, the incidence, prevalence, and DALY burden of schizophrenia vary markedly across countries and regions (2,3). Elucidating the factors underlying these geographic disparities is critical for improving prevention strategies, diagnostic capacity, and global mental health planning.

At the individual level, genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have identified hundreds of common genetic variants associated with schizophrenia risk, thereby providing important insights into its biological underpinnings (4, 5, 6). In parallel, a range of environmental and social risk factors, such as obstetric complications, childhood adversity, and substance use, have also been implicated in schizophrenia susceptibility (7, 8, 9, 10). However, while these studies have advanced our understanding of individual-level risk, the mechanisms that contribute to the pronounced heterogeneity in schizophrenia burden observed across populations remain poorly understood.

From a population genetics perspective, allele frequency variation among populations, shaped by evolutionary history, migration, and gene-by-environment interactions, can influence aggregate health outcomes (11, 12, 13). Recent studies have begun to explore this connection. For example, Jukarainen et al. (14) estimated DALYs attributable to genetic risk factors across biobank cohorts, while Huang et al. (15) examined correlations between population-level polygenic risk and stroke burden across China. However, both approaches relied on biobank or region-specific datasets and did not explicitly test whether allele frequency variation itself is statistically associated with epidemiological burden metrics across diverse populations.

Here, we hypothesized that population-level allele frequency variation would be associated with cross-country differences in schizophrenia burden. We applied a population-based genome-wide association framework linking allele frequencies from the 1000 Genomes Project (16) with epidemiological indicators from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study data (17), including age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR), and age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR). Using linear mixed-effects models controlling for population structure, sociodemographic development, and spatiotemporal effects, we identified SNPs whose frequencies correlated with schizophrenia burden across 20 countries over a 32-year period.

Furthermore, we quantified their explanatory contribution via partial R2 statistics, prioritized genes using integrated functional and regulatory evidence, and evaluated whether these population-level burden-associated loci are linked to individual-level schizophrenia risk using polygenic risk scores (PRSs) derived from the UK Biobank (18). By bridging population genomics with epidemiological modeling, this study provides a novel paradigm for investigating how human genetic diversity shapes the global distribution of mental health burden.

Methods and Materials

Data Source and Processing

GBD 2021 Data

The GBD study provides a comprehensive and standardized assessment of global health metrics, drawing on data from vital registration systems, verbal autopsies, censuses, household surveys, disease-specific registries, health service contact data, and other sources (1). Disease outcomes are systematically classified based on ICD, enabling consistent estimation of incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs across locations and over time.

For this study, we extracted ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR estimates in schizophrenia for both males and females spanning the period 1990 to 2021 across 20 countries from GBD 2021. We also incorporated the Sociodemographic Index (SDI), a composite indicator of national development that is strongly correlated with population health outcomes. The SDI is calculated as the geometric mean of scaled total fertility rate (age <25 years), mean education (ages ≥15 years), and lag-distributed income per capita. Annual SDI estimates for 1990 to 2021 across countries were obtained from GBD 2021.

1000 Genomes Project

The 1000 Genomes Project provides a comprehensive reference of global human genetic variation. We used phase 3 data comprising 2504 individuals from 26 populations, generated primarily through low-coverage whole-genome sequencing. Variant call format (VCF) files were obtained, and SNP genotypes were extracted for unrelated individuals assigned to a single population (i.e., excluding individuals with multiple population labels).

Quality control was applied at the SNP level, including 1) exclusion of variants with a minor allele frequency < 0.01 and 2) removal of SNPs deviating from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (p < .001) in any population. After filtering, 2,621,978 autosomal biallelic SNPs from 2414 unrelated individuals representing 20 countries were included in the burden analyses (Table S1).

Individual-level genotypes were subsequently aggregated to compute population-level reference allele frequencies (RAFs), defined with respect to the reference allele in the VCF based on population identifiers. All genomic coordinates and allele definitions in this study are reported relative to the GRCh38 reference genome.

Genome-Wide Association Analysis

We applied principles of GWASs to investigate associations between genetic variation and schizophrenia burden at the population level (Figure 1). Allele frequency data from the 1000 Genomes Project were matched with schizophrenia burden estimates from GBD 2021 for 20 countries from 1990 to 2021 (Bangladesh, Barbados, China, Colombia, Finland, Gambia, India, Italy, Japan, Kenya, Mexico, Nigeria, Pakistan, Peru, Puerto Rico, Sierra Leone, Spain, Sri Lanka, the United Kingdom, and Vietnam) (Table S1).

Associations between SNPs and disease burden were estimated using a linear mixed-effects model as follows: Schizophrenia burden was specified as the outcome, reference allele frequency was specified as the predictor, and the top 4 principal components of population structure and the SDI were entered as covariates.

Here, Yij represents the schizophrenia burden metric (ASIR, ASPR, or ASDR) for country i (i = 1, 2,…, 20) in year j (j = 1, 2,…, 32). RAFi is the reference allele frequency; ∑PCsi are the top 4 principal components (Figure S1); and SDIij is the SDI. Random intercepts were included for country and year to adjust for spatial and temporal variation.

Statistical significance was evaluated using a genome-wide threshold of p < 1 × 10−5. PLINK version 1.9 was used to define independently associated SNPs (19). SNPs were considered independent if they were not in linkage disequilibrium (LD) (r2 < 0.1) with other significant SNPs within 3-Mb windows. For each index SNP, an LD clump was defined by all SNPs in LD (r2 ≥ 0.1) with it. The leftmost and rightmost SNPs within each clump were used to demarcate the genomic region. Each resulting LD clump was defined as a genomic risk locus; loci within 50 kb of each other were merged.

To facilitate interpretation of effect sizes, regression coefficients (βs) were rescaled into centi-allele frequency units (βCAFs) by dividing them by 100, given that RAFs are expressed on a 0 to 1 scale, representing the change in schizophrenia burden per 0.01 increase in allele frequency.

To estimate the partial R2 for each predictor block (SDI, population structure, or burden-associated genetic components), reduced models were fitted by sequentially removing the relevant term(s) while holding all other model components constant. The 95% CIs for partial R2 estimates were obtained using parametric bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations.

Gene Prioritization

Gene prioritization was performed using a weighted scoring framework adapted from Nievergelt et al. (20), which integrates multiple lines of evidence, including gene mapping (positional mapping, expression quantitative trait loci [eQTL] mapping, and chromatin interaction [CI] mapping), functional annotation, and fine-mapping.

Gene mapping was conducted for protein-coding genes. All mapping analyses were based on candidate SNPs located within burden-associated genomic risk loci. In positional mapping, SNPs were assigned to genes located within ± 10 kb of their genomic coordinates. eQTL mapping linked SNPs to genes with genetically regulated expression, using brain-specific eQTL datasets from PsychENCODE (21), BrainSeq (22), and MetaBrain (23). CI mapping identified long-range interactions between candidate SNP regions and gene promoters or enhancers, enabling the assignment of SNPs to genes based on physical chromatin contacts derived from PsychENCODE and Hi-C datasets generated from neural progenitor cells (24). The CI mapping procedure followed the approach implemented in the FUMA platform (25), with details available through the FUMA documentation (https://fuma.ctglab.nl/tutorial#chromatin-interactions).

Functional annotation considered 3 indicators: Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) scores (26), where values ≥15 suggest a deleterious effect on gene function; RegulomeDB scores (27,28), where values ≤2 indicate likely effects on regulatory binding; and the presence of nonsynonymous exonic variants annotated by ANNOVAR (29), which are more likely to affect protein structure and function.

Fine-mapping was performed using the Sum of Single Effects (SuSiE) method (30). SuSiE estimates the posterior inclusion probability for each SNP within a genomic risk locus, representing the probability that the SNP is causally associated with the disease burden. The maximum number of causal variants allowed per locus was set to 5.

For each locus, cumulative evidence scores were derived by summing the weighted contributions from all evidence categories assigned to each gene. Genes were subsequently ranked based on their total scores to identify those most likely to contribute to the locus-specific association with schizophrenia burden. Genes with total scores ≥16 were considered eligible for prioritization, whereas those scoring <16 were excluded from ranking. Within each locus, the highest-scoring gene was designated as tier 1, representing the most likely candidate underlying the observed association. Other genes within the same locus that achieved scores ≥16 were classified as tier 2, indicating additional plausible candidates supported by multiple lines of evidence. The full set of evidence categories and their corresponding weights is provided in Table S7.

PRS Analysis in the UK Biobank

PRSs were calculated to evaluate whether SNPs associated with population-level schizophrenia burden were also associated with individual-level schizophrenia risk. Schizophrenia cases were defined in the UK Biobank based on ICD-10 diagnostic codes F20–F20.9 and F25–F25.9, yielding 1224 affected individuals. Individuals without a schizophrenia diagnosis (n = 486,907) served as control participants.

For each burden metric (ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR), SNPs with association p values < .05 in the corresponding population-level analyses were selected for PRS construction. This relatively lenient threshold was chosen to maximize SNP inclusion, given the limited overlap of genotyped variants between the 1000 Genomes Project and the UK Biobank dataset. Individual-level PRSs were then computed using PLINK version 1.9 -score function, which calculates a weighted sum of effect alleles using regression coefficients from the burden-based association models.

To facilitate comparisons across groups, PRSs were standardized (z transformed) in the full sample to have a mean of 0 and an SD of 1. Differences in standardized PRSs between schizophrenia cases and controls were assessed using 2-sided t tests.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.3.2. The following R packages were used: lmerTest for constructing and testing linear mixed-effects models (31), PCAtools for principal component analysis (32), partR2 for estimating partial R2 values in mixed models (33), and susieR for fine-mapping analysis (30).

A comparison of locus overlap across burden metrics revealed that ASPR- and ASDR-associated loci showed the greatest concordance, with 13 of 20 loci shared between these 2 measures. This is consistent with the strong epidemiological correlation between ASPR and ASDR in schizophrenia (Figure S3). In contrast, ASIR showed a distinct genetic architecture: Only a single locus overlapped between ASIR and ASDR (ASIR#3 and ASDR#5), and no overlap was observed between ASIR and ASPR.

The most significant association was observed at rs10133155 (p = 5 × 10−8), located upstream of TMEM260 and associated with ASPR (βCAF = 3.19), corresponding to an increase of 3.19 cases per 100,000 people for every 0.01 increase in the T-allele frequency (Figure 2B). This locus also reached significance for ASDR (p = 8.52 × 10−8). Regional association plots for this locus are shown in Figure S4, and visualizations for other index SNPs are shown in Figures S7–S9.

We also compared our burden-associated loci with genome-wide significant loci (p < 5 × 10−8) reported in the most recent Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (PGC) schizophrenia GWAS (4). An ASDR-associated locus within ADGRV1, encoding a G protein–coupled receptor expressed in the central nervous system and implicated in neuronal development, was identified in both analyses (Figure S5). Additionally, an ASIR-associated locus upstream of STAG1 was located adjacent to a genome-wide significant STAG1 locus reported by the PGC (Figure S6). Pathogenic mutations in STAG1 are known to cause neurodevelopmental disorders (34,35), supporting the relevance of this association. We also identified an ASPR-associated locus within the SCZD1 region (MIM: 181510) (36), a genomic region previously implicated in schizophrenia susceptibility.

To further evaluate the robustness of these findings, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses incorporating additional covariates related to population admixture and environmental exposures [migration (37) and urbanization (38)]. Environmental exposure data were obtained from Our World in Data (Supplemental Methods). We also replaced allele-frequency estimates for the Indian population derived from the 1000 Genomes Project with corresponding estimates from the IndiGenomes database (39) to assess the stability of population representation (Supplemental Methods). Across all sensitivity analyses, the overall patterns of association, including the direction and relative magnitude of SNP effect estimates, remained broadly consistent (Spearman’s ρ > 0.99) (see Tables S4–S6). Full summary statistics for the sensitivity analyses are provided at http://www.onethird-lab.com/burdengenome/mental/.

Explanatory Power of Burden-Associated SNPs for Schizophrenia Burden

To quantify the contribution of burden-associated SNPs to variance in schizophrenia burden, we performed blockwise variance partitioning using partial R2 for variables within linear mixed-effects models. The models accounted for SDI, population structure, and genetic components derived from significant burden-associated SNPs (p < 1 × 10−5) (Figure 2C).

The full models demonstrated high overall explanatory power across all 3 metrics (marginal R2 > 0.97). Variance partitioning revealed that burden-associated SNPs explained a substantial proportion of the variation in schizophrenia burden. Specifically, ASIR-associated SNPs explained 21.7% of the variance in ASIR (partial R2 = 0.217; 95% CI, 0.186–0.251), while ASPR- and ASDR-associated SNPs explained 14.2% (partial R2 = 0.142; 95% CI, 0.081–0.197) and 13.4% (partial R2 = 0.134; 95% CI, 0.085–0.181) of the variance in ASPR and ASDR, respectively. In contrast, the explanatory contribution of SDI was relatively small, with partial R2 values of 0.0002, 0.002, and 0.004 for ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR, respectively.

Prioritization of Burden-Associated Genes

A key objective of this study was to identify genes most likely underlying the associations observed at burden-associated loci. We applied the gene prioritization framework proposed by Nievergelt et al. (20), which integrates positional mapping, eQTL mapping, CI mapping, functional annotation, and fine-mapping analyses into a weighted scoring system. Genes were ranked within each locus based on absolute and relative scores, and the highest-ranking gene (tier 1) was designated as the candidate burden-associated gene.

Using this approach, we identified 27 unique burden-associated genes, with 9 linked to ASIR, 11 linked to ASPR, and 15 linked to ASDR. The results of ASPR-associated gene prioritization are shown in Figure 3, with full results for all burden-associated genes provided in Tables S4 to S6.

Eight genes were shared between ASPR- and ASDR-associated loci, including KCNC4, KMT2C, EFR3A, CFAP46, LRRC4C, GPC5, TMEM260, and PVALB. Most of these genes are functionally relevant to neuropsychiatric and neurodevelopmental processes: LRRC4C regulates axonogenesis and synaptic transmission, GPC5 is required for neural tube development, and PVALB encodes a calcium-binding protein in inhibitory interneurons. KCNC4 (40,41), KMT2C (42,43), and EFR3A (44) have been implicated in neurological disorders, including autism spectrum disorder and Alzheimer’s disease. No overlap was observed between ASIR-associated genes and those linked to ASDR or ASPR. For ASIR-associated loci, genes such as ROBO1 and CTNND2 are involved in axon guidance and neuronal connectivity.

PRSs Based on Burden-Associated SNPs in UK Biobank Individuals

Our population-genomic analyses identified SNPs whose allele frequencies are associated with cross-country variation in schizophrenia burden. To assess whether these population-level associations translate to individual-level disease risk, we tested whether SNPs associated with schizophrenia burden were also associated with individual-level schizophrenia susceptibility. This framework posits that if cross-population allele frequency variations reflect underlying biological risk rather than demographic artifacts, individuals carrying a higher aggregate burden of these alleles would be expected to have an increased risk of schizophrenia.

To test this, we constructed PRSs in 488,131 participants from the UK Biobank, including 1224 individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia and 486,907 control individuals. PRSs were generated for each burden metric using SNPs reaching a permissive threshold (p < .05) in the discovery models and were subsequently standardized (z transformed) to facilitate comparison.

Consistent with our hypothesis, schizophrenia cases exhibited significantly higher mean PRSs than controls across all burden types, indicating that these loci capture individual-level genetic risk. The strongest association was observed for ASIR-based PRSs (p = .0024), followed by ASPR-based PRSs (p = .0095). ASDR-based PRSs showed a consistent direction of effect, although the association did not reach conventional statistical significance (p = .063).

Discussion

In this study, we applied a population-genomic framework designed to evaluate how global variations in schizophrenia burden correlate with population-level allele frequency distributions. By integrating high-resolution genetic data from the 1000 Genomes Project with comprehensive epidemiological estimates from the GBD study, we identified 14 ASIR-, 20 ASPR-, and 20 ASDR-associated loci. Notably, 2 of these burden-associated loci overlap with loci reported in the most recent individual-level GWAS by the PGC4, including one within ADGRV1 and another upstream of STAG1. Importantly, we further demonstrated that these population-level genetic signals are also associated with individual-level schizophrenia susceptibility in the UK Biobank cohort. Together, these findings indicate that our framework captures genetic signals consistent with biological risk rather than being driven solely by demographic artifacts or statistical noise, thereby offering a complementary perspective on the global heterogeneity of schizophrenia.

To interpret these findings, it is helpful to distinguish the population-genomic framework used here from traditional individual-level GWASs. Conventional GWASs, typically based on case-control designs, are optimized to identify variants with broadly consistent effects across individuals, prioritizing SNPs that differentiate cases from controls irrespective of the extent to which allele frequencies vary across populations. In contrast, the population-genomic framework is designed to highlight variants that are informative of geographic and demographic heterogeneity, specifically ancestry-informative variants whose allele frequencies differ across populations and may be associated with cross-country variation in disease burden.

In this study, we observed limited overlap between burden-associated loci identified in our population-level analyses and reported by the PGC GWAS. One possible interpretation is that many schizophrenia-associated variants detected at the individual level have similar effects across populations and may contribute less to observable cross-population variation in disease burden. Under this framework, such variants would be less likely to be detected by analyses that focus on population-level heterogeneity.

This distinction is further supported by our analysis of the fixation index (FST) (45), a standard metric for quantifying genetic differentiation between populations. We found that the top 1000 SNPs identified by the PGC GWAS exhibited significantly lower mean FST values (FST = 0.018) than those identified by the population-level analyses (0.021 for ASIR, 0.021 for ASPR, and 0.019 for ASDR). While the absolute numerical difference is modest, these differences reached statistical significance for ASPR- and ASIR-associated SNPs (p < .05). This suggests that population-level analyses preferentially capture variants with greater population differentiation, which may help explain the limited overlap between the specific lead SNPs found in our study and those in traditional GWASs.

Despite this divergence at the single-SNP level, evidence for a shared polygenic architecture emerges when genetic effects are considered in aggregate. The PRS analysis in the UK Biobank used a lenient significance threshold (p < .05) to incorporate thousands of small-effect variants. The significant association between these burden-associated PRSs and individual schizophrenia diagnosis indicates that at a polygenic level, the variants driving population-scale burden patterns overlap with those contributing to individual disease risk. This finding reconciles the 2 frameworks, suggesting that while population-differentiated lead variants may differ from those highlighted by individual-level GWASs, they nonetheless operate within the same broader biological pathways underlying schizophrenia susceptibility.

An additional consideration arises from the differential overlap observed across burden metrics. We found substantial concordance between ASPR- and ASDR-associated loci, with 13 of 20 loci shared, reflecting the strong correlation between prevalence- and disability-based measures of schizophrenia burden. In contrast, no overlap was observed between ASIR- and ASPR-associated loci. This pattern likely reflects fundamental epidemiological differences between these metrics. ASIR captures the rate of new disease onset and therefore may more directly reflect genetic liability at the earliest stages of schizophrenia. In contrast, ASPR is shaped not only by incidence but also by disease duration, mortality, recovery, and health-system factors such as access to care. Consistent with this interpretation, ASIR and ASPR showed only a moderate correlation (Spearman’s ρ = 0.68) (Figure S3). Notably, although no individual loci overlapped between ASIR and ASPR, genome-wide SNP effect sizes were moderately correlated in the 2 analyses (Spearman’s ρ = 0.66) (Figure S10). These findings suggest that genetic signals may be attenuated or obscured in prevalence-based measures by postonset environmental and social influences, which may explain why ASIR-associated SNPs accounted for a larger proportion of variance in our models.

Furthermore, our variance partitioning analyses indicate that burden-associated SNPs explain a substantially larger share of cross-population variation in schizophrenia burden than SDI. Specifically, these genetic components account for approximately 13% to 22% of the variance across burden metrics. However, the relatively small explanatory contribution of SDI should be interpreted with caution. The relationship between national development and disease burden is often complex and bidirectional. High-SDI countries typically benefit from more robust health infrastructures, which may increase disease detection and reporting while simultaneously providing better treatment and social support that reduce disease duration and severity. Within our modeling framework, these counteracting sociodemographic influences may partially offset one another, thereby attenuating the apparent contribution of SDI to overall variance.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, our analyses rely on GBD-derived estimates of ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR, which, although widely used, remain subject to uncertainty and ongoing debate regarding their accuracy and cross-country comparability (46). Second, several well-established environmental risk factors for schizophrenia, such as obstetric complications, infections, season of birth, adverse childhood experiences, and cannabis use (8,47), could not be explicitly modeled due to data limitations and are likely to account for a portion of the unexplained variance. As a result, genetic effects at the population level cannot be fully disentangled from environmental, cultural, and diagnostic influences. Accordingly, effect size estimates derived from our population-level models should be interpreted with caution and viewed as indicative of statistical associations rather than precise causal estimates. Finally, although the 1000 Genomes Project provides a robust and widely validated reference for global genetic structure (48), it cannot fully capture fine-scale genetic diversity within every country. Nonetheless, given its comprehensive continental representation (Figure S11), such limitations are unlikely to systematically generate the broad, consistent correlations observed in this study. Replication in independent global cohorts and with expanded genomic resources will be essential to further validate these findings.

Conclusions

This study provides empirical evidence that population-level allele-frequency variation is associated with global differences in schizophrenia burden. By integrating population genetics with global epidemiology, we have outlined a transferable population-genomic epidemiological framework that complements traditional individual-level approaches. This perspective offers new insights into how human genetic diversity relates to population-level health patterns and provides a foundation for future studies to integrate genomic, environmental, and policy-level data to better understand and address the global landscape of mental health.
