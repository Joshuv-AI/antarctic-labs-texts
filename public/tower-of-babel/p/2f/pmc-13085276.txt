Neurophobia in low- and middle- income countries (LMICs): burden and implications for neurology care and research – a systematic review
Authors: Oladotun V. Olalusi, Obinna Amaji, Tobi Olajide, Samuel Olawale, Joseph Yaria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAlthough neurological research and practice are gaining traction globally with newer therapeutic/diagnostic approaches, many medical students and trainees are reluctant to pursue careers in the neurosciences. Amid fragile health systems and low neurologist-patient ratios, a comprehensive understanding of the burden of neurophobia in low- and middle- income countries (LMICs) is yet lacking. We reviewed the burden of neurophobia and described its implications for neurology care and research.MethodsWe conducted a systematic search of PubMed, EMBASE, Google Scholar, Scopus, and African Journals Online (AJOL) for publications from inception till December 2024, using combinations of relevant search terms for ‘neurophobia’, ‘burden/prevalence/patterns/predictors’, our target populations, and ‘low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)’. Data from selected studies were extracted and populated into a customized spreadsheet under pre-defined headings. The extracted data were subsequently analyzed.ResultsA total of 131 studies from PubMed (n = 25), AJOL (n = 6), EMBASE, CINAHL, Scopus, and Google Scholar (n = 100) were screened. Ultimately, 15 studies were included, with a total sample size of 4,430 participants – comprising 3,629 medical students and 801 doctors. The prevalence of neurophobia was highly variable, ranging from as low as 6.25% to as high as 91.4%. The determinants, however, appeared consistent across study settings, comprising specific individual-level characteristics educational, sociocultural, as well as health system-related factors.ConclusionThe prevalence of neurophobia in LMICs varies widely. To tackle neurophobia, extant health policy, educational, and clinical training initiatives should be geared towards implementing and sustaining evidence-based strategies to improve clinical exposure and case-based learning. This will be critical to ensuring a steady stream of neurology workforce and may help bridge the neurology clinical care and research gap in LMICs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-08972-8.

Introduction

The world is witnessing a change in the epidemiological pattern of non-communicable diseases, and neurological disorders are at the heart of this transition [1]. Developing nations are worst hit as they bear the highest burden of neurological and related disorders [2–5] due to changing lifestyles and population explosion. Paradoxically however, the further advancement of modern neurological sciences is threatened by a reduction in staff strength for both research and neurological care [6–8]. In the absence of tailored, concerted, and context-specific efforts to optimize the number of neurology professionals and retain the existing workforce in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [9–12], current national and geographic shortfalls of neurologists and/or neuroscientists are likely to worsen, exacerbating clinic wait times while reducing access to care and hampering research [13, 14]. In many LMICs, many patients with neurological disorders are often first seen by internists, general practitioners, and medical officers before referral for specialist neurology care [9, 10]. It is expedient that internists and general practitioners, especially those practicing in developing countries with limited resources, brace themselves for the challenges of managing patients with neurological disorders [10, 15].

Globally, neurology/neuroscience care and research are threatened by “neurophobia” - a fearful perception of neurology and the neurological sciences by medical students [16, 17]. Comparatively, its brother term, “neurophilia” is a love for and fascination with neurology, and some have argued it may be just as common in both medical and general circles [18]. Case reports and television medical dramas have featured more neurological cases than those of any other medical specialty, providing evidence of at least widespread interest in the discipline, albeit as observers rather than practitioners. Neurophobia, observed when medical students and workers are asked to take on more active roles or solve neurological problems, is often driven by the perception of neurology as difficult and the perception of one’s skill as inadequate [18]. In 2017, a study documented significant correlations between students’ perceived knowledge of neurology, their self-confidence in diagnosing and managing neurological patients, and their likelihood of specializing in the field of neurology [19]. This underlines the link between neurophobia and the shortage of neurologists [6, 20].

Even though neurological research and practice are gaining traction, with newer therapeutic and diagnostic approaches [21], many medical students and trainees are reluctant to chart a career path in the neurosciences, and many internists and physicians still have limited knowledge of the basic principles of neurologic diagnosis and treatment [9, 11, 17, 22]. Notably, neurophobia is not only prevalent among medical students and trainees but also among all cadres of healthcare professionals and support staff [23–25]. A recent survey from 15 African countries documented a prevalence of nearly 40% and noted that medical trainees experience fear or discomfort with the neurosciences [20]. Despite these grim statistics, a comprehensive understanding of the state of neurophobia among medical trainees and professionals and its implications for care and research in LMICs is however unknown [22, 24, 26]. To address this, we conducted a systematic review to determine the burden of neurophobia in LMICs. Additionally, we explored its implications for research and clinical care, documenting the risk factors and assessing potential strategies to overcome this problem.

Methods

Ethical approval and informed consent were not applicable in this study because all data used were collected from previously published studies and were anonymous. We followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines to report this study [27]. The protocol for this review was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024626463) [28].

Data sources and search strategy

We searched PubMed, EMBASE, CINAHL, Scopus, Google Scholar, and African Journals Online (AJOL) for publications from inception till December 2024. Grey literature, including dissertations, theses, and reports, was also searched through institutional repositories and relevant online platforms. We also screened the references of related publications to identify additional relevant studies. Three researchers (O.A., T.O., O.S.) independently searched the databases.

Definition of low and lower middle-income countries (LMICs)

This included all countries classified as Low-Income Countries or Lower-Middle-Income Countries by the World Bank countries and lending group database. Low-income countries were defined as those with a Gross National Income (GNI) per capita of $1,085 or less, while Lower-Middle-Income Countries referred to those with a GNI per capita from $1,086 to $4,255 [29].

Study selection

Zotero was used to manage the records imported from each database. Duplicated publications were initially identified and removed using DOI numbers, and the remaining records were manually double-checked to ensure accuracy. Titles and abstracts were screened first, and the potentially eligible studies subsequently underwent full-text reviews. Three researchers (O.A., T.O., and S.O.) independently selected studies, and any disagreements were discussed with a fourth researcher (O.V.O.).

Quality assessment

Two independent reviewers, O.V.O. and O.A., conducted the quality assessment of the articles by employing the Joanna Briggs Institute’s (JBI’s) critical appraisal checklist for all types of studies [30]. The results of the two authors’ appraisals informed the final decision to include or exclude. Finally, we ranked the articles based on their methodological quality, calculated as the proportion of questions from the JBI’s critical appraisal checklist marked as “yes”. (Table 1).

Data extraction

Data from selected studies were extracted and populated into a customized spreadsheet under the following headings: paper title, author information, year of publication, study participants, survey response rate, study design, sample size, prevalence, determinants and nature of neurophobia, and implications for neurology care and research. Three authors (O.A., T.O., and S.O.) independently reviewed and extracted data about the first author, year of publication, study design, sample size, study participants (medical students, residents, physicians), prevalence, determinants/risk factors, nature of neurophobia, and documented implications/consequences on clinical care/research. Any discrepancies during the extraction were resolved through discussions with a fourth author (O.V.O.).

Statistical analysis

The extracted data were exported to an MS Excel spreadsheet for analysis. We reviewed the reported prevalence of neurophobia among medical students, residents, and physicians. We also noted reported causes, solutions, and implications for neurology care and research in LMICs. Tables and figures were used to summarize and present the results.

Results

Description of studies

We identified a total of 131 records from PubMed (n = 25), AJOL (n = 6), EMBASE, CINAHL, Scopus, and Google Scholar (n = 100). Seven duplicates were removed. The remaining 124 records went through title and abstract reviews. A total of 91 records were excluded. Thirty-three (33) reports were sought for retrieval, thirty (30) of which were successfully retrieved and assessed for eligibility. Nineteen (19) studies were excluded following full-text reviews due to the following reasons: reviews (n = 3), letters (n = 3), and studies not conducted in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) (n = 13). Therefore, eleven (11) records were deemed eligible after full-text review. Eight (8) additional papers were identified by screening the reference lists of the included studies; however, four (4) of these were excluded due to setting and report type. Ultimately, fifteen (15) studies were included in the current analysis (Fig. 1). The included studies were conducted in twenty-three (23) countries (Tables 2 and 3), covering the period from inception to 2024. Five (5) studies were conducted in Africa, five (5) in Asia, four (4) in Latin America, and one (1) in the Caribbean (Fig. 2). The details of the 15 eligible studies are summarized in Table 2.

Respondent characteristics

Out of the 131 records found, 14 cross-sectional studies and 1 longitudinal study were included. The sample sizes ranged from 41 to 827, with a total sample size of 4,430 (3,629 medical students and 801 postgraduate trainees and interns). The majority of the studies (n = 9, 60%) sampled only undergraduate medical students [31–34, 37–39, 41, 42]; two (13.3%) sampled doctors and postgraduate trainees [36, 40], including interns, non-specialist doctors, and internal medicine residents; three studies (20%) sampled students and doctors [20, 22, 35]; and one study (6.7%) was carried out among undergraduate and postgraduate students of physical therapy [25]. Only one study was conducted among family physician specialist trainees [40]. Additionally, only one study involved preclinical medical students [34]. Covering 23 countries across 4 continents, 5 of the studies (33.3%) were conducted in Africa [20, 31–33, 41], five (33.3%) in Asia [22, 25, 35, 39, 40], one (6.7%) in the Caribbean [42], and four (26.7%) in Latin America [34, 36–38]. Among the countries, Nigeria had the highest representation in the included studies (n= 4, 23.5%) [20, 31, 32, 41]. Two of the studies were originally written in Spanish [36, 38] and translated into English for this review; the other studies were written in English from the outset.

Prevalence of neurophobia

As shown in Fig. 3, the prevalence of neurophobia was as low as 6.25% among clinical medical students in Peru [38] to as high as 91.4% among physical therapy students in Pakistan [25]. Among doctors, the rate ranged from 41.7% among non-specialist doctors in Sri Lanka [35] to 84.0% among Malaysian family medicine specialist trainees [40], with internal medicine trainees in Brazil at 54.0% [36]. Neurophobia increased from the preclinical stage to the clinical stage and from the first year of clinical school to the second and third years, with no significant differences due to age, with older students holding more unfavorable opinions than younger students concerning the likelihood of pursuing a career in neuroscience [31, 37]. Some studies did not directly assess the prevalence of neurophobia but rather compared the perceived difficulty of neurology to that of other medical specialties, including cardiology, respiratory medicine, endocrinology, and gastroenterology [39, 41]. Others assessed the prevalence of neurophobia in addition to comparing the perception of neurology to those of other medical specialties [20, 35, 39, 40]. One Latin American study made a temporal comparison of data and found that, over eight years, neurophobia had worsened, with more than a quarter of participants (28.1%) reporting marked neurophobia [34]. A longitudinal study interestingly observed that self-reported neurophobia worsened after the completion of a neuroscience course, from 19% to 26% [42]. Of the fifteen studies, only one explored neurophobia within the sub-category or sub-context of other neuroscience courses such as neurology, neurosurgery, neuropsychiatry, pediatric neurology, or neuroradiology [41]. Determinants of neurophobia

The determinants of neurophobia are outlined in Table 3. The most consistent factors contributing to neurophobia were the need to learn neuroanatomy and neurophysiology [20, 22, 33–35, 37, 41], complex clinical examination [31, 34, 35, 37], a large number of rare, complex, and difficult-to-diagnose diseases [22, 31, 32, 35, 37, 41] and poor quality or lack of teaching and exposure [22, 25, 31–34, 41]. Others include limited exposure to neurological patients [32, 34, 37, 38, 41], short duration of courses and postings [34, 37], reputation for being difficult [32, 34], lack of definitive treatment for a good number of conditions [33, 34], poor outcomes and having more difficulty dealing emotionally with patients [33], lack of integration between basic neuroscience and clinical neurology [22], female gender [20, 25], fear of making mistakes and lack of confidence in neurological assessment skills [25], and lack of interest in and poor knowledge of neurology course work [31, 32, 40]. Particularly among doctors, lack of basic neurology knowledge and the need to know basic neuroscience [36, 40], self-declared phobia toward neurology or the neurosciences [40], practicing in the government sector [40], and the complexity of clinical examination [40] were reported to encourage neurophobia. Atif et al., nevertheless, found that there was no significant association between any one factor and neurophobia and surmised that neurophobia is a multifaceted problem [25].

Implications for care and proposed solutions

Most studies (n = 10) did not directly evaluate the clinical implications of neurophobia. Among those that did, the principal concern was reduced interest in neurology as a career. In Sudan, up to 31% of participants reported that they would not consider specialising in neurology [33] A Latin American study similarly highlighted a critically low number of neurologists, particularly in underserved regions, with implications for both service delivery and specialist training. Together, these findings suggest that neurophobia may contribute to workforce shortages and, consequently, gaps in neurological care [37].

Proposed solutions centre on strengthening neurology education through increased and improved clinical exposure (e.g., bedside teaching and patient contact), structured case-based discussions, and better integration of basic neuroscience with clinical practice [22, 31, 33–35, 37, 39, 41] Enhancing teaching resources—including online and audiovisual tools—and involving neurologists in preclinical neuroanatomy teaching have also been recommended. Notably, a longitudinal study by Shiels et al. [42] found that case-based learning significantly improved neurology learning outcomes, whereas problem-based and team-based approaches did not, underscoring the importance of targeted instructional design.

Discussion

In this review, we systematically reviewed studies carried out in LMICs, home to approximately six billion persons with high neurology disease burden and fragile healthcare systems. We assessed and analyzed the nature of neurophobia across four metrics, difficulty, interest, confidence, and knowledge, as well as the factors that contribute to it. We also explored the implications for care and research and proposed solutions to improve the teaching of neurosciences. Our findings highlight a highly variable burden of neurophobia among not only medical students (6.25% − 91.4%) but also medical doctors (41.7%−84%) in LMICs. Although neurophobia is a common phenomenon worldwide, our findings indicate that the prevalence may be higher in LMICs than HICs. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported a pooled prevalence of 31% among medical students and postgraduate doctors in Europe, including the UK, Spain, Lithuania, and Ireland. [24].

The prevalence of neurophobia, from our review, varied widely between 6.25% (in Latin America) [38] to 91.4% (in the Middle East) [25]. Several factors may explain this wide discrepancy. First, methodological variation likely played a major role, including differences in study design, sample size, sampling strategy, and the operational definition of neurophobia. Some studies relied on self-reported perceived difficulty relative to other specialties, whereas others used composite scoring systems incorporating confidence, interest, and knowledge, thereby capturing broader constructs. Second, differences in curricula, timing of neurology exposure, and availability of neurologists as educators may influence learner perceptions. Third, health system capacity and workforce density vary substantially across LMIC regions, potentially shaping both exposure to neurological cases and attitudes toward the specialty. Cultural perceptions of neurology as a “complex” or “diagnostically intimidating” field may also differ across contexts. The determinants of neurophobia, however, appeared to be consistent across study settings, ranging from specific individual-level characteristics, to educational, sociocultural and health system-related factors. This is similar to findings from a recent meta-analysis [43], which documented a pooled prevalence of 47.2% overall, 52.3% among medical students and 41.9% among residents/physicians [43]. The low prevalence of 6.25% seen in the Peruvian study [38] contrasts with the overall pattern reported in other studies in LMICs, although it is similar to a cross-sectional multicentric study from Mexico among resident physicians in emergency service [44]. Although no clear reasons have been given to explain this discrepancy, the Peruvian study was conducted in a single institution with a relatively small sample (n= 112), limiting the generalizability of its findings. Notably, site-specific factors, such as teaching approaches and curriculum design, may underlie students’ perceptions of neurology across institutions. Besides, the higher prevalence of neurophobia observed in another Latin American population [36, 37], suggests that the low prevalence among Peruvian medical students may not be indicative of a wider regional trend.

Many of the studies reviewed focused on assessing attitudes towards neurology and making comparisons with other medical specialties. Neurology was often found to be the most challenging of all medical specialties, consistent with findings from high-income countries [16, 24, 45, 46]. Sanya et al. considered other neuroscience specialties, such as psychiatry and neurosurgery, and found that there was greater interest in psychiatry than in its neurological siblings. [41] The need to understand neuroanatomy and neurophysiology, along with the implied difficulty in doing so, was the most cited reason for the perceived difficulty with learning neurology, followed closely by the complexity of neurological clinical examination [31, 34, 35, 37], and those who reported such difficulty were noted to be uninterested in pursuing a neuroscience-related career, reported having poor knowledge of neuroscience, and, consequently, could not manage neurological patients with as much confidence as they could other medical patients [31, 40]. Rodrigues et al., nevertheless, observed that although perceived difficulty and interest may worsen with advancement in training, knowledge and confidence in approaching neurological cases can improve [34]. Comparatively, studies in HICs reveal that students find neurology difficult for similar reasons, including complex clinical examinations, bad teaching experience, limited patient exposure, and the significant emotional toil of dealing with neurological patients [45, 47]. But although these foremost reasons for neurophobia are not exactly unique to the LMICs, they seem to exert a greater impact in these countries due to the peculiar constraints in resources and expertise amid fragile healthcare infrastructure.

The perceived difficulty and declining interest in neurology from the preclinical through the clinical to the internship stages of training may be a pointer to ineffective pedagogical methods, which often fail to improve interest and boost confidence. The fact that many study participants who were new to the neurosciences received poorly taught neuroanatomy and neurophysiology, often from non-neurologists, suggests a negative first impression that seems to persist throughout the rest of their studies, as they also deal with insufficient exposure to neurological patients, short clinical clerkships, and inadequate teaching durations [32, 34, 37, 38, 41] This impression may also be affected by other aspects of teachers’ attitude, besides their competence, including communication, ethics, willingness to provide individual support, and ability to evoke trust. These have been positively correlated with students’ confidence, motivation, and satisfaction in learning [48]. Ultimately, without effective learning interventions, they carry that impression into their professional years as members of the healthcare team. Although the impact of teachers’ attitudes on students’ perceptions of learning in general has been demonstrated at the primary, secondary, and university levels, as well as across specific subjects and fields, it has been largely unexplored in neurology and other neuroscience specialties. [48, 49].

Shiels and colleagues [42] observed that case-based teaching was key to improving the learning experiences of students in neuroscience courses, a finding that aligns with the views of other authors. [47, 50, 51] McGovern et al., in a Paris study, provided further evidence of the efficacy of case-based teaching with roleplay in a non-judgemental environment, showing that it reduced the number of students with neurophobia by 22.3% [51]. The use of case-based learning, flipped classroom approaches, and simulation teaching tools holds promise for tackling neurophobia in many LMICs, given the relative lack of neurology-dedicated faculty and weak diagnostic or interventional capabilities [52]. Improving teaching methods, simplifying the neurology learning experience and providing adequate time for learning will ultimately improve impressions about neurology or, at the very least, reduce the persistence of negative impressions [18, 45–47, 50, 53, 54]. Some authors have proposed a simplified “bare essentials” neuroscience course that presents only the clinically relevant aspects of neuroanatomy, neurophysiology, and neuropathology structured in a continuous clinical context. [53, 54] This can directly address the reported diagnostic complexity while providing the option of an extended neuroscience course with broader, deeper academic objectives for neurophiliacs. Additionally, undergraduate texts may be simplified to expunge complex diagnostic formulations and rare diagnoses while focusing on simplified protocols for effective care. [23].

The field of neuroscience may be intrinsically appealing [18] to some medical students and trainees as it offers ample opportunities to impact lives [55]. However, the phenomenom of neurophobia limits interest in the specialty with a burden which appears to be more multifactorial rather than attributable to a single cause. While gaps in knowledge and understanding of the neurosciences are frequently implicated, the available evidence does not establish poor knowledge as the sole or definitive cause [42]. For example, Lukas et al. [39] reported that higher self-perceived knowledge was associated with greater confidence and an increased likelihood of considering neurology as a career, suggesting that knowledge and confidence may synergistically play contributory roles, rather than as isolated determinants. Similarly, while several educational strategies—such as bedside teaching, structured case discussions, longer clinical exposure, improved integration of basic and clinical neuroscience, and enhanced use of teaching aids [22, 31, 33–35, 37, 39, 41]—have been proposed, current evidence does not conclusively demonstrate that these interventions directly reduce neurophobia. Instead, they represent plausible and contextually supported approaches that may improve learners’ experience, confidence, perceived competence, and engagement with neurology. Future longitudinal and interventional studies will be needed to clarify the causal pathways underlying neurophobia and to determine the most effective educational strategies to mitigate the burden.

Limitations and strengths

The studies included in this review are largely heterogeneous, given the nature of participants, varying study designs, and definitions of neurophobia. All but one of the included studies were cross-sectional, depriving this review of a more robust assessment of the implications of neurophobia over time. Most of the study participants were from urban/academic settings and, therefore, this review has not adequately considered rural practitioners as well as community health workers, who may be the first line of care in some resource-limited settings. In addition, there was no representation of some LMIC regions in this review, such as Central Asia and the Pacific Islands, with underrepresentation from other regions like the Middle East and Southeast Asia. However, these limitations notwithstanding, exploring the burden of neurophobia in LMICs, home to about 80% of the world’s population, represents a key initial step in addressing the burden of neurologic diseases as a public health concern and can provide necessary impetus to strengthen neuroscience education and support broader reforms in neurological healthcare delivery in the region [56, 57]. Our study also boasts of a robust methodology, giving a comprehensive understanding and summary of the evidence on the burden of neurophobia among not only medical students but also a unique population of healthcare workers, who remain the bedrock of the neurology care continuum [56, 57].

Conclusion

Neurophobia has varying prevalence in LMICs and may have implications for the provision of quality and timely neurology care. To tackle neurophobia, extant health policy, educational, and clinical training initiatives should be geared towards implementing and sustaining evidence-based strategies to improve clinical exposure and case-based learning. This will be critical to ensuring a steady stream of neurology workforce and, consequently, may help bridge the neurology clinical care and research gap in LMICs. Future studies should consider neurophobia among other neuroscience specialties and clinical care support staff, further explore its implications on neurology care and research, and assess the effectiveness of proposed interventions in a longitudinal design.
