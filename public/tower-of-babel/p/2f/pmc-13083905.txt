The use of screening tests in panel studies to monitor cognitive functioning in senior participation programme groups using ACE-III and M-ACE
Authors: Beata Kaczmarek, Zofia Ilkowska-Adamczewska, Agnieszka Remlinger-Molenda, Aleksandra Kaluzniak-Szymanowska, Katarzyna Stachnik, Katarzyna Wieczorowska-Tobis, Slawomir Tobis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUsing a longitudinal approach, this study compares cognitive screening tests (MMSE, ACE-III, M-ACE) in older patients. A total of 138 participants (112 women, 26 men; mean age 73.2 ± 7.4 years) underwent two examinations with a 13-month interval. Status change between health, MCI, and dementia categories was analysed. Significant shifts in categorisation were observed for MMSE and ACE-III (survey 1: χ² = 9.03, p = 0.0027; survey 2: χ² = 4.00, p = 0.0455), with more participants scoring lower on ACE-III. Similar shifts occurred for M-ACE (survey 1: χ² = 11.28, p = 0.0008; survey 2: χ² = 4.36, p = 0.0367). The ROC curve showed high AUC values for ACE-III and M-ACE (0.892 ± 0.031; 0.908 ± 0.027, respectively), with consistent sensitivity and specificity (p = 0.1516; p = 0.4534, respectively). ACE-III and M-ACE exhibited high sensitivity and specificity compared to MMSE with significant transitions indicating a high-risk group for dementia.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, Neurocognitive disorders, Dementia, Screening testsSubject terms: Cognitive ageing, Alzheimer's disease

Introduction

More and more effective methods of treatment, advances in technology and better living conditions are all prolonging human life. As a result, the population of older persons is growing systematically. WHO reports that in 2030, one in six people will be over 60 years old. The number of people aged 80 + is predicted to triple over the next 25 years1. Ensuring proper care for the ageing population constitutes a challenge in both social and economic terms. The 2024 Ageing Report estimates that ageing-related expenditure will increase from 25,1% of GDP in 2022 to 26,5% in 2070 in the Euro area2. Research shows that intensified medical support initiatives and participation programmes for older persons can limit the rising numbers of people living with neurocognitive disorders3,4. Livingston concludes in The Lancet (2024)5 that there is a range of initiatives that, if implemented effectively, could reduce the risk of cognitive deficits or delay them. However, adopting such strategies requires an increased social awareness of risk factors and their countermeasures and a means to identify cognitive disorders early enough to introduce preventive measures and remedial actions. There are several methods of early screening diagnostics of cognitive functioning. Researchers continue to search for tools most suited to a given population—patient and medical staff-friendly, available, quick and characterised by high sensitivity and specificity6. Most popular screening tests in Poland include: Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE)7 and Clock-Drawing Test8, Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination (ACE-III), and its shorter version, Mini Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination (M-ACE)9,10, Mini-Cog11, Montreal Cognitive Assessment MoCA, Short Test of Mental Status, (STMS)12, and the Saint Louis University Mental Status Examination (SLUMS)13. These tests have been adapted into Polish; however, there is little research on their usefulness for panel studies in older persons14,15. Previous research has shown that both ACE-III and M-ACE are characterised by high sensitivity and specificity compared to MMSE in detecting symptoms of neurocognitive disorders early16. The following study presents a longitudinal analysis and further findings on the use of these tools across a group of patients at a geriatric health centre and participants of senior support and participation programmes organised at a home hospice in Poznan, Poland.

Objectives

The study aimed to compare the sensitivity and specificity in Polish versions of the most often applied screening tests used to examine cognitive functions in older patients (MMSE, ACE-III, M-ACE). Moreover, the research examined the tests’ predictive potential based on the real-time progression of neurocognitive disorders, which was measured in two separate assessments (surveys). Features of each screening test were compared based on the results obtained during the assessments (on average, 13 months apart). Next, shifts in result categorisations between norm and below norm (healthy/diseased) were analysed, considering also a third category – mild cognitive impairment (MCI) in terms of descriptive statistics. Finally, both assessments were compared and juxtaposed.

Methods

The study was conducted based on an analysis of the psychological and medical records of individuals who were referred for psychological assessment by a geriatrician within the publicly funded geriatric outpatient clinic at the WSWOP “Home Hospice” (Greater Poland Association of Volunteers in Palliative Care, Home Hospice); were referred for psychological assessment by other specialists as part of diagnostic programs conducted at the WSWOP “Home Hospice”, self-referred to participate in senior activation projects conducted at the WSWOP “Home Hospice” and underwent procedures for the screening diagnosis of cognitive functions. The Bioethical Committee of Poznan University of Medical Sciences (Poznan, Poland) confirmed that the study did not require bioethical approval or supervision. Our research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The authors confirm that informed consent was obtained from all subjects.

The study utilised test results for Polish language versions of the three most popular cognitive functioning screening tests.

MMSE – most frequently applied, an internationally recognised, interdisciplinary 30-point scale test; levels of cognitive impairment have been classified as follows: none (score = 24–30), mild (score = 18–24), severe (score = 0–17)17;

ACE-III – a 100-point test which allows initial differential diagnosis; with cut-off points: 88 and 82;

M-ACE – a shorter, 30-point version of the ACE-III test, with cut-off points: 21 or 2518.

Survey protocol

Each individual whose data were used in this study followed a consistent protocol, which included a structured interview on their educational and professional background, selected aspects of social functioning, health, and conditions that could impact cognitive functions. MMSE and ACE-III screening tests were conducted, while the M-ACE results were calculated based on the ACE-III results.

Statistical analysis of the test results collected in the course of two separate surveys

The study included 138 people who had undergone an assessment twice or more under a consistent protocol. Participant profiles were analysed for personal characteristics: gender (Mann-Whitney test), and age, education (years of schooling) and inter-survey interval (Spearman correlation coefficient).

Changes in result scores for MMSE, ACE-III, and M-ACE for each of the two separate surveys were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, which is typically used when measuring the same variable obtained under two different conditions.

McNemar’s test was used to analyse cross-category shifts (norm, below norm), focusing on status change patterns between two methods of measuring the same characteristic (e.g., MMSE vs. ACE III). Additionally, the sensitivity and specificity of each test between surveys 1 and 2 were compared using the ROC curve.

Analysing status change—norms, MCI and dementia—surveys compared and contrasted

The significance of the category status change pattern was calculated for each test, allowing for an MCI range as an important indicator of risk factors and further clinical treatment (Bowker-McNemar’s test).

Results

One hundred and thirty-eight participants qualified for the panel study, including 112 women (81.2%) and 26 men (18.8%). The average age was 73.2 ± 7.4 (71.5; 60–97). Women were younger than men to a statistically significant degree [W: aged 72.5 ± 7.0 (71.0; 60–97 years old) vs. M: aged 76.1 ± 8.5 (77.0; 62–95 years old); p < 0.05]. On average, the time interval between surveys 1 and 2 amounted to 13 months, while the panel study spanned three years.

Discussion

The analysis focused on changes for each test score between survey 1 and 2. ACE-III and M-ACE were the only tests where scores yielded a statistically significant change between the surveys (p < 0.01 and p < 0.05, respectively). Notably, the score results increased in time in ACE-III and M-ACE, with the visual-spatial score being the only significant change in ACE-III. However, no change was observed in the MMSE score results between surveys 1 and 2. Nearly one-third of the participants experienced a fall in MMSE score over time, while one-third experienced no such shifts between surveys 1 and 2. A rise in the MMSE score results was observed for slightly over one-third of participants. In contrast, a study by Mungas et al. (2001)19, where the second MMSE survey followed two years after the initial test, showed that the dynamics of deterioration of cognitive functioning in patients with AD fell with age. In people with vascular dementia, cognitive deterioration proceeded faster for above 80-year-olds19.

Further analysis showed that 37% of participants experienced a drop in ACE-III score results over time. No change in score between the surveys was observed only in (8%) of participants. For over half the participants, the ACE-III score increased over time, which could be related to a reversible character of some discrete deficits or result from ongoing treatment and cognitive stimulation that activated cognitive reserve. In contrast, one in three participants showed a fall in the M-ACE score between the surveys, 12.3% noted no shift in scores, while 52.2% showed an increase in M-ACE scores over time. One possible explanation is that the time interval between the initial and final survey, spanning on average 13 months, is far too short for any deficits to progress noticeably. Healthy people or those living with minor cognitive disorders might have been motivated to improve their functioning, such as clock reading, or to boost their follow-up performance in attention and orientation tasks by exercise. Moreover, as many patients look for medical help not only in geriatric clinics but also in various institutions, it is very likely they might have completed the screening tests multiple times and have simply learnt to perform well in the tests. This practice effect is the greater, the more popular a test is (such as MMSE), but also pertains, to an extent, to those diagnostic tools that are less common, such as ACE-III and M-ACE, as these tests contain activities analogous to the MMSE tasks. In ACE-III and M-ACE, the effect is mitigated by further sufficiently different procedures, which reduce the practice effect. Another factor contributing to the overall test score is related to the psychological aspects of engaging in health and wellness programmes that seniors come across in senior centres. Visiting a geriatric centre allows people to learn about various forms of therapy and exercise, and many decide to actively take part in the activities. Primarily, people with milder forms of cognitive impairment or dementia, provided with holistic support, as well as their caregivers, act to forestall social isolation, depression, physical inactivity, and the onset of somatic conditions, including diabetes. The list comprises factors considered by The Lancet (Livingstone et al., 2024)5 to be the most important potentially modifiable risk factors for dementia. Thus, it seems that those participants who have scored lower on their second or further survey should be particularly cared for as their deteriorating performance can point to progressive deficits, which can no longer be compensated by their cognitive reserve. Significantly higher scores obtained by participants in the second assessment (3 individuals in the MMSE and 2 in the ACE‑III), shifting from the category of severe impairment corresponding to dementia to the normal range, may also be explained by the presence of incidental difficulties on the day of the first assessment. Such factors include stress, transient malaise not reported during the interview, accidental omission of medication, and other modifiable factors influencing the current cognitive state.

The study showed ACE-III to be a valuable tool in differential diagnosis not only of dementia and the normal cognition but in identifying mild cognitive impairments, too20,21. This study analysed status change patterns over a period of time for three diagnostic categories. The results were as follows: for MMSE, 10.8% of participants experienced a shift into a lower category. Out of those 10.8%, most moved by one category, except for one, who showed a fall from the norm to dementia. Similarly, 5.8% of people shifted into a lower category in ACE-III. Simultaneously, in M-ACE test results, the category was lowered for 8.7% of the participants, mostly from the norm to MCI. The analysed relations between the variables over time suggest no statistically significant correlations between gender, age, years of schooling or time gap between each survey and different scores in MMSE, ACE-III and M-ACE. Available references, including the meta-analysis by Arevalo-Rodriguez et al. (2015)22, mention no satisfactory evidence to support the claim that MMSE alone could serve to sufficiently verify whether cognitive disorders are indeed progressing, moving from the norm to dementia in panel studies. In regard to ACE-III and M-ACE, there appear to be few panel studies (despite the need thereof), yet their results report screening tools as promising23.

The current study has several limitations. First, it verifies the correlations only between three tests out of multiple available diagnostic and screening tools. Still, all three diagnostic tools were used for each participant in a coherent procedure to avoid taxing or lengthy test procedures. Moreover, M-ACE was conducted as part of ACE-III, and the score for M-ACE was calculated based on the ACE-III score. It is a recognised and accepted procedure; however, it does not allow conclusions to be absolutely based on the M-ACE score when used independently. Even though published research reports the use of corresponding test procedures in a row, repeated use of the same procedure might cause a practice effect, thus falsifying research results24.

Furthermore, the study was conducted in a geriatric centre where seniors can also participate in activities. Study participants were recruited from a group of people who worried about their cognitive deficits, as well as from active and healthy persons who took part in senior participation programmes. On the one hand, this allows a broader interpretation of the study results. Still, on the other hand, the research location cannot be classified as either a primary healthcare point or a specialist point because it is both. Due to limited access to in-depth diagnostic tools, including neuroimaging, it was impossible to verify the sensitivity of each test for every participant with pathophysiological dementia. Nevertheless, the scores of each test were compared to the results of a screening test standard, namely MMSE. Although this approach cannot clearly discriminate which test is characterised by the highest degree of sensitivity, it can rank the sensitivity of a less popular newer test in relation to the well-established MMSE.

In our study, women were both numerically predominant and significantly younger than men. This distribution reflects the actual demographic of patients utilising geriatric outpatient services (particularly for psychological assessment) and their willingness to engage in senior activation programmes. Beyond demographic variables (e.g., longer female life expectancy), cultural factors—such as reluctance to seek professional mental health support due to stigma, especially among older men—also contribute25. Additional determinants include higher resilience, greater openness to disclose difficulties and seek help, more health-promoting behaviours, and stronger social engagement in women, recognised as one of twelve dementia-prevention factors in the Lancet report by Livingston et al.26. Nevertheless, this sample composition limits the generalisability and representativeness of the study findings.

To facilitate early detection of cognitive functioning deficits, it is imperative to continue developing high-accuracy screening tools for cognitive disorders. The tests must be characterised by high sensitivity and specificity while being quick, patient-friendly and free. The study showed that the three tests lend themselves to being recommended as such, as they meet the above-mentioned criteria. M-ACE is suggested for quick screening tests in first contact points, while ACE-III remains a recommended targeted test meant to complement the initial differential diagnosis and to be used in specialised health centres. An accurate diagnosis supports holistic health care, which includes maintaining cognitive reserve, encouraging wellness activities and administering appropriate treatment. Monitoring cognitive state over time using comparable screening tools will allow health professionals and caregivers to react flexibly to uphold the well-being of people at risk of dementia.

Conclusions

The panel study revealed that the sensitivity and specificity of both ACE-III and M-ACE, compared to MMSE, were very high for surveys 1 and 2. The use of the three tests in panel studies is well-founded.
