Pluripotent stem cell-derived chimeric antigen receptor-natural killer cells targeting epidermal growth factor receptor 2 for cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Jongsuk Han, Chaeyeon Jin, Sae-Byeok Hwang, In Jee Lee, Young Seok Baek, Daun Jung, Ki Yeon Kim, Shoukhrat Mitalipov, Ji Hyang Kim, Hee Jung An, Yeonmi Lee, Eunju Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChimeric antigen receptor-natural killer (CAR-NK) cells are emerging as a promising platform for allogeneic, cell-based immunotherapy. Among available sources, NK cells derived from pluripotent stem cells (PSCs) provide a renewable and scalable option that overcomes the limitations of primary NK cells. Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), a membrane protein frequently overexpressed in many solid tumors, is an attractive target for cancer immunotherapy. In this study, we designed a green fluorescent protein (GFP)-linked anti-HER2 CAR construct and introduced it into PSCs via lentiviral transduction. Three stable PSC-derived clones (CAR-A, CAR-B, and CAR-C) were established, each co-expressing anti-HER2 CAR with GFP. After differentiation under xeno-free and feeder-free culture conditions, CAR expression was maintained in NK cells. The resulting PSC-derived CAR-NK cells displayed phenotypic and functional features comparable to wild-type PSC-derived NK cells, while showing markedly enhanced cytotoxicity against HER2-positive cancer cell lines. These findings demonstrated the potential of the use of PSC-derived anti-HER2 CAR-NK cells as a robust and scalable immunotherapy. In addition, this platform could be extended to produce CAR-NK cells directed against a wide range of tumor-associated antigens.Keywords: Cancer immunotherapy, Chimeric antigen receptors, Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2, Natural killer cells, Pluripotent stem cells

INTRODUCTION

Traditional cancer treatments such as surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy often result in relapse and considerable toxicity. To address these limitations, immune cell-based therapies have been developed. Among them, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) technology, which employs genetically engineered receptors that recognize tumor-specific antigens, has revolutionized adoptive cell therapy (1). CAR-T cells are already approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for treating B-cell leukemia and lymphoma. However, their clinical application is limited by severe adverse effects, including cytokine release syndrome (CRS), graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity, as well as the need for patient-specific manufacturing (2, 3). These challenges have prompted the exploration of alternative CAR-based immunotherapies, with natural killer (NK) cells emerging as a promising option.

NK cells are innate immune effectors that eliminate target cells via granzyme B- and perforin-mediated cytotoxicity, similar to T cells (4). Despite their therapeutic promise, NK cells have a short lifespan that necessitates repeated dosing and exhibit lower transduction efficiency compared to T cells (5, 6). Nevertheless, NK cells confer advantages in allogeneic transplantation because they carry a reduced risk of GVHD and other immune-related toxicities (7).

Several sources are used to generate NK cells, including peripheral blood (PB), umbilical cord blood (UCB), the NK-92 cell line, and pluripotent stem cells (PSCs). Most CAR-NK studies rely on PB-derived NK (PB-NK) cells or NK-92 cells. PB-NK cells are relatively accessible and exhibit a mature phenotype with strong cytotoxicity, but are limited by donor heterogeneity and difficulties in genetic modification (8). Moreover, primary NK cells such as PB-NK and UCB-derived NK (UCB-NK) cells display relatively poor transduction efficiency (8-10). NK-92 cells provide a homogeneous and genetically tractable population, yet they must be irradiated before clinical use to prevent tumorigenicity (8).

In contrast, PSC-derived NK cells present several advantages. PSCs have unlimited proliferative capacity, allowing the establishment of stable, clonally derived NK cell lines. Although PSC-derived NK cells show an immature phenotype, low CD16 expression, and require extended culture (11), they achieve high transduction efficiency at the PSC stage (9). Introducing CAR at this stage eliminates the need for repeated transduction after NK cell differentiation, producing a homogeneous CAR-expressing population (9). Furthermore, PSC-derived NK cells have been reported to demonstrate superior cytotoxicity relative to PB-NK cells, supporting their value as a renewable and scalable source for CAR-based immunotherapy (12).

Research on PSC-derived NK cells has recently advanced toward strategies that enhance therapeutic performance through combination with monoclonal antibodies (13, 14). One notable example is the introduction of a high-affinity, non-cleavable CD16 into induced PSCs (iPSCs), which has been shown to markedly increase antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) in combination with monoclonal antibodies such as rituximab in mouse models (14). This finding illustrates the broad applicability of PSC-derived NK cells.

Beyond antibody-dependent approaches, CAR technology, which provides NK cells with the ability to directly recognize tumor antigens, has become a major focus of contemporary research. CARs have evolved through successive generations based on the design of their signaling domains (15). First-generation CARs, consisting only of CD3ζ, provided proof-of-concept but limited clinical efficacy. Second-generation CARs incorporated a single co-stimulatory domain, such as CD28 or 4-1BB, which improved cell proliferation and persistence. Third-generation CARs combined two co-stimulatory domains to theoretically achieve stronger activation. The most recent fourth-generation CARs (TRUCKs), built on second-generation structures and additionally secreting cytokines such as interleukin (IL)-15, enable them to overcome the tumor microenvironment and activate surrounding immune cells.

Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) is frequently overexpressed in solid tumors, including breast, gastric, liver, and colorectal cancers, making it an attractive target for CAR-based therapies (16). While CAR-NK therapies have been developed against several tumor-associated antigens, the generation of PSC-derived CAR-NK cells targeting HER2 has not yet been reported.

In this study, we introduced an anti-HER2 CAR gene into a PSC line that had previously been shown to differentiate into NK cells exhibiting potent cytotoxicity against tumor cell lines (17). We then differentiated these PSCs into NK cells under xeno-free and feeder-free conditions to establish a homogeneous CAR-expressing NK cell population. Finally, we evaluated the cytotoxicity of these cells against HER2-positive cancer cell lines, thereby demonstrating their potential as a scalable and effective immunotherapeutic strategy for solid tumors.

RESULTS

Lentiviral transduction of PSCs resulted in stable expression of anti-HER2 CAR

Among available PSC lines, we selected human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) because they had previously been reported to produce NK cells with high cytotoxicity against cancer cell lines (17). The experimental workflow involved lentiviral transduction of hESCs with a CAR-expressing vector, followed by puromycin selection. The resulting CAR-expressing PSCs (CAR-PSCs) were differentiated into NK cells, and their phenotype and cytotoxic activity were analyzed against tumor cells (Fig. 1A).

To generate stable CAR-expressing hESCs (CAR-ESCs), we designed a green fluorescent protein (GFP)-linked anti-HER2 CAR construct containing a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) specific for HER2, a CD8 hinge domain, a CD28 transmembrane domain, and a CD3ζ intracellular signaling domain. After lentiviral transduction, successful gene delivery was confirmed via fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 1B). Three distinct fluorescent colonies were manually selected and designated as CAR-A, CAR-B, and CAR-C. Flow cytometry confirmed that all three CAR-ESC clones expressed GFP and anti-HER2 CAR at rates above 90% (Fig. 1C). Taken together, these findings indicated successful transduction of CAR into hESCs, with engineered CAR-ESCs maintaining undifferentiated ESC morphology while stably expressing high levels of CAR.

Characterization of anti-HER2 CAR-ESCs

To determine whether CAR gene transduction altered ESC pluripotency, we assessed the expression of pluripotency markers at mRNA and protein levels. When CAR-ESCs were compared with wild-type ESCs (WT-ESCs), no significant differences were observed at the mRNA level (Fig. 2A). Protein-level analysis also confirmed the expression of pluripotency markers in CAR-ESCs (Fig. 2B). In addition, karyotyping verified a normal chromosomal complement in CAR-ESCs (Fig. 2C). Taken together, these results demonstrated that CAR gene introduction did not disrupt ESC pluripotency or chromosomal stability.

NK cells differentiated from ESCs showed phenotypic maturity

To derive NK cells from ESCs, we adapted and simplified the standard differentiation protocol (Fig. 3A). Our goal was to establish a cost-effective protocol for NK cell differentiation. The major change from the conventional protocol was the substitution of UM729 for IL-7 and FLT-3L during the induction phase of NK cell differentiation. To optimize the protocol, we assessed different combinations of IL-7, FLT-3L, and UM729, along with SCF and IL-15, which are essential for NK cell differentiation (Fig. 3B).

We evaluated differentiation efficiency using three markers: CD56 and CD16, common NK surface markers, and CD3, which identifies T-cell contamination. The IL-7/FLT-3L group and the UM729 group showed comparable expression patterns (Fig. 3B). We further assessed the effects of the modified protocol on NK cell characteristics by analyzing their differentiation rate, morphology, and phenotypes (Fig. 3C-E). After 3 weeks, NK cell-like colonies formed at similar levels in both groups (Fig. 3C). During the 4 weeks of differentiation using our modified protocol, the proportion of pure NK cells (CD56＋CD3−) steadily increased, exceeding 95% by the end of culture (Fig. 3D). The mature subset (CD56＋CD16＋) also expanded, reaching 40%-60% of the total NK cell population (Fig. 3D). Surface receptor and cytolytic granule expression were comparable between the groups (Fig. 3E).

Transduction of the anti-HER2 CAR gene does not affect NK cell differentiation

To generate CAR-NK cells, NK cells were differentiated from the three anti-HER2 CAR-ESC clones (CAR-A, CAR-B, and CAR-C) using the modified protocol (Fig. 4A). CAR expression was detected in NK cells derived from all three clones (Fig. 4B). CAR and GFP expression levels were similar across the clones, and CAR-A was selected as a representative clone for further experiments. When CAR-A clones were induced to differentiate into hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) and then NK cells, their morphology resembled that of WT-ESC-derived cells (Fig. 4C). Both WT-ESC- and CAR-ESC-derived NK cells displayed comparable differentiation potential (Fig. 4D). These results showed that NK cells derived from either WT-ESCs or CAR-ESCs differentiated efficiently into NK cells without producing T-cell populations.

Transduction of the anti-HER2 CAR gene did not affect the phenotype of differentiated NK cells

We further investigated whether CAR gene introduction through lentiviral transduction altered the phenotypes of ESC-derived NK cells. To this end, we assessed the surface receptors and the production of cytolytic granules in the differentiated NK cells. ESC-derived HSPCs were cultured into NK cells for 4 weeks, and flow cytometry was used to analyze receptor expression and granule production (Fig. 5A-E). After 4 weeks, the expression of activating receptors such as NKG2D and NKp30 was comparable between CAR-ESC- and WT-ESC-derived NK cells. DNAM-1, NKp44, NKp46, and CD69 were also expressed at high levels, whereas NKG2C expression remained low, in both groups (Fig. 5A). The killer cell immunoglobulin-like receptors KIR2DL1 and KIR2DL2/L3/S2 were expressed at low levels in both cell types (Fig. 5B). Further analysis of co-stimulatory receptors 2B4 and FasL revealed that the former was expressed in both groups, and the expression level of the latter was low (Fig. 5C). Exhaustion markers, including LAG-3, TIGIT, and PD-1, were also present at low levels in both groups (Fig. 5D). Finally, both CAR- and WT-ESC-derived NK cells expressed the cytolytic granules granzyme B and perforin (Fig. 5E). Overall, no significant differences were observed in the expression of receptors or cytolytic granules between CAR-ESC-derived and WT-ESC-derived NK cells. These findings indicated that CAR transduction did not interfere with ESC differentiation into NK cells.

Anti-HER2 CAR-ESC-derived NK cells demonstrated superior in vitro antitumor activity

We next evaluated the antitumor capacity of CAR-ESC-derived NK cells against HER2＋ tumor cells. Cytotoxicity assays were performed on four target cell types with varying HER2 levels at effector-to-target (E:T) ratios of 1:1, 5:1, and 10:1, and cytotoxic activity was measured by flow cytometry. Target cells included ovarian cancer lines A2780cis and SKOV3 and breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-231 and SKBR3. HER2 was highly expressed on SKOV3 and SKBR3 cells but absent on A2780cis and MDA-MB-231 cells.

We first co-cultured NK cells from the three CAR clones with HER2＋ SKOV3 cells at an E:T ratio of 5:1 to compare their cytotoxicity. All clones showed similar activity, and no significant differences were observed (Fig. 6C). We then compared the cytotoxicity of CAR-ESC-derived NK cells with WT-ESC-derived NK cells. Against HER2＋ SKOV3 and SKBR3 cells, CAR-ESC-derived NK cells displayed significantly enhanced cytotoxicity. In contrast, cytotoxicity against HER2− A2780cis and MDA-MB-231 cells was similar between CAR-ESC- and WT-ESC-derived NK cells (Fig. 6D). Notably, at an E:T ratio of 10:1, CAR-ESC-derived NK cells lysed more than 90% of SKBR3 cells, confirming their strong cytotoxic potential. These results demonstrated that CAR-ESC-derived NK cells exhibit enhanced cytotoxicity against HER2-expressing ovarian and breast cancer cells.

DISCUSSION

CAR-NK cell-based immunotherapy carries a low risk of complications such as GVHD, CRS, and neurotoxicity in allogeneic transplantation, while retaining strong antigen recognition capacity (5-7, 9, 11, 18). In addition, CAR-NK cells can effectively infiltrate solid tissues (19) and respond to diverse antigens on tumor cells, making them suitable for treating a wide range of solid tumors, even those with immune evasion mechanisms (20). Thus, CAR-NK cells represent a promising therapeutic option for solid tumors. CAR-T and CAR-NK cells are clearly valuable platforms, each with distinct strengths and limitations, and the optimal choice depends on disease type and the patient’s condition. Notably, CAR-T cells have already produced striking clinical outcomes in hematologic malignancies and have been far more extensively studied.

CAR-NK cells are under investigation for multiple cancer types, and preliminary results are encouraging. Research on their use in solid tumors is expanding, with in vitro and in vivo studies underway (12, 21-25). However, studies on PSC-derived CAR-NK cells in this context remain scarce. Accordingly, the present study aimed to generate ESC-derived CAR-NK cells for solid tumor targeting and to assess their antitumor activity.

We established a clonal ESC line expressing anti-HER2 CAR, generated CAR-NK cells from these ESCs, and confirmed uniform CAR expression in the ESCs and their NK cell derivatives. The CAR-expressing ESCs maintained typical ESC morphology, indicating preservation of fundamental ESC characteristics, including proliferation and differentiation potential. Similarly, CAR-ESC-derived NK cells displayed the expected ESC-derived NK phenotype, while showing enhanced cytotoxicity against HER2＋ cancer cells compared to WT-ESC-derived NK cells. These findings suggested that the CARs expressed in CAR-ESC-derived NK cells specifically recognize HER2 and that CAR expression strengthens their antitumor activity.

In general, conventional methods for inducing NK cells from PSCs generally use SCF, IL-15, IL-7, and FLT-3L (12, 26, 27), combined with embryoid body (EB) formation or the use of xenogeneic/feeder cells to support efficient differentiation (12, 13, 28-30). In contrast, we developed a xeno- and feeder-free protocol that does not require EB generation and demonstrated that PSC-derived CAR-NK cells can be efficiently obtained under these conditions. A central feature of our method is replacing the costly recombinant proteins IL-7 and FLT-3L with the less expensive small molecule UM729, a change that could improve the cost-effectiveness for future large-scale production. Importantly, NK cells generated with this approach displayed CD16 expression levels comparable to those achieved using conventional cytokine combinations (26, 28, 31).

CD16 (Fcγ3IIIa) is an activating receptor that binds the IgG Fc region, mediating ADCC and influencing NK cell survival and proliferation. CD16 signaling also induces proinflammatory cytokine and chemokine production, which recruits and activates tumor-infiltrating immune cells (12, 32, 33).

The stable CD16 expression observed in our NK cells, therefore, suggested that they might augment antitumor activity when combined with therapeutic antibodies such as trastuzumab. This finding highlighted that, rather than requiring complex genetic modifications to introduce high-affinity, non-cleavable CD16a variants (14), our protocol offers a simpler and practical strategy to generate NK cells with a key ADCC phenotype.

Despite this advantage, a limitation remains: PSC-derived NK cells are less mature than primary NK cells. To overcome this immaturity and improve therapeutic efficacy, future efforts could supplement the differentiation process with cytokines such as IL-21 to promote final maturation and cytotoxicity or refine feeder-free maturation signals using tailored small molecule combinations.

This study reports the generation of ESC-derived NK cells that stably express anti-HER2 CAR. These NK cells maintained stable CAR expression and exhibited strong cytotoxicity against HER2-overexpressing cancer cells. Despite these promising findings, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, validation was performed exclusively in vitro. Given the complexity of the in vivo tumor microenvironment, further validation of our results using animal models is required to determine the clinical potential of our approach. Second, the study focused only on cancer cells with high HER2 expression and a negative control, rendering the efficacy against clinically relevant HER2-low tumors unclear. Third, functional analyses were largely restricted to cytotoxicity assays. Additional assessments, such as cytokine secretion (e.g., IFN-γ) and serial killing capacity, will be important for a more comprehensive characterization of CAR-NK cell function.

Future strategies to address these limitations and improve therapeutic efficacy could include the incorporation of next-generation CAR designs, following comprehensive in vitro and in vivo validation of our current CAR-NK cells. For instance, a fourth-generation “armored” CAR, which co-expresses cytokines such as IL-15 on a second-generation backbone, could be introduced to enhance NK cell persistence and activity in vivo. In addition, evaluation of cytotoxicity following PSC differentiation into NK cells, and subsequent selection of lines with high cytotoxicity for CAR transduction, is expected to further improve therapeutic efficacy. This approach can be applied not only to anti-HER2 CARs but also to CARs targeting other tumor-associated antigens, enabling the development of a broader platform. Such strategies could help maximize antitumor activity and reduce the likelihood of immune escape. Ultimately, CAR-NK cell therapy may become an important component of cancer immunotherapy in the future.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

IRB approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review boards of CHA Bundang Medical Center, CHA University (IRB no. NON2021-003), and CHA University (IRB no. 1044308-202105-LR-025-07).

Resource

The PSC line used in this study did not have an assigned Research Resource Identifier (RRID). It was originally derived and provided by Dr. Shoukhrat Mitalipov in 2017 under a material transfer agreement (as ES4) (34) and is registered with the Korea National Institute of Health (https://nih.go.kr), but no RRID is currently available in public registries. To ensure traceability, we have included complete provenance and registration details. The cells were independently authenticated using short tandem repeat profiling and mitochondrial genome sequencing, yielding a 100% match with the original line. These authentication data were registered with the Korea National Institute of Health, further validating the identity of this line. Furthermore, the PSC line has also been used in previous studies (i.e., as hES3) (35), supporting its established utility. The absence of an RRID does not affect the validity of our conclusions, as the cell line is well documented, registered, and thoroughly authenticated to ensure reproducibility. Finally, four cancer cell lines, i.e., SKOV3 (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA; CVCL_0532), A2780cis (ECACC; CVCL_1942), SKBR3 (ATCC; CVCL_0033), and MDA-MB-231 (ATCC; CVCL_0062), were kindly provided by Prof. H.J.A. in 2022.

Cell culture

Human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) were cultured in StemMACS medium (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) on vitronectin (Gibco, Gaithersburg, MD, USA)-coated 60-mm dishes. Cells were passaged every 7 days using sodium citrate at 37°C for 3 min, as described previously (35).

SKOV3 cells were maintained in McCoy’s 5A medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Atlas Biologicals, Fort Collins, CO, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S; Simply Biologics, Taipei, Taiwan). A2780cis cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) containing 10% FBS and 1% P/S. SKBR3 and MDA-MB-231 cells were grown in Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium (IMDM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% P/S, and 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco) until they reached 70%-80% confluence. All cells were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Mycoplasma contamination was routinely monitored by weekly polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and all results were negative throughout the experimental period.

Construction of lentiviral CAR-encoding vector

The second-generation anti-HER2 CAR lentiviral vector encoded a single-chain variable fragment targeting HER2. A CD8 hinge region was fused to the transmembrane and intracellular co-stimulatory domain of CD28, followed by the intracellular signaling domain of CD3ζ. The EF1α promoter drove CAR and GFP expression.

Lentivirus transduction

hPSCs were treated with sodium citrate and seeded onto vitronectin-coated 60-mm dishes in StemMACS medium supplemented with 10 μM Fasudil (Adooq Bioscience, Irvine, CA, USA). After 24 h, the medium was replaced with 4 ml of fresh StemMACS medium containing lentivirus at a multiplicity of infection of 5 and 4 μg/ml polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Cells were incubated for 24 h, after which the medium was replaced with 4 ml of fresh StemMACS medium. The cells were then incubated for another 48 h. Three days later, successfully transduced hPSCs were selected using 1 μg/ml puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich).

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). cDNA was synthesized from RNA using a cDNA synthesis kit (PCR Biosystems, London, UK). Reverse transcription (RT)-qPCR was then performed with gene-specific primers (Macrogen, Seoul, Republic of Korea), Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix, and the VIIA 7 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems).

The primers used were as follows: OCT4: F-5’-GACAGGGGGAGGGGAGGAGCTAGG-3’, R-5’-CTTCCCTCCAACCAGTTGCCCCAAA-3’; SOX2: F-5’-AGCTACAGCATGATGCAGGA-3’, R-5’-GGTCATGGAGTTGTACTGCA-3’; NANOG: F-5’-TGAACCTCAGCTACAAACAG-3’, R-5’-TGGTGGTAGGAAGAGTAAAG-3’.

qPCR reactions were performed in a total volume of 20 μl, comprising 10 μl of SYBR Green master mix, 4 μl of diluted cDNA (1:10), 1 μl of each primer (10 pmol), and 4 μl of nuclease-free water. PCR reactions were performed under the following conditions: 1 cycle at 95°C for 10 min, then 40 cycles at 95°C for 30 s, 62°C for 1 min, 72°C for 30 s, and finally 1 cycle at 72°C for 5 min. The expression levels of genes were normalized to GAPDH levels, and relative expression levels were compared to fibroblasts.

Immunocytochemistry

CAR-PSCs were seeded on vitronectin-coated 24-well plates. After 5 days, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4°C. Fixed cells were rinsed twice with 0.1% Tween-20 in DPBS (PBST) for 5 min each and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton-X in PBST for 30 min at room temperature (RT). The cells were blocked by incubation with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBST for 30 min at RT.

Primary antibodies against SSEA4, TRA-1-60 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), and OCT4 (Stemgent) were diluted 1:100 in PBST. The cells were incubated with these primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Cells were washed three times with PBST, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies (diluted 1:1000) for 1 h at RT. Goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 488, Abcam) was used for SSEA4 and TRA-1-60, and goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 555, Abcam) was employed for OCT4. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000) for 30 min at RT. Images were captured using a Carl Zeiss inverted fluorescence microscope.

Karyotyping

CAR-PSCs were seeded in vitronectin-coated 25T flasks. Once the cells reached 50%-60% confluence, karyotyping was performed by Dx&Vx (Seoul, Republic of Korea).

Generation of PSC-derived CAR-NK cells

To generate CAR-expressing NK cells, hPSCs were treated with sodium citrate and seeded into vitronectin-coated 6-well plates in StemMACS medium with 10 μM Fasudil. Differentiation was induced by sequential supplementation with growth factors, cytokines, and small molecules as described in a previously published protocol. On day 28, NK cell expansion medium (NKMACS, Miltenyi) was supplemented with 5% human serum albumin, 10 ng/ml IL-15, 200 U/ml IL-2 (Peprotech), and 10 μM Fasudil.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was performed as per a previously described protocol with minor modifications (36). Briefly, the differentiated cells were harvested, washed, and resuspended in DPBS (Welgene, Korea) containing 1% FBS. Fluorescence-conjugated anti-human antibodies were added to the culture, and the mixture was incubated at 4°C for 30 min in the dark. For intracellular staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized with BD CytoFix/CytoPermTM (BD Biosciences) before antibody application. Antibodies used for NK phenotyping are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

CAR expression was assessed by staining cells with human HER2 (ACROBiosystems, Newark, DE, USA). HER2 surface expression in SKOV3, A2780cis, SKBR3, and MDA-MB-231 cells was analyzed using anti-human CD340 (erbB2/HER-2; BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). Samples were run on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), and data were analyzed using the FlowJo software (Treestar Inc., San Francisco, CA, USA).

Cytotoxicity assay

Cytotoxicity assays were performed as described previously, with minor modifications (36). Briefly, tumor cells (1 × 106) were stained with 5 μM Far Red (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) in DPBS for 10 min at 37°C. The cells were then pelleted by centrifugation at 400× g for 3 min, resuspended in 5 ml of medium at a density of 2 × 105 cells/ml, and prepared for co-culture.

Next, 100 μl of tumor cells (2 × 104) were co-cultured with NK cells in 96-well plates at effector-to-target (E:T) ratios of 1:1, 5:1, and 10:1. The plates were then incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 4 h. Following incubation, the cells were centrifuged at 400× g for 3 min, the supernatants were discarded, and the remaining cells were stained with 7-AAD viability staining solution (Invitrogen) at RT for 10 min. Dead tumor cells were identified based on positive Far Red staining and 7-AAD uptake (PC5.5), and cytotoxicity was quantified.

Statistical analyses

All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between the two groups were assessed using Student’s t-test. Significance thresholds were as follows: *P ≤ 0.05, **P ≤ 0.01, ***P ≤ 0.001, and ****P ≤ 0.0001. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA).
