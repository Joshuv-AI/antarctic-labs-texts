Glycolytic-Cholesterol Subtypes of Severe Asthma Reveal Distinct Immune-Inflammatory and Metabolic Phenotypes
Authors: Qibin Lin, Haiyang Ni, Jiudan Zhang, Ting Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSevere asthma (SA) is a heterogeneous disease with unmet therapeutic needs. Metabolic dysregulation involving glycolysis and cholesterol synthesis is implicated in its pathogenesis. This study aimed to systematically profile the glycolysis-cholesterol synthesis axis in SA to identify distinct metabolic subtypes and explore their potential therapeutic implications.Patients and MethodsWe analyzed sputum mRNA expression from 93 SA patients and 16 healthy controls (HC) in the U-BIOPRED cohort (GSE76262). First, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the glycolysis-cholesterol synthesis axis were identified between SA patients and HC. Unsupervised consensus clustering was then applied to these DEGs within the SA cohort to define metabolic subtypes. Immune microenvironment features were characterized using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis. A random forest (RF) model was built to distinguish subtypes, and candidate therapeutic compounds were screened for each subtype via the Connectivity Map (CMap) database based on subtype-specific gene signatures.ResultsThis study revealed that, 47 genes in the glycolysis-cholesterol synthesis axis were dysregulated in SA when compared to HC. Clustering of SA based on these DEGs revealed two distinct metabolic subtypes (Cluster 1, n=52; Cluster 2, n=41). Cluster 1 exhibited an up-regulated axis with an immune landscape enriched for NK and gamma-delta T cells, linked to oxidative phosphorylation. Conversely, Cluster 2 displayed a down-regulated axis with enrichment of eosinophils, neutrophils, mast cells, and Th17 cells, associated with calcium and cAMP signaling. An RF model utilizing PPP2CB and SEH1L achieved accurate subtype discrimination (AUC=0.888), validated by decreased protein expression in an SA murine model. Exploratory drug screening suggested dipeptidyl peptidase inhibitor and mTOR inhibitor were identified as candidate compounds for Cluster 1, while leukotriene receptor antagonist and calcium channel blocker were suggested for Cluster 2 via CMap analysis.ConclusionThis study reveals two novel metabolic subtypes in SA, which offers a classification model and suggests potential targeted therapeutics for personalized management.Keywords: cholesterol biosynthesis, glycolysis, metabolic subtype, personalized treatment, severe asthma

Introduction

Asthma is a heterogeneous chronic airway disease that impacts over 358 million individuals worldwide.1 Although treatment advances have improved outcomes for mild-to-moderate asthma.2 Severe asthma (SA), which is defined as uncontrolled disease despite treatment according to steps 4 or 5 of the GINA guidelines,3 remains a major clinical challenge. Although SA constitutes only 5–10% of all asthma cases, it accounts for nearly half of asthma-related healthcare costs,4,5 with its management complicated by pronounced phenotypic heterogeneity.2,3 Emerging evidence suggests that metabolic dysregulation contributes to asthma pathogenesis, particularly involving glycolysis and cholesterol metabolism.6–10 For example, early-life nutritional factors can reprogram CD4+ T cell glycolysis to promote disease,6 and Chanachon et al reported associations between LDL-C/HDL-C levels and respiratory resistance in children with asthma.11 These observations point to the involvement of a glycolytic-cholesterol synthesis axis in asthma development.

Inflammatory activation is closely linked to metabolic reprogramming, notably an increase in glycolysis,12 which is the central pathway of glucose catabolism and yields pyruvate and ATP.13 Depending on oxygen availability, pyruvate is either oxidized to acetyl-CoA for the TCA cycle and efficient ATP generation under aerobic conditions,14 or reduced to lactate anaerobically.15 Glycolysis is essential for immune cell activation16, where stimulated immune cells shift toward aerobic glycolysis to meet bioenergetic and biosynthetic demands.16,17 It has been found that glycolysis is significantly enhanced in immune cells of asthma patients, such as eosinophils and macrophages.18,19 This metabolic alteration can further exacerbate the progression of asthma.18,19 Upon enhanced glycolysis, the resulting intermediate metabolites cooperatively promote cholesterol synthesis through multiple pathways.20 On the one hand, increased pyruvate is converted into acetyl-CoA under the catalysis of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex. Acetyl-CoA serves not only as an important fuel for the tricarboxylic acid cycle but also as the direct precursor for cholesterol biosynthesis, and it advances along the pathway catalyzed by key enzymes including HMG-CoA reductase. On the other hand, the glycolytic intermediate glucose-6-phosphate can enter the pentose phosphate pathway, thereby generating substantial amounts of NADPH, which provides the necessary reducing equivalents for cholesterol synthesis.18–21Importantly, cholesterol metabolism actively regulates inflammation, influencing B cell phenotypic differentiation and T cell proliferation and function.22–24 This interplay suggests an integrated metabolic cascade wherein inflammation-driven glycolysis supports cholesterol synthesis, which in turn modulates immune activity. We thus propose that a glycolytic–cholesterol synthesis axis contributes to SA pathogenesis by reshaping the immune microenvironment.

In this study, we systematically profiled the glycolytic–cholesterol synthesis axis in SA and identified two novel metabolic subtypes with distinct immune and molecular characteristics. We further developed a machine learning framework for subtype identification and therapy guidance, which facilitated the discovery of candidate therapeutics for each subtype through screening of the Connectivity Map (CMap) database.

Materials and Methods

Data Acquisition and Preparation

Sputum mRNA expression profiles from 93 SA patients and 16 healthy controls (HC) were obtained from the GSE76262 dataset in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database.25 This dataset originated from the U-BIOPRED multicenter cohort study, which aims to identify biomarkers and molecular pathways in SA.25 The GSE76262 dataset from the U-BIOPRED cohort was selected as it is a well-characterized, multicenter study specifically focused on severe asthma, providing deep phenotyping and transcriptomic data from sputum sampling. The cohort composition (93 SA, 16 HC) reflects the inherent challenge of recruiting matched healthy volunteers for such invasive procedures in severe disease studies. While the control group is smaller, it serves the primary purpose of identifying genes broadly dysregulated in SA versus health. All subsequent clustering, subtype characterization, and comparative analyses were performed exclusively within the 93 SA patients, ensuring statistical comparisons (eg., Cluster 1 vs. Cluster 2) were balanced and robust. Therefore, the sample size is adequate for the primary study aims of defining intrinsic subtypes within SA and exploring their biological features. Raw count data were normalized using the DESeq2 package’s median of ratios method. Unless otherwise specified, all downstream clustering and subtype-related analyses were performed using data from the 93 SA patients only. The healthy controls samples were used solely for the initial identification of SA-associated differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Seventy-two glycolytic and 25 cholesterogenic genes were retrieved from the Molecular Signatures Database using the “REACTOME_GLYCOLYSIS” and “REACTOME_CHOLESTEROL_BIOSYNTHESIS” gene sets, respectively (Table S1).26,27 Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using the STRING database and visualized with Cytoscape (v3.8.2). Spearman correlation analysis between glycolytic and cholesterogenic genes was performed in R (v4.2.1) and visualized using the “corrplot” package.

Identification of Differentially Expressed Genes

Differentially expressed glycolysis-cholesterol synthesis axis genes between SA patients and HC were identified using the “limma” package in R. Genes with an P-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant DEGs and used for subsequent clustering. Results were visualized via heatmaps (“pheatmap” package) and boxplots (“tidyverse” package).

Metabolic Subtype Classification

Unsupervised k-means consensus clustering was applied to the DEGs to classify metabolic subtypes among SA patients, using the “ConsensusClusterPlus” package with the following parameters: 1000 iterations (reps), resampling 80% of the patients in each iteration (pItem=0.8), using Pearson correlation distance and k-means partitioning for cluster numbers (k) from 2 to 10. The optimal cluster number (k=2) was determined by evaluating the consensus cumulative distribution function (CDF) plot, the relative change in area under the CDF curve, and the visual stability of the consensus matrices. The resulting subtypes were designated Cluster 1 (n=52) and Cluster 2 (n=41). Cluster stability was assessed by principal component analysis (PCA) and visualized with “ggbiplot”. Differential gene expression between subtypes was evaluated using “limma” and displayed using heatmaps and boxplots.

Immune Microenvironment Analysis

The immune microenvironment was characterized using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) via the “GSVA” package. The analysis utilized a well-curated gene signature matrix comprising 28 immune cell types. The raw ssGSEA enrichment scores for each sample were z-score normalized. Enrichment scores for immune cell types and HLA-related genes were compared between metabolic subtypes using the Wilcoxon test (“tidyverse” package). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Associations between metabolic genes and immune features were assessed by Spearman correlation and visualized with “corrplot”.

Weighted Correlation Network Analysis (WGCNA)

To identify co-expressed gene modules associated with the metabolic subtypes, WGCNA was performed using the WGCNA R package. The analysis was conducted on the top 13,092 genes that were differentially expressed (P < 0.01) between Cluster 1 and Cluster 2. A signed hybrid network was constructed. The soft-thresholding power (β) was set to 6, chosen as the lowest power for which the scale-free topology fit index reached > 0.85. A dynamic tree-cutting algorithm (deepSplit = 2, minModuleSize = 30) was used to identify co-expression modules, which were assigned color labels.28,29 Key modules significantly correlated with the metabolic subtypes were identified for further analysis. Gene significance (GS) was defined as the absolute correlation between a gene’s expression and the metabolic subtype. Module membership (MM) was defined as the correlation between a gene’s expression profile and the module eigengene, representing how well the gene belongs to that module. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses of the key module genes were performed using the “clusterProfiler” package.

Random Forest Model Construction

To develop a parsimonious classifier for the metabolic subtypes, a two-step feature selection and modeling approach was employed. First, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was applied to the 44 glycolysis-cholesterol axis DEGs that were differentially expressed between the two subtypes, using the glmnet package with 10-fold cross-validation to select the optimal penalization parameter (λ). Genes with non-zero coefficients at the optimal λ were retained. Subsequently, a Random Forest (RF) classifier was built using the randomForest package with the retained genes as features, setting the number of trees (ntree) to 500. The SA dataset was randomly partitioned into a training set (75%) and an independent test set (25%) using the caret package to evaluate the model’s performance, reported as the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC).

Identification of Candidate Small-Molecule Compounds

Potential therapeutic compounds were predicted using the CMap database. Hub genes were identified from the DEGs (P < 0.05 and log (fold change) > 0.5 or P < 0.05 and log (fold change) < −0.5) between HC and each metabolic subtype. A PPI network was generated using the STRING database, and hub genes were extracted via the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape. The upregulated and downregulated hub gene sets were submitted to CMap to screen for potential small-molecule therapeutics. The top four candidates with the highest scores for each subtype were selected for further analysis.

Induction of Severe Asthma Murine Model and Western Blot Validation

The animal experiment was conducted to biochemically validate the protein expression of the two key classifier genes (PPP2CB and SEH1L) identified by the RF model, corresponding to the validation results presented in the “Construction of the RF Model” subsection.

Female Balb/c mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12/12-hour light/dark cycle. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (No. 2025-263) and were conducted in accordance with the national standard Guidelines for the Welfare and Ethics of Laboratory Animals (GB/T 35892-2018) of China.

To establish the SA model, mice were sensitized subcutaneously on day 0 with 100 μg house dust mite (HDM) extract (Greer Laboratories) emulsified in complete Freund’s adjuvant (Sigma-Aldrich). On day 14, mice received an intranasal challenge of 100 μg HDM in 35 μL PBS. On day 16, mice were anesthetized via inhalation of 2% isoflurane and euthanized by cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia, followed by rapid tissue collection.30 Regarding the animal experiment sample size, the sample size was set a priori with reference to prior severe asthma studies and study feasibility, in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Total protein was extracted from lung tissues, separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% skim milk, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against PPP2CB (Abcam), SEH1L (Abcam), and GAPDH (Abcam). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied, and bands were visualized using an ECL kit (Thermo). Protein expression was quantified with ImageJ software. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. Differences in continuous variables between two groups were compared using independent samples t-test. All statistical tests were two-sided, and the significance level was set at P < 0.05.

Statistical Analysis

Unless otherwise specified, comparisons of continuous variables between two groups were performed using the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Spearman’s rank correlation was used for correlation analyses. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests. All bioinformatic analyses were performed in R.

Results

The Glycolysis-Cholesterol Synthesis Axis is Dysregulated in Severe Asthma

To investigate the role of the glycolysis-cholesterol synthesis axis in SA, we first examined the differential expression of its component genes. Compared to HC, 47 genes in this axis were significantly dysregulated in SA patients, with 12 upregulated and 35 downregulated (Figure 1A, B and Table S2), indicating widespread metabolic alterations.

Next, to understand how these genes are coordinately regulated, we analyzed their co-expression patterns across all samples (SA and HC combined). A correlation heatmap revealed significant positive and negative interactions among these genes (Figure 1C and Table S3). For instance, IDI1 and PFKFB3 showed a strong positive correlation (r=0.74). Conversely, MVK and NUP54 exhibited a strong negative correlation (r=−0.56), suggesting an inverse relationship in their expression. It is crucial to note that correlation reflects coordinated expression changes, not absolute expression levels. Therefore, a strong correlation can exist between genes that are both highly expressed in HC or between genes with opposing expression trends across the SA/HC groups.

Furthermore, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network constructed from these genes demonstrated direct physical or functional interactions, supporting the biological relevance of this axis in SA (Figure 1D). Collectively, these findings suggest a crosstalk between glycolytic and cholesterogenic pathways may be involved in SA pathogenesis.

Unsupervised Clustering Reveals Two Distinct Metabolic Subtypes of SA

Unsupervised consensus clustering using the 47 differentially expressed genes defined two distinct metabolic SA subtypes (k=2) among the 93 SA patients, designated Cluster 1 (n=52) and Cluster 2 (n=41) (Figure S1A–E and Table S4). Principal component analysis (PCA) confirmed clear separation between the two subtypes (Figures 2A and S1F). To define the metabolic characteristics of these subtypes, we examined the expression of the glycolysis-cholesterol synthesis axis genes. A heatmap revealed a striking global pattern: Cluster 1 (up-regulated axis) exhibited a predominantly up-regulated axis, whereas Cluster 2 (down-regulated axis) showed a down-regulated profile (Figure 2B and Table S5). Specifically, in Cluster 1, 8 cholesterogenic-related genes and 26 glycolytic-related genes were up-regulated, while 2 cholesterogenic-related genes and 8 glycolytic-related genes were down-regulated (Figure S2A, B and Table S5). This indicates the existence of SA subtypes defined by divergent metabolic pathway activities.

Distinct Immune Landscapes are Associated with Metabolic SA Subtypes

Having defined the two metabolic subtypes within the SA cohort, we next compared their immune microenvironment features. All subsequent analyses were performed comparing the two metabolic subtypes (Cluster 1 vs. Cluster 2) within the SA cohort.

First, we examined whether the two subtypes differed in their overall immune cell infiltration landscape. We used ssGSEA to quantify the relative abundance of 28 immune cell types. This analysis revealed markedly different immune landscapes between the subtypes (Figure 3). Cluster 1 was significantly enriched for cell types such as gamma delta T cells, immature dendritic cells, and natural killer (NK) cells. In contrast, Cluster 2 showed higher infiltration of eosinophils, mast cells, neutrophils, and Type 17 T helper (Th17) cells, a pattern reminiscent of mixed granulocytic inflammation (Figures 3, S3A and Table S6).

Distinct immune landscapes associated with metabolic SA subtypes (Comparison between the two metabolic subtypes (Cluster 1 vs. Cluster 2) within the SA cohort). Heatmap and boxplots (right panel) showing the relative enrichment scores of selected immune cell types in Cluster 1 (n=52) and Cluster 2 (n=41) SA patients, as quantified by ssGSEA. The analysis was performed exclusively within the SA cohort.

To understand whether this immune disparity was associated with the metabolic axis itself, we analyzed the correlations between the expression of metabolic axis genes and immune features. Spearman correlation analysis showed that the expression of key glycolysis-cholesterol synthesis axis genes was significantly correlated with the abundance of specific immune cells (Figure S3B and Table S7) and with the expression of HLA-related genes (Figure S3C and Table S8), suggesting a potential link between the metabolic state and immune regulation in the airway microenvironment.

Finally, we investigated whether the expression of HLA genes, which are related to antigen presentation, differed between the subtypes. Comparative analysis indicated that several HLA genes were differentially expressed between Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 (Figure S3D and Table S9), further underscoring the global immune disparity associated with the metabolic subtypes.

In summary, the two metabolic subtypes of SA are associated with profoundly distinct immune microenvironments. Cluster 1 exhibits an immune profile centered on NK and gamma delta T cells, while Cluster 2 is characterized by a mixed granulocytic (eosinophil/neutrophil) and Th17-high inflammation. These immune patterns are correlated with the activity of the glycolysis-cholesterol synthesis axis, highlighting an integrated metabolic-immune phenotype in severe asthma.

Co-Expression Modules Linked to Metabolic SA Subtypes

To move beyond individual genes and identify coherent biological programs that are systematically co-regulated with each metabolic subtype at a systems level, we performed WGCNA. This module-level analysis provides a functional context for understanding the downstream consequences of the metabolic divergence between clusters, which subsequently informed our focus on specific key genes for predictive modeling. WGCNA on genes differentially expressed between the subtypes identified 6 co-expression modules (Figure S4A–C and Tables S10, S11). Among these, the turquoise module was significantly positively correlated with Cluster 1, while the blue module was positively correlated with Cluster 2 (Figure 4A). The strong correlations between gene significance (GS, ie., association with the subtype) and module membership (MM, ie., intramodular connectivity) within these key modules (Figure 4B and C) confirmed their biological relevance to the respective subtypes.

(B) Cluster 1 and the turquoise module exhibited a very significant correlation, P < 0.05. (C) Cluster 2 and the blue module exhibited a very significant correlation, P < 0.05.

To decipher the distinct biological themes encoded by the subtype-specific co-expression modules (turquoise for Cluster 1, blue for Cluster 2) identified by WGCNA, we performed comprehensive Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses. This analysis revealed a stark functional dichotomy (Figures 5 and S5). The turquoise module (associated with the high-glycolysis/cholesterol Cluster 1) was predominantly enriched for processes related to mitochondrial energy metabolism and biosynthesis, including “cholesterol metabolic process”, “TCA cycle”, and “oxidative phosphorylation” (Figures 5A, S5A–C and Tables S12 and S13). This finding extends our initial metabolic characterization by demonstrating that the upregulated axis in Cluster 1 is functionally coupled to a broad program of enhanced aerobic energy production and lipid synthesis. In contrast, the blue module (associated with Cluster 2) showed strong enrichment for terms related to ion transport and cell signaling, such as “calcium ion transmembrane transport”, “regulation of cAMP-mediated signaling”, and “G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathway” (Figures 5B, S5D–F and Tables S12, S13). This suggests that Cluster 2’s pathophysiology may be less driven by core metabolic flux and more by dysregulated cellular signaling and excitability, potentially underpinning its distinct immune-inflammatory profile characterized by eosinophils, mast cells, and Th17 cells. Thus, pathway enrichment of the WGCNA modules moves beyond gene lists to define two coherent, divergent functional states in SA: one (Cluster 1) centered on bioenergetic and anabolic activation, and the other (Cluster 2) on altered signal transduction.

Development and Validation of a Simplified Classifier for Metabolic Subtypes

Having established that the two metabolic subtypes are associated with distinct immune and functional programs, we next sought to develop a parsimonious and clinically translatable tool to distinguish these subtypes. To this end, we focused on the 44 glycolysis-cholesterol synthesis axis genes that were differentially expressed between the subtypes as candidate features.

First, to screen for the most discriminative core features from these candidate genes, we applied LASSO regression for dimensionality reduction. LASSO regression analysis selected 23 non-redundant genes that best discriminated between Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 (Figure 6A, B and Table S14). Subsequently, based on these selected features, we constructed a Random Forest classifier. A Random Forest (RF) model built using these 23 features demonstrated excellent performance in distinguishing the two subtypes, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.888 (Figure 6C and D). The two genes with the greatest predictive value in this model were PPP2CB and SEH1L (Figure 6C), both of which belong to the glycolysis-cholesterol synthesis axis and were downregulated in SA patients.

Finally, to independently validate these two key biomarkers at the protein level, we performed Western blot analysis in an SA murine model. Consistent with the transcriptomic findings and their importance in the RF model, the protein expression levels of both PPP2CB and SEH1L were significantly decreased in the lungs of HDM-induced asthmatic mice compared to controls (Figure 6E and F). This experimental validation not only supports our bioinformatic findings but also strengthens the credibility of PPP2CB and SEH1L as reliable biomarkers for subtype classification.

Thus, we successfully developed and preliminarily validated a simplified two-gene–based classifier capable of accurately identifying the novel metabolic subtypes in SA, providing a potential foundation for future point-of-care diagnostic development.

Identification of Potential Therapeutic Compounds

To explore subtype-specific therapeutic strategies, we employed a computational drug-repurposing approach. First, for each metabolic subtype (Cluster 1 and Cluster 2), we identified their respective sets of DEGs compared to healthy controls (Tables S15 and S16). From these DEGs, we extracted the top 20 upregulated and top 20 downregulated hub genes based on protein-protein interaction network centrality (Figure S6). These subtype-specific “up” and “down” gene signatures were then used as input to query the CMap database. CMap screens for compounds whose gene expression profiles are inversely correlated with a given disease signature (ie., they have the potential to “reverse” the disease state). For Cluster 1, the top-scoring compounds predicted to counteract its gene expression profile included saxagliptin (a dipeptidyl peptidase inhibitor) and sirolimus (an mTOR inhibitor). For Cluster 2, montelukast (a leukotriene receptor antagonist) and nifedipine (a calcium channel blocker) emerged as the leading candidates (Table 1). Thus, through a systematic computational pipeline, we propose distinct pharmacological hypotheses for each metabolic SA subtype: targeting metabolic and immune-regulatory pathways (eg., DPP4, mTOR) for Cluster 1, and addressing inflammatory mediators and calcium signaling (eg., leukotrienes, calcium channels) for Cluster 2. These candidates provide a rationale for future experimental validation.

Discussion

Asthma is a heterogeneous inflammatory airway disorder characterized by the involvement of diverse inflammatory cells and mediators.31 Growing evidence indicates that glycolysis and cholesterol metabolism may play important roles in the pathogenesis of asthma. This study aims to characterize the glycolysis and cholesterol metabolism heterogeneity in SA, identify distinct metabolic subtypes among affected individuals, and explore targeted therapeutic approaches tailored to these specific subtypes.

In this study, we explored the role of the glycolysis–cholesterol synthesis axis in SA. Transcriptomic analysis revealed differential expression of nearly half the genes (47/97) in this axis in patients with SA versus healthy controls, implicating its involvement in disease pathogenesis. This finding aligns with prior reports. Studies have shown elevated serum lactate in asthmatics and increased lactate production in stimulated CD4⁺ T cells from asthmatic humans and mice, indicating enhanced glycolysis.32 Similarly, glycolysis is upregulated in lungs of OVA- and HDM-induced asthma models.12,33 Notably, hydrogen (H2) was shown to mitigate airway inflammation in asthmatic mice by inhibiting glycolytic enzymes,33 further supporting a pathogenic role for glycolysis. Cholesterol metabolism also appears relevant to asthma. Hypercholesterolemia has been linked to a Th2-skewed immune response, potentially fostering allergic asthma.34,35 Consistently, higher total cholesterol levels were reported in asthmatic patients compared to controls.36 Together, these studies underscore the involvement of both glycolysis and cholesterol synthesis in SA.

Notably, our results revealed the coexistence of both up- and down-regulated genes along the glycolysis–cholesterol synthesis axis in SA patients compared with healthy controls. This observation underscores the metabolic heterogeneity and complexity inherent in asthma, suggesting the presence of multiple distinct metabolic phenotypes in severe disease. Supporting this notion, Winnica et al reported that platelets and airway epithelial cells from lean asthma patients exhibited lower basal glycolytic rates than those from healthy controls, whereas the same cell types from obese asthma patients showed elevated glycolysis relative to both lean asthmatics and controls.37 These findings indicate divergent bioenergetic profiles in asthma subtypes. A recent meta-analysis further highlighted metabolic disparities, with pediatric asthma patients exhibiting lower serum HDL-C than non-asthmatic children but no significant difference in LDL-C, whereas adult patients showed higher LDL-C with no significant change in HDL-C.34 Collectively, these studies reinforce the concept of metabolic heterogeneity in SA, prompting us to explore metabolic subtypes within our cohort.

We identified two distinct metabolic clusters in SA. Cluster 1 exhibited significantly elevated expression of glycolysis–cholesterol synthesis axis genes relative to Cluster 2, indicating pronounced inter-cluster metabolic differences. Correspondingly, the immune microenvironment also differed markedly between clusters, implicating the glycolysis–cholesterol synthesis axis in shaping airway immunity in SA. Cluster 1 was characterized by infiltrating gamma-delta T cells, immature dendritic cells, and natural killer (NK) cells. Enrichment analyses (GO and KEGG) revealed significant involvement of electron transport chain, cellular respiration, TCA cycle, cholesterol metabolism, and pyruvate metabolism pathways, suggesting enhanced glycolytic–cholesterol synthetic activity in this subgroup. NK cells have been implicated in asthma pathogenesis through mechanisms involving activation, trafficking, and cytotoxicity.38 In SA, peripheral NK cells show elevated CD69 and NKG2D expression, indicative of activation,39 and NK cell deficiency attenuates airway inflammation in murine models.38,40 Thus, NK cell infiltration may contribute to the more severe symptoms observed in Cluster 1. In contrast, Cluster 2 displayed a distinct immune profile dominated by activated B cells, eosinophils, mast cells, monocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, Th1, and Th17 cells. This pattern resembles mixed granulocytic asthma, which involves both neutrophilic and eosinophilic inflammation.41,42 Recently, studies have demonstrated that both increased eosinophils and neutrophils exhibit greater progressive loss of lung function than either high eosinophils or high neutrophils alone, which indicate that overlapping inflammatory pathways may have greater detrimental effects.42 The concurrent elevation of mast cells, monocytes, macrophages, and Th17 cells further underscores the complexity of inflammation in this subgroup. Therefore, the complex immune infiltration may be the reason for the worst symptoms in Cluster 2. Enrichment analyses highlighted calcium and cAMP signaling pathways in Cluster 2, which may promote bronchoconstriction and airway hyperresponsiveness via airway smooth muscle activation, potentially explaining the severe symptomatology in this subgroup.43

Subsequently, we seek to develop simplified methods for distinguishing these two metabolic SA subtypes, with the ultimate goal of guiding personalized therapeutic strategies. Two key genes involved in glycolysis and cholesterol synthesis, PPP2CB and SEH1L, were selected to construct a model for the metabolic subclassification of SA. Both genes were downregulated in patients with SA, especially in Cluster 2. Both genes were downregulated in SA patients, particularly in Cluster 2. PPP2CB encodes a catalytic subunit of protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A), a key serine/threonine phosphatase.44,45 Impaired PP2A activity has been observed in asthma models and SA patients,46 and its deficiency has been linked to corticosteroid insensitivity.47 SEH1L is a component of the GATOR2 complex, which regulates mTORC1 activation in response to amino acid availability.48 GATOR2 promotes mTORC1 signaling under nutrient-rich conditions but inhibits it during amino acid deprivation, indicating a context-dependent regulatory role.48 The function of GATOR2 in SA pathogenesis, however, remains unclear. In summary, our findings suggest that PPP2CB and SEH1L may contribute to the metabolic heterogeneity of SA. Further investigation is needed to elucidate the role of SEH1L in disease development.

Finally, based on hub genes identified from two distinct metabolic subtypes of SA, we explored potential therapeutic agents for each subgroup. For cluster 1, candidates included an endothelin receptor antagonist (ambrisentan), an integrin inhibitor (firategrast), a dipeptidyl peptidase inhibitor (saxagliptin), and an mTOR inhibitor (sirolimus). Endothelin receptor antagonists have demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects in murine asthma models,47,49,50 though clinical validation is warranted. Dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP4), a serine protease expressed in multiple tissues, modulates inflammation, metabolism, and immunity.51,52 DPP4 inhibitors, approved for type 2 diabetes, alleviate airway inflammation and remodeling in ovalbumin-induced asthmatic mice via NF-κB and TLR4 pathways.53,54 Given the activated glycolysis–cholesterol synthesis axis in cluster 1, DPP4 inhibitors may attenuate glycolysis through glycemic control and reduce cholesterol levels.55 supporting their potential efficacy in this subgroup. The mTOR pathway, implicated in immune regulation and asthma pathogenesis,56,57 is suppressed by mTOR inhibitors, which ameliorate airway inflammation, hyperreactivity, and remodeling in experimental asthma.58–60 For cluster 2, characterized by eosinophil and mast cell infiltration, potential therapeutics included a leukotriene receptor antagonist (montelukast), a histamine receptor antagonist (doxylamine), and a calcium channel blocker (nifedipine). Eosinophils and mast cells contribute to asthma pathogenesis via leukotriene production and histamine release, promoting inflammation, bronchoconstriction, mucus hypersecretion, and edema.61–63 Thus, leukotriene and histamine receptor antagonists may be beneficial. KEGG enrichment indicated involvement of calcium signaling in cluster 2, suggesting a role for calcium channel blockers. Although their use in asthma remains controversial, a recent meta-analysis of 30 studies indicated potential improvement in lung function.64 However, the necessity for these candidate compounds to undergo future experimental validation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study reveals two novel metabolic subtypes in severe asthma with distinct immune phenotypes and proposes a practical classification model. Based on these discoveries, we further employed connectivity mapping to propose testable therapeutic hypotheses, with DPP4/mTOR inhibitors and leukotriene antagonist/calcium channel blockers emerging as computationally prioritized candidates for Cluster 1 and Cluster 2, respectively. Thus, this study not only delineates novel disease heterogeneity but also providing a hypothetical basis for future exploration of personalized treatment strategies.

Future Directions, Translational Implications, and Limitations

This study provides a foundational framework for understanding metabolic heterogeneity in SA. Its immediate translational application lies in guiding the design of prospective clinical trials. The two-gene classifier (PPP2CB/SEH1L) could be developed into a diagnostic or prognostic biomarker panel after technical and clinical validation in independent cohorts. Such a tool could stratify patients at diagnosis or predict exacerbation risk, enabling early, subtype-specific intervention. Therapeutically, the candidate compounds identified through CMap represent mechanistically grounded hypotheses for drug repurposing. Their efficacy and subtype-specificity require rigorous validation.

Several limitations of our study point to critical future research directions. First, while we identified two distinct metabolic subtypes in severe asthma and established an RF model to predict the metabolic subtypes of severe asthma, while the reproducibility of the subtypes needs verification in independent, ethnically diverse SA cohorts to assess their generalizability. Second, the lack of integration with detailed clinical parameters due to constraints in the available public dataset; therefore, future prospective studies linking these metabolic subtypes to clinical outcomes are essential to establish their prognostic and therapeutic relevance. Finally, although we performed a rigorous bioinformatics analysis to identify small molecule compounds with therapeutic potential for each senescence subtype of severe asthma, larger high-quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and subtype-specific models experimental needed to validate these results.
