Temporal improvements in elephant longevity challenge the notion of compromised survival in zoos
Authors: Fiona Sach, Johanna Staerk, Hannah Jenkins, Dalia A Conde, Morgane Tidière, Malcolm Fitzpatrick, Fernando Colchero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe global debate over the ethics and purpose of elephants in zoos has resulted in proposed legislation to cease keeping them, unless zoos can demonstrate the species’ needs are met. Outdated analyses that fail to provide a current and robust appraisal of elephant survival in zoos fuels the debate. We analysed changes in life expectancy and lifespan equality in zoo elephants between 1960 and 2024, and compared these to wild or semi-wild populations. We found an increase of up to 21% in female zoo elephant life expectancy over time, and up to 44% for males. Lifespan equality increased by up to 14% in both sexes. Improvements in care have resulted in comparable or higher survival measures of zoo elephants compared to evaluated wild populations. Our findings provide information to guide zoos to ensure elephants live long meaningful lives that are, at a minimum, comparable to wild counterparts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57425-1.Keywords: Asian elephant, African elephant, Survival, Life expectancy, Lifespan equalitySubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Zoology

Introduction

The debate surrounding the ethics and the purpose of keeping elephants in zoos has garnered global attention, with some countries, such as Canada and the UK, considering legislative action to phase out or stop the practice. This is largely due to concerns about whether zoos can meet the physical, biological, and psychological needs of elephant species, and in turn enable individuals to live long and meaningful lives1–4. Propelling these legislative changes is the perceived reduced survivorship of zoo elephants compared to their wild counterparts1,5–7. Yet, to drive sound evidence-based legislative changes, it is essential to conduct analyses based on the most comprehensive global datasets while using proven measures to evaluate temporal improvements in survival and longevity.

In 2008, Clubb et al.6 reported lower survival in zoo elephants compared to African wild populations and Asian semi-wild populations working in the timber logging industry. In 2022, Scherer et al.8, using a more comprehensive dataset, found gradual improvements in zoo elephant survival over recent decades. In the most recent decade, they reported that survival in zoos was similar or higher than that observed under wild and semi-wild conditions, originally studied by Clubb et al. Although this contribution provides important evidence, it also has limitations that may not entirely resolve the debate. For instance, despite extensive evidence of sex differences in survival in most mammals9,10, the study did not account for sex specific survival, which inadvertently confounds whether improvements could be met in only one sex, or in both sexes. Also, it relied on measures of proportional change in mortality over time, limiting insights into how the full distribution of ages at death may have shifted—such as whether improvements reflect more elephants living to older ages versus a general decline in early mortality.

Here, we build upon this work to analyse sex-specific changes in population-level survival of zoo elephants from 1960 to 2024 using two demographic measures derived from the distribution of ages at death, shown to provide sound complementary metrics to evaluate improvements in lifespan in humans and other mammals, including primates, big cats, and marine mammals11–14. We analysed the most comprehensive global demographic database of zoo African elephants (Loxodonta africana, n = 1,458) and Asian elephants (Elephas maximus, n = 2,728) held in Species360 member zoos, recorded in the Zoological Information Management System15(ZIMS; hereafter ZIMS elephants). We quantified temporal changes in longevity between two periods: 1960–1992 and 1992–2024. We chose 1960 as a lower threshold following Clubb et al.6 and divided the data before and after 1992, maximizing the temporal span of both periods (Fig. S1), while using that year as a threshold after which major regulations in management came into effect.

We measured changes in survival and two summary measures of the distribution of ages at death: life expectancy, as the average age at death, and lifespan equality, which is a unitless measure of the accumulation of deaths at older ages. If progress is made in these survival measures, lifespan equality will be high, with deaths concentrated at older ages, and life expectancy will increase over time. Following previous work6,8, we further compared these survival metrics with available wild and semi-wild population data, namely two datasets on wild African elephants from Amboseli and Samburu, Kenya16,17, and one dataset on Asian elephants from Myanmar18. Note that the Myanmar population is considered to be in semi-wild conditions, because individuals are used in the timber logging camps for part of their life, but are free to forage in forests at night. Thus, this population is described as “semi-wild captive-born” (see details in Methods). We used Bayesian survival trajectory analysis12,19,20 for inference on survival and mortality, which allowed us to include individuals with unknown times of birth, and obtained the corresponding posterior densities of life expectancy and lifespan equality (see Methods, Figs. S2, S3). We used these measures to calculate averages, standard errors, and 95% credible intervals for both measures. Furthermore, to better understand the patterns of change in these longevity measures, we examined advances in zoo management practices over this period. Our results will inform progressive zoos on how to make evidence-based management decisions, to ensure that elephants in their care can live long and healthy lives, at a minimum, comparable on a population level with those of their wild counterparts.

Results

Overall, positive trends were seen in various elephant survival measures in zoos since 1960, including improvements in infant, juvenile, and overall survival, and increases in life expectancy and lifespan equality. Survival, measured as the probability of surviving beyond a given age, increased in females of both species and in males of African ZIMS elephants between 1960 and 2024 (Fig. 1).

To quantify whether the divergences in both longevity measures between the two periods were statistically different, we calculated Kullback-Leibler discrepancies21 (K-L; see Methods) between the bivariate posterior densities of the two periods. The K-L discrepancies provide a measure of the amount of information lost if we were to predict the life expectancy and lifespan equality of the last period from the distribution of the first period. We used a calibration based on McCulloch22, that ranges between 0 and 1, where 0 implies that both posterior densities are equal (absolute overlap), and 1 that there is no overlap between the two. While K-L cannot be used in the context of hypothesis testing within a Bayesian framework, it provides an intuitive measure of significance. We found that the calibrated K-L values were equal to 1 for both species and sexes (Table 2), indicating no overlap between the bivariate posterior densities of the two periods, which suggests that the improvements in the longevity measures were significant.

The improvements in life expectancy and lifespan equality observed in the last period in ZIMS elephants are likely due to averting early deaths leading to an increase in the concentration of deaths at older ages (Fig. 2). These trends are similar to those observed in the transition from pre-industrial to industrial human societies, which have been attributed to advances in welfare accessibility such as medical care and nutrition11. Similarly, recent research has detected temporal improvements in life expectancy and lifespan equality for four species of marine mammals and five species of big cats in zoos, most likely due to historical changes in management promoted by the various zoo associations13,14.

While we found temporal improvements in life expectancy and lifespan equality among the ZIMS elephant populations, it was important to establish whether these gains have led to an overall higher survival compared to wild or semi-wild populations. As shown in previous studies, we confirmed that the population in Samburu had considerably lower survival than the Amboseli population (Fig. 1), most likely due to higher human-elephant conflict and poaching in Samburu compared to Amboseli23. Concerning African elephants during the 1960–1992 period, the survival of ZIMS elephants was lower than Amboseli for both sexes, but higher than Samburu for females. During the more recent period of 1992–2024, male African ZIMS elephants had higher survival than Amboseli males, while female survival was comparable to that of Amboseli (Fig. 1). Concerning Asian female elephants during the 1960–1992 period, the survival of ZIMS elephants was lower than the semi-wild captive-born population. During the more recent period of 1992–2024, however, both sexes of Asian ZIMS elephants had higher survival than the semi-wild captive-born elephants. This high survival observed in both species during the latest period resulted in an increased concentration of deaths at later ages, with the mode of the density of death occurring at similar or even later ages compared to wild and semi-wild captive-born populations for both species and sexes (Fig. 2).

Male infant survival (i.e., probability of surviving beyond age one) was higher in the last periods for African ZIMS elephants compared to both wild populations. In African females and Asian elephants of both sexes, infant survival in the last period was lower compared to the wild and semi-wild captive-born populations (Fig. 2). The posterior densities of infant survival between both periods and between the latest period and the wild and semi-wild captive-born populations were, for the most part, considerably different, as shown by the K-L discrepancies (Table 3). Notably, it is important to stress that first-year survival may be challenging to detect in wild populations, which could lead to an overestimation of first-year survival in the wild24,25. As a result, we also compared juvenile survival, which we define as the probability of surviving to age 10 (following6), which is the age at which the risk of death from Elephant Endotheliotropic Herpes Virus (EEHV) is reduced26 and just past the minimum age of first reproduction in European Asian zoo elephants27. In male African elephants, juvenile survival was higher in the last period in the ZIMS population compared to the wild, and considerably higher than in the Samburu population. Similarly, juvenile survival of male Asian ZIMS elephants was higher in the last period than in the semi-wild captive born population. For African females, juvenile survival was comparable between the 1992–2024 ZIMS elephants and the Amboseli population, as confirmed by the K-L discrepancies (Table 3), but considerably higher than in the Samburu population. In Asian female elephants, juvenile survival in the last ZIMS period was comparable to the semi-wild captive-born Myanmar population, as the posterior densities were not statistically different as measured by the K-L discrepancies (Table 3).

Female life expectancy at birth in the latest period for ZIMS African elephants was comparable to that of Amboseli, while it was 65% higher than in Samburu. Additionally, female Asian elephants in the latest period showed a 6% higher life expectancy compared to Myanmar (Table 1; Fig. 3). African elephant male life expectancy in ZIMS was 17% higher than in Amboseli and 56% higher than in Samburu, while for male Asian elephants it was 19% higher than in Myanmar. Female lifespan equality for African elephants in ZIMS in the last period was 14% higher than in Amboseli, and 164% higher than in Samburu, and for Asian elephants it was 13% higher than in Myanmar. Male lifespan equality in ZIMS for African elephants was comparable to that of Amboseli, while it was 33% higher than in Samburu, and for Asian elephants 14% higher than in Myanmar. Notably, Kullback-Leibler discrepancies between the bivariate posterior densities of the 1992–2024 period and that of the wild and semi-wild captive-born populations show that these distributions do not overlap (Table 2), demonstrating that the joint distributions of life expectancy and lifespan equality are statistically different between these.

As proposed by8, we further examined the influence of environmental conditions on keeping Asian ZIMS elephants by comparing survival between in-range vs. out-of-range countries (Fig. 4). In the last period (1992–2024), in-range female ZIMS elephants have a higher survival, life expectancy, and lifespan equality than the semi-wild captive-born Myanmar elephants and ZIMS elephants living out of range. Out-of-range female Asian ZIMS elephants’ survival, life expectancy, and lifespan equality have improved since 1960, and are approaching the values for in-range female Asian ZIMS elephants.

Discussion

Contrary to Clubb et al.6, our results on improved survival observed in ZIMS elephants might be explained by increased regional and international collaboration, resulting in advancements in elephant management and care standards (Fig. 5). The differences between our and Clubb et al.’s findings can be explained because, in their analysis, Clubb et al. combined historical and modern zoo data, potentially obscuring improvements over time, while they relied on relatively small sample sizes. Here, we examined how survival changed across different periods in zoos, accounting for changes in management practices, many of which occurred after 1990. In agreement with Scherer et al.8, our results clearly contradict the findings in Clubb et al.6, challenging the narrative that survival in zoos is lower than, for instance, that of Asian elephants in working camps in Myanmar.

Furthermore, our study explores not only survival but how different measures of longevity have shifted. Similar to what has been found in humans, primates, big cats, and marine mammals11–14, we found that female life expectancy and lifespan equality increased considerably between the two periods. However, although we found an increase in male life expectancy across both periods in African elephants, lifespan equality remained unchanged. This puzzling result is uncharacteristic of long-lived species, among which, as Colchero et al.11,12 found, early deaths are averted and shifted towards older ages as conditions improve, resulting in life expectancy and lifespan equality increasing in tandem. This commonly results in a more pronounced old-age mode in the distribution of ages at death, as shown in Fig. 2 for female African elephants: the old-age mode is virtually absent in Samburu, whereas it becomes evident in Amboseli and ZIMS during 1992–2024. However, African males exhibit an old-age mode at very early adulthood when conditions are unfavorable (e.g., Samburu or ZIMS 1960–1992) and, as conditions improve, the mode shifts dramatically to older ages (ZIMS 1992–2024). The reasons for this unexpected demographic behavior in male African elephants are unclear and warrant further investigation. It is important to stress, though, that, a few decades ago, males were supplemented from the wild and, when that practice ended, the initial low numbers and the slow reproduction have seemingly limited the number of old adult males28,29.

In terms of sex differences in longevity, we found that females of both species generally exhibited higher survival, life expectancy, and lifespan equality than males across ages and environments. This pattern is consistent with the common female survival advantage observed across most mammalian species, underscoring sex as an important covariate in survival studies9,10. Notably, the increase in life expectancy between the two periods for African ZIMS male elephants was more than twice that of females (~ 9 years vs. ~4 years), suggesting either a stronger response to management improvements in males or that females may be approaching a demographic ceiling in life expectancy. We observed two exceptions to the pattern of higher female survival, which warrant further investigation: males in Samburu had higher lifespan equality, and African males in both periods showed higher infant survival.

Since 2001, best practice guidelines for elephants have been updated by regional zoo associations (i.e., BIAZA, EAZA or AZA) as standards evolve, at least every two years. Furthermore, to align elephant management standards with modern best practice, the UK’s Secretary of States Standards for Modern Zoo Practice were introduced in 2012 and updated in 2017 to include Appendix 8.8 - Elephants30. These standards include key changes to elephant management, including mandates on the provision of browse, enrichment, sand substrate, and bathing as well as minimum enclosure sizes, behaviour and welfare monitoring, and generalised changes in elephant management and training (Fig. 5). Notably, some legislations only apply to small regions (e.g., the UK) and thus only affect a small subset of the ZIMS populations, although generally these changes are then adopted by other regional associations over time. Schmidt and Kappelhof27 have further contributed to the drive towards modern elephant management by highlighting the importance of establishing zoo elephant herds with social structures that mimic those in the wild and increased bull socialisation. Changes in management practices were documented, to improve individual welfare in retrospect, for example, in the introduction of softer and more appropriate substrates for indoor housing from 2003, e.g. sand, which was found to have a lower prevalence of foot issues especially when compared to hard flooring31. For such long-lived mammals, it will take several decades to see the full impacts of these changes on elephant life expectancy and lifespan equality. However, such improvements in management standards not only benefit individual welfare and survival of zoo elephants, but can also provide essential information to improve species management and survival in the wild. As human populations increase and the associated agricultural intensification continues, wild elephants are being forced into smaller fragmented populations32 and increased human intervention may be required to ensure their survival, closing the gap between small population management ex situ and in situ, in line with the One Health Framework33.

Elephants hold three key roles in zoos: in conservation funding generation (Between 2014 and 2018 the AZA-member community invested over US $15 million towards in situ elephant conservation in 722 projects, AZA pers. comm.), in educating and inspiring visitors34, and in conservation science research35,36. Despite their important roles, zoo elephants cannot be subjected to compromised welfare and survivorship. In order for zoos to maintain their social licence to practise, zoos must demonstrate their commitment to animal welfare assessments.

Although our study reveals improvements in survival of the ZIMS elephants over time, a long life does not always equal a good life. Other welfare dimensions, such as behavioral expression, social structure, and environmental enrichment, remain important and merit continued scientific and ethical consideration37. Elephants are highly intelligent, socially complex, long-lived, and ecologically wide-ranging animals with extensive behavioral and environmental needs. Research has shown that elephant welfare depends not only on physical health, but also on opportunities to engage in species-specific behaviors such as foraging, walking long distances, maintaining dynamic social relationships, bathing, mud wallowing, and cognitive stimulation37,38. Zoo environments that fail to meet these ecological and psychological needs can contribute to stress, stereotypic behaviors, reproductive issues, and chronic health problems such as foot and joint disease39. Importantly, longevity alone is not an adequate measure of welfare, as elephants are capable of surviving for many years while still experiencing poor physical or psychological health. A long lifespan does not necessarily indicate a healthy or positive life experience40. Numerous tools exist to assess elephant welfare at an individual level41,42, which, when combined with population-level assessments of survival and, ideally, reproduction, can give a comprehensive overview of the status of zoo elephants and demonstrate improvement over time.

While our dataset did not allow for a facility-level comparison, we identified significant differences in survival outcomes between in-range and out-of-range zoos for female Asian elephants. To help narrow these disparities, progressive out-of-range zoos could look to in-range zoos to consider environmental and social conditions, and potential replication out-of-range in the future to further improve care provision.

In conclusion, our results challenge the notion, proposed over the last two decades, that elephant survival is compromised in zoos5,6. Similar to Scherer et al.8, we demonstrate improvements in infant and juvenile survival and life expectancy in the last few decades. In addition, we found temporal improvements in female, but not male lifespan equality. We strongly believe that the debate surrounding the well-being of elephants and other species in zoos should move beyond mere assessments of survival. Instead, emphasis should be placed on implementing sound indicators of population- and individual-level welfare, supported by stronger regulations that ensure the consistent implementation of emerging best-practice.

Methods

Species data

Records for African and Asian elephants were obtained from the Zoological Information Management System (ZIMS) managed by Species360, a non-profit organisation with more than 1,400 current members across more than 100 countries15, via research request #98487. Species360 member facilities include zoos, aquariums, rescue centers, and wildlife sanctuaries. Most member facilities are located in Europe and North America, and ~ 70% are affiliated with at least one regional or global zoo association13,15. Records included information on all individuals held from January 1st, 1960 to January 22nd, 2024. For Asian elephants, all individuals across the global dataset were included to allow for in-range and out-of-range analysis. As in-range countries, we included those within the species’ current distribution (i.e., Bangladesh, Bhutan, Cambodia, China, India, Indonesia, Laos, Malaysia, Myanmar, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Thailand, and Vietnam43, while out-of-range countries encompassed all others where populations are maintained under human care. To avoid including outliers that resulted from typos (i.e., possible data entry errors), we removed the 1% longest-lived individuals as well as records of unknown sex. We obtained individual records of longevity for 774 female and 297 male African elephants, and 1,652 female and 653 male Asian elephants. Note that records of animals that were either still alive at the date of data extraction or that were “lost-to-follow up” were included as right-censored records in the survival analysis. Records with uncertain or unknown birth dates were also included, given that these records still provide some information and could be estimated using our Bayesian approach. We divided the data into two time periods (1960–1992 and 1992–2024) based on the year individuals entered the study (either through birth into a Species360 institution, or acquisition by one). Following Clubb et al.6, we used 1960 as a lower limit, as data was sparse and of low quality prior to this date. We then divided the data before and after 1992, maximizing the temporal span of both periods time (Fig. S1), while using that year as a threshold after which major regulations in management came into effect (Fig. 5).

For comparison with wild populations, we obtained sex-specific life tables of African elephants in Amboseli16 and Samburu17, Kenya. To obtain the Amboseli data, we extracted survivorship data from Fig. 1016 using a plot digitizing tool. These data include all-cause mortality, including both natural causes and death from human activities. Unlike previous analyses6,8, we did not split the data based on whether human-caused deaths were excluded to approximate “natural” mortality, as most populations in the wild now face considerable anthropogenic threats due to human activities and excluding such deaths would therefore not produce estimates that represent real-world conditions. For Asian elephants, we obtained published Siler mortality parameters18 from the semi-wild population in Myanmar. Note that this population is considered to be semi-wild, since the individuals live in range states and spend part of their life in the timber logging industry, during which they are allowed to forage freely and remain unsupervised in the forests at night and during their free time18. Offspring are captive-born within these camps, but breeding is not managed, and females can breed with wild males. The dataset includes both wild-caught and captive-born elephants. While both undergo similar taming procedures, wild-caught individuals exhibit higher mortality, likely due to the consequences of capture, as well as differences in their response to the taming process. While previous studies6,8 analyzed both wild-caught and captive-born elephants, we focused on the mortality parameters from the captive-born individuals, which have been found to have the lowest mortality. It is important to point out that, for this population, captive-born refers to those individuals born in the timber logging camps, and thus could be described as “semi-wild captive-born”.

Survival analysis

In order to make the results from the ZIMS populations comparable to those from the wild, we digitised the survival curves from the published life tables or used the estimated Siler mortality parameters, and used them, together with the sample sizes for each population, to reconstruct the individual records on ages at death for the wild populations. Then, to draw inferences on age-specific survival from the ZIMS and the wild or semi-wild reconstructed populations, we used Bayesian survival trajectory analysis (BaSTA)19,20 in R44. Life expectancy and lifespan equality

As with other distributions, summary statistics provide information on different aspects of the distribution of ages at death. Here, we used life expectancy and lifespan equality as summary statistics. As shown in Eq. 5, the lifespan inequality, H, is a weighted average of the accumulation of deaths with age, weighted by the survival function and standardised by the life expectancy. When deaths accumulate at older ages, H is low, and when deaths are more uniform starting at early ages, H is large. Since lifespan equality in Eq. 6 is inversely related to H, it provides a measure of the accumulation of deaths at older ages. Note that lifespan inequality theoretically ranges from 0 to ∞, where H = 0 indicates perfect equality, i.e. all individuals die exactly at the same age. In this extreme case, given that it is the logarithm of the inverse of H, lifespan equality would approach infinity. Importantly, this level of equality is unrealistic, as it would result in a fully rectangularized survival curve, implying that there is no stochasticity in the risk of death. Therefore, lifespan inequality can be considered a dimensionless measure, which can only be interpreted within a species-specific context when comparing populations with very high to very low mortality.
