Electroencephalography in stroke Care: From acute monitoring to Post-Stroke epilepsy
Authors: Vincent Leclercq, Nicolas Gaspard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Continuous EEG detects nonconvulsive seizures in up to 18% of acute stroke.•Early EEG abnormalities predict post-stroke epilepsy risk and poor outcomes.•EEG biomarkers enhance post-stroke epilepsy prediction.

Introduction

Stroke is the second leading cause of death and the third leading cause of disability worldwide. Approximately one in four individuals will experience a stroke during their lifetime, representing around 12 million new strokes annually worldwide.(Feigin et al., 2025) As acute stroke care continues to improve, more patients survive the initial event, yet remain at risk for secondary neurological complications, including early seizures (ES) and post-stroke epilepsy (PSE). Among stroke survivors, around three to nine percent will experience ES and up to fourteen percent will develop PSE. Notably, PSE now accounts for nearly half of new-onset epilepsy in older adults.(Misra et al., 2023, Neri et al., 2023, Tanaka et al., 2024).

Electroencephalography (EEG), including its continuous (cEEG) and quantitative (qEEG) modalities, provides a real-time, non-invasive assessment of normal and abnormal brain activity. It might serve different purposes across the stroke care continuum: from detecting nonconvulsive seizures in the acute stroke unit and intensive care unit (ICU) setting to informing functional prognosis and guiding antiseizure medication (ASM) management in outpatient care. Moreover, EEG biomarkers may enhance the prediction of PSE when integrated with validated clinical scores such as SeLECT or CAVE.(Alkhachroum et al., 2022, Claassen, J., Vespa, P., Participants in the International Multi-disciplinary Consensus Conference on Multimodality Monitoring, 2014, Erani et al., 2020, Gururangan et al., 2025, Haksteen et al., 2024, Schubert et al., 2025, Van Stigt et al., 2023).

This article is a narrative review of the literature on EEG across the continuum of adult stroke care. We searched PubMed/MEDLINE for English-language publications on ischemic stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, EEG, cEEG, qEEG, seizures, status epilepticus (SE) and PSE, and we complemented this search by screening the reference lists of relevant articles. Studies were selected on the basis of clinical relevance to diagnosis, monitoring, prognostication and long-term management rather than by formal systematic review methodology.

This article summarizes the current evidence supporting EEG use in stroke care, emphasizing its role in diagnosis, monitoring, prognostication and long-term management. It also identifies knowledge gaps and proposes pragmatic strategies for integrating EEG into modern stroke workflows.

Acute phase of stroke

Definition and epidemiology

Early seizures are defined as seizures occurring within seven days from the onset of a stroke. These are classified as acute symptomatic seizures and are precipitated by the immediate disturbances induced by the stroke. They do not constitute by themselves a diagnosis of epilepsy, and the likelihood of recurrence following a single early seizure is significantly lower compared to that of late seizures.(Balami et al., 2011, Ryu et al., 2024, Sinka et al., 2023).

The incidence of acute symptomatic seizures within the initial week following a stroke is roughly 4% to 6% for ischemic stroke and 8% to 16% for hemorrhagic stroke (Fig. 1).(Balami and Buchan, 2012, Beghi et al., 2011, Derex et al., 2021, Szaflarski et al., 2008) When assessment is completed with cEEG monitoring, allowing for the detection of subclinical and nonconvulsive seizures, the incidence rises up to 30% in hemorrhagic stroke.(Balami and Buchan, 2012, Derex et al., 2021) SE occurrence is rarer in both contexts. However, it is comparatively elevated in hemorrhagic stroke, with rates ranging from 0.3% to 21%, in contrast to ischemic stroke, which ranges from 0.1% to 0.3%.(Balami and Buchan, 2012, Sinka et al., 2023) Acute symptomatic seizures may also occur in the setting of reperfusion therapies. However, current evidence suggests that thrombolysis and/or mechanical thrombectomy do not clearly increase seizure risk independently of the underlying stroke severity and infarct characteristics.(Ferreira-Atuesta et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2023, Kohlhase et al., 2022, Lekoubou et al., 2020, Mushannen et al., 2021, Zöllner et al., 2020).Fig. 1Seizure incidence after stroke across studies by stroke type.(Arntz et al., 2013, Beghi et al., 2011, Bladin, 2000, Cavallini et al., 2003, Davenport et al., 1996, Dromerick and Reding, 1994, Ebbesen et al., 2024; Ferreira-Atuesta et al., 2021; Grau et al., 2001, Heuschmann, P.U., Kolominsky-Rabas, P.L., Misselwitz, B., Hermanek, P., Leffmann, C., Janzen, R., Rother, J., Buecker-Nott, H.-J., Berger, K., German Stroke Registers Study Group et al., 2004, Hong et al., 2008, Indredavik et al., 2008, Johnston et al., 1998, Kalra et al., 1995, Lamy et al., 2003, Langhorne et al., 2000, Lekoubou et al., 2020, Merkler et al., 2018, Navarro et al., 2008, Olafsson et al., 2000, Pinto et al., 1998, Qian et al., 2014, Rocco et al., 2007, Roth et al., 2001, Sinka et al., 2023, Sung and Chu, 1989, Szaflarski et al., 2008; Verburgt; et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2017, Weimar et al., 2002) PSE: post-stroke epilepsy. Although a single acute symptomatic seizure does constitute a diagnosis of epilepsy, it should not be regarded as a benign occurrence. Patients experiencing acute symptomatic seizures are subject to an elevated risk of subsequent PSE, with approximately twenty to forty percent of these patients eventually developing unprovoked seizures.(Herzig-Nichtweiß et al., 2023, Nandan et al., 2023, Sinka et al., 2023) This risk is significantly greater among patients presenting with acute symptomatic SE.(Sinka et al., 2023).

EEG in the acute phase

Consensus recommendations from the American Clinical Neurophysiology Society suggest that stroke patients with unexplained or persistent altered level of consciousness should undergo EEG or cEEG monitoring to identify nonconvulsive seizures and SE.(Herman et al., 2015) EEG or cEEG are also recommended for individuals presenting with acute neurological deficits where seizures are suspected to be either mimics or complications, notably in patients with severe stroke, cortical involvement, or a prior history of seizures, as these groups are at an increased risk of developing epilepsy.(Carrera et al., 2006, Rao et al., 2020, Schubert et al., 2025) In critically ill patients, cEEG is preferred over standard EEG due to its superior ability to detect subclinical seizures. However, it is resource-intensive and its effect on clinical outcome remains uncertain.(Beuchat et al., 2022, Rossetti et al., 2020).

EEG is highly sensitive to acute cerebral ischemia with typical findings including focal slowing. These alterations correlate with infarct size and clinical severity, and can be identified within minutes of stroke onset.(Finnigan and Van Putten, 2013, Shreve et al., 2019, Van Meenen et al., 2022, van Putten and Hofmeijer, 2016, Van Stigt et al., 2023, Wu et al., 2016) cEEG can identify evolving ischemia in real time, sometimes before irreversible infarction.(Erani et al., 2020, Finnigan and Van Putten, 2013, Herman et al., 2015, Van Meenen et al., 2022, van Putten and Hofmeijer, 2016) Recent technological advances in EEG have enhanced its feasibility in prehospital settings, facilitating the acquisition and analysis of EEG for stroke triage. Nonetheless, EEG remains nonspecific for stroke and should be interpreted in addition to clinical evaluations and imaging assessments.(Shreve et al., 2019, Van Meenen et al., 2022, Van Stigt et al., 2023).

The most common EEG patterns observed in the acute phase of stroke include focal slowing, lateralized periodic discharges (LPDs), sporadic focal epileptiform abnormalities, generalized rhythmic delta activity (GRDA) and attenuation of background activity in the affected hemisphere (Fig. 2).(Scoppettuolo et al., 2019) Focal slowing is the most common finding and correlates with the location and extent of the ischemic or hemorrhagic lesion.(Pitkänen et al., 2016, Schaul et al., 1986) LPDs are less frequent but closely associated with early seizures and SE, particularly in severe stroke.(Lima et al., 2017, Mecarelli et al., 2011, Silverman et al., 2002) Generalized slowing and GRDA may be seen in patients with more diffuse cerebral dysfunction or delirium.(Lasek-Bal et al., 2023, Lima et al., 2017, Mecarelli et al., 2011, Silverman et al., 2002) QEEG studies show increased delta/alpha and delta/theta ratios that correlate with hypoperfused tissue volume and stroke severity.(Ajčević et al., 2021, Monai et al., 2025) Abnormalities may also extend to the contralateral hemisphere, reflecting network-level diaschisis and interhemispheric effects.(Monai et al., 2025, Schaul et al., 1986, Vecchio et al., 2023) The presence of epileptiform activity or rhythmic of periodic patterns in the acute phase is associated with a higher risk of ES and poorer functional outcomes.(Lasek-Bal et al., 2023, Lima et al., 2017, Mecarelli et al., 2011, Schubert et al., 2025, Silverman et al., 2002) cEEG monitoring detects approximately 15% to 18% of nonconvulsive seizures or SE in patients with acute stroke, compared to only 3% to 6% when using routine EEG.(Carrera et al., 2006, Limotai et al., 2019, Rossetti et al., 2020, Sutter et al., 2011).Fig. 2Examples of EEG findings during the acute phase of stroke. The optimal duration of EEG monitoring remains incompletely defined, but it clearly extends beyond the duration of routine EEG recordings. In a Finnish cohort, 57.1% of all acute symptomatic seizures occurred within the first 24 h after stroke onset.(Roivainen et al., 2013) A prospective study of 665 ICU patients demonstrated that seizure risk is not simply time-dependent but strongly modulated by clinical and electrographic features. Specifically, patients with impaired consciousness and highly epileptogenic patterns, such as rhythmic and periodic patterns (RPPs), maintained an elevated risk well beyond 16 h, while low-risk patients without these features reached a seizure probability < 5% in under 30 min. These findings underscore the importance of tailoring cEEG duration based on both clinical status and EEG background rather than fixed time thresholds.(Struck et al., 2017) The 2HELPS2B (Table 1) score is a validated EEG-based risk stratification tool designed to estimate the probability of nonconvulsive seizures or SE in hospitalized patients, including those with acute stroke.(Struck et al., 2020) This score incorporates clinical and electrographic features, such as brief ictal rhythmic discharges (BIRDs), LPDs, LRDA or bilateral independent periodic discharges (BIPDs). The cumulative score ranges from 0 to ≥ 2, corresponding to increasing seizure risk: less than 5% for a score of 0, approximately 12% for a score of 1, and greater than 25% for scores of 2 or higher within the 24 to 72 h following the initial EEG. Pathophysiology

The pathophysiology of ictogenesis and epileptogenesis following a stroke remains partially unclear. In the acute phase, several factors are implicated, including disruption of the blood–brain barrier, metabolic disturbances, neuroinflammation, excitotoxicity and acute neuronal injury. Blood-brain barrier dysfunction induces extravasation of serum proteins, thereby increasing inflammation and modifying neuronal excitability, which collectively lowers the seizure threshold and promotes epileptogenesis.(Meijer and Gorter, 2024, Pitkänen et al., 2016, Vera and Lippmann, 2021) Additionally, acute stroke causes local metabolic disturbances such as ionic imbalances and oxidative stress, further increasing neuronal hyperexcitability.(Chung, 2014, Sinka et al., 2023) The neuroinflammatory response, involving microglia and astrocytes, cytokine release, and activation of matrix metalloproteinases, contributes to alterations in neuronal networks, potentially elevating seizure susceptibility.(Altman et al., 2019, Meijer and Gorter, 2024) Furthermore, excitotoxicity resulting from excessive glutamate release during ischemia or hemorrhage worsens neuronal injury and can precipitate acute seizures.(Balami and Buchan, 2012, Sinka et al., 2023).

Electrographic seizures and patterns on the ictal-interictal continuum (IIC), such as LPDs and GPDs, are associated with an increased risk of secondary brain injury. These patterns reflect sustained neuronal hyperexcitability and increased metabolic demand in already compromised tissue, which can exacerbate excitotoxicity, disrupt blood–brain barrier integrity and promote additional neuronal damage and infarct expansion.(Alkhachroum et al., 2022, Bosque‐Varela, P., Machegger, L., Lauth, W., Tsalouchidou, P.E., Knake, S., Zimmermann, G., Jannone‐Pedro, N., Giovannini, G., Meletti, S., Harsono, A.R., Rossini, F., Leitinger, M., Pfaff, J., Beniczky, S., Trinka, E., Kuchukhidze, G., 2026, Rubinos et al., 2018, Zafar et al., 2020) IIC patterns often precede seizures and are themselves associated with poor clinical outcomes, as they can induce peri-ictal MRI abnormalities such as hyperperfusion and cytotoxic edema, which are markers of ongoing injury.(Bosque‐Varela et al., 2026) The observed overlap between IIC and SE suggests that some IIC patterns may represent subclinical ictal activity capable of inflicting harm comparable to overt seizures.(Bosque‐Varela, P., Machegger, L., Lauth, W., Tsalouchidou, P.E., Knake, S., Zimmermann, G., Jannone‐Pedro, N., Giovannini, G., Meletti, S., Harsono, A.R., Rossini, F., Leitinger, M., Pfaff, J., Beniczky, S., Trinka, E., Kuchukhidze, G., 2026, Rubinos et al., 2018).

Although cortical spreading depolarizations (CSD) are another major electrographic event in acute stroke, their monitoring is not part of routine clinical practice.(Hartings, 2017) Currently, their reliable detection depends on invasive electrocorticography in selected neurocritical care settings, whereas scalp EEG detection remains technically challenging.(Hofmeijer et al., 2018) CSD are wave of massive neuronal and glial depolarization that propagates through the gray matter. They result from the local failure of ionic pumps, particularly Na+/K + -ATPases, and the accumulation of extracellular potassium and glutamate and cause an almost complete collapse of transmembrane ionic gradients, neuronal swelling and transient suppression of spontaneous electrical activity.(Dreier, 2011, Dreier et al., 2017) (Dohmen et al., 2008, Lemale et al., 2022) (Dreier, 2011, Dreier et al., 2017, Kramer et al., 2016) In acute ischemic stroke, CSD occur within minutes of arterial occlusion and recur over hours to days due to a mismatch between residual perfusion and metabolic demand.(Dreier et al., 2017) They can exacerbate neuronal injury through energy-demanding repolarization efforts and by inducing severe vasoconstriction in compromised tissue, a phenomenon termed “spreading ischemia”.(Dreier et al., 2017, Lindquist, 2024) Neurovascular coupling plays a central role in this process: while healthy cortex exhibits transient hyperemia in response to CSD, ischemic regions respond with hypoperfusion.(Binder et al., 2022, Dreier, 2011, Dreier et al., 2017) Recurrent CSDs are associated with infarct expansion, edema formation and an unfavorable prognosis.(Hecht et al., 2025, Törteli et al., 2023) Recent studies suggest that monitoring CSDs in neurocritical care may serve as a dynamic marker of metabolic failure and excitotoxicity progression, and that modulation of the CSD response could constitute a therapeutic target.(Díaz-Pérez et al., 2025, Dreier et al., 2017).

Subacute and chronic phases of stroke

Definition and epidemiology

PSE refers to the occurrence of unprovoked seizures more than seven days after stroke. These seizures indicate a persistent predisposition to recurrent ictogenesis due to permanent structural or functional alterations in the brain after the lesion. The occurrence of a single unprovoked seizure at least seven days after the stroke is deemed sufficient for an epilepsy diagnosis, owing to the elevated recurrence risk exceeding 60% within this context.(Balami et al., 2011, Feyissa et al., 2019, Galovic et al., 2021, Pitkänen et al., 2016, Ryu et al., 2024, Sinka et al., 2023).

In a Finnish cohort, 6.3% of stroke survivors experienced seizures during the first year following the stroke, 2.4% during the second year, 1.3% during the third year, and 0.3% thereafter.(Roivainen et al., 2013) In a Danish cohort, the two-year cumulative incidence of PSE was between 3% and 4%, and the ten-year cumulative incidence was 5%.(Ebbesen et al., 2024) In a Dutch cohort, a 5-year risk of 3.7% for ischemic stroke and 7.6% for intracerebral hemorrhage in young adults was reported.(Verburgt; et al., 2025) Overall, approximately 3% to 10% of adult stroke survivors develop PSE within 2 to 5 years (Fig. 1), with higher rates observed following intracerebral hemorrhage (up to 8% to 13%) compared to ischemic stroke (approximately 3% to 8%).(Ebbesen et al., 2024, Pitkänen et al., 2016, Roivainen et al., 2013, Verburgt; et al., 2025).

Risk factors for PSE and prognostic EEG findings

The risk of PSE is two to four times higher following a hemorrhagic stroke compared to an ischemic stroke.(Lahti et al., 2017, Merkler et al., 2018, Nandan et al., 2023, Pozo Putalivo et al., 2025, Verburgt; et al., 2025) In cases of ischemic stroke, several risk factors have been identified, including stroke severity (as indicated by high score on the National Institute of Health Stroke Scale), cortical involvement, early clinical seizures, hemorrhagic transformation, infarcts in the anterior circulation, and, more specifically the middle cerebral artery territory, temporal lobe involvement, multilobar regions, watershed infarction, low Barthel index at discharge, younger age, and non-white race.(Alet et al., 2022, Cai et al., 2025, Ebbesen et al., 2024; Ferreira-Atuesta et al., 2021; Freiman et al., 2023, Galovic et al., 2021, Lahti et al., 2017, Lin et al., 2021, Mafla-Mendoza et al., 2023, Merkler et al., 2018, Nandan et al., 2023, Phan et al., 2022, Pozo Putalivo et al., 2025; Verburgt; et al., 2025) Regarding hemorrhagic stroke, a large hematoma volume is also believed to be associated with an increased risk of PSE.(Lahti et al., 2017, Pozo Putalivo et al., 2025).

Although the absence of EEG abnormalities does not exclude risk for subsequent PSE, acute-phase EEG abnormalities, particularly sporadic epileptiform discharges, highly epileptiform RPPs, such as LPDs, electrographic seizures, as well as regional slowing, are independently correlated with an elevated risk of developing PSE.(Chen et al., 2021, Pitkänen et al., 2016, Schubert et al., 2025, Silverman et al., 2002, Tatillo et al., 2024) The detection of these EEG biomarkers within the first seven days following a stroke enhances the five-year risk of developing epilepsy to 42%, in contrast to 13% among individuals without such findings. Additionally, regional slowing of brain activity doubles this risk.(Schubert et al., 2025, Tatillo et al., 2024).

Pathophysiology

The mechanisms leading to PSE remain only partially understood. Whereas early seizures mainly reflect acute metabolic failure, excitotoxicity and transient network instability, PSE emerges from longer-term structural and functional reorganization, including gliosis, maladaptive synaptic plasticity, disruption of the blood–brain barrier, persistent neuroinflammation and the formation of meningocerebral cicatrices. Gliosis and glial cell activation, mediated by interleukin-6 and other inflammatory mediators, elevate neuronal hyperexcitability and facilitate epileptogenesis.(Altman et al., 2019, Balami et al., 2011, Phan et al., 2022) Maladaptive plasticity, such as aberrant axonal and synaptic sprouting, is promoted by thrombin and protease-activated receptor 1, resulting in permanent circuit dysfunction and epileptic foci.(Altman et al., 2019).

Chronic disruption of the blood–brain barrier is often observed in regions of prior infarction and is strongly associated with PSE, as demonstrated by imaging studies that demonstrate sustained permeability in epileptogenic areas years after the stroke.(Pitkänen et al., 2016) Persistent neuroinflammation, characterized by the upregulation of HLA-DR and downregulation of CD32, alters immune responses and further reduces the seizure threshold.(Phan et al., 2022) The formation of meningocerebral cicatrices and patchy microlesions within partially destroyed tissue create regions of borderline perfusion and metabolic dysfunction, which are susceptible to epileptiform activity.(Balami et al., 2011, Pitkänen et al., 2016).

Small vessel disease, particularly within the temporal lobe, is associated with late-onset epilepsy and may contribute to epileptogenesis through occult microinfarctions and network disruption.(Pitkänen et al., 2016) Cortical injury, particularly after MCA stroke, induces ipsilateral thalamic atrophy.(Reidler et al., 2018, Xia et al., 2021) This secondary thalamic degeneration disrupts intra-thalamic inhibition, potentially altering the balance between excitatory and inhibitory signals.(Johnston et al., 2024) Concomitantly, cortical lesions impair the reticular nucleus of the thalamus (nRT), reducing its intrinsic excitability and weakening inhibitory control over thalamo-cortical relay neurons, thereby fostering excessive thalamo-cortical output and abnormal network dynamics.(Johnston et al., 2024, Van Wijngaarden et al., 2016) In line with these mechanisms, patients with PSE show reduced modularity of thalamic networks, reflecting impaired functional connectivity within thalamic circuits.(Lee et al., 2025) Preclinical stroke models support this view, demonstrating thalamic-origin NCSz in the hyperacute phase that propagate to both the ipsilateral cortex and contralateral hemisphere.(García-Peña et al., 2023) In parallel, hippocampal atrophy resembling sclerosis emerges after stroke, driven by disrupted connectivity and worsened by recurrent seizures, supporting a kindling model of epileptogenesis.(Thijs et al., 2019, Vera and Lippmann, 2021) Changes in intrinsic hippocampal neuron properties and increased excitability further amplify seizure susceptibility.(Vera and Lippmann, 2021) Collectively, these findings position the thalamus as a critical node in secondary epileptogenesis, within a broader framework of network-level dysfunction involving both thalamo-cortical and hippocampo-cortical circuits.

These chronic changes establish a substrate for recurrent unprovoked seizures, thereby distinguishing PSE from ES, which primarily results from transient metabolic and ionic disturbances.

EEG in rehabilitation and long-term monitoring

EEG in rehabilitation settings for the management of PSE is used to monitor ongoing epileptiform activity, guide ASMs management and assess neural recovery and functional connectivity. Outpatient EEG is frequently performed in stroke survivors with PSE or concerns for seizures, particularly when there is uncertainty regarding seizure recurrence or medication efficacy. EEG can detect subclinical seizures, evaluate the effectiveness of ASMs and inform adjustments in therapy, which is especially pertinent for optimizing functional recovery and minimizing cognitive or motor impairments.(Chandan et al., 2023).

Quantitative EEG (QEEG) and serial EEG recordings are valuable tools for monitoring changes in brain activity during rehabilitation. These methods can reveal improvements in interhemispheric symmetry and intrahemispheric coherence, which are associated with better motor and cognitive outcomes. QEEG can also identify focal abnormalities or persistent slowing that may not be evident on clinical examination, thereby prompting targeted neuropsychological testing or therapy. EEG-based biomarkers are increasingly used to predict treatment response and recovery trajectories, thereby supporting individualized rehabilitation plans.(Giaquinto et al., 1994, Keser et al., 2022).

Use of EEG in stroke management

Prehospital phase – EEG for stroke detection and triage

Portable EEG devices, including dry electrode caps, are increasingly used for rapid identification of large vessel occlusion (LVO) stroke. QEEG metrics, such as the Brain Symmetry Index and theta/alpha ratio, have demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy for LVO triage, with area under the curve (AUC) values up to 0.91 for the Brain Symmetry Index in recent research. These diagnostic tools enable early triage to stroke centers for endovascular therapy, but limitations include data quality issues and the need for further integration with other diagnostic modalities to optimize accuracy and workflow efficiency.(Van Meenen et al., 2022, Van Stigt et al., 2023).

Acute phase – EEG for seizure detection and differential diagnosis

In the acute phase, EEG and cEEG are indicated for patients with unexplained or persistent altered consciousness, sudden neurological deterioration or suspected nonconvulsive seizures or SE. EEG is also valuable for distinguishing stroke mimics, such as focal seizures presenting as acute deficits. Early EEG within 24–72 h can identify epileptiform activity, LPDs and electrographic seizures, which are often clinically silent and underdiagnosed without neurophysiological monitoring. cEEG is superior to routine EEG for detecting nonconvulsive seizures and SE, especially in critically ill patients, thus providing more information to guide immediate management decisions, including initiation or adjustment of ASMs.(Alkhachroum et al., 2022, Bentes et al., 2017, Chung, 2014, Gururangan et al., 2025, Mecarelli et al., 2011, Rao et al., 2020, Rossetti et al., 2020, Schubert et al., 2025) A particular situation in older adults is cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), especially in the presence of lobar hemorrhage or CAA-related inflammation. In these patients, EEG may be useful when fluctuating encephalopathy or unexplained focal deficits raise concern for nonconvulsive seizures or SE.(Tabaee Damavandi et al., 2023, Van Der Plas et al., 2024) Although the literature remains limited, seizure burden appears especially high in CAA-related inflammation and in hemorrhagic CAA phenotypes.(Freund et al., 2023, Tabaee Damavandi et al., 2023).

Whether or not cEEG improves outcome is uncertain. Also, cEEG access remains limited in many places. Patients' selection with the 2HELPS2B or SeLECT score might help optimize the allocation of resources. Combining early 1-hour EEG with the 2HELPS2B score can help guide monitoring duration and seizure risk stratification (Fig. 3). Patients with a score of 0 may discontinue monitoring, while scores of 1 or ≥ 2 warrant extended cEEG. Subacute phase – EEG for prognostication and risk stratification

QEEG and cEEG in the subacute phase provide an objective assessment of cerebral dysfunction.(Finnigan and Van Putten, 2013, Finnigan et al., 2007) QEEG indices, such as the delta/alpha ratio (DAR), delta-theta/alpha–beta ratio (DTABR) and interhemispheric asymmetry measures, correlate with stroke severity and functional outcomes and outperform clinical and imaging predictors.(Ajčević et al., 2021, Bentes et al., 2018, Finnigan et al., 2007, Sheorajpanday et al., 2011, Sood et al., 2023) Meta-analyses and prospective cohort studies demonstrate that higher DAR and DTABR are associated with worse disability and higher NIHSS and that interhemispheric asymmetry indices independently predict poor outcomes.(Bentes et al., 2018, Dickey et al., 2022, Sheorajpanday et al., 2011, Sood et al., 2023) QEEG indices also surpass imaging predictors such as infarct volume and Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECTS) in multivariate models when predicting functional outcomes.(Bentes et al., 2018, Sheorajpanday et al., 2011, Zhang et al., 2022) cEEG and QEEG also enable real-time monitoring of cerebral dysfunction, treatment efficacy and risk stratification for complications such as cerebral edema and early neurological deterioration.(Ajčević et al., 2021, Shen et al., 2024, Zhang et al., 2022) EEG also monitors response to thrombolysis and guides decisions about further interventions.(Bitar et al., 2024, Finnigan and Van Putten, 2013, Schubert et al., 2025) QEEG indices are sensitive to changes in cerebral perfusion and neuronal activity and can detect improvements or deterioration following thrombolytic therapy before clinical changes are apparent.(Finnigan and Van Putten, 2013, Jordan, 2004) Although promising, qEEG remains insufficiently standardized for routine implementation in most stroke units and is currently used mainly in specialized centers or research settings.

Chronic phase – EEG in outpatient and rehabilitation settings

Outpatient EEG is indicated for stroke survivors with acute symptomatic seizures, persistent neurological deficits or concerns about PSE. EEG and QEEG are used to monitor ongoing epileptiform activity, assess ASMs efficacy and inform adjustments in therapy.(Chandan et al., 2023, Hasan and Tatum, 2021) In rehabilitation settings, QEEG tracks changes in brain activity, interhemispheric symmetry and coherence, which correlate with motor and cognitive recovery, balanced interhemispheric activity with increased intrahemispheric coherence in the lesioned networks correlates with improved post-stroke recovery.(Cassidy et al., 2020, Keser et al., 2022, Lanzone et al., 2022, Liuzzi et al., 2024, Sood et al., 2023, Vatinno et al., 2022). Outpatient EEG use is driven by prior electrographic seizures, ASMs use at discharge and PSE development, though only a minority of patients show epileptiform abnormalities on follow-up EEG.(Bitar et al., 2024, Chandan et al., 2023, Finnigan and Van Putten, 2013) Interictal epileptiform discharges observed in EEG serve as a predictive biomarker for seizure recurrence in PSE. Patients exhibiting these discharges face a markedly increased risk of recurrent seizures relative to those without such discharges, thereby supporting their role in risk stratification and the guidance of ASM management.(Abe et al., 2022, Grau-López et al., 2024) Periodic discharges, especially those accompanied by superimposed fast activity, are correlated with ongoing neuronal excitation, extended hyperperfusion and both immediate and long-term functional deterioration in PSE. This suggests that such EEG patterns could serve as indicators of patients at an increased risk of unfavorable outcomes and should prompt closer observation or therapeutic measures.(Fukuma et al., 2023) Highly epileptogenic RPPs and regional attenuation without delta (RAWOD) on cEEG are also associated with PSE development and complement risk factors, further supporting the value of EEG in individualizing care.(Tatillo et al., 2024).

Future directions

Gaps in evidence

The existing evidence demonstrates significant shortcomings in standardization and population representation, thereby limiting clinical applicability. Most critically, there is no consensus regarding the specific criteria for EEG monitoring in acute stroke patients. Although numerous studies document epileptiform abnormalities in many monitored individuals(Carrera et al., 2006, Rossetti et al., 2020), eligibility criteria differ markedly, ranging from systematic screening within 24 h to targeted monitoring solely for suspected seizures or unexplained deterioration.(Chung, 2014, Gururangan et al., 2025, Mecarelli et al., 2011, Rossetti et al., 2020) Such heterogeneity prevents the accurate assessment of diagnostic yield in unselected stroke populations and slows the advancement of evidence-based triage algorithms.

Methodological limitations further compromise the interpretability of prognostic findings. Although QEEG indices, particularly delta/alpha ratios, delta-theta/alpha–beta ratios, and brain symmetry measures, exhibit consistent associations with functional outcomes across multiple cohorts, data stem from single-center retrospective studies without blinded interpretation and present a significant risk of bias.(Lanzone et al., 2023, Sood et al., 2023, Vatinno et al., 2022) The lack of prospectively defined EEG acquisition protocols, artifact rejection standards and analysis workflows implies that reported metrics, ranging from simple power ratios to complex network topology measures, cannot be reliably replicated across different institutions.(Lee et al., 2023) This technical heterogeneity is further exacerbated by inconsistent follow-up durations, variable outcome definitions, and limited reporting of missing data or loss to follow-up.

The evidence for management-directed EEG use reveals a significant gap between evidence and practice. Despite its widespread application, only one study has investigated whether EEG findings influence decisions regarding ASM, reporting a change in medication in 21% of monitored patients.(Dhakar et al., 2020) However, the clinical significance of these modifications remains unverified, as no study has employed a comparative effectiveness design to determine whether EEG-guided ASM management enhances seizure control, functional recovery or quality of life relative to empiric treatment. This deficiency is particularly noteworthy given that 73.7% of patients discharged on ASMs following suspected acute symptomatic seizures continue long-term treatment despite 90% never experiencing recurrent seizures and that ASMs are discontinued in fewer than 12% of patients with EEGs negative for epileptiform activity.(Byrnes et al., 2022, Rao et al., 2016) The financial implications are potentially considerable, with EEG-related expenditure per management alteration estimated to be up to $1375 USD, depending on the country, clinical context and EEG modality.(AlGaeed et al., 2021) Nevertheless, there are no cost-effectiveness analyses comparing different monitoring strategies or stewardship approaches for ASMs.

Certain patient subgroups remain systematically underrepresented in research. Patients experiencing posterior circulation strokes are frequently excluded from studies, despite the fact that vertebrobasilar hypoperfusion has been linked to epileptogenesis.(Pitkänen et al., 2016) Individuals with pre-existing conditions such as epilepsy, dementia or severe aphasia are also generally omitted from EEG studies, despite the fact that they comprise a significant portion of the stroke population in clinical settings and may present unique electrographic signatures. Children are entirely absent from the existing literature. Furthermore, ethnic diversity within study cohorts remains limited, predominantly involving populations from Europe, East Asia or North America. This restricts the generalizability of findings to populations with differing vascular risk factors, genetic backgrounds or patterns of healthcare access. The lack of data from low-resource environments is particularly problematic, given that the burden of stroke is greatest in developing countries, where EEG could provide diagnostic benefits over advanced neuroimaging techniques.

The temporal aspects of epileptogenesis and its monitoring remain inadequately characterized. Although numerous studies have documented that seizures tend to occur in two peaks, within 24 h and at 6–12 months post-stroke, the optimal timing and duration of EEG monitoring necessary to detect the transition from acute symptomatic activity to epileptogenesis have not been definitively established.(Balami et al., 2011, Bentes et al., 2017, Chung, 2014; Ferreira-Atuesta et al., 2021; Mecarelli et al., 2011, Pitkänen et al., 2016, Schubert et al., 2025, Winstein et al., 2016) While one investigation reported that 90% of seizures were identified within 36 h through continuous monitoring,(Krishnamurthy et al., 2026) other studies have used monitoring durations ranging from 3 min to 3 days without a systematic evaluation of incremental diagnostic yield.(Lee et al., 2013, Timpte et al., 2023, Wong et al., 2023) Similarly, the critical period for anti-epileptogenic intervention, indicated by observations that RPPs precede seizures by a median of 12 h, has not been exploited in any prospective treatment trials.(Al‐Fatuhi‐Al‐Jundi et al., 2025) Although the evolution of electrographic biomarkers from hyperacute to chronic phases is qualitatively described, it lacks quantitative trajectories that could facilitate personalized risk stratification. Primary prevention of PSE remains an unmet need. Current evidence does not support routine ASMs prophylaxis after stroke, but EEG biomarkers in the acute phase, such as electrographic seizures, LPDs and highly epileptogenic RPPs, may help identify enriched high-risk populations for future antiepileptogenic or seizure-prevention trials.

Finally, the existing literature offers minimal guidance regarding the discontinuation of monitoring or treatment. In an Italian cohort, while 18 patients were started on ASMs upon admission to the intensive rehabilitation unit, only 6 maintained therapies at six months.(Scarpino et al., 2024) This indicates significant clinician uncertainty concerning the optimal duration of treatment. No prospective studies have evaluated the safety of ASM withdrawal guided by serial EEG assessments. The lack of predefined stopping rules or de-escalation protocols results in many patients remaining on lifelong therapy without clear justification, thereby exposing them to unnecessary adverse effects, potential drug interactions and additional costs.

Conclusion

EEG could play a crucial role in stroke management. Yet, it is still underexploited. Throughout the continuum from hyperacute assessment to chronic outpatient care, it offers distinctive physiological insights that complement clinical examinations and imaging modalities. In the acute phase, EEG and cEEG enhance the detection of nonconvulsive seizures and SE, particularly among critically ill patients, while also providing valuable prognostic information. During the subacute and chronic phases, EEG abnormalities function as early biomarkers of epileptogenesis and can be incorporated into validated risk assessment scores to inform long-term management strategies. In rehabilitation and outpatient settings, serial EEG and QEEG guide ASM stewardship, facilitate the identification of subclinical seizures, and support personalized recovery protocols.

Despite these benefits, the current evidence remains constrained by methodological heterogeneity, underrepresentation of essential patient populations and lack of prospective interventional studies. There is an urgent need for standardized EEG acquisition protocols, outcome-oriented biomarker validation and cost-effectiveness analyses. Pragmatic clinical trials should be conducted to determine whether EEG-guided interventions can enhance patient-centered outcomes. Until such evidence is available, clinicians are advised to employ EEG judiciously within stroke care, prioritizing scenarios with high diagnostic yield and integrating findings within a comprehensive clinical framework.
