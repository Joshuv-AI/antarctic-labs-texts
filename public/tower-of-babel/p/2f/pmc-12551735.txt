Effect of Geospatial and Sociodemographic Factors on Live Birth After Fertility-Sparing Treatment for Breast and Gynecologic Cancers
Authors: Alexa Kanbergs, Gabrielle Perkins, Chi-Fang Wu, Alexander Melamed, Nuria Agusti, David Viveros-Carreño, Karla Barajas, Alexandra S. Bercow, Jose Alejandro Rauh-Hain, Roni Nitecki Wilke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Patients with early-stage breast or gynecologic cancer receiving fertility-sparing treatment had similar live-birth odds regardless of sociodemographic or geographic disadvantage once care was accessed.

In the United States, approximately 10% of all cancer cases are diagnosed among reproductive-aged women,1 with breast and gynecologic cancers comprising the largest proportion.2 As more individuals delay childbearing,3 fertility preservation has become a growing priority for young women with cancer. The opportunity to achieve childbearing often is central to quality of life and long-term well-being, influencing both treatment decision-making and survivorship satisfaction.4,5 Recognizing the importance, the American Society of Clinical Oncology recommends discussing fertility preservation options with all patients before initiating cancer treatment.6

Fertility-sparing treatment, including fertility-sparing surgery and assisted reproductive technology (ART), can be offered to select patients with breast or gynecologic cancers. However, a recent study found that less than a quarter of eligible patients undergo fertility-sparing treatment, suggesting barriers to access.7 Prior studies have identified significant disparities in access to these services on the basis of race and ethnicity,7,8 education,9 socioeconomic status,8,10 insurance,7 and geographic location.11–13 However, little is known about whether these geospatial and sociodemographic factors influence fertility outcomes—such as live birth—among patients who are able to receive fertility-sparing treatment. Outside of oncology, studies among women with infertility have demonstrated persistent disparities in pregnancy and live-birth rates across racial, economic, and geographic groups.14 Identifying patient factors associated with live birth allows for appropriate patient counseling, addresses issues of equity, and allows for targeted efforts to address existing disparities.

The objective of this study was to evaluate whether sociodemographic and geospatial characteristics are associated with live birth among patients with early-stage breast or gynecologic cancers who pursued fertility-sparing surgery or ART.

METHODS

This analysis used population-based data from three sources: the California Cancer Registry, birth and discharge datasets from the California Office of Statewide Health Planning and Development (now known as the Department of Health Care Access and Information), and SART CORS (the Society for Assisted Reproductive Technology Clinic Outcome Reporting System). The California Cancer Registry, a statewide cancer surveillance system, has been collecting data since 1988 on nearly all cancer cases diagnosed in California and has been used in multiple epidemiologic studies that examined trends and outcomes among a variety of different diagnoses.15 SART CORS includes more than 80% of clinics that provide ART and is subject to annual review and verification; 90% of all ART cycles in the United States are performed at SART clinics (Appendix 1, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/E372).16,17 The California Office of Statewide Health Planning and Development birth and discharge dataset contains infant vital statistics data (birth and death certificate data) linked to infant and maternal hospital discharge summaries for the 9 months before and 1 year after delivery.18 We obtained approval for this study from the MD Anderson IRB, the California Office of Statewide Health Planning and Development, the California Cancer Registry, the State of California Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects, and the Society for Assisted Reproductive Technology. Given the retrospective nature of the data, a waiver of consent was obtained.

The data from the California Cancer Registry, California Office of Statewide Health Planning and Development, and SART CORS were linked using birth dates, first and last names, Social Security numbers, and children’s birth dates (when applicable), allowing for the identification of women with breast or gynecologic malignancies and the determination of their ART statuses and birth outcomes. The linked data spanned from January 1, 2000, to December 31, 2012 (the latest available data from California Office of Statewide Health Planning and Development).

Using the linked databases, we identified patients with breast cancer (stages I–III), cervical cancer (stage IA, stage IB), endometrial cancer (grade 1, stage IA, or stage IB), or ovarian cancer (stage IA, stage IC) who were aged 18–45 years at the time of diagnosis and underwent fertility-sparing treatment between January 1, 2000, and December 31, 2012. These stages and grades were selected to identify a population eligible for fertility-sparing treatment. Fertility-sparing treatment was defined as either fertility-sparing surgery or ART after cancer diagnosis. Fertility-sparing surgery was defined as the retention of at least one ovary and the uterus and included loop electrosurgical excision, conization, and trachelectomy, with or without ovarian transposition or lymph node evaluation, for cervical cancer; hormonal management with progestin using intrauterine devices or oral medications for endometrial cancer; and unilateral oophorectomy without hysterectomy with or without additional procedures for ovarian cancer. Assisted reproductive technology after a cancer diagnosis was defined as one or more autologous embryo or oocyte freeze cycles or embryo transfer cycles after the date of diagnosis.

Cancer staging was conducted using the American Joint Committee on Cancer’s staging manual (3rd edition for diagnosis years 2000–2003,19 6th edition for 2004–2009,20 and 7th edition for 2010–2012).21 Diagnostic and procedure codes were based on the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revisions.22 Only patients with histologies that were eligible for fertility-sparing treatment were included, identified using International Classification of Diseases for Oncology codes (Appendix 2, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/E372).19 Patients with missing geospatial variables were excluded.

The case group was composed of patients who underwent fertility-sparing treatment and had live births during the follow-up period, defined as any time after cancer diagnosis and up to December 31, 2012. Patients were excluded from the study if their births were missing a delivery date, had implausible combinations of living status and gestational age (before 22 weeks or after 46 weeks), lacked complete data to estimate a date of fertilization, or occurred before a gestational age of 20 weeks. The control group was composed of patients who underwent fertility-sparing treatment and did not have live births during the follow-up period (any time after cancer diagnosis and up to December 31, 2012). We used propensity score matching in a 2:1 ratio to match patients in the control group to patients in the case group and assemble a study population balanced on observed covariates that might confound the association between geospatial and sociodemographic factors and live birth. Covariates included age at diagnosis (18–35 years, older than 36 years), Charlson Comorbidity Index score (0, 1 or higher), parity at diagnosis (0, 1, 2 or more, unknown), cancer type (breast, cervix, endometrial, ovary), diagnosis year (2000–2003, 2004–2007, 2008–2012), cancer stage (I, II, or III for patients with breast cancer), cancer grade (I, II, III, unknown), receipt of radiotherapy (yes, no, unknown), and receipt of chemotherapy (yes, no, unknown).

The outcome variable was live birth between 22 and 45 weeks of gestation (yes or no). The geospatial exposure variables considered were HPI (California Healthy Places Index) score, separated by quartile (Q); distance in miles to the nearest gynecologic oncology clinic and in vitro fertilization (IVF) (measured by the distance from the center of the patient's ZIP code to the nearest clinic); population density of the patient's ZIP code (median residents per square mile); number of gynecologic oncology clinics in the patient's ZIP code (0 or at least 1); and number of IVF clinics in the patient's ZIP code (0 or at least 1). The HPI is a tool for measuring community well-being at the Census tract level. It uses 25 indicators across eight domains of public health to assign communities an index score (Table 1).23 A Census tract in the 25th percentile or below qualifies as a disadvantaged community. Sociodemographic variables considered were age at diagnosis (18–35 years, older than 36 years), race and ethnicity (Asian or Pacific Islander, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, other or unknown), marital status at diagnosis (single, married, other, unknown), insurance (private, public, uninsured or self-pay, other or unknown), and sociodemographic status (lowest, lower-middle, middle, upper-middle, highest). Socioeconomic status was determined using the Yost score.24 Missing or unknown values were coded and analyzed as an independent category for each categorical variable. Race and ethnicity were reported in this study because they have been associated with differential access to care. Race and ethnicity were included as a study variable to evaluate potential disparities in access to and outcomes of fertility-sparing treatment, because prior research has identified differences in cancer care and fertility outcomes by race and ethnicity.

We performed additional subanalyses, stratified by treatment type (fertility-sparing surgery, ART, or fertility-sparing surgery and ART) and cancer type (gynecologic or breast).We used ArcGIS Pro 3.1.1, augmented with the Network Analyst and StreetMap Premium extensions, to conduct a comprehensive analysis of spatial relationships. An origin–destination cost matrix was created to determine distances between the center of a patient's ZIP code and the nearest IVF and gynecologic oncology facilities. ZIP code centroids were selected to calculate actual distances, because street addresses for patients were unavailable. This analytical approach, using ArcGIS Pro's sophisticated geospatial tools and Network Analyst's advanced routing algorithms, facilitated the calculation of point-to-point travel distances, allowing for a nuanced understanding of accessibility patterns.

We compared differences in demographic and clinical characteristics between patients who did or did not have a live-birth outcome after fertility-sparing treatment. Categorical variables were compared using the Pearson χ2 test or Fisher exact test, when appropriate. P<.05 was considered statistically significant. To match patients in the case group and patients in the control group, we used greedy nearest neighbor propensity score matching without replacement in a 1:2 ratio of case-group patients to control-group patients, with a caliper width set to 0.2 times the SD of the propensity score. However, if matches within our defined caliper of 0.2 could not be found for a given case-group patient, fewer than two individuals in the control group (or even none) would be selected to that case-group patient. This approach prioritizes covariate balance over fixed matching case–control ratios. We restricted matches to only those control-group patients whose propensity score are sufficiently close to the case-group patients', thereby reducing bias introducing by mismatched and avoiding poor covariate balance. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed using logistic regression within a generalized linear mixed model framework with a logit link to estimate the association between each exposure variable and live birth. Univariate models were used to assess the independent association of each exposure variable with the outcome; the multivariable model included all covariates of interest to account for potential confounding. To account for the hierarchical structure of the data and potential correlations among observations within the same geographic area, all models included random intercepts. This allowed the model to capture variability between clusters defined by geographic regions, as well as among individuals nested within those clusters. By accounting for unmeasured heterogeneity at both the cluster and individual levels, this approach enabled a more accurate estimation of fixed effects. The multivariable model was adjusted for population density. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise Guide 7.1.

For this study, we excluded patients with missing geospatial information rather than imputing those data because our dataset did not include the necessary variables to support valid imputation. When we compared included and excluded patients' demographic and clinical characteristics, we found no significant differences between the case group and the control group (Appendix 3, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/E372).

RESULTS

We identified 1,480 patients within the linked database who had been diagnosed with breast cancer in stages I–III or stage I gynecologic cancer who underwent fertility-sparing treatment (fertility-sparing surgery or ART) between January 1, 2000, and December 31, 2012. Of these, 265 had live births after fertility-sparing surgery or ART and 1,215 did not. On univariate analysis (Table 3), none of the geospatial variables demonstrated a statistically significant association with the primary outcome of live birth. Among sociodemographic variables, being of Hispanic ethnicity was significantly associated with higher odds of live birth compared with non-Hispanic White ethnicity (OR 4.03; 95% CI, 1.18–13.79, P=.027). Being married at diagnosis was also significantly associated with higher odds of live birth compared with being single (OR 3.67; 95% CI, 1.22–11.02, P=.021). No statistically significant associations were found for other racial or ethnic groups, insurance, or socioeconomic status.

The results of the multivariable analysis can be found in Table 3. None of the geospatial exposures demonstrated a statistically significant association with the outcome of live birth. Compared with individuals living in ZIP codes within the lowest HPI quartile (0–25%), those in higher quartiles had similar odds of live birth (Q2: OR 1.16; 95% CI, 0.45–2.96, P=.76; Q3: OR 1.29; 95% CI, 0.46–3.59, P=.62; Q4 or unknown: OR 1.19; 95% CI, 0.39–3.62, P=.75). Geographic access to specialty clinics also was not associated with live birth. Living more than 50 miles from a gynecologic oncology clinic (OR 1.07; 95% CI, 0.35–3.25, P=.90) or IVF clinic (OR 0.47; 95% CI, 0.04–5.31, P=.54) had similar odds of live birth compared with those living within 50 miles. Similarly, the presence of at least one gynecologic or IVF clinic in a patient's ZIP code did not significantly affect odds of live birth (gynecologic oncology, OR 1.34; 95% CI, 0.46–3.87, P=.58; IVF, OR 1.08; 95% CI, 0.47–2.45, P=.86).

Sociodemographic characteristics, including race and ethnicity, marital status, insurance, and individual-level socioeconomic status were not significantly associated with live birth. Compared with non-Hispanic White individuals, the odds of live birth were similar for non-Hispanic Black (OR 1.33; 95% CI, 0.34–5.22, P=.68), Hispanic (OR 1.66; 95% CI, 0.84–3.30, P=.14), Asian or Pacific Islander (OR 1.23; 95% CI, 0.60–2.54, P=.57) individuals, and those of other or unknown race (OR 1.36; 95% CI, 0.22–8.53, P=.74). Compared with single individuals, those who were married (OR 1.47; 95% CI, 0.84–2.54, P=.17), those who identified as other (OR 1.69; 95% CI, 0.46–6.16, P=.43), and those whose marital status was unknown (OR 1.58; 95% CI, 0.36–7.01, P=.54) had comparable odds of live birth. Similarly, insurance was not significantly associated with live birth; compared with individuals with private insurance, those with public insurance (OR 1.52; 95% CI, 0.66–3.51, P=.33), no insurance (OR 2.05; 95% CI, 0.50–8.39, P=.31), or unknown insurance (OR 1.11; 95% CI, 0.25–5.02, P=.89) had similar odds. Individuals in higher socioeconomic strata also showed similar odds of live birth compared with those in the lowest socioeconomic group (lower-middle: OR 1.05; 95% CI, 0.36–3.09, P=.92; middle: OR 0.82; 95% CI, 0.25–2.69, P=.75; upper-middle: OR 1.14; 95% CI, 0.35–3.78, P=.82; highest: OR 1.16; 95% CI, 0.32–4.21, P=.82).

On additional multivariate analyses by treatment type and cancer type, no significant associations were found between geospatial or sociodemographic variables and odds of live birth; the results are shown in Appendices 5–9, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/E372.

DISCUSSION

In this matched case–control study of patients diagnosed with breast cancer in stages I–III or stage I ovarian, uterine, or cervical cancer who underwent fertility-sparing treatment, we found no significant association between geospatial and sociodemographic exposures and odds of live birth. Furthermore, in subanalyses by treatment type and cancer type (breast vs gynecologic), we also found no significant association between exposures of interest and odds of live birth.

Existing research in this population has primarily focused on reporting live-birth rates descriptively or comparing outcomes between patients with cancer and those without, often demonstrating lower live-birth rates among those with cancer.16,25–31 In contrast, disparities in live-birth outcomes after ART have been more extensively studied in the general population, where racial, economic, and geographic disparities have been well-documented.14

Additionally, research has examined disparities in access to fertility-sparing treatment for women with cancer. Both fertility-sparing surgery and ART use have been shown to vary by sociodemographic status, race, insurance, and geographic factors.7–9,12 For example, a previous study found that women living in neighborhoods with lower HPI scores and those residing farther from gynecologic oncology and IVF clinics had decreased odds of using fertility-sparing treatment.12

Building on prior evidence, our study suggests that sociodemographic and geospatial characteristics were not independently associated with live-birth outcomes among women with breast cancer or early-stage gynecologic cancer who accessed fertility-sparing surgery or ART, These findings differ from broader trends in maternal and neonatal health in the general U.S. population, where disparities in outcomes by race, socioeconomic status, and geography remain prevalent. This may reflect a standardization of care once fertility-sparing treatment is initiated, which could help minimize disparities in outcomes. In contrast, disparities may exist primarily in access to fertility-sparing care, itself. This pattern has been observed outside of oncology as well; for example, Chung et al32 reported that, although lower income and lack of insurance are significant barriers to initiating infertility evaluation and treatment, these factors do not affect pregnancy and live-birth outcomes once care is established. Furthermore, this cohort of patients who received fertility-sparing treatment may reflect a highly selected group of patients with the resources, health literacy, and clinical support to pursue fertility-sparing options—potentially attenuating differences in outcomes that might otherwise be observed across broader populations.

Additionally, the findings of this study provide useful guidance for clinicians when counseling patients on pursuing fertility-sparing treatment. Women with cancer often are counseled using data derived from the general population, yet their fertility outcomes may differ due to physiologic and treatment-related factors. Future studies also should evaluate additional reproductive outcomes, such as maternal and neonatal morbidity and time to becoming pregnant among cancer survivors who undergo fertility-sparing treatment and how they may be affected by sociodemographic variables or geographic variables.

The strengths of this study are the use of a large population-based data source with a novel linkage and that the study addresses an understudied but clinically relevant outcome. We acknowledge that there are limitations to this study. Our study used a sample from a single state with a small number of patients who underwent ART, potentially limiting generalizability. Additionally, the most recent data available are from 2012, and there have been changes to fertility-sparing management and guidelines for premenopausal patients since that time. Furthermore, there is evidence that there is underreporting of race in SART CORS, limiting our understanding of how race affects birth outcomes.33 Given our case–control study design, as opposed to a time-to-event study design, we may be underestimating the effect of geospatial and socioeconomic factors; however, given matching on year of diagnosis and other relevant treatment variables, the likelihood of biasing toward the null is low. Finally, we acknowledge that despite matching, we are unable to control for all confounders that could alter our results, and, additionally, we were limited by the variables available in the dataset and were unable to match on body mass index (BMI), which can affect fertility.

In this case–control study, sociodemographic and geospatial factors were not associated with the odds of live birth for patients who underwent fertility-sparing treatment after a diagnosis of early-stage breast cancer or gynecologic cancer. Future research should aim to better understand barriers to accessing fertility-sparing care, because disparities may be more pronounced in who is able to pursue treatment rather than in the outcomes of that treatment.
