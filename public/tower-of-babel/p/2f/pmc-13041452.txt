Effects of the consumption some dietary tropical vegetable oils on glucose metabolism in high fat induced- diabetic rats: a case-control study
Authors: Simon Koffie, Alfred Effah, Joseph Badu Gyapong, Oscar Simon Olympio Mensah, Edwin Ferguson Laing, Robert A. Ngala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSome vegetable oils, which are rich in antioxidants, and other phytochemical properties have been found to be useful for glycaemic control in animal studies. The effects of some tropical vegetable oils on glycemic control and some associated mechanisms in diabetic rats were therefore, assessed.MethodsTwenty (20) high-fat-induced type 2 diabetes male Spraque-Dawley rats selected from 30 high-fat diet fed rats were randomly divided into four groups of five each. The first group was fed on chow (control), whereas the remaining three groups were each fed on chow supplemented by 10% vegetable oil by weight of different oil: coconut oil, groundnut oil or palm oil added to rodent chow. After 42 days of feeding, 3.0 ml fasting blood sample was taken from each rat. Plasma glucose, was enzymatically determined. Insulin, IL-6 and anionic phospholipid levels and the expression of Glut 2 and 4 were assessed by ELISA method. Data was analysed using ANOVA, Tukey’s test and Pearson correlation analysis.ResultsFasting blood glucose (FBG) was significantly reduced by 35% (p < 0.05), and 40% (p < 0.05), in the coconut and groundnut oil fed rats, respectively, however, palm oil non significantly reduced FBG levels by 17.4%. Rats fed on the groundnut oil diet presented significantly (p < 0.05) increased insulin, PI3K and GLUT 4 and non-significant change in the GLUT 2 levels. There was a reduced insulin resistance (p < 0.05), despite significantly increased levels of IL-6 (p < 0.05). There was a nonsignificant negative correlation between IL-6 and insulin resistance in each experimental group.ConclusionThe results reveal that groundnut and coconut oils significantly reduced fasting blood glucose levels (p < 0.05), whereas palm oil tended to reduce fasting blood glucose levels. In addition, groundnut oil showed a significantly (p < 0.05) improved insulin levels and increased sensitivity. However, the groundnut oil group also presented elevated levels of IL-6, indicating potential complexity in the interactions among vegetable oils in blood glucose regulation, insulin, and inflammation.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus, a metabolic disorder characterized by hyperglycaemia is a growing global health concern. Recent estimates from the International Diabetes Federation reveals that 9% of the world’s adult population, amounting to 415 million people, currently have diabetes and projections indicate that this number could surge to over 642 million in the next two decades [1]. Type 2 diabetes, which was almost nonexistent in sub-Saharan Africa from the 1960s to the1980s, has now emerged as a significant health threat, especially in urban areas [2, 3].

Type 2 diabetes arises from either inadequate insulin production by the pancreas or the inability of cellular tissue to respond effectively to insulin [4]. Insulin resistance is a pivotal factor in the etiology of type 2 diabetes, and is associated with various other health concerns including hypertension, hyperlipidaemia and atherosclerosis [5]. Among the adipose-secreting factors (adipokines), the inflammatory regulator interleukin-6 (IL-6) has emerged as a potential mediator that links obesity-derived chronic inflammation with insulin resistance. These adipokines may induce defective insulin signalling in all tissues and the downregulation of major insulin-responsive glucose transporters, GLUT 2 and GLUT 4 [6]. Studies have shown that the functions of these glucose transporters are influenced by their lipid environment, where specific binding of anionic phospholipids to various membrane proteins stabilizes their structure and modulates their function [7, 8]. This observation raises the question of whether these vegetable oils also affect the functions of GLUT 2 and GLUT 4 [9].

Several management strategies for diabetes, including dietary modification, exercise, oral hypoglycaemic agents, and insulin therapy, have been in use for decades. However, these regimens have not adequately been proven to prevent the complications associated with diabetes mellitus [10]. A study in a Chinese population, showed that the consumption of some fats and oils increased the risk of type 2 diabetes [11]. However, some vegetable oils such as groundnut oil, coconut oil, and palm nut oil have all been found to be useful in the management of diabetes in rodents. This study is therefore aimed to further determine the effects of vegetable oils on insulin secretion and insulin resistance, anionic phospholipids and glucose transporters and the interplay between interleukin-6 and insulin resistance on glucose homeostasis in diabetic rats on the basis of our earlier finding that these vegetable oils significantly induce glucose uptake with no dyslipidemic effects in diabetic mice [12].

Materials and methods

Ethical consideration

All procedures and techniques used in this study were in accordance with the National Institute of Health Guide lines for the care of Laboratory Animals (NIH) (Department of Health Service Publication No 83-23, revised 1985). Approval protocol for this study was obtained from the Health Service Ethics committee, School of Medical Sciences, Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology Kumasi Ghana.

Sampling

Thirty (30) 8 weeks old male Spraque-Dawley rats were used in this study. Each of them weighing between 200 and 250 g and a mean weight of 220 g were obtained from the Department of Animal Experimentation, Noguchi Memorial Institute for Medical Research, University of Ghana. The rats were housed in groups of two at 25–30 °C with 12 h daylight. The rats were fed ad libitum with normal rodent chow (prepared by the Ghana food distribution cooperation) and water.

High-fat diet (HFD), preparation and the induction of diabetes

High Fat Diet was prepared by mixing 50 g of standard rat chow, 20 g sucrose and 30 g of rendered lard from pork to obtain 30% by weight of fat in the diet as is expected of a standard high-fat diet [13, 14].To prepare the lard, tallow from pork was used. The tallow was sliced into smaller pieces and rendered at low heat to obtain the lard. The lard was cooled, poured into a glass bottle, and stored in the refrigerator until ready.

All the experimental rats were fed on normal chow and water ad libitum during the one week of acclimatization, after which the rats were fed on a high-fat diet for four weeks. Overnight fasting glucose levels were determined after four weeks of feeding. The rat tail tips were cleaned with 70% ethanol. The tail tips were pricked with a lancet and a drop of blood obtained on a glucometer to measure the blood glucose levels. Only the rats whose fasting glucose level was ≥ 11.0 mmol/L were classified as diabetic [15] and selected for the experiment and those whose fasting glucose levels was < 11.0 mmol/L were selected as the nondiabetic and anaesthetized with pentobarbital 60 mg/kg by Pi and killed by cervical dislocation and disposed of.

Test diet preparation

The test diets were prepared by mixing 10 g of the vegetable oils with 90 g of normal commercial rodent chow to obtain a 10% by weight vegetable oil. Three different 10% fat diets were prepared by adding red palm oil, groundnut oil and coconut oil, and pure chow was used as the control diet were kept in a refrigerator until used.

Experimental design

Twenty diabetes-induced rats selected from the 30 high-fat diet fed rats were randomly divided into four groups of five each: Group I was the control group, which was fed chow the remaining diabetic rats were fed on the appropriate vegetable oil diet.

Group I: diabetic rats fed chow (control).

Group II: diabetic rats on a red palm oil diet.

Group III: diabetic rats fed a groundnut oil diet.

Group IV: diabetic rats on a coconut oil diet.

Blood sample collection and biochemical assay

After 42 days of treatment, the rats were fasted for 12 h and anaesthetized with pentobarbital.

(Nembutal) 60 mg/kg bd/wt, intraperitoneal (IP) injection).

Approximately 3.0 ml of blood sample was taken from each rat by cardiac puncture. Two millilitres (2 ml) were dispensed into a gel separator tube to clot, while 1 ml of the blood was put into separate EDTA coated tubes. The tubes were centrifuged at 3000 x g for 10 min to obtain the serum and plasma. The plasma was used to assay for fasting plasma glucose whilst the serum was used for other biochemical assays. The rats were then killed by cervical dislocation and disposed of.

Biochemical assay

Fasting blood glucose (FBG) was estimated enzymatically via an automated chemistry analyzer (LE SCIENTIFIC HORIZON, 850).

The sandwich ELISA method (Greenstone Swiss Co. Ltd, China), which uses a polystyrene microtiter plate [Biobase Biodustry (Shandong) Co., Ltd., China] according to the manufacturer’s instructions was used for the estimation of insulin, GLUT2 and GLUT4, anionic phospholipids, interleukin-6, and phosphoinositide-3-kinases.

For insulin estimation, a Rat Insulin Solid-phase sandwich ELISA (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) was designed to measure the amount of the target bound between a matched antibody pair. Similarly for the rest, target-specific antibodies were precoated in the wells of the supplied microplate. The samples, standards, or controls were then added to these wells and bound to the immobilized (captured) antibody. The sandwich was formed by the addition of the second antibody (detector), and a substrate solution was added that reacts with the enzyme-antibody-target complex to produce a measurable signal. The intensity of this signal was directly proportional to the concentration of the target present in the original sample.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was performed via Graph Pad Prism version 8.00 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego California, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s test was used for multiple comparisons between metabolic groups. The Pearson correlation analysis was used to determine the correlation between IL-6 and insulin resistance. P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Discussion

Some vegetable oils have also been shown to improve glucose metabolism in diabetic animal studies. These oils contain different fractions of fat and other components that have varying effects on glucose metabolism [12] by improving insulin resistance or insulin secretion [16, 17]. However, the pathophysiology of insulin resistance and its reversal process by certain vegetable oils [17] remain largely understudied in sub-Saharan Africa, where rates of type 2 diabetes are increasing rapidly [18].

Insulin is secreted primarily in response to glucose, while other nutrients such as free fatty acids and amino acids can augment glucose-induced insulin secretion. Several studies have shown that coconut oil, palm oil and groundnut oil-induce glucose uptake in animal studies is mediated through insulin secretion [19]. Groundnut oil supplemented diet significantly reduced fasting blood glucose (FBG) by 40%, (p < 0.05) coconut oil by 35% (p < 0.05) and nonsignificant 17.4% reduction by palm oil (Fig. 1A). These reductions corresponded with significant upregulation of insulin secretion and GLUT4, increased PI3K, and reduced insulin resistance (p < 0.05) for rats fed on groundnut oil supplemented diet, although a nonsignificant change was observed for palm oil supplemented diet, yet followed the trend of fatty acid stimulated glucose uptake signalling pathway (Fig. 1B–D).

The binding of fatty acid induces insulin to its receptors and initiates a signal transduction cascade, resulting in the activation of PI3K and subdequently Akt. Akt acts on the glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4) containing vesicles in the cytosol facilitating their translocation to the cell membrane. Increased GLUT4 molecules in the membrane, increases the rate of GLUT4-mediated glucose uptake.

Coconut oil, a medium-chain saturated fatty acid, mostly composed of the lauric acid, has grown in popularity in recent decades due to its anti-inflammatory, and lipid-lowering effect [20]. Coconut oil supplementation diet among diabetic rats resulted in significant reduction in FBG by 35%. In a study of streptozotocin-induced diabetic rats given various fat feeds, coconut oil-fed rats demonstrated the greatest positive response to an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) when compared to palm and groundnut oil fed rats [21], which is consistent with this findings. IL-6 expression was not significant (Fig. 3B). It has been hypothesized that consuming coconut oil reduces the demand for pancreatic enzyme synthesis, hence boosting insulin secretion and blood glucose utilization [22, 23], . However, in this study, coconut oil supplementation did show a trend in improve insulin secretion but not its sensitivity among diabetic rats (Fig. 1A, B), which is consistent with a previous study that assessed the effects of virgin coconut oil supplementation on the glucose and lipid homeostasis in nondiabetic and alloxan-induced diabetic rats. In that study, alloxan-induced diabetes rats, supplementated coconut oil feed did not ameliorate the diabetes condition [24]. This may partly be true in our case since insulin resistance among diabetic rats did not improve following coconut oil supplementation (Fig. 1C). There was also a nonsignificant negative correlation between insulin resistance and the IL-6 levels (Fig. 3B).

Groundnut oil contains 46% and 32% of monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) respectively [25].

There was a significant 40% reduction in FBG following the supplementation of the diabetic rat diet with groundnut oil. Previous study noted a reduction in peak plasma glucose concentration with the consumption of a MUFA-rich diet [26] which is consistent with the current findings. This finding is also consistent with our previous study, which reported a significant reduction in FBG following the fortification of rodent diet with 10% groundnut oil [12].

This reduction was probably mediated through significantly reduced insulin resistance and increased insulin secretion (Fig. 1B, C) despite a significant increase in IL-6 levels in the diabetic rats (Fig. 1C). IL-6 inhibits insulin signalling by impairing insulin receptor phosphorylation and increasing suppressor of cytokine signalling 3 (SOCS-3) expression [27] consequently, there is inhibition of insulin receptor substrate-1 (IRS-1) [28] resulting in reduced glucose uptake and insulin sensitivity.

Groundnut oil contains high amounts of omega-6 fatty acids, which induce inflammation in some situations [29]. Thus, the high IL-6 levels observed in groundnut oil-fed diabetic rats could be a consequence of this diet-induced inflammation. Interestingly, despite the elevated IL-6 levels, groundnut oil-fed diabetic rats presented reduced insulin resistance. Increased PI3K levels are associated with improved insulin sensitivity [30]. PI3K activity induce an increased translocation of GLUT4 transporters to the cell membrane, facilitating cells to the efficient absorption of glucose [31, 32]. It was therefore not surprising to observe increased levels of both PI3K and GLUT4 expression among diabetic rats fed on groundnut oil supplements in this study (Figs. 1E and 2B).

GLUT 2 levels were not significantly changed by any of the oil supplemented diets (Fig. 2A). GLUT 2 facilitates glucose transport in the liver, pancreas, intestine, kidney, and brain. It bidirectionally transports glucose in and out of the cell owing to its low affinity and high capacity [33].

The effect of palm oil consumption in type 2 diabetes is controversial and some evidence suggests that a diet supplemented with palm oil impairs glucose tolerance in mice [21]. In this study, a palm oil supplemented diet induced a nonsignificant 17.4% reduction in plasma glucose, similar to insulin secretion, and a reduction of insulin resistance and an associated nonsignificant inhibition of PI3Kinase and IL-6 reduction (Fig. 1A–C, E).

Compared with myristic, linoleic acid or α-linolenic acid, the effect of palmitic acid on the increased secretion of IL-6 in 3T3-L1 adipocytes was reported by Laugerette et al., who also demonstrated the effects of palmitic acid and lipopolysaccharides in modulating inflammation through different dietary fat contents in these cells [34]. However, in this study insulin resistance did not significantly change corresponding to the non-significant increase in IL-6 (Figs. 1C and 3A) which also corresponded to the nonsignificant increase in glucose uptake. Palm oil contains high amounts of antioxidants [35]. It may probably be inferred that the antioxidant effects are counteracting the inhibitory effect of the inflammatory markers on glucose uptake.

All the vegetable oils did not significantly suppress the activity of anionic phospholipids (Fig. 1D). The regulated transport of glucose across cell membranes is mediated by facilitative glucose transporters (GLUTs) which are embedded in lipid bilayers [36]. The Anionic Phospholipids (phosphatidic acid, phosphatidylserine, phosphatidylglycerol, and phosphatidylinositol) are lipid ions that mediate glucose transport across the cell membrane by activating and stabilizing the membrane structure and therefore enhancing diffusion [9]. The nonsignificant suppression of GLUT2 expression by all the vegetable oils (Fig. 2A) seems consistent with the suppressive effect on the anionic phospholipids. Even though these effects are nonsignificant the trend suggests that there is a correlation between the anionic phospholipids and GLUT2 transport. However, GLUT4 transport may be induced differently by the oils. Despite the low level of anionic phospholipids, the expression of GLUT4 was significantly increased in diabetic rats fed on the groundnut oil supplemented diets whereas the other oils did not induce a significantly change (Fig. 2B). The difference in the mechanism of action between GLUT2 and GLUT4 is that GLUT2 does not rely on insulin to carry out protein-facilitated glucose movement across cell membranes whereas GLUT4 relies on insulin to carry out protein-facilitated glucose movement across cell membranes [36]. This finding is corroborated by the fact that both GLUT4 and insulin are upregulated in the groundnut oil supplemented diets (Figs. 1B and 2B).

Conclusion

Both groundnut and coconut oil demonstrated a significantly reduced fasting blood glucose levels. Groundnut oil-stimulated glucose uptake seems to be mediated through insulin secretion, whereas the other oils showed similar tendencies. In addition, a diet supplemented with groundnut oil improved insulin sensitivity.

However, the groundnut oil group also presented elevated levels of IL-6, indicating potential complexity in the interactions among cooking oils, blood glucose regulation, insulin, and inflammation. Therefore, the present study emphasizes the need for more in-depth study and may highlight vegetable oil supplementation as a promising approach for glucose homeostasis in diabetes, meriting inquiry in follow-up clinical studies.

Limitation

The chow from which the test diets were prepared from contain some amount of fat that was not accounted for in the 10% vegetable oil diet formulation.
