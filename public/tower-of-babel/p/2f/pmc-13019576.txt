Adaptive rightward gaze shifts support configural object recognition during perceptual challenge
Authors: Emanuela L. Dan, Ioana Țincaș, Cécile Gal, Mihaela Dînșoreanu, Vasile V. Moca, Raul C. Mureșan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe investigated gaze lateralization during object and face recognition using stimuli containing only low spatial frequency information. Using Dots stimuli that isolate configural structure while allowing precise control of sensory evidence, we recorded eye movements during free-viewing and time-restricted exploration across four experiments. Gaze shifted reliably toward the right visual hemifield as perceptual demands increased, appearing as reduced leftward bias, or clear rightward lateralization. This shift was strengthened by lower visibility, intrinsic ambiguity, and higher task demands. In free viewing, gaze shifts were dynamically modulated by sensory evidence and stimulus familiarity, whereas task-general priors alone did not account for lateralization. Under time-restricted viewing, strong rightward shifts emerged rapidly, indicating early optimization when access to information was limited. Faces differed from objects in their exploration dynamics, although lateralization did not differ between conspecific and heterospecific faces. These results indicate that rightward gaze shifts reflect flexible, adaptive strategies supporting configural visual processing.

Introduction

Demands raised by social interaction shape the development of specific visual strategies.1 However, it is not yet completely understood what influences visual exploration during day-to-day life. One interesting visual behavior is the so-called gaze lateralization, or gaze bias, whereby gaze is preferentially directed to a certain side of a visual target.2,3,4 Right gaze bias was found in humans performing visual line bisection tasks.2 On the other hand, left gaze bias has been observed during the processing of face stimuli in visual sampling studies,5,6,7 both in humans and in animals.3 In humans, left gaze bias may also be influenced by reading direction habits.8

Sometimes confusing results have been reported in the literature about two distinct concepts: perceptual bias and gaze bias, which are not trivially related.9 Perceptual bias was discovered in studies using chimeric faces (composed of the left- or right-sides of a face, mirrored around midline), where participants have to judge certain characteristics of an individual (e.g., age, gender, or identity). It was found that the left side of the face (from the viewer’s perspective), also called left hemiface, contributed more to the perceptual decision than the right hemiface, in both humans10 and apes.11 This can be corroborated with the right hemisphere (RH) dominance hypothesis,5,12 whereby optimal face processing requires input to the RH, i.e., from the left visual hemifield. In particular, the right face-fusiform area seems to play a dominant role in face processing, even when viewed centrally, i.e., without shifting gaze from the center of the face.13 However, critique has also been formulated for RH dominance in face perception, at least for the case of fMRI data.14

By contrast, gaze bias refers to a shift in the location of fixations relative to the center of the viewed object. Some face perception studies have linked left gaze bias with left perceptual bias because gaze dwelled for longer5 and/or more frequently6 on the left side of the face. It is unclear how this could be reconciled with the RH dominance hypothesis for faces, because gazing on the left side places most of the face in the right visual hemifield, which is processed by the left hemisphere (LH).15

An insight into this conundrum comes from lesion studies, which have shown that the two hemispheres contribute differently to so-called “egocentric” and “allocentric” lateralization. The former refers to lateralization regarding the left or right visual hemifield, while the latter refers to lateralization relative to the viewed object’s side. Patients with RH lesions have a neglect in the left visual hemifield, whereas those with an LH lesion have difficulties processing the right side of the object, irrespective of presentation side.15 Drawing on early studies using hierarchical figures,16 it was shown17 that global (configural) object structure is processed optimally when displayed in the left visual hemifield (egocentric lateralization), whereas local (featural) properties are best processed when localized on the right side of the viewed object, independently of presentation side (allocentric lateralization). Therefore, gazing on the left or right side of the object changes egocentric lateralization, but does not impact the allocentric one. It is unclear if perceptual bias is influenced by egocentric or allocentric lateralization, or both.

Egocentric gaze lateralization can be related to the coarse-to-fine visual perception hypothesis,16 which assumes the examination of the global, configural structure of an object before its featural, composing elements.18,19 Low spatial frequencies (LSFs; coarse information) are conveyed by the magnocellular pathway, which transmits information to the primary visual cortex faster than the other, parallel processing stream, the parvocellular pathway, which decodes high spatial frequencies (HSFs).20 Consequently, directing attention to the configural features is likely mediated by LSFs, requiring more peripheral eccentricities, compared to the featural, predominantly HSFs, which are processed more foveally.21

Several lines of evidence support the idea that tasks relying on LSFs display an RH advantage, while an LH predominance is seen when processing stimuli with HSFs.21,22 This can also partly explain why, after initially gazing at the face center and processing configural information mostly with the RH, subjects often gaze to the left side of the face image,5 thus shifting to the processing of detailed, featural information using the LH.21

Given such a complex interplay of spatial frequency and lateralization, gaze bias should be studied under conditions that explicitly control for the spatial frequency of the visual input, because processing HSFs or LSFs may determine distinct lateralization patterns in order to take advantage of the distinct hemispheric specializations. Gaze bias may be influenced by both configural (LSF) and featural (HSF) processing demands. As shown before, configural processing is inescapable,16 because it comes first in the processing chain. This can be studied in isolation, using specialized stimuli, which convey only LSF information,18,20 such as the Dots.23 For such stimuli, the prediction is that a right gaze bias should be observed, taking advantage of the RH’s propensity to process holistic object structure.17 Indeed, we recently reported right gaze bias for stimuli whose recognition relies on Gestalt principles, where peripheral vision can help bind separate points into meaningful, global contour patterns.24 The nature of this right gaze bias, which likely favors holistic object processing by the RH, is not really understood. Right gaze bias might be influenced by task difficulty, development of priors during the experiment, conspecific versus heterospecific types in face processing, or may even show differences between visual processing of faces versus objects.

Task difficulty may affect right gaze bias. Carbary and colleagues report that increased self-reported difficulty in processing of chimeric faces is associated with a reduction in RH involvement and an increase in reliance on the LH.10,25 Indeed, if both LSF and HSF components were present in the stimulus, more difficult face discrimination tasks would require reliance on more detailed, HSF information. But what happens if such information is not available in the stimulus, and only configural information (LSF) is present? To the best of our knowledge, no controlled experiment has been conducted before where only LSF is available to the subjects and difficulty is systematically manipulated. When HSF details are missing, difficulty cannot be compensated by relying on fine details required for discrimination, such as in the case of face identification. Instead, for general object recognition tasks, difficulty can emerge from two other major sources: (1) the amount of object-related information in the visual input, and (2) temporal availability, i.e., the time duration while the subject has access to such information (presentation duration in a controlled experimental environment). The impact of difficulty on gaze bias in general object recognition tasks, and when only LSF information is retrievable from the stimulus, is not yet known.

Priors that are developed during the task involve memory retrieval processes and may also influence lateralization. Musel et al. (2013) specify that the involvement of memory processes in categorization tasks of stimuli with different spatial frequencies activate the left superior frontal gyrus. They also indicate that a greater activation of the LH in memory tasks may be connected to attempts of retrieving a prototype match for a stimulus from memory.21 However, a clear distinction must be made between different types of memory-retrieval processes, which are associated with different types of priors. For example, task-general priors reflect the adaptation of the subject to the experimental paradigm, and involve acquiring general knowledge about the task (spatial location of stimuli, average size of stimuli, etc.). By contrast, task-specific priors reflect evidence that accumulates and helps the subject directly resolve the task at hand (e.g., familiarity of the stimulus set).24 The impact of these two types of priors on gaze bias has not yet been investigated.

Finally, for the perception of faces, a factor that may influence gaze bias is conspecificity. Indeed, visual sampling strategies have long been analyzed using faces, and evidence suggests that there is a preferential visual processing for own species (conspecific) faces.26 This is not limited to humans, but seems to be present across many species.27,28,29,30 It is unclear if conspecific faces determine a different gaze lateralization pattern compared to heterospecific ones.

The aim of the present study was to assess gaze lateralization (bias) during object recognition addressing all the issues described previously. To probe if right gaze bias supports an RH advantage in egocentric configural processing, we used stimuli that retain only global contour information. We determined how gaze bias is influenced by five factors: (1) accessibility of visual information (either due to the limited information in the stimulus or due to time-limited presentation); (2) emergence of task-general and task-specific priors during task execution; (3) difficulty of the task; (4) involvement of specialized face-processing strategies versus generic object recognition; (5) conspecificity in the case of face perception. To this end, we recorded behavioral and eye-tracking data in four experiments: three including free (unlimited time) and one including time-limited visual exploration tasks. We quantified gaze asymmetry and changes in gaze asymmetry using an experimental paradigm with visual stimuli that contain only LSFs, while precisely controlling the amount of stimulus-related information available to the subject.23,24 Stimuli were derived from a wide gallery of source images, including objects, human faces, and monkey faces. Our results indicate that task difficulty and priors jointly shape gaze lateralization during configural processing. While rightward gaze bias predominates overall, some participants display a leftward baseline bias. Importantly, the latter still shift their gaze rightward under increased configural demands, following the same modulation pattern as right-biased participants.

Results

Participants with normal or corrected-to-normal vision were recruited from undergraduate psychology students and volunteer pools and were assigned to one of four experiments. Experiment I involved an object recognition task using stimuli presented with varying visibility levels and different presentation orders, including ascending, descending, random, and a reduced object subset (see method details). Experiment II examined face perception using a protocol contrasting different face types. Experiment III compared recognition of meaningful objects with configurally scrambled versions lacking coherent global structure. Experiment IV investigated visual perception under time-restricted viewing conditions.

The first three experiments were free-viewing exploration experiments. Experiment I included Dots stimuli derived from 50 objects belonging to different categories (objects, tools, fruits, and whole-body animals). The main dataset contained 350 stimuli (50 objects × 7 blocks) presented across three visibility-order groups (ascending, descending, and random). In addition, we included a dataset using a subset of 30 objects presented with the same ascending visibility protocol, yielding 210 stimuli (30 objects × 7 blocks). In experiment II we used 180 face stimuli (human and monkey faces) across 9 levels of visibility (g = 0.14 to 0.42 in increments of 0.07 and then down back to 0.14 in decrements of 0.07). In experiment III, we presented 40 objects and their 40 corresponding scrambled versions (“non-objects”; global structure randomly shuffled) at two visibility levels (g = 0.2 and 0.3). Finally, for the time-restricted visual exploration task (experiment IV), we used 200 stimuli consisting of objects, cars, human faces, and animal faces (with partial overlap with experiment I). While for experiments I, II, and III subjects were allowed to gaze for as long as they needed to reach a perceptual decision (free viewing), for experiment IV stimuli were displayed for a fixed duration of 1500 ms.

Source images were selected from several sources.23,31,32 Images were centered, background information was removed, and they were rescaled to 600 × 400 pixels. Stimuli were generated from these images using the Dots technique (Figure 1),23 a generative method that controls precisely the amount of information from the original image that is actually present in the stimulus. Dots stimuli encode the local contours from source images via the displacement of dots laid on a regular grid; thus resulting stimuli feature only LSF information.23 The displacement is controlled by a single parameter, g, which is calibrated in a certain range (e.g., 0–0.3), such that 0 corresponds to no information about the object being present in the stimulus (no dot displacement), and 0.3 corresponds to a highly visible and easily recognizable object. Intermediate g levels are used to manipulate task difficulty, where the amount of sensory evidence about the object is proportional to the g parameter.

Conceptual framework and hypotheses across experiments

The four experiments investigated five hypotheses, according to which gaze bias is influenced by: (1) the level of visibility (experiments I, II, and III), (2) the information content of the explored stimuli (all experiments), (3) task difficulty (experiment III, but also experiment II vs. experiment I), (4) the task-time limit (experiment IV vs. I-III), and (5) conspecific face (experiment II) versus generic object discrimination (experiments I, III, and IV). To investigate hypothesis (1), in experiment I we used generic objects and divided the subjects into three groups, according to the presentation visibility order of stimuli (see experimental block structure and visibility manipulation). One group of subjects received only ascending visibility stimuli (g = 0 first), the second group only descending visibility stimuli (g = 0.3 first), and the third had visibility distributed randomly across the seven experimental blocks. To further investigate hypothesis (1) and to test if gaze bias is conspecific (5), in experiment II we used only face stimuli.

To determine the impact of task difficulty, in experiment III we used stimuli corresponding to 40 objects presented at two visibility levels (g = 0.2 and 0.3), and also 40 corresponding stimuli generated from the configurally scrambled images of the 40 objects (by rearranging global structure), which we call “non-objects”. Non-objects were also presented at the same two visibility levels (g = 0.2 and 0.3). Hypothesis (4) was investigated in experiment IV, which consisted of a time-restricted visual exploration task. Finally, we tested hypotheses (1) and (2) in experiments I-III by precisely controlling the available information through the visibility parameter, g. Experimental block structure and visibility manipulation

Stimuli were presented in blocks of multiple trials, with a pause in between blocks when the subject could rest. We used stimuli with different levels of visibility (g),23 optimizing the visibility parameter ranges according to each experiment. For experiment I, we used 7 blocks of 50 stimuli, corresponding to 7 visibility (g) levels, and with three distinct groups of subjects as a function of visibility order (ascending, descending, and random). For the ascending case we pooled in an additional dataset using the same stimulation protocol, but only with a subset of 30 objects of the 50 (filtered to select the objects most reliably recognized; for example, some participants had a systematic difficulty recognizing an AK47 gun in the original 50 object set—such stimuli were eliminated). For the ascending group, g levels evolved in increments of 0.05, from g = 0.00 (no information present in the stimulus) to g = 0.30 (object information easily retrieved from the stimulus). For the descending protocol, the stimulus visibility parameter started from g = 0.30 for the first block and decreased to g = 0.00 for the last block. For the random protocol, visibility levels were randomly spread across the blocks for each object.24 Importantly, stimuli from blocks with g = 0.00 did not contain any information about the source object, but dots were placed on the grid with a small random jitter to avoid the instant recognition that information was missing. For details, please see.23

In experiment II, we used 9 blocks of 20 stimuli. Each of the 9 blocks consisted of 20 stimuli (10 human faces, 10 monkey faces). Blocks were presented in the following order of visibility: ascending from g = 0.14 (block 1), to g = 0.42 (block 5), and descending back to g = 0.14 (block 9). Thus, from one block to the next, the difference in g value was +0.07 (ascending) or −0.07 (descending). As a result, visibility followed a symmetric up and down profile, enabling us to also study hysteresis effects.23,33 In experiment III, we used 8 blocks of 20 stimuli each. Each block consisted of stimuli of 10 objects and 10 non-objects. Half the stimuli were generated at visibility level g = 0.2 and half at g = 0.3. Thus, each block had a balanced mixture of objects and non-objects, and balanced visibility levels. The assignment of objects/non-objects × g value across blocks was made randomly, while meeting the balancing constraints. Experiment IV consisted of 8 blocks of 25 stimuli per block. In this case, the visibility level was fixed to g = 0.25.

Task instructions and perceptual decision procedures

In experiment I, subjects were instructed to decide if they saw a meaningful object in the Dots stimuli (yes/no/uncertain) and to subsequently report the identity of the object in case they saw it.23 If they were uncertain, they were asked to guess. In experiment II subjects had to decide between two perceptual outcomes: if the presented stimulus represented the face of a human or a monkey. In experiment III, we designed a more elaborate strategy. A subset of (10 subjects) were told that they should decide if the stimulus represented some meaningful object, in which case they had to name it, or if it did not represent anything known (non-object). Another subset (6 subjects) were naive: they were only asked what they saw, without receiving any hint that some stimuli do not make any sense. In addition, all the subjects underwent an alternating fixation instruction for odd and even experimental blocks: they were either instructed to fixate in the center of the stimulus (odd blocks), or could freely execute saccades and fixations across the stimulus (even blocks). In all cases, they had unlimited time to reach a perceptual decision. In experiment IV, subjects had to either report what they see, or simply say that they do not know (without having to guess).

For the free-viewing experiments (I-III) subjects were instructed to press or release a keyboard button when a perceptual decision was made. In the time-limited exploration experiment (IV), stimulus presentation was fixed to a duration of 1500 ms. Subjects were instructed to verbalize their response only after the button press (experiments I-III) or after trial timeout (experiment IV). In each trial, during the baseline period (prior to stimulus presentation), subjects were instructed to fixate on a dot placed in the center of the screen for 1.5–2 s (uniform random distribution). Thus, in each trial, the initial location of the gaze was in the center of the screen, which corresponded to the center of the object.

Qualitative and quantitative evidence for rightward gaze lateralization

Eye movements were recorded in all experiments using a calibrated eye-tracking system while participants viewed centrally presented stimuli. Data were corrected for calibration drift using baseline fixation and were preprocessed with in-house software to remove blinks, artifacts, and invalid trials, followed by automated detection of saccades and fixations based on velocity thresholds and manual verification of trace quality. Representative raw and curated eye-tracking traces are shown in Figure S1. Gaze lateralization for each trial was quantified as the mean horizontal position of fixations relative to stimulus center, with negative and positive values indicating leftward and rightward bias, respectively. Spatial exploration was additionally characterized by computing the horizontal spread of fixations separately in the left and right visual hemifields (see Method Details).

To illustrate the visual exploration strategies of subjects in terms of gaze direction for the different experiments, we generated several heatmaps (Figure 2) using custom MATLAB code. To that end, Gaussian filtering was applied to smooth the distribution of fixation count, using a Gaussian standard deviation of 12.5 pixels, which represents 0.18 visual degrees. Figure 2 indicates a variety of visual behaviors. In experiment I, fixation count and spread follows the observations reported in the studies by Moca et al. and Gal et al.,23,24 being strongly dependent on the direction of visibility manipulation and priors. However, a clear predominance of lateralization is difficult to observe for the selected examples.

By contrast, a systematic right gaze bias is observed for faces in experiment II: a higher proportion of fixations were directed at the right hemiface, from the viewer’s perspective (i.e., in the right visual hemifield). This gaze bias was consistent for all visibility (g) levels and for both human and monkey faces. Importantly, the task in experiment II was much more difficult to solve than that in experiment I because, in the absence of high spatial frequency details, subjects had a hard time disentangling the face of a human from that of a monkey. Average lateralization per condition reached as much as 17% (0.6°) of face width (3.6°) in this case.

In experiment III, while lateralization is not seen for objects that are easily visible (g = 0.2 or 0.3), for the case of non-objects, which present significant challenges to process (especially for naive subjects), a clear right gaze bias is observed. Finally, a clear right-gaze bias is also observed for experiment IV, where the availability of the stimulus was time-limited (1500 ms).

These qualitative observations suggest that gaze bias has a tendency to shift toward the right side, either when the task is more difficult, or when the visual information is available for a limited time. To go beyond the qualitative heatmap observations, we next performed quantitative analysis of gaze lateralization. Lateralization of gaze was analyzed for each subject, in each experiment. We investigated the gaze bias associated to stimuli depending on their g levels and in relation to presentation order (experiment I: ascending, descending, or random group) or as a within subject variable (experiment II: block number). For experiment III we grouped the analyses by objects/non-objects and visibility level variables. For the time-restricted exploration task (experiment IV), gaze bias was analyzed according to the four stimulus categories (animal faces, cars, objects, and human faces). We quantified gaze bias per trial, which was measured as the distance between the center of the stimulus and the fixations’ center of mass (see Methods). Gaze bias values were expressed in visual degrees.

To compare gaze bias values (trial averages) with chance level, we performed two-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for each experiment (α = 0.05) with Bonferroni corrections for multiple comparisons. In experiment I, for the task involving 50 objects, a significant gaze bias was observed for g = 0.10 in the ascending condition, g = 0.25 to 0.15 in the descending condition, and all g levels for the random condition (Table 1). For the task involving the reduced object set, presented in ascending visibility order, we found significant lateralization in the first three and two intermediate blocks (g = 0.00 to 0.10 and g = 0.20 to 0.25; Table 1). In experiment II lateralization was significantly different from zero in all conditions and across all visibility levels (Table 1). In experiment III, significant lateralization was only observed for non-objects, while in experiment IV it was observed for all stimulus categories (Table 1). In the random condition a robust right gaze bias was visible that decreased linearly as experimental blocks progressed (Figure 3A, third column). Statistical testing showed both that there is a significant difference between the first and the last block (paired t tests), and that lateralization decreased following a monotonic trend (Mann-Kendall tests) (Table 2). A similar trend was observed when data from the Random presentation dataset were grouped according to visibility (Figure 3A, fourth column), indicating that both visibility and priors24 modulate gaze lateralization.

At individual-subject level, gaze lateralization exhibits substantial variability, evident in all conditions, with participants differing both in the magnitude and direction of gaze lateralization (Figure S2A). Despite this variability, a systematic modulation of gaze bias with visibility can be observed across subjects. In the ascending and descending conditions, individual trajectories are more heterogeneous, consistent with additional contributions from expectation and learning effects induced by stimulus order. Nevertheless, across all three presentation strategies, a majority of subjects show relatively more rightward gaze lateralization at lower visibility levels and reduced or reversed lateralization as visibility increases, indicating that the effect is not driven by a small subset of participants but reflects a general tendency across individuals.

In an additional dataset in experiment I, we used only a subset of 30 objects from the original 50, selecting only the objects most reliably recognized at low visibility. In this case, right gaze bias decreased systematically with increasing visibility (Figure 3B). There was a significant difference between lateralization in the first block (no visibility) and that in the last block (full visibility). In addition, the trend for decreasing lateralization was monotonic (Table 2). As in the previous datasets, substantial inter-individual variability is evident, with subjects differing in both the magnitude and direction of gaze lateralization (Figure S2A). However, compared to the case with 50 objects presented in ascending order, gaze lateralization on the reduced set is notably rightward in the first block. This pattern likely reflects differences in perceptual strategies during the initial, zero-information block (g = 0.00): some participants invested substantial effort in attempting to recognize the stimulus, whereas others disengaged quickly and proceeded to the next trial. Consistent with this interpretation, all subjects who spent more than 6 s on average per trial in the first block showed moderate to strong rightward gaze lateralization (Figure S2B). Overall, while in both tasks, using 50 and 30 objects, a reduction of rightward gaze lateralization with increasing visibility is observed, the results on the reduced object set suggest earlier and stronger engagement, likely driven by differences in stimulus familiarity and strategy. In all cases, however, we found that increased perceptual and cognitive effort is associated with a stronger rightward gaze bias.

This was further confirmed in the next analysis. Despite pronounced inter-individual variability, we observed that stimuli that were more difficult to process (low visibility; g = 0.10 or g = 0.15) elicited stronger rightward gaze lateralization—or, equivalently, reduced leftward gaze bias—compared to stimuli that were easier to recognize (g = 0.25 or g = 0.30). When data from all datasets in experiment I were pooled (29 subjects) and grouped by visibility level (difficult vs. easy), gaze lateralization had consistently more positive values (i.e., rightward) for difficult than for easy stimuli (Figure 3C; p = 9.87 × 10−6, post-hoc power = 0.99; paired t test; see Table 2).

In experiment II, we found a pronounced effect of visibility on lateralization for monkey and human faces, regardless of stimulus order (Table 2 and Figure 4A). For this experiment, visibility was modulated upward, from low to high, and then back downward, across successive experimental blocks, following an inverted “U” shape. By contrast, gaze lateralization followed exactly the opposite pattern: large for low visibility and low for high visibility. Significant or close-to-significant contrasts were observed between the blocks with largest (peak) lateralization and the blocks with lowest lateralization (middle blocks, highest visibility), for both human and monkey face stimuli (Table 2). This modulation was evident and highly consistent also at single subject level (Figure S2C). Matching the previous observations that visibility and gaze bias tend to evolve in opposite directions, a significant negative correlation (Pearson) was found between visibility (g) and lateralization (correlation was computed by considering visibility level and lateralization across individual trials; Figure 4B). Across free-viewing exploration experiments, gaze bias revealed a robust perceptual optimization mechanism that adapts to stimulus identification difficulty and accumulated familiarity. Specifically, lower-visibility and otherwise more difficult stimuli (e.g., non-objects) or tasks (human vs. monkey faces) consistently elicited stronger rightward gaze lateralization—or a reduction of leftward gaze bias—whereas increased visibility and familiarity were associated with reduced or reversed lateralization. This pattern was further modulated by perceptual effort, with greater task engagement linked to more pronounced rightward gaze biases. Importantly, these effects generalized across stimulus categories, including objects and faces, and were observed similarly for human and monkey faces, indicating that gaze lateralization reflects a general adaptive visual strategy rather than category-specific processing.

Task constraints modulate the early emergence of gaze lateralization

Since differences in task demand, such as free viewing versus time-restricted exploration, could lead to distinct visual integration strategies, we investigated whether gaze bias depended not only on the difficulty (visibility) of the stimuli but also on the specific task. We compared the gaze bias for g = 0.25 of the free viewing exploration experiment (experiment I) and the gaze bias for the objects condition of the time-restricted exploration experiment (experiment IV). These sets of stimuli are similar: same level of visibility, g = 0.25, and similar source images for the objects, but they corresponded to two different tasks: free and time-limited exploration. We investigated if, besides the lateralization magnitude’s dependency on visual difficulty, gaze bias also depended on visual integration strategies along the trial. Therefore, we analyzed gaze bias corresponding to the first fixations after stimulus onset. In early trials stimulus properties dominate over prior expectations

Further, we performed a post-hoc investigation based on Gal et al.’s (2024) previous work, where it was reported that priors could influence gaze bias.24 They found a larger lateralization when stimuli were presented in random visibility order (random g values within blocks), which was also linked to an impairment of the participants’ ability to build stimulus-specific priors. This suggests that there is a relationship between priors and lateralization: stronger priors are associated with a smaller lateralization. We have reanalyzed data from Gal et al. (2024), which includes the data from experiment I presented here. To determine whether gaze lateralization is driven purely by prior expectations, or by the inherent properties of the stimuli (such as visibility), we analyzed the first block of trials for the three groups (ascending, descending, and random). In these early trials, subjects had not yet acquired stimulus-specific priors—pre-existing knowledge or expectations about the stimuli—allowing us to isolate the influence of stimulus visibility on gaze lateralization. We tested this by looking at the correlations between the trial number (index) and the corresponding lateralization for all three groups. For the random group, we also computed correlations between the g values in the trials of the first block and their corresponding lateralization, as well as compared several g value pairs.

As illustrated in Figure 9, we found no association between trial number and lateralization, i.e., lateralization did not follow any trend as the first experimental block progressed. Statistically significant differences were only found when comparing a small g value (g = 0.05) to the largest g values (g = 0.20–0.30) for the random group. Importantly, since in the first block subjects were experiencing for the first time each of the 50 individual objects (no familiarity effect at this point), these findings suggest that gaze lateralization was primarily driven by the inherent properties of the stimuli, such as their visibility. Rightward dominance in spatial gaze exploration across experiments

Gaze bias was computed as the offset from the center of the stimulus of the average across all fixation locations in each trial (average position). In a final analysis, we investigated how the gaze was spread to the left and right visual planes (relative to the center of the screen). For example, we wanted to know if, e.g., low visibility influences only spread to the right side or if it actually leads to a larger, general spread, with more lateralized fixations on both sides, but with a more prominent spread to the right side. Results in Figure 10 provide interesting answers. In all experiments the lateral spread of fixations (average across subjects) was systematically larger across the right side, indicating a propensity to more extensively explore the right side for Dots stimuli. In experiment I, for ascending and descending presentations, both gaze spreads to the left and to the right side were modulated by visibility level, albeit in different ways. In conditions of low visibility and without priors, subjects explored both sides extensively, however, covering larger lateral spreads on the right side (Figure 10A, Dots50 ascending). When full information was provided early in the experiment, spread on both sides followed a “U” shape, with generally larger spread to the right, especially for intermediate visibility levels (Figure 10A, Dots50 descending). For the random case, lateral spread was more flat across blocks, with a tendency to slightly decrease compared to the other tasks (Figure 10A, Dots50 random). For the reduced object set presented in ascending order of visibility, results were similar to the ascending case for the full object set but the trend was more linear, confirming the stimulus set optimization resulting in more uniform recognition (Figure 10A, Dots30 ascending). Overall in experiment I, general dominance of rightward spread is observed in 21 of 29 subjects, with the others expressing intermediate effects (Figure S5A).

In experiment II, fixation spread across visibility levels remained relatively stable in the left hemifield, whereas in the right hemifield it was larger and varied as a function of visibility, following a “U”-shaped pattern with the smallest spread at the highest visibility level (Figure 10B). This pattern was observed for both conspecific and heterospecific faces and, compared to experiment I, it suggests that visual exploration strategies for faces differ from those employed for generic objects. The pattern we observed was consistent across individual subjects (Figure S5B).

For experiment III, a slight familiarity effect is seen across experimental blocks (decreasing spread trend; Figure 10C, left & middle), with a slight tendency for right spread to dominate. However, the stronger effect on spread was induced by the instruction (fixate or free exploration), which is expected given that ballistic eye-movements were more constrained in the “fixate” condition (Figure 10C, right). In a majority of cases, right fixation spread tended to dominate, especially for the case of non-objects (Figure 10C, right). At individual subject level and across conditions (objects & non-objects × fixate & free exploration), rightward spread was dominant (32 cases), compared to very slight leftward spread dominance (18 cases), and balanced left-right spread (14 cases) (Figure S6A).

Finally, in the time-restricted experiment IV, fixation spread was highly unbalanced, with a strong bias toward the right hemifield, both at group (Figure 10D) and individual subject level (Figure S6B). At block level, we did not observe a clear trend for left and right gaze spread. This can be contrasted with the case of the random task in experiment I (Figure 10A, random), where both the fixation spread to the left and the spread to the right side tended to decrease with the accumulation of priors.24 While in experiment I the same set of objects was presented in each block (albeit at different, random visibility), experiment IV used different sets of objects in different blocks, preventing development of task-specific priors (knowledge about objects). Nevertheless, we would have expected at least task-general priors to develop in experiment IV, but this is not observed. A confounding factor that we cannot rule out is the temporal constraint on the response of the subject. It is possible that an optimization of fixation spread cannot be achieved in the time-restricted task due to the lack of sufficient time for visual exploration.

Discussion

Eye movements, or gaze behavior, are correlated with perceptual and cognitive processes relevant to the observer’s interactions with the environment.34 Gaze patterning over time reflects an active visual sampling that depends on cognitive demands34 and changes with task difficulty.35 Moreover, stimulus properties, such as spatial frequency, are also expected to drive gaze patterning because processing of such properties displays hemispheric lateralization.21 Faces are special in this regard, as they produce a stereotypically lateralized fixation pattern in the left visual hemifield.3,36

Here, we investigated whether a specific type of gaze pattern, namely gaze lateralization, depends on the experimental task, and studied its association with conspecific-sensitive facial information in stimuli containing only LSF features. We used Dots stimuli, which have several advantages: they contain only LSF components, luminance is rather constant across the whole set of stimuli, and potentially confounding information such as texture, color, shading, etc., is not present in the stimulus. Only inferred contour information (Gestalt) is available and, importantly, the amount of such information (the visibility) can be controlled precisely by a single parameter, the visibility level, g.23,24 Dots stimuli thus convey controllable configural information about object identity, stripping away other confounds, and enabling the robust study of lateralized configural processing. Abundant evidence exists about the propensity of the RH to process configural structure,17,21 but it has remained unclear whether the visual system actively optimizes this lateralized processing through adaptive visual sampling, specifically by shifting gaze toward the right visual field. Such a shift may manifest either as a reduction of an initial leftward gaze bias or as the emergence of a rightward gaze lateralization. Our results show that configural information is indeed extracted via active sampling strategies that dynamically adjust gaze position toward the right as perceptual demands increase. Importantly, the magnitude and direction of this gaze shift are modulated by sensory evidence, task constraints, and the acquisition of task-specific priors over the course of the experiment.

First, our findings indicate that visual adaptation strategies are dependent on accumulation of task-specific priors during task execution. Specifically, during free-viewing exploration (experiment I) gaze lateralization is determined by a non-linear interaction between stimulus familiarity (having seen the stimulus recently) and the magnitude of sensory evidence. When sensory evidence progressively increases (Figure 3A, ascending) or decreases (Figure 3A, descending), gaze bias exhibits an inverted “U” shape, indicating the initial accumulation of task-specific priors, which may be associated with higher cognitive loads,37 accompanied by an increase in gaze bias. In the following blocks, the magnitude of this bias decreases as the subjects become more familiar with the stimuli. When sensory evidence is random, and is not systematically manipulated across blocks, familiarity drives the magnitude of gaze bias progressively down, from block to block (Figure 3A, random), but slower because the development of robust task-specific priors is impaired by randomness.24 Interestingly, a higher cognitive load during random fluctuation of sensory evidence is accompanied by an overall larger right gaze bias.24

To determine if task-general priors (knowledge about object location, object size, etc.)24 have an impact on gaze lateralization, we analyzed the first block of the ascending (g = 0), descending (g = 0.30), and random (random g values) groups, where task-specific priors (familiarity) are absent. According to our results, there is no relation between trial progression and gaze lateralization, at least on a short term (within the first block), indicating that task-general priors, accommodating the subject to the task, do not seem to drive lateralization bias alone. We conclude that task-specific (familiarity) rather than task-general priors are more reliably modulating the right gaze bias.

An interesting observation emerges when considering results for the random group. Previous work24 showed that random manipulation of stimulus visibility impairs participants’ ability to form explicit task-specific priors, such as object familiarity, resulting in poorer behavioral performance compared to both ascending and descending conditions. Despite this impairment at the behavioral level, we observed a systematic modulation of gaze bias across successive blocks in the random condition (Figure 3A, random), suggesting that some form of familiarity or learning may still be operating at an implicit level. This dissociation raises the possibility that visual sampling strategies can adapt to repeated exposure even when such adaptation does not translate into improved overt performance. In other words, while random visibility may prevent the formation of stable, explicit priors that support decision making, it may still allow the gradual tuning of oculomotor behavior, reflecting an unconscious or low-level form of perceptual learning that guides gaze allocation independently of conscious recognition.

Second, our results confirmed the hypothesis that the magnitude of sensory evidence influences gaze bias. For the free-viewing exploration experiments (experiment I and II), gaze bias was influenced by the level of visibility (higher gaze offset values for lower visibility), irrespective of the order of stimulus presentation (ascending, descending, or random). Furthermore, bias did not depend on the objects’ degree of symmetry, indicating that it cannot be explained by a spatial property of the stimulus, but rather by the visual exploration strategy of the subject. The relationship between gaze bias and sensory evidence is emphasized by “U”-shaped curves for experiment II (Figure 4), with the lowest gaze bias values for the highest g values (most visible stimuli). This may indicate a perceptual optimization mechanism that compensates stimulus difficulty (lack of visibility) with increased right gaze lateralization.

Third, our results demonstrate that gaze lateralization is strongly modulated by task difficulty independently of stimulus visibility alone. In experiment III, configurally scrambled non-objects—despite being matched in visibility to objects—elicited significantly stronger shifts of gaze toward the right visual field in both naive and informed participants. This effect indicates that difficulty arising from the lack of meaningful global structure, rather than from reduced sensory evidence per se, is sufficient to induce enhanced rightward gaze shifts. Importantly, this finding complements the results of experiment I, where lower visibility increased gaze lateralization, by showing that difficulty can also emerge from stimulus interpretability. Moreover, comparing experiments I and II further supports this interpretation: face discrimination in experiment II was overall more demanding than generic object recognition in experiment I under LSF-only conditions, and was associated with stronger and more consistent rightward gaze shifts. Together, these comparisons indicate that gaze lateralization scales with the overall perceptual challenge posed by the task, whether driven by limited sensory evidence, reduced stimulus familiarity, or intrinsic ambiguity of the stimulus.

Fourth, comparing free-viewing exploration (experiment I) and time-restricted exploration (experiment IV), we found that the time available to access information influenced gaze bias (Figure 8). After the first fixation, when there was little or no lateralization due to task design (baseline fixation), the early stages of visual sampling seemed to benefit from a rapid divergence from the center, with considerably steeper divergence for time-limited vs. free-viewing tasks. An increased lateralization in the early stages of visual perception could be linked to similar mechanisms used in the encoding of gist,36,38 where gist is understood as the conceptual (semantic) and perceptual aspects of a stimulus.39 Indeed, gist seems to be supported by more peripheral vision,40 relying more on LSF information. It would be interesting to study to what degree gist recognition is also lateralized, e.g., relying more on information in the left visual hemifield than on the right one.

Fifth, analysis of the spatial extent of visual exploration (fixation spread) in the left and right visual planes (Figure 10) revealed a robust tendency for subjects to explore a larger region of the right visual field when viewing Dots stimuli. Beyond this overall asymmetry, fixation spread was systematically modulated by stimulus visibility and the accumulation of priors, in a manner that depended on task demands and stimulus category. For generic objects, reduced visibility led to broader exploration on both sides of the stimulus, indicating increased sampling under uncertainty; however, the magnitude of the asymmetry between left and right spread varied with visibility progression and with the development of task-specific priors. In contrast, for faces, fixation spread in the right visual field was strongly modulated by visibility, whereas spread in the left visual field remained relatively stable, suggesting a distinct exploration strategy for this stimulus class. Finally, fixation spread tended to decrease with the accumulation of task-specific priors, but not task-general priors, consistent with reduced perceptual uncertainty as familiarity increased. This systematic reduction in lateral exploration was evident in free-viewing tasks but was largely absent in the time-restricted condition, where limited viewing time likely constrained the optimization of spatial exploration.

Our findings are summarized in Figure 11, which presents a qualitative model capturing the main factors that shape rightward gaze shift for stimuli containing only LSF information. For simplicity, linear relationships are illustrated, although the underlying dependencies are likely to be non-linear. In this model, gaze bias emerges from the interaction of three principal variables: stimulus familiarity (task-specific priors), temporal availability of sensory information, and perceptual difficulty. Notably, perceptual difficulty can arise from multiple sources, including reduced sensory evidence (lower visibility levels), intrinsic stimulus ambiguity (e.g., non-objects), and elevated task demands, such as challenging discrimination requirements—for example, distinguishing human from monkey faces in the absence of HSF. As stimulus familiarity increases, rightward gaze shift decreases, reflecting reduced uncertainty. Conversely, reduced temporal availability, as in time-limited viewing, leads to increased rightward gaze shift. Likewise, increased perceptual difficulty—regardless of whether it stems from limited visibility, ambiguity, or task demands—results in stronger gaze shifts toward the right. For any given task, rightward gaze shift is thus expected to emerge from the combined influence of these factors. For face stimuli, robust rightward lateralization patterns were observed but they did not differ across stimulus species (human vs. monkey faces), at least under LSF conditions. This suggests that any conspecific gaze bias, if present, may depend on HSF information, which was absent in our stimuli. Nevertheless, during free-viewing exploration, faces (both human and monkey) elicited stronger gaze bias than generic objects, fruits, and whole-body animals (experiment I vs. experiment II; Figure 3 vs; Figure 4). One possible explanation for this difference is that experiment II imposed higher perceptual demands than experiment I, as distinguishing human from monkey faces based solely on LSF information is intrinsically difficult. Alternatively, or in addition, faces may recruit specialized perceptual mechanisms that engage distinct exploration strategies even when only configural information is available. Consistent with this latter interpretation, the modulation of gaze bias by visibility differed between faces and generic objects: for objects, reduced visibility led to broader exploration on both the left and right sides, whereas for faces, low visibility selectively increased exploration toward the right visual field. This dissociation suggests that faces may constitute a special stimulus category with respect to visual sampling strategies. Importantly, however, no differences were observed between conspecific and heterospecific faces when using Dots stimuli containing only LSF information, consistent with previous findings showing that face species has little influence on gaze patterns and that eye movements are primarily guided by general facial configuration.41 Finally, in the time-restricted exploration task (experiment IV), no preferential exploration strategy for faces was observed, as gaze lateralization did not differ across stimulus categories, suggesting that temporal constraints may limit the deployment of face-specific exploration strategies that are otherwise evident during free viewing.

The RH is widely regarded as specialized for visuospatial processing.4 For example, RH dominance in spatial tasks has been demonstrated in line bisection studies: Ishiai et al. (1989) showed that accurate line bisection is facilitated by directing gaze toward the right, thereby placing a larger portion of the stimulus in the left visual field. In this context, shifting gaze toward the right—rather than maintaining central fixation—was argued to be functionally advantageous because it optimally engages RH processing of spatial information.42 Subsequent studies have similarly reported systematic rightward shifts of gaze during free-viewing visual bisection tasks, independent of stimulus length, or orientation.2 More generally, it has been proposed that, in visuospatial tasks, shifting gaze slightly to the right of center enhances processing by biasing visual input toward the left visual field and, consequently, the RH.4 In line with these findings, our results show that when visual input is restricted to LSF information, object categorization is accompanied by systematic shifts of gaze toward the right.

Our results are consistent with the theory of an RH dominance for processing configural information encoded in LSFs17,21 and suggest that this may be a universal mechanism involved in object recognition. Indeed, we observed a predominantly right gaze bias in all the experiments involving Dots stimuli, irrespective of object category. Faces were no exception. At a first glance, our findings may seem inconsistent with the left gaze bias reported in the literature for faces.3,36 This divergence cannot be explained by a difference in stimulus size: Faces used in our study spanned on average 3.5°× 4.9°, which is not dramatically smaller than stimulus sizes used in other studies.5,8 However, another, simpler explanation may exist: For unfiltered face stimuli, when HSF information is also present, gazing on the left hemiface enables the extraction of fine details around the central part of the face (nose, mouth), which fall into the right hemifield and are processed by the HSF-sensitive LH.21 This is indeed needed for the fine discrimination that is required for face identification. In our case, object categorization did not require extracting such fine information, but processing only LSF was sufficient for achieving a reasonable task performance. Hence, gaze lateralization is driven by necessity, reflecting dynamic and flexible processes that are modulated by the specific visual context.

Our results reveal a complex interplay of factors—such as task difficulty and the development of priors—that shape gaze lateralization during configural object processing. Although a rightward gaze bias emerges as the predominant pattern at the group level, individual differences exist: some participants show an overall leftward baseline bias. Critically, however, these individuals exhibit the same rightward shift with increasing configural demands as right-biased participants. Thus, the key phenomenon is not the absolute direction of baseline gaze, but the systematic rightward modulation of gaze under low-information or high-demand conditions. We conclude that both the rightward bias and the rightward shifts observed in left-biased individuals reflect a common perceptual optimization mechanism.

Although some individual datasets involved a modest number of participants, the central finding—a systematic shift of gaze toward the right under increased perceptual demand—was replicated across multiple independent datasets, task paradigms, and sources of difficulty. Pooled subject-level analyses yielded consistent effects, and the large number of trials per participant enabled reliable estimation of gaze lateralization. Nevertheless, results from individual experiments should be interpreted with appropriate caution, and the conclusions of this study are best supported by the convergence of evidence across experiments rather than by any single dataset.

Handedness has been shown to influence perceptual lateralization in certain domains, particularly in face processing, where it can modulate hemispheric specialization and visual field preferences43,44 However, evidence for a consistent effect of handedness on configural form recognition is mixed and highly task-dependent, with several studies reporting no significant handedness effects in simple form recognition or dot-based tasks.45,46 Given that our experiments specifically target configural processing using low spatial frequency stimuli, existing evidence suggests that handedness is unlikely to exert a strong or systematic influence on the mechanisms under investigation. Consistent with this view, the small number of left-handed and ambidextrous participants in our sample did not exhibit gaze patterns that differed qualitatively from those of right-handed participants. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the predominance of right-handed participants represents a limitation of the present study, and future work should explicitly examine the role of handedness in shaping gaze lateralization and visual sampling strategies under conditions of perceptual uncertainty.

An open question concerns whether the magnitude of gaze shifts toward the right scales with stimulus size. Although such scaling might be expected in principle, this relationship is unlikely to be straightforward in the context of Dots stimuli. Object size is tightly coupled to inter-dot spacing, viewing distance, and the formation of Gestalts that support configural recognition; scaling the stimulus would therefore alter not only its spatial extent but also perceptual organization and task difficulty. In addition, larger stimuli would likely elicit qualitatively different exploration strategies, including increased fixation counts and broader scan paths, making it difficult to dissociate true scaling effects from strategy changes. As stimulus size was held constant in the present study, this issue represents a limitation and an important direction for future work, which would require a dedicated design that systematically varies size while controlling perceptual and oculomotor factors.

Although the Dots stimuli used in this study are artificial and do not resemble natural images, they offer a key methodological advantage by isolating configural information while minimizing confounding low-level features such as texture, shading, and color. This controlled design allows precise manipulation of the amount of global structure available in the stimulus and facilitates the systematic investigation of configural processing mechanisms. Importantly, conditions in which visual perception relies predominantly on LSF information are common in real-world vision. Such situations include reduced visibility environments (e.g., fog or low contrast), brief stimulus exposure where only coarse information can be extracted, and visual impairments associated with aging, such as cataracts, which disproportionately degrade high spatial frequency information. In this sense, Dots stimuli provide a useful abstraction of perceptual regimes in which global structure must be inferred from sparse sensory input, making them well suited for probing adaptive visual sampling strategies that are likely to operate beyond laboratory settings.

Our study may also be relevant for the development of machine vision in artificial intelligence systems. Recent work has highlighted fundamental differences between human and artificial vision systems, showing that humans rely strongly on configural cues for object recognition, whereas many deep neural networks exhibit little sensitivity to relational structure. As a result, current models often fail to capture a core aspect of human object perception, underscoring the need for computational systems to be trained on tasks that explicitly require configural integration.47 Addressing this limitation is particularly important given that neural networks are in principle capable of exploiting configural information to differentiate categories even when local features are identical; incorporating such mechanisms more explicitly would allow models to more closely approximate human neural responses and behavioral patterns.47,48 Complementing this perspective, recent eye-tracking work has shown that human fixations align selectively with hierarchical feature representations extracted from deep convolutional neural networks, providing direct evidence of which visual features guide gaze and, by extension, which representations computational models must capture to reflect human perceptual strategies.49 In this context, our finding that gaze systematically shifts toward the right visual field as stimuli become more difficult to process—consistent with a right-hemisphere advantage for configural processing—offers a behavioral constraint that may inform the development of more perceptually aligned neural network models. Specifically, interactions between featural and configural processing could be implemented through hierarchical or recurrent architectures in which early global structure guides or constrains subsequent feature-level refinement, mirroring the human tendency to rely on coarse relational information and to adapt visual sampling strategies under conditions of limited sensory evidence. Spiking neural networks may be crucial for the implementation of such models because they naturally incorporate time as a coding dimension,50 enabling coarse-to-fine recognition mechanisms which unfold successively in time.16

In summary, our findings show that rightward gaze lateralization during visual exploration reflects an adaptive perceptual strategy that optimizes the integration of configural information when sensory evidence is limited. By systematically manipulating visibility, familiarity, task difficulty, and temporal constraints using LSF stimuli, we demonstrate that gaze bias is dynamically regulated rather than fixed, and is closely linked to perceptual effort and uncertainty. This bias is implemented through asymmetric exploration of the visual field, with an expanded fixation spread toward the right hemifield, and generalizes across objects and faces without being driven by conspecificity. These results suggest that gaze lateralization is a flexible behavioral mechanism that supports efficient visual processing under constrained conditions, revealing how active sampling strategies interact with hemispheric specializations during early perception.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of the present study should be considered. Although the central rightward gaze shift was replicated across multiple paradigms, some individual experiments involved modest sample sizes, and therefore the strongest support for the conclusions derives from the consistency of effects across datasets rather than any single experiment. In addition, the use of Dots stimuli containing only low spatial frequency information enabled precise control of configural structure but necessarily reduces ecological validity, and future work should determine whether similar adaptive gaze shifts emerge when both coarse and fine spatial information are available in natural images. Finally, because the present study relies on behavioral eye-tracking measures, the neural mechanisms underlying adaptive gaze lateralization remain to be directly established.

Experimental model and study participant details

Subjects

Sixty subjects took part in this study (both females and males), age ranged between 19 and 44 years. They were either undergraduate psychology students (participation course credits incentive) or unpaid volunteers. The subjects had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, were right-handed (except one ambidextrous and one left-handed participant), and were assigned to one of four experiments. Twenty-nine subjects participated in Experiment I (16 females, mean age 27.06 years, SD 5.89 years). Of these, 18 performed a recognition task of 50 objects with varying visibility,23,24 presented in different orders of visibility across blocks (see below). Six subjects performed the “Ascending” protocol, 6 the “Descending” protocol, and 6 the “Random” protocol. The other 11 subjects performed a recognition task on a subset of 30 of the 50 objects. A number of seven (4 females, mean age 23.5 years, SD 7.71 years) subjects participated in Experiment II, undergoing a conspecific face perception protocol. In Experiment III, we contrasted perception of objects with that of their configurally scrambled version (Non-Objects), testing a total of sixteen subjects (12 females, mean age 21.98 years, SD 3.71 years). Finally, in Experiment IV we investigated visual perception in a time-restricted setup, testing eight subjects (6 females, mean age 25.75 years, SD 3.45 years). All experiments were performed with the participants’ prior informed consent and were approved by the local ethics committee (approval 1/CE/08.01.2018), while observing the relevant national (Romanian Law 190/2018) and European (Directive (EU) 2016/680) legislation for protection of personal data.

Method details

Stimulus presentation

A constant luminosity display was used to present stimuli to participants, calibrated using a Voltcraft 1300 Luxmeter. Stimuli were shown in a controlled environment and cheek-rests were used to stabilize the subjects’ head and maintain constant distance from the display. The stimulus interdot spacing corresponded to 0.1015 visual degrees, yielding a total stimulus span of 8.7° × 5.6°. Each object or face was precisely localized in the center of the corresponding stimulus, thus avoiding spurious lateralization biases. The stimuli that were used for the four experiments can be freely downloaded from here: https://tins.ro/sources/lateralization/Stimuli_Gaze_Lateralization.rar.

Eye-tracking data and computation of gaze lateralization

For each experiment, eye-tracking measurements (ASL EyeStart 6000 system; 50 Hz sampling rate; 0.5° accuracy and 0.1° resolution) were performed, while stimuli were presented on a 22-inch monitor (Samsung SyncMaster, 226BW LCD) placed 1.1 m in front of the subjects. To compensate for eye-tracking offsets and noise, a 9 points eye-tracker calibration was performed before each block. Fixations during the baseline period (see above) were further used during data analysis to correct for any potential eye-tracking calibration drifts along each block. Fixations in each trial were re-referenced post-hoc such that the baseline fixation would be perfectly aligned with the fixation dot’s position (center of the screen).

Data were pre-processed with an in-house software,23,24 which enables data cleaning (e.g., for record loss, blinks, velocity threshold adjustments), offline filtering, and manual curation to eliminate potential recording issues. Offline filtering consisted of excluding data outside trial boundaries, removing outlier pupil diameter values, excluding trials with an insufficient number of valid samples, removing data points outside the screen boundaries, detecting and marking blinks, and applying impulse and ramp removal to the horizontal and vertical position signals. Detection of fixations and saccades was performed with the same software, which uses a simplified version of the two-threshold velocity detection algorithm,51 adapted to the 50 Hz sampling rate of our eye-tracker. In short, two thresholds were computed per trial from the statistics of each trial’s velocity. A threshold of 3 × standard deviation was used to detect saccades. Once detected, saccades were expanded while velocity was above a lower threshold of 1.5 × standard deviation. Fixations were then defined as the intervals between successive saccades.

Following automatic detection, eye-tracking traces were visually inspected on a trial-by-trial basis using the “Regard” (in-house) software interface, allowing direct interaction with horizontal (H) and vertical (V) position signals, raw and filtered velocity profiles, and pupil-related signals. Specifically, fixations that did not reach the velocity threshold were manually corrected by adjusting the velocity classification (when high-velocity fluctuations were not correctly detected automatically). In addition, trials in which no reliable velocity signal could be identified were marked as “Excluded manually”. We have also excluded trials with more than half of the samples marked as bad (e.g., pupil loss, cornea loss, or record loss), trials in which the baseline fixation could not be determined, and trials containing no valid fixations. Furthermore, segments incorrectly classified as fixations due to threshold-related velocity fluctuations were manually corrected by assigning a lower velocity state, based on concurrent inspection of horizontal and vertical raw position traces.

Manual decisions were guided by the combined evaluation of H and V raw signals and their corresponding filtered traces, such that a fixation was considered valid only when both dimensions exhibited stable, velocity behavior. This combined inspection ensured that fixations reflected genuine gaze stability rather than threshold-driven segmentation artifacts. Additional details on fixation analysis can be found in.23,24

For a given trial, t, the gaze bias (lateralization) was computed as the average across horizontal positions (horizontal center of mass) of all fixations within the trial:(Equation 1)GBt=1Nt∑i=1Ntxiwhere, Nt is the number of fixations in trial t, and xi is the horizontal coordinate of fixation i, expressed in visual degrees relative to the center of the stimulation image (center of the screen). By convention, the center of the screen (and stimulus) has coordinates (0,0), such that fixations on the left side have negative coordinates, while those on the right have positive ones. A negative value of GB indicates left gaze bias, while a positive one indicates right gaze bias.

We defined the spread on the left side, SL, as the standard deviation of positions of all fixations within trial t that fall on the left side:(Equation 2)SLt=1NLt−1∑i=1NLt(xi−xL¯)2,xi<0where, NLt is the number of fixations on the left side, and xi is the horizontal coordinate of fixation i on the left side, xL¯ is the average horizontal position across fixations on the left. Similarly, the spread on the right side was computed by considering only fixations on the right side (xi > 0).

Trials with no fixations were excluded from the analysis. Similarly trials that had no fixation on one side were excluded from the statistics computed on that side.

Quantification and statistical analysis

To test whether gaze bias differed from zero (no lateralization), we used two-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for each experimental condition (Table 1). For planned within-condition comparisons across blocks/visibility levels and stimulus classes, we used paired t-tests (Table 2). For monotonic trends across ordered blocks, we used the Mann–Kendall trend test (Table 2). Associations between continuous variables (e.g., visibility parameter g and gaze bias in specified analyses) were tested with Pearson correlation and, where appropriate, Spearman rank correlation. All tests were two-tailed unless stated otherwise. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, with Bonferroni correction applied where multiple comparisons were performed (Table 2). Effect sizes are reported alongside key tests (e.g., Cohen’s d for t-tests; Kendall’s τ for Mann–Kendall), and post-hoc power is reported for many comparisons in Table 2. Data are summarized as means with SEM where indicated (error bars and shaded regions in figures denote SEM; see figure legends).

To assess whether the normal distribution model adequately described the data, Shapiro–Wilk tests were applied for each experimental condition. Normality assessments varied across comparisons: in some cases the data did not show a significant departure from normality, whereas in others normality was clearly violated, indicating pronounced asymmetry. Because the distribution properties differed across analyses, both parametric and nonparametric tests were next used as appropriate, with nonparametric tests providing robustness against departures from normality and against outliers. All statistical analyses were performed in MATLAB.
