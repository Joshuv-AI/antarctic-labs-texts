Signal-driven interplay between lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis orchestrates osteoarthritis degeneration
Authors: MdF Kulyar, Jolita Pachaleva, Sandra Brazaite, Muhammad Akhtar, Mohammad Mehedi Hasan, Saeed Kaboli, Ignas Lebedis, Eiva Bernotiene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoarthritis (OA) is progressively documented as a whole-joint disorder in which oxidative stress, iron dysregulation and intercellular communication together drive progressive tissue degeneration. Among the chief oxidative mechanisms, lipid peroxidation has arisen as a critical contributor to cartilage destruction and inflammatory signalling cascade. Reactive aldehydes produced during lipid peroxidation, mainly malondialdehyde (MDA) and 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal (4-HNE), function as electrophilic mediators that change signalling pathways, extracellular matrix components and proteins, including NF-κB and MAPK. Persistent oxidative stress converges on ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death characterized by impaired GPX4 activity, lipid peroxide accumulation and glutathione depletion. Ferroptotic chondrocytes also amplify osteoarthritic progression by releasing matrix-degrading enzymes and inflammatory cytokines that impact neighbouring osteoclasts, osteoblasts and synoviocytes. Additionally, mechanical stress contributes to this pathological network through Piezo1/TRPV4-mediated mechanotransduction, connecting aberrant biomechanical loading to intracellular calcium imbalance, ferroptosis activation and iron accumulation. Accumulative evidence specifies that dysregulated iron homeostasis plays a dual role in OA pathogenesis, as iron overload promotes reactive oxygen species generation through Fenton chemistry, whereas iron deficiency impairs antioxidant defence and osteogenesis mechanisms. Therapeutically, biomaterial-based nano-delivery systems, antioxidant compounds, iron chelators, and ferroptosis-targeted interventions have confirmed potential in restoring redox balance and suppress OA-associated inflammation. Cerium oxide, selenium, and MnO2 nanozymes, together with smart intra-articular delivery platforms, provide targeted reactive oxygen species scavenging and improve therapeutic localization within inflamed joints. By integrating mechanotransduction, iron metabolism, ferroptosis and lipid peroxidation, inter-tissue communication into a unified mechanistic framework, this review highlights emerging diagnostic biomarkers and translational strategies for the development of disease-modifying therapies in osteoarthritis.The translational potential of this articleThis review proposes an effective cascade mechanism wherein mechanical and inflammatory stimulation results in iron deregulation, lipid peroxidation, ferroptosis, and finally the development of osteoarthritis. The assessment of biomarkers related to ferroptosis such as MDA, 4 HNE adducts, labile iron, and GPX4 enzymatic activities can be useful for stratifying early-stage osteoarthritis and monitoring the disease. Meanwhile, antioxidant molecules, iron chelating compounds, and nanotechnology-based intraarticular drug delivery systems represent promising strategies for the prevention of osteoarthritis development.Keywords: Chondrocyte signalling, Ferroptosis, Iron homeostasis, Lipid peroxidation, Osteoarthritis, Redox signalling

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a complex whole-joint disease involving coordinated pathological interactions among extracellular matrix (ECM) components, immune cells, chondrocytes, subchondral bone, and synovium. Cartilage degeneration and lipid peroxidation are closely interrelated during OA progression, as chondrocyte lipid peroxidation facilitates collagen oxidation, cartilage degradation, accelerated cartilage ageing, and altered mitochondrial dynamics [1]. As reviewed by Kan et al., collagen oxidation heightens cartilage susceptibility to mechanical fatigue and aggravates OA by dysregulating cartilage injury responses [1]. Studies in post-traumatic osteoarthrosis rat and in-vitro chondrocyte models have demonstrated that early-stage OA activates lipid peroxidation, accompanied by elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydroxyl radical production, contributing to osteoporotic bone loss and synovial inflammation, though direct validation in human OA joints remains pending [2,3]. These findings indicate that lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress are significant mediators that link inflammation, progressive joint degeneration and mechanical injury in OA.

Among products derived by lipid peroxidation, 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal (4-HNE) is the most biologically active and toxic aldehydes implicated in different pathological conditions, e.g., rheumatoid arthritis (RA), cancer, cardiovascular diseases, neurological syndromes, inflammatory diseases and diabetes [[4], [5], [6]]. 4-HNE, a highly reactive electrophilic aldehyde, interacts with lipids, proteins and nucleic acids, modulating NF-kB, Nrf2 and ERK, and influencing oxidative stress, cell death, cell proliferation, and inflammation [7]. Based on analytical analysis, MDA is a documented lipid peroxidation biomarker and it reacts with amino acids to generate MDA-glycine, MDA-lysine, and malondialdehyde-acetaldehyde adducts, that are implicated in pancreatitis, atherosclerosis, RA and inflammatory diseases [6,8].

Ferroptosis is a distinct type of regulated cell-death associated with iron-dependent oxidative damage. Its core features has also been consolidated around iron accumulation, phospholipid peroxidation, impaired glutathione-GPX4 defence and catastrophic membrane injury. In transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis of human temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis (TMJ)-OA synovial fluid, osteoarthritic joints are exposed to chronic abnormal loading, low-grade inflammation, disturbed lipid metabolism, and altered iron handling, thus, these features have become relevant [9]. As established in recent reviews, targeting ferroptosis has emerged as a potential therapeutic strategy for osteoarthritis as ferroptotic signaling is associated with inflammatory injury, synovial inflammation, mechanical stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid peroxidation, iron overload, subchondral bone remodeling and cartilage degradation [10,11].

Current therapeutic approaches emphasis to suppress lipid peroxide accumulation, enhance endogenous antioxidant defenses, re-establish iron homeostasis and block ferroptosis-related signalling [10,11]. In the murine destabilized medial meniscus (DMM) OA and in-vitro chondrocyte models, Deferoxamine is an iron chelator that could reduce ROS generation, decrease intracellular iron accumulation and safeguard cartilage integrity in experimental OA model, though in-vivo confirmation in human studies is required [12]. In human cross-sectional study, conventional antioxidants show poor joint retention, limited targeting efficiency and rapid clearance and an increasing attention has been shifted toward nanomaterial-based delivery systems [13,14]. These advances position ferroptosis modulation as a potential disease-modifying approach for osteoarthritis rather than a purely symptomatic intervention.

This review presents lipid peroxidation, mechanotransduction, ferroptosis, iron metabolism and biomaterial-based therapeutic strategies as distinct themes rather than as associated components of a single pathological sequence. Thus, this review aims to organize the core mechanisms around a coherent causal framework: inflammatory and mechanical stimuli → iron dysregulation → lipid peroxidation → ferroptosis → OA progression → therapeutic targeting. This integrated approach clarifies evidence hierarchies, distinguishes associative findings from validated mechanisms, and identifies redox-related biomarkers and therapeutic targets for disease-modifying OA interventions. This cohesive approach might help explain evidence levels, differentiate associative findings from validated mechanisms and recognize therapeutic targets and redox-related biomarkers for disease-modifying OA interventions. In particular, we addressed bidirectional inter-tissue communication, including synoviocyte-to-chondrocyte, chondrocyte-to-osteoclast, and osteoclast-to-chondrocyte signalling axes and presented a more complete picture of OA pathobiology.

Mechanical and inflammatory stimuli that start the cascade

In OA cartilage, chondrocytes do not enter ferroptosis randomly; rather, they are first subjected to excessive mechanical forces, inflammatory cytokines, and altered paracrine signals from neighbouring tissues. This section delineates how these upstream stimuli converge to prime the joint microenvironment for iron dysregulation and oxidative injury.

It has been found that in in-vitro and in-vivo models, mechanical overloading induces chondrocyte ferroptosis through calcium influx and Piezo1 activation. Blocking calcium entry or Piezo1 augmented GPX4 expression, decreased ferroptotic features and reduced OA severity, directly connecting mechanotransduction to ferroptosis rather than treating ferroptosis as a late by-product of joint damage, though confirmation in human studies is required [15]. In OA, mechanical input is not uniformly damaging. It is also observed that moderate treadmill exercise in monosodium iodoacetate (MIA)-induced OA rats and cyclic tensile strain in chondrocytes decreased matrix degradation and ferroptosis. This study recommends that protective mechanical loading increases SLC7A11 and GPX4, suppresses NF-κB p65 signalling, activates Nrf2 and decreases p53 and MMP13. This context-dependence underscores that OA is not simply a disease of “mechanical stress,” but a disease in which the context of stress and pattern define whether mechanotransduction is adaptive or damaging [16].

Inflammation converges on the same pathway. Exosomes from IL-1β-activated synovial fibroblasts triggered OA-like changes in chondrocytes, indicating that inflammatory synoviocytes could transmit pathology without direct cell contact. Specifically, it has also been observed that OA fibroblast-like synoviocyte (FLC) exosomes worsen OA damage by delivering miR-19b-3p to chondrocytes, targeting SLC7A11 and increasing cartilage ferroptosis, offering a vibrant synovium-to-cartilage route by which inflammatory stress lowers anti-ferroptotic defence, and validation in human OA joints still needs to investigate [17]. Bidirectional communication also warrants consideration: chondrocytes under oxidative stress release HMGB1 and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that, in turn, activate synovial macrophages and fibroblasts, perpetuating a feed-forward inflammatory loop. Additionally, synovial-fluid extracellular vesicles from severe OA patients triggered a catabolic chondrocyte and pro-inflammatory phenotype with greater MMP1, MMP3, MMP13, IL-6, and pain-related mediators. This study strengthens the logic that OA joint microenvironment itself could prime chondrocytes for oxidative injury and matrix breakdown [18]. Inflammatory mediators released during cascade initiation disturb joint iron handling and convert external stress into intracellular iron overload.

Bidirectional inter-tissue signalling and ferroptosis crosstalk in OA

OA progression depends on isolated cellular events within individual tissue compartments and also on coordinated signal exchange among chondrocytes, synovial fibroblasts, osteoblasts, osteoclasts and macrophages. Understanding the reverse signalling dimensions is indispensable for identifying OA therapeutic targets.

Chondrocyte-to-synoviocyte reverse axis. The communication of reverse axis is not unidirectional. Chondrocytes subjected to oxidative injury and ferroptotic stress release DAMPs, e.g., S100A8/A9 alarmins, extracellular ATP and HMGB1 into the synovial fluid [19,20]. These DAMPs are recognized by receptor for advanced glycation end-products (RAGE) and TLR4 on FLS and synovial macrophages, triggering NF-κB-associated pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α [20,21]. Type II collagen degradation products and fibronectin fragments are released during chondrocyte ferroptosis and act as matrikines, which stimulate synovial hypertrophy, MMP production and FLS proliferation, though direct validation in human OA joints remains pending [22,23]. This reverse axis therefore constitutes an active amplification mechanism with which cartilage ferroptosis escalates synovial inflammation, sustaining joint destruction cycle.

Osteoblast-to-osteoclast axis. Within subchondral bone, 4-HNE-mediated lipid peroxidation in osteoblasts establishes a paracrine signalling axis, promoting pathological bone resorption. 4-HNE accumulation and oxidative stress in osteoblasts increase receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (RANKL) expression, whereas suppress osteoprotegerin (OPG) and augment RANKL/OPG ratio, governing osteoclast differentiation [24,25]. This shift drives osteoclast activation and osteoclastogenesis through RANKL-RANK signalling, promoting subchondral bone resorption [24,25]. Concurrently, 4-HNE triggers cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) in osteoblasts through ATF-2/CREB and p38 MAPK pathways and produces prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), stimulating bone resorption and osteoclast differentiation via EP2/EP4 receptor signalling [26,27]. 4-HNE-produced increased IL-6 stressed osteoblasts and synergistically acts with RANKL to increase osteoclastogenesis, providing a mechanistic insight into subchondral bone sclerosis and advanced OA [25].

Osteoclast-to-chondrocyte reverse axis. Reciprocally, osteoblast-derived signals activate osteoclasts and release IGF-1, TGF-β1 and Ca-Pi complexes that feed back to the overlying cartilage through subchondral vascular channels and microcracks, sensitizing chondrocytes to oxidative injury. Active osteoclasts secrete tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) produces ROS via ferroxidase activity, contributing to the local oxidative burden at the subchondral bone-cartilage interface [28,29]. Elevated ROS impairs chondrocyte antioxidant defences and upsurges susceptibility to GPX4-dependent ferroptosis in deep-zone chondrocytes. Cathepsin K, released during osteoclastic bone resorption, cleaves type II collagen and generates bioactive collagen degradation fragments, penetrating deep cartilage and activating chondrocyte catabolic responses through integrin α2β1-mediated signalling [23,30]. These integrin signals converge on Src pathways and focal adhesion kinase, which encourage MMP upregulation and NF-κB activation in chondrocytes, hastening ECM degradation and creating an oxidative microenvironment permissive for ferroptosis.

Chondrocyte-to-osteoclast axis. Under oxidative stress, chondrocytes increase RANKL expression, which diffuses from articular cartilage to the subchondral bone, potentially promoting osteoclastogenesis within the subchondral bone marrow [31,32]. In response to IL-1β or TNF-α stimulation, inflamed chondrocytes produce a CSF1R ligand, CSF-1 (M-CSF) and promotes macrophage differentiation towards osteoclast precursors in the joint environment, connecting subchondral bone erosion and cartilage inflammation [33,34]. Also, 4-HNE and MDA produced during lipid peroxidation in OA articular tissues have been detected in the osteoblasts of subchondral bone, where 4-HNE modifies bone cell metabolism by enhancing pro-osteoclastic mediators including PGE2 and COX-2, encouraging the differentiation osteoclast precursor and augmenting bone resorption [35,36]. Although it signifies a plausible mechanistic link between subchondral bone erosion and cartilage ferroptosis in late-stage OA, the relative contribution of this axis compared with osteoblast and synovial-mediated pathways warrants additional authentication in human in-vivo studies.

Mechanical stress-induced ferroptosis: role of mechanotransduction

Ferroptosis, an iron-coordinated lipid peroxidation, is distinct from necrosis and apoptosis [37]. In a recent review, physical forces, i.e., shear, tension, and compression, applied to cells/tissues are termed mechanical stress, while the mechanism by which cells sense and convert such mechanical signals into biochemical cues is termed mechanotransduction [38]. Recent evidence indicates that ferroptosis is closely linked to mechanical stress and that mechanotransduction modulates stress-mediated cell death, though confirmation in human studies is required [39]. Mechanotransduction, ferroptosis, and mechanical stress together form a destructive triad that drives chronic inflammation and joint [40].

Such mechanical forces constantly affect articular cartilage during joint movements, and abnormal, repetitive, and excessive mechanical stresses disrupt matrix synthesis and cause joint degeneration [41]. At the cellular levels, mechanical stress-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction disrupts antioxidant capacity and increases ROS production, leading to an imbalance in redox reactions [42]. Unbalanced redox triggers lipid peroxide accumulation in membranes, a hallmark of ferroptosis, leading to GPX4 downregulation and failure to detoxify lipid hydroperoxides in chondrocytes [42]. Concurrently, the cystine/glutamate antiporter system Xc−, which imports cysteine and synthesizes glutathione, is decreased, thereby reducing cellular antioxidant reserves [43]. Iron overloading could also increase chondrocyte sensitivity for mechanical stress-mediated ferroptosis [44]. In human OA and RA, cartilages exhibit unbalanced expression of ferroportin (FPN) (increase) and transferring receptor 1 (TFR1) (decrease), which are responsible for iron export and uptake, respectively, leading to labile iron accumulation within cells [45]. Based on mechanistic hypothesis, iron accumulation generates hydroxyl radicals via the Fenton reaction, thereby exacerbating lipid peroxidation [46]. As mechanically stressed-mediated joints have higher levels of iron and its handling proteins, such an environment promotes ferroptosis [39].

Chondrocytes recognize mechanical stress via cytoskeletal elements, primary cilia, TRPV4 and Piezo1 ion channels, and integrins, which transfer mechanical signals intracellularly through Hippo-YAP (Yes-associated protein)/TAZ (transcriptional co-activator with PDZ-binding motif), Rho/ROCK, PI3K/AKT, and MAPKs [47]. As mechanical load or matrix stiffening increases in arthritic joints, YAP/TAZ is translocated into the nucleus, regulating oxidative stress, matrix remodeling, and cell survival-associated gene transcription [47]. Nevertheless, continuous activation of YAP enhances ACSL4 expression in an in-vitro model, which catalyzes the incorporation of polyunsaturated fatty acids into the phospholipid membrane, thereby increasing their susceptibility to peroxidation, though direct validation in human OA joints still needed [48]. Concurrently, YAP decreases GPX4 expression, leaning towards ferroptosis [48]. A novel mechanical pressure is generated by hyperplastic synovium in the subchondral bone and cartilage during RA and interacts with TNF-α and IL-1β, the proinflammatory cytokines, thereby promoting ferroptosis [49]. In an in-vitro chondrocyte model, IL-1β downregulates GPX4 and SLC7A11, thereby further increasing chondrocyte vulnerability to ferroptosis [50]. Because arthritis can cause joint bleeding, which releases iron from hemoglobin, it can exacerbate pain in patients with RA due to the inflamed synovium [51]. Mechanotransduction also contributes to ferroptosis during arthritis. Inflammatory and mechanical stimuli trigger neutrophils and macrophages to produce nitric oxide and ROS, activating lipid peroxidation in surrounding cells [40,52]. Further, in murine and chondrocyte models, synovial inflammation alters macrophage polarization, producing M1 macrophages that are more associated with ferroptosis and tissue damage [53]. Chondrocytes cytoskeleton, including intermediate filaments, microtubules, and actin filaments, is also related to ferroptosis and mechanotransduction sensitivity because mechanical stress-mediated remodeling in the cytoskeleton influences the functions of the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria [52,54]. Endoplasmic reticulum stress enhances inflammatory cytokine secretion and disrupts glutathione synthesis in an in-vitro model, while mitochondrial fragmentation promotes lipid peroxidation and ROS generation [55,56]. Disruption of the cytoskeleton disrupts the trafficking of proteins involved in iron handling, altering iron homeostasis and increasing chondrocyte stress in chondrocyte OA model [57]. These studies indicate that mechanotransduction, ferroptosis, and mechanical stress, a destructive triad, drive chronic inflammation and degeneration in joints in OA, warranting further investigation in clinical settings. Too much free iron sparks Fenton chemistry and extra oxidative stress, while too little iron impairs the bone-building cells that support the joint surface. MRI R2∗ mapping or labile-iron assays could highlight which imbalance is present in each patient [45,46,58]. Combining local iron chelators with systemic iron repletion forms a two-way strategy to calm inflammation and keep subchondral bone healthy. Such personalized iron management is a clear step from bench discovery to bedside care.

Iron dysregulation converts joint stress into biochemical vulnerability

Based on the mechanical and inflammatory stimuli, iron dysregulation represents the critical biochemical step that converts upstream joint stress into cellular ferroptotic vulnerability. Iron dysregulation drives ferroptosis through oxidizing membrane phospholipids beyond cellular repair systems. Iron dependence of ferroptosis has been recognized GPx4 as the principal enzymatic brake [59]. Ferroptosis sensitivity is determined by ACSL4 through enriching membranes with polyunsaturated fatty acid substrates, whereas GCH1-BH4 and FSP1-CoQ10 act as auxiliary protective systems upon compromising GPx4. Typically, iron provides the spark, polyunsaturated fatty acid membrane sets the fuel load and the GPx4/FSP1/GCH1 arrangements regulate whether the oxidative damage is contained or progresses to cell death [11].

Iron maldistribution vs iron overload in OA

Although iron is indispensable for cell function but weakly bound or free iron is dangerous because it could intensify radical chemistry. Cellular iron balance is dependent on controlled uptake, storage, release and recycling. Iron normally arrives the cell through divalent metal-ion transporter1 (DMT1)-related transport and transferrin receptor-dependent pathways, is buffered in ferritin and exits through ferroportin. Ferritinophagy adds another layer: NCOA4 could transport ferritin to autophagosomes, freeing stored iron back into the labile pool. When this system becomes biased toward import and release rather than safe storage and export, cells become vulnerable to ferroptosis [60]. This imbalance is observable in OA-like inflammatory conditions. Another study revealed that iron overload accelerates OA progression and DMT1-mediated iron influx is particularly important, supporting that pro-inflammatory cytokines disturb chondrocyte iron homeostasis by enhancing DMT1 and TFR1, while decreasing ferroportin, thus encouraging intracellular iron retention, oxidative stress and cartilage degeneration [60]. Tis mechanisms has also been confirmed by several animal and tissue studies. Systemic iron overload exacerbated OA in a guinea pig model and hereditary hemochromatosis-related iron overload augmented OA progression under mechanical stress in mice. Recently, iron-overload experiments in human joint tissue explant cultures and in mice revealed an altered joint iron metabolism associated with elevated OA markers in synovium and cartilage, indicating a correlation that iron excess could actively intensify joint pathology [61].

Cell-intrinsic susceptibility also has its own importance. In primary chondrocytes from Hfe-knockout mice, exposure to iron produced an OA-like phenotype, which is marked by elevated metalloprotease expression and a weaker extracellular matrix production and greater susceptibility to intracellular iron accumulation. The implication is that OA-relevant iron toxicity is not only about how much iron is present in the organism but also about whether joint cells could store and export it safely [62]. Several studies support the damaging role of iron maldistribution or iron overload. Nevertheless, not all data support a simple “more iron is always worse” model. In the OA model of guinea pig, systemic iron reduction through an iron-deficient diet decreased cartilage lesion severity. On the other hand, a 6-year human cohort found a U-shaped relationship between knee OA progression and dietary iron intake with both low and high intake associated with increased risk outside a middle range. Thus in OA, a precise wording is that iron imbalance matters and the strongest mechanistic evidence still points toward dysregulated intracellular iron handling, retained iron or overload rather than a straightforward deficiency mechanism [63]. Human biomarker studies add further complexity. A recent study revealed that serum iron and transferrin saturation were positively related to OA incidence. Another study described a positive causal relationship for transferrin saturation and an inverse one for transferrin but not for every iron marker. Yet another study indicated that high systemic iron status might be causal for hip OA and total hip replacement, particularly through the HFE-linked variant rs1800562. Other studies also linked higher ferritin to symptomatic knee OA in men and to greater arthroscopic cartilage damage. Thus, the human literature supports clinical relevance, but it is not yet perfectly consistent across study designs, joints and biomarkers [64]. Ferritin is useful but it is also an acute-phase reactant. Soluble transferrin receptor could add information and is broadly used to distinguish iron deficiency from inflammatory iron restriction, yet even soluble transferrin receptor (sTfR) is influenced by inflammation to some degree. A practical OA translation strategy is therefore to interpret ferritin together with transferrin saturation and C-reactive protein (CRP) and to consider sTfR as an adjunct rather than a stand-alone answer [65]. Having established how iron dysregulation creates the biochemical conditions for oxidative damage, the following section examines how lipid peroxidation products, principally MDA and 4-HNE propagate this damage through chondrocyte and bone cell signalling processes.

Roles of 4-HNE in chondrocytes during OA

Lipid peroxidation is mechanistically active than just a passive marker in OA cartilage. 4-HNE modifies chondrocyte redox status, decreases collagen II, and upregulates MMP-13 catabolism [66]. Proteomics of human OA chondrocytes recognized 4-HNE-modified protein, e.g., histone targets, endorsing that lipid peroxidation leaves a quantifiable molecular signature in human cartilage [67]. ACSL4 and 4-HNE are increased in mouse and human OA cartilage with functional improvement following AMPK/ACC-directed treatment, supporting ACSL4 as a driver [68]. In OA cartilage, higher 4-HNE disturbs both catabolic and anabolic functions of chondrocytes through transcriptional/post-translational regulation of collagen II and matrix metalloproteinase-13 (MMP13) [6]. Specially, at the mRNA level, 4-HNE hinders collagen II and activates MMP13, whereas accelerates collagen II degradation and activates MMP13 at the post-translational level [6]. 4-HNE binds with collagen II or other proteins and alters chondrocyte phenotype/function, disrupting extracellular matrix homeostasis and encouraging cartilage degeneration [69]. HNE-modified histone deacetylase, H2A/H2B and histone proteins could develop adducts with SOX9 in OA chondrocytes, contributing to matrix degradation via ubiquitin-mediated pathway [6]. 4-HNE is also involved in inflammatory responses and apoptosis in articular tissues and chondrocytes in OA by upregulating cyclooxygenase-2 via activating transcription factor/cAMP response element (ATF/CRE) and inhibiting inducible nitric oxide synthase via NF-kB deactivation [10,70]. Reactive oxygen species-driven oxidative damage, inflammatory stimulation and apoptosis contribute to cartilage degeneration and arthritis progression, as 4-HNE suppresses Akt kinase activity and triggers p53 and Fas/CD95 expression in chondrocytes [6]. In opposition, glutathione S-transferase A4-4 modulates 4-HNE cytotoxicity and its knockdown promotes HNE-mediated chondrocyte death [6]. Further, carnosine could abolish 4-HNE production and decrease cartilage degeneration, whereas aldehyde dehydrogenase 2 increases 4-HNE detoxification, alleviating mono-iodoacetate-triggered oxidative stress, inflammation and apoptosis in chondrocytes by suppressing aquaporin 4 [71,72].

Roles of MDA as an oxidative stress marker in OA

MDA is an alternative main lipid peroxidation product that is recognized as an oxidative stress marker, facilitating collagen oxidation in cartilage. In anterior cruciate ligament transection-mediated OA, serum concentrations of collagen type-II (C2C) degradation marker and MDA are increased, demonstrating a positive association between cartilage breakdown and oxidative injury [2,73]. Likewise, elevated serum levels of carboxy propeptide, C-telopeptide degradation products of C2C, and MDA were observed in STR/Ort mice compared with CBA mice, though direct validation in human OA joints remains pending [74]. Some clinical reports also reported elevated MDA concentrations in synovial fluid and plasma of OA patients. However, further inquiry is still needed to explain its prognostic and diagnostic value [75]. MDA could react with acetaldehyde, forming malondialdehyde-acetaldehyde (MAA) adducts that are immunogenic, exhibiting pro-fibrogenic and pro-inflammatory properties implicated in alcoholic liver injury and atherosclerosis [6,76]. In RA, MDA-derived antibodies and antigens are involved in synovial pathogenesis and hypermutated anti-MDA immunoglobulin G clones encourage joint destruction and osteoclastogenesis by stimulating glycolysis and lipid biosynthesis in osteoclasts [6,76]. Together, these findings specify that lipid peroxidation products, principally MDA and 4-HNE substantially contribute to OA and/or RA pathogenesis through altered communication, apoptosis, extracellular matrix degradation, inflammation, and oxidative stress among osteoclasts, osteoblasts, chondrocytes and synovial tissues [6]. The above discussion reveals how lipid peroxidation products mediate tissue damage, the following section discusses iron metabolism that fuels this process at systemic and cellular levels.

Iron metabolism and homeostasis in cartilages and bones; comparing the iron deficiency vs iron overload in OA

Iron is an essential element in humans, playing a vital role in numerous physiological processes, particularly in cellular and systemic iron metabolism in cartilage and bone. Iron helps form a porphyrin complex termed “heme,” and iron's effective electron transfer capability enables it to accept or donate electrons, acting as a main catalytic cofactor in several biochemical reactions [77]. The duodenum, a part of the small intestine, absorbs almost all the iron available in ingested food. After absorption, Fe3+ is reduced to a more stable form, Fe2+, in the higher pH environment of the stomach/duodenum, and Fe2+ DMT1 helps transport this ion into the duodenum epithelium. After absorption, iron is assimilated by entering the blood circulation via a particular iron exporter, ferroportin (FPN). Hephaestin assists in oxidizing Fe2+ to Fe3+ and exporting iron from enterocytes to the iron pool in the systemic circulation [78]. In normal homeostasis, iron primarily binds to transferrin (TF) and is utilized during erythropoiesis, while senescent erythrocytes are eliminated by macrophages, and iron is subsequently transferred to the systemic iron pool via a ferroportin-mediated iron recycling mechanism [79]. Liver-secreted hepcidin hormone controls iron availability through ferroportin in the host body. In the spleen and gut, iron recycling and absorption are regulated by RNF217 (E3-ubiquitin protein ligase) via ferroportin ubiquitination and degradation [80]. Intake, consumption, and efflux are the three procedures with which iron transport in the cartilage and bone is processed in the cells. Iron uptake in the mammalian cells is facilitated by heme, ferritin, non-transferrin-bound iron (NTBI), and TF internalization [77]. TF-mediated internalization of circulating iron (Fe3+) in plasma is a normal chelator with higher affinity for Fe3+-binding sites and is facilitated by docking to the transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1). As TFR1 exhibits nanomolar affinity for TF-mediated iron binding and very low/no affinity for apotransferrin (apo-TF), reducing pH to 5.5 helps release Fe3+ from the TF/TFR1 complex upon endocytosis-mediated internalization, thereby recycling apo-TF and TFR1 [78]. Through ferrireductase action of six transmembrane epithelial antigens of prostate 3, Fe3+ is reduced to Fe2+, which is then released to the cytoplasm by DMT1 [81]. In in-vitro osteoclast and Rc3h1-deficient mice cultures, osteoclast function and differentiation are positively related to the TFR1 level, and osteoclast differentiation requires higher iron, which activates TFR1 expression via post-transcriptional regulation, indicating that TFR1-facilitated iron intake boosts bone resorption and osteoclast differentiation, though confirmation in human studies is required [82]. Iron could also be obtained via NTBI uptake through calcium channels, SLC39A8 (ZIP8), SLC39A14 (ZIP14), and DMT1, while ferritin uptake and porphyrin-bound iron absorption are other ways for internalizing the iron. In mitochondria, heme synthesis and the respiratory chain are facilitated by the iron transfer through mitoferrin 1/2. While the liver is a main iron storage organ, it is also stored in the form of cytosolic ferritin, unbound iron/labile-iron pool, and ferritin-bound iron. FPN entirely regulates iron efflux, maintaining iron homeostasis intracellularly and preventing iron-induced toxicity. FPN constrains osteoclast differentiation via modulating JNK, NF-κB, and TFR1 signalling in bones [78]. Iron metabolism is crucial for regulating osteoblast and osteoclast differentiation, as iron overload or deficiency dysregulates osteoclast/osteoblast differentiation, affecting bone resorption/production and triggering bone loss, thereby promoting OA [83]. The receptor activator of NF-κB ligand (RANKL) and/or osteoprotegerin (OPG) signalling controls osteoclast activation and differentiation, thereby affecting bone mass and bone integrity. In osteoclast differentiation, several mitochondria are required for energy provision, which is dependent upon iron processing, as during iron intake, TF and heme-dependent genes are upregulated, and ferritin transporters, i.e., Tim-2 and Scara-5, and NTBI iron-transporter, i.e., SLC39A14, are undetectable or downregulated, indicating that osteoclasts mostly get iron through TFR1. Thus, excessive iron triggers the promotion of the RANKL/OPG ratio, which activates osteoclast differentiation/bone resorption through TFR1-facilitated iron absorption. On the contrary, lactoferrin inhibits osteoclast-bone resorption and upsurges mineral density of bones, decreasing RANKL/OPG ratio, warranting further investigation in clinical settings [78]. Runt-related transcription-factor2 (Runx2) is an important transcription factor for osteogenesis because Runx2 knockout mice exhibit decreased bone formation and osteoblast deficiency causes their early death [84]. These findings suggest that iron overload promotes osteoclast activity and development, contributing to bone resorption.

Increasing serum iron and transferrin levels, including transferrin receptor, are positively related to the OA incidence, whereas total iron-binding capacity is negatively related to OA [64]. In mouse models of iron overload, iron regulatory factors are dysregulated, iron accumulation is promoted, and expression of disintegrin/metalloproteinase and thrombospondin motifs-5/matrix metalloproteinase-13 is increased [85]. Further studies in an animal model showed that pro-inflammatory parameters disrupt iron homeostasis by increasing the expression of DMT1 and the transferrin receptor-1 (iron import) and decreasing the expression of ferroportin (FPN) (iron export), thereby increasing the burden of iron overload in cells [86]. Thus, inhibiting DMT1 could decrease IL-1β expression and extracellular matrix degradation by inhibiting MAPKs and AKT/PI3K/NF-κB signaling. Iron overload is also closely linked with ferroptosis because ferric ammonium citrate and IL-1β promote iron overloading by increasing lipid ROS, apoptotic-associated parameters, and type-II collagen and reduced MMP-13, exhibiting that chondrocytes are more susceptible to ferroptosis-mediated OA under excessive iron and inflammation [78]. These studies reveal that iron metabolism is mainly controlled via intestinal absorption, recycling, and transporting.

Disturbances in iron homeostasis affect osteoclast differentiation via TFR1 and inhibit osteogenesis. Iron overload is associated with increased OA risk through altered expression of iron regulatory proteins, ADAMTS5, and MMP-13. Excessive iron encourages ferroptosis in chondrocytes via inflammatory, i.e., MAPK, PI3K, AKT, NF-κB pathways and oxidative stress. After iron intake in the diet, duodenal cytochrome b (DcytB) reduced the Fe3+, transferring to the enterocytes of the duodenum by DMT1. The epithelium of the duodenum absorbs almost all dietary iron, and heme oxygenase 1 (HO 1) degrades the dietary heme. After being transported to the bloodstream by ferroportin (FPN), Fe2+ is oxidized to Fe3+ by hephaestin, and then Fe3+ binds with the transferrin, forming holo-transferrin (holo TF or TF-Fe2). TF-Fe2 then travels to the tissues and is utilized there for erythropoiesis. Macrophages in the blood and liver Kupffer cells recycle iron by degrading senescent erythrocytes. Liver is a main organ for iron storage, and extra iron is stored in hepatocytes by the transporters located on cell's surface, i.e., TFR1 (Fe3+) and SLC39A14 (Fe2+). FPN regulates intracellular iron release and promotes iron export from the body, thereby maintaining stable iron levels. Liver synthesized another hormone, hepcidin, is a chief iron regulator that controls iron homeostasis by suppressing FPN. Aging stimulates iron accumulation and abnormal iron metabolism, producing free radicals (ROS) that elevate lipid peroxidation in chondrocytes and osteoblasts. Ferroptosis inhibitor protein 1 (FSP1) and GPX4 have antioxidant capacity, and declines in their levels disrupt the antioxidant system and oxidative stress response, leading to ferroptosis and, ultimately, to degenerative osteoarthritis. Understanding the mechanisms and molecular signatures of ferroptosis in OA

With upstream mechanical stimuli, iron dysregulation and lipid peroxidation now established, this section describes the specific molecular machinery by which ferroptosis is executed in OA joint tissues. OA has become a critical health concern in elderly individuals. OA is characterized by severe pain and joint swelling and is associated with reduced disability and mobility [87]. Functional and morphological changes in mitochondria, MDA accumulation, ACSL4 upregulation, and GPX4 downregulation are specific changes associated with ferroptosis-mediated cartilage in in-vitro human FLS cell culture, worsening OA pathogenesis [88,89]. For diagnosing and treating OA, transcriptomic analyses using GSE55457 and GSE51588 datasets indicate that Lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3) and osphogluconate dehydrogenase (PGD) may be potential diagnostic markers. Compared with healthy individuals, OA patients exhibit higher lipid peroxide levels, which facilitate ferroptosis in the affected synovium. In the case of hand osteoarthritis, Single-cell RNA sequencing indicated that the number of fibrocartilage and inflammatory chondrocytes increased in the affected joint cartilage under ferroptosis signalling [90,91]. It results in higher serum ferritin levels, which are significantly associated with hand osteoarthritis prevalence, suggesting that osteoarthritis may be initiated by the osteoarthritis-inflammation cascade in fibrocartilage chondrocytes, warranting further investigation in clinical settings. A higher iron level in the synovial fluid is positively and significantly associated with the OA severity because Fe3+, Fe2+, and the total iron were found significantly higher in OA cartilage, indicating iron overloading stimulated iron deposition in OA cartilage [92]. It is also noted that serum ferritin actively contributes to the cartilage injury in patients with symptomatic knee OA, suggesting its positive association with cartilage severity [93]. In human prospective observational and in-vitro and in-vivo studies, appropriate nutritional balance and iron intake can be easily managed, as adequate iron intake helps reduce OA progression, whereas excessive iron intake increases OA risk and progression [94,95]. Further studies have found that estrogen levels in premenopausal/postmenopausal women play crucial roles in OA progression. As menopause is a critical risk factor, premenopausal women face a lower risk of OA development compared with postmenopausal women [96]. The expression of the estrogen receptor, a G protein-coupled receptor30 (GPR30), was found to be lower in the OA cartilage compared with the normal cartilage. An application of GPR30 agonist G1 significantly promotes motor ability and chondrocyte viability by inhibiting ferroptosis in mice via YAP1/FTH1 signalling, whereas applying GPR30 antagonist G15 exhibited contrary effects, proposing that hormone-mediated ferroptosis could be an encouraging target to alleviate OA in postmenopausal women [92]. An intracellular iron level could be regulated through Nuclear Receptor Coactivator 4 (NCOA4) because NCOA4 was found to be higher in inflammatory chondrocytes, OA mice, and OA patients. JUN binds with the NCOA4 promoter, initializing its transcription factor, promoting ferritinophagy degradation, resulting in ferroptosis and degradation of the extracellular matrix in chondrocytes [97]. Patients with OA exhibited a downregulated antioxidant defense system, as GSH and oxidized GSSG levels were lower in synovial fluid. Thus, regulating the Nrf2 antioxidant system prevents ferric ammonium citrate (FAC)-or IL-1β-induced ferroptosis in OA chondrocytes via altering SLC7A11 and GPX4 levels [92,98]. In membranes of senescent cartilage, ferroptosis could be combated using excitatory amino acid transporter 1 (EAAT1), which intracellularly increases glutamate levels and triggers GSH synthesis, while binding of HSPA5 to GPX4 positively modulates its expression level [99]. SND1, RNA-binding protein, interacts with the 3′UTR of HSPA5 and destabilizes HSPA5 mRNA, suppressing GPX4 and enhancing ferroptosis in OA [100]. Further in human study and animal and in-vitro models, P21 alters GPX4 recruitment, acts as an anti-ferroptosis compound, and regulates GPX4 stability. Ferroptosis could be inhibited, and the degradation of the extracellular matrix could be prevented using FOXO3, which reduces OA progression through activating SLC7A11/GPX4 and suppressing NF-κB/MAPK signalling in chondrocytes [92,101]. As Piezo1 is a calcium channel that senses pressure in chondrocytes, it activates iron overload-mediated ferroptosis, thereby worsening calcium influx, as observed in GPX4 knockout cells. Thus, mechanical stress-mediated ferroptosis could be prevented by blocking calcium inflow [6,15]. The heterologous angiogenesis could be reduced, and the microstructure of subchondral osteosclerosis could be improved using metformin in OA [102]. As various lipids are associated with chondrocyte ferroptosis and promote pathogenesis, Lipoxin A4 could be used as a potent anti-inflammatory agent to suppress inflammatory processes by inhibiting inflammatory signals. Lipoxin A4 promotes estrogen receptor beta in fibroblast-like synoviocytes and GPX4 expression, and reduces MMP13/MMP3 expression, improving the pain and alleviating the cartilage and synovial lesions in OA rats via suppressing ferroptosis [92]. In in-vitro and in-vivo OA models, ATF3 and PTGS2 are the ferroptosis driving factors, and lipid peroxide, ROS, and Fe2+ are the ferroptosis accumulation markers triggered by tertbutyl hydroperoxide in chondrocytes and are inhibited by the nitrogen oxide XJB-5-131, a potent mitochondria-targeting antioxidant. Meanwhile, FTH1 and GPX4, which inhibit ferroptosis, could be used to promote cartilage anabolism, offering protective effects on chondrocytes [103]. Jianpi Tongluo Formula (JTF) decreases joint edema and prevents the degradation of extracellular matrix by inhibiting the NCOA4-HMGB1-GSK3β-AQPs axis [10]. Exosomes from mesenchymal stem cells promote the renewal of injured tissue, subchondral bone, and cartilage by regulating inflammatory response through activating Nrf2/HO-1 via GOT1/CCR2 pathway [104]. These studies reveal that ferroptosis is crucial in the progression of osteoarthritis (OA) by enhancing chondrocyte death, extracellular matrix degradation, and inflammatory responses, and by specifically disrupting the antioxidant defense system, lipid peroxidation, and iron metabolism. YAP1/FTH1, NCOA4, ACSL4, and GPX4 are key molecules that contribute to cartilage degeneration and are exacerbated by ferroptosis, mechanical stress, and hormonal imbalances (Fig. 2; Table 1). Loss of cartilage in OA is subject to the imbalance between catabolic and anabolic activities of chondrocytes. Matrix metalloproteinase, an extracellular-matrix catabolic enzyme, is upregulated in catabolic activity during OA, while proteoglycans and collagen type II, which are extracellular matrix elements, are decreased in OA [111]. Remodeling of subchondral bone is another important OA feature, and osteoclasts, supported by osteoblasts or osteocytes, release various mediators and increase bone resorption. In the synovium, fibroblasts and macrophages release pro-inflammatory cytokines, which diffuse into the articular cartilage, leading to cartilage damage [112]. Further, excessive iron activates intracellular ROS and downregulates GPX4, increasing chondrocyte sensitivity to oxidative stress, promoting the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL1β, TNFα, IL6) and matrix metalloproteinase (MMP13), thereby aggravating extracellular matrix degradation and accelerating osteoarthritis progression, as demonstrated in iron overload and IL-1β-induced chondrocyte culture, though confirmation in human studies is required [113]. When glutathione runs low, the enzyme GPX4 could no longer detoxify lipid peroxides, and chondrocytes undergo ferroptosis. Measuring GPX4 activity together with peroxide load could select patients for trials of ferroptosis-blocking drugs or glutathione boosters-agents already entering oncology studies. Protecting GPX4 function aims to save living cartilage rather than just easing pain, matching the disease-modifying goals of translational orthopaedics [114].

Ferroptosis centered mechanism connecting cartilage, subchondral bone, and synovium. Within chondrocytes, excess labile iron and impaired antioxidant defenses promote lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis, with system xc activity and GPX4 identified as protective nodes. Downstream, catabolic enzymes such as MMP13 and inflammatory mediators, including IL-6, TNF alpha, and IL-1, amplify extracellular matrix breakdown and propagate signals to synovium and bone. Osteoclast and osteoblast-derived mediators contribute to structural degeneration, illustrating bidirectional crosstalk between tissues.

Interaction between lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis in OA progression

Having established the individual roles of iron dysregulation, lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis in OA, this section discusses the dynamic interaction between ferroptosis and lipid peroxidation as it unfolds in OA progression, Lipid peroxidation closely interacts with ferroptosis of chondrocytes in OA. Ferroptosis is a type of cell death triggered by lipid peroxidation and iron overload. Inactivating the antioxidant system, i.e., coenzymeQ10 (CoQ10), glutathione, and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) encourages ferroptosis, which is mediated by LOOH accumulation, GPX4 depletion, and ROS generation, contributing to the pathogenesis of OA [44]. It has also been demonstrated in a recent review that chondrocyte ferroptosis plays a significant role in OA progression as ferric ammonium citrate and IL-1β trigger ferroptosis-associated proteins, i.e., SLC7A11, P53, ACSL4, and GPX4, and ROS/lipid ROS accumulation. GPX4 can suppress ferroptosis by eliminating lipid peroxides [115]. In OA, Xc−/GSH/GPX4 is regarded as a crucial signalling pathway because GPX4 regulates chondrocyte ferroptosis by extracellular matrix (ECM)-mediated suppression of NF-κB and MAPK signaling, suggesting a new idea for treating OA by targeting chondrocyte ferroptosis/GPX4, warranting further investigation in clinical settings [116]. However, ferroptosis is promoted by IL-1β by decreasing GPX4 levels in chondrocytes and is mediated by SND1, an RNA-binding protein, which facilitates ferroptosis by modulating the HSPA5-GPX4 axis, thereby revealing a new mechanism in which IL-1β triggers ferroptosis in chondrocytes [100]. Further, another study found that transient receptor potential vanilloid-1 (Trpv1) prevents ferroptosis by upregulating GPX4 expression. Activating Trpv1 reestablishes Tert-Butyl Hydroperoxide (TBHP)-triggered GPX4 decline in primary chondrocytes, whereas Trpv1 instigation was significantly restored [115]. It is known that exposing chondrocytes to extreme mechanical loading accelerates OA development, and the Piezo-type mechanosensitive ion-channel component-1 (Piezo1-) on the cell membrane serves as a mechanotransducer. Mechanical overburdening triggers GPX4-mediated ferroptosis in chondrocytes during OA via the Piezo1 network through calcium influx. It was also found that treatment with CoQ10 and ferroptosis-suppressor protein (FSP1) attenuates OA progression in GPX4-lacking mice [6]. These studies indicate that mechanical overloading is strongly related to OA progression via GPX4-depended on ferroptosis pathway.

On the other hand, in primary chondrocyte cultures and an in iron overload-induced murine OA model, chondrocyte ferroptosis could also influence OA independent to GPX4. Although a balanced iron level/homeostasis is crucial for maintaining joint health, excessive iron is strongly associated with OA pathogenesis because iron overload could trigger apoptosis and stimulate oxidative stress in chondrocytes, thereby degrading cartilage via mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress induction [95]. Further, treatment with ferric ammonium citrate (FAC) promotes the apoptotic marker of caspase-3 (cleaved) in chondrocytes. However, higher FAC doses facilitate chondrocyte apoptosis by inducing ROS generation and inhibiting collagen II production. FAC increases ROS/lipid ROS levels, promotes ACSL4 and P53, inhibits SLC7A11 and GPX4, and FAC, along with IL-1β, augments lipid peroxidation and ROS in chondrocytes [6,117]. MDA, being a lipid peroxidation marker, was also elevated after FAC/IL-1β treatment. IL-1β triggers catabolic activities in chondrocytes by disrupting iron balance/homeostasis, increasing iron influx, triggering chondrocyte ferroptosis, and promoting IL-1β-mediated matrix metalloproteinase activity. It is observed that IL‐1β stimulates intracellular and mitochondrial Fe2+ concentrations in ATDC5 cells, promoting nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4), which aggravates OA by triggering ferritinophagy and ferroptosis in chondrocytes. Thus, IL-1β-mediated disruption of iron metabolism is responsible for ferroptosis in chondrocytes and cartilage degradation during OA [6]. To address this, balancing iron metabolism and homeostasis is important for preventing ferroptosis in OA. For this purpose, an iron chelator could be used to treat this disorder. Deferoxamine (DFO) is a potent iron chelator that could relieve iron overload by chelating with free iron [77]. It has also been found that in rat temporomandibular joint arthritis, DFO could suppress IL-1α-induced free radical formation by inhibiting collagenase-mediated type-II collagen cleavage, COL10A1 expression, and cytokine production, and by upregulating Krebs cycle genes and adenosine monophosphate-activated protein-kinase in osteoarthritic cartilage. DFO could also recover the traumatic spinal-cord injury by slowing down disc degeneration through suppressing ferroptosis and alleviating iron overload-mediated extracellular matrix degradation in FVIII-deficient hemophilic mice [6]. Additionally, it has been demonstrated that OA can be alleviated by DFO through suppression of chondrocyte ferroptosis. Subchondral bone modifications, i.e., histopathological changes, late-stage bone sclerosis, and early-stage bone loss, facilitate OA pathogenesis and are associated with iron accumulation/ferroptosis. Excessive iron-mediated bone loss is characterized by increasing ferroptosis/lipid peroxidation in osteoblasts [115]. However, antioxidant N acetylcysteine and iron chelator DFO could prevent iron overload-mediated bone loss. It has also been found that OA influences peri-articular tissues and the synovium, stimulating synovial inflammation, a main pathological change promoted by excessive iron accumulation, which is further interrelated with synovial hyperplasia in hemophilic arthropathy. EGR1, IL-1β, IL-6, and ATF3 are ferroptosis-related genes identified in synovial tissues and associated with synovial hyperplasia [6]. These reports suggest that ferroptosis plays a significant role in the structural integrity and homeostasis of subchondral bone in OA via targeting lipid peroxidation, iron metabolism, and osteoblast ferroptosis. However, further investigation in clinical settings is still warranted. Lipid peroxidation regulation in OA is shown in (Fig. 3) and Table 2.

Therapeutic targets in OA

Lipid peroxidation as a nanomaterial therapeutic target: from iron-driven membrane injury to intervention

The mechanistic cascade established in preceding sections from mechanical stimuli, iron dysregulation, lipid peroxidation, ferroptosis and OA progression identifies multiple nodes amenable to therapeutic targeting. Nanomaterials closely interact with lipid peroxidation via interacting with the oxidative stress signaling within joints during OA [13]. Chronic inflammation in the joints is linked to excessive ROS production during RA or OA, which triggers lipid peroxidation and the formation of 4HNE and MDA, toxic by-products [6], 4HNE and MDA, which activate redox-sensitive transcriptional factors and disrupt cellular structures, thereby further increasing inflammation [120]. Nanomaterials, which are nano-sized particles bearing adaptable surfaces, are important to inhibit lipid peroxidation via reestablishing redox balance in OA tissues [14]. Cerium oxide nanoparticles (CeO2-NPs) are among the most broadly reported nanomaterials that could inhibit lipid peroxidation in in-vitro and in-vivo OA models [121]. Cerium in CeO2-NPs can also exist in the Ce3+ and Ce4+ oxidation states, allowing it to switch between these states. This dynamic redox switch facilitates the scavenging of hydrogen peroxide and ROS [122]. CeO2-NPs significantly decrease lipid peroxidation in subchondral bone, cartilage, and synovial fluid, preventing tissue injury and OA [121,123]. Further, CeO2-NPs significantly inhibited oxidative and mitochondrial stresses, decreased superoxide levels, and prevented RA by suppressing TNF-α and IL-1β [121]. Selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs) also have a strong potential to modulate lipid peroxidation in OA joints [124]. SeNPs promote the activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase, and glutathione peroxidase in the tissues of arthritis, detoxifying byproducts of lipid peroxidation [124]. Further, In human OA synovial fluid, a rodent anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT) OA model, and in-vitro chondrocyte cultures, TNF-α and IL-1β are the major ROS inducers and trigger oxidation of lipid membranes [125], and SeNPs inhibit these pro-inflammatory cytokines [126], protecting against oxidative destruction and degeneration in the bone cartilages [127]. Manganese-dioxide nanoparticles (MnO2-NPs) also possess robust ROS scavenging ability and decrease lipid peroxidation in bovine chondrocyte cultures, cartilage explants, and a rat intra-articular injection model, though direct validation in human OA joints remains pending [128,129]. MnO2-NPs detoxify hydrogen peroxide and superoxide by mimicking catalase and SOD activities. In the arthritic joint's microenvironment, MnO2-NPs reduce lipid peroxidation by releasing manganese ions [129]. Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) also have anti-oxidative and anti-inflammatory properties and could regulate lipid peroxidation in collage-induced arthritic joints in rats [130]. Conjugating AuNPs with quercetin, resveratrol, and curcumin could reveal radical-scavenging competencies. AuNPs manage lipid peroxidation by decreasing lipoxygenase activity and suppress lipid peroxidation via neutralizing ROS [131]. It has been discussed in a recent review that curcumin-loaded-AuNPs protect cartilage structure by inhibiting inflammation in joints and protecting against bone loss [132]. After functionalizing iron oxide nanoparticles (Fe3O4-NPs) with polyethylene glycol or dextran, Fe3O4-NPs encapsulated with Kartogenin and disguised with bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell membrane described an excellent biocompatibility and enabled rapid and high-quality cartilage regeneration with a hyaline-like organized structure in a rat cartilage defect model [133]. Fe3O4-NPs reduce oxidative damage indicators, such as electrolyte leakage and Thiobarbituric acid reactive substances, thereby protecting against membrane damage and lipid peroxidation [134]. Carbon-based nanomaterials, i.e., carbon nanotubes, graphene oxide, and fullerenes, also exhibit antioxidant potentials [135]. An in-vitro evidence reveals that fullerenes are radical scavengers that could stabilize ROS molecules, reducing lipid peroxidation via trapping radicals and inhibiting the spread of lipid-oxidation chains, though validation in human OA joints remains pending [136]. Likewise in rat adjuvant-induced and collagen-induced arthritis and in-vitro models, carbon nanotubes and graphene oxide may reduce ROS production and mitigate peroxidative damage in chondrocytes and synovial fibroblasts [88,137]. These studies indicate that such nanomaterials interact closely with lipid peroxidation and reduce oxidative stress in the joints during OA, and could serve as potential carriers to modulate lipid peroxidation. Some examples of nanomaterials and their interactions with lipid peroxidation via oxidative stress are given in Table 3. MDA and 4-HNE build up in joint fluid when cartilage first comes under oxidative stress. Simple ELISA-style tests for these aldehydes could let clinicians catch knee or hip osteoarthritis before the cartilage is permanently lost [6]. Together, these nanomaterial-based strategies characterize a mechanistically grounded approach to interrupting the lipid peroxidation cascade that drives OA cartilage destruction. By targeting the upstream oxidative events, particularly membrane peroxidation, 4-HNE and MDA accumulation and ROS generation, Fe3O4, AuNPs, MnO2, SeNPs and CeO2 offer synergistic and complementary redox-correcting competences in the inflamed joins. Pairing the read-out with routine arthroscopy or outpatient blood draws keeps the workflow practical for busy orthopaedic clinics. Early identification opens the door to antioxidant treatment while damage is still reversible.

Biomaterials-based modulation of ferroptosis and oxidative stress in osteoarthritis

Complementing nanomaterial-based strategies, biomaterials offer broader platforms for modulating ferroptosis and oxidative stress at multiple points in the pathological cascade. Biomaterials are used to implement diagnostic or therapeutic strategies in humans, both independently and in combination with other components [141]. After being embedded in the body, biomaterials can elicit inflammatory responses through foreign body reactions (FBRs) [142]. To regulate immune responses and FBRs, biomaterials promote anti-inflammatory cytokine (IL-10) production, suppress oxidative stress and its associated factors, i.e., nitrites and ROS, and regulate IL-6, IL-1, and TNF-α in a clinical cohort of 770 OA patients undergoing knee or hip joint replacement [143]. In rat mandibular, cranial bone defect and periodontitis models and in-vitro stem cell cultures, different types of natural/synthetic biomaterials have been applied to treat OA and support bone tissue regeneration, including polyesters, gelatin, collagen, cellulose, alginate, hyaluronic acid, and chitosan in hydrogels or microparticles [144,145]. These biomaterials are primarily used to maintain the appropriate release of the responsive drug, to enable active or passive drug delivery to the target site, and to load and protect the drug [146,147]. For example, CeO2 (CeO2@PP) is a synthesized biomaterial in the form of nanorods that helps decrease inflammation by polarizing M1 macrophages into M2 macrophages, thereby lowering ROS [148]. Se-MBG is another bioactive material that is prepared by mixing selenium with mesoporous bioactive glass. Se-MBG regulates polarization of macrophages and scavenges extra ROS, which decreases inflammation and promotes bone regeneration by increasing GPX4 expression on macrophages [76]. In a murine and in-vitro OA models, hollow mesoporous cerium oxide nanospheres catalytically eliminated ROS through the Ce3+/Ce4+ redox cycle, decreased macrophage-mediated inflammation and prevented cartilage from oxidative degradation, warranting further investigation in clinical settings [149]. Biomaterials based on polyphenol, i.e., resveratrol, hinder NO and ROS, exert anti-oxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [150]. In a rat OA model, intra-articular administration of poly D, L-lactide-coglycolide acid-encapsulated resveratrol nanoparticles maintained sustained drug release, decreased oxidative stress and inflammation and prevented OA progression [151]. Resveratrol nanoparticles exerted anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing TNFα, IL-6, and NO [151]. In an in-vitro and a murine OA models, curcumin-loaded nanoparticles promoted macrophage polarization from M1 to M2, decreased oxidative stress and reduced TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β and attenuated OA progression [152]. Selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs) are transported through OHA/HAADH@SeNPs hydrogel by crosslinking hyaluronic acid-adipic-acid dihydrazide with oxidized hyaluronic acid in in-vitro chondrocyte cultures and DMM-induced rat OA models [153]. These nanoparticles target GPX1, restore redox equilibrium, and decrease TNFα and IL1β by eliminating ROS in OA [153]. SeNPs promote bone regeneration, indicating their therapeutic efficacy. It has been shown in an in-vitro evidence that the rough surfaces of nanoparticles are conducive to the attachment of osteoblasts, increasing their proliferation [154]. Further, in-vitro and in-vivo evidence reveals that selenium nanoparticles stimulate GPX-dependent antioxidant defenses, decrease chondrocyte apoptosis, and protect subchondral bone from oxidative damage in OA [155]. SeNPs also promote the differentiation of stem cells into osteoblasts through oxidative stress regulation [156]. Similarly, SELENOW regulates physiological homeostasis in bone remodeling by suppressing the overactivity of osteoclasts [157]. Another ROS-scavenging nanoparticle, PEG-MnO2, protects cartilages from inflammation and decreases TNFα expression in classically stimulated macrophages [158]. LDH@TAGel bioactive material protects against apoptosis or inflammation-mediated oxidative stress in chondrocytes [159]. Fe/ZIF-8/Gel nanozymes maintain SOD activity at an intrinsic level, block MDA, inhibit IL-6 and MMP13, and suppress inflammatory responses [160]. Cu-EGCG nanosheets support macrophage transition from polarizing M1 into M2 and decrease IL-6, IL-1β, iNOS, and TNFα by regulating Nrf2 activation and NF-κB inactivation in OA, contributing to promoting anti-inflammatory effects [161]. In order to enhance bone regeneration, superoxide dismutases (SOD), i.e., CuZn-SOD in cytoplasm, Mn-SOD in mitochondria, and EC-SOD in the extracellular milieu, help neutralize ROS by converting it into less deleterious compounds, exerting antioxidant abilities to regulate oxidative stress [162,163]. Biomaterials could also be utilized as the mesenchymal stem cell's carriers, regulating stem cells' behaviors, improving cell retention, and enhancing cell survival, stimulating stem cells' therapeutic efficacy [164]. Additionally, Janus biopatch has been reported as a potential biomaterial system to help regenerate bone, having several advantages, i.e., safe in vivo and excellent capability to regenerate bone tissue [144,145] (Fig. 4). These studies indicate that OA could be treated by applying biomaterials, which help reduce oxidative stress by eliminating ROS [165] (Table 4). Cerium-oxide, manganese-oxide and selenium nanozyme particles mimic natural antioxidant enzymes and can be packed into injectable hydrogels. A single arthroscopic injection lets these particles sit exactly where peroxide levels are highest, quenching radicals while sparing the rest of the body. Early animal and ex-vivo data support their safety and cartilage-protective effects, clearing the path for first-in-human studies [169]. Targeting iron homeostasis using smart drug delivery platforms

Having addressed nanomaterial and biomaterial strategies for lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis, this section focuses on smart drug delivery systems (SDDS), particularly targeting iron homeostasis—the biochemical pivot point of the entire pathological cascade. Iron homeostasis is a gentle steadiness in iron utilization, storage, transport, and absorption, and is vital in immune function, energy metabolism, and oxygen transport [170]. Nevertheless, disturbances in iron homeostasis increasingly contribute to OA and RA pathogenesis, characterized by joint pain, joint destruction, and persistent joint inflammation [171]. SDDS are a promising approach for precise targeting of iron homeostasis, thereby enhancing therapeutic outcomes, reducing systemic toxicity, and increasing specificity [172]. SDDS facilitates drug delivery to inflamed joints and reduces arthritis progression by regulating iron-associated mechanisms, thereby revolutionizing the treatment of arthritis [172]. In the context of arthritis, SDDS exploit the unique microenvironment of the arthritic joint, which exhibits increased expression of specific receptors, immune cell infiltration, and enhanced vascular permeability [173]. Leaky vasculature enables the accumulation of nanoparticles in arthritic/inflamed joints and is mediated by increased permeability and retention effects, termed passive targeting [174]. Whereas in active targeting, ligand-conjugated nanoparticles bind to specific overexpressed receptors in arthritic joints, e.g., integrins, CD44, and folate receptors [174]. Drugs that modulate hepcidin expression and inhibit iron accumulation can reduce synovial inflammation; however, systemic administration often results in insufficient joint penetration or joint toxicity [45,175]. SDDS encapsulates drugs in nanocarriers, such as human serum albumin (HSA)-based systems, polymeric nanoparticles, or liposomes, thereby enhancing controlled release and bioavailability of the administered drugs [172,176]. Recent evidence suggested that SDDS has great potential in treating arthritis [177]. For example, the delivery of methotrexate (MTX) (an antirheumatic drug) into inflamed joints is enhanced by incorporating it into poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) nanoparticles, thereby reducing side effects by sustaining drug release, improving retention, and reducing MTX toxicity, warranting further investigation in clinical settings [178,179]. Likewise in animal RA model, phenethyl isothiocyanate is incorporated into a dual-drug nanoparticle-loaded hydrogel by formulating it as a nano emulsion and combining it with MTX [180]. This combination exerted synergistic effects by significantly increasing the inflammatory marker myeloperoxidase, increasing protective enzyme levels, and decreasing oxidative enzyme levels in the adjuvant-induced rat arthritic model [180]. Magnetic iron-oxide nanoparticles (MIONs) are developed to target iron homeostasis during RA [133]. Glucose and dexamethasone-coated MIONs effectively deliver drugs to the inflamed part in collagen-induced arthritis in mice, supported with an external magnetic field to increase the accumulation of nanoparticles [133,181]. This method achieved better therapeutic outcomes, underscoring the efficacy of targeted drug delivery. Moreover, MIONs could also trigger the release of drugs using magnetic or light stimuli, exploiting magneto-calorific or photo-thermal impacts in inducing a burst secretion of the drug on the inflamed site [182,183]. Biomimetic nano systems also support SDDS's precision through mimicking the natural structures of cells [184,185]. As an example, nanoparticles encapsulated with neutrophil membrane maintain host-cell's antigenic properties, permitting targeted-drug delivery into the inflamed joints, inhibiting the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, and protecting cartilage damage during collagen arthritis models [186,187]. Likewise, the liposomal system offers both active and passive targeting capabilities and improves the drug's stability and reduces side effects [188]. In in-vitro chondrocyte cultures, human OA cartilage and a murine OA model, uniting such drug delivery systems with iron-modulating mediators, e.g., deferoxamine, facilitates chelation of excess iron, thereby enhancing efficacy in arthritic joints [189]. In guinea pig and murine arthritis models, integrating SDDS with other developing therapies, i.e., peptide-siRNA conjugates, could offer additional potential for targeting iron homeostasis in arthritic joints [172,190]; however, limited details are available regarding the direct association of SDDS with iron homeostasis. Such conjugates are designed to silence pain- and inflammation-driving genes, thereby tailoring regulation of the iron-associated pathway, i.e., involved in ferroptosis and hepcidin [92,190]. Peptide-siRNA conjugates could target fibroblasts in synovium during OA, and could be applied to modulate iron metabolism during RA as well [191]. These studies demonstrate that SDDS has significant potential for the treatment of arthritis. Precise and targeted drug delivery could address dysregulation of iron homeostasis, and SDDS could enhance patient outcomes by minimizing side effects, reducing oxidative stress, and decreasing joint inflammation. In summary, SDDS signifies a precision-oriented therapeutic layer to target iron homeostasis in OA, the upstream biochemical driver of the ferroptosis cascade. Exploiting active targeting through integrins and passive targeting through leaky inflamed joint vasculature, CD44, and folate receptors, SDDS platforms, including peptide-siRNA conjugates, neutrophil membrane-encapsulated systems, liposomes, MIONs, HSA-based nanocarriers and PLGA nanoparticles allow localized iron chelation.

Challenges, limitations and future perspective

Despite the mechanistic clarity offered by the causal framework developed in this review, several challenges must be addressed to translate ferroptosis-targeted strategies into clinical OA therapy. Although early studies linked ferroptosis to chondrocyte death in osteoarthritis (OA), the exact molecular mechanisms remain unclear. OA is a complex disease that involves ageing, metabolic dysfunction, inflammatory cytokine signalling, and mechanical stress, and these overlap with ferroptosis-associated pathways. Most mechanistic evidence derives from in-vitro chondrocyte cultures or murine OA models that may not fully recapitulate the complexity of human OA pathophysiology. Nevertheless, the exact triggers of ferroptosis in OA joint tissues and cartilages have not been fully elucidated. Without a comprehensive understanding of these parameters, developing effective, targeted therapeutic interventions is difficult. Understanding ferroptosis could provide protective effects in OA by reducing oxidative damage and preventing chondrocyte death. However, immune cells, osteoblasts, osteoclasts, synoviocytes, and chondrocytes all respond to ferroptosis differently. For example, hindering ferroptosis could protect chondrocytes; it might inadvertently promote the survival of senescent or inflammatory cells in the synovium. Likewise, systemic administration of iron chelators or ferroptosis inhibitors could disrupt the functions of other organs, such as the brain and liver. Thus, attaining cell-specific effects in OA joints without disturbing other cells/tissues remains an important challenge. OA pathogenesis involves the death of multiple cell types, mediated by autophagy, necroptosis, and apoptosis. The mechanisms of these cell deaths are interconnected through overlapping signalling. Ferroptosis is triggered alongside apoptosis under oxidative stress, complicating the outcomes of ferroptosis-related therapies and raising concerns about compensatory cell death mechanisms that may undermine therapeutic benefits.

Ferroptosis-related biomarkers: limitations and resolution strategies

The clinical translation of material-based ferroptosis therapies depends critically on validated biomarkers for therapeutic response assessment, disease monitoring and patient stratification. Clinically validated composite biomarker panel for monitoring ferroptosis activity in OA is limited, as existing panels remain bioinformatically derived and await prospective clinical validation. Below, each proposed biomarker is paired with its specific limitation and a corresponding resolution strategy to render the translational outlook actionable.

(1)Serum Ferritin

Serum Ferritin

Limitation: Although transferrin saturation and serum ferritin are proposed markers, they did not contain specificity and could, therefore, be affected by metabolic disorders or inflammation. Ferritin is substantially confounded by its role as an acute-phase reactant, concentrations rise during liver disease, metabolic syndrome and systemic inflammation independently of true iron excess. In OA patients, who frequently exhibit low-grade systemic inflammation, ferritin may overestimate joint iron burden and misclassify patients.

Resolution strategy: Ferritin should not be interpreted in isolation. A composite iron panel combining CRP, transferrin saturation and serum ferritin enables better discrimination between inflammation-driven ferritin elevation and iron overload. sTfR serves as an adjunct that is less sensitive to inflammation and could distinguish true iron deficiency from anaemia of chronic inflammation. Standardizing this composite panel across OA clinical trials would help improve diagnostic specificity and allow real-time monitoring of iron dysregulation in OA patients.

(2)MDA and 4-HNE in synovial fluid

MDA and 4-HNE in synovial fluid

Limitation: MDA and 4-HNE accumulate in joint fluid at the earliest stages of cartilage oxidative stress, hypothetically proposing an early diagnostic window. Nevertheless, neither is routinely measured in clinical orthopaedic practice and reference ranges for OA-specific diagnostic thresholds have not been recognized. Both markers could also be affected by systemic oxidative conditions unconnected to joint disease.

Resolution strategy: Development of ELISA-based point-of-care microfluidic assays capable of quantifying MDA and 4-HNE adducts in small-volume synovial fluid samples, obtainable during routine arthrocentesis or arthroscopy would integrate these biomarkers into existing clinical workflows without additional patient burden. Multicenter studies establishing OA-stage-specific reference ranges for synovial fluid MDA and 4-HNE are identified as an immediate research priority.

(3)GPX4 Activity

GPX4 Activity

Limitation: GPX4 is the master brake on ferroptosis and its reduction is a defining molecular event in ferroptotic OA chondrocytes. Nevertheless, GPX4 activity measurement needs synovial fluid cell analysis or tissue biopsy, rendering it invasive and poorly amenable to serial monitoring and technically challenging for routine clinical laboratories.

Resolution strategy: Indirect surrogate biomarkers of GPX4 functional status should be developed and validated. Circulating microRNAs characterize promising non-invasive proxies: miR-19b-3p, delivered from inflamed FLS to chondrocytes and detectable in plasma, reflects downstream GPX4 vulnerability and SLC7A11 suppression. Urinary 8-iso-prostaglandin F2α (8-isoPGF2α), a validated systemic marker of lipid peroxidation, may serve as an accessible surrogate for the systemic lipid peroxidation burden forecasting joint ferroptotic risk, though joint-specificity requires further validation through paired synovial fluid studies.

(4)Labile Iron Pool

Labile Iron Pool

Limitation: The labile iron pool comprises weakly chelated and catalytically active Fe2+, is the mechanistically relevant iron fraction driving Fenton chemistry and ferroptosis. Its direct measurement necessitates fluorescent iron-sensing probes that are limited to in-vitro or ex-vivo contexts and could not be applied non-invasively in clinical practices.

Resolution strategy: MRI R2∗ (R2-star) mapping signifies the most promising non-invasive surrogate for tissue iron deposition. R2∗ is sensitive to paramagnetic iron and has been validated for hepatic and cardiac iron quantification in clinical practice. Standardization of joint MRI R2∗ protocols across OA clinical trial sites, including field strength calibration and region-of-interest definition for cartilage versus subchondral bone is an immediate priority to enable its usage as a monitoring biomarker in ferroptosis-targeted OA trials.

Translational challenges for SDDS and nanomaterial-based therapies

SDDS also faces numerous challenges in targeting iron homeostasis. Particularly, drugs used for systemic modulation by targeting iron could also cause disruption in healthy tissues, contributing to toxicity. Dose and timing optimization is another challenge, complicated by the heterogeneity of arthritis across patients. Furthermore, concerns about large-scale efficacy and safety data also hinder clinical translation, and the higher costs of some nanocarriers, e.g., liposomes and HAS, could limit accessibility. Conducting wide-ranging clinical trials to validate safety, applying imaging-guided drug delivery to improve precision, and increasing the use of patients′ specific biomarkers for tailored SDDS designs could be some solutions. Engineering nanoparticles with cartilage-penetrating ligands such as hyaluronic acid could improve deep cartilage targeting. Surface modification of nanoparticles could reduce immunogenicity and systemic toxicity. Application of biodegradable nanocarriers enables sustained intra-articular drug release, and producing biocompatible carriers could reduce overall costs. Although applying nanoparticles to treat OA is a good strategy, heterogeneous cartilage in different OA patients may limit penetration into the joints and impair drug delivery. Some off-target nanoparticles could also lead to systemic toxicity, triggering immune reactions and joint inflammation. As OA progression varies across patients, it interferes with standard dosing. Nanoparticle targeting could be increased by engineering them to bind hyaluronic acid, a cartilage-penetrating ligand. The surfaces of the nanoparticles could also be modified, thereby decreasing toxicity and immunogenicity and ensuring safe systemic use. An imaging-directed nanoparticle formulation could address variability in different OA patients. Additionally, applying biodegradable nanoparticles could show promising effects on sustaining drug release in inflamed joints, improving efficacy and reducing side effects.

Absence of clinical trial evidence

The most critical point is that many ferroptosis studies were conducted in animal models or were restricted to in vitro trials. Although lipid peroxidation inhibitors and iron chelators could exhibit chondroprotective properties in preclinical trials, the clinical trials evaluating their efficacy and safety in OA patients are still missing. So, robust clinical evidence remains required to implement ferroptosis-targeted therapies for the treatment of OA patients. Prioritizing such trials, guided by the composite ferroptosis biomarker panel proposed above and informed by the mechanistic framework presented in this review, represents the key translational step needed to advance ferroptosis-targeted therapies toward clinical adoption.

Future perspectives

Looking forward, several high-priority research directions are identified. The osteoclast-to-chondrocyte reverse signalling pathway following bone resorption remains mechanistically undercharacterized in the context of OA ferroptosis; dedicated in-vivo studies using osteoclast-specific conditional knockout models would clarify whether suppressing bone resorption indirectly reduces chondrocyte ferroptotic susceptibility. Consistent biomarkers are vital for evaluating disease development and therapeutic response across all clinical settings. Artificial intelligence-assisted integration of multi-omics data, including lipidomics, metallomics and transcriptomics from OA patient cohorts holds promise for identifying ferroptosis-associated molecular subtypes that predict disease trajectory and therapeutic response, ultimately enabling precision medicine approaches to ferroptosis-targeted OA therapy. Realizing the full translational potential of this field will require the validated composite biomarker strategies proposed above, robust clinical trial evidence, and scalable nanomaterial formulations designed for the unique physicochemical demands of the inflamed joints.

Conclusions

Osteoarthritis (OA), a principal cause of disability, is the most common and debilitating disease and has close interaction with ferroptosis, lipid peroxidation, and iron metabolism. Ferroptosis is a programmed cell death process and a critical process in various pathological and physiological disorders. Distinct from autophagy, necrosis, and apoptosis, ferroptosis is primarily driven by lipid peroxidation and iron accumulation, resulting in an unbalanced cellular redox state. This exclusive process of ferroptosis distinguishes it from autophagy, necrosis, and apoptosis and highlights its interactions with these forms of cell death. Notwithstanding these insights, substantial challenges remain in understanding the full scope of ferroptosis, particularly its clinical applicability, biomarkers, and molecular mechanisms. Ferroptosis is crucial in maintaining bone regeneration and homeostasis through iron homeostasis. Oxidative stress and excessive iron are closely related to abnormal bone metabolism and cartilage degradation, making ferroptosis-targeting an encouraging approach for treating age-associated joint diseases. Nevertheless, the lack of specific biomarkers for early-phase ferroptosis detection remains a challenge. Even morphological changes associated with mitochondrial ferroptosis are difficult to recognize due to technical limitations. P53, Nrf2, GPX4, and SLC7A11 are central regulators of ferroptosis implicated in cell death pathways, complicating efforts to elucidate specific ferroptosis mechanisms. For example, erastin, an inducer of ferroptosis, could also activate caspase-9-mediated mitochondrial apoptosis, thereby activating autophagy in ferroptotic cells. Thus, it is vital to investigate the full, complex mechanism of ferroptosis rather than focusing solely on individual regulators. Clarifying the multifaceted collaborative networks and identifying essential molecular pathways would provide deeper insights into ferroptosis mechanisms, thereby facilitating the development of therapeutic and diagnostic approaches for age-associated joint diseases.

Lipid peroxidation is a hallmark of ferroptosis and is dynamic during osteoarthritis progression. MDA and 4HNE are lipid peroxidation byproducts that serve as chief biomarkers and play key roles in modulating osteoclast activity, inflammation, chondrocyte apoptosis, and cartilage catabolism. 4HNE-modified proteins in the chondrocytes of OA represent a promising avenue for exploring chondrocyte adaptation to oxidative stress. GSH, GSTs, ALDH2, and GSTA4-4 are the parameters of the 4HNE detoxification pathway, offering unique therapeutic avenues. Although MDA is often used as an OA marker, additional studies are essential to determine its concentration in joint fluid and correlate it with serum levels. The involvement of ferroptosis in OA synovitis, subchondral bone remodeling, and cartilage degradation underscores its role in OA progression. Preventing ferroptosis and lipid peroxidation in the chondrocytes could offer a new treatment approach. Furthermore, as ferroptosis promotes post-injury inflammation, it may contribute to tissue remodeling and ROS accumulation, thereby affecting repair processes in joints, skin trauma, and other organs. Regulatory molecules such as FSP1, ACSL4, and GPX4 are essential in the above process. Targeting abnormal effector cells that trigger ferroptosis helps manage inflammatory responses and improve clinical outcomes. Additionally, smart drug delivery systems (SDDS) and nanoparticles could offer an encouraging approach to treating OA through targeted drug delivery. Applying nanoparticles could efficiently address OA by delivering drugs into the inflamed joints in a controlled, precise manner. Lastly, although noteworthy advances have been made in understanding the role of ferroptosis in tissue repair during OA, inconsistencies remain in safety concerns and therapeutic responses. Recognizing non-invasive biomarkers and finding optimal interventions are crucial for OA patient recovery. Multifaceted advances that modulate multiple ferroptosis pathways may enhance therapeutic efficacy; however, additional safety assessments are essential. As our OA understanding progresses from a “wear and tear” model to the complex pathology of ferroptosis, metabolic dysregulation, inflammation, and signalling pathways, it offers an encouraging frontier for therapeutic improvement in OA patients.
