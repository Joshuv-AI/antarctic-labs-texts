A rare case of inflammatory myofibroblastic tumour of the thigh : A case report and literature review
Authors: Yassine Batou, Taha El Aissaoui, Amine El Farhaoui, Brahim Zeryouh, Adnane Lachkar, Fouad Zouaidia, Najib Abdeljaouad, Hicham Yacoubi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTowards the beginning of the twentieth century, inflammatory myofibroblastic tumors were described as neoplasms characterized by myofibroblastic spindle cells with an infiltrate of inflammatory cells. These rare tumors occur mainly in children and young adults with a preferential pulmonary and abdominal location. They have intermediate biological potency with a tendency to local recurrence and rarely metastasize. We describe the case of an adolescent who presented with an inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor of the thigh, a very rare entity that has only been reported in the literature as case reports, and its clinical, radiological, echographic and MRI appearance. The patient underwent complete surgical resection of the mass with healthy margins and did not receive any additional treatment. No sign of recurrence was detected after 6 months of follow-up.Keywords: IMT, Thigh, MRI, Enhancement, Biopsy, Spindle cells, Resection, Margins, Recurrence

Introduction

Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumors (IMTs) are intermediate biological potential neoplasms with a tendency to local recurrence and rare cases of metastasis. According to the World Health Organisation, they represent an entity of lesions composed of myofibroblastic spindle cells alongside an inflammatory infiltrate of plasmocytes, lymphocytes and eosinophils [1]. These tumors occur most often in the first 2 decades of life, mainly affecting the lung and digestive tract, although some rare localizations in the extremities have been described as case reports [2].

The lack of specificity of the clinical and biological findings explains why this diagnosis is rarely mentioned [3], although MRI, anatomopathological and immunhistochemical studies provide a great contribution to the diagnosis [4].

Surgical resection with healthy margins is the rule to reduce the higher risk of local recurrence without the need for neoadjuvant radiotherapy or chemotherapy [5].

Our case

A 16-year-old adolescent with no previous medical history presented with a painless mass on the medial aspect of the left thigh that had been progressively increasing in size over the past 2 months, with no history of trauma, fever or deterioration of general condition. Clinically, the mass measured approximately 4 × 3 cm, with firm consistency, mobile in relation to the deep and superficial tissues, painless, without inflammatory cutaneous signs, and the lymph nodes were free (Fig. 1).

Clinical examination showing a mass in the adductor region of the left thigh.

The standard X-ray examination revealed no bone or soft tissue abnormalities. Additional ultrasound revealed an irregular hypoechoic soft tissue mass on the medial aspect of the upper third of the thigh, with spiculated contours, measuring 24 × 16 × 20 cm, showing mixed vascularization on Doppler with infiltration of the surrounding fat and respect of contiguous muscles (Fig. 2). The magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed the presence of a soft tissue mass anterior to the gracilis and medial to the adductor longus. This formation showed a hyposignal on T1 weighted images, hypersignal on T2 and Fat Saturation (FAT SAT) sequences (Fig. 3), heterogeneous hypersignal on Short Tau Inversion Recovery (STIR) sequence (Fig. 4) with intense contrast enhancement and diffuse infiltration of the adjacent soft tissues (Fig. 5). Surgical biopsy was in favor of an inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor due to the presence of a proliferation of spindle cells with spindle and round nucleus associated with inflammatory elements. The mass was resected as a monobloc carcinological procedure with cutaneous-musculo-fatty safety margins (Fig. 6).

Macroscopic examination of the resection specimen revealed a grey-white mass with calcific deposits. Microscopically, it had the same biopsy findings, with proliferation of spindle-shaped cells with spindle-shaped nuclei alongside a proliferation of inflammatory cells (Fig. 7), with healthy surgical resection margins.

Microscopic examination reveals proliferation of spindle-shaped cells with spindle-shaped nuclei, along with a proliferation of inflammatory cells (A : ×100, B : ×150).

A thoracic-abdominal-pelvic scan was performed and did not reveal any secondary locations, in particular the lung. Our patient did not undergo adjuvant therapy, and no signs of clinico-radiological recurrence were reported after 6 months.

Discussion

IMTs were recently described around the beginning of the twentieth century as a distinct entity with characteristic anatomopathological and molecular features [6], these neoplasms have always been recognized with a broad spectrum of lesions under the name of inflammatory pseudotumor [7]. They are characterized by myofibroblastic spindle cells with the presence of an inflammatory infiltrate [1]. These tumors are rarely found in the limbs and more rarely in the soft tissues of the extremities, their preferential sites being the lungs and in the abdominal cavity [8]. The pediatric population and young adults are the most commonly concerned, with an average age of 10 years [9], with no sexual predominance [3].

IMTs are classified as benign tumors with a remarkable potential for local recurrence (up to 25% in extra-pulmonary locations according to Coffin [10], infiltration of adjacent tissues and a minor risk of sarcomatous transformation, although they rarely metastasize to distant sites [11].

Clinical features differ according to location; in the extremities there are no specific signs other than a mass which is sometimes painful without fever or weight loss [12].

Ultrasonographic findings are not specific; the lesion may be hypoechoic or hyperechoic, well or poorly defined with hyper vascularization on Doppler [13]. The magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings of soft tissue IMT are not specific. It has been reported that these lesions often occur in the medial region of the thigh as a progressively growing mass, hypointense in T1 sequence and variable intensity in T2 sequence [3]. In our case, the mass was located in the upper third of the thigh, medial to the adductor fossa, which is hyposignal in T1 and hypersignal in T2. The literature reports that these lesions show variable enhancement after injection of gadolinium depending on the cellular and fibrous component of the mass [3]; high enhancement was observed in our case. It is essential to highlight that the diagnosis cannot be made on MRI studies only, as several lesions such as MFH (malignant fibrous histiocytoma), fibro sarcomas and MPNST (Malignant Peripheral Nerve Sheath Tumors) can present the same characteristics [6].

Confirmation of the diagnosis is based on a histological study of the biopsy or surgical resection specimen, which shows myofibroblastic and fibroblastic spindle cells with the presence of an inflammatory infiltrate of lymphocytes, plasma cells, eosinophils and histiocytes [3]. Immunihistochemical studies can detect the ALK (anaplastic lymphoma kinase) marker in 50% of cases of IMT, especially in children; the positivity of this factor may be associated with the risk of local recurrence, but its presence is not specific to IMT [7].

Treatment of localized forms of IMT is primarily surgical, involving resection of the entire mass with R0 margins, and does not require any adjuvant therapy [14]. Chemotherapy and radiotherapy are of interest in advanced, multifocal and recurrent forms [3,15].

Conclusion

IMT of the extremities is a rare entity with no specific clinical, biological or radiological features, and it is the histological study that generally makes the diagnosis. Monobloc resection with healthy margins is the mainstay of treatment to eradicate the tumor and prevent local recurrence.
