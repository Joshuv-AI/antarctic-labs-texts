Bats attack birds in the air
Authors: Dai Fukui, Emyo Fujioka, Megumi Watanabe, Yoshiaki Watanabe, Yushi Osawa, Keiko Osawa, Noboru Numata, Shizuko Hiryu, Seiichi Hara
Published: 2026

Bats are the second most species‐rich mammalian group after rodents and exhibit a wide range of dietary habits ranging from insects and plants to vertebrates (Patterson et al., 2003). Among these, 14 species from the families Phyllostomidae, Megadermatidae, Vespertilionidae, and Nycteridae are known to feed on terrestrial vertebrates through gleaning, with six species regularly or seasonally preying on such animals (Gual‐Suárez & Medellín, 2021). More recently, three species of aerial hawking bats from the family Vespertilionidae, which include the birdlike noctule Nyctalus aviator in Japan, have been found to prey on birds during the spring and autumn bird migration periods (Fukui et al., 2013; Ibáñez et al., 2001; Thabah et al., 2007). DNA metabarcoding analyses revealed that these bats preferentially target small migratory birds (Gong et al., 2021; Ibáñez et al., 2016, 2020).

However, how bats capture relatively large prey such as birds was, until recently, unclear. Two hypotheses had been proposed: that bats attacked resting birds in their roosts (Dondini & Vergari, 2000), or that they intercepted birds during flight (Russo et al., 2024). Recent GPS telemetry and behavioral experiments have suggested the latter (Gong et al., 2022). Additionally, Stidsholt et al. (2025) used onboard sound and movement tags to support that European Nyctalus lasiopterus tracks and preys on birds in the air while descending from altitudes of over 1000 m. This European study was the first across any species or region to provide direct evidence that bats prey on birds while flying. However, this was based on acoustic observations, and no visual observations of bats capturing birds in flight have been reported to date.

The Tsugaru bird observatory in northern Japan offers a rare opportunity to observe nocturnal bird migrations visually. Using dim ground lighting and searchlights, observers can detect and identify birds flying below an altitude of 150 m (Senzaki et al., 2022). In autumn 2022, we witnessed a large bat—likely N. aviator—attacking a small bird. We then conducted systematic visual observations in autumn 2023, quantifying bird and bat pass rates and bat behavior through visual counts, photography, and infrared and thermo video recordings. Additionally, to determine the species of bat that attacked the bird, we collected the bird dropped by the bat and analyzed DNA from the wound using DNA barcoding techniques (Appendix S1: Section S1).

During the visual observation period, we recorded 56 instances of bats pursuing birds, some of which resulted in a chase or attack, and six that resulted in successful captures. Furthermore, DNA sequences matching N. aviator were detected in wound samples from a Japanese Bush Warbler (Horornis diphone) that had been captured and subsequently dropped by bats within the visual observation area. Together, these visual observations and molecular evidence confirmed that N. aviator preys on birds during flight (Figure 1; Videos [Link], [Link]; Appendix S1: Section S2 and Figure S1). While various bird species of different sizes were migrating at night, the bat pursued both small and relatively large species, such as the Japanese Green Pigeon (Treron sieboldii). However, the actual attacks were limited to smaller birds (Figure 2A). This suggests that bats may detect birds regardless of size during search flights but assess prey size during pursuit before deciding to attack. Future analyses of flight trajectories and echolocation calls may help to shed light on this process.

During our visual observations, the success rate of bird predation appeared low (Figure 2A). Because our visual observation area was small and the visual observation period was short, we cannot say with certainty that N. aviator's predation success rate on birds is always low. The predation success rate may vary depending on the time of year and altitude. This will be an issue for future studies when considering the adaptive significance of bird predation.

Our visual observations confirmed that bats were able to hold onto a bird and continue flying after successfully capturing it, but we were unable to observe how they subsequently consumed it. The next step is to understand how bats consume the birds they prey on. In the European N. lasiopterus, which has similar size and functional traits to N. aviator, Stidsholt et al. (2025) indicated that bats bite off parts of birds in mid‐air and chew them while flying. This issue may be solved by using equipment such as stronger or multiple floodlight projectors to observe a wider area, along with high‐precision cameras and sound recorders.

The frequency of bat appearance, chasing, and attacks observed at low altitudes (<150 m) varied greatly among nights and appeared to increase on nights when larger numbers of small birds (<30 g) passed through the survey site (Figure 2B). However, these visual observations represent only a subset of bat–bird interactions occurring within the airspace. Ia io, another bird‐eating bat, is physiologically adapted to high‐altitude flight (Gong et al., 2022). Stidsholt et al. (2025) observed N. lasiopterus begin tracking a bird from an altitude of over 1000 m. Because our visual observations were limited to altitudes below 150 m, our data do not allow reliable estimation of overall prey capture success, nor do they capture bat foraging activity occurring at higher elevations. On nights when few birds were observed at low altitudes, birds may have been migrating above the observer's visual range, and bats may have adjusted their flight altitude accordingly. Future studies integrating visual observations with altitude‐resolved tracking or telemetry will be essential to determine how prey availability across altitudes influences bat foraging behavior and prey capture success.

Weather conditions may also affect bat predation. During our study, bat activity was nearly absent on 16 and 17 October when westerly winds prevailed (Figure 2C). In contrast, easterly winds dominated on other nights, and bat predation was most frequent on 18 October when wind speeds were the highest. Although quantitative statistical analysis cannot be performed due to the short visual observation period, these patterns suggest that wind conditions affect both bird migration and hunting behavior in bats. However, whether wind influences bat behavior indirectly through bird behavior or directly remains unclear. For example, GPS loggers or altimeters are necessary to clarify the relationship between bat foraging routes, height, and weather conditions during this season.

This study presents the first visual observation of bats preying on flying birds, a previously undocumented behavior across any species or region. Our visual observations augment the few reports of bat predation on birds (Fukui et al., 2013; Ibáñez et al., 2001; Thabah et al., 2007) and include visual evidence of predation on small songbirds, the mode of predation, and the possible relationship of predation to windspeed and direction. Due to the limited visual observation period and area, detailed quantitative analysis was not possible. In addition, behavioral characteristics may differ depending on the bat species. Further research will offer valuable insights into the evolution of bat predation strategies and contribute to our understanding of the ecological diversity of bats.
