Rapid cross-sectional impact assessment of an earthquake on the continuity of antenatal care for pregnant women living with hepatitis B in Vanuatu
Authors: Leila Bell, Aleesha Kalulu, Nicole Allard, Kali Ameara, Emily Deed, Zeshi Fisher, Caroline SE Homer, Jessica Howell, Kaylene Kalmos, Navin Karan, Leias Obed, Sereana Natuman, Mark Stoové, Annie Taissets, Florita Toa, Margaret Hellard, Caroline van Gemert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeNatural disasters such as earthquakes, cyclones, and volcanic eruptions directly and indirectly impact health and wellbeing. This work aimed to understand how access to, and use of, peripartum health services were impacted following a large 7.3 magnitude earthquake in Vanuatu in the context of an ongoing field trial looking at the effectiveness of universal peripartum antiviral prophylaxis for pregnant women living with hepatitis B to prevent mother-to-child transmission.MethodsParticipants were pregnant women enrolled in the intervention-arm of a field trial at sites in the earthquake-affected area. During routine monitoring calls, we asked additional questions about the impact of the earthquake on continued access to health services and continued use of daily hepatitis B antiviral prophylaxis with tenofovir disoproxil fumarate.ResultsSeventeen participants of 26 women enrolled in the trial could be contacted and answered all questions from early-February to early-March 2025. Self-reported continued use of tenofovir disoproxil fumarate was high, with only two of 17 participants reporting interruptions to use in the weeks following the earthquake. Seven participants (41%) reported missing at least one routine antenatal or postnatal appointment in the three months following the earthquake.ConclusionsIn natural disaster-prone settings, it is critical that field research take an adaptive approach that can adjust and respond to health emergencies. While natural disasters are inevitable, health systems must implement risk mitigation strategies to limit interruptions to routine health services to reduce negative short-, medium-, and long-term health impacts of natural disasters.Keywords: Vanuatu, Earthquake, Access to health services, Hepatitis B, Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate, Antenatal care, Maternal health

Purpose

On 17 December 2024 a magnitude 7.3 earthquake struck approximately 30 km west of Vanuatu's capital, Port Vila, causing substantial damage to much of the main island, Efate Island, where approximately 30% of the population lives and where the capital city, Port Vila, is located [1]. There were 14 deaths and over 200 injuries directly attributed to the earthquake [2]. Following the earthquake, the central business district of Port Vila became an exclusion zone due to the impact on buildings and infrastructure, many of which have already been demolished [3]. One of the major antenatal care (ANC) facilities, located at Vanuatu Family Health Association, was in this exclusion zone. In the months following the earthquake, there were major traffic disruptions due to the exclusion zone, causing issues to access health services, including Vanuatu National Hospital (formerly Vila Central Hospital), the national referral hospital and where many women access ANC and give birth. As of mid-April 2025, the central business district has partially reopened [4].

Vanuatu is consistently ranked among countries most at risk for natural disasters, including earthquakes (e.g., over 2000 earthquakes from January to 8 April 2025), tropical cyclones, and volcanic activity (e.g., seven active volcanoes in 2025) [5], [6], [7]. These public health emergencies directly and indirectly impact on people's health and wellbeing. There are direct impacts, such as disaster-related injury and fatalities or damages to homes and livelihood. Indirect impacts include disruptions to health systems [8] and public health services [9]. Additionally, there is evidence that risks to mental health and wellbeing may be higher for those exposed to multiple natural disasters, as is the case in Vanuatu [10]. Extreme weather events and natural disasters can also impact research through destruction of physical assets and infrastructure and interruption of health care including access to research trials and the capacity of people to participate [11]. In the case of public health research, the impacts may be significant due to destruction of health facilities and supply chains that enable participation and data collection, overwhelmed health systems, and pressures on human resourcing [11].

There is an ongoing field trial in Vanuatu, the Protektem Pikinini Blong Yu (PPBY) Trial which is investigating the use of universal antiviral peripartum prophylaxis for pregnant women living with hepatitis B to prevent mother-to-child transmission of hepatitis B [12]. At intervention sites, pregnant women living with hepatitis B are prescribed daily tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) regardless of hepatitis B viral load, aligned with the 2024 WHO recommendations for settings where hepatitis B e antigen or hepatitis B virus DNA tests are not accessible [13]. Prior to the earthquake, a total of 26 women were enrolled at PPBY intervention sites in Efate, Vanuatu. Following the earthquake, the focus for research staff prioritised the assessment of the safety of participants followed by assessing impacts on access to routine health services, and damage or loss of TDF to coordinate re-dispensing to maintain study fidelity and participant benefit. There is a risk that interruptions to TDF can cause viral flares, although there are conflicting results on the impact of interrupted TDF use on maternal health outcomes [13], [14], [15], [16].

This work aims to understand how health systems are impacted by climate related disasters, and this trial provides a unique opportunity to study the effects of this natural disaster on maternal and child health service delivery. This work aims to understand how trial infrastructure and adaptive approaches could support early return to care following a natural disaster. Additionally, learnings about the impact and subsequent trial response could be utilised to adapt health services more broadly and to study how health services and health research is impacted by disasters to inform future actions and preparedness, knowing that disruptive climate related disasters will continue to occur in Vanuatu.

Methods

The PPBY trial aims to understand the effectiveness of universal peripartum antiviral prophylaxis with TDF for prevention of mother-to-child transmission of hepatitis B by comparing an intervention group (universal peripartum antiviral prophylaxis from second trimester to 14 weeks postpartum, regardless of HBV DNA viral load) with a real-world control group undergoing routine care (no TDF) [12]. This trial aims to provide evidence for updated global guidelines which are conditional and based on low-certainty evidence [13]. There are several trial design elements that are aimed at mitigating risks of non-adherence among participants in the intervention arm, including incorporating hepatitis B care directly into antenatal care (ANC) appointments, routine monitoring calls from the research, delivery of medication to participants who miss appointments, and dispensing 35 tablets at each appointment to allow for slight delays in attending appointments. The full study protocol has been published previously [12]. This study adhered to the STROBE guidelines for cross-sectional studies [17] (Supplement 1).

Eligibility

To understand the impact of the earthquake on access to health services, the focus of this study is the participants who had enrolled in the study at intervention sites in Efate, Vanuatu and were under active monitoring and follow-up at the time of the earthquake, including participants who were pregnant and participants who have already given birth but were within 14-week postpartum at time of the earthquake.

Data collection and analysis

During routine monitoring calls from 3 February to 7 March 2025, participants were asked an additional set of questions relating to the impact of the earthquake on their living situation after the earthquake, access to healthcare services, and continued use of TDF. Questions (Supplement 2) were adapted from the United National Development Programme “Guidelines for assessing the human impact of disasters” [18] by asking questions about ANC specifically in addition to the general questions in the guidelines. Most questions were closed (with “yes and “no” as answer options), with follow-on questions being quantitative (such as the number of days required to relocate or number of days of TDF missed) and open-ended (such as reason for missing ANC appointments).

The primary outcome measure was missed ANC appointments in the immediate period following the earthquake, defined as self-report of not having attended all scheduled ANC or maternal and child health (MCH) appointments since the earthquake and its aftershocks. Secondary outcome measures captured the broader impact of the earthquake on housing stability, medication continuity, and access to health services. These included: (1) housing damage (no damage, partially damaged, or severely damaged); (2) displacement status, defined as temporary or permanent relocation due to the earthquake or its aftershocks; (3) disruption to HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis, assessed through self-reported loss or damage to tenofovir supply and the ability to continue daily tenofovir use in the days following the earthquake, including estimated number of missed days if applicable; (4) interruption to other daily or regular medications; (5) changes in site of ANC or MCH care since the earthquake; and (6) perceived access to ANC or MCH services after the disaster compared with the pre-earthquake period (better, same, worse, or unsure).

All primary and secondary outcomes were assessed using structured, interviewer-administered questionnaires during routine monitoring calls. Attempts were made to collect data as soon as possible after telecommunications were reestablished after the earthquake, however on occasion it took several weeks to be able to contact participants. Responses were recorded on a paper form, and then entered by research staff into a password-protected RedCAP database. Each entry is linked to a specific participant using a unique identifier that is already used within the larger PPBY field trial. Responses were summarised using frequencies and proportions based on the number of participants responding to each item, and further context to answers were summarised.

Ethics

These post-earthquake questions are covered under the scope of the ethical approval for the broader Protektem Pikinini Blong Yu Trial from the Vanuatu Health Research Ethics Committee and the Alfred Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC/105780-Alfred-2024).

Results

At the time of the earthquake (17 December 2024), a total of 26 pregnant women living with hepatitis B were enrolled at PPBY trial intervention sites in Efate, Vanuatu, all of whom were receiving routine weekly monitoring calls by the research team to monitor TDF adherence and any other concerns. The immediate safety of study participants was the primary concern following the 17 December 2024 earthquake. The study team was able to contact all participants in the earthquake-affected area to confirm their safety within three weeks from the earthquake. Following this, additional information was collected to understand the ongoing access and adherence to daily TDF, which is also critical for the health of participants and their infants. Among the 26 participants, six women had already given birth and were still within 14-weeks postpartum and therefore still taking TDF and enrolled in the trial. From 3 February 2025 to 7 March 2025, 18 participants were asked questions specifically about the impact of the earthquake (see Fig. 1). The remaining eight participants were not able to be contacted for monitoring calls during that period. This is due in part to challenges with mobile phone access from the earthquake, movement from Efate to other areas of Vanuatu, and also the willingness of some participants to answer telephone calls from the research team more broadly (not just around the time of the earthquake). One participant did not answer all questions and is therefore removed from reported data. However, all these participants remain enrolled in the field trial. Seven (41%) participants were unable to attend all their routine ANC or maternal child health (MCH) appointments post-earthquake. One reported being afraid of leaving the house after the earthquake, three did not attend because of clinic closures or relocation, one reported missing the first appointment post-earthquake and then stopped attending, and two were not earthquake related. Additionally, seven (41%) participants also reported changing the location where they sought ANC or MCH care following the earthquake, including two of the three participants who reported missing some routine ANC or MCH appointments due to clinic closures or relocations, while the remaining participant continued to miss appointments due to concerns about longer queueing and wait times at clinics. Some participants reported differences in access to care following the earthquake. While nine (53%) reported access was the same post-earthquake as it was pre-earthquake, three (18%) reported access was better and three (18%) reported access was worse, although only one participant reporting worse access reported changing services. Finally, only one person reported that nearby health centres were damaged and among the 14 participants that responded to the question about alternative options for healthcare facilities, two (14%) reported no nearby facilities while the other 12 reported available facilities, including six of the 12 who reported these facilities to be close to home.

Of the 17 respondents, seven (41%) participants reported their homes to be severely or partially damaged, with one person reporting they had to relocate due to damage from the earthquake. This individual reported being unable to access their TDF supply during this time and missed approximately 30 days of tablets. A second participant reported that they were unable to continue taking daily TDF because they misplaced their supply although days missed was not reported. Neither of these two participants reported taking other daily medication. Of note 11 participants (65% of total participants) reported taking other daily medication in addition to daily TDF with none reporting interruption to this use following the earthquake.

Conclusion

This study explored the impact of the 17 December 2024 earthquake in Vanuatu on access to health systems by pregnant women involved in a study providing hepatitis B care and treatment. The findings demonstrate that the earthquake impacted over 40% of participants' access to routine ANC or MCH services. However there was limited reported impact on the study intervention in the form of continued daily use of TDF with only two women reporting not continuing to take all their TDF, despite a number having their clinic relocated.

One of the main study sites, Vanuatu Family Health Association ANC Clinic, where 13 of the 17 respondents were recruited, is located within the central business district in an area that people are not able to access, although only five of these participants reported having to change where they seek ANC or MCH care after the earthquake. The clinic was closed for approximately two weeks following the earthquake and was then relocated to another site after the earthquake, but capacity to provide ANC at the new site was limited by the size of the clinic. ANC attendees were initially encouraged to seek services elsewhere. This was the same advice given to people who were receiving care at Vila Central Hospital, with the public strongly advised to access care in community health centres to allow the hospital to focus on critical earthquake-related cases. Perceptions of access to ANC and MCH services was reportedly worse for three women, although only one reported that they changed services so other factors may have influenced these perceptions. Uncertainty around where to access care, as well as interruptions directly due to the earthquake, may have contributed to why five women missed scheduled appointments.

Of note, all eleven women who reported taking other daily medication in addition to daily TDF, reported no interruptions to taking tenofovir following the earthquake, compared to two of the six women who did not take other medication. Per current protocols in Vanuatu, all pregnant women should be taking daily combination tablets of iron and folic acid, so it is interesting to note that six women (including the two who missed TDF doses) did not report taking other medication. Additional information is needed to understand if participants who did not report other daily medication had been prescribed iron and folate but forgot to report this, did not considered it to be a medication, have chosen not to take it or had been missed in the antenatal policy. This information may provide some insights into potential levels of future antenatal TDF adherence outside a study setting.

Continued access to ANC and MCH services following natural disasters is imperative to reduce the risks of adverse outcomes [19], [20]. Earthquakes have been shown to have negative impacts on maternal health, including mental health, and some evidence of early delivery and size at birth, although other settings did not see a difference in preterm birth rates [21], [22], [23]. Natural disasters can have a large impact on access to health services for socially vulnerable people who may not have access to the resources to seek alternative sources of care [24], [25].

For people receiving hepatitis B treatment, it is also important that there be continued access to and use of TDF as interruptions may increase the risk of viral flares and negate some of the benefits of antiviral prophylaxis [26]. In a setting like Vanuatu, where natural disasters are guaranteed, it is important to consider how to pre-empt and mitigate the impacts on health and health service access. The PPBY protocol has some mitigation strategies built into it to reduce the risk of loss-to-follow-up and promote adherence, including dispensing of five weeks of TDF, weekly monitoring phone calls and direct access to study staff for participants with questions or concerns and deliveries of medication to participants' homes in some situations. Despite these strategies, there were still interruptions to services. Efforts are required, including provision of information to participants about how to contact study coordinators and how to access adapted health services, to facilitate access following a natural disaster. Additionally, preparedness plans for individual health services must be developed, communicated and implemented to facilitate continued access.

External to the PPBY trial, health workers in Vanuatu are encouraged to recommend that their clients keep all essential medications in a bag that can be easily carried in the event of a disaster necessitating an evacuation (personal communication). This may be more effective during emergencies that can be anticipated, such as a tropical cyclone, compared to an acute and unpredictable event such as an earthquake. Studies from the Caribbean found different approaches for non-communicable disease care were required following natural disasters, including changes in medication dispensing practices, education programmes and surveillance systems [9]. There is evidence that the severity and frequency of extreme weather events has increased [27] further highlighting the need for planning and preparedness efforts to enhance health care resilience to limit the direct and indirect impacts of these events while also strengthening of health facilities and services to ensure clients can access essential services, including to replace lost or damaged medication for chronic conditions, like hepatitis B.

There are several limitations that should be considered. Firstly, interruption and attendance to ANC and MCH services are reported by the individual and not verified. Secondly, continued use of TDF or other daily medication is also based on self-reporting, and has not been verified with pill counting. Thirdly, nearly one-third of PPBY participants enrolled at intervention sites did not take part in the survey. It is possible that non-respondents may have important differences in continued access to services and adherence with daily TDF which may bias the results presented. Finally, the research team asked questions across a five-week period, there may have been differences across that period of time regarding client access to services, awareness of damages or accessibility to clinics, changes in service provision, or other factors.

The impact of any interruptions due to the earthquake or other factors on the prevention of mother-to-child transmission of hepatitis B within the PPBY trial will be assessed during the follow-up period of the study. This study shows that advanced planning and communication is needed for continued engagement in antenatal care following a natural disaster. It is paramount that field research conducted in natural disaster-prone settings such as Vanuatu have an adaptive approach that is able to adjust and respond to health emergencies, particularly when interruption in research can result in negative health or social impacts. The lessons learnt from these approaches can be expanded to the broader health system, to reduce the negative health impacts of natural disasters.
