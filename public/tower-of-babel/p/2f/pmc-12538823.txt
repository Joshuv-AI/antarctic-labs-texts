Clinical value of albumin-corrected anion gap combined with lactate dehydrogenase in predicting sepsis-associated liver injury in children: a single-center retrospective cohort study
Authors: Chengzhu Xu, Xiyang Wang, Danqun Jin, Yao Sheng, Jing He, Yuanyuan Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe purpose of this study was to examine the predictive value of albumin-corrected anion gap (ACAG) for the early detection of pediatric sepsis-associated liver injury (SAIL).MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on clinical data of pediatric patients with sepsis who were admitted to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) at Anhui Provincial Children's Hospital between January 2021 and February 2024. Patients were divided into two groups based on the presence of liver injury: 118 cases in the non-liver injury group and 30 cases in the liver injury group. Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify risk factors for SAIL in children. The predictive value of each independent risk factor for the early diagnosis of SAIL was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. ResultsIn a group of 148 pediatric patients diagnosed with sepsis, 30 individuals, representing 20.27%, developed SAIL. The SAIL group exhibited a significantly higher mortality rate than the non-SAIL group (P<0.05), with patients having abdominal infections as the primary infection site being more susceptible to SAIL. Significant differences were observed between the SAIL and non-SAIL groups in terms of albumin (ALB), platelet (PLT) count, ACAG, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), serum creatinine (SCr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatine kinase (CK), creatine kinase isoenzyme (CK-MB), international normalized ratio (INR), fibrinogen concentration (FDP), prothrombin time (PT), thrombin time(TT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and D-dimer levels (P<0.05). No significant differences were found between the two groups regarding age, sex, birth status, white blood cell count and alkaline phosphatase (ALP), Cholinesterase(CHE) (P>0.05).Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified ACAG (OR = 1.017, 95% CI: 1.016–1.207, P = 0.021), LDH (OR = 1.037, 95% CI: 1.009–1.066, P = 0.009), intra-abdominal infection (OR = 5.450, 95% CI: 1.190–24.958, P = 0.029), and severe lactic acidosis (OR = 2.585, 95% CI: 1.129–5.922, P = 0.025) as independent risk factors for SAIL. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis showed that the combination of ACAG and LDH yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.906 (95% CI: 0.843–0.962) for predicting SAIL. At a cutoff value of 21.88, the sensitivity was 90.00% and the specificity was 82.20%. ConclusionACAG, LDH, severe lactic acidosis, and abdominal infection were identified as independent predictors of sepsis-associated liver injury (SAIL) incidence in children, the combination of ACAG and LDH had a better predictive value than individual indicators.

Introduction

Sepsis is a primary cause of mortality among patients in the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) and represents a substantial risk to pediatric health. Organ dysfunction is the most critical clinical outcome of sepsis and is strongly associated with patient mortality. The liver, as the largest component of the reticuloendothelial system, is significantly impacted during sepsis. SAIL is identified as an independent risk factor associated with poor prognosis in pediatric sepsis [1, 2]. However, the pathogenesis of sepsis-associated liver injury (SAIL) remains unclear, and the early clinical identification of SAIL continues to be challenging. Sepsis patients frequently demonstrate metabolic acidosis due to the accumulation of acidic substances, such as lactic acid (Lac), resulting from systemic hypoxia, inadequate perfusion, and endothelial cell dysfunction [3]. Anion gap (AG) is an essential indicator of acid-base balance in arterial blood gas analysis and is directly influenced by changes in serum albumin (ALB) levels; a decrease in ALB can result in a falsely normal or decreased AG value [4]. Literature indicates that the albumin-corrected anion gap (ACAG), as a nonspecific marker, may serve to evaluate the prognosis of sepsis [5]. However, related studies are scarce, particularly in the field of pediatrics. This study retrospectively analyzes the predictive value of the ACAG indicator for the early diagnosis of SAIL in children.

Subjects and methods

Study subjects

A retrospective analysis of clinical data was performed on children with sepsis admitted to the PICU of Anhui Provincial Children’s Hospital between January 2021 and February 2024. This study has been approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Children’s Medical Center of Anhui Medical University. It does not involve any prospective interventions or randomization. During the data collection process, the authors did not have access to any information that could identify individual participants. All data were anonymized prior to analysis to ensure the privacy and confidentiality of the participants. The inclusion criteria for sepsis in this study required the following conditions: (1) Age between 29 days and 18 years; (2) Diagnosis according to the “Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Pediatric Septic Shock (Infectious Shock) (2015 Edition)“ [6] and the “Sepsis Campaign: International Guidelines for the Management of Sepsis and Septic Shock (2016 Edition)“ [7–9]. The standard for liver function injury was total bilirubin (TBIL) ≥ 4 mg/dL (68 µmol/L) or alanine aminotransferase (ALT) greater than twice the normal value for the same age [10–12]. The criteria for exclusion were as follows: (1) Primary liver diseases, genetic metabolic disorders, liver injury due to hepatitis viruses, cytomegalovirus, drugs, or toxins; (2) Individuals with methanol, propanol, and ethylene glycol intoxication; (3) PICU admission duration less than 48 h; (4) Individuals with insufficient clinical information.

Statistical methods

Data were processed and graphed using SPSS 27.0 and GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Normality tests were conducted on all variables. Continuous data that follow a normal distribution are represented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD), with comparisons between two groups conducted using the independent sample t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous data are expressed as median (interquartile range) [M(Q1, Q3)], and inter-group comparisons were made using non-parametric tests. Count data are presented as n (%), and comparisons between two groups were conducted using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Logistic regression analysis was used to ascertain factors that may affect liver injury. All variables in the multivariate regression analysis were tested for collinearity, and the variance inflation factor (VIF) was less than 5, signifying the absence of multicollinearity. The relevant indications in predicting SAIL were selected, and the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was plotted. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was determined. A p-value of < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Laboratory measurement

Blood gas analysis was performed using the Roche Cobas b123 automatic analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) with Roche proprietary reagents (e.g., cobas b 123 Fluid Pack COOX 400). Complete blood count was assayed using the Sysmex XN-350 hematology analyzer (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan) with Sysmex compatible reagents for XN-350. Biochemical analysis was conducted on the Beckman Coulter Au5800 automatic biochemistry analyzer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) with Beckman Coulter proprietary reagents (e.g., low-density lipoprotein cholesterol calibrators, multi-biochemical calibrators).

Results

Comparison of basic information

This study comprised 148 children with sepsis, including 82 males and 66 females, with an age range of 24.0 (5.0, 84.0) months. Of these, 30 cases were in the SAIL group, resulting in an incidence rate of 20.27%. No statistically significant differences were observed in age, sex, and birth situations between the two groups (P > 0.05). The mortality rate in the SAIL group was significantly higher than that in the non-SAIL group (P < 0.05). The most frequent infection site among the 148 children with sepsis was the lungs. Comparison of laboratory results at admission

Upon admission to the PICU, the SAIL group demonstrated elevated levels of ACAG, creatine kinase (CK), creatine kinase isoenzyme (CK-MB), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), aspartate transaminase (AST), gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT), serum creatinine (SCr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), thrombin time (TT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), prothrombin time (PT), international normalized ratio (INR), and D-dimer, all suggesting statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) when compared to the non-SAIL group. The SAIL group had significantly lower levels of serum albumin (ALB) and platelets (PLT) compared to the non-SAIL group (P < 0.05). Children with arterial lactate levels greater than 2 mmol/L were considered to have hyperlactatemia, with 2–5 mmol/L considered mild hyperlactatemia and levels above 5 mmol/L as severe hyperlactatemia [10]. The prevalence of severe hyperlactatemia among children was substantially higher in the SAIL group compared to the non-SAIL group (P < 0.05) (Table 2).

ACAG, CK, LDH, SCr, BUN, INR, PT, APTT, FDP, GGT, TT, D-dimer, AST, PLT, abdominal infection, and severe hyperlactatemia. Binary logistic regression analysis showed that ACAG, LDH, severe hyperlactatemia, and abdominal infection were independent risk factors for the development of SAIL in children (Table 3).

Predictive value of ACAG combined with LDH for SAIL

ROC curve analysis was employed to evaluate the predictive effectiveness of ACAG and LDH for pediatric SAIL. For LDH > 16.32 umol·s−1/L, the sensitivity for predicting pediatric SAIL was 83.33%, specificity was 86.44%, and the area under the curve (AUC) was 0.888. In cases where ACAG exceeds 15.92 mmol/L, the sensitivity for predicting pediatric SAIL is 90.00%, the specificity is 79.66%, and the AUC is 0.874. The combined use of ACAG and LDH yielded a sensitivity of 90.00%, a specificity of 82.20%, and an AUC of 0.906 for predicting pediatric SAIL (Table 4 Fig. 1).

Discussion

Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. The liver is one of the primary target organs affected during the course of sepsis [14]. SAIL might cause hepatocyte necrosis or apoptosis, ultimately leading to liver failure, which is a core component of multi-organ dysfunction [15]. In the present study, laboratory indicators such as SCr, BUN, CK, and CK-MB in the SAIL group of children were significantly higher than those in the non-SAIL group. This suggests that children with sepsis experience liver injury alongside concurrent injuries in other organs, such as the kidneys and myocardium. Therefore, exploring the clinical characteristics of SAIL and identifying easily diagnosable and early effective clinical predictive indicators is particularly important for the early identification and timely treatment of pediatric SAIL.

The findings of this study suggest that sepsis resulting from intra-abdominal infections is more frequently linked to SAIL. Intra-abdominal infection is a prevalent cause of sepsis and ranks as the second most common source of infection incidence and mortality among ICU patients, after pulmonary infections [16]. Intra-abdominal infections encompass various conditions, including peritonitis, cholangitis, pancreatitis, intestinal perforation, and abdominal trauma. Due to its unique anatomical structure, the liver can be directly affected by pathogens of intra-abdominal infection [17]. Lactate (Lac) is an intermediary product of glucose metabolism. Intra-abdominal infections, particularly when accompanied by sepsis, lead to an increase in gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis, raising blood glucose levels, while the rate of glucose conversion diminishes, resulting in increased Lac generation [18]. Lac levels can rise abnormally under pathological conditions such as respiratory or circulatory failure [19]. Research by Downes et al. [20] confirmed a close relationship between hyperlactatemia and organ dysfunction. The findings of this study demonstrate that severe hyperlactatemia serves as an independent risk factor for pediatric SAIL, corresponding to earlier clinical research that identified Lac as an independent risk factor for SAIL [21]– [22].

Maintaining acid-base balance is essential for normal metabolic function in human cells. Hyperlactatemia can disrupt acid-base balance, and anion gap (AG) is an important indicator that assists in assessing metabolic acidosis and various mixed acid-base imbalances. Related studies have shown that AG effectively predicts prognosis in sepsis patients [23]. Multiple factors can influence AG values, with plasma ALB concentration as the primary determinant. Each 1 g/dl reduction in plasma ALB corresponds to an approximate 2.3 mEq/l decrease in the AG value; conversely, each 1 g/dl increase in plasma ALB results in a 1 mEq/l increase in the plasma AG value [24]. Hypoalbuminemia frequently occurs in critically ill patients and may result in reduced plasma AG values, leading to discrepancies in assessing the body’s acid-base status. The concept of ACAG proposed by Hatheril et al. [25] can correct the AG values in patients with hypoalbuminemia, thereby minimizing errors in representing internal homeostasis and disease severity. Consistent with the findings of Zhang et al. [26], In the present study, the serum ALB levels in SAIL children significantly differed from those of non-SAIL children. Therefore, using the ACAG index to assess the acid-base balance in septic children and exploring the correlation between ACAG and pediatric SAIL has potential clinical value. Binary logistic regression analysis revealed that ACAG and LDH serve as independent risk factors for pediatric SAIL.

LDH is a commonly used laboratory indicator for monitoring diseases such as tumors, cardiac diseases, and liver diseases. Elevated serum LDH levels generally signify tissue injury or pathological conditions. Recent studies have indicated that is statistically associated with sepsis-associated liver injury (SAIL) [27], which is consistent with the results of the present study. ACAG adjusts the AG values based on the concentration of ALB in the body, and this indicator is convenient to obtain clinically. A crucial pathophysiological process in SAIL is the imbalance between oxidative stress and inflammatory responses [16]. The results of the ROC curve analysis in the present study demonstrate that ACAG combined with LDH possesses better predictive value for pediatric SAIL than individual indicators. ACAG, LDH, severe lactic acidosis, and abdominal infection were identified as independent predictors of SAIL incidence in children, the combination of ACAG and LDH had a better predictive value than individual indicators.

This study investigated the clinical significance of ACAG in forecasting pediatric SAIL, offering new perspectives for subsequent clinical research on pediatric SAIL. However, this study also had limitations: (1) It was a single-center retrospective case-control analysis with a limited sample size; (2) This study, being a retrospective analysis, relied on existing records for data collection, which may lead to information bias, particularly in the measurement and documentation of clinical indicators; (3) Although we have considered as many risk factors as possible related to sepsis-associated liver injury in children, there may still be potential factors that were not included in this study. Further research is needed to validate these factors; (4) It focused solely on the predictive value of ACAG for pediatric SAIL at the acute stage upon admission to the PICU, without tracking the dynamic changes in ACAG values throughout pediatric SAIL. Consequently, further multi-center prospective studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate these findings.
