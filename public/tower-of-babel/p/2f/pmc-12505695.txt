Performance evaluation and ranking of health non-governmental organizations in iran: a nationwide assessment of governance and operational performance, and social health impact
Authors: Kamal Gholipour, Amir Sadeghi, Mohammad Qolipour, Seyyede Maryam Haeri, Hadi Hamrahi Yengejeh, Khatereh Mohammadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionEvaluating the performance of Non-Governmental Organizations (NGOs) is a critical step in identifying their capacities and improving their effectiveness. The present study aimed to evaluate and rank health NGOs in Iran based on their intervention in social health, focusing on their contributions to addressing health challenges and promoting community well-being.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included all health NGOs affiliated with medical universities and healthcare service providers across the country. Performance was evaluated using a validated 56-item checklist, covering seven domains: International Relations, Communication Strategies, Volunteer Utilization, Education and Awareness, Successful Models, Transparency/Accountability, and Financial Participation. The level of compliance with specified indicators in the evaluation form was assessed by evaluators approved by the Ministry of Health. After calculating the total scores, each NGO was ranked accordingly. Provincial clustering incorporated socioeconomic and healthcare access variables. The K-means clustering method was used to categorize NGOs into groups based on their performance scores, implemented using STATA-17 software.ResultsA total of 980 health NGOs were evaluated and categorized into five performance clusters using K-means clustering. The ranking distribution revealed: 94 one-star NGOs (9.6%, mean score = 19.55 ± 6.44), 209 two-star (21.3%, 37.09 ± 4.44), 329 three-star (33.6%, 50.33 ± 3.54), 281 four-star (28.7%, 62.48 ± 3.79), and 67 five-star NGOs (6.8%, 79.61 ± 6.55). Performance varied significantly by operational level, with national-level NGOs achieving the highest average score (64.6), followed by provincial (49.38) and district-level organizations (47.51). Three-way ANOVA confirmed significant relationships between performance scores and structural variables (type of licensing authority, provincial cluster, and activity scope; P < 0.001).ConclusionThis study highlights the vital role of NGOs in addressing health challenges and promoting social health. The evaluation and ranking of 980 health NGOs revealed significant differences in performance, with the type of licensing authority playing a crucial role in determining outcomes. NGOs operating at the national level demonstrated the highest performance, underscoring the importance of broader operational scopes and resource availability. Collaborative efforts between NGOs and governmental institutions are essential to enhance social health outcomes and ensure comprehensive healthcare coverage. These findings emphasize the need for targeted support, capacity-building initiatives, and policy reforms to strengthen the effectiveness of NGOs in the health sector.

Introduction

The most effective strategy for achieving sustainable development is comprehensive development that leverages public participation, capacities, and capabilities in all dimensions [1, 2]. Governments increasingly seek to engage with NGOs to address social, cultural, environmental, and economic challenges [3, 4]. NGOs are recognized as crucial tools for mobilizing and organizing public participation, particularly in crisis management and disaster response, due to their agility and ability to engage communities effectively [1, 5, 6].

The term “NGO” (Non-Governmental Organization) was first used in 1945 in Article 71 of Chap. 10 of the United Nations Charter, referring to any voluntary, non-profit group of citizens operating at local, national, or international levels [7–9]. In Iran, as in many other countries, NGOs have a long history of non-profit activities [10]. By 2022, the number of NGOs and charities in Iran had reached 56,367, operating in 11 main sectors and 50 sub-sectors across 469 cities [5, 7, 11].

Given the importance of health as a fundamental human right and the inability of governments alone to cover health expenses, the role of NGOs in health promotion is increasingly vital [12]. According to the Ministry of Health, in 2018, 723 health NGOs and 422 charitable organizations supporting patients with AIDS, addiction, cancer, and rehabilitation were identified. NGOs can significantly contribute to sustainable development by enhancing public access to health services, reducing mortality rates, and promoting social equity [4, 7, 13].

The evaluation of health NGOs faces several persistent challenges. First, there is a lack of standardized performance metrics, making cross-organizational comparisons difficult [5]. Many NGOs struggle with financial sustainability, with over 70% depending on unstable funding sources that hinder long-term planning [13]. Governance gaps also exist, as weak monitoring systems and unclear accountability mechanisms compromise effective oversight [11]. Additionally, significant geographic disparities persist, with Tehran-based NGOs outperforming provincial organizations. Licensing authority variations further complicate evaluations, as NGOs registered with different entities (e.g., Ministry of Interior vs. Welfare Organization) operate under inconsistent regulatory frameworks.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, NGOs played a crucial role in providing employment opportunities, facilitating vaccination campaigns, and identifying and referring patients [7]. However, NGOs also face challenges such as financial instability, weak relationships with the government, and a lack of structured planning and evaluation mechanisms [5, 11, 14, 15]. These issues underscore the need for effective evaluation frameworks to assess the quality and impact of NGO activities [2, 11]. Performance evaluation refers to the process of measuring activities, which is often equated with assessing the effectiveness of those activities [3, 16]. Evaluation should be comprehensive and take into account the requirements and expectations of all various stakeholders involved in this field [15, 17].

Existing studies on NGO evaluation suffer from key limitations. Most rely on qualitative assessments rather than quantitative ranking systems [3], leaving a gap in objective performance benchmarking. The NGO Reference Model provides a comprehensive framework encompassing governance, portfolio and program management, change management, process and quality management, and risk management. It emphasizes accountability through stakeholder engagement, systematic monitoring, and evaluation processes, facilitating continuous improvement and transparency in NGO operations [18]. ​No studies in Iran have systematically compared NGO performance across licensing authorities, despite significant score differences. The potential of clustering methods for NGO classification remains underexplored, even though such approaches could enable targeted capacity-building interventions. Furthermore, prior research has not adequately addressed the relationship between operational metrics (e.g., governance, financial participation) and social health outcomes. Finally, there is a lack of actionable frameworks to translate evaluation findings into policy improvements, particularly for underperforming NGOs. Given the importance of the role of non-governmental organizations (NGOs) in preserving and promoting community health, as well as the necessity of having a mechanism for evaluating and ranking NGOs, this study aims to evaluate and rank health NGOs in Iran based on their intervention in social health, providing insights into their performance and areas for improvement.

Methods

This cross-sectional descriptive-analytical study included all health NGOs affiliated with medical universities and healthcare service providers in Iran. The study is census-based, and the sample size includes all 980 health NGOs whose activity licenses were issued by authorities such as the Ministry of Interior, the Welfare Organization, and the Ministry of Sports and Youth.

Iranian NGO regulatory context

The licensing process for health NGOs in Iran operates through a multi-tiered regulatory system with clearly defined roles for different government entities. At the national level, the Ministry of Health serves as the specialized technical authority, responsible for evaluating whether proposed NGO activities fall within defined health domains, ensuring compliance with healthcare regulations, and verifying the health-related qualifications of founding members. Provincial medical universities act as the Ministry’s executive arms, conducting local verification of documentation and ongoing supervision of operational compliance. Crucially, the Ministry of Interior remains the sole legal authority for issuing operational licenses, with specialized agencies like the Welfare Organization (for charities) and Police having limited, sector-specific authorization capacities. The Ministry of Sports and Youth operates under a specific memorandum of understanding (MoU) with the Ministry of Interior, granting provisional five-year activity licenses only to NGOs whose founding board members are exclusively youth. However, these youth-focused NGOs must still obtain final approval from the Ministry of Interior to achieve formal registration in the national companies’ registry. This integrated yet hierarchical system ensures technical oversight by health authorities while maintaining centralized legal control, with medical universities serving as both pre-licensing validators and post-licensing monitors of health service standards. The process requires NGOs to demonstrate both technical health sector qualifications and organizational viability before receiving final approval from the Ministry of Interior’s National Council for NGOs.

Data collection

In 2021, the level of compliance with the specified indicators in the evaluation form for health NGOs affiliated with medical universities and healthcare service providers was assessed by evaluators approved by the Ministry of Health [19]. Subsequently, in the summer of 2022, the collected data were extracted from the NGO department of the Ministry of Health.

The data collection process implemented a three-phase approach: initial e-notifications were sent to NGO directors via university portals, followed by three rounds of SMS and phone reminders for non-respondents, culminating in in-person visits for the remaining portion of organizations requiring follow-up. Data were collected using three validated instruments: the NGO Profile Form administered through a secure internal platform, the Field Assessment Checklist completed during random surprise visits using paper questionnaire, and a Financial Audit Tool that analyzed three-year expenditure reports against national health accounts.

The performance evaluation of the NGOs was conducted based on a checklist comprising four forms. The contents of the forms are briefly described as follows: affiliated medical University Information, NGO, and Visiting Team Members, Standards for Ranking and Evaluation, Indicators for Ranking and Evaluation, Indicators for Financial Participation Evaluation [19].

The performance evaluation model previously was developed in a third phase Delphi, incorporating the results from the Delphi rounds, 56 items. The model was tested through an exploratory factor analysis (EFA), which identified 7 key dimensions, collectively explaining 81.98% of the total variance. A rotated factor matrix was used to determine correlations between variables, with items showing high correlations (> 0.6) grouped under the same factor. The framework assessed seven key factors identified through exploratory factor analysis. The first factor, International Relations, was evaluated using six items focusing on cross-border collaborations and global health engagement. The second factor, Communication Strategies, was measured through eight items assessing stakeholder engagement and public outreach effectiveness. The third factor, Volunteer Utilization, was examined via six items related to volunteer management and workforce development. The fourth factor, Education and Awareness, was analyzed using six items covering training programs and public education initiatives. The fifth factor, Successful Models and Best Practices, was evaluated through ten items documenting exemplary programs and replicable interventions. The sixth factor, Transparency and Accountability, was assessed with twelve items examining governance structures and reporting mechanisms. The seventh and final factor, Financial Participation, was measured through seven items tracking funding sources and resource allocation. This comprehensive model enabled systematic evaluation across all critical operational domains of health NGOs in Iran. All indicators were validated through expert review (CVI = 0.775) and pilot testing, with final scores calculated as weighted sums based on factor analysis results. The evaluation employed a mixed-methods approach combining administrative data analysis, field assessments, and financial audits to ensure comprehensive measurement across all performance dimensions. The total scores for each indicator were calculated, with the maximum score for each indicator being 100 [19].

The financial participation score was determined based on predefined criteria, with each item assigned a specific score. The maximum score for this indicator was also 100. The items examined included: The monetary value of financial resources attracted since establishment, The monetary value of financial resources attracted in the previous year, The monetary value of participation in creating health and treatment infrastructure since establishment, The monetary value of participation in the field of client support in the previous year, The monetary value of participation in prevention and self-care projects in the previous year, The monetary value of participation from international sources since establishment, The monetary value of participation using the capacity of companies, economic enterprises, and personal branding since establishment. The performance evaluation scores for the NGOs were calculated in the six areas mentioned above. It should be noted that the total score for each indicator was capped at 100 [19].

Data analysis

For each item in the questionnaire, frequency and percentage were reported. For the scores related to each domain, the mean and standard deviation were calculated (or the median and interquartile range (IQR) in cases of non-normal distribution). To examine the relationship between performance scores and nominal multi-state factors (scope of activity, licensing authority, and provincial level based on provincial clustering), ANOVA was used. The significance level for the tests was set at 0.05, and STATA-17 software was used for data analysis.

To categorize all NGOs into homogeneous groups and rank them, the K-means clustering method was used based on the total scores of the NGOs. K-means clustering aims to partition n observations into k clusters, where each observation belongs to the cluster with the nearest mean, which serves as the prototype [20]. Based on the star-rating pattern, the clusters were divided into five main categories. NGOs in the first cluster with the highest average total score were labeled as five-star NGOs (*****), while those in the fifth cluster with the lowest average total score were labeled as one-star NGOs (*).

There are various methods for transforming data into similar or homogeneous groups. The K-means method is one of the simplest and most popular methods used in data mining. In multivariate cases, different features of the data must be used to classify and cluster them. This results in dealing with multidimensional data, where each dimension is referred to as a feature or attribute. Given this, the use of various distance functions becomes relevant. Some features may be quantitative, while others may be qualitative. What matters is having a method to measure the degree of similarity or dissimilarity between data points, which must be considered in clustering methods. The K-means method is an iterative approach that attempts to define distinct, non-overlapping subgroups of data points, known as clusters, where each data point belongs to only one group. This method aims to make the data points within a cluster as similar as possible while keeping the clusters as distinct (distant) from each other as possible. The K-means clustering method is the simplest method for clustering data and is part of the partitioning clustering group. Its advantages include speed, ease of use, and applicability to large datasets. Additionally, it guarantees convergence, considers the best positions for cluster centers, easily adapts to new samples, and allows data to be grouped into different clusters. It is a centroid-based method where each cluster is associated with a center, and its primary goal is to minimize the sum of distances between data points and their corresponding clusters.

To achieve the study’s objectives, provincial clustering was performed. For this purpose, the provinces of the country were clustered based on variables such as urban and rural population covered by health insurance, the number of general practitioners, specialists, and subspecialists, the number of health insurance contract centers, the economic participation rate, the employment ratio, the unemployment rate, the literacy rate, urban and rural household income and expenditure. The K-means method was used for clustering, implemented using STATA-17 software. The data for the variables were extracted from the published statistics of the Statistical Center of Iran. (The complete file related to provincial clustering is attached in the appendices.)

Ethical considerations

All stages of the study, including data collection, storage, analysis, and reporting, were conducted confidentially and securely. All information was accessible only to the research team members. All questionnaires were completed without mentioning names, and the participants’ opinions were included in the report and articles without reference to their names. The respect and rights of the research participants were carefully considered.

Results

A total of 980 health NGOs affiliated with medical universities across the country were evaluated by assessors approved by the Ministry of Health. Of these, 758 NGOs (77.3%) obtained their activity licenses from the Ministry of Interior, 197 NGOs (20.1%) from the Welfare Organization, and 25 NGOs (2.6%) from the Ministry of Sports and Youth.

The scope of scope of activity for all NGOs was classified into three levels: district-level, provincial-level, and national-level. Out of the 980 NGOs, 445 (45.4%) operated at the city level, 438 (44.7%) at the provincial level, and 97 (9.9%) at the national level.

Using the K-means clustering method, provinces were grouped based on variables such as urban and rural population covered by health insurance, the number of general practitioners, specialists, and subspecialists, the number of health insurance contract centers, the economic participation rate, employment ratio, unemployment rate, literacy rate, and urban and rural household income and expenditure. The highest average total score was achieved by NGOs in Tehran province (63.65), followed by Ilam province (57.22) and Kerman province (56.81). A one-way ANOVA test was used to determine the relationship between the total scores and the scope of activity of the NGOs. The results showed a statistically significant difference between the city-level, provincial-level, and national-level groups (P < 0.001). A Tukey HSD post-hoc test was conducted to further examine the differences between the groups. The results indicated that the national-level NGOs had a significantly higher average performance score compared to the city-level NGOs (P < 0.001), with a difference of 17.09 units. The national-level NGOs also had a significantly higher average performance score compared to the provincial-level NGOs (P < 0.001), with a difference of 15.22 units. The results of the three-way ANOVA showed a statistically significant model (P < 0.001), with the type of licensing authority, provincial cluster, and scope of activities significantly related to the NGOs’ performance scores. NGOs operating at the national level had the highest average scores (64.41), followed by those operating at the provincial (48.42) and city levels (46.14). The provincial cluster variable remained significant both before and after adjusting for other variables (P = 0.029). The activity scope and licensing authority variables were not significant after adjusting for other variables (P = 0.289 and P = 0.835, respectively). Discussion

The present study was designed and conducted with the aim of evaluating and ranking health NGOs in the country in terms of their intervention in social health. The findings of this study revealed that the lowest average score achieved by the NGOs was in the area of international relations. Several factors may contribute to this weakness, including inappropriate policy-making by the studied NGOs in the field of international relations, various international sanctions against the country, restrictive domestic laws, the inability of NGOs to adapt to rapidly changing international dynamics, and the lack of effective financial support for participation in the international community, among others.

On the other hand, the highest average score achieved by the NGOs was in the area of volunteer utilization. Reasons for this success include the expansion of mass media, the prioritization of policies aimed at engaging public participation in NGO activities, and the increase in individual and social awareness among the public. A study by Afzali et al. showed that the level of public cooperation in NGO activities is adequate and can be considered a major strength of NGO operations. However, most NGOs cited legal restrictions as a barrier to their effective functioning. Another major issue is that NGOs perceive themselves as financially dependent and are unable to achieve financial self-sufficiency [21]. Mondal and colleagues, in their study, stated that NGOs perform best in stimulating active community participation and, due to their high flexibility, are considered the most important tool in modern public management for attracting voluntary public participation [22]. Lang (2012) examines how NGOs in developing countries increasingly become potential instruments for ideological agendas. The study concludes that while radical modern perspectives are useful for diagnosing accountability issues in commercial research contexts, they require adaptation when analyzing NGOs’ strengths and limitations in civil society reform. Lang ultimately proposes a dialectical framework to conceptualize NGOs’ public role - one that avoids alienation while preserving critical engagement with societal structures [23]. This study advances Lang’s model by quantifying how accountability manifests across governance, operations, and impact dimensions - a tripartite framework absent in earlier qualitative work (23). The integrated reporting framework proposed by Gazzola and Amelio (2023) shows partial relevance to our context, particularly regarding financial efficiency. However, their economic dimensions fail to capture the volunteer-driven resourcing that characterizes Iran’s top-performing NGOs [24]. This gap suggests the need to augment international frameworks with localized metrics, including a volunteer productivity and geographic equity adjustments to address regional disparities.

The average scores of NGOs affiliated with Universities of Medical Sciences, all located in Tehran, showed a significant difference compared to the performance scores of universities in other cities. The reasons for this uneven distribution can be attributed to the concentration of attention in the capital, insufficient authority of provincial managers, the fragmented approach of senior health system officials toward NGO activities, and the lack of a systemic approach in managing NGOs, among others. A study by Damari et al. found that more than 70% of the studied NGOs effectively utilized public participation in their activities. Factors contributing to the success of NGOs included having strategic and operational plans, the ability to establish network communications, effective engagement of target communities, strong monitoring mechanisms, accurate needs assessment of target groups, and proper financial management. The main obstacles to NGO activities were identified as limited financial resources, inadequate communication between NGOs and the government, the government’s weak belief in the role of NGOs, the lack of a performance evaluation system for NGOs, centralized decision-making in the capital, and unclear laws governing NGO activities [5]. In a study by Anbazhagan and Surekha, it was emphasized that, given the increasing number and intensity of global health challenges, the participation of NGOs in shaping global health policies, financing, evaluating, and implementing global health programs through connections with international agencies has become more essential than ever. Unfortunately, many NGOs are weak in the area of international activities [25]. Piotrowicz and Cianciara, in their study, highlighted that the growing role of NGOs in providing social services and their increasing involvement in community policy-making underscores the need for a mechanism to evaluate the quality and effectiveness of their activities. Such evaluations play a crucial role in accrediting NGOs and allocating financial resources to them [11]. Comparison with Boateng et al.‘s five performance measures reveals both convergence and divergence. While their stakeholder involvement dimension weakly correlates with our licensing authority effects, indicating Iranian NGOs’ stronger vertical than horizontal accountability, their financial measures overemphasize absolute funding levels compared to our findings about funding predictability’s greater impact on stability [26]. These contrasts highlight the importance of context-specific adaptations when applying global frameworks.

The results of the statistical tests used in this study showed that as the geographical scope of NGO activities expands, the average total score achieved by the NGOs increases. Reasons for this include increased oversight of NGOs as their scope of activity expands, improved evaluation criteria for activities, greater attention to networking and communication as the level of activity broadens, adequate financial support, and better communication between NGOs and the government, among others. According to a study by Habibpour, the most significant problem faced by NGOs is financial dependence, which leads to political dependence. The primary role of the studied NGOs was in the field of education, while their weakest performance was in policy-making. Strengthening the financial and political independence of these organizations can be achieved through retraining NGOs, evaluating them, ranking them, and developing and implementing financial support packages for NGOs [13].

From the comparison of the average performance scores of different provinces with provincial clustering, it can be inferred that NGOs in provinces within the same cluster had similar average performance scores. Furthermore, the analysis of performance scores based on provincial clusters using a one-way ANOVA test revealed that NGOs in Cluster 1 had better performance scores compared to other clusters, and NGOs in Cluster 2 had better average performance scores than those in Cluster 3. Cluster 1 includes only Tehran province, and the reasons for the better performance scores of NGOs in Tehran can be attributed to the appropriate needs assessment of the target population by these organizations, suitable organizational structures, well-planned activities, faster access to important information and news, and greater availability of resources for their activities. Cluster 3 includes 19 provinces but has the weakest performance in terms of average scores. Given that this cluster comprises a significant portion of the country’s provinces, improving the performance of NGOs in this cluster and expanding their activities could lead to significant progress in the health status of the community. Al Tehewy and colleagues, in their study, stated that the evaluation and accreditation of NGO activities are essential steps in allocating financial resources to these organizations, and accrediting NGOs prevents their bankruptcy and financial dependence [27]. A study by Karanja and Karuti found that the most important factor for an organization’s success in achieving its goals is financial sustainability. To achieve financial sustainability, NGOs must shift from reactive management to goal-oriented and effective management [14]. The clustering results further recommend geographic prioritization - allocating 30% of annual NGO development funds to organizations serving under developed and marginalized regions in Cluster 2. These evidence-based recommendations address both the immediate performance gaps and structural barriers identified in our analysis, moving beyond theoretical conclusions to deliver implementable solutions for health policymakers.

The study’s practical implications emerge clearly when contextualized with Coupet et al.‘s DEA benchmarking approach and Newcomer et al.‘s implementation challenges [28, 29]. We propose a hybrid evaluation system combining DEA for top-tier NGOs with our factor-based scoring for developing organizations, directly addressing measurement literacy barriers through embedded training programs. Donor policies should shift toward outcome-contingent funding using our social impact factors while phasing in integrated reporting requirements based on performance tiers.

For policymakers, we recommend mandating this evaluation framework through Ministry of Health circulars, with compliance incentives including 15% funding increases for NGOs demonstrating annual cluster-tier improvements and fast-track licensing for sustained high performers. Provincial incubators should target cluster-specific deficits, focusing on specific strategies in each setting considering its contextual factors. These evidence-based recommendations balance international best practices with the operational realities revealed by our nationwide assessment, offering a nuanced pathway for strengthening Iran’s health NGO sector while maintaining academic rigor and practical applicability.

Conclusion

This study highlights the critical role of NGOs in addressing health challenges, particularly in social health interventions. The evaluation of 980 health NGOs revealed significant differences in performance, with the type of licensing authority playing a crucial role in determining outcomes. The findings also indicate that NGOs operating at the national level tend to perform better than those operating at provincial or city levels. This could be attributed to greater access to resources, more extensive networks, and stronger organizational structures at the national level. However, the study also identified areas for improvement, particularly in international relations, where NGOs scored the lowest. This may be due to limited exposure to global health initiatives, lack of funding for international collaborations, or restrictive government policies.

The study underscores the importance of collaborative efforts between NGOs and governmental institutions to enhance social health outcomes. By addressing the identified challenges and leveraging the strengths of NGOs, it is possible to achieve more comprehensive and equitable healthcare coverage. Some strategies for strengthening non-governmental organizations (NGOs) include: empowering NGOs in strategic management, training them in developing operational plans, and teaching them how to attract multi-source funding (governmental, private, and philanthropic). Empowering NGOs to achieve financial self-sufficiency through the production and sale of services or products to the public or private sectors. Informing and raising social awareness among citizens about the activities of NGOs. Conducting in-depth studies to fully understand the areas of NGO activities. Avoiding arbitrary or factional approaches in dealing with NGOs. Clearly defining the framework of NGO activities based on the needs of society. Enhancing inter-sectoral coordination. Utilizing the capacities of NGOs in macro-level policy-making for society. Removing restrictive laws on NGO activities and providing government incentives.

Limitations

This study has several methodological and contextual limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the reliance on self-reported data from NGOs may introduce response bias, particularly in sensitive areas like financial transparency and governance practices. However, to mitigate this limitation, all reports were thoroughly assessed and verified for accuracy by responsible specialists at the relevant universities of medical sciences. Second, while the sample included all registered health NGOs under medical universities (N = 980), the exclusion of unregistered or informal health organizations limits generalizability to the broader NGO ecosystem in Iran. Third, the binary (yes/no) scoring system, while simplifying implementation, may oversimplify complex performance dimensions that would benefit from graded assessments. Fourth, the model’s weighting system, though expert-validated, carries inherent subjectivity in assigning importance to different indicators. Fifth, the cross-sectional design captures performance at a single timepoint, unable to assess longitudinal trends or intervention impacts. Finally, while the model explains 81.98% of variance in NGO performance, the remaining 18.02% suggests unmeasured factors like political influences or community trust levels may affect outcomes. These limitations highlight opportunities for future research to incorporate mixed-methods approaches, longitudinal tracking, and expanded inclusion criteria for more comprehensive evaluations.
