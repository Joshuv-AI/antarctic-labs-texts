Low-dose chidamide maintenance therapy following allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation in T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia or lymphomas: a phase II, open-label, multicenter, single-arm trial
Authors: Yanmin Zhao, Weihao Chen, Yi Yu, Yi Luo, Jian Yu, Huarui Fu, Xiaoyu Lai, Lizhen Liu, Guifang Ouyang, Jimin Shi, Lixia Sheng, Weijie Cao, He Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundDespite allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (allo-HCT), relapse remains a leading cause of treatment failure for patients with T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) or T-cell lymphomas (TCL). The primary endpoint was 2-year relapse-free survival (RFS). Secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR), non-relapse mortality (NRM), chronic GVHD, and safety. Research in context

Evidence before this study

Patients with T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) or T-cell lymphoma (TCL) who relapse after allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (allo-HCT) have poor outcomes. Effective and tolerable post-transplant maintenance strategies are therefore needed to reduce relapse risk in this population. We searched PubMed in July, 2023, without language restrictions, using the terms “chidamide” AND (“T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia” OR “T-ALL” OR “T lymphoblastic lymphoma” OR “T-LBL” OR “T-cell lymphoma”) AND (“transplantation” OR “allo-HCT” OR “allo-HSCT”) AND “maintenance”. We found only two small, prospective, single-center studies evaluating chidamide as post-transplant maintenance therapy in patients with T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia or T-cell lymphoblastic lymphoma. At the time this trial was designed, no multicenter prospective study had evaluated chidamide as maintenance therapy after allo-HCT for T-cell hematological malignancies. Previous clinical studies have suggested that chidamide has antitumor activity and an acceptable safety profile in T-cell hematological malignancies. In addition, preclinical data indicate that chidamide has both direct antitumor activity and immunomodulatory effects. These findings provided the rationale for investigating whether chidamide maintenance after transplantation could reduce relapse.

Added value of this study

This study is the first multicenter prospective trial to evaluate chidamide as post-transplant maintenance therapy in patients with T-ALL/TCL. Our findings suggest that chidamide maintenance after allo-HCT has encouraging efficacy and acceptable toxicity in this population.

Implications of all the available evidence

Chidamide maintenance could represent a promising strategy to reduce post-transplant relapse in patients with T-ALL/TCL. These findings support further evaluation of chidamide maintenance in larger prospective studies with randomized designs to determine its efficacy, optimal duration, safety profile, and the patient subgroups most likely to benefit.

Introduction

T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) and T-cell lymphomas (TCL) are biologically heterogeneous malignancies with an aggressive clinical course.1,2 Although allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (allo-HCT) offers curative potential and improves long-term survival in selected patients, 20–50% of individuals still experience relapse after transplantation.1,3, 4, 5 Post-transplant relapse remains a major therapeutic challenge because of frequent resistance to salvage therapies and poor subsequent outcomes. Therefore, strategies that effectively reduce relapse risk after allo-HCT in T-ALL/TCL represent an important unmet clinical need.

Currently, post-transplant maintenance strategies with proven survival benefit are limited. Targeted maintenance approaches, such as incorporating tyrosine kinase inhibitors for Philadelphia chromosome-positive ALL or FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) inhibitors for acute myeloid leukemia with FLT3-internal tandem duplication (FLT3-ITD) mutation—particularly in patients with FLT3-ITD measurable residual disease (MRD) during the peri-transplant period—have demonstrated efficacy.6, 7, 8, 9 However, their clinical utility is strictly confined to specific molecular subsets. Prophylactic donor lymphocyte infusion (DLI) may augment the graft-vs-leukemia/lymphoma (GVL) effect to reduce relapse rates, but its utility is constrained by an increased risk of graft-vs-host disease (GVHD).10 In addition, while hypomethylating agents are generally well tolerated as maintenance therapy, particularly for myeloid neoplasms, a survival benefit has not been consistently demonstrated in different studies.11,12 Thus, for patients with T-ALL/TCL, there remains a critical unmet need for effective and tolerable maintenance strategies after allo-HCT.

Chidamide (tucidinostat) is a novel, orally available, benzamide-based histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor that selectively blocks class I (HDAC1, 2 and 3) and class IIb (HDAC10) HDACs.13 It is approved for the treatment of relapsed peripheral T-cell lymphoma (PTCL) and adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma in China and Japan. Clinical studies have established its safety and efficacy across a broad spectrum of T-cell malignancies, either as monotherapy or in combination with other agents.14, 15, 16, 17 As an epigenetic modulator, chidamide exhibits both direct antitumor activity and immunomodulatory effects that enhance the cytotoxic activity of NK cells and CD8+ T cells in preclinical studies.13,18 Prior reports have suggested that allo-HCT can induce a clinically beneficial GVL effect against lymphoid malignancies, although the magnitude of this effect may vary across disease entities.19,20 This effect is thought to be mediated primarily by donor-derived alloreactive T-cell subsets and NK cells.21 Based on these results, we hypothesized that chidamide could synergize with the GVL effect and thereby reduce the risk of post-transplant relapse in T-ALL/TCL. We therefore conducted a phase II trial to assess the efficacy and safety of chidamide as maintenance therapy following allo-HCT in this population.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a phase II, single-arm, multicenter, open-label trial conducted at three transplantation centers in China (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05991973). Patients with T-ALL/TCL undergoing first allo-HCT were eligible if they were aged 14–70 years, had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0–2, confirmed donor engraftment and established stable hematopoiesis (absolute neutrophil count ≥ 1 × 109/l, platelet count ≥50 × 109/l), were >30 days and ≤100 days from allo-HCT, had their primary disease in complete remission (CR) and MRD negativity within 4 weeks before initiating maintenance, and had no uncontrolled infections or severe liver, kidney, lung, or heart disease. T-ALL/TCL were diagnosed according to the 5th edition of WHO classification. Chidamide exposure before allo-HCT was permitted. There were no disease-related remission criteria required prior to transplant, nor were there restrictions on conditioning regimens, donor type, or graft source.

The key exclusions included uncontrolled acute GVHD (aGVHD), creatinine clearance ≤60 mL/min (Cockcroft-Gault formula), aspartate aminotransferase and alanine aminotransferase ≥3 times the upper limit of normal (ULN), total bilirubin ≥2 times ULN, active cardiovascular disease (uncontrolled arrhythmia or hypertension, prolonged QTc on electrocardiogram, congestive heart failure, New York Heart Association functional class 3 or 4, or myocardial infarction within 6 months before screening), active bacterial, fungal, or viral infection, and life expectancy <8 weeks. Further information on eligibility criteria and concomitant medications permitted is provided in the Supplemental Appendix. Race and ethnicity were not used as eligibility criteria and were not prespecified variables for subgroup or outcome analyses. Information on race and ethnicity was obtained from medical records. All enrolled patients were recorded as Chinese. The study protocol was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (IIT20230004C-R2), and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients or their legal guardians provided written informed consent before treatment.

Procedures

Transplantation protocols were consistent with our previous study.22 Donor sources consisted of haploidentical donors, matched sibling donors as well as unrelated donors. Detailed descriptions of conditioning regimens and prophylaxis measures for GVHD and infection are provided in the Supplementary Materials. Before maintenance therapy, patients were comprehensively evaluated for disease status, utilizing either bone marrow (BM) assessment (involving aspiration/biopsy for morphology and 10-color multiparameter flow cytometry [MFC] for MRD) or positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT). Oral chidamide was initiated between days 30 and 100 post-transplant at 10 mg twice weekly (BIW) and was continued for a maximum of 24 cycles (28 days per cycle) or until relapse, withdrawal of consent, unacceptable toxicity, or death. Dose escalation to a maximum of 20 mg BIW was permitted upon MRD emergence at the physician's discretion.

The protocol was amended once from the start of enrollment to final analysis. None of these amendments affected the sample size or primary endpoints. Detailed amendments are present within the protocol.

Study endpoints and definitions

The primary endpoint was 2-year relapse-free survival (RFS), defined as the time from initiation of chidamide maintenance to relapse, progression or death due to any cause, whichever occurred first. Disease relapse or progression was defined as the presence of any circulating blasts or >5% blasts in the bone marrow, and/or active extramedullary disease for T-ALL, or as the Deauville score of 4–5 on PET/CT for TCL according to the 2014 Lugano response criteria.23

The 2-year overall survival (OS) and cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR) were the key secondary endpoints. Other secondary endpoints comprised 2-year cumulative incidence of non-relapse mortality (NRM) and cGVHD, and adverse events (AEs) graded by the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) v5.0. OS was defined as the time from initiation of chidamide maintenance to death from any cause or the last follow-up. NRM was defined as death without prior relapse or progression.

CR was defined as morphological remission without extramedullary involvement for T-ALL, and as a Deauville score of 0–3 on PET/CT for TCL. MRD was assessed by MFC using a threshold of 0.01%. aGVHD was evaluated and reported according to the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Consensus Criteria and the MAGIC grading system.24,25 cGVHD was evaluated according to the 2014 NIH Consensus Conference criteria.26 Additional details on trial endpoints and assessments are provided in the Supplemental Appendix.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated according to the assumption that the 2-year RFS for patients without post-transplant maintenance is 50% on the basis of published data.27 Assuming that chidamide as a post-transplant maintenance agent would improve the 2-year RFS to 70%, with a two-sided alpha level of 5% and a power of 80%, 1 year of enrollment, and 2 years of follow-up, 44 patients were required. The sample size calculation was performed using PASS software platform (version 15).

Although not prespecified in the initial statistical design, we assembled a contemporaneous control cohort of patients with T-ALL/TCL who underwent allo-HCT during the same period but did not receive any maintenance treatment, using a 1:2 matching ratio. Eligible controls were selected randomly from the institutional database using identical inclusion criteria as the trial population. This post-hoc comparison was exploratory and intended as supportive evidence. Baseline clinical characteristics were subsequently compared between the trial and control cohorts and were found to be broadly similar. In addition, all control patients were required to have survived for more than 100 days after transplantation, corresponding to the upper bound of the predefined window for initiating chidamide maintenance, thereby ensuring a consistent time origin and minimizing immortal time bias.

RFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared between groups using the log-rank test, with hazard ratios (HRs) estimated using Cox proportional-hazards models. The cumulative incidence of each endpoint (relapse, NRM, and GVHD) was analyzed using competing-risks methods and compared between groups using Gray's test, with sub-distribution HRs estimated using Fine–Gray models. The competing risk for relapse was death without relapse, whereas the competing risk for NRM was relapse or progression. For GVHD, competing risks were relapse, progression, or death prior to GVHD. Differences in continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test with Yates' continuity correction for 2 × 2 contingency tables and the Fisher-Freeman-Halton test for contingency tables larger than 2 × 2. Cox proportional-hazards models were also applied for the exploratory subgroup analyses, which were not powered for formal hypothesis testing. When comparing differences between the trial group and control group, tests were two-sided, with a significance level of 0.05. Median follow-up time was calculated by the reverse Kaplan–Meier method. The statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software platform (version 4.4.2; R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

Role of the funding source

The funding source had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, interpretation, or writing of the report.

Efficacy

As the primary endpoint, the 2-year RFS was 83.7% (95% CI, 73.3%–95.6%) after maintenance (Fig. 2A). OS and CIR were analyzed as key secondary endpoints, yielding a 2-year OS rate of 89.6% (95% CI, 80.3%–100%) and 2-year CIR of 11.8% (95% CI, 1.9%–21.7%) after maintenance (Fig. 2B and C).Fig. 2Clinical outcomes in patients with chidamide as maintenance treatment. Clinical outcomes in patients with chidamide as maintenance treatment. (A) relapse-free survival (RFS); (B) overall survival (OS); (C–E) cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR), non-relapse mortality (NRM) and chronic GVHD (cGVHD). CI, confidence interval.

Seven RFS events were observed during follow-up, consisting of five relapses (range, 1.1–13.2 months) and two deaths without relapse. Relapses were hematologic in three patients and extramedullary in two. Regarding outcomes of relapsed patients, one died of refractory disease after chemotherapy, while another achieved CR with salvage therapy and donor lymphocyte infusion but later succumbed to pulmonary cGVHD. The remaining three patients achieved a second CR following salvage therapy and remained in remission at the last follow-up. The 2-year NRM was 4.5% (95% CI, 0%–10.7%) among patients receiving chidamide maintenance (Fig. 2D). During follow-up, MRD positivity was detected in two patients. One subsequently developed hematologic relapse, whereas the other achieved MRD negativity after chidamide dose escalation and DLI and remained in remission thereafter. At the end of the study, 40 patients were alive and four had died. Two deaths were related to relapses, while the other two resulted from severe pulmonary infection.

Considering the potential immunomodulatory effects of chidamide, we further investigated the incidence of cGVHD following maintenance therapy. The cumulative incidence of cGVHD at 2 years was 38.3% (95% CI, 23.0%–53.5%) (Fig. 2E). Notably, the 2-year cumulative incidence of moderate-to-severe cGVHD was 6.8% (95% CI, 0%–14.4%).

We next examined whether clinical outcomes differed between the two disease subtypes. Overall, no statistically significant differences in prognosis were observed between patients with T-ALL and those with TCL. The 2-year RFS was 92.3% (95% CI, 82.6%–100%) in the T-ALL group and 71.3% (95% CI, 52.8%–96.3%) in the TCL group (p = 0.095), while 2-year OS was 90.5% (95% CI, 78.6%–100%) and 88.9% (95% CI, 75.5%–100%), respectively (p = 0.62) (Figure S2A and B). The 2-year CIR was 7.7% (95% CI, 0%–18.1%) for T-ALL and 17.6% (95% CI, 0%–36.5%) for TCL (p = 0.40) (Figure S2C). The 2-year NRM was 0% in the T-ALL group and 11.1% (95% CI, 0%–26.1%) in the TCL group (p = 0.086) (Figure S2D). The cumulative incidence of cGVHD at 2 years was also comparable between the two subtypes (T-ALL: 35.6% [95% CI, 16.3%–55.0%] vs TCL: 41.7% [95% CI, 16.4%–67.0%], p = 0.85) (Figure S2E).

Comparison with control cohort

To contextualize the findings of this single-arm study, we compared our outcomes with a contemporaneous control cohort in the post-hoc analysis. The baseline characteristics of the chidamide and control cohorts are presented in Table S1. The specific subtypes of TCL in the control cohort are shown in Figure S1B. There were no statistically significant differences in age, sex, disease subtypes, disease status at allo-HCT, donor/patient sex matching, human leukocyte antigen (HLA) match or conditioning intensity.

There was a trend toward improved 2-year RFS post-transplant in the chidamide group compared to the control group (83.7% vs 67.0%; HR, 0.48 [95% CI, 0.21–1.09]; p = 0.074). The 2-year OS after allo-HCT was significantly higher in the chidamide group than in the control group (93.1% vs 74.8%; HR, 0.27 [95% CI, 0.08–0.90]; p = 0.022) (Fig. 3A and B). Similarly, the 2-year CIR after transplantation was numerically lower among patients receiving maintenance therapy compared with controls (11.8% vs 26.1%, HR, 0.44 [95% CI, 0.17–1.19], p = 0.091), whereas NRM at 2 years did not differ significantly between the two groups (4.5% vs 6.9%, p = 0.60) (Fig. 3C and D). Regarding cGVHD, the 2-year cumulative incidence after transplantation did not differ significantly between the two groups (38.2% vs 48.9%; p = 0.30; Fig. 3E). The cumulative incidence of moderate-to-severe cGVHD was 6.8% (95% CI, 0%–14.4%) in the chidamide group, numerically lower than the 20.8% (95% CI, 9.7%–31.8%) observed in the contemporaneous control cohort (p = 0.11, Fig. 3F). Exploratory subgroup analysis of RFS across key clinical variables, including age, sex, disease subtype, disease status at transplantation, HLA match, and conditioning intensity, suggested that treatment effects were generally consistent across subgroups, although a greater benefit was numerically observed in male compared with female patients (Figure S3). Safety

Low-dose chidamide was generally well tolerated as maintenance therapy after allo-HCT in patients with T-ALL/TCL. The cumulative incidence of treatment-emergent grade III–IV aGVHD at 100 days after treatment was 2.2% (95% CI, 0%–6.7%). Among 44 recipients treated with chidamide, the most common treatment-emergent AEs (TEAEs) of any grade were leukopenia (5 [11%]), thrombocytopenia (5 [11%]), nausea and vomiting (5 [11%]), neutropenia (4 [9%]), fatigue (4 [9%]), infection (4 [9%]), diarrhea (3 [7%]), and elevated transaminases (3 [7%]) (Table 2). Most grade 1–2 TEAEs resolved with symptomatic or supportive treatment. Grade ≥3 TEAEs were uncommon and comprised thrombocytopenia (2 [5%]) and infections or pneumonia (2 [5%]) (Table 2). Two patients discontinued chidamide maintenance because of grade 4 pneumonia and subsequently died from infectious complications. Discussion

Patients with T-cell malignancies face a poor prognosis even following allo-HCT, representing a significant unmet medical need for novel post-transplant maintenance strategies. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first multicenter prospective study to investigate the efficacy and safety of chidamide as maintenance therapy for patients with T-ALL/TCL following allo-HCT. Our findings demonstrate that post-transplant chidamide maintenance is associated with promising survival outcomes, achieving a 2-year OS rate of 89.6% (95% CI, 80.3%–100%) and RFS of 83.7% (95% CI, 73.3%–95.6%). Importantly, compared with a contemporaneous control cohort, chidamide maintenance resulted in a significantly improved OS and a trend toward superior RFS, with a favorable safety profile and no exacerbation of GVHD. These data suggest that chidamide could be a promising maintenance strategy to reduce the risk of relapse in this high-risk population.

Our results accord with previous retrospective studies, showing that post-transplant chidamide maintenance might reduce relapse and improve survival for individuals. Mechanistically, the therapeutic benefit of chidamide observed in our clinical cohort may be underpinned by both direct antitumor effects and immunomodulatory functions. HDACs normally regulate a wide range of cell survival and signaling pathways relevant to T-cell malignancies (e.g., NF-κB, Notch, JAK/STAT3, and PI3K/AKT).28 Consistent with the oncogenic role of HDACs, multiple studies have reported that the elevated expression of HDAC1, HDAC2, HDAC3, HDAC6 and HDAC8 in T-ALL/LBL and/or PTCL was associated with reduced overall survival.29, 30, 31 Genomic analyses of the GSE26713 dataset also revealed that 117 T-ALL patients exhibited significantly higher levels of these HDACs compared to 7 normal bone marrow controls, underscoring their potential as valuable epigenetic targets for T-ALL/LBL treatment.32 A previous study indicated chidamide could exert an antitumor effect on T-ALL/LBL and promote a more inflammatory form of cell death.32 Xi et al. also found that chidamide exerted an antitumor effect on T-ALL cell lines and primary cells by inhibiting the NOTCH1-MYC signaling axis, with preliminary evidence suggesting a reduction in MRD in T-ALL patients.33 Furthermore, several clinical studies have indicated that chidamide, either as monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy, improves remission rates and prolongs progression-free survival in patients with refractory/relapsed or untreated T-ALL/LBL, as well as those with PTCL.14, 15, 16, 17 These experimental and clinical data collectively substantiate the mechanistic basis for employing chidamide to prevent recurrence in T-cell malignancies.

In addition to the direct antitumor effects, chidamide also enhances the cytotoxic activity of NK cells and CD8+ T cells, promotes T-cell chemokine expression and augments IFN-γ response in preclinical studies.13,18,34,35 Moreover, in the allo-HCT setting, HDAC inhibitors such as panobinostat and vorinostat can suppress GVHD, while preserving the beneficial GVL effects. For example, in models of GVHD, vorinostat suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokines, regulates antigen-presenting cells through induction of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase, enhances T-regulatory functions, and preserves GVL responses.36, 37, 38 Accordingly, in our study, chidamide maintenance did not increase the risk of aGVHD or moderate/severe cGVHD. The cumulative incidence of grade III–IV aGVHD at 100 days after treatment was 2.2% (95% CI, 0%–6.7%). Of note, the moderate was only 6.8% (95% CI, 0%–14.4%), lower than the 15%–40% reported in historical cohorts and the 20.8% (95% CI, 9.7%–31.8%) observed in our contemporaneous control cohort.

Chidamide was generally well tolerated, with a favorable safety profile consistent with previous reports in hematologic malignancies.14, 15, 16, 17 The most common TEAEs were grade 1–2 hematologic toxicities (leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, neutropenia) and gastrointestinal symptoms, which resolved with symptomatic management or temporary dose adjustments. Grade ≥3 TEAEs were rare, and only 2 patients discontinued therapy due to severe pneumonia, which was considered more likely related to post-transplant immunosuppression rather than direct drug toxicity, as infections are a common complication in allo-HCT recipients. The low toxicity of chidamide may be attributed to its selective HDAC inhibition, which avoids pan-HDACi-related severe toxicities, and the low maintenance dose. This safety profile supports the feasibility of long-term maintenance therapy.

Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the single-arm design and the use of a contemporaneous control cohort introduce the potential for selection and immortal time biases, despite the baseline characteristics being well-balanced. The post-transplant comparison with the control cohort was exploratory and descriptive only and should be interpreted with caution. Second, the follow-up duration, while sufficient to assess 2-year outcomes, may not capture late relapses or very long-term toxicities. Additionally, the heterogeneity of T-cell subtypes, although reflecting real-world practice, requires further stratification in future studies.

In conclusion, this phase II study demonstrates that chidamide maintenance following allo-HCT is a feasible and effective strategy for patients with T-cell hematological malignancies. Given its potential clinical benefits, further evaluation of chidamide maintenance in larger, randomized controlled trials is warranted to determine whether it could become a standard-of-care maintenance strategy in this high-risk patient population.
