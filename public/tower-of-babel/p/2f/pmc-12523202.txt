Effects of exercise on physical performance and quality of life in individuals with limb amputation: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Shuo Chang, Jian xin Yan, Liang Mu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAlthough growing evidence supports the benefits of physical exercise for amputees, existing studies present inconsistent findings. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate whether exercise interventions can effectively enhance physical performance and quality of life in individuals with lower-limb amputation.MethodsSeven databases (PubMed, Cochrane Library, OVID, Scopus, PsycINFO, CINAHL, and Web of Science) were systematically searched up to April 21, 2025. Study quality was assessed using the RoB 2 tool, and meta-analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.3).ResultsA total of 18 randomized controlled trials were included, with 7 studies incorporated into the meta-analysis. Evidence ranging from low to moderate quality suggests that exercise interventions yield significant benefits for individuals with limb amputation. Compared to usual care, exercise significantly improved gait speed [WMD = 0.009, 95% CI (0.03, 0.16), P = 0.004], 2-min walk test performance [WMD = 8.81, 95% CI (1.92, 15.7), P = 0.012], and Timed Up and Go test scores [WMD = –2.01, 95% CI (–3.90, –0.13), P = 0.037]. No significant difference was observed in the Life-Space Community Index [WMD = –0.44, 95% CI (–2.48, 1.61), P = 0.676]. Qualitative findings further supported the beneficial effects of exercise, although outcomes varied depending on exercise modality and assessment tools. In addition, heterogeneity in intervention protocols and participant characteristics limited the ability to conduct formal subgroup analyses or draw definitive conclusions.ConclusionTargeted exercise prescriptions can improve physical performance and quality of life in individuals with limb amputation. These findings support the integration of individualized exercise strategies into rehabilitation programs. However, heterogeneity in participant characteristics (e.g., age, gender, etiology, and amputation level) remains underexplored. Future research should address this diversity to optimize rehabilitation strategies for different subpopulations.

Introduction

Amputation is a surgical procedure to remove a limb or part of the body [1], commonly caused by chronic vascular diseases, diabetes, or trauma [2, 3]. Over the past 25 years, the incidence of amputations has risen significantly, with projections indicating a potential doubling in the next 15 years [4]. Individuals with limb amputation face substantial challenges in functional independence, medical complications, and financial burdens. They are also prone to secondary health issues—such as degenerative joint disease, osteoporosis, and chronic lower back pain—resulting from compensatory movement patterns, prosthetic misfit, or coexisting chronic conditions [5, 6].

Physical exercise plays a pivotal role in the rehabilitation of individuals with lower-limb amputation [7], positively impacting both physical performance and quality of life (QoL), which are critical to effective rehabilitation. Physical performance encompasses physiological fitness, psychological readiness, physical development, biomechanical proficiency, and tactical awareness [8], all of which significantly influence individuals with limb amputation’ QoL. Exercise enhances biomechanical function, physiological health, and psychological well-being, leading to improved physical performance and, consequently, enhanced QoL. QoL is defined as an individual’s perception of their life within their cultural and value systems, relative to their goals, expectations, standards, and concerns [9]For individuals with limb amputation, QoL is shaped by physical condition, mental health, independence, social relationships, and environmental interactions, all of which are positively influenced by improved physical performance. Thus, exercise not only boosts physical capacity but also fosters psychological resilience, functional independence, and social engagement, significantly enhancing QoL.

Key approaches to improving physical performance and QoL include prosthetic fitting, physical therapy, and targeted rehabilitation programs [10]. These strategies promote autonomy, enhance physiological health [11, 12]. However, existing studies in this field are limited by several methodological shortcomings, including small sample sizes, heterogeneous intervention protocols, and a lack of standardized outcome reporting. Moreover, most prior reviews focus on isolated aspects of physical function (e.g., gait or balance), without integrating broader domains such as cardiopulmonary endurance, muscular strength, or health-related quality of life. To date, no systematic review has comprehensively synthesized the evidence on exercise interventions and their simultaneous effects on both physical performance and QoL among individuals with limb amputation. This gap highlights the need for a more holistic evaluation of exercise as a multidimensional rehabilitation tool [13, 14]. We prioritized physical performance and quality of life as the two primary outcomes because they are among the most clinically relevant indicators of functional recovery and psychosocial well-being in this population. These domains directly reflect both objective and subjective rehabilitation progress, and are frequently emphasized in clinical decision-making, prosthetic training, and long-term reintegration strategies.

This systematic review aims to synthesize and critically appraise existing literature on physical activity among individuals with lower-limb amputation. It seeks to provide clinicians and healthcare professionals with evidence-based insights into how physical activity affects physical performance and QoL, ultimately informing the development of optimized rehabilitation protocols.

Methods

Search strategy

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database (registration number: CRD42023431591).

A systematic literature search was independently performed by two reviewers based on a predefined search strategy. The following seven electronic databases were searched: PubMed, Cochrane Library, OVID, Scopus, PsycINFO, CINAHL, and Web of Science. The search covered all records from database inception to April 21, 2025. Gray literature sources, such as conference proceedings, dissertations, or trial registries, were not included in this review.

The search strategy combined free-text terms and controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms), using the following keywords:"Amputees","Multiple Amputees","Limb Loss","Limb Prosthesis","Exercises","Physical Activity","Aerobic Exercise", and"Walking". In addition, the reference lists of relevant systematic reviews and meta-analyses were manually screened to identify any potentially eligible studies that may have been missed during the electronic search.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Studies were included if they met all of the following criteria: (1) randomized controlled trial (RCT) design; (2) participants were adults (≥ 18 years) with limb amputation due to any cause—including vascular disease, trauma, tumor, infection, or burns—who were at least six months post-amputation, ensuring they had completed acute postoperative recovery and initial prosthetic training. This timeframe reflects a clinically stable stage suitable for structured exercise interventions [15]; (3) the intervention involved any form of exercise-based program, including physical activity, structured exercise training, physical therapy, or rehabilitation exercises, with an emphasis on active engagement of the musculoskeletal or neuromotor systems. This included condition-specific approaches such as phantom limb training and mirror therapy when they involved motor imagery, voluntary residual limb activation, or other forms of patient-initiated movement; (4) the study reported at least one relevant outcome related to physical performance or quality of life, including cardiopulmonary function (e.g., VO₂, VO₂max, anaerobic threshold), muscular strength, flexibility, gait speed, Timed Up and Go (TUG) test results, 2-Minute Walk Test (2MWT) performance, Locomotor Capabilities Index (LCI), pain scores (e.g., VAS, SF-36 Pain subscale), or balance confidence as measured by the Activities-Specific Balance Confidence (ABC) Scale.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following conditions: (1) use of a non-randomized or observational design; (2) absence of extractable data necessary for meta-analysis, even after contacting the authors; (3) serious methodological flaws such as unclear randomization, lack of blinding, or high attrition rates, or incomplete outcome reporting; (4) poor study quality or lack of clarity in data presentation; or (5) publication type was a review, editorial, conference abstract, or any other form of non-original research.

Study selection and data extraction

Two reviewers independently and blindly screened all records by title, abstract, and full text according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached; if disagreement persisted, a third reviewer was consulted to make the final decision. Inter-rater agreement during abstract and full-text screening was assessed using Cohen’s Kappa statistic [16].

A standardized data extraction form was developed to capture key study characteristics, including author and publication year, study design, participant demographics (sample size, gender, age range), intervention components (type, intensity, frequency, duration), and outcome measures.

Classification of interventions

Exercise interventions were categorized according to the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) guidelines, which offer a standardized framework for classifying training modalities (e.g., aerobic, strength, flexibility, neuromotor). Although primarily developed for general and clinical populations, these principles are applicable to post-rehabilitation individuals with lower-limb amputation and have been widely adopted in rehabilitation research. The classifications are as follows: muscular endurance training, designed to improve muscle groups’ ability to sustain repeated contractions using low resistance and high repetitions; muscular strength training, aimed at increasing maximal force output through high resistance and low repetitions; aerobic training, conducted below the AT to enhance cardiorespiratory endurance; functional training, involving neuromotor exercises to improve balance, coordination, gait, agility, and proprioception; flexibility training, focused on enhancing the range of motion of major muscle–tendon units through stretching; and combined training, integrating two or more of the aforementioned modalities.

Risk of bias assessment

The revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) [17] was used to assess potential biases in five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Each domain was rated as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk,” and the overall risk of bias for each trial was determined based on the highest risk rating across all domains. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or adjudicated by a third reviewer when necessary.

Statistical analysis

Effect sizes were calculated using either standardized mean differences (SMDs) or mean differences (MDs), along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), depending on the outcome measure. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, categorized as low (≤ 25%), moderate (50%), or high (≥ 75%) [18]. For each meta-analysis, the choice between a fixed-effects or random-effects model was based on the degree of heterogeneity. When heterogeneity was low (I2 ≤ 50%) and study populations/interventions were deemed sufficiently comparable, a fixed-effects model was applied to provide a more precise pooled estimate. When moderate to high heterogeneity (I2 > 50%) was detected, or when there was notable clinical or methodological diversity among studies, a random-effects model was used to account for between-study variability. All meta-analyses were conducted using R version 4.1.3.

Quality of evidence assessment

The quality of evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) [19], which considers five domains: risk of bias, publication bias, indirectness, imprecision, and inconsistency. Each outcome was graded as high, moderate, low, or very low quality. High quality: further research is unlikely to change the confidence in the effect estimate. Moderate quality: further research may have an important impact on the confidence in the estimate and may change the result. Low quality: further research is likely to have a significant impact and likely to change the estimate. Very low quality: the estimate is very uncertain.

A total of 18 randomized controlled trials involving 603 participants were included. Most participants were male, and the age range varied widely across studies. The majority of studies were conducted in developed countries, with a few conducted in developing regions. Quality assessment of included studies

Overall, the risk of bias among the included studies was predominantly rated as “some concerns,” while relatively few studies were judged as having “low risk” or “high risk” of bias. Details are illustrated in Table 1.

Physical performance

Cardiopulmonary function

Two studies evaluated cardiopulmonary function using bicycle ergometry. In one study, endurance training significantly increased maximal oxygen uptake (VO₂max) and AT by an average of 36.5% and 26%, respectively, compared with baseline (p < 0.05) [27]. Another study reported a significant reduction in oxygen consumption during a 1 m/s walking test following a 10-week comprehensive exercise program (including warm-up, balance and coordination training, hip strengthening, and cool-down) (p < 0.05) [28].

Muscular strength

Three studies assessed lower limb muscle strength using isokinetic dynamometry at various positions. An 8-week combined training program that included resistance training significantly improved bilateral lower limb peak torque in individuals with limb amputation (p < 0.01) [24]. In another study, hip abductor strength during seated testing significantly improved after 8 weeks of targeted hip abduction training (p < 0.05) [29], whereas no significant changes were observed in side-lying or supine positions. The control group receiving 8 weeks of upper-body ergometer training showed no significant improvements in lower limb strength (p > 0.05). A 6-week intervention combining traditional prosthetic resistance training with proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation (PNF) led to significant strength gains in the hip abductors, flexors, extensors, and knee flexors. In contrast, traditional prosthetic resistance training alone did not yield significant improvements in lower limb strength [30].

Flexibility

One study evaluated joint flexibility by measuring maximum hip flexion–extension (during swing phase), maximum knee flexion (swing phase), and maximum ankle dorsiflexion (terminal stance). A 10-week hip-dominant comprehensive training program significantly improved hip flexion–extension on the amputated side and hip extension on the intact side (p < 0.05) [22]; however, no significant changes were observed in hip flexion on the intact side, maximum knee flexion, or ankle dorsiflexion. Another study assessed dynamic flexibility by measuring changes in the distance between anatomical reference points before and after active trunk movements (flexion, extension, lateral bending, and rotation). Compared with a control group that followed a home-based booklet-guided routine, a 12-week targeted exercise program—including resistance and stretching, spinal stabilization, and dynamic residual limb exercises—resulted in greater improvements in trunk lateral bending, rightward rotation, and trunk extension–flexion flexibility [31].

Gait Speed

Four studies [20–23] evaluated the effect of exercise interventions on gait speed in individuals with limb amputation using consistent measurement tools. The weighted mean difference (WMD) was calculated to synthesize the effect size. Moderate heterogeneity was detected across studies (P = 0.191, I2 = 36.9%). TUG

Four studies [20, 21, 23, 24] investigated the impact of exercise on TUG performance. As the outcome measures were consistent across studies, WMD was used for meta-analysis. Moderate heterogeneity was observed (P = 0.064, I2 = 58.7%). A random-effects model showed that the exercise group had significantly shorter TUG times compared to the control group [WMD = –2.01, 95% CI (–3.90, –0.13), P = 0.037] (Fig. 2B).

One study [32] examined dual-task versus single-task training effects, finding significant within-group improvements in the dual-task group (p < 0.05), but no significant between-group differences (p = 0.161). Another study comparing neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) training with a standard military amputee rehabilitation program found no significant between-group differences at 6 weeks, but a significant difference at 12 weeks (p < 0.05) [33]. Additionally, an 8-week hip abductor strength training program significantly improved TUG performance (p < 0.01), whereas upper-body ergometer training had no significant effect. However, the between-group difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.358) [29].

2MWT

Five studies [20, 21, 23, 25, 26] assessed the effects of exercise on the 2MWT in individuals with limb amputation using uniform protocols. WMD was used for effect size estimation. Heterogeneity was minimal (P = 0.523, I2 = 0.0%), and a fixed-effects model was applied. The pooled result indicated that the exercise group walked significantly farther than the usual care group [WMD = 8.81, 95% CI (1.92, 15.7), P = 0.012] (Fig. 2C).

Both NMES training and standard military rehabilitation were found to significantly improve 2MWT performance, but no significant between-group difference was observed [33]. Similarly, an 8-week hip abductor strengthening program led to significant within-group improvements (p < 0.05), while the upper-body ergometer group showed no meaningful change. However, the between-group difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.235) [29].

Quality of life

LCI

Two studies [23, 25] assessed the impact of exercise interventions on the LCI among individuals with limb amputation using consistent measurement tools. Weighted mean difference (WMD) was used to pool the effect sizes. Heterogeneity was minimal (P = 0.566, I2 = 0.0%), and a fixed-effects model was applied. The results indicated no significant difference in LCI between the exercise and usual care groups [WMD = –0.44, 95% CI (–2.48, 1.61), P = 0.676] (Fig. 2D). One additional study found that PNF training significantly improved LCI scores compared with conventional prosthetic training, with the PNF group demonstrating superior outcomes (p = 0.006) [34].

Pain scores

One studies [35]. used the SF-36 pain subscale to evaluate the effect of interventions on pain perception in individuals with limb amputation. A 4-week intervention comparing phantom limb training (defined as motor imagery combined with voluntary residual limb muscle activation, without visual feedback) and mirror therapy showed a significant difference favoring mirror therapy. In another study, adding mirror therapy to phantom limb training showed no significant difference at 2 weeks (p = 0.644), but a significant difference emerged at 4 weeks, favoring the combined approach (p = 0.012) [35].

Three studies [31, 35, 36] used the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) to quantify pain intensity. Both a 2-week back health education program and a targeted exercise intervention significantly reduced pain intensity (p < 0.001), with the latter showing superior effects (p < 0.001) [31]. A 4-day intervention involving mirror therapy (p = 0.003) and transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) (p = 0.001) also significantly reduced pain intensity, though no significant difference was found between the two groups (p = 0.223) [36]. Another study reported no significant difference between combined mirror and phantom limb therapy versus mirror therapy alone at baseline (p = 0.182), but a significant difference emerged after 4 weeks (p = 0.003) [35].

ABC scores

Three studies used the ABC scores to assess participants’ confidence in maintaining balance during specific daily activities. A 12-week comprehensive training program including strength, balance, gait endurance, flexibility, and cardiovascular components did not yield significant improvements (p = 0.518) [37]. However, an 8-week hip abductor strengthening program significantly improved ABC scores (p < 0.05), while no significant changes were observed in the upper-body ergometer control group (p > 0.05) [29]. A 4-week Wii.n.Walk combined exercise and cognitive training program did not result in significant changes in ABC scores [25].

Quality of evidence assessment

The quality of evidence for the four outcomes included in the quantitative synthesis ranged from low to moderate. This grading was primarily influenced by methodological limitations such as inadequate or unclear randomization procedures, insufficient allocation concealment, and potential risk of bias due to lack of blinding. Additionally, clinical heterogeneity among participants may have contributed to inconsistency across studies. The relatively small sample sizes in some studies also introduced imprecision in the effect estimates. Discussion

This study is the first to systematically review and meta-analyze the effects of exercise interventions on physical performance and quality of life among individuals with lower-limb amputation. A total of seven studies were included in the quantitative synthesis, and the results—based on low- to moderate-quality evidence—demonstrated that exercise significantly improved performance on the 2MWT, TUG test, and gait speed. However, no significant effect was observed for the LCI. Narrative synthesis of studies not included in the meta-analysis further supported the positive influence of exercise on both physical performance and quality of life in this population. Nevertheless, variations in exercise prescription may lead to inconsistent effects across different measurement domains, even when targeting the same outcomes.

Sources of heterogeneity

The heterogeneity observed in the studies included in this review may stem from variations in exercise modalities, participant characteristics (e.g., age, comorbidities, type of amputation), and intervention protocols (e.g., duration, intensity, frequency). These factors have likely contributed to the variability in outcomes. For instance, differences in amputation etiology (vascular vs. trauma) and the absence of standardized core outcome sets for this population may be key contributors. Additionally, the wide variation in what constitutes"exercise"across studies, from aerobic activities to strength training, further adds to the complexity of interpreting the evidence. Understanding these sources of heterogeneity is crucial for tailoring interventions more effectively in future clinical practice. These findings represent a solid step forward in understanding the role of exercise in rehabilitation for individuals with lower-limb amputation.

Effects of exercise on physical performance in individuals with limb amputation

The reviewed studies indicate that exercise training improved VO₂max and AT among individuals with lower-limb amputation. Additionally, oxygen consumption at a given submaximal workload was significantly reduced following intervention [27, 28], suggesting improvements in cardiopulmonary health, endurance, and overall exercise efficiency. These findings align with evidence from other rehabilitation populations. For instance, Schleifer et al. [38] reported that six weeks of functional electrical stimulation (FES) training significantly enhanced VO₂max and AT in patients with spinal cord injury, noting that a higher AT may facilitate sustained high-intensity exercise and induce favorable physiological adaptations. However, Nolan [28] noted that earlier studies failed to show that cycling, walking, or resistance training alone improved submaximal oxygen consumption in individuals with lower-limb amputation. The observed improvements in oxygen efficiency following the 10-week comprehensive exercise program [28] may not be attributable solely to cardiorespiratory adaptation. Possible contributing factors include body weight reduction during the training period, training-induced changes in gait mechanics, or increased muscle strength. These changes may have led to more energy-efficient movement patterns. Nonetheless, whether these factors account for between-group differences remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

Isokinetic testing revealed that targeted training of the lower limb musculature can significantly enhance muscle strength in individuals with lower-limb amputation. This finding is consistent with the results of a systematic review by Bouzas et al.[40]. An 8-week comprehensive exercise program that included resistance training significantly improved peak torque in bilateral lower limb muscles (P < 0.01)[24]. Additionally, a targeted 8-week hip abductor strength training program led to a significant increase in seated hip abductor strength (p < 0.05)[29]. These results suggest that focused training of specific muscle groups yields substantial improvements, which may have direct implications for daily activities and prosthetic use among individuals with limb amputation. However, no significant changes in strength were observed in the side-lying and supine testing positions, indicating that strength gains may not be uniformly reflected across all testing postures. Alternatively, these positions may not accurately capture the functional strength improvements relevant to individuals with limb amputation. Moreover, a control intervention involving 8 weeks of upper-limb ergometer training did not significantly affect lower-limb strength (p > 0.05)[29], further emphasizing the importance of specificity in exercise interventions. Furthermore, a 6-week traditional prosthetic strength training program combined with PNF yielded significant improvements in the strength of the hip abductors, hip flexors, hip extensors, and knee flexors[30] These findings underscore the potential of PNF-based interventions to enhance muscular strength and overall motor function in this population.

Flexibility training is considered a critical component in the rehabilitation process following limb amputation[22]. Beissner et al. emphasized that flexibility is one of the strongest predictors of Activities of Daily Living (ADL) performance[39]. In this review, various indicators were used to assess flexibility among individuals with amputation, including maximal hip flexion–extension, maximal knee flexion, and maximal ankle dorsiflexion. A 10-week comprehensive training program centered on hip strength significantly improved hip flexion–extension on the amputated side and hip extension flexibility on the intact side [22]. However, it did not significantly affect hip flexion on the intact side, maximal knee flexion, or ankle dorsiflexion. Several factors may explain these differential effects: (1) The training modalities may not have been appropriate for improving flexibility in certain joints—for instance, protocols emphasizing strength over mobility. (2) The body’s adaptive mechanisms may limit gains when exercises do not challenge the functional range beyond habitual movement patterns. (3) Some improvements in flexibility may have been too small to reach statistical significance, yet could still be clinically meaningful. Additionally, variability in measurement techniques could influence these findings. These results suggest that even well-targeted exercises may not yield broad improvements across all aspects of flexibility. Therefore, rehabilitation programs for individuals with limb amputation may require either a more comprehensive or more joint-specific approach to address distinct movement limitations. Another study found that a 12-week targeted intervention—including muscle strengthening and stretching, spinal stabilization, and dynamic residual limb exercises—effectively improved trunk lateral flexion, rightward rotation, and overall flexion–extension capacity[31]. However, no significant changes were observed in forward flexion or backward extension. This highlights the importance of exercise specificity for addressing particular functional impairments, and points to the potential need for more holistic and refined training strategies to comprehensively enhance flexibility and mobility in individuals with limb amputation.

Most existing studies focus on short-term effects; future research should explore the long-term impact of flexibility training on both functional outcomes and health-related quality of life in this population.

Walking capacity in individuals with limb amputation was assessed using gait speed and the 2MWT. Four studies [20–23] evaluated the impact of exercise on gait speed, revealing a statistically significant improvement in walking speed among individuals with limb amputation following intervention (MD = 0.009, p < 0.01), consistent with findings from Abou et al.[40]. However, a 12-week telehealth-based intervention did not yield meaningful improvements in gait speed, which may be attributed to low adherence to the prescribed walking dose. The authors noted that only 10% of participants achieved the target walking dose [20]. Pooled results from five studies [20, 21, 23, 25, 26] demonstrated a significant increase in 2MWT distance following exercise intervention (MD = 8.81, p = 0.012). Both NMES and traditional military rehabilitation programs significantly improved 2MWT performance, with no significant difference observed between the two interventions[33]. This suggests comparable efficacy despite their distinct modalities and objectives. Potential underlying mechanisms may include increased muscle strength, enhanced neuromuscular coordination, or improved motor control, warranting further investigation. In particular, an 8-week hip abductor strengthening program resulted in a significant increase in 2MWT distance among individuals with limb amputation.

[29]. This may be explained by the critical role of hip abductor strength and endurance in maintaining stability and balance, especially in individuals with lower limb loss. Targeted strengthening of these muscles appears to be a key strategy for enhancing walking performance. In contrast, no significant improvements in 2MWT were observed following upper-limb ergometer training [29]. Although this modality did not directly enhance lower-limb endurance, it may still confer benefits by improving upper-limb strength and cardiovascular fitness—outcomes that are not fully captured by the 2MWT. Thus, exercise selection should consider both targeted and systemic benefits, depending on the individual’s rehabilitation goals.

The TUG test is a widely used assessment tool for evaluating mobility and balance in older adults and individuals with disabilities, including individuals with limb amputation [41]. For individuals with limb loss, mobility and balance are critical components influencing both quality of life and the ability to perform daily activities. One study reported significant within-group improvements in TUG performance in a dual-task training group (p < 0.05); however, the between-group difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.161) [32]. In another study comparing NMES with a traditional military amputation rehabilitation program, no significant difference in TUG scores was observed at the 6-week mark, but a statistically significant improvement emerged after 12 weeks of intervention (p < 0.05) [33]. Furthermore, a study focusing on hip abductor strengthening showed that an 8-week intervention significantly reduced TUG completion time (p < 0.01), whereas upper-limb ergometer training did not lead to any notable changes. No significant between-group differences in TUG performance were observed (p = 0.358) [29].These findings suggest that although exercise interventions generally improve TUG performance in individuals with limb amputation, the specific effects may vary depending on the type and duration of training. While some interventions may not yield significant improvements in TUG scores, they could potentially benefit other aspects of physical function not captured by this single measure. The observed heterogeneity among studies further underscores the need for future research to clarify optimal intervention modalities and durations to enhance functional outcomes in amputee populations.

Effects of exercise on quality of life in amputees

The LCI is a widely used instrument designed to assess mobility and prosthetic use individuals with lower-limb amputation, and it serves as a proxy measure for quality of life in this population [42]. Although exercise has been shown to positively impact various aspects of function in individuals with limb amputation, our findings indicated no statistically significant improvement in LCI scores following exercise interventions. This result is consistent with prior research by Bouzas et al.[43]. As a self-reported outcome measure [44], LCI is inherently subject to individual perception and may be influenced by factors such as psychological status, self-efficacy biases, interpretation of questionnaire items, and response sincerity. These factors may partly explain the absence of significant improvements in LCI despite functional gains. Moreover, it is possible that objective improvements in physical performance following exercise may not be fully captured by the LCI. This instrument emphasizes basic daily living activities and may lack sensitivity to detect changes in more advanced or complex motor abilities. In this context, while exercise may enhance specific aspects of mobility or physical function, such improvements might not be reflected in LCI scores. Another consideration is the duration of intervention: exercise programs may require longer periods to yield detectable changes in self-reported functional outcomes such as the LCI, necessitating longitudinal research to better characterize this relationship. Notably, PNF training was found to be more effective than conventional prosthetic training in improving LCI scores [30]. This suggests that targeted and specialized exercise modalities may have a greater potential to enhance functional mobility in individuals with limb amputation. Future research should therefore focus on optimizing exercise type, intensity, frequency, and duration to maximize quality-of-life outcomes in this population. Additional reasons for the lack of improvement in LCI scores may include the insufficient specificity of the exercise interventions and the individual variability in response to rehabilitation.

The SF-36 Bodily Pain subscale and the VAS offer valuable insight into the impact of pain on individuals with limb loss. The SF-36 subscale includes two items assessing pain intensity and the extent to which pain interferes with daily activities, thus providing a functional perspective on pain-related quality of life. In contrast, the VAS offers a more direct and intuitive assessment, requiring patients to mark their perceived pain level on a continuum from “no pain” to “worst imaginable pain.”This systematic review examined various interventions—including phantom limb training, mirror therapy, health education, and specific exercise programs—for their effectiveness in alleviating pain among individuals with limb amputation. When mirror therapy was added to phantom limb training, the combined approach demonstrated significantly greater pain reduction at 4 weeks compared to mirror therapy alone [35]. The findings suggest that mirror therapy may offer stronger short-term analgesic effects, though its long-term efficacy requires further investigation. Among the three studies utilizing the VAS, back health education combined with specific exercise training significantly reduced pain intensity after two weeks, outperforming home-based exercise interventions [31]. Additionally, both mirror therapy and TENS were found to reduce pain intensity within just 4 days, although no significant difference was found between the two approaches [36]. When phantom limb training was combined with mirror therapy, the synergistic effect yielded significantly greater pain reduction after 4 weeks compared to mirror therapy alone [35] These results underscore the time-dependent variability in intervention efficacy. While most modalities demonstrated some degree of analgesic benefit, certain approaches—particularly mirror therapy—may exert more pronounced effects in the short term. However, the duration of intervention appears to influence outcomes, and the optimal time course for each treatment remains to be established. Moreover, combining interventions may produce additive or synergistic benefits. These insights offer important guidance for clinical decision-making and future research aimed at optimizing pain management strategies for individuals with limb amputation and enhancing their overall quality of life.

The ABC scores is widely used to assess participants’ confidence in maintaining balance during specific tasks. This self-reported measure is a key indicator of rehabilitation progress and has been closely associated with functional recovery and quality of life among individuals with limb loss. In a 12-week comprehensive exercise intervention—including strength, balance, gait endurance, flexibility, and cardiovascular training—no statistically significant improvement in balance confidence was observed compared to the control group that did not receive targeted training [37]. This suggests that general physical conditioning may not be sufficient to enhance balance confidence in individuals with limb amputation. Rather, more precise and task-specific training strategies may be necessary. This notion is further supported by two additional studies. In one, an 8-week hip abductor strength training program led to significant improvements in ABC scores[29]. Similarly, a 4-week Wii.n.Walk intervention showed a positive trend in balance confidence, although the change did not reach statistical significance [25]. This lack of significance may be partially explained by a ceiling effect in ABC scores, whereby baseline values were already close to the maximum possible, limiting the scale’s sensitivity to detect further improvements. These findings collectively highlight the critical importance of tailored, goal-directed interventions in improving balance confidence among individuals with lower-limb amputation. Nevertheless, the small sample sizes, heterogeneity in participant characteristics (e.g., type of amputation), intervention protocols, and outcome assessment timings across studies warrant caution when interpreting these results. Future studies with larger cohorts and standardized methodologies are needed to confirm these preliminary findings and guide clinical practice.

Due to the lack of consistency in QoL measurement tools, we were unable to pool QoL data for a meta-analysis. The variability in the tools used to assess QoL makes it difficult to compare and synthesize results across studies. This is a key finding of our review, highlighting the lack of standardization in how QoL is measured in this population. While there is a clear consensus on measuring physical performance outcomes, such as gait speed and endurance, QoL lacks a unified and standardized approach. This disconnect between physical performance measures and QoL suggests a significant gap in the field. In future research, it is essential to prioritize the development and adoption of a core outcome set for QoL in limb loss rehabilitation research. Standardizing QoL measurement will improve the consistency of outcomes and allow for more meaningful comparisons across studies, ultimately advancing the field of limb loss rehabilitation.

Clinical relevance and practical exercise prescription guidelines

The findings of this study demonstrate that exercise interventions have a clinically significant impact on individuals with lower-limb amputation. Improvements in physical performance, including gait speed, endurance, and muscle strength, directly contribute to enhanced functional independence, better prosthetic use, and overall QoL. For clinical rehabilitation, these results highlight the importance of incorporating exercise as a core component in rehabilitation programs to facilitate mobility, reduce dependency, and promote psychological well-being. We recommend that clinical practitioners emphasize psychological support throughout rehabilitation, addressing both physical and emotional challenges faced by individuals with limb amputation. Additionally, machine learning can be utilized to personalize rehabilitation plans, predict recovery, and optimize treatment, improving both physical and psychological outcomes [45].

The heterogeneity observed in the studies included in this review may stem from variations in exercise modalities, participant characteristics (e.g., age, comorbidities, type of amputation), and intervention protocols (e.g., duration, intensity, frequency). These factors may have contributed to variability in outcomes, but nonetheless, the findings from this review represent a solid step forward in understanding the role of exercise in rehabilitation for individuals with lower-limb amputation. Based on our findings, we recommend the following exercise prescription guidelines for clinical practice. Exercise Types: A combination of aerobic training (e.g., walking, cycling) and muscular strength training (focusing on major muscle groups such as hip abductors and knee extensors) should be prioritized. Frequency and Duration: 3–5 sessions per week, with each session lasting 30–60 min, depending on the patient’s progress and rehabilitation stage. Intensity: Exercise intensity should be moderate (50–70% of maximal heart rate) to ensure both cardiovascular benefits and muscle strengthening while avoiding overexertion. These guidelines should be individualized based on the patient’s functional capacity and rehabilitation goals.

Limitations of the study

This review has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the number of included studies, particularly those eligible for meta-analysis, was limited, reducing the statistical power and generalizability of the results. Second, most participants were adult males with unilateral lower-limb amputations caused by trauma, which limits the applicability of the conclusions to other populations such as women, elderly individuals, or those with bilateral or vascular-related amputations. Third, the heterogeneity in participants’ baseline functional status and in the types, intensities, and durations of exercise interventions contributed to variability in outcomes, making it difficult to isolate specific intervention effects. Fourth, many studies lacked sufficient detail on intervention protocols, including progression criteria and individualization strategies, limiting reproducibility and practical guidance. Fifth, although we extracted information on exercise frequency, duration, and session time from the included studies, the heterogeneity in intervention protocols and outcome measures precluded a formal dose–response analysis. As such, our ability to make specific evidence-based recommendations regarding optimal exercise dosage was limited. Future trials should aim to standardize and report dosage parameters to enable clearer interpretation and comparison. Lastly, this review included only English-language publications and did not consider gray literature, which may have introduced publication bias and limited the comprehensiveness of the evidence base. The exclusion of non-English studies and gray literature may have limited the comprehensiveness of the evidence base, as studies published in other languages or in non-peer-reviewed formats could have provided additional insights or alternative findings. In addition, several of the included studies relied on self-reported measures, which may be subject to reporting biases, including social desirability or recall bias, and could have affected the accuracy of outcome reporting.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis explored the effects of exercise interventions on individuals with lower-limb amputations, revealing significant improvements in cardiopulmonary function, muscle strength, flexibility, and gait speed. These findings underscore the potential of exercise-based rehabilitation to enhance physiological outcomes in individuals with limb amputation and offer valuable guidance for future training programs. However, the impact of exercise on quality of life remains inconclusive, potentially due to limitations in outcome measurement (e.g., LCI), small sample sizes, and varied intervention durations. Moreover, discrepancies in outcomes across different anatomical regions suggest that individualized and goal-specific exercise prescriptions are essential. Despite these promising results, there is still no consensus on the most effective training protocols or how to tailor interventions for diverse subgroups of individuals with limb amputation (e.g., by age, sex, amputation cause, or level). High-quality, large-scale, multicenter randomized controlled trials are warranted to provide more robust evidence, enhance generalizability, and clarify optimal exercise strategies to meet the specific needs of different individuals with limb amputation. Future research should focus on standardizing exercise protocols, exploring long-term effects, and addressing the diverse needs of subgroups within the limb amputation population.
