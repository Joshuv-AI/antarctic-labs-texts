Effects of Secondary Amine and Molecular Weight on the Biological Activities of Cationic Amphipathic Antimicrobial Macromolecules
Authors: Zeyu Shao, Hao Luo, Thi Hanh Quyen Nguyen, Edgar H H Wong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCationic amphipathic antimicrobial agents inspired by antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) have shown potential in combating multidrug-resistant bacteria because of minimal resistance development. Here, this study focuses on the development of novel cationic amphipathic macromolecules in the form of dendrons and polymers with different molecular weights that employ secondary amine piperidine derivative as the cationic moiety. Generally, secondary amine compounds, especially at low molecular weights, have stronger bacteriostatic, bactericidal, and inner membrane disruption abilities than those of their primary amine counterparts. Low molecular weight D2 dendrons with two cationic centers and one hydrophobic dodecyl chain produce outstanding synergistic activity with the antibiotic rifampicin against Escherichia coli, where one-eighth of the standalone dose of D2 dendrons could reduce the concentration of rifampicin required by up to 4000-fold. The low molecular weight compounds are also less toxic and therefore have higher therapeutic index values compared to compounds with larger molecular weights. This study thus reveals key information that may inform the design of future synthetic AMPs and mimics, specifically, the design of low-molecular-weight compounds with secondary amine as the cationic center to achieve high antimicrobial potency and biocompatibility.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) represents one of the most pressing global healthcare challenges of our time. A comparison between the World Health Organization’s latest 2024 Bacterial Priority Pathogens List and its initial report in 2017 reveals a concerning stagnation in the development of antibiotics against multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains.1,2 Notably, most of the nine new antibiotics developed for “critical” pathogens have already encountered resistance.3,4 Despite the urgency, two of the three pathogens classified as “critical” in 2017 remain in the same category seven years later, with only one strain downgraded to the “high” priority group. This persistent threat underscores the critical need for accelerated efforts in antimicrobial research and development.5

One effective strategy for combating AMR involves the use of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) and mimics thereof (namely, antimicrobial polymers) as they are less likely to induce resistance development in bacteria because of their ability to exert multimodal mechanisms, including the notable membrane disruption pathway.6−8 The disruption of bacterial cell membranes generally relies on the cationic character and global amphipathicity of these molecules where the cationic groups will initially bind to the negatively charged surface of bacteria cell membranes via electrostatic interactions and/or hydrogen bonding, followed by the insertion of the hydrophobic regions into the membrane, thereby causing perturbation and ultimately resulting in cell death.9,10 Although AMPs and antimicrobial polymers have proven to be highly effective in combating MDR bacteria, their potential applications in clinical settings are often marred by toxicity/selectivity issues given that the cationic moieties, which are necessary for establishing interaction with bacteria, could also bind indiscriminately with mammalian cells.11,12 Thus, great efforts have been made to investigate and understand the structure–activity relationship of this class of compounds (or to employ complementary strategies) to make them more selective by either increasing their antimicrobial potency and/or reducing toxicity (i.e., improving biocompatibility).13−17

For instance, besides the traditional optimization of hydrophobic balance, the incorporation of neutral hydrophilic monomers such as poly(ethylene glycol) was shown to “dilute” the net positive charge of antimicrobial polymers, which led to enhancement in biocompatibility and reduced protein fouling in biological media.18−23 In addition, the manipulation of architecture and topology to generate complex molecular structures such as dendritic,24−26 branched,27,28 star-shaped,29−31 bottlebrush,32,33 and multiblock macromolecules34,35 has been shown as an effective way to increase multivalency and/or alter the presentation of functional groups, which in turn affects the overall biological properties. The toxicity of such membrane-active antimicrobial agents could also be reduced in synergistic combination therapy with traditional antibiotics since lower doses are required to produce the same antimicrobial effect as a standalone agent.36−40 This approach not only reduces toxicity but also has the added advantage of reviving the “lost” activity of antibiotics against MDR bacteria. In addition, we recently demonstrated the prodrug method where the cationic amines of antimicrobial agents are specifically masked (hence less toxic), only to undergo uncaging immolative reactions in response to specific triggers such as light or enzyme to confer antimicrobial activity.41,42 This specific approach, termed the amine uncaging strategy (AUS), substantially improved the overall biocompatibility of cationic amphipathic antimicrobial agents while potentially enabling the selective and precise targeting of bacteria.

Despite the different approaches, a common theme exists in almost all the above-mentioned examples (and general literature) in that the cationic character of the antimicrobial agents originated from primary amine or guanidine, which is not surprising since most AMPs consist of lysine and arginine amino acids in their chemical structures. Although there are several examples in the literature that looked at different cationic centers in AMP mimics apart from primary amine and guanidine, such as tertiary amine, quaternary ammonium, phosphonium and sulfonium groups, only few reports can be found for secondary amine.43−53 In fact, to the best of our knowledge, heterocyclic secondary amines such as piperidine derivatives have yet to be investigated in antimicrobial macromolecules despite their ubiquitous application as synthetic building blocks for drug design in medicine.54−56

Therefore, the aim of this study is to investigate the biological activities of cationic amphipathic antimicrobial dendrons and polymers of varying molecular weights (i.e., different numbers of cationic centers per molecule) containing secondary amine piperidine derivative, in comparison to analogs made of primary amine (Figure 1). In particular, this study explores (i) the antimicrobial activities against several Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria including MDR strains, (ii) the propensity to perturb bacteria cell membrane, (iii) the capability to synergize with a chosen antibiotic, rifampicin, and (iv) the hemolytic property and mammalian cell toxicity. It is anticipated that the key findings from this study will help inform the design of future AMP mimics.

Experimental Section

Materials

4-Pentynoic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, 95%), 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDCI) (Chem-Supply, 98%), dodecylamine (Sigma-Aldrich, 98%), hexylamine (Sigma-Aldrich, 99%), Nα,ε-bis-Boc-l-lysine N-hydroxysuccinimide ester (Chem-Inpex International, 99%), hydrogen chloride solution (Sigma-Aldrich, 4 M in 1,4-dioxane), 5-hexynoic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, 97%), 3-bromopropylamine hydrobromide (Sigma-Aldrich, 98%), di-tert-butyl dicarbonate (Sigma-Aldrich, ≥99%), sodium azide (Sigma-Aldrich, ≥99%), 4-bromopiperidine hydrobromide (Sigma-Aldrich, 98%), basic alumina (Al2O3) (LabChem), piperazine (Sigma-Aldrich, 99%), acrylic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, 99%), copper(I) iodide (Sigma-Aldrich, 98%), N,N,N′,N″,N″-pentamethyldiethylenetriamine (PMDETA) (Sigma-Aldrich, 99%), N-[2-(Boc-amino)ethyl]acrylamide (Accela ChemBio, ≥95%), N-butylacrylamide (Ambeed, 98%), 2-(butylthiocarbonothioylthio)propanoic acid (Boron Molecular, 97%), 2,2′-azobis(2-methylpropionitrile) (AIBN) solution (Sigma-Aldrich, 12 wt % in acetone), polymyxin B solution (Sigma-Aldrich, 20 mg/mL in water), melittin from honey bee venom (Sigma-Aldrich, ≥85%), propidium iodide (Sigma-Aldrich, ≥ 94%), sodium deoxycholate (Sigma-Aldrich, ≥97%), and rifampicin (Sigma-Aldrich, ≥95%) were used as received. Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), sodium chloride (NaCl), sodium sulfate (Na2SO4), magnesium sulfate (MgSO4), sodium hydrogen carbonate (NaHCO3), hydrochloric acid 32%, triethylamine, 1,4-dioxane, dimethylformamide, methanol, chloroform, ethyl acetate, hexane, and diethyl ether were obtained from Chem-Supply. Milli-Q water with a resistivity of >18 MΩ·cm was obtained from an in-line Millipore RiOs/Origin water purification system.

Chemical Characterizations

All 1H and 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopies were performed by a Bruker 300 or 400 MHz spectrometer using deuterated solvents (obtained from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories) as reference solvents and at a sample concentration of approximately 10–20 mg mL–1.

Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) was carried out on a Shimadzu liquid chromatography system equipped with an SIL-20A autoinjector, a Polymer Laboratories 5.0 μm bead-size guard column (50 × 7.5 mm2) followed by three linear PL (Styragel) columns (105, 104, and 103 Å) and an RID-10A differential refractive-index (RI) detector operating at 50 °C. Dimethylacetamide (containing 0.03% w/v LiBr and 0.05% w/v 2,6-dibutyl-4-methylphenol) was used as the eluent at a flow rate of 1 mL min–1. The system was calibrated with poly(methyl methacrylate) standards with molecular weights from 200 to 106 g mol–1.

ζ-potential measurements were performed via a Malvern Zetasizer Ultra-Red instrument (Malvern Panalytical). Compounds (512 μg mL–1) were analyzed at 25 °C in deionized water, where the sample solution was filtered through a 0.45 μm pore size filter and pipetted into a folded capillary cell (DTS1070, Malvern Panalytical).

Synthesis of Cationic Amphipathic Single Molecule Dendrons

The synthesis of the cationic amphipathic single molecules involved two key steps, namely, the click reaction between the azide-functionalized Boc-protected amines (6 or 8) with the alkyne scaffolds (1, 1*, or 4) to yield the Boc-protected precursors, followed by a final Boc deprotection step (Scheme S6, Supporting Information). In general, the click and deprotection steps each followed a very similar standard protocol irrespective of the final compound made. The general protocols for these two steps are outlined below.

For the click reaction, the azide 6 or 8 (1 mol equiv and usually at 1 mmol basis) and the alkyne 1, 1*, or 4 (1 mol equiv ) were solubilized in dimethylformamide (2 mL per 1 mmol of azide). Copper(I) iodide (1 mol equiv) was then added into the flask, and the contents were degassed with N2 for 10 min. Next, PMDETA (1 mol equiv) was added into the flask under N2 atmosphere, and the contents were degassed with N2 for another 10 min. The reaction mixture was stirred at 25 °C for 1 h under a N2 atmosphere. Afterward, chloroform (∼25 mL) was added into the flask and the combined organic phase was washed against 0.02 M EDTA solution (2 × 20 mL) and water (1 × 20 mL). The organic phase was subsequently mixed with MgSO4 and basic alumina, filtered, and dried in vacuo to yield a wet solid that contained trace amounts of DMF. The high boiling point solvent was removed from the wet solid by mixing with diethyl ether (25 mL), followed by centrifugation and decanting steps to discard the filtrate. The recovered solids were then dried in vacuo to yield the Boc-protected compounds as pale-yellow solids (typically 40–60 mol % yield). Synthesis of Cationic Amphipathic Polymers

The synthesis of the cationic amphipathic polymers was achieved using the RAFT polymerization technique, where Boc-protected amino monomers were copolymerized with N-butylacrylamide in a fixed 4:6 molar ratio. The RAFT-derived Boc-protected polymers were then deprotected to yield the amphipathic amine polymers. Both the RAFT polymerization and Boc removal steps followed the same standard protocols.

For the RAFT polymerization, the RAFT agent, 2-(butylthiocarbonothioylthio)propanoic acid (1 mol equiv), Boc-protected monomer N-[2-(Boc-amino)ethyl]acrylamide or 9 (4, 16, or 40 mol equiv), N-butylacrylamide (6, 24, or 60 mol equiv), and 2,2′-azobis(2-methylpropionitrile) (0.25 mol eq ) were mixed in 1,4-dioxane such that the total monomer concentration is 1 M. The total mass of compounds in each reaction vial was approximately 200–400 mg to ensure enough materials are produced for biotesting. The solution was degassed by bubbling it with N2 for 15 min. The reaction mixture was then stirred at 70 °C for 20 h (to achieve near full monomer conversion) under a N2 atmosphere before cooling in an ice bath. The mixture was diluted with acetone (approximately 2–4 mL) and precipitated into hexane (∼10× the amount of solvent in the polymerized sample). The precipitate was isolated by centrifugation, redissolved in acetone, and precipitated once more, followed by drying in vacuo to yield the Boc-protected polymers P4-PriBoc, P16-PriBoc, P40-PriBoc, P4-SecBoc, P16-SecBoc, and P40-SecBoc (typically 80–95 mol % yield).

The Boc-protected polymers were deprotected under similar conditions to the above-mentioned for the dendron molecules.

Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) Determination

The MICs of synthetic dendrons and polymers were determined by the broth microdilution method against two standard pathogens (Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853 and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213) and two MDR isolates (PA31 and SA60)1 according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. This is used to evaluate the bacteriostatic efficacy of the compounds. Briefly, a single bacterial colony was cultured in 10 mL of Mueller–Hinton broth (MHB) at 37 °C with 180 rpm shaking overnight. Subsequently, a subculture was prepared from the overnight culture by diluting 1:100 in 10 mL of MHB and allowed to grow to mid log phase, then diluted to the appropriate concentration for the MIC test. A 2-fold dilution series of 100 μL of compounds solution in PBS were added into 96-well microplates (Costar, Corning), followed by the addition of 100 μL of the subculture suspension. The final concentration of bacteria in each well was approximately 5 × 105 cells mL–1. After the plate was incubated at 37 °C for 20 h, the absorbance at 600 nm was measured using a microtiter plate reader (FLUOstar Omega, BMG Labtech). MIC values were defined as the lowest concentration of sample that showed no visible bacteria growth and inhibited more than 90% bacteria growth. Positive controls without compounds and negative controls without bacteria were included. All assays included duplicates and were repeated in at least three independent experiments.

Planktonic Bacteria Killing Study

The bactericidal efficacy of synthetic dendrons and polymers was evaluated against the MDR strain PA31 or SA60 in PBS. The bacterial suspension was prepared in the same manner as with the MIC assay, albeit using PBS as the dilution medium in lieu of MHB. Gradient concentrations of each compound were selected and incubated with bacteria suspension for 1 h. The viability of planktonic cells was then determined by a drop plate method, where the planktonic cells were serially diluted in sterile PBS and plated onto Luria–Bertani (LB) agar. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, bacteria colonies were counted, and colony forming unit (CFU) analysis was performed. All assays included two replicates and were repeated in at least three independent experiments.

Inner Membrane Permeability Study

An inner membrane disruption kinetic assay with temporal resolution was applied based on the propidium iodide (PI) staining method.2 To initiate the assay, a concentrated bacteria suspension of PA31 or SA60 in PBS (approximately 4 × 108 cells mL–1) was prepared via centrifugation (2000g, 10 min) and subsequent resuspension. The compounds at five different concentrations (final concentrations of 0.25 × MIC, 0.5 × MIC, 1 × MIC, 2 × MIC, and 4 × MIC) were then loaded on a flat-bottom, black cell-culture-grade 96-well plate (Costar, Corning), followed by the addition of PI (5 μmol, Sigma-Aldrich) and bacteria suspension. The fluorescence intensity was monitored kinetically for 1 h using a microtiter plate reader (FLUOstar Omega, BMG Labtech) with excitation and emission wavelengths of 544 nm and 620 nm, respectively. Negative controls without compounds were included. The fluorescence intensity was normalized by the ratio to the negative control. Data sets were presented as heat maps with temporal and concentration resolutions using MATLAB. All assays included duplicates and were repeated in at least two independent experiments.

Outer Membrane Permeability Study

The outer membrane permeability of the compounds was examined using a similar protocol to our previous study.2 Briefly, a concentrated bacteria suspension (P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853, approximately 1 × 109 cells mL–1) was prepared from an overnight culture from a single colony, where the cells were spun down via centrifugation (3800 rpm, 10 min), washed, and resuspended in PBS. The prepared cells were subjected to 10 min of incubation (37 °C, 180 rpm) with synthetic compounds at their respective 1 × MIC concentration. The cells were then collected via centrifugation (3800 rpm, 10 min) and resuspended in PBS containing 0.5% sodium deoxycholate. The suspension was further incubated for 10 min at 37 °C and 180 rpm. Checkerboard Assay

The checkerboard assay between synthetic compounds and commercial antibiotic rifampicin was performed in 96-well microplates (Costar, Corning) against Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 in MHB. Concentration gradients of polymers or dendrons and rifampicin were prepared in vertical and horizontal directions, respectively. Bacterial suspensions were prepared in the same manner as the MIC test and added to the plates. Positive and negative controls, without antimicrobial agents and bacteria, respectively, were also included. Mammalian Cell Viability Assay

The mammalian cell viability study was determined by PrestoBlue (Thermo Fisher Scientific) assay on murine embryonic fibroblasts (MEF) CF-1 (ATCC SCRC-1040), which was kindly provided by the Cell Culture Facility of the Mark Wainwright Analytical Centre at UNSW. Results and Discussion

Design Rationale, Synthesis, and Characterization

In general, the dendrons and polymers were made to contain only cationic and hydrophobic groups without the addition of a “third” neutral hydrophilic monomer like we normally do for simplicity. Moreover, simple linear alkane structures were chosen to represent the hydrophobic group, as these were sufficient to impart activity. Most importantly, we have set the molar ratio between the number of cationic groups and the total number of carbon atoms in the hydrophobic alkyl moiety as 2:12 to give the most optimal balance between antimicrobial activity and biocompatibility based on our previous experience in designing antimicrobial polymers. Hence, for the lowest molecular weight entries in this study, which are dendrons D1-Pri and D1-Sec that contain only one cationic center per molecule, the hydrophobic moiety is simply a n-hexyl (C6) chain. Correspondingly, for the next highest molecular weight compounds, dendrons D2-Pri and D2-Sec that contain two cationic centers, a n-dodecyl (C12) chain was used. Meanwhile for the polymers, regardless of the different targeted degree of polymerizations (10, 40, and 100), the molar ratio of cationic acrylamide monomers to the hydrophobic n-butyl acrylamide (C4) monomer was all fixed at 4:6 (such that the ratio of cationic centers to the total number of carbon atoms in the hydrophobic tails was 4:24). It is worth noting that all the dendrons and polymers are designated with D and P symbols, respectively, whereas the numbering next to them indicates the number of cationic centers per molecule, while the designation Pri or Sec simply signifies if the compound contains primary or secondary amine, respectively. It is also important to note that given our interest in investigating the effects of different number of cationic centers per molecule and the challenges in reproducibly synthesizing well-defined oligomers (<5 monomer units), the low molecular weight adducts here (i.e., dendrons) were synthesized in a different manner than the polymers.

In essence, the amphipathic dendrons were synthesized using copper-catalyzed azide–alkyne cycloaddition click chemistry57,58 between alkyne-functionalized hydrophobic scaffolds (1 or 4; see Supporting Information) with azide-functionalized protected amine precursors (6 and 8 for primary and secondary amine, respectively (Supporting Information)), followed by the removal of tert-butyloxycarbonyl (Boc) protecting groups under acidic conditions. On the other hand, all the statistical copolymers were synthesized via reversible addition–fragmentation chain transfer (RAFT) polymerization59,60 of n-butyl acrylamide and Boc-protected amino acrylamide monomers where the reactions were taken to near full monomer conversion. In a similar fashion, the Boc protecting groups on the polymers were subsequently removed using hydrochloric acid solution to produce the final antimicrobial polymers.

The chemical structures of all of the compounds, including the precursors, were confirmed by 1H and 13C NMR analysis (Supporting Information, Figures S1–S48). For example, the successful click reaction in making the dendrons was confirmed by the appearance of triazolyl hydrogens at δH = 7.7–7.9 ppm. In addition, the quantitative removal of Boc groups to yield all final compounds was verified by the disappearance of the methyl protons at δH = 1.3–1.5 ppm. GPC analysis (using dimethylacetamide as the eluent) was also performed on the Boc-protected P4, P16, and P40 polymers and D2 dendrons to check the molecular weight distribution (Figure 2). As expected, both dendrons D2-PriBoc and D2-SecBoc have the narrowest distribution with a dispersity (Đ) of 1.02 given that these are unimolecular compounds (Table 1). It is worth noting that due to inherent column broadening effects, the experimental Đ value of the dendrons deviated slightly from the true value of 1. The GPC traces of the polymers showed a monomodal distribution except for the highest molecular weight species with 100 monomer repeat units (i.e., P40-PriBoc and P40-SecBoc) that have a minor shoulder on the higher molecular weight side that could be attributed to conventional bimolecular termination products. Nonetheless, all the Boc-protected polymers yield low Đ values of <1.25, thus indicating good control over the polymerization. It is noteworthy that the obtained number-averaged molecular weight (Mn) of the Boc-protected secondary amine compounds was lower compared to the primary amine counterparts according to GPC analysis, even though the opposite is supposed to be true. This could be attributed to the difference in hydrodynamic volume of the analytes during GPC analysis where the macromolecular structure of the secondary amine compounds adopted a more compact configuration by being in a less solvated state compared to the primary amine versions which adopted a more open conformation in the polar GPC eluent, dimethylacetamide. The GPC analysis perhaps suggests that the heterocyclic secondary amine structure is slightly more hydrophobic than the linear primary amine. After the removal of the Boc groups, the ζ potential was measured to compare the overall cationic character of the compounds (Table 1). Two interesting general trends were apparent: (i) the molecules became more cationic (i.e., larger ζ values) with increasing molecular weights for both primary and secondary amine series, and (ii) for compounds with more than one cationic center per molecule, the secondary amine counterparts have higher net positive charge than the primary amines. The increase in ζ with higher molecular weights is not surprising and could be attributed to the multivalency effect as molecules with multiple cationic centers tend to collectively exhibit stronger cationic character,61 whereas the observed overall larger ζ values for secondary amines compared to primary amines suggests that the piperidine derivative is more basic than primary amine, which made sense since the extra -CH2- group in the piperidine ring creates a stronger electron-donating inductive effect and hence stabilizes the conjugate acid better.

Antimicrobial Activity

The antimicrobial potency of the dendrons and polymers was evaluated by first determining their bacteriostatic activity in vitro via the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) test, using AMP melittin as the reference compound (Table 2). The MIC is defined as the minimum drug concentration that inhibits bacterial growth by ≥90%. Several model Gram-negative (Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Escherichia coli) and Gram-postive (Staphylococcus aureus) bacteria obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) were selected for this study. Additionally, MDR isolates of Gram-negative (PA31, isolate from microbial keratitis) and Gram-positive (SA60, isolate from conjunctivitis) bacteria were included in the screening.62 In general, all primary and secondary amine compounds with at least two cationic centers per molecule (i.e., D2, P4, P16, and P40) demonstrated similar (±2-fold) MIC values to melittin against both ATCC and MDR P. aeruginosa strains. This outcome was consistent with our previous antimicrobial polymer screening studies,18,34,63−65 which reported similar MIC values (∼32 μg mL–1) against P. aeruginosa. For D1-Pri and D1-Sec that contained only one cationic amine per molecule and a hydrophobic hexyl tail; however, no antimicrobial activity was observed even at the highest tested concentration of 256 μg mL–1 against P. aeruginosa (and S. aureus). This shows that for monocationic agents, the 1:6 molar ratio of cationic groups to the number of carbon atoms in the hydrophobic alkyl moiety is insufficient to impart antimicrobial activity. The lack of activity shown by D1 dendrons prompted us to make and assess alternative monocationic D1* versions, where the molar ratio of the cationic group to the number of carbon atoms in the hydrophobic chain was set at 1:12 instead of 1:6. With increased hydrophobicity, the D1* dendrons exhibited comparable activities to the other nonmonovalent cationic compounds and melittin against P. aeruginosa. Since D1 dendrons were inactive, further biological tests were only performed on D1* dendrons and not the former. A similar pattern was observed for the compounds against E. coli, though peculiarly the D2 dendrons exhibited low activity (MIC = 128 and 256 μg mL–1 for D2-Pri and D2-Sec, respectively).

In terms of the bacteriostatic activity against Gram-positive S. aureus, the compounds in general showed declining activity with an increasing molecular weight. This could be attributed to the sieving effect, as the highly cross-linked and hydrophilic peptidoglycan mesh found in Gram-positive bacteria may prevent larger amphipathic agents from traversing across this layer to induce antimicrobial activity, as noted in other reports.32,66 Although only noticeable for low molecular weight species, D1*-Sec has potent activity (MIC = 32 μg mL–1) against S. aureus whereas D1*-Pri was impotent. The bacteriostatic study all in all hints that the secondary amine enables AMP mimics to have stronger bacteria growth inhibition than the primary amine.

Next, the antimicrobial activity was further investigated by determining the bactericidal activity of the compounds via a colony-forming unit (CFU) analysis. The MDR pathogen P. aeruginosa PA31 was selected as the main target isolate since the compounds showed similar bacteriostatic effects against it. For each compound, multiple concentrations were used to measure the reduction of viable planktonic bacteria cells after 1 h treatment in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.4. This assay began with approximately 5 × 105 CFU mL–1 bacterial loading, and the bactericidal concentration was defined as having ≥3-log10 reduction in CFU mL–1 (i.e., 99.9% killing efficiency). This assay is within the recommended Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines. As shown in Figure 3, most primary and secondary amine compounds exhibited a significant bactericidal effect compared to that of the untreated control group. However, an exception was observed for D1*-Pri. Despite being bacteriostatic, D1*-Pri did not show any noticeable reduction in bacterial cell viability even at high concentrations (2 and 4 × MIC). In contrast, D1*-Sec, which features a secondary amine center, demonstrated bactericidal activity against PA31 at 1 × MIC. The few compounds that were bacteriostatic toward S. aureus (i.e., D1*-Sec, D2, and P4) were also assessed for their bactericidal activity against MDR S. aureus SA60 (Supporting Information, Figure S49). Apart from D2 dendrons where the bactericidal activity between primary and secondary amine compounds was similar, the secondary amine analogs of D1* and P4 were evidently stronger than the primary amine ones. D1*-Pri was not even bacteriostatic against S. aureus whereas P4-Sec could achieve 99.9% killing efficiency at 0.125 × MIC compared to P4-Pri that needed 1 × MIC. These findings suggest that the secondary amine structure may endow AMP mimics with stronger bactericidal potency compared to primary amine, particularly in low molecular weight compounds and oligomers.

The effect of molecular weight (i.e., number of cationic groups per molecule) on bactericidal activity against P. aeruginosa PA31 was clear, as surmised in Table 3. The concentration (MIC fold) required to result in ≥3-log10 reduction in viable bacteria cells clearly decreased with increasing number of cationic centers per molecule. This observation reaffirms the general notion that multivalency plays a significant role in increasing antimicrobial potency against Gram-negative bacteria. It is noteworthy that the bactericidal concentration values of the compounds were generally lower than their MIC values because the assay was performed for 1 h in PBS. Unlike the culture medium Mueller–Hinton broth used for MIC determination, PBS does not contain proteins that will promote bacteria cells growth or form undesirable complexes with cationic compounds and thus is a good medium to directly determine the bactericidal activity of a compound at short time scales (≤1 h) with minimal interference.

Antimicrobial Mechanism

Subsequently, the antimicrobial mechanism of the compounds was investigated. Given the wide acceptance that amphipathic antimicrobials disrupt membrane integrity, the ability of the compounds to permeate the inner membrane of MDR pathogen P. aeruginosa PA31 was assessed using a propidium iodide (PI) assay. PI, a red fluorescent nucleic acid probe, can only traverse membrane barriers and bind with DNA or RNA when the cellular membrane is compromised.67,68 Thus, if the bacteria membrane is disrupted by the amphipathic antimicrobials, thereby allowing membrane-impermeant PI to pass through and bind to intracellular targets, the activation of PI and the resulting red fluorescence provide a reasonable estimation on the extent of membrane permeabilization. Heatmap plots based on PI red fluorescence intensity as a function of exposure time to different compound concentrations are shown in Figure 4. The primary amine small molecule D1*-Pri did not cause inner membrane disruption, which coincided with its inability to exert any bactericidal activity despite having a bacteriostatic potential. We have previously observed that the lack of bactericidal activity is correlated to the lack of inner membrane disruption and vice versa for amphipathic antimicrobial agents, and hence, the outcome of D1*-Pri was not unexpected. Conversely, D1*-Sec, which has the secondary amine structure, exhibited membrane disruption capabilities at 1 × MIC within 15 min of treatment. This suggests that secondary amine induces stronger membrane disturbance than primary amine especially for low molecular weight compounds.

Inner membrane disruption study against MDR P. aeruginosa PA31. The membrane permeability variation, as quantified by a dimensionless constant (the fluorescence fold change between the treatment group and the negative control group, measured at excitation and emission wavelengths of 544 and 622 nm, respectively), was illustrated as a heat map with the resolution of time and concentration based on linear regression. Melittin and polymyxin B were included as positive controls.

For polymers P4 to P40, the increase in molecular weight gradually amplifies the inner membrane disruption capability, as evidenced by the shorter time required to achieve maximum PI fluorescence. However, there is no significant difference between the primary and secondary amine polymers with the same number of cationic centers, although P4-Sec showed a slightly more rapid membrane disruption than P4-Pri. It can be postulated that as the molecular weight and number of cationic centers of amphipathic antimicrobials increase, the multivalency effect of the overall macromolecule takes precedence over the structural effect posed by different amine types. In general, all of the synthetic compounds (D2, P4, P16, and P40), which share the same 1:6 molar ratio of number of cationic groups to hydrophobic carbon atoms, displayed similar or stronger inner membrane disruption activity compared to conventional AMPs such as melittin and polymyxin B against P. aeruginosa.

Compounds D1*-Sec, D2, and P4 that showed activity toward MDR S. aureus SA60 were also analyzed for their inner membrane disruption capability against this Gram-positive pathogen (Supporting Information, Figure S50). The PI heatmap plots corroborated the results from the bactericidal study, where the secondary amine adducts again generally showed stronger membrane disruption ability than the primary amines, apart from the D2 dendrons that were indistinguishable from one another.

The outer membrane permeability of P. aeruginosa was also investigated via the lytic action of sodium deoxycholate after sensitizing with the compounds at 1 × MIC for 10 min. If a compound sensitizes the outer membrane of P. aeruginosa, cell lysis induced by the subsequent addition of the anionic surfactant sodium deoxycholate would correspond to a change in optical density compared to that of the untreated PBS control (Supporting Information, Figure S51). At least 40% cell lysis was achieved by the compounds, thus demonstrating their ability to cause outer membrane perturbation. However, both primary and secondary amine compounds displayed similar level of outer membrane sensitization. It is interesting to note that despite being nonbactericidal and showing zero inner membrane activity, D1*-Pri could sensitize the outer membrane of P. aeruginosa (and resulting in bacteriostatic activity, vide supra). This was unlike D1*-Sec that demonstrated activity across all bacteriostatic, bactericidal, inner, and outer membrane perturbation assays.

Taking into account all the bacteria challenging studies, it is clear that the secondary amine piperidine derivative reigns superior over primary alkyl amine for low molecular weight compounds in terms of antimicrobial performance (against both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria), though that superiority diminishes at larger molecular weights as the multivalency effect occupies greater importance than amine type. This stronger activity shown by the secondary amine could be due to its higher cationicity and basicity (as supported by the ζ potential data above) that leads to more efficient binding with bacteria cells and ultimately more potent antimicrobial activity compared to the primary amine.

Synergy with Antibiotic Rifampicin

The use of membrane-active amphipathic antimicrobial agents in combination with conventional antibiotics has been shown to be an efficient method to combat MDR bacteria, as lower dosages are required to achieve the same antimicrobial activity as standalone agents in synergistic combinations. Not only does this approach reduces toxicity and side effects because of the lower dosages, the “lost” activity of antibiotics against MDR bacteria could be “revived” in combination therapy with AMPs and mimics, thus allowing for the repurposing of antibiotics. Given the advantages of combination therapy, the membrane-active agents D2, P4, P16, and P40 were screened with a commercial antibiotic, rifampicin, against E. coli. The decision to screen with rifampicin was based on the encouraging results shown in a study by Si et al. that showed strong synergy between rifampicin and a glycosylated cationic antimicrobial polymer against E. coli.38 Here, the search for synergistic activity was performed via checkerboard assays using two concentration gradients of rifampicin and the synthetic compounds prepared in the horizontal and vertical directions of a 96-well plate followed by the addition of bacterial cells. To assess for synergistic points, the fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) (eq 1) was used as a usual parameter, where a FICI value of ≤0.5 indicates synergy.

Looking at the checkerboard plots in Figure 5, it is evident that only D2 dendrons and P4-Pri (all low molecular weight macromolecules) have synergistic interactions with rifampicin, whereas P4-Sec and the higher molecular weight polymers P16 and P40 did not. In fact, the synergy for D2-Pri and D2-Sec dendrons was extremely strong, with FICI values as low as 0.13 and 0.06, respectively. For example, the coadministration of the D2 dendrons at one-eighth of their individual MIC values was sufficient to reduce the dosage of rifampicin by up to 4000-fold, which represents a significant reduction in combination therapy systems. Although not as strong as D2 dendrons, P4-Pri registered a FICI value of 0.25, which was still very good compared with the reported literature. As a heavy and hydrophobic antibiotic, rifampicin is naturally discouraged from passing the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria, but in the presence of membrane-active antimicrobials, rifampicin could pass this barrier to bind with RNA polymerase at the plasma membrane more effectively and exert its activity. Nevertheless, this explanation alone is not sufficient to explain the observed powerful synergistic activities of D2 dendrons with rifampicin and suggests that other factors may be at work. Furthermore, the lack of synergy observed for the higher molecular weight polymers even though all of the compounds tested have similar overall hydrophobicity hints at the possible unique interaction between rifampicin and D2 dendrons that produced such strong synergistic effects.

Biocompatibility

The biocompatibility of the compounds was last assessed by determining the hemolytic activity and mammalian cell cytotoxicity on sheep red blood cells (RBCs) and murine embryonic fibroblast (MEF) cells, respectively. Specifically, the hemolytic concentration (HC50), defined as the compound concentration that causes 50% lysis of RBCs, and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50), defined as the compound concentration that reduces the viability of mammalian cells (in this case MEF cells) by half, were calculated based on the hemolysis and cell viability curves (Supporting Information, Figure S52). The therapeutic index (TI), also known as the selectivity index, which estimates the selectivity of an antimicrobial agent to preferentially target bacteria over mammalian cells, was also determined as the ratio of HC50 or IC50 to the MIC (i.e., TI = HC50/MIC or IC50/MIC). Essentially, the multivalency effect appears to cause more damage to mammalian cells than against bacteria cells given that P40 polymers exhibited TI values of ≤0.6. Meanwhile for D1* dendrons that have double the hydrophobic content, both primary and secondary amine versions were very toxic to MEF cells and yielded TI values of 0.7 and 0.3, respectively. Against RBCs, D1*-Sec was similarly toxic as well, registering a HC50 value of 28 μg mL–1. However, D1*-Pri demonstrated high hemocompatibility with an HC50 value of 791 μg mL–1. Bearing in mind that the bacteriostatic D1*-Pri neither causes inner membrane disruption nor exerts bactericidal activity, it can be assumed that the absence of these phenomena may well indicate excellent hemocompatibility.

Conclusions

In summary, a library of cationic amphipathic antimicrobial dendrons and polymers containing primary or secondary piperidine amines and varying molecular weights (number of cationic centers) was created and investigated for their biological properties, including their bacteriostatic and bactericidal activities against Gram-negative and Gram-positive pathogens, membrane disruption capabilities, potential for synergy with antibiotics, and biocompatibility. Generally, the synthetic antimicrobials showed good bacteriostatic activity against P. aeruginosa and to some extent against S. aureus though only for low molecular weight compounds. Importantly, the secondary piperidine amine structure conferred stronger antimicrobial potency than primary alkylamines, with the effect being most prominent at lower molecular weights. As the molecular weight increases, the multivalency effect trumps the influence offered by the different amine structures. The ability to perturb the bacteria inner membrane strongly correlated to bactericidal activity. The stronger activity endowed by the secondary amine was suspected to be derived from its higher cationic character and basicity, as suggested by the ζ potential results. When assessed for synergy with rifampicin against E. coli, the low molecular weight adducts again showed the most promising results, especially for D2 dendrons that enabled up to 4000-fold reduction in dosage from the original standalone dose of rifampicin if coadministered with one-eighth of the original standalone dose of D2. This outstanding level of synergy is rare and most likely due to some unique interactions between D2 dendrons and rifampicin. In terms of biocompatibility, the low molecular weight compounds, D2 dendrons and P4-Pri polymer, generated the best therapeutic index values, whereas higher molecular weight compounds generated higher toxicity, thus indicating that the same multivalency effect would also lead to poorer biocompatibility. The outcomes of this study suggest that to achieve high antimicrobial potency and better biocompatibility for antimicrobial peptides and mimics thereof, the use of secondary amine structures over primary alkyl amine as the cationic center should be considered and incorporated as low molecular weight antimicrobial agents with approximately less than 10 cationic groups in total (i.e., low multivalency).
