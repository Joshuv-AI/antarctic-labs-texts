Impact of Uninterrupted Versus Interrupted Oral Anticoagulation on 30-Day Outcomes Following Coronary Angiography
Authors: Ting-Yu Wang, Robin Jacob, Leah Puglisi, Curtiss T Stinis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe rising prevalence of patients undergoing coronary angiography who require oral anticoagulation (OAC) presents a challenge in periprocedural management. While current North American guidelines advise OAC interruption before nonemergent coronary angiography, the impact of continuation vs interruption on outcomes remains unclear.MethodsThis retrospective study included patients who underwent elective outpatient transradial coronary angiography from August 1, 2018, to March 20, 2023 at a tertiary medical center. The primary end point included 30-day outcomes of hematoma, pseudoaneurysm, perforation, arteriovenous fistula, compartment syndrome, and stroke or transient ischemic attack.ResultsOf the 470 patients, 225 patients (48%) continued OAC (uninterrupted group), and 245 patients (52%) had OAC stopped periprocedurally (interrupted group). There were no differences in baseline demographic characteristics and clinical characteristics. The uninterrupted group trended toward higher rates of warfarin use (17.3% vs 10.6%) and lower rates of rivaroxaban use (13.3% vs 20.4%; P = .031). The interrupted group had higher rates of conversion to the alternate radial access (6.1% vs 1.8%; P =.019) and percutaneous coronary intervention (46.5% vs 24.0%; P < .001). No significant differences were found between the interrupted and uninterrupted groups with respect to 30-day rates of hematoma (2.9% vs 4.0%; P = .495), pseudoaneurysm (0.8% vs 0%; P = .5), and stroke or transient ischemic attack (1.2% vs 0.4%; P = .625), respectively. No perforations, arteriovenous fistula, or compartment syndrome were observed at 30 days.ConclusionsIn this institutional registry of patients who underwent elective outpatient transradial coronary angiography, an uninterrupted OAC strategy was not associated with higher rates of 30-day complications.Keywords: complications, coronary angiography, outcomes, percutaneous coronary intervention, radial access

Introduction

Advancements in modern medicine have led to increased life expectancy, resulting in a growing population with both coronary artery disease (CAD) and atrial fibrillation (AF). Up to 30% of patients with AF and on oral anticoagulation (OAC) therapy will present with stable CAD that necessitates coronary angiography,1,2 and the proportion of patients on OAC therapy who have concomitant CAD is expected to rise as the population ages. Periprocedural management of OAC in this population demands meticulous consideration, balancing the risks of stroke and thromboembolism against bleeding.

The optimal periprocedural management of OAC therapy in those undergoing nonemergent coronary angiography remains unclear and has long been a point of debate. Guidelines and expert consensus differ in their recommendations, reflecting the complexities of balancing thromboembolic and bleeding risks. While the European and North American guidelines both recommend an uninterrupted strategy for those who present with acute coronary syndrome,3,4 they differ in their recommendations regarding nonemergent procedures. Specifically, the 2018 ESC expert consensus support an uninterrupted strategy in vitamin-K antagonist (VKA)-treated patients and an interrupted strategy in direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC)-treated patients,2,5 while the North American guidelines recommend an interrupted approach for all comers regardless of their anticoagulation regimen.6,7

Despite existing guideline recommendations and expert consensus, there remains a lack of robust data and large randomized controlled trials to inform physicians on periprocedural OAC management for patients undergoing elective coronary angiography, particularly those undergoing radial access in the contemporary era. Previous literature largely focused on the periprocedural management of VKA in elective coronary angiography cases utilizing a femoral access approach and have found an uninterrupted VKA strategy to be safe.8,9 While this information is valuable, elective coronary angiography has largely shifted to a predominantly radial approach due to its ease, improved patient comfort, and lower postprocedural bleeding complications compared with the femoral approach. Additionally, DOACs have replaced VKAs as the first-line choice in many clinical conditions owing to to their efficacy and excellent safety profile. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of an uninterrupted OAC strategy on postprocedural outcomes in VKA-treated and DOAC-treated patients who underwent elective outpatient transradial coronary angiography.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective cohort study assessed patients who underwent elective outpatient coronary angiography between August 1, 2018, and March 20, 2023, at a tertiary medical center. The cohort comprised patients who were scheduled to undergo a planned transradial procedure, who were on ongoing oral anticoagulation (OAC) therapy including apixaban, rivaroxaban, warfarin, dabigatran, or edoxaban (n = 470). They were divided into the uninterrupted OAC group, who continued their outpatient OAC regimen, and the interrupted OAC group, who had their OAC therapy stopped in the periprocedural period.

Outcome definitions

Baseline demographic characteristics, clinical characteristics, and procedural data were collected. We examined the rates of 30-day clinical outcomes in hematoma, pseudoaneurysm, perforation, arteriovenous fistula, compartment syndrome, and strokes or TIAs between the interrupted and uninterrupted OAC groups.

Statistical analysis

Demographic characteristics and clinical data are displayed as frequencies and percentages for categorical data and as mean ± SD for continuous data. The χ2 and Fisher exact tests were used to compare categorical data. T tests were used to compare numeric variables between the 2 groups. Statistical significance was set at .05, and all analyses were conducted in R software, v4.3.3 (R Core Team).

This study was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board at Scripps Clinic (Protocol No.: IRB-23-8277). The board has approved the use of protected health information in this study. The conditions for waiving the need to obtain individual authorization have been reviewed and determined to meet the regulations described in 45 CFR 164.512(i).

Results

Of the 470 consecutive patients who underwent elective outpatient coronary angiography via a planned radial approach, 225 patients were in the uninterrupted OAC group (48%), and 245 patients were in the interrupted OAC group (52%). No significant differences in baseline demographic characteristics and clinical data were noted between the 2 groups (Table 1). Specifically, there were no differences in the age at the time of procedure (76 years) or body mass index (28.4 vs 28.31 kg/m2), and most patients were male and White. Between the interrupted and uninterrupted OAC groups, there were no differences in the rates of cancer (15.5% vs 15.1%; P = .904), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (15.9% vs 20.4%; P = .203), diabetes mellitus (33.5% vs 33.3%; P = .975), previous coronary artery bypass graft (21.6% vs 19.6%; P = .578), obstructive sleep apnea (32.2% vs 28.0%; P = .317), peripheral arterial disease (5.7% vs 6.7%; P = .668), and stroke or transient ischemic attack (17.6% vs 22.7%; P = .166). The uninterrupted OAC group had higher rates of previous myocardial infarction (33.3% vs 25.3%), but this was not statistically significant (P = .056).

The baseline OAC regimen of the study patients is also detailed in Table 1. Patients in the uninterrupted OAC group were more likely to be on warfarin (17.3% vs 10.6%) and less likely to be on rivaroxaban (13.3% vs 20.4%; P = .031). Most of the study patients were on OAC for AF (91.8% vs 93.8% in the interrupted and uninterrupted OAC groups, respectively), and there were no differences in the reason for anticoagulation (P = .323).

Procedural data are shown in Table 2. Most patients required only a single radial access attempt to achieve successful cannulation. In the interrupted group, 243 of 245 patients underwent a radial procedure. In this group, 17 patients required more than 1 radial access attempts before successful cannulation. Of these, 15 experienced failure at the initial radial access site and subsequently underwent successful cannulation via the contralateral arm, and 2 patients, following unsuccessful radial access attempts in both arms, were converted to femoral access. In contrast, all 225 patients in the uninterrupted group underwent a radial procedure, and none required conversion to femoral access. In this cohort, 17 patients required more than 1 radial access attempts before successful cannulation, and 4 patients required access via the contralateral arm following failure at the initial site. Radial access attempts differed significantly between groups. Fewer patients in the interrupted group required 2 attempts (0.8% vs 5.8%), while more required 3 attempts (5.3% vs 1.8%; P = .001). Conversion to contralateral radial access was more frequent in the interrupted group (6.1% vs 1.8%; P = .019), with no difference in femoral access conversions. Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) was more commonly performed in the interrupted group (46.5% vs 24.0%; P < .001).

The rates of postprocedural complications at 30 days are shown in Table 3 and Figure 1. No significant differences were observed between the interrupted and uninterrupted groups with respect to the rates of postprocedural hematoma (2.9% vs 4.0%; P = .495), pseudoaneurysm (0.8% vs 0%; P = .5), and stroke or transient ischemic attack (1.2% vs 0.4%; P = .625), respectively. There were no perforations, arteriovenous fistula, or compartment syndromes at 30-days in either group. Radial artery ultrasound was performed within 30 days after coronary angiography in 8.2% of patients in the interrupted OAC group and 9.3% in the uninterrupted OAC group, and 2 patients in the interrupted OAC group were found to have an access site hematoma on ultrasound examination.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first observational study of its kind to compare short-term outcomes after elective transradial coronary angiography between patients who continued oral anticoagulation therapy (uninterrupted OAC) and those who stopped it periprocedurally (interrupted OAC), irrespective of their baseline OAC regimen. Our study included patients on various OAC therapies who underwent a planned transradial procedure, reflecting real-world practices in the contemporary era. We found no statistically significant differences in 30-day outcomes of hematoma, pseudoaneurysm, perforation, arteriovenous fistula, compartment syndrome, or stroke/transient ischemic attack between the groups (Central Illustration). Notably, although radial artery perforation, a relatively rare event, was not observed in our study, managing such an occurrence in a patient on uninterrupted OAC would present considerable challenges. Importantly, our study showed that the uninterrupted strategy did not result in higher complication rates at 30 days compared with the conventional interrupted approach, which is currently endorsed by guidelines and expert consensus. An uninterrupted approach may therefore represent a reasonable alternative for patients undergoing elective transradial coronary angiography.

European guidelines for VKA-treated patients undergoing elective coronary angiography

The 2020 ESC guidelines recommend an uninterrupted strategy in VKA-treated or DOAC-treated patients undergoing coronary angiography for non–ST elevation acute coronary syndrome,3 which is supported by literature.4 The 2018 ESC expert consensus also supports an uninterrupted OAC strategy in VKA-treated patients undergoing elective PCI. Notably, existing guidelines make no distinction between radial and femoral access routes. This is well-supported by literature, which demonstrated that uninterrupted VKA is at least as safe as interrupted VKA and at least as safe as or safer than interrupted VKA with bridging anticoagulation in elective coronary angiography. A meta-analysis that included 2325 patients from 7 observational studies and 1 randomized controlled trial10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 demonstrated no statistically significant differences in major adverse cardiac events (P = .46) and major bleeding (P = .49) between the uninterrupted and interrupted VKA groups, but significantly lower rates of both major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) and major bleeding in the uninterrupted VKA group than those in the interrupted VKA with bridging group (P = .03 for MACCE; P = .03 for major bleeding).18 Of these 8 studies, some included only femoral cases, while others included both radial and femoral cases. A randomized trial9 of 110 transfemoral cases found no increase in access site complications with uninterrupted VKA compared with those with heparin bridging, and Jessup et al8 reported no difference in complication rates between uninterrupted and interrupted VKA with femoral access. Ziakas et al,19 in a prospective study of 56 VKA-treated patients, endorsed the uninterrupted approach for both femoral and radial routes, noting fewer complications with radial access in those undergoing PCI. Likewise, Baker et al,20 in a retrospective analysis, also found significantly fewer vascular and bleeding complications with radial vs femoral access in VKA-treated patients.

North American guidelines for VKA-treated patients undergoing elective coronary angiography

In contrast to the European guidelines, the 2020 ACC expert consensus and the 2021 Update of the North American Perspective support an interrupted strategy in all VKA-treated patients undergoing elective coronary angiography. To our knowledge, there is sparse and possibly no published data in recent years that supports an interrupted VKA strategy. The North American guidelines recommend reserving the uninterrupted strategy for urgent procedures, such as in the case of an ST elevation myocardial infarction or high-risk non–ST elevation myocardial infarction.6,7

European and North American guidelines for DOAC-treated patients undergoing elective coronary angiography

Finally, the current European and North American guidelines and expert consensus agree on an interrupted DOAC strategy in elective coronary angiography, given the paucity of data and lack of strong evidence supporting an uninterrupted strategy. Two studies assessed the impact of periprocedural DOAC management on complications following elective transradial coronary angiography.21,22 Chongprasertpon et al21 prospectively studied 49 patients on uninterrupted DOAC who underwent elective diagnostic coronary angiography and found no significant differences in complication rates compared with controls. Tuǧba Kemaloǧlu et al22 compared 60 patients on uninterrupted DOAC with 100 controls who were not on a DOAC and underwent diagnostic transradial coronary angiography and similarly found no increased complications in the DOAC group at 1 month.

The real-world experience: VKA-treated and DOAC-treated patients undergoing elective transradial coronary angiography

To our knowledge, this single-center, retrospective analysis is the first cohort study of its caliber that examined an uninterrupted approach in both VKA-treated and DOAC-treated patients undergoing elective outpatient transradial coronary angiography. Only those for whom a transradial approach was planned were included in our study, as radial access is the preferred and predominant access approach at our institution and in contemporary practice. Our study also included patients regardless of their outpatient OAC regimen, reflecting contemporary clinical practices as well. DOACs were the predominant OAC (89.4% in the interrupted OAC group and 82.6% in the uninterrupted OAC group) in our study population, and most were on long-term apixaban treatment (67.8% in the interrupted OAC group and 66.2% in the uninterrupted OAC group). This reflects the growing body of literature supporting the efficacy and safety of DOACs in recent years, in contrast to 2021, when the majority of patients in the study by Venetsanos et al were on warfarin.23

Compared with those in the uninterrupted group, more patients in the interrupted group had 3 radial access attempts before successful cannulation (5.3% vs 1.8%; P = .001), converted to the alternate radial artery (6.1% vs 1.8%; P = .019), and received PCI (46.5% vs 24.0%; P < .001). These differences in procedural management may partially explain why 30-day complication rates did not significantly differ between the 2 groups in our study.

A retrospective analysis of the SWEDEHEART registry23 addressed a similar question by including both VKA-treated and DOAC-treated patients who underwent either transfemoral or transradial coronary angiography. Among the 3322 patients in the interrupted group and 3163 patients in the uninterrupted group, there were no significant differences in the rates of MACCE or bleeding (12.6% vs 12.9%), stroke (2.3% vs 2.2%), and major bleeding (5.6% vs 6.0%) at 120 days. In contrast to our study, in which fewer than 20% of patients were on warfarin, the SWEDEHEART cohort was predominantly treated with warfarin (85.4% in the interrupted group and 81.2% in the uninterrupted group). Additionally, while our study exclusively included patients undergoing planned radial procedures, the SWEDEHEART registry encompassed both planned transradial and transfemoral procedures, with transfemoral access accounting for 41.7% and 27.2% in the interrupted and uninterrupted groups, respectively.

Although our study observed lower rates of postprocedural stroke (1.2% in the interrupted group vs 0.4% in the uninterrupted group) and bleeding (2.9% vs 4.0%) compared with those of the SWEDEHEART registry, both studies consistently demonstrated no statistically significant differences in bleeding and stroke outcomes between the interrupted and uninterrupted strategy—at 30 days in our study and at 120 days in the SWEDEHEART registry. Despite differences in patient populations and procedural approaches, the concordance in safety outcomes between our study and the SWEDEHEART registry, which included a larger and more heterogeneous population with extended follow-up, is reassuring and supports the safety of uninterrupted OAC in selected patient populations undergoing coronary angiography.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective, single-center observational study, it is subject to selection bias, unmeasured confounders, and limited generalizability. Second, while patients in the interrupted group were instructed to discontinue OAC therapy 24 to 48 hours before their elective outpatient coronary angiography, the precise duration of OAC interruption was not documented, which can introduce variability in anticoagulation status at the time of the procedure. Third, data regarding intraprocedural administration of intravenous heparin and the use of postprocedural antiplatelet therapy was not collected, limiting our ability to assess the impact of intraprocedural anticoagulation and postprocedural antiplatelet use on outcomes. Additionally, patient allocation to the interrupted vs uninterrupted groups was determined by operator preference, with 2 operators routinely using the interrupted strategy and 1 favoring an uninterrupted approach at our institution. As such, operator-dependent variations in procedural technique and equipment selection may have influenced outcomes in ways that are not fully captured or controlled for in our analysis.

Finally, in our study, the number of PCI procedures performed was nearly twice as high in the interrupted group compared to the uninterrupted group, although the reason for this in unclear. Procedural details such as coronary complexity or use of adjunctive calcium modification therapies were not collected but may have contributed. No cases in the uninterrupted group had PCI deferred due to anticoagulation status. To avoid duplication, only the first procedure was included for patients with multiple procedures during the study period; in some instances, this may have been a diagnostic procedure with a subsequent staged PCI excluded, which may have disproportionately affected the uninterrupted group. Additionally, as group allocation was based on operator preference, variability in thresholds for performing PCI may have also influenced the results.

Conclusion

Our study found that an uninterrupted strategy was not associated with higher rates of 30-day complications in all comers undergoing elective outpatient radial artery catheterization, regardless of their outpatient OAC regimen. While our study did not stratify patients based on VKA vs DOAC therapy, the majority of patients were on a DOAC. Therefore, an uninterrupted strategy could potentially be safe and noninferior to an interrupted strategy in DOAC-treated patients. Given the black box warning on DOACs emphasizing the transient increased risk for thrombotic events with cessation, it may not always be desirable to interrupt DOAC therapy for elective cardiac catheterization, especially in light of the findings of this study. Larger, randomized controlled trials are warranted to further guide physicians and to elucidate the optimal periprocedural management strategy for this population.
