Homologous recombination status drives disease-free survival in patients with advanced ovarian cancer undergoing hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy at the time of interval debulking surgery
Authors: Khrystyna Levytska, SeungYong Han, Allison E. Axtell, Malaika W. Amneus, Brandon Ing, Scott E. Lentz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•HIPEC and PARP inhibitors have improved survival in patients with advanced ovarian cancer.•HIPEC with cisplatin alone or cisplatin/paclitaxel did not improve disease-free survival compared to surgery without HIPEC.•Adding paclitaxel to cisplatin during HIPEC did not increase peri-operative morbidity.•HRD patients had the longest disease-free survival compared to HRP patients, regardless of treatment regimen.

Introduction

Hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC) involves circulating heated chemotherapy directly into the abdominal cavity at the time of a debulking surgery (Chambers, 2020). Several trials have evaluated the use of HIPEC in the treatment of advanced ovarian cancer (Antonio, 2022, Lee, 2023, Lim, 2022, van Driel, 2018, Filis, 2022). In 2018, OVHIPEC-1, a multi-center, open-label phase 3 trial conducted in the Netherlands, compared interval debulking surgery (IDS) with and without HIPEC in patients with advanced ovarian cancer (van Driel, 2018). The authors showed improved recurrence-free survival of 10 months compared to 14 months with the addition of HIPEC, as well as a sustained overall survival advantage in the final analysis (Aronson, 2023). Therefore, current NCCN guidelines recommend consideration of HIPEC with cisplatin at the time of IDS for advanced stage ovarian, fallopian tube, or primary peritoneal cancers (NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology, 2025).

Another therapy which has significantly improved outcomes of ovarian cancer patients was the discovery of poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors. PARP inhibitors block repair of DNA damage in cells with altered DNA repair mechanism through the concept of synthetic lethality (Lord and Ashworth, 2017). The benefit of PARP inhibitors is mostly pronounced in patients whose tumors harbor mutations in genes involved in the homologous recombination (HR) DNA repair pathway, specifically BRCA1/2. Several practice-changing studies have shown significant advances in progression-free and overall survival, with hazard ratios ranging from 0.30 to 0.57, in patients who are HR deficient (Ray-Coquard, 2019, Moore, 2018, Gonzalez-Martin, 2019, Coleman, 2019, Monk, 2022).

In 2021, a single-institution prospective cohort study comparing 64 patients who underwent HIPEC with cisplatin or cisplatin/paclitaxel showed a significantly longer median progression-free survival in the cisplatin/paclitaxel combination group (11 vs 22 months) (Chambers et al., 2021). This led to a change in our institution’s practice to add paclitaxel to cisplatin during HIPEC at the time of IDS for advanced ovarian cancer. Therefore, objectives of our study were to: (1) compare peri-operative outcomes between patients undergoing IDS without HIPEC, HIPEC with cisplatin alone, and HIPEC with cisplatin/paclitaxel, and (2) stratify DFS by treatment group and HR status (HR deficient or HRD vs HR proficient or HRP).

Methods

The study was an IRB-approved retrospective chart review (IRB#12597) of patients with advanced-stage high-grade epithelial ovarian, fallopian tube, or primary peritoneal cancer undergoing IDS with or without HIPEC at two tertiary centers in Los Angeles, CA, between September 2018 and May 2024. Patients were identified using diagnosis and procedure codes. Patients met all of the following inclusion criteria: had diagnosis of Stage III/IV cancer; received 3–4 cycles of neo-adjuvant platinum-based chemotherapy with radiologic and laboratory evidence of response; and had > 6 months of post-operative surveillance available (data cutoff March 2025). Patients were then separated into 3 groups according to the treatment regimen: (1) IDS without HIPEC, (2) IDS with HIPEC using cisplatin alone, and (3) IDS with HIPEC using cisplatin and paclitaxel. Only patients undergoing IDS via exploratory laparotomy and those with complete (no residual macroscopic disease) and optimal (macroscopic residual disease with maximum diameter < 1 cm) cytoreduction were included. Disease-free survival was defined as time from surgery to radiologic recurrence or last oncologic surveillance visit.

All HIPEC cases were performed at Kaiser Permanente-Los Angeles Medical Center (2018–2024) and all IDS without HIPEC cases were performed at Kaiser Permanente-Panorama City (2019–2024). Between 2018 and 2021, HIPEC treatment consisted of cisplatin at 100 mg/m2 at 41–43 °C over 90 min; as of August 2021, the protocol was adapted to include paclitaxel and cisplatin (administered as paclitaxel 135 mg/m2 and cisplatin 100 mg/m2 at 41–43 °C over 90 min) based on a previously presented study (Chambers et al., 2021). The abdomen was shaken for 90 min once the temperature of 41 °C was reached. Once the target temperature was reached, 50 % of the dose of the single or both agents were infused at 0 min, followed by 25 % of the dose at 30 min, followed by final 25 % of the dose at 60 min. All patients undergoing HIPEC received sodium thiosulfate to avoid nephrotoxicity. Closed abdomen technique was used for all HIPEC procedures. Active patient cooling measures (cooled IV fluids, ice packs) were employed during HIPEC. Prior to March 2021, HIPEC patients were admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for at least 24 h per the institutional protocol. In 2021, enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocol was applied to all HIPEC patients, allowing post-operative recovery in the routine surgical unit, unless otherwise clinically indicated.

All patients had germline and/or somatic testing available to assign HR status. HRD status was assigned if patients had one or more of the following: germline and/or somatic BRCA1/2 pathogenic variants, HRD positive result on molecular profiling, or non-BRCA HR-associated germline and/or somatic pathogenic variants. Molecular profiling was performed using next-generation sequencing (Caris Life Sciences, Myriad MyChoice CDx, or Neo Genomics NeoTYPE Analysis HRD + Profile).

Descriptive statistics were reported as n (%), mean ± SD, or median [IQR]. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-Square test. Continuous variables were assessed for normality; those not normally distributed were compared the Kruskal-Wallis non-parametric test. The Kaplan-Meier plot and log-rank test were used to compare differences in survival distributions between groups. Significance was set as p < 0.05 for all analyses. GraphPad Prism V 10.4.2 was used for data generation and analysis.

Post-operative course is summarized in Table 2. Post-operatively, none of the patients who had IDS without HIPEC were admitted to the ICU. This was in contrast to patients who underwent HIPEC, where the majority of patients prior to March 2021, spent at least one day in the ICU per institutional protocol. After the adoption of the ERAS protocol to HIPEC surgeries, patients who underwent HIPEC received routine post-operative care. Patients undergoing HIPEC-cisplatin (prior to ERAS protocol change) spent 0.54 ± 0.75 post-operative days (POD) in the ICU, compared to 0.08 ± 0.39 days in the group which received HIPEC-cisplatin/paclitaxel (p < 0.001). The rate of emesis within the first 48 h were similar between groups (p = 0.21). Return of bowel function across all patients occurred on POD 3.15 ± 1.06, and did not differ between groups (p = 0.17). Rates of Clavien-Dindo Grade II or Grade III/IV complications did not differ between groups (p = 0.39). Overall, 11 (14.9 %) patients received a blood transfusion or experienced anemia (hemoglobin < 7 g/dL) post-operatively; rates of blood transfusion did not differ between groups (p = 0.35). On average, patients were discharged on POD 4.01 ± 1.37, with longest length of stay (POD 4.50 ± 1.20) noted amongst patients in HIPEC-cisplatin group, and shortest length of stay (POD 3.65 ± 0.85) in the HIPEC-cisplatin/paclitaxel group (p = 0.002). There were no differences in hospital readmissions related to surgery at 30 and 90 days across all groups (p = 0.54).

Cancer-directed treatments are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. All patients included in our study had received neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Median number of neoadjuvant chemotherapy cycles was 3 (Lee, 2023, Lim, 2022) and did not differ between groups (p = 0.71). Most frequently, patients received carboplatin/paclitaxel (55, 74.3 %). HIPEC-cisplatin group of patients received bevacizumab in addition to carboplatin/paclitaxel at the lowest rate (1, 3.6 %), compared to the no HIPEC (5, 25.0 %) and HIPEC-cisplatin/paclitaxel (7, 26.9 %) groups (p = 0.04). Time to adjuvant chemotherapy after surgery was the longest in the HIPEC-cisplatin/paclitaxel group (33.92 ± 9.51 days), and shortest in the no HIPEC group (27.40 ± 9.69 days) (p = 0.009). Median number of adjuvant chemotherapy cycles was 3 (Lee, 2023) (p = 0.20). Carboplatin and paclitaxel was most frequently used adjuvant regimen (63, 85.1 %). HIPEC-cisplatin group had the lowest rates of carboplatin/paclitaxel with bevacizumab use (1, 3.6 %) (p = 0.04). At the completion of upfront chemotherapy, 37 (50 %) patients received maintenance. Highest rate of maintenance was amongst the no HIPEC group (12, 60.0 %), and lowest amongst HIPEC-cisplatin group (11, 39.3 %); these differences were not significant (p = 0.33). PARP inhibitors were the most frequently used maintenance across all groups (23, 31.0 %), followed by bevacizumab (9, 12.2 %). Rates of maintenance with any regimen did not differ between groups; bevacizumab maintenance was the highest in the no HIPEC group (4, 20.0 %), and lowest in the HIPEC-cisplatin group (1, 3.6 %) (p = 0.20).

Germline and somatic testing results are summarized in Table 3. All patients had undergone germline genetic testing at diagnosis. Across three cohorts, 14 (18.9 %) patients had one or more germline pathogenic variant identified, with similar rates in all treatment groups (p = 0.98). BRCA1/2 pathogenic variants were identified in 10 (13.5 %) participants. When broken down by treatment category, 3 (15.0 %) patients in the no HIPEC group had a pathogenic variant in BRCA1/2, 2 (7.1 %) in HIPEC-cisplatin group, and 5 (19.2 %) in the HIPEC-cisplatin/paclitaxel group (p = 0.42). Germline pathogenic variants in the HR-associated genes (including BRCA1/2) were noted in 13 (17.6 %) patients across all three groups, and did not differ between treatments (p = 0.84). Somatic testing was available in 67 (90.5 %) patients; pathogenic variants were found in 64 (95.5 %) cases. The most common abnormality was TP53, found in 62 (83.8 %) of all patients. BRCA1/2 pathogenic somatic variants were identified in 11 (16.4 %) patients, while HR-associated pathogenic variants (including BRCA1/2) were noted in 16 (23.9 %) tumors. Proportion of tumors harboring HR-associated pathogenic variants did not differ between groups (p = 0.83). Patients who had BRCA1/2 pathogenic variants on somatic or germline testing, and/or were “HRD” on molecular profiling, and/or had non-BRCA HR-associated pathogenic variants on somatic or germline testing, were classified as HRD and comprised 39.2 % of the study population (29 patients). The highest number of patients classified as HRD was in the no HIPEC group (9, 45.0 %), compared to the lowest number in the HIPEC-cisplatin/paclitaxel group (8, 30.7 %) (p = 0.55). Across all groups, 8 patients had HR-associated pathogenic variants (other than BRCA1/2) which qualified them to receive a PARP inhibitor maintenance. Median DFS was also compared between HRD and HRP patients within each treatment group. Discussion

Our study evaluated peri-operative outcomes and disease-free survival in patients with advanced-stage ovarian cancer who underwent HIPEC with cisplatin alone or cisplatin and paclitaxel at the time of IDS. In our cohort, addition of paclitaxel to cisplatin during HIPEC did not impact intra- or post-operative course. As expected, HIPEC was associated with a longer length of procedure compared to IDS without HIPEC. Post-operatively, rates of emesis and time to return of bowel function was similar between groups. There was no difference in 30-day and 90-day readmission rates, however, we did see a longer time (by 3–6 days) to start of adjuvant chemotherapy in the HIPEC groups. We also compared disease-free survival between HIPEC and no HIPEC groups, stratified by HR status. Across the entire cohort, 39 % of patients harbored pathogenic variants or molecular profile which qualified them to receive a PARP inhibitor. Across all treatment groups, patients who were classified as HRD had a significantly longer DFS (40 vs 11 months); this finding was consistent across all treatment types. To our surprise, we did not see a progression-free survival benefit in patients who received HIPEC compared to patients who underwent IDS alone.

Our findings in the context of the perioperative morbidity are in line with prior studies, which showed comparable side-effect and safety profiles of patients receiving IDS with and without HIPEC (van Driel, 2018, Chambers et al., 2021). However, our findings of no impact on PFS in patients treated with HIPEC compared to those treated with IDS alone are in contrast to randomized controlled trials conducted in the Netherlands and South Korea, which showed significant improvement in survival with the addition of HIPEC to IDS (Lim, 2022, van Driel, 2018); and a single institution prospective study which showed a significant benefit to progression-free survival with addition of paclitaxel during HIPEC (Chambers et al., 2021). The use of PARP inhibitors in the HRD patient population has changed the practice of gynecologic oncology. Although HRD status is nowadays synonymous with PARP inhibitor use in clinical practice, our study was not powered to elucidate the distinct contributions of HIPEC vs PARP inhibitors to the survival advantage in patients undergoing IDS for ovarian cancer. Trials studying PARP inhibitors were overlapping with trials evaluating potential benefits of HIPEC (Lim, 2022, van Driel, 2018, Ray-Coquard, 2019, Moore, 2018, Gonzalez-Martin, 2019). The use of maintenance therapies was not reported in trials establishing HIPEC as superior to surgery alone (Lim, 2022, van Driel, 2018). Only 3 patients in OV-HIPEC-1 had received upfront maintenance therapy with olaparib, and exploratory post-hoc analysis suggested that patients who were HRD non-BRCA-mutated (vs BRCA-mutated) appeared to derive the most benefit from HIPEC (Aronson, 2023).

Our study attempted to understand the role of HR status on progression-free survival in patients with advanced stage ovarian cancer undergoing IDS with or without HIPEC. This study has several limitations, some of which are inherent to the nature of a retrospective study design. The change in our institutional protocol to adopt ERAS to HIPEC surgeries in March 2021 (shortly before the transition to the combination cisplatin/paclitaxel HIPEC regimen in August 2021) is likely a confounding factor in perioperative outcomes, such as the length of ICU stay, day of discharge, and use of invasive hemodynamic monitoring. The switch from cisplatin monotherapy to cisplatin-paclitaxel combination in HIPEC naturally resulted in the longest overall follow-up in the HIPEC-cisplatin group; this could have potentially skewed the DFS outcomes. However, across all treatment groups, only 12 % of patients had less than 12 months of follow-up available post-operatively. Furthermore, patients undergoing HIPEC vs IDS alone were treated at different sites within our hospital system, which could also have accounted for differences such as length of surgery. Additionally, upfront maintenance used in our patient population reflects the changing landscape of ovarian cancer treatment during our study period, for example, some HRD patients not receiving a PARP inhibitor vs some HRP patients receiving one. Lastly, we chose to use a broader definition of HRD in our study, beyond those who had a BRCA1/2 pathogenic variant or were “HRD” on molecular profiling. The definition of HRD is heterogeneous and varies across the practice-shaping trials which established the use of PARP inhibitors for HRD positive ovarian cancer patients (Ray-Coquard, 2019, Gonzalez-Martin, 2019, Coleman, 2019, Monk, 2022). The traditional definition of HRD in these clinical trials focused on germline and/or somatic BRCA1/2 pathogenic variants or elevated genomic instability scores (Stewart, 2022). However, studies have shown that benefit of PARP inhibitors extends beyond those with BRCA-mutated tumors (Yi, 2019, Hodgson, 2018, Konstantinopoulos, 2020, Pan, 2023). Therefore, to better reflect the practical use of PARP inhibitors, we classified patients with HR-associated non-BRCA pathogenic variants as HRD.

The question remains whether HIPEC adds an additional benefit to an already significant benefit of PARP inhibitors in HRD patients, and whether it offers any benefit to those patients who are ineligible to receive a PARP inhibitor. In this study, we attempted to elucidate the effect of HR status on survival in patients with ovarian cancer undergoing IDS and HIPEC; however, our study was not powered to understand the compounded benefit of concurrent use of PARP inhibitors and HIPEC, if any. The question of whether HIPEC and PARP inhibitors act synergistically, and which patients would gain the most benefit from either treatment, remains to be answered. Randomized controlled trials, such as GOG-3068 (NCT05659381), will offer additional insight (Foundation, 2025).

Conclusions

Overall, our findings suggest that patient HR status and tumor genetics remain the main drivers of response to treatment in patients with advanced ovarian cancer.
