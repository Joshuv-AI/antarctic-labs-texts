A case report of potentially inappropriate medication-induced lead encephalopathy mimicking autoimmune encephalitis
Authors: A. Afeef, Sunil Jyani, Bhrigu Jain, Avinash Chakrawarty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLead encephalopathy is an uncommon but potentially fatal condition that can present with nonspecific neurological symptoms. In older adults, it rarely mimics autoimmune diseases, leading to misdiagnosis. This case underscores the need to recognize hidden sources of lead exposure, particularly the prolonged use of herbal medicines, which is prevalent in South Asia, and to critically assess potentially inappropriate medications in geriatric care.Case presentationAn 80-year-old male with a history of benign prostatic hyperplasia presented with subacute cognitive decline, bradykinesia, tremor, and a focal impaired consciousness seizure with right-sided motor manifestations. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis showed lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein, though autoimmune and paraneoplastic panels were negative. FDG-PET revealed basal ganglia hypermetabolism with cortical hypometabolism, raising suspicion for autoimmune encephalitis. The patient showed no improvement with corticosteroid pulse therapy. On further evaluation, a history of chronic use of Ayurvedic preparations was elicited. Blood toxicology revealed elevated lead levels, confirming lead encephalopathy. He was treated with oral dimercaptosuccinic acid (DMSA), resulting in marked neurological improvement. During the two-year follow-up, the patient remained functionally independent.ConclusionsThis case demonstrates how lead encephalopathy can closely mimic autoimmune neurological conditions in older adults, potentially delaying accurate diagnosis and treatment. It reinforces the critical role of detailed history-taking, especially regarding alternative medicine use. The patient’s recovery with chelation therapy highlights the reversibility of symptoms when lead toxicity is promptly identified and managed, underscoring the need for caution with unregulated therapies in geriatric care.

Background

Lead (Pb) is a toxic metal naturally found in the Earth’s crust. Its widespread use has led to global contamination, exposure, and public health issues. The primary sources of lead exposure include paints, leaded gasoline, water, dust, food and some cases have also been reported with the inappropriate use of Ayurvedic medications [1, 2]. Lead exerts toxic effects across multiple organ systems—including the renal, hematopoietic, gastrointestinal, and central nervous systems—with its neurological manifestation, lead encephalopathy, associated with mortality rates as high as 65% if left untreated [3]. In acute exposure, it may present with seizures, altered mental status, decreased consciousness, cranial nerve palsies, hemiparesis, hypertonia, and gait disturbances, while chronic toxicity often manifests as cognitive impairment, persistent headaches, tremor, and parkinsonian features [4].

We report a rare case of lead encephalopathy mimicking autoimmune encephalitis, with favorable recovery, emphasizing the importance of thorough history-taking especially alternative medicine use.

Case report

This 80-year-old male patient, known to have benign prostatic hyperplasia, presented with a history of fatigue and forgetfulness for three months, bradykinesia for two months, one episode of a focal impaired consciousness seizure with right-sided motor manifestations not further characterized 2 weeks before admission and a progressive decline in alertness and attention, manifesting as disorientation, incoherent speech, reduced verbal communication, and decreased food intake, over the past 2 weeks. On admission, vital signs were stable, and the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) was 13 (E3V4M6); the reduced eye-opening component of the GCS was attributed to drowsiness, which limited formal bedside cognitive testing (eg. Mini mental status examination). Neurological examination revealed low- to medium-amplitude low frequency tremor involving the distal left arm, more prominent at rest. Asymmetric rigidity was noted, more pronounced in the right arm compared to the other three limbs. Bradykinesia was also evident on examination; together with the rest tremor and asymmetric rigidity, these findings constituted asymmetric parkinsonian syndrome. Cranial nerves, deep tendon reflexes and plantar responses were unremarkable. Meningeal signs and nystagmus were absent. Gait and limb power could not be assessed, and apart from this a focused examination for systemic manifestations of lead toxicity showed no Burton line over the gingiva and no bony tenderness. There was no clinically evident wrist or foot drop to suggest overt motor neuropathy, although nerve conduction studies were not performed. The remainder of the systemic examination was unremarkable.

Initial routine investigations, complete blood counts revealed (see Table 1) low hemoglobin (7.8 g/dl), which was normocytic normochromic with occasional microcytes on peripheral blood film (PBF), and neutrophilic leukocytosis (15,460/μL), with no atypical cells or blasts. The corrected reticulocyte count was 3.5%. Serum electrolytes, thyroid function test, renal and liver function tests were within normal limits. Non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT) of the head showed age-related cerebral atrophic changes with no evidence of acute disease. Urine Microscopy showed increased pus cells and urine culture grew E. coli. He was treated with antibiotics, but there was no clinical improvement (Leukocytosis resolved). On 3rd day of antibiotics cerebrospinal fluid examination revealed 10 cells, all of which were lymphocytes, with elevated protein levels (149 mg/dl), a normal CSF:serum glucose ratio of 0.54 (see Table 1) and we ruled out common infective etiology with available panel including negative HSV PCR, cryptococcal antigen, Gram stain, AFB, and TB GeneXpert; CSF ADA was 5 U/L (Lab Biological Reference Range up to 10 U/L). The electroencephalogram showed generalized theta-delta slowing. A final diagnosis of lead encephalopathy mimicking Autoimmune encephalitis with anemia was made, and the patient started on lead chelation therapy with Dimercaptosuccinic acid (DMSA) at 10 mg/kg/dose every 8 h for five days, followed by 10 mg/kg/dose every 12 h for 14 days and all alternative medications were stopped. Apart from this, Levetiracetam was initiated on the first day of admission for seizure prophylaxis, continued for two months, and then gradually tapered off.

The patient’s alertness and cognition improved, along with an increase in hemoglobin (see Table 1). Over two years of multiple follow-ups, the patient has maintained independence in activities of daily living, has returned to intellectual work and no further drop in hemoglobin was noted.

Discussion

Lead is a well-established neurotoxin, and both acute and chronic exposures can result in a spectrum of neurological manifestations, including encephalopathy, particularly in susceptible populations such as the elderly [5]. Pathologically, acute lead encephalopathy is characterized by cerebral edema, whereas chronic exposure leads to widespread tissue degeneration, including cavitation, venous thickening, and cellular disorganization [6]. It also interferes with NMDA receptor activity, impacting glutamate neurotransmission and hippocampal function, which may explain the observed cognitive decline, seizures, and Parkinsonian features [7]. In our patient, these mechanisms likely contributed to the gradual onset of memory impairment, bradykinesia, resting tremor, and a seizure episode—clinical features that initially mimicked autoimmune or degenerative neurological disorders.

At presentation, the patient fulfilled two of the three Graus et al. (2016) criteria for possible autoimmune encephalitis: subacute onset of altered mental status with working-memory impairment, and at least one supportive feature—a new focal seizure together with CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis [8]. In keeping with that algorithm, empirical pulse corticosteroids were justified while antibody results were awaited. However, the third criterion—reasonable exclusion of alternative causes—was ultimately not satisfied, as toxicological evaluation identified chronic lead exposure as the true etiology, reclassifying the presentation as a toxic encephalopathy mimicking autoimmune encephalitis. The MRI white-matter abnormalities were more consistent with lead encephalopathy [9, 10], than with a limbic or encephalitic pattern; therefore, the neuroimaging criterion was not fulfilled. The FDG-PET findings, although initially interpreted as supportive, are non-specific. Although characteristic cerebral metabolic patterns have been described in autoimmune encephalitis, FDG-PET is not part of the formal criteria for possible autoimmune encephalitis and provides supportive rather than diagnostic information [11]. In our patient, basal ganglia hypermetabolism is also described in toxic and metabolic encephalopathies, as well as in neurodegenerative and epileptic processes, and is therefore consistent with the final diagnosis of lead neurotoxicity [12, 13]. Likewise, CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis is not a typical feature of lead neurotoxicity and was absent in the comparable case reported by Manuraj et al.; In our patient, mild CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis remained non-specific in the available data and contributed to the initial diagnostic uncertainty.

In our case, the patient’s prolonged use of herbal (Ayurvedic) medications emerged as the pivotal etiological factor, with toxicological analysis confirming elevated blood lead levels consistent with chronic exposure. This finding is supported by literature indicating that nearly 20% of Ayurvedic preparations contain heavy metals—such as lead, mercury, and arsenic—at concentrations exceeding permissible limits [2, 8, 14, 15]. A comparable report by Manuraj et al. described a patient with lead encephalopathy that closely resembled autoimmune encephalitis, characterized by neuropsychiatric symptoms, seizures, and basal ganglia abnormalities on MRI [16]. In this case, occupational exposure to lead-containing cosmetics and microcytic anemia helped establish the diagnosis. Neuroimaging in lead encephalopathy commonly demonstrates symmetrical T2/FLAIR hyperintensities involving the basal ganglia, subcortical white matter, cerebellum, and brainstem, reflecting toxic demyelination and vasogenic edema [9, 10, 16]. Our patient showed bilateral subcortical white-matter hyperintensities involving the occipital, temporal, frontoparietal, and basal ganglia regions on T2/FLAIR sequences, consistent with reported radiological features of lead neurotoxicity. Together, these cases emphasize the importance of detailed history-taking to identify hidden toxic exposures and avoid misdiagnosis [16]. Additionally, our case exemplifies the broader issue of polypharmacy and the use of potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) in the elderly, highlighting the risks associated with unregulated or prolonged use of alternative therapies.

Interestingly, despite the delayed presentation, our patient demonstrated remarkable clinical improvement—likely attributable to the brain’s partial resilience to mitochondrial damage, effective lead clearance through chelation therapy, and the absence of major cognitive risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, smoking, hearing loss, depression, social isolation, and physical inactivity [17].

This case is limited by the absence of direct toxicological analysis of herbal medication, although product labeling and online information indicated lead content. Nerve conduction studies were not performed; therefore, subclinical peripheral motor neuropathy could not be definitively excluded. Skeletal manifestations of chronic lead toxicity were not specifically evaluated radiologically. Post-chelation blood lead levels and follow-up neuroimaging (MRI-Brain and FDG-PET) and ANA were not obtained. Testing for other broad spectrum of autoimmune, paraneoplastic markers, CSF oligoclonal bands and viral PCRs other than HSV were not feasible. Nonetheless, the marked clinical improvement following chelation strongly supports the diagnosis.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the diagnostic challenge of lead encephalopathy in older adults, where symptoms mimic autoimmune encephalitis. A detailed history revealed long-term Ayurvedic medication use as the hidden source of toxicity. Timely chelation therapy led to significant recovery, underscoring the importance of early recognition and the need to reassess potentially inappropriate medications in the elderly.
