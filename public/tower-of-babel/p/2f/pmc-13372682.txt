Field evaluation of novel experimental nootkatone-based products for control of Ixodes scapularis and Amblyomma americanum (Acari: Ixodidae)
Authors: Chantal Sengsourinho, Megan C Dyer, Alexis White, Steven R Alm, Thomas N Mather
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA total of eight nature-based products were tested against nymphal blacklegged ticks Ixodes scapularis Say or lone star ticks, Amblyomma americanum (L.) during the summers of 2023 and 2024. Field trials were performed using a micro-plot arena system in a natural field setting. The arena system has previously been used to successfully evaluate products against blacklegged nymphs and was modified for use with lone star nymphs. One of the eight products evaluated in the study, the commercially available BugArmor Zero-Bite, did not exhibit any significant tick-killing activity. The other seven products were nootkatone-based (NootkaSHIELD™) experimental formulations provided by the Swiss company Evolva and still in the product-development process. Of those samples, Evo A (10% nootkatone) and Evo C (10% nootkatone) provided over 94% knockdown control (efficacy within the first two weeks of application) of blacklegged ticks, and 81.2% and 91.0% knockdown control of lone star ticks, respectively. Both formulations also displayed significant residual control (efficacy after 2 weeks post treatment) but results for Evo A (10% nootkatone) varied greatly between 2023 and 2024. Evo B (10% nootkatone) also demonstrated high levels of efficacy, with 78.5% knockdown control and 80.8% residual control. Treatments using the three NootkaSHIELD™ formulations containing 3% nootkatone all resulted in knockdown efficacies below 45%. The results of this study suggest that NootkaSHIELD™ products containing 10% nootkatone are promising candidates for future commercial availability to control ticks.

Introduction

Ticks are medically important vectors of various pathogens worldwide. In the United States, blacklegged ticks, Ixodes scapularis Say, transmit pathogens that cause human diseases such as anaplasmosis, babesiosis, ehrlichiosis, Lyme disease, and Powassan virus disease (Eisen and Eisen 2018). Another tick of medical concern is the lone star tick, Amblyomma americanum (L.), which is known to transmit pathogens causing ehrlichiosis, tularemia, Bourbon virus disease, and Heartland virus disease. Lone star tick bites also are associated with STARI (southern tick-associated rash illness) and alpha-gal syndrome (AGS), a debilitating and occasionally life-threatening allergy to red meat or other mammalian-based products containing the sugar molecule alpha-gal (Commins 2011, CDC 2024a). Both blacklegged and lone star ticks have been experiencing a rapid expansion in range throughout parts of the United States (Eisen et al. 2016, Monzón et al. 2016, Jordan and Egizi 2019, Rochlin et al. 2022, Eisen and Eisen 2023). This is a serious public health concern because together, these two species carry many pathogens that cause thousands of cases of tickborne diseases every year in the United States. As populations of ticks grow in number, the chances of exposure to ticks infected with disease agents also increase, causing a great demand for effective tick management and tick-bite prevention.

One commonly used strategy for residential tick control involves using commercially available pesticides to treat the environment surrounding the home (Adenubi et al. 2018). The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends using acaricides registered by the United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) such as synthetic pyrethroids like bifenthrin, cyfluthrin, and permethrin for spraying backyards and other residential areas to kill ticks and prevent tick bites (CDC 2024b).

A factor complicating residential tick control is that different tick species behave in different ways, and therefore, may not respond similarly to tick control methods. Lone star ticks prefer to feed on medium- and large-sized mammals like white-tailed deer, whereas larval and nymphal blacklegged ticks often feed on small animals like rodents and birds. Methods that work for controlling blacklegged ticks, such as rodent bait boxes and tick tubes, are not deemed effective for controlling lone star ticks as rodents are not their host of choice (White and Gaff 2018). Some acaricide formulations that successfully suppress blacklegged ticks, such as granular carbaryl, have been reported to have little effect on lone star ticks (Schulze et al. 2000), perhaps due to questing behavior differences. A study testing whether blacklegged and lone star ticks would climb objects when exposed to heat stimuli, such as they do when questing in vegetation, found that nymph and adult lone star ticks were attracted to heat and willing to climb but blacklegged ticks were not attracted to heat under the circumstances of the experiment (Otálora-Luna et al. 2022).

Despite growing public awareness that numbers of ticks and tick bite-related health risks are increasing, national surveys show few respondents routinely take action to prevent tick bites using effective prevention measures (Eisen 2022, Kluger 2023, Stobbe 2024). Surveys in Lyme disease endemic areas of Connecticut and Maryland indicated 31% of participants used insect repellents, 23% used chemical pesticides on their property, and 15% used natural pesticides on their property most or all the time (Niesobecki et al. 2019). Another survey of pest control firms, also conducted in Lyme disease endemic areas in the United States, revealed fewer than 25% of homeowners use acaricides on their properties, but most homeowners (97%) would consider less toxic, perceived as eco-friendly alternatives if proven effective (Jordan and Schulze 2020).

Interest in developing minimum-risk pesticides (MRPs) and biopesticides to control or repel ticks has increased considerably in recent years due to perceived minimal risk for people and pets as well as non-target arthropod species, concern over evolution of resistance to synthetic acaricides in some tick species, fear of contamination of meat and milk from livestock, and fear of other negative environmental impacts (Aenishaenslin et al. 2015, Nwanade et al. 2020). These alternative products to control or repel ticks are based on plant products, extracts, and essential oils. Many of these naturally derived active and inert ingredients are exempt from Section 3 registration by the EPA under the Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act (FIFRA) because the ingredients were determined to pose “little to no risk” to human or environmental health (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency [U.S. EPA] 2024). Numerous MRPs have been made available to the public but are not reviewed, evaluated, or recommended by the EPA and their tick-killing or tick-repelling efficacies are largely unknown (Eisen 2024). The growing incidence of tick-borne diseases across the United States and the rise in popularity of eco-friendly, naturally derived products present an urgent need to evaluate products claiming to protect against ticks, especially those based on MRPs and not required to be evaluated by the EPA. Different products can vary greatly in tick-killing efficacy due to differences in formulation, manufacturing, compound volatility, and evaluation methodology (Dyer et al. 2021, Gonzaga et al. 2023). To best protect against tick bites and prevent disease, consumers and commercial tick control services need accurate information about the differences in effectiveness of products to kill and repel ticks.

In 2020, an active ingredient (a.i.) named nootkatone was registered by the EPA as a biopesticide for use in insecticides and repellents (U.S. EPA 2020). Nootkatone is a pesticide derived from nature, found in Alaska yellow cedar trees (Callitropsis nootkatensis D. Don) and grapefruit skin. A product containing this active ingredient may appeal to consumers seeking a safe, naturally derived alternative to DEET and other products (Clarkson et al. 2021). To facilitate development of nootkatone-based products for public use, the CDC began collaborating with an industry partner, Evolva, now Lallemand/Danstar Ferment AG, based in Switzerland (CDC 2024c). These companies possess a manufacturing process allowing nootkatone to be produced in large amounts at a low cost. However, further testing is needed for products developed with nootkatone as an a.i. and the companies need to submit their results to the EPA for review before nootkatone-based products can become commercially available to control or repel ticks.

The objective of this study was to evaluate the effect of nootkatone-based products (NootkaSHIELD™) developed by Evolva (now residing with Lallemand/Danstar Ferment AG) against nymphal blacklegged ticks and nymphal lone star ticks under field conditions. In these studies, a pre-existing, successful field evaluation system was modified for use with lone star ticks which have been described as more aggressive and active in host-seeking than blacklegged ticks (Goddard and Varela-Stokes 2009, Dyer et al. 2021).

Materials and Methods

Ethics Statement

Animals used in this experiment were housed in the Mather Laboratory at the University of Rhode Island (Kingston, RI, USA). All animal protocols complied with ethical requirements and were approved by the URI Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC).

Tick Rearing

The nymphal ticks used in these experiments were from F1 colonies maintained in the laboratory (University of Rhode Island IACUC AN08-04-017). Field-collected adult males and females were blood fed in the laboratory on New Zealand white rabbits (Oryctolagus cuniculus), and engorged females were held in an incubator under 23.5 °C, ≥95% RH, and 14 L/10D conditions for egg-laying and hatching. Blacklegged tick larvae were fed on white-footed mice (Peromyscus leucopus, Rafinesque) or Golden Syrian hamsters (Mesocricetus auratus, Waterhouse). Following tick attachment, animals were placed over pans containing water and engorged larvae were collected as they fell off into the pans. Lone star larvae were fed on New Zealand white rabbits and were contained by stocking net bags affixed to the rabbits’ ears. Engorged larvae were held in an incubator under 23.5 °C/≥95% RH and 14L/10D conditions until molting. Molted nymphs were held in the incubator for 2 to 3 months to ensure active questing behavior before being released into arenas for their respective field experiments.

Field Study Design

Blacklegged Nymph Trials

Field trials involving nymphal blacklegged ticks were conducted in a semi-cleared forested site at the University of Rhode Island’s East Farm in Kingston, Rhode Island from June to July 2023 and 2024. The site was covered predominantly by oak canopy and left undisturbed each year from the end of summer until spring of the following year to create an environment simulating typical wooded buffers in residential settings. Five large plots were constructed within the study site and further divided into 13 micro-plots, each of which contained two 0.3-m diameter PVC rings (referred to as arenas) with the centers placed 1.5 meters apart and tamped into the ground with 4 to 5 cm of the 10-cm arena remaining aboveground to contain the ticks (Fig. 1). Each of the micro-plots was treated with a different product based on a randomized block design (Fig. 2). One of the two arenas in each micro-plot was used for a knockdown assessment (K), which measured tick-killing efficacy (or questing inhibition as a proxy) of a product during the first 14 days after product application. The other arena in the micro-plot was used for assessing residual activity (R), which measured efficacy of the product by adding a new cohort of ticks after 14 days had elapsed in total, each product was sprayed in 10 arenas with 5 arenas used to test knockdown activity and 5 arenas used to test residual activity. Lone Star Nymph Trials

A preliminary field trial evaluating the effects of NootkaSHIELD™ on lone star ticks was conducted in Jamestown, Rhode Island in May 2024. The habitat contained a mix of grass, wild raspberry, wild grape, poison ivy, goldenrod, and maple forest. The site was previously sampled for ticks using a white drag-cloth; both lone star and blacklegged ticks were collected, ensuring it was suitable tick habitat. Prior to establishing the plot, the area was sampled by 4 to 5 individuals by dragging in multiple directions for a minimum of 30 min, and any ticks found were removed from the site to avoid interference with the experiment.

One main plot (15 × 3 m) was laid out within the study area and further divided into 20 1.5-m2 micro-plots. Each micro-plot contained one 0.3-m-diameter arena, and 1.5 m of distance were maintained between the center of arenas. The arenas were tamped into the ground so that 4 to 5 cm remained aboveground. Due to the need for added containment of the more motile A. americanum nymphs, a second arena lined with double-sided Gorilla mounting tape (Gorilla Glue, Inc., Cincinnati, OH), tree banding (Contech Enterprises, Victoria, BC), insect barrier adhesive (AP&G Co, Bayonne, NJ), and white duct tape (Shurtape Technologies, LLC, Avon, OH) was fitted on top of each ground arena to prevent lone star nymphs from escaping. Each of the arenas was treated with an assigned product based on a randomized block design (Fig. 4).

Treatment Preparation and Application

The products evaluated included two commercially available products, Talstar® P(bifenthrin 7.9%) and Bug Armor Zero Bite (geraniol, clove, and peppermint oil) and several experimental materials (NootkaSHIELD™) supplied by Evolva AG (Table 1): Evo A (Placebo), Evo A (3% nootkatone), Evo A (10%), Evo B (3%), Evo B (10%), Evo C (3%), and Evo C (10%) (Evolva AG, Reinach, Basel, CH). Evo A was formulated as a spray to be applied on the skin as a personal repellent for humans. Evo B was formulated as a spray for application to hard surfaces and Evo C was intended for homeowners to apply as a spray in their yards. The products within each group are composed of the same ingredients except for the concentration of nootkatone: either 3%, or 10%. We also tested a 0% concentration of Evo A as a placebo (a 0% placebo was not available for Evo B or C). The full list of components is proprietary to Evolva and subject to a non-disclosure agreement. Great Value Purified Drinking Water (Walmart Inc., Bentonville, AR) and deionized water (Evolva AG, Reinach, Basel, CH) were used as independent negative controls. Products tested in 2023 determined to have low efficacy were not retested in 2024. Products tested in both years were either (i) controls or (ii) products exhibiting high efficacy (>80% reduction in questing nymphs) in 2023 and tested again to assess reproducibility of results.

All products were prepared according to label specifications or provided instructions. 30 mL of solution was poured into Bellspray 282P plastic bottles (Bellspray Inc., Opelousas, LA) which were then attached to a Bellspray CO2 sprayer. A 0.91-m2 piece of plexiglass was used to create a 0.3-m-diameter spray shield, which was placed inside of the arenas to prevent overspraying outside of the arenas. A 0.3-m-diameter lid with a hole in the center was placed on top of the spray shield. A full-cone TeeJet TG-0.3 sprayer nozzle (TeeJet, Glendale Heights, IL) was fit into the hole of the lid and 30 mL of product was sprayed at 20 psi (138 kPa) in a circular motion for even distribution of product within the arenas.

Experimental Procedure

Blacklegged Nymph Trials

The day before product application, vials containing 60 nymphal blacklegged ticks were counted in the morning and then released later that day into each knockdown arena to allow ticks to acclimate to the environment for 24 h. A total of 300 blacklegged nymphal ticks were released for each product. The next day, all 120 arenas in the study site were sprayed with their respective products. A two-week sampling procedure was followed for sampling of the knockdown arenas (see Sampling Procedure below) where three sample dates within 14 days after treatment were combined to determine knockdown efficacy (Figs 5 and 6). After the fourteenth day, vials containing 60 nymphal blacklegged ticks were again counted and released in each residual arena. The 2-week sampling period was then repeated for only those arenas. Following tick release, all arenas were covered with 3.2-cm2 mesh hardware cloth secured with metal stakes to prevent local wildlife from disturbing the sites. The hardware cloth was removed for product application and sampling purposes and replaced thereafter.

Sampling calendar for blacklegged nymph trials in 2023. Sampling of arenas normally occurred at 3 to 4-day intervals, but experimental days may have been shifted due to rainy weather.

Sampling calendar for blacklegged nymph trials in 2024. Arenas were usually sampled at 3 to 4-day intervals. The day of tick deployment for residual arenas was shifted due to extreme heat that had potential for causing ticks to desiccate.

Lone Star Nymph Trials

The lone star trial evaluated products only for the two-week “knockdown” period in which many pesticides claim to be most effective (Fig. 7). The day before product application, 50 nymphal lone star ticks were counted into vials, released into each of the arenas, and allowed to acclimate for 24 h. In total, 200 nymphal lone star ticks were released for each product. Following nymphal lone star tick release, the arenas treated with tape and adhesive were fitted on top of the ground arenas. The top arena was removed for product application and sampling purposes and replaced once each task was completed. All arenas were sprayed with their respective products the next day. The sampling period began three days after spraying.

Sampling calendar for lone star nymph trials in 2024. The sampling procedure for blacklegged nymph trials was followed, except no residual treatment following the first 14 days occurred.

Sampling Procedure

Arenas were sampled at 3–4-day intervals within a 2-week period for a total of three sampling days (Figs 5 and 6). Some of the experimental days were shifted due to rain. A round 0.3-m-diameter wooden pressboard with a center handle, wrapped with a white flannel ‘bonnet’ was pressed into the leaf litter in each arena (about 8 to 10 times) for 10-s increments (Fig. 3B). Afterwards, the pressboard was checked for questing nymphs; any found were removed with fine-pointed tweezers and placed in a vial. Sampling of an arena was repeated until three consecutive samples produced no ticks. Separate pressboards were used for each treatment, and bonnets were laundered between sampling days to prevent cross-contamination of products.

Statistical Analysis

The mean total number of nymphal ticks calculated across replicate arenas per product were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Knockdown and residual treatments were analyzed separately. Differences in the mean number of ticks collected from each product were analyzed post-hoc using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD). All analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (v4.0.0; R Core Team 2024) and RStudio (Posit Team 2024).

Discussion

In the blacklegged nymph trials, NootkaSHIELD™ products containing 3% nootkatone did not perform as well as NootkaSHIELD™ products containing 10% nootkatone. Due to the relatively low knockdown efficacy of Evo A (3% nootkatone) in 2023 (26.9%) we chose not to evaluate it again in 2024. The other two products were more effective than Evo A (3%) in knockdown trials, with Evo B (3%) having an efficacy of 41.9% and Evo C (3%) having an efficacy of 43.6%. A field study testing 2% pure nootkatone found that 95% of both nymphal blacklegged and lone star ticks were controlled through 35 days (Jordan et al. 2011). The difference in efficacy despite the lower concentration (2% vs. 3%) of nootkatone could be due to the different formulations: 98.5% nootkatone crystals dissolved in 93% d-limonene, mixed with water and EZ-Mulse, compared to 3% nootkatone concentration mixed with an unspecified proprietary formulation in our study. It would be interesting to see if Evolva could increase the efficacy of the 3% NootkaSHIELD™ products by adjusting the formulations.

In contrast, Evo A (10%) and Evo C (10%) were highly effective for controlling nymphal blacklegged ticks in knockdown trials and displayed high residual activity. In 2024, Evo C (10%) demonstrated 94.2% efficacy in blacklegged tick knockdown trials and 85.0% efficacy in blacklegged tick residual trials. Further testing of Evo C (10%) in field settings should be performed to confirm whether it is consistent in controlling both species of ticks effectively. Evo A (10%) showed the highest efficacy among the products containing 10% nootkatone and consistently maintained this high level of efficacy in knockdown trials in 2023 (97.4%) and 2024 (98.8%), making it a strong candidate for further commercial development. The residual activity of Evo A (10%) was more varied, with 61.8% residual efficacy in 2023 and 82.5% efficacy in 2024. It would be helpful to perform additional residual efficacy treatments using Evo A (10%) to assess if any environmental or product-associated factors were potentially driving the observed variation in efficacy. In lone star tick field trials, Evo A (10%) was a bit less effective (81.2%) than Evo C (10%) (91%) but still significantly more effective than Evo A (Placebo) (38.5%), showing that the 10% concentration of nootkatone is likely having a significant acaricidal effect on nymphal lone star ticks.

The addition of a second arena combined with a three-layer barrier approach was successful for containing lone star nymphs in our open-air arenas and should be adopted for future study trials testing acaricides on lone star ticks in tick-enriched arenas. We recaptured 62% of lone star nymphs released in water control arenas (Table 4) which is comparable to the mean recovery of nymphal blacklegged ticks (58%) released in water control arenas (Table 3). Most of the recaptured live lone star nymphs found crawling on the arenas were either on the top rim of the lower arena or the bottom rim of the upper arena and appeared to be avoiding the sticky insect adhesive. Of the 1,000 nymphs released, only four were found stuck to the duct tape and one was found stuck in the adhesive.

An issue with the altered design was that removal of the adhesive and mounting tape was extremely messy and time-consuming. Vegetable oil and Goo Gone Goo & Adhesive Remover Spray Gel (Goo Gone, Gurnee, IL) were needed to remove the adhesive, and patience was required for removing the mounting tape. Future trials using this study system with lone star ticks may wish to find alternatives for the double-sided mounting tape. However, we chose to use this tape due to its ability to withstand rain, which it did during the experiment; it may be difficult to find another tape that can last outdoors for 28 days to complete assessment of both a knockdown and residual treatment.

The only currently commercially available MRP evaluated in this study, BugArmor Zero-Bite, did not show any significant tick-killing ability. Most MRPs previously assessed using the micro-arena system also did not display significant acaricidal activity, except for pyrethrin (EcoPCO EC-X) and one formulation of rosemary and peppermint oil, Essentria IC3, which exhibited a knockdown effect (53.8%) that was higher than any of the 3% NootkaShield™ products (Dyer et al. 2021). However, Essentria IC3 was discontinued and has since been replaced by Essentria IC Pro, which has not yet been formally assessed against ticks in the field. Given there are commercial products and products in development with higher proven efficacy, consumers may want to explore higher efficacy products for tick control (Eisen 2024).

Talstar® P, which contains 7.9% bifenthrin, is a pest control industry standard for tick control and was used as a positive control in this study. It displayed 100% knockdown efficacy and greater than 95% residual efficacy against blacklegged nymphs in both 2023 and 2024 trials, and 100% knockdown activity against lone star nymphs. Currently, it is considered an industry standard for perimeter treatments and residential tick control due to its high rate of efficacy and low cost. However, bifenthrin’s negative effects on non-target organisms like fish and bees may encourage the public to shift toward more environmentally friendly products, like the nootkatone-based products we tested.

Due to cross-Atlantic shipping issues, Evo A was the only group to be evaluated in 2023. The placebos for Evo B and C were not tested in either year, so further testing is still needed to properly determine that the nootkatone in the samples, rather than any of the other ingredients, are causing an acaricidal effect on blacklegged and lone star nymphal ticks.

Further work should aim to examine the efficacy of the NootkaSHIELD™ products for use against adult blacklegged ticks, and for use against nymphal and adult lone star ticks after the first two weeks. Additionally, due to the discrepant results of the Evo (A) placebo treatment between 2023 and 2024 trials, it might be worth further evaluating Nootkatone-free formulations (placebo) from the manufacturer. Overall, NootkaSHIELD™ experimental products containing 10% nootkatone exhibited high levels of efficacy in killing both blacklegged and lone star nymphal ticks, suggesting they are promising candidates for further development.
