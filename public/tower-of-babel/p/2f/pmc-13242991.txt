Voriconazole-Induced Agranulocytosis in a Cirrhotic Patient with Influenza-Associated Pulmonary Aspergillosis: A Case Report
Authors: Xiaofei Lin, Yubin Du, Hui Mai, Xiaoling Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe diagnosis and treatment of influenza-associated pulmonary aspergillosis (IAPA) present significant challenges, and voriconazole, as the first-line treatment for IAPA, rarely causes the serious adverse event of agranulocytosis. We first report a case of voriconazole-associated agranulocytosis in a patient with IAPA complicated by cirrhosis and systematically describe the complete process of diagnosis, treatment, and adverse event management. A 68-year-old male with a history of liver cirrhosis presented with cough, dyspnea, and fever. Testing confirmed influenza A, and chest computed tomography (CT) showed diffuse bilateral pulmonary inflammation. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) and culture detected Aspergillus fumigatus, confirming the diagnosis of IAPA. The patient received voriconazole. On Day 20 of hospitalization, agranulocytosis developed and resolved after voriconazole discontinuation and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) administration, consistent with voriconazole-associated agranulocytosis. After neutrophil recovery, voriconazole was resumed with leukocyte support and close monitoring of complete blood counts (CBC) and drug levels. At one-month follow-up, no recurrence of infection or agranulocytosis was observed. This case emphasizes the value of mNGS in timely diagnosis of IAPA, underscores the importance of closely monitoring CBC in such patients during triazole antifungal therapy, and proves the feasibility of resuming antifungal treatment-including the cautious re-administration of the initially sensitizing agent under strict monitoring-after the correction of agranulocytosis. These findings contribute to a better understanding of the disease and may help to optimize its clinical management.Keywords: influenza-associated pulmonary aspergillosis, metagenomic next-generation sequencing, voriconazole, adverse event, agranulocytosis

Background

Among 453 confirmed species, the genus Aspergillus includes Aspergillus fumigatus is the major global etiological agent of invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA).1 IPA has traditionally been considered a complication of severely immunocompromised patients. However, with improved diagnostic sensitivity, its occurrence in immunocompetent hosts has gained increasing attention.2,3 Influenza virus infection has emerged as a distinct risk factor, and the resulting clinical syndrome is defined as influenza-associated pulmonary aspergillosis (IAPA).4,5 IAPA represents a highly lethal secondary fungal infection in severe influenza patients, with mortality rates exceeding those of influenza alone by more than twofold.6 Consequently, early recognition and diagnosis are critical. However, the clinical and radiographic manifestations of IAPA often lack specificity, making diagnosis highly dependent on pathogenetic evidence. Current diagnostic methods include galactomannan (GM) testing in blood or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples, Aspergillus-specific PCR, direct microscopy, and culture.7 In recent years, metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has gained increasing application as a non-culture-based, broad-spectrum pathogen detection technique. Growing evidence indicates mNGS holds significant value for the early and rapid diagnosis of IAPA.8,9

Once IAPA is suspected, antifungal therapy should be initiated immediately. As the first-line treatment, therapeutic drug monitoring of voriconazole is necessary. It can predict treatment efficacy, reduce the incidence of adverse events, and potentially improve clinical outcomes.10 Voriconazole is associated with various adverse events, including common toxicities such as hepatotoxicity, cardiotoxicity, and neurotoxicity, as well as rare but severe bone marrow suppression such as agranulocytosis. Limited clinical reports exist, and voriconazole-associated agranulocytosis in IAPA has not been reported.11 Notably, voriconazole exhibits a high discontinuation rate (as high as 37.7%) due to adverse events in clinical practice.12 Discontinuation may increase the risk of infection recurrence and adversely affect patient prognosis. Therefore, balancing treatment benefits against risks while effectively managing adverse events and exploring subsequent therapeutic strategies represent significant clinical challenges. This case report presents the first documented instance of agranulocytosis secondary to IAPA in a patient with cirrhosis, confirming that the agranulocytosis was associated with voriconazole therapy, and systematically describes the complete process of diagnosis, treatment, and adverse event management, aiming to increase awareness of this disease among clinicians.

Case Presentation

A 68-year-old retired male worker presented to our hospital’s emergency department on January 23, 2025, with a 12-day history of cough, dyspnea, and fever. He had a history of alcoholic cirrhosis. He tested positive for influenza A antigen in another medical facility 12 days before admission to our hospital. Despite receiving oseltamivir and symptomatic treatment, he experienced recurrent fever. Blood gas analysis at our emergency department revealed a partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) of 60.2 mmHg and a partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2) of 25.0 mmHg. A complete blood count (CBC) showed a white blood cell (WBC) count of 7.66×109/L, a neutrophil count of 5.30×109/L, a neutrophil percentage of 69%, and a C-reactive protein level of 108.1 mg/L (Table 1). Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed the diffuse bilateral pulmonary inflammation involving multiple lobes (Figure 1).

Upon transfer to our department on the same day, the patient was conscious and alert. The physical examinations were as follows: temperature, 38.3°C; pulse rate, 84 beats per minute; respiratory rate, 20 breaths per minute; blood pressure, 141/66 mmHg; fingertip pulse oximetry saturation, 99% (on 4 L/min nasal cannula oxygen). No palpable superficial lymph node enlargement was noted throughout the body. Coarse breath sounds were present in both lungs, accompanied by minimal moist rales and wheezing. Heart rate was 84 beats per minute with a regular rhythm. No murmurs were heard in any cardiac auscultation area.

On Day 1 of hospitalization, intravenous moxifloxacin (400 mg once a day) and piperacillin-tazobactam (4.5 g every 8 hours) were initiated, along with hepatoprotective therapy. But the patient has a persistent low-grade fever, indicating our treatment failure. To identify the pathogen, the patient underwent bronchoscopy on Day 3 of hospitalization, which revealed bronchial inflammatory changes. Concurrently, a BALF sample was collected for mNGS, which was performed on the NextSeq 2000 platform following the standard Illumina sequencing protocol. mNGS results identified Aspergillus fumigatus (sequence number 955), Aspergillus flavus (sequence number 120), and Influenza A virus (sequence number 899). BALF culture revealed Aspergillus fumigatus growth. Serum (1-3)-β-D-glucan level was 273.0619 pg/mL (normal value <60 pg/mL). Based on influenza diagnosis, pulmonary infiltrates, and positive BALF culture, the patient qualified as a probable case of IAPA on Day 4 of hospitalization.8,13 On the day of diagnosis, the patient was administered oral baloxavir marboxil (40 mg once). Intravenous voriconazole was commenced at a loading dose (400 mg every 12 hours for two doses), after which the regimen was reduced to a maintenance dose (200 mg every 12 hours) on Day 5 of hospitalization (Figure 2). The patient’s temperature normalized, and subsequently, laboratory results demonstrated the improvement in inflammation and organ function (Table 1). Throughout this period, the patient maintained normal neutrophil counts and therapeutic voriconazole concentrations (Table 1). On Day 11 of hospitalization, the regimen was switched to oral voriconazole (200 mg every 12 hours).

Timeline of the patient’s clinical course and treatment.

On Day 20 of hospitalization, the patient developed a fever again with a temperature of 39.7°C. CBC showed a WBC count of 1.15×109/L, a neutrophil count of 0.00×109/L, a neutrophil percentage of 1.7%, and a C-reactive protein level of 59.8 mg/L (Table 1). A chest CT scan revealed reduced scattered inflammation in both lungs compared with previous imaging (Figure 1). Voriconazole anti-infective therapy was discontinued, and subcutaneous injections of “granulocyte colony-stimulating factor” (G-CSF) were immediately initiated. Daily CBC was monitored. During this period, the patient experienced repeated fevers. On Day 25 of hospitalization, CBC showed a WBC count of 13.25×109/L, a neutrophil count of 9.81×109/L, a neutrophil percentage of 74.0%, and a C-reactive protein level of 83.5 mg/L. Subcutaneous G-CSF injection was discontinued, and the patient did not recur the fever. Based on the patient’s clinical symptoms, physical examination, and laboratory analysis, it is considered to be voriconazole-associated agranulocytosis. At this point, the patient’s antifungal treatment regimen was insufficient, presenting a therapeutic dilemma. While considering the re-initiation of antifungal therapy, we are also concerned about the recurrence of agranulocytosis. Therefore, On Day 25 of hospitalization, a repeat bronchoscopy was carried out, and the BALF sample was collected for mNGS. The mNGS results identified Enterococcus faecalis (sequence number 144), Acinetobacter baumannii (sequence number 47), Enterobacter cloacae complex (sequence number 11), and Aspergillus fumigatus (sequence number 7). And due to financial difficulty, the patient refused to switch from voriconazole to itraconazole. Oral voriconazole was resumed at a dose of 200 mg every 12 hours, together with linezolid (600 mg every 12 hours) for infection management. Concurrently, diyu shengbai tablets were administered to elevate white blood cell counts, along with hepatoprotective agents. On Day 34 of hospitalization, chest CT showed nearly complete resolution of diffuse bilateral inflammation with only minimal residual changes (Figure 1), accompanied by overall normalization of inflammatory markers and organ function indicators (Table 1). The patient was discharged on Day 37 of hospitalization in stable condition, presenting no significant cough, sputum production, fever, or dyspnea. The patient was in generally good condition with no recurrence of fever at the follow-up visit two weeks and one month after discharge. Follow-up CBC and liver function tests were basically normal.

Discussion

IPA is a severe and life-threatening disease when Aspergillus conidia are inhaled into the respiratory tract and invade the airways or lung tissue.14 IPA predominantly affects immunocompromised patients (chronic neutropenia, hereditary or acquired immunodeficiency, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, or lung transplantation). Increasingly, however, focus is being placed on IPA patients who lack traditional risk factors-such as those with severe respiratory viral infections, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, liver failure, or other critical conditions.4 For instance, our patient presented with both liver cirrhosis and influenza virus infection. The failure of both antiviral and empirical antimicrobial therapy should raise strong suspicion for IPA. Liver cirrhosis or advanced liver disease increases the risk of IPA infection due to immune deficiency and “immune paralysis”, which diminish the liver’s surveillance function against various pathogens, including fungi, as well as the use of corticosteroids in treatment.15–17 Influenza has been confirmed as a risk factor for IPA in immunocompetent patients,5,6 potentially due to reduced immune recognition of Aspergillus following influenza infection.18,19

Influenza causes approximately 650,000 deaths annually, while IAPA represents a severe complication characterized by histological evidence of fungal hyphal invasion into lung tissue. Among influenza patients, IAPA has a prevalence of approximately 20%, with in-hospital mortality rates reaching up to 50%.19,20 Notably, the diagnosis of IAPA is challenging, which would significantly impact the disease prognosis. Current case definitions for IAPA predominantly adopt diagnostic criteria proposed by international experts in 2020.21 The diagnosis of IAPA primarily relies on pulmonary histopathology, but its clinical application is limited because many patients cannot tolerate the tissue biopsy. The definition of probable IAPA is as follows: (1) Confirmed influenza with pulmonary infiltrates, meeting at least one of the following criteria: serum GM index > 0.5, or BALF GM index ≥ 1.0, or positive BALF culture; (2) Influenza confirmed by cavitary infiltrates (not attributable to other causes), meeting at least one of the following: positive sputum or tracheal aspirate culture. This case meets all three criteria for influenza diagnosis, pulmonary infiltrates, and positive BALF culture, thus qualifying as a probable IAPA. Since the culture process is time-consuming and has low sensitivity,7 we initiated antifungal therapy upon detection of Aspergillus by mNGS, rather than waiting for a positive culture result from BALF. Currently, early clinical evidence for IAPA primarily relies on GM testing in serum and BALF. However, studies indicate that serum and BALF GM testing exhibit lower sensitivity and specificity in immunocompetent patients-such as the patient in this case.22

mNGS is an emerging clinical microbiology testing method that is capable of analyzing nucleic acid sequences of microbial pathogens in patients’ respiratory or blood samples, and it is widely used in clinical practice nowadays.23 Although mNGS has yet to be incorporated into the formal diagnostic criteria for IAPA, a growing body of evidence suggests that it can facilitate early diagnosis. Its reported sensitivity and negative predictive value may even surpass those of conventional microbiological tests.8,9,24 One study found that BALF mNGS demonstrated a sensitivity of 92.31% for diagnosing IPA in immunocompetent patients.9 Our case further corroborates this perspective, demonstrating that mNGS provides diagnostic information earlier than BALF culture. Given the challenges posed by the limited sensitivity and early diagnostic difficulty of traditional methods for IAPA, mNGS may provide clinicians with a valuable supplemental tool.

Voriconazole and itraconazole are both first-line treatments for IAPA.21 Clinically, once highly suspected, they should be initiated as early as possible with strict therapeutic drug monitoring. Voriconazole has a broad range of adverse events, with visual disturbances and hepatotoxicity being the most common. Skin reactions, neurological symptoms, gastrointestinal discomfort, and bone disorders have also been reported. In contrast, agranulocytosis is a rare adverse events.25,26 Given the patient’s underlying liver cirrhosis, intensive therapeutic drug monitoring was initiated concurrently with voriconazole treatment. Close attention was paid to the risk of hepatotoxicity, supplemented throughout with hepatoprotective therapy, which maintained stable liver function. However, on Day 20 of hospitalization, a follow-up CBC revealed agranulocytosis. Following discontinuation of voriconazole and administration of G-CSF, the patient’s neutrophil count rapidly returned to normal and remained within the normal range during subsequent follow-up. After excluding other potential causes such as hematologic disorders and rheumatic/immune diseases, we determined this agranulocytosis to be an adverse event to voriconazole. Literature indicates this represents a voriconazole-induced leukemoid reaction.27 Notably, the patient’s underlying cirrhosis may have been a risk factor for this severe adverse event, as studies confirm that hepatic impairment increases the incidence of voriconazole-related adverse events.28,29 To our knowledge, this represents the first reported case of neutropenia following voriconazole use in a patient with cirrhosis and IAPA. This case highlights the unique risks associated with voriconazole use in this specific population, providing important evidence to deepen clinicians’ understanding of the comprehensive management of IAPA and the potential for serious adverse events associated with triazole medications.

Available evidence indicates that voriconazole treatment discontinuation rates due to drug-related adverse events can reach up to 37.7%.12 However, IAPA typically requires continuous antifungal therapy for 6–12 weeks, and premature discontinuation is significantly associated with disease recurrence risk-with reports indicating that over 40% of pulmonary aspergillosis patients experience recurrence within 6 months after stopping antifungal treatment.30 This case similarly encountered this clinical dilemma: the patient developed agranulocytosis following voriconazole therapy. After neutrophil counts recovered, we adopted a strategy of restarting antifungal treatment while continuing the leukocyte-enhancing treatment. Although literature suggests itraconazole carries a lower risk of drug-related adverse events than voriconazole and may serve as a safer alternative,31 switching medications was not feasible due to the patient’s financial constraints. Therefore, under close monitoring of CBC and drug concentrations, we continued voriconazole and completed the full course of treatment. Follow-up confirmed the strategy’s efficacy, with no recurrence of infection or reappearance of bone marrow suppression. This case provides clinical reference for subsequent antifungal therapy following voriconazole-induced agranulocytosis, demonstrating that continuing the original drug to complete the full course of treatment may be a viable clinical management pathway under adequate hematopoietic support and therapeutic drug monitoring.

Conclusions

In summary, this case first reports voriconazole-associated agranulocytosis in a patient with cirrhosis complicated by IAPA and systematically outlines the diagnostic process and management strategy. This case confirms that mNGS is an effective auxiliary detection method for IAPA and demonstrates the feasibility of restarting antifungal therapy (including reintroduction of the original sensitizing agent under close monitoring) after correction of agranulocytosis. It emphasizes the need for heightened vigilance regarding the potential for severe myelosuppression when using triazole antifungals to treat IAPA, particularly in special populations such as those with concomitant hepatic impairment. This case provides important practical guidance for managing such complex scenarios and holds positive implications for enhancing the comprehensive diagnosis and treatment of IAPA.
