Veiled in Gynecomastia: A B3 breast lesion in a male patient with subsequent “upgrade” to invasive malignancy
Authors: Isobel Hatrick, Kirsten Stafford, Ritu Chhikara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
B3 breast lesions are a heterogeneous group with uncertain malignant potential. While female-specific guidelines often recommend vacuum-assisted excision (VAE) followed by surveillance, these strategies are less applicable in male patients. This case report highlights a male patient initially diagnosed with a B3 lesion—specifically atypical intraductal epithelial proliferation (AIDEP)—that was later “upgraded” to invasive carcinoma and ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) following surgical excision. The case illustrates the limitations of current protocols when applied to men and supports considering surgical excision as a first-line management strategy in high-risk B3 lesions. It also highlights the potential diagnostic value of mammography in high-risk males, particularly when ultrasound findings are inconclusive. Overall, this report emphasizes the need for male-specific, evidence-based guidelines to ensure timely and accurate diagnosis and treatment of B3 breast lesions in men.

Introduction

B3 lesions comprise of a small group of breast diseases with uncertain and differing malignant transformation potentials [1,2]. The risk of malignant transformation and “upgrade” to malignancy of the different B3 lesions varies greatly [[1], [2], [3]], yet the current UK guidance in is for vacuum assisted excision (VAE) [4,5] regardless on the subtype of the B3 lesion and there is no consensus on the management of B3 lesions in men; currently the guidance is the same as for women. VAE in males is not possible due to due the small volume of breast tissue therefore surgical excision is the inevitable treatment option.

We know that atypical intraductal epithelial proliferation (AIDEP) has one of the highest risks of “upgrade” on further tissue sampling and a high risk of malignant transformation [1,3,[5], [6], [7]]. This case demonstrates the management of AIDEP in a male detected on a core biopsy, which was upgrade to invasive malignancy and ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) at wide local excision. It opens up discussion on current UK guidance on the management of B3 lesions in women and demonstrates the importance of recognizing the different risks of malignant “upgrade” and transformation in these B3 lesions and their subsequent management.

Case presentation

A 75-year-old man was referred to the breast clinic by his GP with a few weeks' history of a right breast lump. There was no associated nipple discharge or left breast changes. Relevant in his past medical history was a history of prostate cancer in 2008; whilst undergoing treatment he developed bilateral gynecomastia which was treated with radiotherapy. He also had atrial fibrillation and had previously had a non-ST elevation myocardial infarction and mumps. His current medication included metoprolol, allopurinol, rivaroxaban, simvastatin and lansoprazole. He was a non-smoker and consumed moderate volumes of alcohol. There was no family history of breast or ovarian cancer. On examination, he had bilateral gynecomastia with an indeterminate tender lump in the right breast. There was no axillary lymphadenopathy and abdominal and testicular examination where unremarkable.

He was referred to the breast radiology department for an ultrasound which demonstrated mild bilateral gynecomastia. In the right breast there was a more focal region of hypoechogenicity measuring 23 mm which corresponded with the palpable lump on examination (Fig. 1). The core biopsy samples were then sent for histology which demonstrated AIDEP, a B3 lesion, with no invasive malignancy observed. After discussion at the breast MDT, the decision was made to perform a wide local excision as further methods of methods of obtaining more tissue, such as a VAE or VAB (vacuum assisted excision or vacuum assisted biopsy), were not considered in a male patient.

He proceeded to surgery and a WLE (wide local excision) was performed. The histology from the specimen came back as containing an 11 mm grade 3 invasive mammary carcinoma amidst high grade solid and comedo DCIS. There was no lymphovascular invasion however the margins of the specimen were involved by the tumor. The tumor was oestrogen and progesterone receptor positive and HER2 negative (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Histopathology from the wide local excision. The case was then again discussed at the breast MDT and the decision was made to proceed with a completion mastectomy and a sentinel lymph node biopsy. An US of the axilla was also performed which demonstrated sonographically normal lymph nodes.

He proceeded with a completion mastectomy and sentinel lymph node biopsy which showed 4 mm of residual grade 3 invasive mammary carcinoma adjacent to the previous WLE cavity, which was subsequently completely excised by at least 5 mm. 4 lymph nodes were sampled, all of which were free of any metastases. His overall staging was therefore pT1c N0.

Genetic testing was performed which was negative for pathogenic mutations. In addition, Prosigna was performed on the specimen as per guidelines which came back as Luminal B type with a high risk of recurrence of 26% at 10 years and with a ROR (risk of recurrence) score of 76.

In light of this, adjuvant chemotherapy was commenced consisting of 3 cycles of docetaxel and 3 cycles of epirubicin and cyclophosphamide as well as hormonal treatment and zoledronic acid.

The surveillance plan decided on was a mammogram of the contralateral left breast for 5 years, as per guidance for women. Discussion

The management of B3 lesions is complex and under constant debate for several reasons. Firstly, they comprise a heterogenous group of lesions either with or without atypia. A few examples include, atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH), also referred to as AIDEP, flat epithelial atypia (FEA), lobular neoplasia (LN), including invasive lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS), radial scars and papillary lesions [1,2]. Secondly, all these lesions have different risks of “upgrade” to malignancy on further tissue sampling and also carry a varying overall risk of malignant transformation. The percentage of B3 lesions with immediate “upgrade” on further tissue sampling varies greatly between different studies. One study demonstrated the risk to be as high as 22.7% [1]. However, another study demonstrated the percentage “upgrade” to be lower at 2.8% [4]. A further study evaluated the different risk of “upgrade” to either DCIS or invasive mammary carcinoma on further tissue sampling to be as high as 9.4% and 4.7% respectively [3].

Many studies that have assessed the “upgrade” to malignancy rates of different B3 lesions after surgical excision and results suggest that AIDEP are considered B3 lesions with one of the highest rates of “upgrade” to malignancy on further tissue sampling [3,[5], [6], [7]]. A study composed of 113 patients diagnosed with AIDEP on core biopsy found that the “upgrade” rate on diagnostic excision to be 28.3%, of which 6% were “upgraded” to invasive cancer and 22.1% DCIS. In this study, 81 patients were not “upgraded” however underwent 5 years of mammographic surveillance of which 9.88% subsequently received a breast cancer diagnosis [6]. A further study performed by Renshaw and Gould demonstrated an “upgrade” rate of 30.3% with AIDEP [7].

Another single institution study analyzed 192 B3 lesions initially diagnosed on primary tissue sampling that subsequently underwent surgical excision and demonstrated that the “upgrade” after further tissue sampling of AIDEP was as high as 28.6% compared to 0% for RS [3]. A meta-analysis found that the total “upgrade” of AIDEP was 22%. The variation in “upgrade” to malignancy demonstrated in the above studies may be due to the histopathological uncertainty between low grade DCIS and AIDEP. The histological difference is dependent on the size of the changes seen, which on core biopsies and vacuum assisted biopsies (VABs) is difficult to determine [5].

Currently there is no uniform consensus for the management of these B3 lesions regarding whether these lesions should be treated with surgical excision or vacuum assisted excision (VAE). Guidelines recommended by AGO (Arbeitsgemeinschaft Gynäkologische Onkologie, German Gynaecogical Oncology Group) Task Force and The Second International Consensus Conference on B3 lesions, advice surgical excision as the first option to pursue after a B3 lesion is identified after a core biopsy [5]. In contrast, the UK National Health Service Breast Screening Programme Working Group recommended excision under VAE, after which, provided there is no evidence of malignancy on VAE, these patients are followed up with annual mammographic surveillance [4,5].

In June 2023, at the 3rd International consensus conference of B3 lesions, the majority of panellists recommended surgical excision after a diagnosis of ADH/AIDEP or Phyllodes tumors on core biopsy. For other B3 lesions VAE was recommended [8]. Furthermore, a recent meta-analysis attempted to determine factors that were associated with a low “upgrade” rate however again, the conclusion advised surgical excision for all AIDEP/ADH [9].

In this case discussed above, as part of the current UK guidance, despite the AIDEP being identified on core biopsy, this patient would routinely have been managed with VAE if he had been a female. As a male, he proceeded to surgical excision which identified an 11 mm grade 3 invasive mammary carcinoma and DCIS. This case is an example of a AIDEP B3 lesion being “upgraded” on further tissue sampling.

We know that male breast cancer is rare, accounting for 1%-2% of all breast cancers around the world [10] and that DCIS is even more rare, accounting for 5%-7% of all male breast cancers [11]. There are only a few documented case reports of isolated DCIS [[11], [12], [13]]. Risk factors for male breast cancer include age, family history, obesity, genetic mutations and conditions that induce a high oestrogen state [12,14]. In this case, the patient had significant risk factors having previously been treated for prostate cancer, developing gynecomastia during the process, and subsequently receiving radiation therapy to the breasts.

The diagnosis and management of male breast cancer is conferred by research in women [15]. In addition, the imaging guidance for assessing male breast symptoms is variable, often resulting in an inconsistent approach [16]. In the case discussed here, US was the first line modality used to assess the breast lump, with no mammography performed pre-operatively. Current UK guidance would advise that in men with unexplained or a suspicious unilateral breast lump US should be performed in men below the age of 50 and above the age of 50, mammography or ultrasound should be considered. Subsequent core biopsy is suggested in patients with P3-5 or U3-5 findings [17]. Given the age of the patient discussed in the case report, the first line imaging modality following UK guidance would have been mammography. Perhaps calcification would have been seen on the mammogram raising suspicion of DCIS or malignancy, not appreciable on the US. A retrospective review of 1362 male patients attending the Cambridge Breast Unit was performed which demonstrated a sensitivity of 84.6% for mammography compared to 78.6% with US, evidence which supports the current UK guidance. Both mammography and US demonstrated high specificity (99.4% and 98.9% respectively), PPV and NPV [16]. The American College of Radiology would also advise mammography for men over the age of 25 with consideration of further imaging with US [14].

From our literature search, there are very few case reports on ADH/AIDEP in males and no documented examples of AIDEP in males with subsequent “upgrade” to invasive mammary carcinoma and DCIS. There is also no specific guidance on the management of B3 lesions in male patients specifically. Given the lack of breast tissue in men, a surgical excision was performed over the usual UK guidance of VAE in this case [5].

This case highlights the caution that needs to be taken when assessing breast lumps in men, particularly with significant risk factors such as prior breast radiotherapy. The B3 lesion was “upgraded” to invasive mammary carcinoma with DCIS, which was not identified on the primary core biopsy. No mammographic imaging was performed prior to surgical excision. The case raises the question as to whether mammography should be considered in these patients in with significant risk factors – would the DCIS have been visible on mammogram? In addition, traditionally as per current UK practice, women with AIDEP/ADH on core biopsy would proceed to VAE, which was not possible in this case, hence surgical excision was advised. The literature reviewed in this case report suggests that in cases of AIDEP/ADH, given the high risk of “upgrade” to malignancy, surgical excision should be the first line management over VAE. Should we therefore be considering excision biopsy for all AIDEP/ADH cases in both men and women going forward?

Conclusion

Care needs to be taken when using US to assess unilateral breast lumps in men with significant risk factors to ensure that amidst the gynecomastia, there is not a discrete breast lesion. This case highlights a possible advantage of using 2 methods of imaging, US and mammography. Would mammography have detected calcification not visible on US resulting in a change of management? And finally, current UK guidance on the management of B3 lesions, regardless of their subtype, is for VAE. On reviewing the literature, the consensus appears to be that management of AIDEP/ADH, given their high risk of malignant “upgrade,” should be surgical excision.
