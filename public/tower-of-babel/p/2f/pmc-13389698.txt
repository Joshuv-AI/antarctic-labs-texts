Orbitofrontal noradrenaline supports adaptive learning-rate adjustment in probabilistic reversal learning
Authors: Hadrien Plat, Coline Chevallier, Alessandro Piccin, Alain R. Marchand, Jérémie Naudé, Etienne Coutureau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceAdaptive behavior in uncertain and changing environments requires adjusting how strongly new evidence influences learning. Classical reinforcement-learning models assume a fixed learning rate, whereas recent work proposes that learning speed should adapt to environmental statistics. Rats’ behavior in a probabilistic two-armed bandit task was poorly captured by fixed-learning-rate models but was better explained by adaptive models in which learning rates adjust to estimated stochasticity and volatility. Noradrenergic release in the orbitofrontal cortex tracked trial-by-trial volatility estimates, whereas selective inhibition of locus coeruleus-to-orbitofrontal cortex projections reproduced the behavioral deficits predicted by disruption of this adaptive learning-rate regulation. Together, these findings identify a circuit mechanism supporting adaptive learning in uncertain environments.

Adaptive behavior requires anticipating the likely consequences of possible actions and updating those expectations when circumstances change. When the statistical structure of the environment shifts over time, successful adaptation requires monitoring whether observed outcomes deviate from expectations and revising action–outcome (A–O) associations accordingly (1–3). Many real-world environments are both stochastic, in that outcomes are noisy, and volatile, in that contingencies can change suddenly. These two forms of uncertainty place opposing demands on learning: Stochasticity should slow updating to avoid overreacting to noise, whereas volatility should accelerate updating so that recent outcomes exert greater influence (4–6). The challenge is to identify when variability reflects a true shift rather than background noise. Thus, adaptive behavior requires balancing stability and flexibility as a function of environmental uncertainty.

Classical reinforcement-learning (RL) models typically use a fixed learning rate that determines how strongly new outcomes influence value estimates (7). In contrast, recent adaptive or meta-RL frameworks propose that the learning rate itself is dynamically regulated according to internal estimates of stochasticity and volatility, decreasing when outcomes are noisy and increasing when evidence suggests that contingencies have changed (1, 3, 8–11). These models provide a principled account of how learning speed could adjust to the demands of a dynamic environment. However, the neural mechanisms through which such volatility- and stochasticity-dependent learning-rate modulation is implemented remain unclear.

Neuromodulatory systems are well positioned to support metalearning, that is, the dynamic adjustment of learning rates, through their widespread projections to cortical and subcortical regions (10–13). Among them, the locus coeruleus–noradrenaline (LC-NA) system has been proposed to signal unexpected events and shifts in task state (14), thereby facilitating behavioral adaptation when environmental contingencies change (12, 15–18). Recent work suggests that LC activity correlates with learning rates and uncertainty estimates in humans (6, 19, 20) and with flexibility in rodents (21). Pharmacological studies in humans further suggest that noradrenergic signaling may modulate how strongly new outcomes update beliefs in volatile environments (22–24).

The LC is the primary source of NA in the forebrain and provides dense innervation to prefrontal regions, including the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) (25–31). The OFC has been critically involved in updating A–O associations to support flexible, goal-directed behavior (31–35). These anatomical and functional properties position the LC→OFC pathway as a strong candidate to mediate adaptive learning under uncertainty, which we examined using a probabilistic reversal learning task that captures the demands of learning in uncertain and changing conditions.

To address this question, we combined computational modeling, fiber photometry recordings, and pathway-specific chemogenetic manipulations in rats to dissect the contribution of OFC noradrenergic signaling to flexible learning. Chemogenetic inhibition of OFC neurons selectively impaired postreversal adaptation, and modeling indicated that this behavioral phenotype was best accounted for by a reduction in learning rate. By varying outcome stochasticity, we found that rats’ behavior was better captured by an adaptive RL model in which learning rates adjusted according to model-estimated stochasticity and volatility than by fixed-rate models. Fiber photometry revealed that NA release in the OFC around reversals closely reflected model-estimated volatility. Finally, selective inhibition of LC→OFC projections reproduced the behavioral deficits predicted when this adaptive learning-rate adjustment was disrupted, with effects emerging in probabilistic but not deterministic conditions. Together, these results suggest that noradrenergic signaling in the OFC contributes to behavioral flexibility through learning-rate adjustment based on internal estimates of volatility.

Results

Rats Learn a History-Dependent Strategy in a Probabilistic Reversal Task.

To investigate how rats use reward information to guide decisions under uncertainty, we implemented a two-armed bandit probabilistic reversal-learning (PRL) task (Fig. 1A). On each trial, rats chose between two levers associated with complementary reward probabilities (P(high) = 1 − P(low)). Whenever the high-probability lever was selected on eight consecutive trials, independently of reward delivery, reward contingencies reversed, prompting animals to adjust their choices to changing outcomes (Fig. 1B).

This task exposes animals to two forms of uncertainty: volatility, arising from changing reward contingencies, and stochasticity, arising from probabilistic outcomes. In an RL framework, this creates a trade-off where value estimates must be updated at a rate that avoids overreacting to noise while remaining sensitive to genuine change. Expressed in a standard Q-learning model, a low learning rate α leads to slow adjustment after reversals, whereas a high α makes behavior overly sensitive to noise and promotes erratic switching (Fig. 1C). These model predictions suggest that rats would benefit from adjusting their learning rate to environmental statistics.

We examined this possibility by first training rats on 80/20 contingency (P(high) = 0.8, P(low) = 0.2). Performance improved from naive to intermediate and expert stages, reflected by a progressive increase in the proportion of choices to the high-probability lever (Friedman test: χ2(2) = 44.33, P < 0.0001; Fig. 1D). This improvement was accompanied by fewer consecutive errors following reversals (Friedman test: χ2(2) = 20.58, P < 0.0001; Fig. 1E) and faster postreversal adaptation (two-way RM ANOVA: Training stage, F(1,23) = 150.2, P < 0.0001; Fig. 1F), indicating that rats became more proficient at extracting relevant information from noisy feedback.

Rats responded quickly (latency = 0.672 ± 0.062 s), with reaction times influenced by training stage (Fig. 1G). To quantify how past events shaped choice behavior, we applied a logistic regression predicting stay/switch decisions from reward outcomes over recent trials (Fig. 1H; see Materials and Methods). Positive coefficients indicate a tendency to repeat the previous choice, whereas negative coefficients indicate switching. Expert rats displayed strong positive weights following rewarded trials and strong negative weights following unrewarded trials, indicating sensitivity to feedback across multiple past trials, whereas naive rats showed no significant influence of past outcomes on their decisions.

To disentangle the influence of past outcomes and past choices on switching, we computed the conditional probability of switching for all possible two-trial choice-outcome sequences preceding the current decision (Fig. 1I). The empirical frequency of each sequence is provided in SI Appendix, Table S1. Naive rats increased switching primarily when they had switched on the previous trial, independently of whether recent trials were rewarded or not (three-way RM ANOVA: Actiont-1, F(1,23) = 73.93, P < 0.0001; Outcomet-1, F(1,23) = 0.4672, P = 0.501; Outcomet−2, F(1,23) = 0.5010, P = 0.486; three-way interaction, F(1,23) = 6.41, P = 0.0186; Fig. 1J). By contrast, expert rats used both recent choice and outcome history to guide switching (Fig. 1J), with significant main effects and an interaction between these factors (three-way RM ANOVA: Actiont-1, F(1,23) = 128.8, P < 0.0001; Outcomet-1, F(1,23) = 956.3, P < 0.0001; Outcomet−2, F(1,23) = 5.997, P = 0.0224; three-way interaction, F(1,23) = 9.553, P = 0.0052). This emergence of structured, history-dependent switching indicates that rats learned to use recent feedback to guide behavior.

Inhibiting OFC Neurons Disrupts Learning Rate During Probabilistic Reversal-Learning.

To test whether the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) contributes to adaptive learning in uncertain environments, we chemogenetically inhibited CaMKII+ neurons in the ventral and lateral OFC (VO/LO) using an AAV8-CaMKIIα-hM4Di-mCherry construct (Fig. 2 A and B). After training on the 80/20 schedule, rats were tested under vehicle (VEH) and DCZ inhibition conditions (Materials and Methods).

DCZ treatment did not affect the total number of reversals completed (paired t test: t(11) = 0.6606, P = 0.5224; Fig. 2C), but silencing OFC neurons significantly impaired adaptation after reversals while sparing performance during stable periods (two-way RM ANOVA: treatment × phase, F(1,11) = 17.00, P = 0.0017; post hoc: postreversal, P = 0.0005; stable, P = 0.83; Fig. 2 D and E). This impairment was accompanied by an increase in consecutive errors following reversals (paired t test: t(11) = 4.52, P = 0.0009; Fig. 2F), indicating greater perseveration. In addition to initial perseveration on the previously high-reward lever, rats also showed increased switching away from the new high-probability lever during the postreversal period (paired t test: t(11) = 2.826, P = 0.0165; Fig. 2G), consistent with impaired updating following reversals.

Conditional switching analyses further showed that OFC inhibition altered how rats used recent choice-outcome information (Fig. 2H): rats were less likely to switch after an Aa sequence (paired t test: t(11) = 2.451, P = 0.0322) and more likely to switch after aB sequences (paired Wilcoxon test: P = 0.0161). To determine whether this phenotype was better explained by reduced learning from outcomes or altered perseveration, we implemented an RL model augmented with a choice kernel capturing perseveration (37), parameterized by learning rate α, inverse temperature β, and kernel amplitude κ and decay τ (Fig. 2I). We fit the four-parameter model to VEH data and then asked which single parameter change best reproduced the DCZ phenotype while holding the others fixed (Fig. 2 J–M). A reduction in α provided the best overall account: It reproduced the unchanged number of reversals completed (Fig. 2J), the increase in perseverative errors (Fig. 2M), and the selective impairment in postreversal adaptation while sparing stable-period performance (Fig. 2 K and L). Although increasing κ also reproduced the increase in perseverative errors and postreversal impairment, it incorrectly predicted more reversals completed per 100 trials (Fig. 2J). Changes in β or τ likewise failed to capture the overall pattern (Fig. 2 J–M). Thus, even when perseveration is incorporated into the model, OFC inhibition is best explained by a reduction in learning rate rather than by altered choice stochasticity or perseverative tendencies.

Rats Adaptively Adjust Learning Rates Across Stochasticity Levels Through Internal Estimates of Stochasticity and Volatility.

To further determine how the OFC contributes to learning regulation, we next investigated how rats adjust their learning rates across different levels of outcome stochasticity. We reasoned that learning and adaptation should accelerate as outcomes become more reliable. We formalized this prediction in a standard RL model, showing that there is an optimal learning rate α that maximizes reward in the 80/20 PRL task (Fig. 3B). Extending this analysis across contingencies revealed that the optimal α increased with reward predictability, with less stochastic environments favoring higher optimal learning rates (Fig. 3C). The model thus suggests that rats’ learning rates would benefit from adjusting to the statistical structure of the environment, enabling efficient adaptation to genuine change while minimizing overreactions to noise.

To test this prediction, we manipulated outcome stochasticity across different reward schedules (70/30, 80/20, 90/10, and 100/0) in the same rats (n = 24; Fig. 3A; see Materials and Methods). Performance improved as stochasticity decreased, reflected by a higher likelihood of selecting the advantageous lever (Friedman test: χ2(3) = 72.00, P < 0.0001; Fig. 3D). Following reversals, rats adapted more rapidly in less stochastic environments (two-way RM ANOVA: main effect of Contingency, F(3,69) = 250.0, P < 0.0001; Trials × Contingency interaction, F(21,483) = 20.41, P < 0.0001; Fig. 3E), and perseveration likewise decreased as stochasticity declined (Friedman test: χ2(3) = 61.04, P < 0.0001; Fig. 3F), consistent with the idea that noisier environments require greater evidence accumulation before adaptation.

To assess how rats adjusted their choices based on recent experience, we analyzed switching behavior using the trial-sequence coding system shown in Fig. 1I (Fig. 3G). Rats consistently switched more often after unrewarded than rewarded outcomes across contingencies, and this effect depended on recent outcome history and stochasticity (three-way RM ANOVA: Outcomet-1, F(1,23) = 3377, P < 0.0001; Outcomet−2, F(1,23) = 97.48, P < 0.0001; Contingency, F(3,69) = 48.54, P < 0.0001; Contingency × Outcomet-1, F(3,69) = 218.2, P < 0.0001; all other interactions P > 0.07; Fig. 3G). In repeated-choice sequences (AA, aA, Aa, aa), switching increased with the number and recency of losses, and this relationship varied with contingency (three-way RM ANOVA: Outcomet-1, F(1,23) = 1534, P < 0.0001; Outcomet−2, F(1,23) = 275.8, P < 0.0001; Contingency × Outcomet-1, F(2,46) = 40.97, P < 0.0001; Fig. 3 G, Left). In alternating-choice sequences (AB, aB, Ab, ab), switching also depended on recent outcomes and was modulated by stochasticity (Outcomet-1, F(1,23) = 1001, P < 0.0001; Outcomet−2, F(1,23) = 279.1, P < 0.0001; Contingency × Outcomet-1, F(2,46) = 12.25, P < 0.0001; Contingency × Outcomet−2, F(2,46) = 8.05, P < 0.01; Fig. 3 G, Right). Outcome stochasticity further shaped these switching patterns. For repeated-choice sequences (Fig. 3 G, Left), switching after rewarded trials was highest in the most stochastic schedule (70/30), whereas switching after one or two unrewarded trials (Aa, aa) increased as stochasticity decreased, consistent with negative outcomes carrying stronger evidence for contingency change in more deterministic environments (AA: one-way RM ANOVA, F(3,69) = 33.03, P < 0.0001; aA: F(2,46) = 7.486, P < 0.01; Aa: F(3,69) = 71.56, P < 0.0001; aa: Friedman test, χ2(3) = 51.35, P < 0.0001). For alternating-choice sequences (Fig. 3 G, Right), switching remained relatively stable for uninformative outcome pairs (AB, ab) but varied strongly with stochasticity for informative opposite-outcome combinations (aB: F(3,69) = 57.18, P < 0.0001; Ab: Friedman test, χ2(3) = 38.15, P < 0.0001).

To test whether a fixed learning rate could account for behavior, we simulated performance across stochasticity levels using a standard Q-learning model with constant α (Fig. 3H). This model failed to reproduce key features of the data: It generated only minor differences in postreversal performance across contingencies (Fig. 3I), predicted nearly constant perseveration (Fig. 3J), and mischaracterized sequence-dependent switching (Fig. 3K). In particular, for Aa, aa, and AB sequences it predicted the wrong direction of stochasticity effects, whereas for aB and Ab it failed to capture the observed stochasticity-dependent modulation. Together, these discrepancies indicate that a constant-learning-rate model cannot account for the adaptive behavioral changes observed across uncertainty levels.

A more stringent possibility is that each contingency is best captured by its own fixed learning rate rather than by a single α shared across schedules. We therefore repeated the analysis using a fixed learning-rate RL model augmented with a perseveration kernel and the corresponding optimal α for each contingency (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B). Although this improved the overall increase in performance and faster postreversal adaptation across conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D and E), it still failed to reproduce the full reduction in perseverative errors and several key sequence-dependent switching effects (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 F and G). This partial improvement nevertheless suggests that the learning rate required to best capture behavior varies systematically with environmental stochasticity, motivating a model in which learning speed is adjusted online as a function of experienced task statistics.

To overcome the limitations of fixed learning-rate models, we developed a Meta-RL model in which α varied trial by trial as a function of internal estimates of stochasticity (ε) and volatility (λ) (Fig. 3L). In this framework, unsigned prediction errors were used to estimate stochasticity, whereas volatility was estimated from action-outcome patterns that deviated from expectations, which we termed incompatibility (Materials and Methods). The learning rate increased with estimated volatility and decreased with estimated stochasticity. This adaptive learning-rate formulation provides a simple mechanism for modulating learning in response to the combined demands of stochasticity and volatility.

The adaptive learning-rate model successfully captured the main behavioral effects of stochasticity. It reproduced the contingency-dependent differences in postreversal choice accuracy and perseveration (Fig. 3 M and N), showing faster adaptation and fewer consecutive errors as outcomes became more reliable. It also captured the hierarchical organization of conditional switching across action–outcome sequences, as well as the way these switching patterns scaled with stochasticity (Fig. 3O). Together, these findings show that rats dynamically adjust their learning rate to environmental structure, updating rapidly when outcomes are reliable and more cautiously when they are noisy, and that this pattern is captured by a model in which α varies with internal estimates of stochasticity and volatility.

Importantly, these conclusions were not specific to our Meta-RL implementation. A normative model from Piray and Daw (9), which jointly estimates stochasticity and volatility using a particle-filter approximation (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C), reproduced the same overall qualitative pattern across contingencies, including improved performance, faster postreversal adaptation, and fewer perseverative errors as stochasticity decreased (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 H–J). However, these effects were captured less faithfully than by our Meta-RL model, particularly for postreversal adaptation, perseverative errors, and several sequence-specific switching effects (SI Appendix, Fig. S1K). Adding a perseveration kernel to our Meta-RL model did not alter the main interpretation, as the extended model reproduced the same overall pattern across contingency conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 L–O).

Orbitofrontal Noradrenergic Release Reflects Internal Estimates of Volatility.

We next sought a neurobiological mechanism that could support adaptive modulation of learning rate in the OFC (Figs. 2 and 3). Because the locus coeruleus–noradrenaline (LC–NA) system is implicated in signaling unexpected change and promoting behavioral flexibility, we asked whether NA release in OFC might reflect computations related to stochasticity and volatility. To test this, we monitored OFC–NA dynamics using GRABNE1m fiber photometry (Fig. 4 A and B). Animals were trained to expert performance on the 80/20 schedule before recording and showed behavior comparable to previous experiments (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–I).

We first asked whether OFC–NA responses reflected immediate trial outcomes (t0). NA showed a biphasic, outcome-dependent pattern across the trial (Fig. 4C): it decreased at lever press for both rewarded and unrewarded trials (two-way RM ANOVA: Phase, F(2,12) = 19.00, P = 0.0002; Outcome, F(1,6) = 69.70, P = 0.0002; Phase × Outcome interaction, F(2,12) = 21.55, P = 0.0001), with a larger decrease following rewarded outcomes (post hoc, P = 0.0003). After unrewarded outcomes, NA rose over the intertrial interval (post hoc, P = 0.0205), whereas after rewarded outcomes it continued to decline (post hoc, P = 0.0047), yielding lower end-of-trial NA levels on rewarded trials (paired t test: t(6) = 6.719, P = 0.0005; Fig. 4D).

We next asked whether NA also reflected recent outcome history. Prepress NA was higher following an unrewarded trial on t−1 (two-way RM ANOVA: Outcomet-1, F(1,6) = 24.34, P = 0.0026; Fig. 4E), indicating that NA carried information from the previous choice. This influence extended across the three preceding trials: prepress NA differed across outcome histories spanning t−3 to t−1 (one-way RM ANOVA: F(7,42) = 6.422, P < 0.0001; Fig. 4F). This ordering was informative. If OFC–NA encoded stochasticity, the maximally decorrelated triplets (+−+ and −+−) should have produced the largest responses, which they did not. Instead, the pattern followed a recency-weighted accumulation of negative outcomes, consistent with a volatility-like signal reflecting how strongly recent evidence departs from the expected contingency.

We next examined how OFC–NA activity responded to contingency reversals. To do this, we aligned NA release to reversal events and quantified its dynamics over the sixteen trials surrounding the switch (Fig. 4G). NA declined across stable prereversal trials, then increased immediately after reversal before returning toward baseline as behavior adapted (one-way RM ANOVA: F(7,42) = 2.957, P = 0.0130; Fig. 4G). Across animals, postreversal NA was higher than prereversal NA (paired t test: t(6) = 3.320, P = 0.0160; Fig. 4H).

We then asked whether this reversal-aligned OFC–NA signal could reflect internal estimates of environmental uncertainty. To test this, we compared OFC–NA dynamics with the trial-by-trial volatility estimates generated by the adaptive learning-rate model. Model-estimated volatility showed the same reversal-aligned profile, declining before reversal and rising immediately after contingency change (one-way RM ANOVA: F(7,203) = 49.43, P < 0.0001; Fig. 4I), and was likewise higher after than before reversal (paired t test: t(29) = 11.77, P < 0.0001; Fig. 4J). A similar pattern was obtained with the Piray and Daw model (one-way RM ANOVA: F(7,42) = 10.74, P < 0.0001; paired t test: t(6) = 4.525, P = 0.004; Fig. 4 K and L). To test whether OFC–NA reflects experienced volatility rather than programmed stochasticity, we trained the same animals in a variant in which the reversal criterion was increased from 8 to 12 consecutive high-probability choices (PRL12; SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–E). Because outcome probabilities were unchanged, this manipulation lengthened stable periods while keeping reversals performance-triggered, resulting in fewer reversals and higher stable-period performance, with no change in perseverative errors relative to PRL8 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–D). The Meta-RL model reproduced this behavior without refitting (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 F–I), indicating that volatility estimates adjusted online to the longer stable periods in PRL12. OFC–NA showed a matching pattern: The postreversal transient was preserved, but the prereversal low-NA period and postreversal return toward baseline were stretched relative to PRL8 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3E). The model reproduced the same temporal stretching in the reversal-aligned volatility signal (SI Appendix, Fig. S3J), supporting the idea that OFC–NA tracks experienced volatility rather than nonspecific environmental uncertainty. Together, these findings suggest that OFC–NA tracks unexpected changes in outcomes and closely parallels model-derived volatility estimates.

Chemogenetic Inhibition of LC→OFC Neurons Reproduces Model-Predicted Effects of Disrupted Learning-Rate Adjustment Driven by Model-Estimated Volatility.

If NA in the OFC contributes to learning-rate adjustment based on internal estimates of volatility, then simulating a perturbation of this signal should produce specific behavioral impairments. To test this idea, we used two complementary approaches. To generate behavioral predictions, we simulated a reduction in learning-rate adjustment driven by model-estimated volatility within our adaptive Meta-RL framework (Fig. 5A). We then evaluated this prediction experimentally by chemogenetically inhibiting LC→OFC noradrenergic projections using a retrograde CAV2-PRS-hM4Di construct injected into the OFC (Fig. 5B and SI Appendix, Fig. S4A).

In the 80/20 probabilistic condition, the computational lesion predicted a selective deficit in postreversal adaptation, characterized by a slower increase in choosing the newly rewarded lever (Fig. 5C), while leaving performance during stable phases intact (Fig. 5D). It further predicted increased perseveration after reversals (Fig. 5E) and a reduction in switching after unrewarded outcomes when the same lever was repeated, without affecting switching following rewarded trials (Fig. 5F).

In vivo LC→OFC inhibition produced the same behavioral deficits. Performance during stable blocks was unchanged, while adaptation following reversals was significantly impaired (two-way RM ANOVA: Treatment × Session phase: F(1,15) = 5.259, P = 0.0367; post hoc: postreversal, P = 0.0029; stable, P = 0.7794; Fig. 5 G and H), despite animals completing a similar number of reversals under both conditions (paired t test: t(15) = 0.905, P = 0.3798; SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). LC→OFC inhibition increased consecutive errors after reversals (paired t test: t(15) = 3.214, P = 0.0058; Fig. 5I) and selectively reduced switching after losses on the new low-probability lever (paired t test: t(15) = 3.565, P = 0.0028; SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). Moreover, consistent with model predictions of reduced switching after unrewarded outcomes when the same lever was repeated (Fig. 5F), LC→OFC inhibition decreased switching after repeated omissions on the same lever “aa” (paired t test: t(15) = 2.803, P = 0.0134; Fig. 5J). A two-way RM ANOVA on switching after unrewarded trials (ΣOmission: F(2,30) = 20.16, P < 0.0001; Treatment: F(1,15) = 9.407, P = 0.0078; ΣOmission × Treatment: F(2,30) = 1.872, P = 0.1714; SI Appendix, Fig. S4D) showed that the treatment effect was most pronounced when more than one consecutive omission occurred.

We next examined whether the same manipulation would produce similar effects under deterministic conditions. In the 100/0 condition, the computational lesion predicted no behavioral impairment, with postreversal performance, perseveration, and switching behavior remaining unchanged (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A–D).

In vivo, LC→OFC inhibition produced the same outcome: DCZ treatment did not alter postreversal performance (two-way RM ANOVA: Treatment, F(1,15) = 0.074, P = 0.7893; Treatment × Session phase interaction, F(1,15) = 0.517, P = 0.4831; SI Appendix, Fig. S5 E and F), perseveration (paired t test: t(15) = 0.329, P = 0.7470; SI Appendix, Fig. S5G), or conditional switching in the deterministic condition (paired t tests, all P > 0.05; SI Appendix, Fig. S5H). Thus, the model accurately predicted both the presence of behavioral deficits in the probabilistic environment and their absence in the deterministic one.

Together, these results reveal a clear correspondence between the computational predictions and the behavioral effects of LC→OFC inhibition, reproducing the impact of disrupting learning-rate adjustment based on internal estimates of volatility.

Discussion

Updating action–outcome associations is essential for adapting behavior in uncertain environments. Here, we combined behavioral, photometry, chemogenetic, and computational approaches to identify noradrenergic signaling in ventrolateral OFC as a mechanism supporting this process. Silencing OFC CaMKII+ neurons impaired postreversal adaptation, and modeling indicated that this phenotype was best accounted for by a reduction in learning rate. Behavior was better captured by an adaptive reinforcement-learning model in which learning rates were adjusted according to internal estimates of stochasticity and volatility, whereas fixed-rate models failed to reproduce behavior across uncertainty levels. Fiber-photometry recordings revealed that noradrenaline release in OFC closely reflected model-derived volatility estimates, and LC→OFC inhibition caused selective deficits in probabilistic conditions, as predicted by disrupting learning-rate control based on internal estimates of volatility.

Behavioral Analysis and Modeling Approach.

Reversal behavior has often been modeled with classical RL frameworks using fixed parameters (38), sometimes augmented with separate learning rates (35), stickiness terms (39), counterfactual updating (38), or forgetting (34, 40). Although such models can fit the overall choice series well according to penalized-likelihood scores, they may fail to reproduce the sharp adjustments that follow reversals or the qualitative differences observed across levels of uncertainty. Following Palminteri et al. (41), we therefore evaluated our models not only by predictive fit but also by generative validity. When simulated, the constant-α model failed to reproduce key behavioral patterns across stochasticity levels. Using a different fixed α optimized for each contingency improved performance relative to a single fixed learning rate across all schedules, but still failed to capture the full behavioral pattern. This strengthens the case for models in which learning rate is adjusted online to environmental statistics. In changing environments, learners must decide how much past experience should influence current behavior and how quickly to update when contingencies shift (42). Adaptive learning models formalize this principle. Piray and Daw (9) developed a model in which learning rate is dynamically adjusted according to joint estimates of stochasticity and volatility using a particle-filter approximation. A related metalearning account showed that serotonergic neurons adjust learning speed according to expected and unexpected uncertainty (10), further supporting the idea that neuromodulatory systems regulate learning dynamics. Applying the Piray and Daw model to our data yielded the same overall qualitative interpretation as our Meta-RL model, reproducing the main behavioral signatures across stochasticity conditions and generating reversal-aligned volatility estimates that paralleled both our own model output and OFC noradrenergic dynamics. Hence, two computationally distinct frameworks, one normative and one based on simple prediction-error-driven heuristics, independently support the same interpretation of the behavioral and neural data. Some effects were nevertheless weaker in the Piray and Daw model. We suggest this reflects features of that framework in the context of our task rather than a limitation of joint stochasticity-volatility estimation itself. First, its volatility update does not distinguish whether an unexpected outcome follows repetition of the previously chosen lever or a switch to the alternative lever, a distinction captured by our incompatibility term and relevant for sequence-dependent switching in the present task. Second, once the filter stabilizes, learning-rate changes remain relatively limited around reversals, reducing the behavioral impact of volatility fluctuations. This attenuation may be amplified by binary rewards, for which mean reward and uncertainty covary deterministically with reward probability, limiting the extent to which stochasticity can be dissociated from other task statistics. By contrast, our Meta-RL model estimates stochasticity and volatility using prediction-error-driven signals, which provide a better account of the sequence-dependent switching patterns and reversal-aligned dynamics observed in the task. One specific limitation common to both models is the AB sequence, which is structurally rare under deterministic schedules (SI Appendix, Table S1) and therefore especially sensitive to sampling. The PRL12 experiment further supports this interpretation. Although programmed stochasticity was unchanged relative to PRL8, the longer criterion produced longer stable periods and fewer reversals. The same Meta-RL parameterization reproduced both PRL8 and PRL12 without refitting, indicating that the model’s volatility estimate adjusted online to the longer stable periods experienced in PRL12. If λ merely reflected overall dataset-level uncertainty, refitting should have been required. Probabilistic reversal behavior has also been modeled using inference-based accounts in which animals explicitly represent hidden task states and update their belief about which option is currently the high- vs. low-reward lever (43, 44). Here, we instead adopted an adaptive value-based account in which learning rates vary as a function of recent outcome patterns, capturing uncertainty effects without invoking state inference. Our approach nevertheless bears a conceptual proximity with inference, because our volatility estimate is derived from the incompatibility measure introduced by Mishchanchuk et al. (43). Behavioral differences between the two studies may reflect training history: Inference-based strategies may be promoted by deterministic pretraining, whereas our animals were trained directly under probabilistic contingencies. Consistent with this, how OFC reflects hidden states or reward statistics depends on the training stage in temporal (45) or foraging tasks (46). Overall, these findings support the view that flexible reversal behavior is best captured by models in which learning rate dynamically adjusts to two internally estimated forms of uncertainty, stochasticity, and volatility.

The Role of the Orbitofrontal Cortex in Adaptive Behavior.

The OFC has long been linked to behavioral flexibility, particularly when learned action–outcome associations must be updated after contingencies change (32). Across species, OFC lesions produce perseveration on previously rewarded options, indicating a deficit in updating learned associations rather than in response inhibition. Consistent with this view, we previously showed that ventrolateral OFC inactivation disrupts goal-directed control after reversal of outcome identity (33). In probabilistic reversal learning, OFC activity carries signals relevant for feedback-based updating: in rats, OFC neurons encode both the expected value of the chosen action and the subsequent prediction error, with earlier and stronger responses than those observed in medial prefrontal regions (47), and in mice, OFC populations encode option-value signals on a trial-by-trial basis, while OFC silencing abolishes the influence of recent outcome history on choice (40).

Several findings converge with this interpretation. In a closely matched rat PRL task, beta power in OFC differentiated high- and low-probability options before reversal but collapsed immediately after contingencies switched, when rats had to reaccumulate reward evidence (48). Importantly, beta power in lateral OFC was positively correlated with model-estimated learning rates, consistent with our finding that the behavioral effects of OFC inhibition are best accounted for by a reduction in learning rate. In a similar rat PRL task, pharmacological OFC inhibition selectively impaired postreversal adjustment and decreased learning-rate parameters without affecting stable-period performance (35), and disrupting OFC function likewise reduces adaptation to low-probability feedback that is critical for learning in probabilistic environments (49). Our OFC inhibition results are also broadly consistent with a recent study (50) showing in a de novo probabilistic reversal task that OFC coding of choice and outcome became more informative as uncertainty increased and that bilateral OFC inhibition attenuated learning and reduced Win-Stay behavior. However, that study manipulated stochasticity across fixed reversal schedules, whereas our bilateral OFC inhibition experiment was performed under a single 80/20 schedule with performance-dependent reversals. Not all studies report equivalent OFC involvement, and these discrepancies likely reflect task demands. Jeong et al. (51) found minimal effects of OFC perturbation in a two-option probabilistic spatial bandit task, whereas a macaque study reported deficits in a more complex multioutcome reversal task but not in a simpler deterministic two-option version of the same task (52), a pattern also reported in rats (49). Together, these observations suggest that OFC contributions become especially important when adaptation requires integrating uncertainty and rebuilding value estimates after contingency change.

A useful framework for interpreting the present findings comes from Riceberg and Shapiro (53, 54), whose lesion and recording studies showed that OFC contributions to reversal learning are strongest under stable contingencies: OFC lesions impaired adaptation when contingencies remained stable for extended blocks, but improved performance when reversals occurred very frequently, and only stable contingencies supported predictive coding of the upcoming goal in OFC. Unlike those studies, our reversals were performance-triggered in a probabilistic environment. In expert 80/20 sessions, this yielded experienced block lengths of roughly 30 completed trials on average, making our task structurally closer to their stable regime, although the probabilistic nature of our design prevents a direct one-to-one comparison. Importantly, OFC inhibition did not alter the number of reversals completed, arguing against a trivial change in reversal frequency as the source of the observed impairment. Our data are therefore consistent with the idea that OFC is especially important when reward history can accumulate sufficiently to support updating after contingency change in a noisy environment. Similar conclusions emerge from multioption probabilistic tasks, including evidence that disrupting OFC→nucleus accumbens core projections impairs learning from negative feedback and slows postreversal adaptation (34), suggesting that the role of OFC in reversal learning extends beyond simple two-option settings and may depend, at least in part, on OFC→striatal projections.

Noradrenergic Modulation of the Orbitofrontal Cortex for Adaptive Behavior.

Yu and Dayan (12) proposed that LC-NA signals unexpected uncertainty, thereby increasing learning rate when outcome variability exceeds expectations. Sales et al. (13) similarly framed LC-NA as a state-prediction error that dynamically regulates learning speed, enabling shifts between stable exploitation and flexible updating when contingencies change. Our results are consistent with recent human studies implicating noradrenergic signaling in adaptive learning under uncertainty. In predictive-inference and probabilistic bandit tasks, pupil-linked and pharmacological measures suggest that noradrenergic activity covaries with trial-by-trial adjustments in learning rate and contributes causally to scaling learning with volatility (6, 19, 22–24, 55). Together, these studies support a role for noradrenergic signaling in regulating learning speed when contingencies change.

Rodent work similarly supports a role for LC-NA in adaptive updating. In mice, baseline LC firing predicts and drives faster rule updating in a Go/No-Go reversal task (21). In rats, noradrenergic input specifically to ventrolateral OFC is required for updating reversed specific action–outcome associations, whereas orbitofrontal dopamine and LC→mPFC noradrenergic pathways do not produce comparable effects (56). Recent studies further show that LC noradrenaline facilitates OFC remapping and behavioral flexibility following reversal learning (30, 31). Extending these findings, we observed that NA release in OFC increased specifically at moments requiring behavioral updating, likely reflecting an internal estimate of trial-by-trial volatility as in our adaptive learning model. A complementary test came from the PRL12 variant, in which programmed stochasticity was unchanged relative to PRL8. OFC–NA dynamics were consistent with the idea that this signal tracks experienced volatility rather than a nonspecific measure of overall environmental uncertainty. Chemogenetic inhibition of LC→OFC projections reproduced the behavioral deficits predicted by disrupting learning-rate modulation driven by model-estimated volatility, selectively impairing postreversal adaptation in probabilistic conditions while sparing performance under deterministic contingencies.

This convergence across model predictions, neural dynamics, and causal perturbation indicates that noradrenergic LC projections to OFC support the dynamic regulation of learning speed when environmental contingencies change.

Materials and Methods

Behavioral Apparatus and Subjects.

Behavioral training and testing were conducted in operant chambers equipped with a central food magazine and two retractable levers. Rats were habituated to the chambers and trained to lever press before the reversal-learning task. A total of 59 Long–Evans rats were used across the experiments. Animals were housed in pairs, food restricted before testing, and maintained at approximately 90% of free-feeding weight. All procedures were approved by the French Ministry of Higher Education and Innovation (APAFIS#27928-2020110918011853 v2) and complied with national and European regulations for animal experimentation.

Behavioral Task.

Rats were trained in a two-choice probabilistic reversal-learning task. Daily sessions consisted of 200 trials in which both levers were associated with complementary reward probabilities (39, 49, 57). Reward contingencies reversed after eight consecutive choices of the currently advantageous lever, independently of reward delivery. Behavioral experiments examined expert performance across 70/30, 80/20, 90/10, and 100/0 contingencies, using the last four sessions of each condition for analysis. Chemogenetic and fiber-photometry experiments were conducted in expert rats performing the 80/20 contingency.

Chemogenetic and Fiber-Photometry Experiments.

o test the contribution of orbitofrontal noradrenergic signaling to adaptive learning, CaMKII-positive neurons in ventral/lateral orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) were chemogenetically inhibited using AAV8-CaMKII-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry (33), and locus coeruleus (LC) neurons projecting to OFC were targeted using a retrograde CAV2-PRS-hM4D(Gi) strategy (31, 56), allowing pathway-specific inhibition of LC→OFC noradrenergic inputs. In both chemogenetic experiments, deschloroclozapine (DCZ; 0.1 mg/kg, i.p.) and vehicle sessions were compared within animals in a counterbalanced design (31, 56). In a third experiment, noradrenergic dynamics were recorded in OFC using GRABNE1m fiber photometry (58).

Computational Modeling.

Choice behavior was compared with several reinforcement-learning models implemented as generative accounts of performance in the task, including a standard fixed-learning-rate model, a contingency-specific fixed-learning-rate model augmented with a perseveration kernel, and an adaptive Meta-RL model in which learning rate varied trial by trial as a function of internal estimates of stochasticity and volatility (59). Model predictions were compared with empirical measures of reversal performance, perseverative errors, and conditional switching across contingencies.

Full experimental procedures, viral constructs, surgeries, behavioral protocols, photometry preprocessing, computational modeling, and statistical analyses are provided in SI Appendix.
