Integration of inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers with machine learning enhances prediction of progesterone response in fertility-preserving endometrial carcinoma management
Authors: Yue Qi, Xinyi Bi, Yuman Wu, Xingchen Li, Jianliu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•First model integrating inflammatory and nutritional scores for EC/AEH CR prediction.•Multimodal nomogram achieved superior accuracy with an AUC of 0.933 in validation.•Lasso regression and 101 machine learning algorithms identified six key features.•Risk stratification identifies high-risk patients to guide personalized treatment.

Introduction

Endometrial carcinoma (EC), one of the three most prevalent gynecological malignancies, exhibits a rising incidence among younger populations, with epidemiological studies reporting a 2.5% annual increase in premenopausal cases over the past decade[[1], [2]]. Atypical endometrial hyperplasia (AEH), a well-established precursor lesion to EC, necessitates definitive surgical management as per current clinical guidelines [3]. This therapeutic paradigm poses a substantial clinical dilemma for reproductive-age patients with EC/AEH, particularly those seeking fertility preservation, as surgical intervention directly compromises future childbearing potential. Emerging evidence highlights that over 40% of AEH cases progress to EC within five years without timely intervention [4]. While progesterone-based therapy has emerged as a primary fertility-sparing strategy, marked heterogeneity in complete remission (CR) rates (ranging from 50% to 80%) underscores the urgent need for personalized prognostic tools to optimize treatment selection and timing [[5], [6]].

Current prognostic models for EC/AEH predominantly rely on clinicopathological parameters such as BMI, histologic grade, and hormone receptor expression status. However, emerging evidence suggests systemic inflammation and nutritional status may critically influence therapeutic response tumor prognosis through immune modulation and metabolic reprogramming [7]. Inflammation orchestrates multiple facets of malignant progression, regulating oncogenesis, tumor cell proliferation, metastatic dissemination, senescence evasion, and apoptosis resistance through dynamic crosstalk with the tumor microenvironment [8]. In clinical practice, circulating immune cells (e.g., neutrophils, lymphocytes, and monocytes) and acute-phase proteins such as C-reactive protein (CRP) serve as conventional biomarkers reflecting systemic inflammatory status. Accumulating evidence underscores the prognostic value of these inflammatory indicators across diverse cancer types, with neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and CRP-based scores demonstrating significant associations with survival outcomes in solid and hematologic malignancies [9,10]. Notably, emerging research suggests that the integration of composite inflammatory indices—combining cellular, proteomic, and cytokine profiling data—may enhance prognostic stratification by capturing the multidimensional nature of tumor-associated inflammation. Advanced machine learning algorithms further enable the construction of inflammation signature panels that improve predictive accuracy beyond conventional univariate biomarkers.

Cancer-related malnutrition arises from a multifactorial etiology involving metabolic dysregulation, treatment toxicities, and systemic inflammation, placing over 80% of patients at risk of nutritional depletion across the disease trajectory [11]. This metabolic crisis manifests as a continuum from subclinical nutrient deficiencies (20–40% incidence) to severe cancer cachexia (30–60% in advanced stages), with gastrointestinal and pancreatic malignancies exhibiting the highest prevalence (60–85%) [11,12]. Critically, malnutrition independently predicts reduced treatment tolerance, accelerated functional decline, and 10–20% of direct cancer mortality through cachexia-driven organ failure and immunosuppression [13,14]. Despite these consequences, clinical implementation gaps persist: only 30–60% of high-risk patients receive guideline-directed nutritional support due to inadequate screening protocols and clinician awareness [15]. Current nutritional assessment relies primarily on anthropometric measures (e.g., BMI) and serum proteins (albumin, prealbumin), which show prognostic value in multiple cancers [16,17]. However, few studies have comprehensively integrated inflammatory-nutritional biomarkers into predictive frameworks, while existing models often lack validation across heterogeneous cohorts.

To address these limitations, we hypothesized that a composite model integrating systemic inflammation and nutritional biomarkers could enhance CR prediction accuracy and provide actionable risk stratification. This study aimed to: (1) identify key inflammatory-nutritional predictors through machine learning-driven feature selection; and (2) develop and validate a multimodal nomogram combining clinical and biomarker variables; and (3) evaluate the clinical utility of this model across distinct patient subgroups. By leveraging a systematic integration of 10 machine learning algorithms and Leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV), we developed a robust prognostic signature from candidate biomarkers. The validated nomogram not only achieved superior discriminative power compared to conventional models but also facilitated personalized risk stratification through composite inflammatory-nutritional scoring. By identifying high-risk patients requiring intensified monitoring, this tool may guide precision fertility-sparing strategies and improve oncological outcomes in young EC/AEH populations.

Materials and methods

Patient selection and dataprocessing

A retrospective study was conducted of patients diagnosed with EC/AEH at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Peking University People's Hospital (PKUPH), from January 2012 to January 2025. The inclusion criteria were: 1) Age ≤40 years with a strong desire for childbearing; 2)Pathological type: well-differentiated (G1) endometrioid adenocarcinoma; 3)Imaging confirms tumor confined to endometrium; 4)Positive expression of both ER and PR; 5)Molecular subtype: non- specific molecular profile (NSMP); 6)No contraindications to progesterone treatment; 7)Assessed by genetic and reproductive experts before treatment, no other fertility-impairing factors; and 8)Signed informed consent and suitable for follow-up. The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee Board of PKUPH (approval No. 2022PHB379– 001), in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects.

Initially, clinical information was collected from 359 EC/AEH patients with 41 variables. The variables included baseline clinicopathological variables and pre-treatment hematological variables. Thirty patients were excluded because they lacked baseline clinical data, resulting in a final cohort of 329 patients with 41 variables.

Of the 41 variables originally collected, seven indicators (including lymphocyte subsets and pro-inflammatory cytokines) were excluded because their missing data rates exceeded the 20% threshold (ranging from 39.5% to 53.6%). Baseline information and clinical features of EC patients.

To facilitate accurate data imputation, continuous variables were pre-standardized using the scale function in R. To minimize the influence of extreme values, all continuous variables were Winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles. Following imputation and outlier handling, a final Z-score normalization was applied to all variables to provide a mathematically rigorous and consistent input for LASSO regression and machine learning modeling.

Ultimately, 329 patients with 34 variables were incorporated into the study. Sample size was determined via power analysis (α=0.05, β=0.20). Based on preliminary data (CR rate=65%), a minimum of 278 patients were required to detect a 15% difference in AUC between models. Our cohort (N = 329) exceeded this threshold, ensuring adequate statistical power.

Extraction and construction of inflammatory-nutritional scores

The comprised 34 variables consisted of 13 clinicopathological variables and 21 biochemical variables. Baseline clinicopathological variables included demographic characteristics (age, BMI, gravidity and parity), menstrual history, family history of cancer, comorbidities (hypertension, thyroid disease, PCOS, metabolic syndrome and diabetes), time to CR and disease information (tumor type and pathological grade). Pre-treatment biochemical variables were assessed, including leukocyte, neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes, red blood cell (RBC), hemoglobin (Hb), platelet count, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), serum albumin (ALB), creatinine (Cr), bilirubin, fibrinogen (FIB), glucose, insulin, serum cholesterol, serum C-reactive protein (CRP), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), CA125 and HOMA-IR.

From the 21 biochemical variables, we calculated 17 (12 inflammatory and 5 nutritional) biomarkers using established formulas. Systemic inflammation biomarkers were assessed according to the above variables, including lymphocyte-to-CRP ratio (LCR), CRP-to-albumin ratio (CAR), modified Glasgow prognostic score(mGPS), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), systemic inflammation index (SII), glucose-to-lymphocyte ratio (GLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR), systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV), immune positivity score ( IPS) and derived neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio(dNLR). Systemic nutrition biomarkers were assessed according to the above variables, including the advanced inflammation index (ALI), prognostic nutritional index (PNI), nutritional risk index (NRI), geriatric nutritional risk index (mGNRI), and the controlling nutritional status score (CONUT). These biomarkers were calculated using formulas reported in previous studies [[18], [19], [20], [21]].

Initially, 17 biochemical indicators were subjected to LASSO regression analysis. The regularization parameter (λ) was optimized via 10-fold cross-validation based on the minimum partial likelihood deviance (λmin). Through this variables selection phase, the variables were filtered down to 9 inflammatory and 4 nutritional biomarkers with non-zero coefficients. In the subsequent Score Construction phase, the continuous Inflammatory Score (IS) and Nutritional Score (NS) were formulated via linear combinations weighted by their respective regression coefficients (β):IS=∑i=1nβi·XiandNS=∑j=1mγj·Yj

where βi and γj represent the regression coefficients derived from the LASSO model for the i th inflammatory and j-th nutritional biomarkers, respectively; Xi and Yj denote the corresponding standardized values.

Consequently, the final integrated feature set used for subsequent model development consisted of 15 candidate predictors: the 13 original clinicopathological variables plus the two derived scores (IS and NS).

Model exploration, optimization, and multivariable predictor selection

Initially, patients were randomly assigned to the Training cohort (n = 219) and Validation cohort (n = 110) at a 2:1 ratio. Within the training cohort, an integrated framework of ten distinct machine learning paradigms—including random survival forest (RSF), StepCox, CoxBoost, and Lasso—was deployed to evaluate 101 algorithm combinations using Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV). Concurrently, a standard univariate Cox analysis screened a baseline panel of 8 candidate features. To overcome sample size constraints, LOOCV was selected to evaluate the 101 algorithm pairs. Crucially, this framework served purely as an objective performance-testing standard to identify the optimal modeling method, rather than as the final model deployment tool.

To ensure maximum clinical parsimony and reproducibility, the definitive multivariable predictor selection was governed exclusively by the classic multivariable LASSO—Cox regression framework. The explicit variable retention criterion was strictly defined as features maintaining robust non-zero regression coefficients (β) at the minimum partial likelihood deviance (λmin), rigorously optimized via 10-fold cross-validation. Consequently, multi-collinear, redundant elements (HOMA-IR and CA125) were shrunken directly to zero, leaving exactly six independent risk features—BMI, menstrual history, IS, NS, metabolic syndrome, and histological type—to align into the final prognostic Nomogram.

The final model's formulas were used to calculate the predicted CR probability for each patient. To test how well the model distinguishes between patients who achieved CR and those who did not, we compared the AUCs and C-indices using DeLong’s test and 1000-times bootstrapping, applying Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. To evaluate the model's accuracy, we grouped patients into ten equallysized groups (deciles) based on their predicted probabilities. For each group, the average predicted probability was plotted against the actual, biopsy-confirmed CR rate using a smooth calibration curve (LOESS) with 95% confidence intervals.

Model robustness verification and validation

We evaluated the performance and reliability of our final nomogram using a clear two-step approach:(1)Internal Correction: Within the Training cohort, we ran 1000 times of Bootstrap resampling. This step mathematically adjusted our model's performance to remove any over-fitting bias and show its true, internal stability.(2)Independent Testing: We then tested the model in the Validation cohort , which was completely untouched during the modeling phase. In this independent group, we plotted ROC curves to check discrimination and generated smooth calibration curves with 95% confidence intervals to measure accuracy. We also replicated the cumulative CR curves, DCA curves, and risk stratifications. This successfully confirmed that the scoring system possesses robust sorting capability and high clinical utility in an independent patient population.

Full cohort applicability and stratified subgroup analysis

We evaluated the model across the overall population and various clinical sub-populations:(1)The Overall population: Given that the full cohort mathematically overlaps with our training data, these analyses are intended purely to show clinical utility rather than standalone validation. In this overall group, we tested predictive accuracy by running full ROC, calibration, cumulative CR, and DCA curves.(2)Stratified Subgroup Validation: To test if the model remains robust for patients with different clinical conditions, we performed subgroup analyses based on BMI (23 or 25 thresholds), Menstrual history (regular vs. irregular), diagnosis (EC vs. AEH), and Metabolic syndrome (with vs. without). We generated Kaplan-Meier curves and risk-stratification profiles within each subgroup, successfully confirming that the model's ability to separate patient risk is highly stable and versatile across different real-world clinical categories.

We generated Kaplan-Meier curves and risk-stratification profiles within each subgroup, successfully confirming that the model's ability to separate patient risk is highly stable and versatile across different real-world clinical categories.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies with percentages and analyzed via the Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. Cumulative complete response probabilities over the follow-up span were calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared via the log-rank test. All statistical analyses, regularized modeling, and diagnostic visualizations were executed using R software (version 4.4.0).

The construction of a multimodal nomogram integrating inflammatory-nutritional biomarkers and clinical variables for complete response prediction in EC/AEH

A nomogram model was constructed using the variables screened by the LASSO-Cox model (Fig. 5A), including BMI, menstrual history, IS, NS, metabolic syndrome and histological type. Besides, the probability calibration plot of CR rates showed the predictions of the nomogram were in good agreement with the actual observations (Fig. 5B). To further investigate the impact of inflammatory and nutritional factors on CR, predictive models were constructed by integrating four clinicopathological characteristics with or without inflammatory-nutritional scores. The results demonstrated that the inclusion of inflammatory-nutritional scores (INS) significantly improved the area under the curve (AUC) from 0.807 to 0.915, highlighting the critical role of these composite biomarkers in enhancing predictive accuracy (Fig. 5C). Concurrently, to control for over-fitting, a 1000-times bootstrap resampling validation was conducted. The underlying algorithm yielded an apparent AUC of 0.928 and an apparent accuracy of 90.3%. After introducing the over-optimism penalty (2.5%), the bootstrap-corrected accuracy was 87.9% ± 2.7%, and the corrected Kappa coefficient was 0.591 ± 0.079, confirming the excellent internal stability of the predictive system. The CR curve in the training set revealed that high score patients had higher potential to CR (Fig. 5D). Decision curve analysis (DCA) (Fig. 5E) evaluated the clinical utility of the nomogram by quantifying net benefit across threshold probabilities. The composite model provided higher net benefit than the 'treat-all' or 'treat-none' strategies within a clinically relevant threshold range, supporting its utility in guiding personalized treatment decisions. Validation and clinical applicability of the nomogram for complete remission prediction in EC/AEH

The predictive performance of the nomogram for estimating CR probability in EC/ AEH patients was systematically validated. In the validation cohort, incorporation of the inflammatory-nutritional score significantly enhanced model performance, improving the area under the ROC curve (AUC) from 0.83 to 0.933 (Fig. 6A). Calibration curves showed excellent agreement between predicted and observed CR probabilities (Fig. 6B), while DCA confirmed substantial clinical utility across a wide probability threshold range (Fig. 6C). Based on the nomogram, patients were stratified into low-, medium-, and high-score groups, demonstrating significantly distinct CR probabilities across these categories (p < 0.001; Fig. 6D). In the overall population, the model maintained high predictive accuracy (Fig. 6E–H). Discussion

This study developed and validated a multimodal inflammatory-nutritional nomogram integrating machine learning algorithms to predict CR in young EC/AEH patients undergoing progesterone therapy. By systematically analyzing 17 biomarkers (12 inflammatory, 5 nutritional) and clinical variables through a two-stage LASSO Cox regression and LOOCV framework, we identified BMI, menstrual history, inflammation score, nutritional score, metabolic syndrome and histological type as key predictors. The composite model achieved superior discriminative performance (AUC=0.915 in the training cohort, 0.933 in validation) compared to conventional clinical models (AUC=0.807). Risk stratification revealed that patients with high inflammatory-low nutritional scores exhibited significantly reduced CR rates (p < 0.001), highlighting the clinical utility of this tool for personalized fertility-sparing strategies.

Inflammatory cells within the tumor microenvironment orchestrate tumor cell elimination while paradoxically promoting pro-angiogenic processes, concurrently modulating therapeutic response through dynamic immunomodulatory mechanisms. Lymphocytes play a pivotal role in cell-mediated immune responses by recognizing and eliminating malignant cells. Clinically, lymphopenia has been associated with adverse therapeutic outcomes and poor prognosis across multiple cancer types [22]. Conversely, neutrophils exhibit tumor-promoting functions via cytokine release, which facilitates tumorigenesis and enhances cancer cell proliferation and metastatic potential [23]. Given the extensive reporting of inflammatory indices such as NLR and LMR as prognostic parameters, this study developed a composite inflammatory score based on LASSO regression analysis of previously reported biomarkers to predict therapeutic response. The prognostic value of SIRI and NLR aligns with their roles in modulating tumor-associated inflammation [24]. Elevated NLR reflects neutrophil-driven suppression of cytotoxic T lymphocytes via IL-6 and TNF-α overproduction [25,26], which may impair progesterone sensitivity by promoting estrogen receptor phosphorylation and progesterone resistance [27,28].

Malnutrition is intricately linked to tumor progression, angiogenesis, and clinical deterioration, mediated through metabolic reprogramming and immunosuppressive microenvironment modulation. ALB concentration serves as a critical biomarker for nutritional status assessment. Almost all nutritional prognosis scoring systems cover serum ALB, as hypoalbuminemia seems to be caused by increased catabolism subsequently leading to cachexia and additionally by advanced tumor stage. Conversely, low mGNRI and PNI scores indicate malnutrition-induced immunosuppression, characterized by hypoalbuminemia and impaired antigen presentation [[29], [30], [31]]. Systematic inflammatory response due to tumors suppresses albumin synthesis, which results in reduced treatment response, increased risk of chemotherapy-induced toxicity and poor prognosis.

The rationale for combining inflammatory and nutritional parameters lies in the intricate biological crosstalk between these two systems. Systemic inflammation acts as a metabolic drain, promoting protein catabolism and resting energy expenditure, which directly leads to nutritional depletion. Conversely, malnutrition impairs immune surveillance, creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment that fosters tumor escape and progesterone resistance. Therefore, a single biomarker reflects only one facet of this vicious cycle, whereas the combined Inflammatory-Nutritional Score captures the holistic status of the host-tumor interaction.

The integration of immunological and nutritional parameters into a composite scoring system synergistically enhances prognostic stratification by holistically evaluating patients' systemic status and improving outcome prediction accuracy. In addition, our study incorporated four clinical parameters to comprehensively evaluate the probability of CR in EC/AEH patients. To date, most studies have used BMI to measure the obesity level especially in EC/AEH patients. The obesity-related signaling pathways, such as JAK/STAT and MAPK, play crucial roles in energy metabolism, chronic inflammation, and hormone resistance [32]. The JAK/STAT signaling pathway plays a central role in adipose tissue inflammation and insulin resistance, and STAT3 activation is closely associated with leptin signaling. Studies have found that leptin activates the STAT3 and MAPK pathways, upregulates ERα expression, enhances estrogen signaling, promotes breast cancer cell proliferation, and counteracts the effects of hormone therapies such as tamoxifen [33]. Metabolic syndrome promotes cancer progression through chronic inflammation (e.g., leptin-mediated adipokine imbalance), insulin resistance, and hormonal metabolic disturbances (e.g., elevated estrogen levels) [34]. Therefore, the integration of BMI and metabolic syndrome further underscores the interplay between adiposity-related inflammation (e.g., leptin-mediated STAT3 activation) and hormonal therapy resistance, explaining the enhanced predictive accuracy of the composite model.

Our findings extend prior research by integrating inflammatory and nutritional biomarkers, a novel approach in EC/AEH prognosis. While studies have demonstrated immune-nutritional factors could be a favorable predictor for elderly patients undergoing emergency gastrointestinal surgery [35], no existing model has combined these dimensions for EC/AEH. The innovation of this study is multidimensional. First, unlike previous studies that focused solely on overall survival, this study is the first to demonstrate the predictive value of this combined score specifically for CR in fertility-preserving settings. Second, by employing advanced machine learning algorithms, we identified the most robust predictor combination. Finally, and most importantly for clinical translation, our model utilizes predictors derived entirely from routine clinical blood tests. This approach eliminates the need for additional invasive inspections or expensive genetic testing, offering dual benefits: it minimizes the physical burden on patients and significantly conserves medical resources. Crucially, by leveraging these readily available metrics, the model serves as a cost-effective early warning system, enabling clinicians to promptly identify high-risk patients for intervention without increasing healthcare costs.

The modified Glasgow Prognostic Score (mGPS), a scoring system based on inflammatory markers -CRP and nutritional status -ALB levels, is widely used to evaluate the prognosis of cancer patients. However, in this study, its predictive ability for CR in EC/AEH patients undergoing fertility-preserving treatment appeared limited. Possible reasons for this limitation include the following: First, mGPS does not incorporate molecular subtypes (MMR-d, POLE-mutated, TP53-mutated, NSMP) or genetic alterations (PTEN/PIK3CA mutations), which are strongly associated with progesterone sensitivity and recurrence risk [36]. Second, therapeutic interventions such as hysteroscopic resection might enhance CR rates independently of systemic inflammation, potentially masking the prognostic significance of mGPS. Third, sample heterogeneity (e.g., small subgroup sizes of specific molecular subtypes) and dynamic changes in inflammatory status during treatment could further reduce its predictive accuracy. Additionally, confounding factors such as obesity and metabolic comorbidities may independently influence CRP and albumin levels, complicating the interpretation of mGPS in this clinical context.

This nomogram addresses a critical unmet need in fertility preservation: identifying high-risk patients who may benefit from early intervention (LNG-IUD combined with metformin) or closer surveillance. The clinical application of this scoring system is substantial for personalized decision-making. For patients stratified into the low-risk group by our nomogram, standard progesterone therapy can be prescribed with high confidence. Conversely, for high-risk patients, clinicians should consider distinct management strategies. These may include: (1) integrating metabolic interventions to reverse the inflammatory-metabolic deficit; (2) implementing more frequent hysteroscopic evaluations to detect non-response early. This risk-stratified approach moves beyond the one-size-fits-all paradigm, optimizing oncological safety for young women.

Several limitations warrant meaningful consideration. First, the retrospective design and single-center nature of the study inherently introduce selection bias. Although we utilized strict inclusion criteria and LOOCV to mitigate overfitting, the lack of an external validation cohort limits the confirmed generalizability of our findings. Second, while our sample size is substantial for this specific cohort, it remains relatively limited for high-dimensional machine learning tasks, underscoring the need for larger multi-center collaborations. Third, due to the study's time span, molecular classification was not universally available. Future prospective studies should investigate the dynamic changes of these scores during treatment to refine predictive accuracy further.

Conclusions

In this study, a novel system combining inflammation and nutritional scores was developed for the first time and proved to be a simple and effective prognostic stratification tool for patients with EC / AEH, which could provide a valuable reference for CR probability prediction and treatment strategy formulation.
