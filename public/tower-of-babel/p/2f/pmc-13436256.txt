Clinical significance and future applications of standardized imaging parameters for assessing paraspinal muscle fatty infiltration in lumbar degenerative disease: a narrative review
Authors: Honghao Dai, Hao Zhang, Shuzhuo Zhang, Junlong Huang, Zhibin Liu, Qianfu Gong, Zhongshu Shan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParaspinal muscle fatty infiltration (FI) is common in lumbar degenerative disease (LDD) and is associated with pain, functional impairment, and adverse postoperative outcomes. However, the association between FI and clinical outcomes remains inconsistent across studies. These inconsistencies may partly reflect differences in how FI is measured and reported. Standardized measurement and reporting are needed.MethodsThis study was conducted as a topic-oriented narrative review. Relevant literature published between January 1, 2015, and March 31, 2026, was identified through searches of PubMed and the Web of Science Core Collection. The search terms covered the paraspinal muscles, FI, imaging-based FI measurement methods, and LDD and its major subtypes. After duplicate removal, title and abstract screening, and full-text eligibility assessment according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 35 articles were included. The included studies were narratively synthesized and classified as clinical or methodological evidence to summarize the clinical significance of FI, heterogeneity in imaging-based measurement, and the need for standardization.Main bodyFI is assessed using a variety of methods, but key measurement parameters and reporting practices remain inconsistent. Across studies, substantial variation exists in imaging sequences, acquisition settings, region of interest (ROI) delineation, sampling levels, and post-processing workflows. This heterogeneity reduces reproducibility and limits comparisons across studies. In addition, FI is still most commonly quantified using manual, two-dimensional, single-slice measurements. This approach is time-consuming and fails to capture the three-dimensional heterogeneity of the paraspinal muscles. Its applicability in large-cohort and multicenter studies is therefore limited. The aim of this narrative review is to summarize the major methods used for FI assessment, outline their strengths, limitations, and appropriate applications, and propose a three-layer standardization framework, together with standardized measurement and reporting procedures, to improve reproducibility and comparability across studies.ConclusionIn FI-related clinical research, standardized reporting of measurement parameters and processing workflows is essential to improve reproducibility and comparability across studies. Such standardization will also provide a foundation for multicenter studies, automated segmentation, three-dimensional quantification, and broader clinical application of FI assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-06993-z.Keywords: Paraspinal muscle fatty infiltration, Lumbar degenerative disease, Standardization, Automated segmentation

Introduction

Lumbar degenerative disease (LDD) is a major cause of low back and leg pain, as well as functional impairment [1]. It mainly includes lumbar disc herniation (LDH), lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS), and degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis (DLS) [1]. Historically, clinical practice and research in spine surgery have focused on “hard-tissue” structures, including the intervertebral disc, vertebrae, facet joints, and ligamentum flavum. The three-column theory has remained central to disease classification and surgical decision-making. In recent years, paraspinal muscles have increasingly been recognized as an “active pillar” that maintains lumbar stability and facilitates load transfer. Paraspinal muscles are now considered key contributors to the development and progression of LDD. Paraspinal muscle fatty infiltration (FI) has been regarded as a degeneration phenotype with both structural and metabolic implications [2, 3]. Nevertheless, compared with research on disc, vertebral, and facet-joint degeneration, paraspinal muscle studies have remained underrepresented.

A persistent imbalance has existed between research on spinal hard tissues and paraspinal soft tissues. Using a unified search strategy, a bibliometric comparison was performed using PubMed and the Web of Science Core Collection (2015–2025). Publications on structural topics—primarily the intervertebral disc, facet joints, vertebral bodies, and ligaments—numbered 3882 and 4351, respectively. In contrast, only 370 and 406 publications addressed FI during the same period. Over the 10-year period, the aggregate ratio of structural to FI-related publications was approximately 10.5–10.7:1, indicating sustained underattention to paraspinal muscle research. Notably, FI-related publications have increased more rapidly than publications on traditional structural topics over the past five years. The ratio has narrowed to approximately 8:1, suggesting increasing recognition of the clinical and scientific value of FI. Even so, the current evidence base remains insufficient relative to its potential value.

The paraspinal muscles, as a key component of the “dynamic stabilization system,” play a critical role in symptom development and functional limitation [4]. Studies over the past decade have shown that FI is associated with pain, disability, and selected postoperative outcomes in patients with LDD [5]. FI should therefore not be regarded as a passive imaging byproduct. Instead, FI should be considered a key phenotype that reflects declines in spinal muscle quality and function.

Methods for FI assessment have evolved from subjective visual grading to semi-quantitative threshold-based approaches. More recently, quantitative protocols based on chemical shift encoding–based water–fat magnetic resonance imaging (CSE-MRI) have been used to quantify the proton density fat fraction (PDFF). Methods have expanded, but key parameters and reporting standards within the same class of measurements remain inconsistent. As a result, reproducibility and cross-study comparability have been limited. Although PDFF is a physics-based metric with theoretical advantages for cross-scanner and cross-center reproducibility [6, 7], measured values remain highly dependent on acquisition and reconstruction settings. In addition, PDFF-based workflows still rely on manual delineation of a two-dimensional, single-slice region of interest (ROI). This procedure is labor-intensive and may not capture the overall function of paraspinal muscle groups. Scalability is therefore limited in large-cohort studies [8]. These issues increase heterogeneity across studies and hinder cross-study comparison and evidence synthesis.

Recently, emerging approaches, including three-dimensional assessment and deep learning–based automated segmentation, have enabled high-throughput FI quantification [9, 10]. Early evidence suggests that these methods can reduce manual workload and observer-related bias. Spatial distribution of FI within the paraspinal muscles can also be characterized. However, external validation remains insufficient. Moreover, acquisition and reconstruction parameters and reporting standards have not been harmonized. This lack of standardization remains a key barrier to routine clinical implementation of FI quantification [11, 12].

Against this background, this review focuses on three main aspects. First, the principal clinical significance of FI across different LDD subtypes is summarized. Second, the technical features, limitations, and key parameters of imaging-based FI assessment are systematically reviewed. In light of incomplete parameter reporting and heterogeneous findings in the existing literature, a three-layer standardization framework for FI assessment, together with key reporting elements, is proposed. Third, recent advances in three-dimensional assessment, automated segmentation, radiomics, and artificial intelligence (AI) for refined FI evaluation and risk stratification are outlined. Their translational potential under standardized conditions is also discussed. Overall, this review aims to promote the more standardized integration of FI, as a quantifiable and comparable imaging phenotype, into research and clinical workflows for LDD.

Review methodology

This study was conducted as a topic-oriented narrative review. The literature search was conducted in PubMed and the Web of Science Core Collection. The search covered the period from January 1, 2015, to March 31, 2026. The search strategy combined terms related to the paraspinal muscles, FI and muscle fat composition, imaging-based FI measurement, and LDD and its major subtypes, including LDH, LSS, and DLS. Boolean operators, including AND and OR, were applied according to the search rules of each database. The complete search strategy is provided in Additional file 1.

Study eligibility was determined according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) clinical studies involved patients with LDD or its major subtypes, including LDH, LSS, and DLS, whereas methodological studies were directly related to imaging-based FI measurement or standardization; (2) lumbar paraspinal muscles were assessed, including the MF, ES, PM, or the lumbar paraspinal muscles as a whole; (3) FI, fat fraction (FF), muscle fat index (MFI), fat infiltration rate (FIR), PDFF, Goutallier grade, computed tomography (CT)-derived muscle density, or other muscle fat composition-related indices were evaluated; and (4) the study provided evidence relevant to the clinical significance of FI, postoperative outcomes, imaging measurement methods, ROI definition, measurement level, thresholding strategy, reliability, automated segmentation, or reporting standardization.

Studies were excluded if they focused on non-lumbar regions, such as the cervical or thoracic spine; primarily investigated non-LDD conditions, including fracture, tumor, infection, inflammatory disease, trauma, or spinal deformity; did not assess FI or related muscle fat composition indices; were not based on imaging; or involved animal or cadaveric samples. Case reports, conference abstracts, editorials, and letters were also excluded if they lacked substantive original or methodological content.

The initial search identified 885 records: 396 from PubMed and 489 from the Web of Science Core Collection. All records were imported into Zotero, and duplicates were removed. After duplicate removal, 520 records underwent title and abstract screening. Titles and abstracts were screened by one investigator. After initial screening, 89 articles were selected for full-text eligibility assessment. Full-text eligibility was then assessed independently by two investigators. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. The final narrative synthesis included 35 articles: 31 original studies, three systematic reviews or meta-analyses, and one review or consensus article.

According to their primary role in this review, the included studies were classified as clinical evidence or methodological evidence. Twenty-two studies were classified as clinical evidence. These studies were used to summarize the clinical significance of FI in LDD and its major subtypes and to describe inconsistent findings regarding the association between FI and clinical outcomes. Thirteen studies were classified as methodological evidence. These studies were used to analyze sources of heterogeneity in imaging-based FI assessment, including image acquisition, ROI definition, measurement level, thresholding strategy, reliability assessment, automated segmentation, and reporting standardization. They also provided the basis for the standardization framework proposed in this review. The detailed screening process is shown in Fig. 1, and the included studies are listed in Additional file 2.

Anatomy of the lumbar paraspinal muscles and their role in spinal stability

Research on lumbar paraspinal muscles has primarily focused on the multifidus (MF), erector spinae (ES), and psoas major (PM) (Fig. 2) [13]. The MF is a prototypical local stabilizer and is primarily involved in resisting segmental shear and rotational loads [14]. MF atrophy and fatty infiltration have been associated with chronic low back pain, segmental instability, and poor postoperative functional recovery [15]. The ES is the principal extensor of the spine. It plays a dominant role in maintaining lumbar lordosis, supporting an upright weight-bearing posture, and enabling postural transitions. Degeneration of the ES has been linked to reduced extensor endurance. It has also been associated with sagittal imbalance, degenerative spondylolisthesis, and scoliosis [13]. The PM connects the lumbar spine to the hip. It contributes to pelvis–spine coordination and helps control hip flexion and anterior pelvic tilt. Degeneration of the PM may weaken anterior active support of the lumbar spine. Anterior shear stress across the intervertebral disc may also be increased [16]. When degeneration affects these muscle groups, the capacity of the spine’s “active stabilization system” is reduced. Progression of LDD may therefore be promoted. The risk of adverse postoperative outcomes may also be increased [17].

Clinical significance of paraspinal muscle fatty infiltration in lumbar degenerative disease

Lumbar degenerative disease (LDD) mainly comprises three major subtypes: lumbar disc herniation (LDH), lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS), and degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis (DLS) [1]. Associations between FI and pain, functional impairment, and postoperative outcomes have been widely reported across these LDD subtypes, although the findings have been inconsistent. Accordingly, this section focuses primarily on higher-level evidence, including systematic reviews and meta-analyses, to summarize the most consistent clinical implications of FI in each LDD subtype.

Clinical significance of FI in lumbar disc herniation

In LDH, the principal value of FI lies not in reflecting the disc lesion itself, but in identifying segment-specific deterioration in muscle quality and reduced postoperative recovery reserve at the affected level [18, 19]. Current higher-level evidence supports two relatively consistent conclusions. First, more severe preoperative FI is associated with a higher risk of same-level recurrence or reoperation after lumbar discectomy, as well as persistent low back pain and poorer functional recovery [18]. Second, patients with unilateral LDH often show relatively consistent ipsilateral abnormalities of the multifidus (MF), and increased FI appears to be more stable than changes in muscle size alone [19]. Further evidence suggests that patients with LDH may show increased FI in the MF, erector spinae (ES), psoas major (PM), and gluteus medius (GM), whereas differences in cross-sectional area (CSA) are not always significant. This finding suggests that FI may, in some cases, reflect early localized deterioration in muscle quality more sensitively than CSA [20]. Therefore, in LDH, the value of FI lies not in replacing conventional morphologic indices, but in being interpreted together with the affected segment, laterality, and CSA. This combined approach may allow more accurate identification of localized muscle quality loss, reduced recovery reserve, and postoperative recurrence risk [18–20]. In LDH-related research, reporting CSA alone is often insufficient to capture the most clinically informative signal. Incorporating FI into a composite imaging assessment framework may better support risk identification, recovery prediction, and rehabilitation stratification [18, 20].

Clinical significance of FI in lumbar spinal stenosis

In LSS, the clinical significance of FI lies less in reflecting the severity of spinal canal narrowing itself than in indicating paraspinal muscle functional reserve and recovery potential under chronic neural compression [21–23]. Analysis of the Lumbar Stenosis Outcome Study (LSOS) cohort by Getzmann et al. showed that increased FI was associated with poorer functional status and lower quality of life, whereas the cumulative lumbar degeneration score did not show the same association [23]. This finding suggests that FI reflects more than overall structural degenerative burden alone. Banitalebi et al. found that higher preoperative FI was associated with less improvement in leg pain 2 years after decompression surgery [22]. In addition, a 5-year longitudinal cohort study by Wesselink et al. showed that patients with lower preoperative MF FI achieved better outcomes after decompression. This suggests that MF FI may better reflect postoperative recovery potential [21]. Therefore, in LSS, FI, particularly MF FI, is better regarded as an imaging marker of chronic functional decline and limited postoperative recovery than simply as an indicator of stenosis severity [21–23]. Its clinical value lies mainly in identifying patients with similar imaging-defined stenosis severity but substantially poorer functional reserve and more limited postoperative recovery potential when FI is more severe [21–23].

Clinical significance of FI in degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis

In DLS, FI should not be viewed merely as an accompanying feature of general degeneration, but rather as an imaging manifestation of failure of the segmental active stabilizing system and the resulting secondary mechanical compensation [13, 24]. In a cohort of patients with single-level DLS, Liu et al. found that FI in the MF, ES, and PM was higher than in controls, whereas CSA of the MF and PM was lower, supporting a close association between FI and spondylolisthesis [13]. Da et al. further showed that the percentage of vertebral slip was positively correlated with FI, indicating an association between FI and slip severity [24]. Yang et al. also reported that in patients with DLS, FI increased with greater pelvic tilt and pelvic incidence, suggesting that FI participates in sagittal compensatory changes after instability develops [25]. Ding et al. further noted that degeneration in DLS is more pronounced in the MF and is concentrated at the slip-related segment, indicating that the MF may reflect segmental loss of stability more directly than other muscle groups [26]. Therefore, in DLS, the core value of FI lies in indicating segmental instability, sagittal compensation, and muscle-specific degeneration rather than simply reflecting generalized degenerative change [13, 25, 26]. At the surgical level, a systematic review has shown that preoperative FI is associated with mechanical complications such as nonunion, screw loosening, and adjacent segment degeneration, although most of the available evidence has been derived from broader populations undergoing surgery for degenerative lumbar disorders [27]. In fusion studies specifically involving DLS, MF FI has also been associated with an increased risk of adjacent segment degeneration [28].

The clinical significance of FI across different LDD subtypes is clearly disease-context dependent and should not be regarded simply as a common degenerative phenotype that differs only in severity. In LDH, FI primarily reflects segment-specific impairment of muscle quality, reduced recovery reserve, and increased recurrence risk [18–20]. In LSS, it more closely reflects inadequate paraspinal muscle functional reserve and limited postoperative recovery potential under chronic neural compression [21, 23]. In DLS, its significance further extends to segmental instability, sagittal compensation, and the risk of postoperative mechanical failure [13, 27, 28]. In other words, the value of FI lies not in serving as a uniform marker of overall disease severity, but in providing more targeted information for functional assessment, prognostic evaluation, and risk stratification across different LDD subtypes.

Clinical associations of paraspinal muscle fatty infiltration and sources of measurement heterogeneity

Existing studies suggest that FI serves as an imaging phenotype of reduced muscle quality in LDD and is associated with pain, functional impairment, and selected postoperative outcomes [14, 29]. Some studies have further suggested that FI may also be associated with an increased risk of adverse events, including nonunion, screw loosening, and reoperation [30–34]. This finding suggests potential value for risk stratification and prognostic assessment. However, inconsistent findings have been reported across studies, even within similar disease contexts. Importantly, this inconsistency has been observed even when comparable quantitative FI methods were used. Differences have been noted in statistical significance and in the outcome domains assessed. In this review, the divergence was not explained solely by the diversity of method types. A more critical issue was identified. Key parameters within the same FI assessment approach have not been standardized. In addition, core parameters and operational steps have often been reported incompletely. Reproducible measurement details have sometimes not been provided [35, 36]. As a result, reproducibility and cross-center comparability have been limited. To illustrate this problem, studies of lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) were summarized in parallel (Table 1). Across these studies, substantial variation was observed in several aspects, including whether muscle groups were analyzed separately and which sampling level was used. Variation was also observed in ROI boundary definitions and thresholding strategies, the reporting of scanner and sequence information, and the handling of confounders. Accordingly, conclusions were inconsistent. Some studies reported that higher FI was associated with worse postoperative functional recovery and worse pain outcomes. A lower likelihood of achieving clinically meaningful improvement was also reported [37–39]. Another study reported associations only for specific dimensions (e.g., leg pain), with no significant associations observed on function-related scales [22]. These discrepancies suggest that parameter-driven methodological heterogeneity may partially obscure true clinical signals or may exaggerate them (Table 1).

These parameters directly influence FI estimates and determine their comparability and reproducibility. First, it is critical whether muscle groups are distinguished. MF, ES, and PM differ in anatomy and function. Pooled measurements may therefore dilute associations between specific muscles and clinical outcomes [35]. Second, the sampling level and segment selection are also important. FI is typically higher in the lower lumbar spine than in the upper lumbar spine [40]. Third, ROI boundary rules and thresholding strategies (fixed versus adaptive thresholds) determine which pixels are classified as fat. FI values therefore change when pixel-intensity thresholds are altered (Fig. 3) [41]. In addition, robustness and generalizability can be affected by the completeness of reporting for scanner and sequence information. This information includes the manufacturer, field strength, coil, and TR/TE; for PDFF, echo settings and T2* correction are also required [42]. Reliability assessment and confounder handling can also affect robustness and generalizability [42]. When these elements are missing or defined inconsistently, FI values cannot be compared across studies. Reproducibility and cross-cohort comparability are then reduced. Evidence synthesis and multicenter translation are also limited [36]. Therefore, key factors affecting the reproducibility and comparability of FI assessment should be clearly reported in the Methods section [36]. Such reporting is necessary to reduce error introduced by methodological heterogeneity and to improve the reproducibility of FI research and its capacity for evidence synthesis.

Imaging assessment of paraspinal muscle fatty infiltration and key points for standardization

Core imaging indices for evaluating paraspinal muscle degeneration and fatty infiltration

Imaging-based quantification of paraspinal muscle degeneration has long relied primarily on measuring paraspinal muscle cross-sectional area (CSA) on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT). In patients with LDD, multiple studies have shown that reduced CSA serves as an important morphologic marker of muscle atrophy. Relative CSA (rCSA = muscle CSA/vertebral body CSA at the same level) is often used to partially adjust for differences in body size [7, 29]. However, CSA reflects only the total area enclosed by the fascia and does not distinguish contractile muscle from tissue replaced by fat or fibrous connective tissue. A systematic review further suggested that, when LDD-related symptoms and outcomes are interpreted on the basis of CSA alone, the observed associations are generally weak and inconsistent [29]. Accordingly, recent research has shifted from muscle quantity assessment, represented by CSA, toward muscle quality assessment, with FI serving as a core imaging phenotype. Additional indices, including functional cross-sectional area (fCSA; defined as the area of pure muscle) and proton density fat fraction (PDFF), have also been introduced to characterize paraspinal muscle quality and functional status more accurately [11, 44].

Although FI may reflect paraspinal muscle degeneration more accurately than CSA, no universally accepted normal reference range or pathologic threshold has yet been established. Current evidence suggests that FI generally increases with age, is higher in women than in men, occurs more readily in the multifidus (MF) than in the erector spinae (ES) or psoas major (PM), and is usually greater in the lower lumbar spine than in the upper lumbar spine. In addition, FI estimates may vary according to the measurement method used. Physiologic FI may also be present in asymptomatic individuals, whereas symptom-related FI is typically higher than in age- and sex-matched asymptomatic controls [44–46].

Therefore, the significance of FI in LDD cannot be interpreted in isolation from age, sex, muscle group, spinal level, and measurement method. Accordingly, in addition to reporting core indices such as CSA, rCSA, fCSA, and PDFF, studies should clearly state whether fascia, vessels, and superficial subcutaneous fat were excluded during ROI delineation (Fig. 4). These factors directly affect FI measurement and the comparability of findings across studies. This further highlights the need for a standardized framework for FI parameters and reporting [7, 11, 35].

Qualitative grading of FI

In clinical research, the modified Goutallier grading system is the most widely used qualitative approach. This system is based on visual assessment of axial MRI and classifies FI on a 0–4 scale. Grade 0 indicates normal muscle, grade 1 indicates occasional fatty streaks, grade 2 indicates fat < 50%, grade 3 indicates fat at approximately 50%, and grade 4 indicates fat > 50% (Fig. 5) [8, 36]. The method is fast and practical, requires no additional software, and has a short learning curve. However, it is subjective and is insensitive to subtle changes in FI. Interobserver agreement is also strongly dependent on reader experience [8, 36]. For these reasons, qualitative grading is best suited for screening and descriptive reporting and is not intended for precise quantification.

Semi-quantitative assessment of FI

Semi-quantitative FI assessment can be classified into two categories. The first includes signal-intensity ratio–based metrics, such as the muscle fat index (MFI) and related ratio measures. The second includes area-segmentation–based quantification of the fat infiltration rate (FIR) [36, 49]. Both approaches can be implemented using routine T1- or T2-weighted MRI sequences. MFI is typically derived by delineating ROIs for MF, ES, and PM within the same slice. Fat content is then inferred from signal-intensity ratios, either across muscle groups or between the target muscle and a reference tissue, and a muscle fat index is calculated. By contrast, FIR (Fig. 6) is obtained by outlining the target-muscle ROI on the slice of interest. A pixel-intensity threshold is then applied to segment high-signal fat pixels within the ROI. Fat infiltration is expressed as the proportion of fat-pixel area relative to the total ROI area (0–100%) [36, 50, 51]. Compared with qualitative grading, MFI and FIR are more objective, provide continuous measures, and typically show better interobserver agreement [36, 51]. However, these methods still rely on slice-by-slice manual ROI delineation and threshold-based segmentation. This workflow is time-consuming and remains partly subjective. As a result, scalability is limited in large-cohort or high-throughput studies. In addition, MFI and FIR are sensitive to ROI placement, threshold selection, and acquisition parameters. Without protocol standardization, systematic differences across studies are likely [36, 49].

Accordingly, MFI and FIR are best suited to single-center studies or longitudinal follow-up performed on the same scanner with identical sequences. These measures can also serve as an objective supplement for FI assessment in resource-limited or retrospective settings. For cross-center comparisons or meta-analyses, the thresholding strategy, sequence type, and key acquisition parameters should be reported in the Methods to improve interpretability and comparability [36, 48].

Quantitative MRI assessment of FI

Quantitative MRI assessment of FI primarily relies on chemical shift encoding–based water–fat MRI (CSE-MRI). In most protocols, multi-echo spoiled gradient-echo Dixon sequences or iterative decomposition of water and fat with echo asymmetry and least-squares estimation (IDEAL) are used. Water-only and fat-only images are acquired in a single scan. A proton density fat fraction (PDFF) map is then generated (Fig. 7) [52]. PDFF is typically calculated as the ratio of the fat proton signal to the sum of fat and water proton signals (0–100%). PDFF is widely considered the reference standard for FI quantification [53]. Although PDFF has a uniform physical definition, measured values can be influenced by the acquisition sequence and reconstruction algorithm [54]. To improve reproducibility and cross-center comparability, key technical details should be reported in the Methods. These include field strength, vendor, sequence type, echo settings, T1/T2* correction, and post-processing software [55, 56].

CT-based assessment of FI

In CT, tissue attenuation of X-rays is measured and expressed as Hounsfield units (HU), which reflect tissue density. Skeletal muscle typically shows higher HU values than adipose tissue. A reduction in muscle HU generally indicates increased fat content and impaired muscle quality. As intramuscular FI increases, mean muscle HU decreases progressively. It can be used as an indirect quantitative marker of reduced muscle density (Fig. 8) [57]. Compared with MRI, CT is easier to implement and allows osseous structures to be evaluated concurrently. CT is therefore useful when MRI is unavailable or when concurrent assessment of skeletal and muscular degeneration is required [57, 58]. However, soft-tissue contrast between fat and muscle is limited, and HU measurements are sensitive to acquisition parameters. Standardized protocols are lacking, and ionizing radiation is used. CT is therefore not recommended for frequent follow-up or for high-precision fat quantification. In studies requiring accurate fat quantification or long-term follow-up, MRI should be preferred [59].

Three-dimensional assessment and automated segmentation of FI

With advances in MRI and computing capacity, three-dimensional (3D) assessment combined with automated segmentation is increasingly used for FI measurement. Deep learning (DL) models based on convolutional neural networks (CNNs), such as U-Net and nnU-Net, have been used to automatically segment MF, ES, and PM on routine lumbar T2-weighted images [60–62]. Compared with manual ROI delineation and visual grading, automated segmentation can reduce measurement time and minimize operator-dependent variability. As a result, FI quantification can be made more reproducible. Whole-lumbar 3D segmentation also enables muscle volume and fat distribution features to be extracted. Spatial heterogeneity across muscle groups can therefore be characterized. FI–outcome associations in LDD may also be interpreted more effectively than with single-slice, two-dimensional metrics [47, 63]. However, DL models rely on high-quality annotations and consistent scanning protocols. Domain shifts across centers and sequences can reduce generalizability [60, 61]. In addition, most current 3D assessments are based on thresholding of T2-weighted images and therefore provide only “pseudo-quantitative” estimates. Systematic differences from PDFF are expected; the two measures are not interchangeable [47, 62]. Accordingly, 3D assessment and automated segmentation are best positioned as support tools for clinical research. Measurement burden can be reduced in large cohorts, but manual quality control and methodological standardization remain necessary. In the future, automated segmentation could be integrated more tightly with quantitative MRI workflows if acquisition protocols are standardized and external validation is performed. This integration may facilitate automated 3D FI measurement in clinical practice [47, 62, 64].

Radiomics- and AI-driven refined FI assessment and risk stratification

Radiomics and AI-based methods are expanding the scope of FI assessment. Unlike conventional metrics, which mainly reflect overall fat content, texture features can further characterize the spatial heterogeneity of fatty infiltration [6]. Dieckmeyer et al. showed that texture features derived from PDFF maps were significantly associated with paraspinal muscle strength and might provide greater predictive value than mean PDFF alone [6]. Guo et al. further showed that a machine learning model based on paraspinal muscle MRI radiomics had potential to predict poor postoperative functional recovery, suggesting a role for radiomics in preoperative risk stratification [65]. Meanwhile, the most mature application of AI in this field remains automated segmentation and quantitative analysis. Multiple studies have shown that deep learning models can achieve high-accuracy segmentation of paraspinal muscles on MRI or CT. They can also automatically generate volume, cross-sectional area, and FI-related metrics, with good agreement with manual measurements [60, 66]. However, current evidence is still derived mainly from retrospective studies focused on single disease entities. Substantial heterogeneity also remains in ROI definition, imaging sequences, feature extraction, and external validation [10, 66]. Therefore, although radiomics and AI may improve refined phenotypic characterization of FI and risk stratification, their clinical translation will still depend on further standardization of image acquisition, analysis, and reporting.

A three-layer standardization framework for FI assessment and key implementation considerations

Although a wide range of methods is available for FI assessment, the core challenge lies not in the lack of techniques, but in methodological heterogeneity across studies in technical pathways, measurement strategies, and result generation and reporting. Systematic reviews have shown that, even when the same class of FI assessment method is used, substantial variation remains across studies in scanning conditions, ROI rules, measurement levels, metric definitions, and reliability assessment [36, 69]. In addition, reporting is often incomplete. As a result, reproducibility, comparability, and the value of evidence synthesis are reduced [36]. On this basis, a three-layer standardization framework for FI assessment is proposed. This framework addresses the key factors influencing the reproducibility and comparability of FI research at the levels of acquisition, measurement, and result generation and reporting, and outlines corresponding implementation considerations (Table 3).

Layer 1. Acquisition standardization

FI measurements are first influenced by image acquisition and reconstruction conditions. In conventional T1- and T2-weighted imaging, sequence differences may affect gray-level distribution and fat identification by threshold-based methods. In CSE-MRI, acquisition parameters and reconstruction workflows can further affect the accuracy of fat quantification and cross-platform consistency [8, 48, 70]. Therefore, although muscle fat indices derived from different sequences and reconstruction settings may be correlated, they are not inherently equivalent and should not be assumed to be directly interchangeable [8, 48].

The goal of acquisition standardization is not to enforce a single scanner or sequence, but to ensure a minimum level of technical transparency [71]. At a minimum, studies should report the imaging platform (vendor, field strength, and coil), sequence type, scanning protocol, and key acquisition parameters, including TR, TE, slice thickness, slice orientation, voxel size, and bandwidth [36]. For CSE-MRI, echo settings, flip angle, water–fat separation method, reconstruction algorithm, T2* or B0/B1 correction strategy, and the name and version of the post-processing software should also be reported [70, 71]. For CT, reconstruction kernel, slice thickness, and HU calibration information should also be reported [57]. In radiomics studies, consistency of acquisition parameters and reconstruction workflows is particularly important because image gray-level distribution and texture features are more sensitive to sequence-related variation [36]. In short, technical transparency is a prerequisite for ensuring that FI results are traceable, interpretable, and comparable.

Layer 2. Measurement standardization

The measurement stage is where methodological heterogeneity in FI research is most concentrated. FI assessment involves multiple components, including muscle-group handling, sampling-level selection, ROI boundary definition, segmentation method, and threshold setting. Lack of standardization in any of these steps may reduce result reproducibility and comparability [36, 48].

ROI boundary definition is one of the major sources of measurement error. If boundary rules are not clearly specified, the actual structure being quantified may differ even when the same muscle group is nominally measured. This issue is particularly relevant at the posterior boundary between the multifidus (MF) and erector spinae (ES), where inclusion or exclusion of perifascial fat may introduce systematic differences [35, 36]. Therefore, ROI rules should be explicitly reported, including whether fascia, vessels, extramuscular fat, and subcutaneous fat were excluded. To improve methodological transparency, an ROI schematic should be provided in the Methods section whenever possible. MF, ES, and psoas major (PM) should preferably be reported separately. If combined analysis is performed, the rationale should be stated [36].

Sampling strategy should also be predefined. Because FI is often unevenly distributed across lumbar levels and between the left and right sides, the segment and level selected for measurement should be clearly specified, such as the upper or lower endplate level, disc-center level, or the level with the most severe lumbar degeneration. It should also be stated whether assessment was performed unilaterally or bilaterally, on a single slice, across multiple slices, or in three dimensions [47, 72]. In patients with prior lumbar surgery or spinal implants, the effect of imaging artifacts on level selection and the corresponding handling strategy should also be described. In addition, the segmentation method should be reported as manual, semiautomated, or fully automated. For threshold-based methods, it should be specified whether a fixed or adaptive threshold was used. For fixed thresholds, the exact value should be reported, whereas for adaptive thresholds, the method used to determine the threshold should be described. Different thresholding strategies should not be assumed to be directly comparable [48, 60, 62].

In summary, the purpose of measurement standardization is not to prescribe a single correct method, but to ensure that the measurement process is predefined and fully reported so that FI results are reproducible and comparable [36, 48, 72].

Layer 3. Result generation, quality control, and reporting standardization

Even when acquisition conditions and measurement workflows are similar, comparability across studies may still be limited because of differences in result generation, quality control, or reporting rules. Therefore, this layer focuses on standardizing the conversion of raw measurements into final study variables and on improving comparability through quality control and standardized reporting [42, 51, 54, 71].

This layer of standardization includes three main aspects. First, studies should clearly define how final analytic variables are generated, including whether the analysis is based on a single-slice value, a multislice mean, a segmental mean, a bilateral mean, a maximum value, or the value from the side with more severe fatty infiltration. For FI-related indices such as FIR, MFI, PDFF, fCSA, and rFCSA, definitions, calculation formulas, and units should also be explicitly reported [7, 51, 55]. Second, basic quality control requirements should be incorporated, including rater training, repeated-measurement design, and assessment of intra- and interobserver agreement. When automated or semiautomated segmentation is used, the manual review process should also be described [60, 62, 73]. For multicenter studies, information on cross-center repeatability or protocol consistency should be provided whenever possible. For artificial intelligence or radiomics studies, the feature extraction workflow, feature selection method, model construction strategy, validation approach, and external validation procedures should be further specified [60, 62, 70, 71]. Third, because FI may be influenced by factors such as age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and surgical level, studies should also report adjustment for key confounders [55, 74]. Overall, the aim of this layer is to establish definitions of analytic variables, quality control procedures, and minimum reporting requirements, so that FI can be advanced from a measurable imaging marker to a reproducible and comparable research variable [36, 42, 71].

Based on the three-layer framework described above, standardization of FI should not be understood as forced uniformity of a single technical pathway. Rather, it should be viewed as a layered strategy directed at the major sources of error: improving technical transparency at the acquisition level, standardizing measurement workflows at the measurement level, and harmonizing variable definitions, quality control, and minimum reporting requirements at the result level (see Table 3). This framework preserves the technical diversity of current FI assessment methods while providing a stronger methodological foundation for cross-study comparison, multicenter validation, and future applications in automated analysis, radiomics research, and clinical translation [36, 62, 71].

Clinical translational value of standardized FI parameters

The clinical translational value of standardized FI parameters lies not only in improving comparability across studies, but also in enabling FI to serve as an interpretable, trackable, and scalable clinical parameter [36, 71]. Only when acquisition conditions, measurement procedures, and result reporting are relatively consistent can changes in FI be interpreted with confidence [36, 54, 71]. Otherwise, whether FI is compared before and after rehabilitation interventions or during preoperative and postoperative follow-up, it remains difficult to determine whether an observed change reflects a true alteration in muscle status or merely measurement error [54, 71]. In addition, standardized FI parameters provide an essential foundation for multicenter studies, public database development, and artificial intelligence applications [60, 62, 66]. Only under relatively consistent standardized workflows can FI data acquired at different time points and from different sources be considered comparable and reproducible. This is essential for cross-center data integration, model development, external validation, and broader implementation [60, 62, 66]. Standardization should therefore not be regarded as an optional requirement external to clinical application, but rather as a prerequisite for the clinical translation of FI [36, 66, 71].

Method adaptability and cost-effectiveness of standardized FI parameters in real-world clinical practice

In real-world clinical practice, the choice of FI assessment method should not be based solely on technical accuracy, but should also take into account institutional resources, clinical objectives, and implementation costs [36, 53]. Here, cost-effectiveness primarily refers to the practical balance between implementation costs, including time, labor, and additional examinations, and the clinical value obtained.

For outpatients and routine inpatients, qualitative methods represented by the Goutallier grading system can be applied directly to existing lumbar MRI scans without additional imaging or complex post-processing. They are therefore better suited for use as initial screening tools in daily clinical practice and may be used for rough risk assessment and rehabilitation counseling [36, 64]. In addition, CT-based assessment and methods such as MFI can often be performed within routine image-viewing systems through ROI delineation and direct calculation, without requiring dedicated software. They therefore also have value for screening [51, 57].

When FI is used for preoperative risk stratification, comparison of rehabilitation interventions, or postoperative follow-up, the key requirement is stable comparability across repeated measurements over time [36, 54, 71]. Under this premise, standardized semi-quantitative MRI-based methods, such as FIR and MFI, can be used to quantify FI on routine lumbar MRI. These approaches are more suitable for perioperative risk stratification and longitudinal comparison during follow-up, although they usually still require additional software and post-processing steps [36, 51]. By contrast, quantitative MRI methods such as PDFF offer greater accuracy in fat quantification, better reproducibility, and stronger cross-center comparability. They are therefore more suitable for institutions with the necessary technical infrastructure and may be preferred for high-precision FI quantification, as well as for prospective and multicenter studies [36, 53, 54, 71]. Because FIR and MFI are more susceptible to methodological heterogeneity, their direct comparability across centers is relatively limited. These methods are therefore more suitable for single-center and retrospective studies. If they are to be used in multicenter settings, unified standards for acquisition, measurement, and reporting are required to improve comparability [36, 54].

From a long-term cost-effectiveness perspective, automated assessment and structured reporting represent key pathways for realizing the clinical value of standardized FI evaluation [36, 63]. Although initial development and deployment require substantial investment, these systems can markedly reduce the time and labor required for each analysis once they become mature [36, 63]. Compared with manual segmentation, which is time-consuming and highly dependent on operator experience, fully automated deep learning models can process a single case in approximately 21.9 s while maintaining high accuracy and consistency [63]. More importantly, automation makes opportunistic assessment, structured output, and large-scale deployment feasible. In related fields, AI systems for sarcopenia and body composition analysis have already been integrated into PACS, with an average processing time of approximately 4.12 s and a technical success rate of 100% [75]. These findings suggest that, once automation is built on clearly defined input standards and robust external validation, FI may evolve from a research-oriented quantitative marker into a clinical parameter that can be embedded in routine reporting workflows [62, 71, 75]. Thus, automation should not be considered a substitute for standardization, but rather an amplifier of its mature implementation [62, 63].

Overall, clinical translation of FI assessment should not rely on a single technical solution. Instead, methods should be selected in a stratified manner according to clinical objectives and resource conditions. Routine clinical care places greater emphasis on accessibility. Perioperative evaluation and follow-up place greater emphasis on the consistency of standardized measurement. Automated assessment together with structured reporting represents an important direction for reducing implementation costs and integrating standardized FI assessment into clinical practice [36, 53, 71].

Barriers to FI clinical translation and potential solutions

The clinical translation of FI still faces two major barriers. First, compared with more intuitive structural abnormalities such as disc herniation, spinal stenosis, or spondylolisthesis, FI primarily reflects deterioration in paraspinal muscle quality. Its impact on health is often gradual and indirect. As a result, its visibility and priority in clinical decision-making remain limited [36, 69]. Second, FI quantification involves multiple steps, including acquisition, measurement, and result generation, all of which increase the time and labor burden on clinicians. At present, a degree of mismatch remains between its potential clinical benefit and the additional resources required, which further limits routine implementation [36, 71].

In response to these challenges, a more practical implementation pathway should follow the sequence of “standardization first, validation second, and integration third.” First, a minimum consistency framework for acquisition, measurement, and reporting should be established to improve the comparability and reproducibility of FI results [36, 71]. Second, multicenter prospective studies should be conducted under standardized conditions to further validate the association between FI and clinical outcomes in LDD [1, 67]. Finally, automated FI quantification tools and structured reporting should be promoted on the basis of standardization to reduce the burden of manual measurement and improve feasibility in routine clinical practice [60, 62, 63].

Limitations and future directions in FI research

Current evidence suggests that FI is associated with pain, functional status, and postoperative outcomes in LDD, but important limitations remain. First, most studies have been single-center and cross-sectional; therefore, causal relationships between FI and clinical outcomes remain difficult to establish [69]. Second, within-method standardization has been insufficient, and reporting has often been incomplete. Substantial variation exists across studies in ROI boundary rules, selection of measurement level and segment, sequence and reconstruction parameters, and muscle-group reporting. As a result, FI estimates are difficult to reproduce, and cross-study comparisons and evidence synthesis are constrained [36, 50, 76, 77]. In addition, FI has often been assessed using two-dimensional, single-slice sampling. This approach limits evaluation of overall paraspinal muscle status and its clinical relevance in LDD [36, 72]. In practice, both semi-quantitative (MFI/FIR) and quantitative (PDFF) approaches still rely heavily on manual ROI delineation and post-processing. These procedures are time-consuming and prone to operator-dependent variability. Scalability is therefore limited in large-cohort studies [36, 72]. Deep learning–based automated segmentation can reduce manual workload, but current applications have largely been limited to single-center and single-sequence settings. External validation remains insufficient, and generalizability has not been established [36, 62]. Radiomics- and AI-based predictive modeling studies are still largely based on small-sample, single-disease retrospective designs, and substantial heterogeneity remains in feature extraction, model construction, and validation workflows [10, 66].

Future studies should incorporate three-dimensional assessment, automated measurement, and radiomics/AI analytic workflows under relatively standardized conditions for acquisition, measurement, and result reporting, in order to improve the reproducibility of FI quantification and support multicenter validation [36, 47, 60, 62, 66]. On this basis, multicenter data sharing and quality control under de-identification could be promoted. Age-, sex-, muscle-, and level-specific reference ranges for FI could also be established. In addition, integrated analyses incorporating paraspinal muscles, vertebral bodies, intervertebral discs, and sagittal parameters could be performed to better support clinical research and risk stratification in LDD [13, 55, 62, 67].

Conclusion

As an imaging phenotype reflecting reduced paraspinal muscle quality, FI has potential value for risk stratification and clinical assessment in LDD. At present, FI assessment remains limited by inconsistent key parameters and operational procedures within the same class of measurement methods, incomplete reporting, and continued reliance on two-dimensional, single-slice sampling and manual measurement. In response to these issues, this review summarizes the technical characteristics, sources of heterogeneity, and clinical significance of current imaging-based FI assessment methods. It also proposes a three-layer standardization framework together with key reporting elements to improve reproducibility and comparability across studies. Future work should build on standardized workflows and further integrate automated segmentation, three-dimensional quantification, and radiomics to advance FI assessment toward greater objectivity, reproducibility, and clinical translational value.
