Research on the impact of digital infrastructure construction on enterprise carbon emissions
Authors: Yuxia Du, Mingjie Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper uses the data of Chinese cities and A-share listed companies from 2010 to 2022 and utilizes the implementation of Broadband China as a quasi-natural experiment to assess the impact of digital infrastructure construction, as exemplified by Broadband China, on enterprise carbon emissions. By applying the difference-in-differences method, the findings indicate that developing digital infrastructure can help reduce carbon emissions in enterprises. This reduction is achieved primarily by improving the efficiency of energy utilization in urban areas and enhancing corporate green technological innovation. Additionally, the impact of digital infrastructure on lowering carbon emissions varies by corporation and region.Keywords: Digital infrastructure construction, Carbon emissions, Broadband ChinaSubject terms: Energy and society, Environmental economics, Sustainability

Introduction

According to the Copernicus Climate Change Service, the European Union’s climate monitoring agency, global average temperatures were recorded at 2.07 °C and 2.06 °C higher than the preindustrial average (1850–1900) on November 17 and 18, 2023, respectively. Global greenhouse gas emissions increased by 1.2% from 2021 to 2022, exceeding the Paris Agreement’s goal of limiting global warming to between 1.5 and 2 °C. As countries continuously miss their emission reduction targets, global temperatures are rising further, intensifying the climate crisis and increasing the frequency of extreme weather events. China’s CO2 emissions increased from 2.924 billion tons in 1997 to 11.408 billion tons in 2022, a 3.9-fold increase. The Chinese government announced on September 22, 2020, that it would increase its nationally determined contributions, implement more robust policies, and aim to peak carbon dioxide emissions before 2030 to achieve carbon neutrality by 2060. In December 2021, the National Development and Reform Commission, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, and other departments jointly released a plan titled "Promoting Green and High-Quality Development of New Infrastructure, Such as Data Centers and 5G, in Accordance with Carbon Peaking and Carbon Target Requirements." This plan states that data centers and 5G are strategic resources and essential public infrastructure that will support future economic and social development. It is also the most important link for energy conservation and reducing consumption in new infrastructure. The plan clearly outlines that constructing new infrastructure aids digital transformation across various industries, contributes to achieving carbon peaking, and establishes a strong foundation for realizing carbon neutrality. In light of the changing domestic and international economic landscape and the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic, businesses are taking the opportunity to embrace digital transformation as a way to alleviate operational pressures and achieve their economic objectives. However, they are also facing increasing pressure to simultaneously reduce their emissions. The development of digital infrastructure offers essential support for businesses undergoing digital transformation. However, further investigation is needed to understand how this infrastructure can aid in achieving carbon peaking and carbon neutrality.

In recent years, there has been a significant increase in research on carbon reduction by scholars, with this research developing extensively and in depth. The focus is mainly on the analysis of the causes of the impact on carbon emissions, as well as the influence of related carbon emission policies on the economy and decarbonization. Zhang Youguo1, Shao Shuai2, and Hao Liangfeng3 studied the impact of environmental regulations on carbon emission reduction. Additionally, Pan Jiahua4, Shen Hongtao5, and Liu Chuanming6 researched the emission reduction effects of market-based tools, such as carbon emissions trading. Duan et al.7 believe that economic scale and industrial structure are important factors affecting carbon peak, Zhou et al.8 believe that digital technology has played a significant role in climate governance and has become a transformative force promoting low-carbon transformation, mainly achieving carbon emission reduction through innovation capabilities, structural optimization and efficiency improvement. Yue et al.9 analyzed the long-term impact of the carbon border adjustment tax of the European Union on China’s foreign trade pattern and industrial decarbonization, Wang et al.10 analyzed the impact of carbon emission trading rights policies on enterprises’ green performance innovation and financial performance, Zheng Minggui et al.11 analyzed the impact of digital transformation on the carbon emission performance of resource-based enterprises, mainly by increasing production capacity utilization and enhancing internal control capabilities to improve the carbon emission performance of resource-based enterprises, Wang10 analyzed the green spillover effect generated by the disclosure of ESG performance of similar enterprises on the carbon emission reduction actions of key enterprises.

The impact of digital infrastructure on carbon emissions is currently mainly studied at the urban level. Xue Fei et al.12 believe that the implementation of the “Broadband China” policy can reduce urban carbon emissions through green technological innovation, Jing Guowen13 also believes that the implementation of the “Broadband China” policy mainly reduces carbon emissions through regional technological innovation and industrial structure upgrading, Chen Bo et al. (2023), Guo Feng et al.14, and Wang Zhen and Chu Erming15 all believe that the construction of digital infrastructure can reduce carbon emissions and improve carbon emission efficiency; Xu Wenwen and Zhao Yanyun16 believe that the construction of digital infrastructure can have a positive impact on ecological carbon sink capacity and green transformation potential; Xu17 believes that the digital process brought about by the implementation of the “Broadband China” policy reduces marginal emission costs through promoting green technological innovation and factor-biased technological progress; Nie18 takes the China Digital Economy Innovation and Development Pilot Zones as the policy research object and believes that distribution, technology, and agglomeration effects are the main mechanisms by which this policy promotes carbon emission reduction; Li19 believes that the implementation of the “Broadband China” policy has stimulated public to lead an environmentally friendly life through improving urban innovation capabilities, optimizing the industrial structure, and thereby reducing urban carbon emissions.

At present, the carbon emission effects of digital infrastructure construction have been verified at the city and regional levels, and the mechanism of its operation has also been largely clarified. However, the way in which digital infrastructure construction affects the behaviors of micro entities to achieve a reduction in carbon emissions, as well as the effect and mechanism of its operation, have not been verified. Broadband, as a strategic infrastructure, the popularization of broadband fiber networks is an important aspect of digital infrastructure construction. The “Broadband China” strategy was proposed by the State Council on August 17, 2013. In 2014, 2015, and 2016, 117 demonstration cities and clusters for broadband were approved in three separate batches. This initiative aims to establish a leading national broadband network in the designated pilot areas. The strategy is guided by the State Council and various departments, including the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, with minimal interference from local governments. Its primary goal is to enhance the country’s digital infrastructure, which is not directly linked to improvements in the regional environment. Therefore, it aligns with the external requirements for policy evaluation. Therefore, this paper also uses “Broadband China” as an exogenous shock to analyze the impact of digital infrastructure construction on enterprise carbon emission reduction and to clarify the underlying mechanism of the impact of digital infrastructure construction on carbon emission reduction.

Theoretical analysis and research hypotheses

Digital infrastructure primarily encompasses the latest generation of information and communication technologies, including advancements such as 5G, data centers, cloud computing, artificial intelligence, the Internet of Things (IoT), and blockchain technology. Additionally, it comprises various digital platforms that are built on these technologies. Digital infrastructure is a system driven by data innovation, built on communication networks, and focused on data computing power facilities. In addition to the “investment effect” attributed to traditional infrastructure, there exists a range of new types of infrastructure that can effectively improve efficiency and structure by leveraging digital technology, resulting in significant positive externalities. The development of digital infrastructure offers several benefits. First, it enhances the energy structure20 and improves energy efficiency21. Additionally, it supports stronger government and public regulation, which can encourage the establishment of a carbon trading market. Second, digital infrastructure improves the internal organizational structure of companies, concentrates human capital, and lowers transaction costs. These changes contribute to improved production efficiency22 and decreased energy intensity. Additionally, the development of digital infrastructure helps mitigate knowledge spillovers and technology diffusion stemming from the information asymmetry in businesses. This, in turn, fosters the advancement of green technology innovation and helps lower enterprises’ carbon emissions. This paper primarily explores how the construction of digital infrastructure can improve energy efficiency and enhance corporate green technological innovation. Following this reasoning, Fig. 1 illustrates the theoretical framework for analyzing the impact of digital infrastructure development on enterprise carbon emissions.

Theoretical analysis framework.

First, from the perspective of energy development, constructing digital infrastructure offers essential technical support and assurance for the exploration and development of new energy sources. It also facilitates the development and utilization of clean energy, ultimately achieving carbon reduction directly at the source, thereby increasing source reduction efforts. Second, from the perspective of energy utilization, constructing digital infrastructure benefits the energy industry by creating a comprehensive digital energy supply chain. It facilitates the transformation and upgrading of energy production, operational processes, and sales services while optimizing the efficiency of energy allocation. Additionally, it reduces the information asymmetry within processes related to energy production, transportation, storage, and consumption, ultimately improving energy efficiency. For non-energy enterprises, the widespread adoption of digital technologies can lower communication costs, monitor energy usage and carbon emissions, improve energy utilization efficiency, and allow timely adjustments in energy consumption and carbon emissions. Third, the construction of digital infrastructure provides important means and platforms for the government and the public to regulate and marketize carbon emissions. From a fundamental perspective, the carbon emission issue is a problem of externalization of environmental costs. Among the important ways to solve this problem, apart from government environmental regulations, another very important means is marketization. The construction of digital infrastructure provides infrastructure support for the marketization of the carbon emission issue. The realization of the carbon emission trading market can enhance the energy utilization efficiency of enterprises.

The construction of digital infrastructure facilitates the aggregation of human capital, improves the efficiency of knowledge dissemination, and reduces learning costs by increasing the acquisition and absorption of traditional knowledge. The internet, characterized by its universality and strong penetration, has transformed the production methods and organizational models of businesses23. The new business models, influenced by innovative production methods, have facilitated the accumulation of human capital. This development has improved both production efficiency and the allocation of resources within enterprises, ultimately leading to their transformation and upgrading24. An improvement in production efficiency and resource allocation by enterprises significantly reduces carbon emission intensity25. Digital infrastructure, particularly network infrastructure, plays a crucial role in reducing market information asymmetry and improving the accessibility of enterprise information. It helps alleviate the knowledge challenges that companies face in research, development, and innovation, providing a necessary support for them to develop new technologies26. Additionally, digital infrastructure enhances regional technological innovation by lowering transaction costs, optimizing resource allocation, and facilitating technology spillovers27. Furthermore, the construction of digital infrastructure can foster the advancement of green technology innovation by decreasing the costs of storing and transferring resources, increasing the availability of innovative knowledge, and accelerating the diffusion and outflow of this knowledge12. As a result, these advancements contribute to carbon reduction efforts. Furthermore, the development of information infrastructure can enhance this innovation by improving the level of informatization, increasing media attention, and strengthening corporate governance28. Additionally, the establishment of network infrastructure is conducive to increasing the level of green technology innovation among enterprises29.

Therefore, this paper proposes the following hypotheses:

H1: The construction of digital infrastructure can promote the reduction of carbon emissions by enterprises;

H1: The construction of digital infrastructure can promote the reduction of carbon emissions by enterprises;

H2: The construction of digital infrastructure can reduce the carbon emissions of enterprises by improving energy efficiency;

H2: The construction of digital infrastructure can reduce the carbon emissions of enterprises by improving energy efficiency;

H3: The construction of digital infrastructure can reduce carbon emissions by enhancing the level of green technological innovation of enterprises.

Research and design

Sample and data sources

As of 2019, a total of 117 cities across the country have been designated demonstration cities for Broadband China. This initiative is led by the State Council and the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology. It involves less local government intervention and does not have a direct connection to environmental improvements, thus aligning with the external requirements outlined in the policy assessment. The “Broadband China” strategy was implemented in phases, and a progressive difference-in-differences approach was used to evaluate the impact of digital infrastructure development on corporate carbon emissions. In this study, the cities designated Broadband China demonstration cities are considered the treatment group, whereas the remaining cities serve as the control group. The data for the “Broadband China” demonstration cities are obtained from the official website of the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology. The remaining city-level data come from the China Urban Statistical Yearbook and the China Statistical Yearbook.

At the enterprise level, this study selected samples from China’s A-share listed companies in the period from 2010 to 2022. The selection process involved several criteria: (1) companies classified as special treatment (ST, ST*) were excluded; and (2) companies with missing data were eliminated. Only those firms with complete data for at least three consecutive years were retained. Additionally, all consecutive variables were truncated to exclude values above 1 percent. The data used in this research were sourced from the CSMAR database, the annual reports of listed companies, social responsibility reports, official company websites, and environmental department websites and the China Urban Statistical Yearbook.

Explanation and description of the variables

The dependent variable

Enterprise carbon emissions are calculated via the methodology established by Wang Hao (2022). This approach assesses the carbon emissions associated with enterprises’ consumption of fossil fuels, electricity, and heat. The calculations follow the "Guidelines for Accounting and Reporting of Enterprise Greenhouse Gas Emissions" and the "IPCC Guidelines for National Greenhouse Gas Inventories" (2006). To calculate the total carbon emissions of an enterprise, first, the direct carbon emissions from sources that the company owns or controls are summed. Next, the indirect carbon emissions from greenhouse gases resulting from the electricity and heat purchased and consumed by the enterprise are added. The carbon emission data for listed companies are categorized into the following classifications: (1) combustion and escape emissions; (2) emissions from the production process; (3) waste discharge; and (4) emissions resulting from changes in land use, such as converting forests to industrial land.

The final emissions reflect the sum of the four items above. As a robustness test, we utilize carbon emission intensity as a key indicator, which is calculated by dividing total carbon emissions by operating income.

The independent variable

The “Broadband China” policy was taken as an explanatory variable. In this study, the independent variable is defined as 1 for the year that a city begins the Broadband China pilot and for all subsequent years, whereas it is 0 for any other year. As mentioned in the policy background, the “Broadband China” pilot program has been carried out in three phases. The program’s demonstration cities include provincial-level counties, urban agglomerations, and cities at or above the prefectural level. In this paper, we focus on cities at or above the prefectural level to eliminate any estimation bias that may arise from differences in administrative levels. The treatment group consists of 109 cities, whereas the control group includes the remaining 191 cities.

Control variables

This paper builds on the research approach of Yu Chang31 by controlling for additional factors that may influence a company’s carbon emissions. These factors include company size (size), company age (firm ange), roe (Sale), and the debt-to-asset ratio (tl). Furthermore, since the characteristics of an enterprise’s location can impact its carbon emissions, we include the regional population density (popind) and the level of regional environmental regulation (ER) as control variables. Parallel trends test

This paper estimates the coefficients based on the year prior to the implementation of “Broadband China”. The results show that the model basically passes the parallel trends test. Since Broadband China was established in batches, dummy variables were needed to set relative time values for the establishment of each policy implementation area. Before constructing the multiperiod difference-in-differences model for estimation, the identification conditions of the method need to be tested, and the model of the event study method is constructed.

The parallel trend test and dynamic effect analysis in Fig. 2 show that, compared with the base year of 2014, the estimated coefficients for each year before the establishment of the “Broadband China” pilot cities were not significant, and the P-values were all greater than 15%, indicating that there was no significant difference in carbon emissions between the treatment group and the control group before the establishment. Due to the lagging nature of policy effects, the estimated coefficients for each year one year after the policy implementation all passed the 1% significance level test. The policy effect was the greatest in the first year after implementation, and it could last for 5 years. In the fifth year, carbon emissions could still be reduced. The parallel trend assumption of the difference-in-differences estimation was satisfied. From the perspective of dynamic effect changes, the estimated coefficients before the establishment of the “Broadband China” pilot cities changed from not being significant to being significantly negative, gradually showing a certain improvement effect of carbon emission reduction, which can indicate that the impact of digital infrastructure construction on enterprises’ carbon emissions has an expected positive effect.

Explanatory variables lagged by one period

To mitigate the estimation bias caused by the endogeneity problem of the core explanatory variable and the enterprise characteristic variables, this paper re-estimates the first-order lag terms of both the core explanatory variable and the enterprise characteristic variables simultaneously in the model to test whether there is a lag effect in the implementation of the Broadband China policy on the carbon emissions of listed companies. After adding the first-order lag term of the core explanatory variable and the first-order lag term of the enterprise characteristic variables in Column (2) of Table 3, the coefficient of the core explanatory variable remains significant at the 1% level, verifying the previous benchmark estimation results.

Other policy effect tests

Concurrent policy parallelism may also have an effect on the baseline estimates in this paper. For example, the "low-carbon city" pilot program has also had an effect on the carbon emission performance of cities (Yan et al. 2025) and enterprises19. This paper collates all the cities under the "low-carbon city" pilot policy. If a city is a "low-carbon city", the identification variable low-carbon did value of the city is set to 1 and 0 otherwise. The results in Column (3) of Table 3 suggest that the impact of the Broadband China policy on the carbon dioxide emissions of listed companies is negative and significant at least at the 1% level. Thus, the main conclusion of this paper remains robust.

In addition, considering that some cities in the study sample may have both direct and indirect effects on reducing corporate carbon emissions while implementing the “Broadband China” policy and conducting “smart city” pilot projects, this paper re-estimates whether the cities are “smart city” pilot projects in reality (1). If a city included in the “smart city” pilot policy in year t, the variable smart did takes the value of 1 in year t and subsequent years and 0 otherwise. The results in Column (4) of Table 3 show that after controlling for the dummy variable of smart cities, the coefficient of the variable broadband did of “Broadband China” remains positive and significant at the 1% level, once again confirming the basic conclusion of this paper.

Placebo test

Considering that the reduction in carbon emissions in listed companies in cities implementing the “Broadband China” policy might be attributed to other factors as well, this study aims to verify whether the impact of the “Broadband China” policy on corporate carbon emissions is not caused by other random factors. To identify the randomness of the policy effect, this paper uses a placebo test to distinguish the accidental nature of the “Broadband China” policy’s impact. Following the approach of La Ferrara et al.32, based on the distribution of the “Broadband China” policy variable in the baseline regression, 500 random samples were drawn to construct "pseudo-policy dummy variables", and the model (1) was re-estimated to test the coefficient and P-value distribution. Figure 3 shows the distributions of the estimated coefficients and p values after randomization. The curve is the kernel density distribution of the estimated coefficients, with the estimated coefficients mainly at approximately 0 and the p values mostly above 0.1, which is not significant at the 5% level. This indicates that the impact of the “Broadband China” policy on corporate carbon emissions is not caused by other random factors. The conclusion obtained above is reliable.

Heterogeneity analysis

The empirical analysis above has verified from various perspectives the hypothesis that the construction of digital infrastructure, represented by the “Broadband China” policy, can exert the effect of reducing the carbon emissions of enterprises. However, the effect on reducing the carbon emissions of enterprises in different regions may be subject to regional factors and may vary due to variations in enterprise ownership, the different intensities of regional environmental regulations and different regional locations. Therefore, this paper examines heterogeneity based on four aspects: whether it is a state-owned enterprise, the strength of environmental regulation, whether the city is in the Yangtze River Economic Belt, and whether the city is located in the east, central or western region.

This article distinguishes whether a company is a state-owned enterprise based on the actual controller of the listed company. If the company is a state-owned enterprise, the variable SOE is set to 1 to distinguish different enterprise types. The first two columns of Table 5 report the estimated results based on the heterogeneity of enterprise ownership nature. It can be seen that for state-owned enterprises, the impact of the “Broadband China” policy on their carbon emissions is negative but not significant. The possible reason is that the carbon emissions of state-owned enterprises may be more affected by other administrative orders, but for non-state-owned enterprises, the implementation of the “Broadband China” policy effectively promotes the reduction of their carbon emissions.

Environmental regulation intensity

This article uses the proportion of the frequency of environmental protection-related words in the local government’s work report to represent the intensity of the local environmental regulation. For regions above the average intensity of environmental regulation, the variable ER_dummy = 1 is set; for regions below the average intensity of environmental regulation, the variable ER_dummy = 0 is set to distinguish the intensity of environmental regulation in different cities. Columns (3) and (4) of Table 5 report the estimation results of the heterogeneity of the intensity of environmental regulation. In cities with weaker environmental regulation intensity, the β coefficient of digital infrastructure construction is significant at the 10% level, indicating that the impact of digital infrastructure construction on corporate carbon emissions is more powerful in cities with weaker environmental regulation intensity. This result indicates that digital infrastructure construction can complement the impact of environmental regulation policies on carbon emissions.

Whether the city is part of the Yangtze River Economic Belt

We analyze the heterogeneity of the impact of digital infrastructure construction on corporate carbon emissions based on whether the location of a city contributes to the Yangtze River economy. If the city is located in the Yangtze River Economic Belt, the city takes the value of 1 and 0 otherwise. The estimates are shown in Columns (5) and (6) of Table 5. The results show that in cities located in the Yangtze River Economic Belt, the impact of digital infrastructure construction on corporate carbon emissions is significantly negative, whereas in cities outside the Yangtze River Economic Belt, the impact of digital infrastructure construction on corporate carbon emissions is not significant. The Yangtze River Economic Belt spans the eastern, central and western regions of China, covering 11 provinces and municipalities, with a population and economic output accounting for over 40 percent of the country’s total. It is an important pillar of China’s economy, with an enormous economic scale and a strong industrial agglomeration effect. It is a leader in innovation-driven development and industrial upgrading. The construction of digital infrastructure plays a more significant role in reducing carbon emissions in this region.

Whether the city is located in the eastern, central and western regions

The heterogeneity of the impact of digital infrastructure construction on enterprise carbon emissions is analyzed based on whether the city where the enterprise is located is an eastern, central or western city. If it is in the eastern region, it is assigned the value of 1; if it is in the central region, it is assigned the value of 2; and if it is in the western region, it is assigned the value of 3. The estimates are shown in Columns (7), (8), and (9) of Table 5. The results suggest that in eastern cities, the impact of digital infrastructure construction on corporate carbon emissions is more significant. As exemplars of innovation and openness, the eastern coastal areas have an economic foundation and industrial scale that can create better energy intensification effects, higher information transmission efficiency, and thus higher energy utilization efficiency and enterprise production efficiency. Western cities have a weaker economic foundation and rely more on high-energy-consuming industries (such as energy and metallurgy). Digital technology can quickly reduce carbon emissions by optimizing production processes (such as using IoT to monitor energy consumption). The combination of the western large-scale development strategy and digital construction forms a “policy dividend”, accelerating the penetration of green technologies. Digital infrastructure construction can play a better supplementary role in reducing carbon emissions, achieving a “leapfrog” effect in the impact of digital infrastructure construction on carbon emissions. On the contrary, the economic foundation and industrial conditions of the central region are not as good as those of the eastern region. It also bears the responsibility of transferring traditional industries from the eastern region. The proportion of traditional manufacturing is relatively high, and digital transformation has not been completed. Its technological catch-up effect is not as good as that of the west. Moreover, the implementation of policies has a less significant effect on reducing carbon emissions.

Conclusions and implications

In the context of global warming and digitalization, this paper focuses on the urban and enterprise levels, takes the implementation of “Broadband China” as a quasi-natural experiment, builds a difference-in-differences model using data from listed companies and cities from 2010 to 2022, and analyzes the impact of digital infrastructure construction on enterprise carbon emissions based on real-world scenarios. The results show that the construction of digital infrastructure can better promote a reduction in corporate carbon emissions, mainly by improving energy utilization efficiency and enhancing corporate green technological innovation. Heterogeneity analysis reveals that the impact of digital infrastructure construction on corporate carbon emissions varies across enterprise ownership, regions with different intensities of environmental regulation and different urban locations. In non-state-owned enterprises regions with weak environmental regulation intensity, the impact of digital infrastructure construction on corporate carbon emissions is more significant. In the Yangtze River Economic Belt region and the eastern or Western urban region, the effect of digital infrastructure construction on corporate carbon emissions is more significant.

Based on the research above, the following policy implications are drawn. First, digital infrastructure construction, represented by “Broadband China”, can promote corporate carbon emission reduction. Therefore, the pace of digital infrastructure construction should be further accelerated, and the role and mechanism of digital infrastructure construction in carbon emission reduction should be further expanded. The government can promote the construction of digital infrastructure in an orderly and steady manner on the basis of the current implementation of the “Broadband China” policy, making digital infrastructure an important tool for carbon peaking and carbon reduction. Second, it is estimated that the cities implementing the “Broadband China” policy will be closely connected with surrounding cities to jointly accelerate the construction of digital infrastructure, form a strong infrastructure network, and jointly promote carbon emission reduction. Given the heterogeneity in the role of digital infrastructure in reducing carbon emissions, on the one hand, it is necessary to further combine the enterprise ownership and intensity of urban environmental regulations so that can form a closed loop with the government’s environmental control policies to jointly play a role in reducing carbon emissions. On the other hand, in the non-Yangtze River Economic Belt and the central regions, due to the possible insufficiency of economic ties and innovation-driven development, the impact of digital infrastructure construction on corporate carbon emissions is currently not significant. Therefore, after creating a good demonstration effect in the eastern and western regions and the Yangtze River Economic Belt, experience promotion will be carried out in the central regions and the non-Yangtze River Economic Belt. At the same time, the industrial division of labor should be deepened, and the intensity of research and development with regard to green technology innovation should be increased. The role of digital infrastructure construction in improving energy efficiency and the energy structure at the macro level should be further leveraged. Third, at the micro level, the role of digital infrastructure in reducing search costs for enterprises, lowering transaction costs, and optimizing resource allocation and technology spillover effects should be leveraged, thereby promoting an improvement in production efficiency for enterprises to reduce carbon emissions.
