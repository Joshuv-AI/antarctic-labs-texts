Hippopotamus optimization–tuned sigmoid PID controller for load frequency control of a two-area thermal power system with renewable energy sources
Authors: Özay Can, Mustafa Şinasi Ayas, Ali Kıvanç Şahin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing penetration of renewable energy sources (RES) in modern interconnected power systems introduces significant challenges for load frequency control (LFC) due to increased nonlinearities, the governor dead-band (GDB) effect, parameter uncertainties, and continuous power fluctuations. These factors reduce the effectiveness of conventional fixed-gain controllers and make it difficult to maintain frequency and tie-line power deviations within acceptable limits under varying operating conditions. To address these limitations, this study proposes a sigmoid proportional-integral-derivative (SPID) controller for LFC in a two-area thermal power system integrated with RES. In the proposed approach, the controller gains are adaptively adjusted through a sigmoid-based nonlinear mapping, and the corresponding parameters are optimally tuned using the Hippopotamus Optimization (HO) algorithm by minimizing the Integral of Time-weighted Absolute Error (ITAE) performance index. The performance of the proposed HO-SPID controller is evaluated under four time-domain scenarios: a baseline case without GDB and RES, a GDB-inclusive case without RES, a case with variable RES effects, and a robustness analysis of the baseline system under simultaneous ± 25% and ± 50% variations in all system parameters. Additionally, to demonstrate the stability of the proposed controller, frequency-domain stability is examined using Bode analysis. Simulation results demonstrate that the proposed HO-SPID controller significantly outperforms state-of-the-art PID-based metaheuristic controllers in terms of ITAE minimization, settling time, and undershoot, while the robustness analysis under parameter uncertainties and the Bode-based frequency-domain assessment jointly confirm its stable operation.Keywords: Load frequency control, Sigmoid proportional-integral-derivative controller, Hippopotamus optimization, Two-area thermal power system, Renewable energy sources, Governor dead-band

Introduction

The fundamental objective of contemporary electrical grid systems is to ensure the provision of power to end users in a manner that is safe, dependable, and of excellent quality. In recent years, meeting this requirement has become increasingly challenging due to the rapid growth in power consumption driven by urbanization, global industrialization, and the proliferation of electronic devices. The equilibrium between energy production and consumption, which is vital for maintaining grid stability, has evolved into a complex optimization challenge that requires innovative solutions. Active power balance, a pivotal aspect of power system operation that directly influences grid frequency, represents the primary means of achieving this delicate equilibrium1. The grid frequency remains within permissible limits as long as the active power balance is maintained, at either 50 Hz or 60 Hz, depending on the geographical region. However, deviations from this equilibrium may lead to frequency fluctuations that, if unchecked, can harm electrical equipment and destabilize the overall power system. In other words, frequency declines when power demand exceeds generation, and frequency increases when generation exceeds demand. Such frequency-related dynamics highlight the critical need for an effective control mechanism capable of mitigating frequency deviations and preserving system stability. To address this requirement, LFC serves as a fundamental control technique that continuously monitors the system and regulates both system frequency and tie-line power exchanges within predefined limits2.

Despite this well-defined control objective, LFC has become significantly more challenging due to the increasing penetration of RES in modern power systems. In particular, the large-scale integration of wind turbine (WT) and photovoltaic (PV) generation into power networks traditionally dominated by thermal and hydroelectric generation introduces pronounced variability and intermittency into system dynamics. As a result, both the design of appropriate LFC control structures and the achievement of the desired regulation performance become considerably more difficult, giving rise to major challenges in LFC implementation and operation2,3.

To address these challenges, LFC has been extensively investigated in the literature under a wide range of operating conditions and power system configurations. Existing studies span from relatively simple single-area systems, which provide a convenient framework for controller design and fundamental performance analysis3, to more complex multi-area interconnected power systems, including two-area4,5 and three-area6 structures. Given the continuous exchange of power among interconnected regions to balance supply and demand in modern power grids, multi-area LFC studies have gained increasing importance because they more accurately capture dynamic interactions among different control areas. This shift toward interconnected multi-area operation, combined with renewable-induced variability and nonlinearities, has increased the control burden and motivated the adoption of more advanced LFC strategies.

Owing to this increased dynamic complexity, the control approaches employed in LFC have been significantly improved over time. In the early stages, traditional linear controllers such as proportional-integral (PI)7, proportional-integral-derivative (PID)8, and PID with filter (PID-F)9 were widely preferred in LFC applications and played an important role for a long period. Although these controllers have demonstrated satisfactory performance in many practical scenarios, they often fall short of achieving the desired/optimal regulation performance in power systems where uncertainties and nonlinear effects become pronounced, particularly under high renewable penetration. Therefore, the investigation and implementation of advanced controller structures that offer greater flexibility and robustness have become increasingly important.

In this context, substantial research has focused on advanced control paradigms to mitigate the limitations of conventional linear controllers. Representative examples include fractional-order (FO) controllers (e.g., FOPI–FOPD10) and fuzzy logic–based schemes (e.g., FPID11, FOFPID12,13, and adaptive FPID14). These approaches have been reported to improve LFC performance over classical PID controllers by offering enhanced flexibility and robustness. However, their effectiveness may still be constrained under nonlinear and rapidly varying operating conditions. In particular, fuzzy PID controllers often involve rule-based inference mechanisms that increase computational complexity, whereas fractional-order controllers rely on fixed fractional parameters and high-order approximations, which may introduce additional numerical burden. Consequently, there remains a clear need for alternative controller structures capable of ensuring robust and reliable LFC performance with reduced implementation complexity.

Once an appropriate controller structure is adopted to cope with the aforementioned nonlinearities and operating-condition variations, achieving the desired LFC performance becomes highly dependent on proper parameter tuning. In practical multi-area LFC applications, the tuning task typically exhibits a challenging optimization landscape due to nonlinear dynamics, coupled inter-area interactions, and RES-induced fluctuations, which often results in multimodality and limits the effectiveness of conventional gradient-based methods. For this reason, metaheuristic optimization algorithms with robust and efficient search capabilities have been widely employed for controller tuning. Representative examples include sperm swarm optimization (SSO)15, stochastic fractal search (SFS)16, grasshopper optimization algorithm (GOA)17, artificial rabbits optimization (ARO)18,19, whale optimization algorithm (WOA)20,21, chaos game optimization (CGO)5, dragonfly search algorithm (DSA)22, coyote optimization algorithm (COA)23, particle swarm optimization (PSO)24, the RAO algorithm25, jellyfish search optimizer (JSO)26, fox optimizer algorithm (FOA)27, and chaos quasi-opposition-based sea-horse optimization (CQOSHO)28. Although these approaches have demonstrated effectiveness in various settings, their relative performance remains problem-dependent. In line with the No-Free-Lunch theorems for optimization29, no single method can be universally optimal across all problem classes, which motivates continued research on effective and problem-oriented optimization strategies.

Motivated by these observations, this study develops an advanced LFC framework for a two-area thermal power system integrated with WT and PV generation. By combining the sustainability of renewable energy sources with the operational reliability of conventional thermal units, the considered configuration provides a realistic representation of contemporary hybrid power systems. To cope with rapidly varying operating conditions, an SPID controller is employed. By embedding a sigmoid function within the classical PID structure, the SPID scheme enables smooth and bounded gain adaptation, allowing the controller to accommodate operating-condition variations while keeping the gains within predefined limits. This bounded adjustment mitigates excessive gain excursions and reduces the likelihood of undesirable oscillatory behavior that may arise in unbounded adaptive schemes. The parameters of the SPID controller are tuned using the HO algorithm. HO maintains a balanced exploration and exploitation mechanism in the search space, enabling reliable identification of well-tuned SPID parameters. Consequently, the proposed HO-SPID approach provides improved suppression of frequency and tie-line power deviations in the considered two-area power system. Controller design

This section presents the proposed SPID controller for LFC. First, the fundamental principles of conventional PID control in the LFC framework are outlined, highlighting its main strengths and limitations. Then, the operational framework of the proposed SPID technique is described.

Conventional PID controller

This subsection introduces the conventional PID controller used in LFC. The control input for each area is generated through proportional (), integral (), and derivative () actions, as given in Eq. (9). The proportional term responds to the instantaneous control error, the integral term mitigates accumulated errors to reduce steady-state deviations, and the derivative term anticipates the error trend to improve the transient response. Owing to its simple structure and ease of implementation, PID control is widely adopted in practical LFC applications in power systems. However, in interconnected multi-area power systems, frequency dynamics are strongly coupled through tie-line power exchanges, and the system exhibits pronounced variability due to nonlinearities such as the GDB as well as fluctuations induced by RES. Since fixed-gain PID tuning is typically performed around a nominal operating point, it cannot adequately accommodate the resulting inter-area interactions and dynamic variations as operating conditions change. Consequently, the control performance may degrade under large load disturbances and RES-induced fluctuations, which motivates the bounded adaptive SPID formulation presented in the next subsection.

Based on this sigmoid-based gain adaptation mechanism, when the ACE remains small, the controller gains vary only within a limited range, resulting in PID-like behavior around the nominal frequency. In contrast, when the ACE magnitude increases due to large load disturbances or the intermittent nature of renewable energy sources, the sigmoid function progressively moves toward saturation, producing a stronger gain adaptation while remaining within the predefined bounds, which enables more effective suppression of frequency deviations and tie-line power oscillations. In this way, abrupt gain variations are avoided, control effort is limited, and frequency stability in the LFC system is improved. Scenario IV: robustness analysis

Internal or external factors can induce parameter fluctuations, potentially preventing power systems from operating within nominal conditions. Therefore, a robustness test is essential to assess the resilience of the system to such parameter variations. In this context, the system behavior is investigated by simultaneously varying the time constants of system components, along with the governor droop characteristic, frequency bias factor, power system gains, and tie-line synchronization coefficient, by ± 25% and ± 50%. The corresponding system responses under these combined parameter variation scenarios are illustrated in Fig. 12. The results demonstrate that all considered parameter variations yield consistent settling times for the system frequency and tie-line power deviations. This stability indicates that the simultaneous variations in time constants and other key system parameters do not adversely affect the settling time, thereby confirming the robustness of the proposed controller. Nevertheless, a discernible correlation exists between the magnitude of the applied parameter variations and the resulting overshoot and undershoot levels in the frequency and tie-line power responses. Specifically, the + 25% and + 50% variation cases tend to be associated with larger transient deviations, whereas the − 25% and − 50% cases lead to reduced overshoot and undershoot magnitudes. This behavior is consistent with expected control system dynamics and provides valuable insight into controller performance under parametric uncertainty. Notably, in all tested scenarios, the system responses remain within acceptable operational limits despite these combined parameter variations. This finding is particularly significant as it demonstrates the resilience of the system to parameter uncertainty, which is a crucial attribute for real-world power system applications subject to fluctuating operating conditions.

Bode analysis provides insight into controller behavior through indicators such as gain margin, phase margin, and bandwidth, which are widely used to assess robustness under different operating conditions and to evaluate disturbance attenuation over a wide frequency range. Classical Bode analysis assumes fixed controller gains; however, the HO-SPID controller employs adaptive gain terms , , and with that vary over time. These parameters, defined in Eqs. Conclusion

In this study, the LFC problem in modern power systems is addressed using an SPID controller whose parameters are optimally tuned via the HO algorithm. As a preliminary step, the optimization capability of the HO algorithm is systematically evaluated using nine benchmark functions and comparatively assessed against several state-of-the-art metaheuristic algorithms. This assessment confirms that HO exhibits strong convergence characteristics and provides an effective and reliable framework for controller parameter optimization. Building on this algorithmic validation, the HO-tuned SPID controller is applied to the LFC problem and its dynamic performance is thoroughly investigated in the time domain. The simulation results demonstrate that the proposed HO-SPID controller significantly reduces the settling time, effectively suppresses undershoot, and consistently achieves improved ITAE performance. Furthermore, the controller efficiently damps frequency and tie-line power oscillations under different disturbance profiles, adapts well to the intermittent and stochastic nature of renewable energy sources, and maintains robust operation even in the presence of considerable parameter uncertainties. To further substantiate the observed time-domain performance, comprehensive Bode analyses are conducted to evaluate the closed-loop stability characteristics in the frequency domain. The resulting gain margin, phase margin, and bandwidth values confirm that the proposed controller preserves satisfactory stability margins and robustness over a wide operating range, thereby supporting the effectiveness of the HO-SPID structure from a frequency-domain perspective.

Overall, the results indicate that the HO-SPID controller provides a reliable, stable, and computationally efficient solution for LFC in modern power systems with high renewable energy penetration, thereby enhancing the practical applicability of LFC strategies in renewable-dominated power systems. In future work, real-time hardware implementation and experimental validation of the proposed controller using the OPAL-RT platform will be considered.
