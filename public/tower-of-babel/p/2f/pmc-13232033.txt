From phenomena to phrasing: rethinking seizure classification through history
Authors: Aayesha J Soni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Soni argues that modern seizure classifications change too often, confusing clinicians and patients. By contrasting them with the stable, descriptive approaches of Hippocrates, Galen, and Ibn Sina, she calls for simpler, enduring language to improve epilepsy care.

The patient was still convulsing when I arrived—eyes rolled back, froth at her lips, limbs jerking and breathing in a shallow pattern. We moved methodically as we always do—rescue medication, airway, suction, oxygen—a choreography drilled by repetition. Amidst the urgency, a colleague asked how to record it: focal secondary bilateral tonic-clonic or generalized onset?

Someone muttered the new terms under their breath like a spell; another reached for the ILAE (International League Against Epilepsy) chart. The nurse glanced up from the bed, the parents stood frozen. Even here, in a moment that demanded clarity, the language itself slowed us down. In certain contexts, particularly where training levels vary, the evolving terminology can momentarily distract from the otherwise intuitive work of managing seizures.

Modern epileptology is awash with terminological makeovers. In just over a decade, clinicians have been asked to shift from ‘partial’ for ‘focal’, ‘complex partial’ for ‘focal impaired awareness’, and most recently, transition ‘awareness’ back to ‘consciousness’.

The ILAE has pursued these changes with good intentions: to align terminology with advances in neuroscience and to move away from stigmatizing terms like benign, malignant, idiopathic or cryptogenic. The 2017 update followed nearly three decades without revision; the 2025 version refines it further, contracting 63 seizure types into 21.1,2 This may outwardly look like refinement but to many of us working in the clinical field every day, it feels like a drift. Language that should guide action instead generates hesitation and administrative churn. The words keep changing, but the seizures do not.

The trouble is not the desire to classify but the way in which we now do it: by baking unstable theoretical constructs into the names themselves. When the construct moves, the name moves, and the foundation shifts beneath clinicians, educators and patients alike. Historically, writers who shaped our understanding of epilepsy used a different style: stable, phenomenological description with transparent causal theories (right or wrong) and clear guidance.3 In settings like mine, this instability carries real cost. I work in South Africa, a country of over 60 million people and 11 official languages, where I am the first female adult epileptologist. Each change in terminology reverberates through a fragile system: across translation, education and patient understanding. What may feel like refinement in well-resourced contexts becomes confusion where clinicians are few and language is already a barrier. In resource-limited settings, shifting vocabulary does not advance care; it interrupts it.

Hippocrates, Galen and Ibn Sina did not agree on mechanisms, but they did agree on clarity. Their work suggests three virtues worth reclaiming: describing what happened plainly, separating observation from interpretation and keeping names stable enough to support coherent care and teaching.

Hippocrates opened the treatise On the Sacred Disease with an audacious claim: epilepsy is ‘no more sacred than any other disease’.4 Its causes, he insisted, are natural and brain based. He described what he saw: ‘They lose their voice, they foam at the mouth, their hands are clenched, the eyes distort, and they become insensible.’ Whatever one thinks of his mechanism (Hippocrates proposed that phlegm from the brain obstructed passages leading to epilepsy), his rhetorical method is exemplary: plain signs first, theory second and never confounding the two. The link to care was immediate: diet, regimen and prognostic caution and even though the humoral theory has not aged well; the phenomenological backbone has. We still recognize the illness he described, almost 25 centuries later, because his language rested on what could be seen and not what was merely hypothesized. Could the same be said about the current ILAE classification?

Eight centuries after Hippocrates, Galen complicated the picture without clouding it. He declared epilepsy ‘not one disease but many’.5 He echoed the humoral model but added nuance stating that the aura was ‘a sensation that arises in one part and mounts toward the head’, that obstruction of pneuma (vital spirit) occurred in the cerebral ventricles and that a careful patient history was the centre of diagnosis. He described convulsions with loss of sense as distinct from other spasms and valued any intervention that connected observation to action, including the famous ligature to stop a rising aura ‘before the vapors ascend’. His pathophysiology was wrong, yet his logic was clear in situating a seizure onset in lived experience instead of metaphysics.

Ibn Sina’s Canon of Medicine was, above all, a synthesis. In Book III (Fen I, Tractatus V, ch. viii), he compiles causes (humors, ventricular obstruction, ‘vapors’ ascending from the stomach, spleen or limbs), signs (convulsions, loss of consciousness, frothing, stertorous breathing, postictal weakness), and remedies (regimen, herbal decoctions, ligature, cautery)6 (Fig. 1). He linked menstrual suppression to the accumulation of ‘toxic elements’ producing vapors rising to the brain. Much of this came from Hippocrates, Galen and Aretaeus and while it was incorrect in mechanism, it was lucid, structured and usable. As Owsei Temkin noted, Ibn Sina’s achievement was ‘to consolidate and transmit’, not to innovate, and it is precisely this exemplary structure that allowed later generations to move beyond him.5,7

Despite disparate cosmologies, Hippocrates, Galen and Ibn Sina shared three habits that made their writings endure:

(i) They anchored language in the visible. Seizures were described in ordinary terms: foaming, choking, stiffening, staring, loss of speech, confusion. Theories were appended, not baked into the labels themselves.

(ii) They kept names stable. Even when their causal ideas shifted, their descriptors changed little. Students could learn the language once and carry it forward.

(iii) They made the path from word to action obvious. A name implied what to do—diet, ligature, cautery—not just what to call it. This is why their work is still legible; and while their ideas died, we cannot deny that the clarity lived.

No one doubts the ILAE’s good faith. Simplification is welcome, serial reinvention is not; each iteration erodes trust that this wheel will stop turning. We cannot deny that serial rewordings blur communication, complicate records and risk semantic instability without clear gains in prognosis or treatment. Two tensions keep resurfacing. First, construct-driven labels versus experience-driven ones: when terms like ‘awareness’ or ‘consciousness’ are built on theories we cannot verify at the bedside, they over-promise precision and when the theory shifts, the word shifts leading to splintering charts, letters and conversations. Second, granularity versus utility: multiplying subtypes may look scientific, but they rarely change first-line care—something the 2025 contraction quietly admits without confronting the deeper problem of churn. Simplification in principle becomes complication in practice.

History does not tell us to return to phlegm, pneuma or vapors. It tells us how to talk about epilepsy in a way that survives our changing theories. A practical, historically literate approach would commit to three rules:

(i) Phenomenological primacy. The use of stable, patient-centred descriptors as the backbone: abrupt stare, loss of speech, bilateral tonic–clonic convulsion, automatisms, postictal confusion, lateralizing signs. These are what patients see, nurses chart and trainees can learn once. They do not change every decade because the phenomena do not.

(ii) Theory as annotation, not label. Place mechanisms (network spread, channelopathy, hormonal modulation) in structured notes rather than the name itself. This was Hippocrates’, Galen’s and Ibn Sina’s instinct: observe plainly; argue causes beneath. Ibn Sina too arrays causes and remedies under a heading and never inside it.

(iii) Renaming as last resort. Reserve renaming for when terms are harmful or misleading and not merely when constructs evolve. The concept is that it is better to stabilize a lean vocabulary than engineer a perfect but perishable one.

Hippocrates desacralized epilepsy with everyday words. Galen multiplied mechanisms without multiplying names. Ibn Sina compiled with lucidity and practical intent. None of them solved epilepsy. But they spoke about it in a way that survived their own mistakes. The ILAE’s task ahead should be not to abandon theory, but to ensure that the language of care prioritizes coherence over cleverness. A classification that clinicians can carry for 20 years, and that patients can learn once, would honour both our history and our future.
