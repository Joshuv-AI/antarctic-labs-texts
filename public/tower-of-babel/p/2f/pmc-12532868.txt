Multisystemic manifestations of a rare Glu89Lys (p. Glu109Lys) transthyretin amyloidosis: a case report of an East Asian female
Authors: Linhuan Huang, Houshi Zhou, Junling Chen, Ting Fang, Qi Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTransthyretin amyloidosis (ATTRv) presents with highly variable, multisystem phenotypes, complicating diagnosis.Case presentation We describe a diagnostically challenging case of ATTRv caused by the rare Glu89Lys (p. Glu109Lys) variant in an East Asian female—the first reported instance in this ethnic group. The patient exhibited a complex multisystem phenotype spanning progressive axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy with autonomic dysfunction, cerebral infarction, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, vitreous opacities, and unexplained cachexia, culminating in a 4-year diagnostic odyssey. The lack of clinical suspicion delayed definitive diagnosis, ultimately achieved through combined tissue biopsy confirming amyloid deposits and targeted genetic sequencing confirming Glu89Lys variant.ConclusionOur findings highlight the imperative for heightened clinical vigilance and early utilization of multimodal diagnostic strategies in suspected ATTRv cases.

Background

Transthyretin amyloidosis (ATTR) is an uncommon multisystem disease. It is caused by deposition of misfolden mutant transthyretin(TTR)protein in multiple organs [1]. Diagnosing ATTR can be challenging of the numerous vague symptoms and signs that could be attributed to other diseases. Because of the innovative, effective treatments that can slow disease progression, early diagnosis is important. ATTR can be categorised as two types: wild-type ATTR(ATTRwt), or hereditary ATTR(ATTRv) [2]. There are over 140 different pathogenic TTR gene mutations identified worldwide [3], Val30Met is the most common type [4]. Here we reported the clinical and molecular manifestation of ATTRv in an East Asian female with a rare Glu89Lys mutation.

Case presentation

A 58-year-old Chinese woman presented to our hospital with progressive paresthesias, gait instability, blurred vision, and unexplained 15 kg weight loss over 4 years. Initial symptoms—bladder urgency and intermittent constipation—appeared at the age of 54. Several months later, ascending numbness and paresthesias began in her feet, spreading to bilateral distal upper and lower extremities within 6 months. Approximately 18 months after symptom onset, she was diagnosed with bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome(CTS) and peripheral neuropathy at a local hospital but showed no improvement with mecobalamin. Six months later, symptoms worsened—including hand weakness, cold-like pain in bilateral lower extremities, and blurred vision—promoting re-diagnosis of peripheral neuropathy. One year of prednisolone therapy provided no benefit. Symptoms progressed slowly over the following year, then deteriorated markedly in the 8 months preceding admission with worsening gait instability, exertional dyspnea, and severe blurred vision. She had no history of smoking, hypertension or diabetes. The patient reported a paternal family history of limb numbness: her father (died at 64) and uncle (died at 67). They also had unspecified heart disease, and her brother experiences mild limb numbness.

Neurological examinations revealed distal atrophy and weakness of all extremities. Deep tendon reflexes were absent in all extremities. She had severe superficial sensory deficit in distal lower extremities and proprioceptive disturbances, manifested as the deficit of kinesthesia position sense and vibration sense. The Romberg test was positive. No dysarthria or dysphagia was found.

Electromyography showed symmetric axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy. The compound muscle action potential(CMAP)amplitudes remarkable declined in bilateral median, ulnar, tibial nerves. Sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) amplitudes were also significantly decreased in bilateral median, ulnar, tibial and peroneal nerves. Transthoracic echocardiogram(TTE) demonstrated left atrial enlargement(41 mm left atrial diameter) and severely reduced left ventricular systolic function, quantified by left ventricular ejection fraction(LVEF) of 35% and fractional shortening(FS) of 16%. Myocardial hypertrophy was present with increased interventricular septum(16. 3 mm IVS diameter) and left ventricular posterior wall (LVPW) thickness(12. 9LVPW diameter). Small pericardial effusion was present. Bilateral lens opacity and right retinal tear were found under ophthalmic examination. Ocular Ultrasound revealed bilateral vitreous opacities. Despite genetic counseling emphasizing the autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, all first-degree relatives had consequently refused predictive genetic testing to date, citing personal reservations.

While tafamidis was initiated as disease-modifying therapy following its recent inclusion in China’s National Reimbursement Drug List (NRDL), treatment adherence remained suboptimal. The patient’s socioeconomic constraints render even the subsidized copayment (approximately ¥3, 200/month post-reimbursement) financially unsustainable long-term. No alternative disease-specific therapies are currently accessible through domestic healthcare channels.

Discussion

Globally, ATTR is a rare disease. It is caused by mutations that destabilize the TTR protein (ATTRv) or the accumulation of non-mutated TTR protein (ATTRwt) [5, 6]. Among the ATTRv patients worldwide, Val30Met(p. Val50Met) remained the most prevalent pathogenic variant.

The p. Glu109Lys variant is a rare cause of ATTRv. This variant has been classified as “Pathogenic/likely pathogenic” in the ClinVar database. The first case of ATTRv due to the p. Glu109Lys variant was reported in 2000 by Nakamura et al. [7]. The largest cohort study of patients with Glu89Lys(p. Glu 109Lys) ATTRv to date was described in 2023 by Fernando de Frutos et al. [8]. There are only other 7 case reports worldwide. This is the first report of ATTRv with p. Glu109Lys variant in East Asia. It expands the ethnic diversity of ATTRv genotypes.

The phenotypic presentation of ATTR is heterogeneous in different regions and races. Patients with ATTRv typically present with a predominant neuropathy or predominant cardiomyopathy phenotype, or a mixed phenotype [9, 10]. Ophthalmopathy (e.g., glaucoma, vitreous opacities), leptomeningeal amyloidosis (e.g., transient focal neurologic episodes, subarachnoid and/or intracerebral hemorrhages) and nephropathy can coexist in symptomatic patients [11–14]. ATTRwt most often causes cardiomyopathy [15]. Among the 34 symptomatic ATTR patients carrying the p. Glu109Lys pathogenic gene reported in the past 7 studies, the predominant phenotype was mixed phenotype (79%), followed by neurologic phenotype (9%) and cardiac phenotype (12%) [7, 8, 12, 16–19] (Table 2).

Our patient exhibited a mixed phenotype encompassing neuropathy, cardiomyopathy, and ocular involvement. The neuropathy presented as a sensorimotor polyneuropathy, accompanied CTS, ischemic stroke and autonomic dysfunction. This represents the first documented case of cerebral infarction in ATTRv with p. Glu109lys variant, expanding the phenotypic spectrum of this variant to include cerebrovascular involvement. Autopsy and imaging studies have confirmed the presence of cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) in ATTR patients with the Val30Met mutation [20, 21], although this typically manifests as cerebral hemorrhages. Our patient’s severe cardiac involvement—including hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and left atrial enlargement, both established risk factors for atrial fibrillation and intracardiac thrombus formation—suggests that cardioembolic mechanisms may contribute to the pathogenesis [22].

Early diagnosis is important because the long-term extension studies had demonstrated earlier therapeutic intervention was associated with better survival and greater benefit [23–25]. But diagnosing ATTRv in non-endemic regions presents significant challenges. The mean interval from onset to the diagnosis was reported up to be 3–5 years in non-endemic areas [26–28]. Compounding this difficulty is the striking phenotypic mimicry, where ATTRv symptoms closely resemble more common disorders such as chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP), diabetic neuropathy, idiopathic axonal polyneuropathy, or primary aging-related cardiomyopathy. Up to 20% of the cases with peripheral polyneuropathy were misdiagnosed and treated for CIDP instead [26]. Misdiagnosis was observed in 26. 6% of the symptomatic Patients in Cruz’s study, with CIDP being the most common diagnosis [29]. In Yann’s survey about the IVIg-NR patients with suspected CIDP who underwent TTR genetic testing, 13. 7% of their TTR mutation were positive suspected CIDP patients tested positive for a TTR mutation [30]. Furthermore, the disease exhibits a highly heterogeneous presentation, manifesting variably as sensorimotor polyneuropathy, autonomic dysfunction, cardiomyopathy, CTS, or ocular issues, without a single definitive pattern. Moreover, incomplete or absent family histories can effectively mask the hereditary nature of ATTRv.

Despite these challenges, certain “red flags” should prompt clinicians to consider ATTRv. Pierre Lozeron’s reported that pain, dysautonomia, small fiber sensory loss above the wrists, upper limb weakness, and absence of ataxia were predictors of ATTR neuropathy[31]. Sekijima suggested that the coexistence of a family history, autonomic dysfunction, cardiac involvement, CTS, gastrointestinal disturbances, unexplained weight loss and resistance to immunotherapy was a red flag for potential ATTRv amyloidosis [32]. The Chinese consensus on transthyretin amyloidosis polyneuropathy (ATTR-PN) recommends that the diagnosis of ATTR-PN should be considered in adult patients presenting with predominantly sensory and autonomic peripheral neuropathy or bilateral CTS, with or without family history, particularly when accompanied by involvement of one or more systemic organs including cardiac, ocular, renal, or central nervous system manifestations.

Our patient required over four years to receive a confirmed diagnosis. The clinical presentation of our patient, characterized by axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy with autonomic involvement, suspected CTS, cardiac manifestations, vitreous opacities, unexplained weight loss and poor response to immunotherapy, was highly suggestive of ATTR. This diagnostic suspicion prompted us to perform neuromuscular biopsy and genetic testing for confirmation. In patients presenting with neuropathy lacking a clear common etiology, pursuing biopsy and genetic confirmation becomes paramount, especially if ATTRv is suspected based on clinical features or red flags. Genetic testing identifies pathogenic TTR variants, confirming ATTRv, enables cascade testing of at-risk relatives for early diagnosis and intervention, and provides critical information for genetic counseling and reproductive planning.

The recent inclusion of tafamidis in China’s NRDL marks a crucial step forward in ATTRv management. But its long-term prohibitive out-of-pocket burden poses a critical barrier to adherence, as starkly illustrated in this case. This case demonstrates a widespread affordability challenge: sustaining treatment costs remain prohibitive for many ATTRv and rare disease patients in healthcare systems like China’s. We provided some supportive care plan to the patient, including symptom-specific management, functional and nutritional support, further genetic counseling and mental health support addressing depression and anxiety related to progressive disability. While these measures can not alter disease progression, they significantly impact quality of life and reduce emergency presentations.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration in this case report. First, the inability to obtain genetic testing in first-degree relatives due to their refusal prevented definitive assessment of variant pathogenicity. Additionally, the lack of genetic data and detailed clinical records for deceased paternal relatives (father and uncle) hampered conclusive evaluation of variant origin and penetrance.

Conclusions

We presented a case demonstrating a prolonged diagnostic odyssey of ATTRv caused by the p. Glu109Lys pathogenic variant. This case highlights the importance of suspecting ATTR and conducting further investigations when neurologists encounter patients presenting with multiple sensorimotor polyneuropathy accompanied by associated features such as CTS, autonomic symptoms, cardiac manifestations, or ocular involvement. Expeditious establishment of the diagnosis of ATTR and early initiation of treatment substantially improve clinical outcomes in affected patients.
