A dynamic yard space reservation algorithm based on reward-penalty mechanism
Authors: Beng Xuan, Chengji Liang, Xiaoming Yang, Haobin Li, Zhen Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Increasing throughput at container terminals leads to higher yard occupancy rates, reduced yard service capacity, and inefficient use of space. These issues result in longer waiting times for trucks, underutilized yard slots, and longer distances between berth positions and the yard, particularly under high occupancy conditions. Traditional methods for allocating yard space fail to adequately consider dynamic container arrival patterns, conflict between container operations, and efficient use of yard resources. This paper introduces an innovative approach to improve yard efficiency by dividing yard container retrieval into time intervals and dynamically reserving yard slots within each interval. This method aims to minimize operational conflicts, reduce distances between blocks and berths, and avoid excessive reservations. We propose a dynamic reservation algorithm based on a reward-penalty mechanism to handle the problem's complexity and real-time demands. Furthermore, a time-series reward-penalty mechanism, which considers past reservation feedback and future reservation potential, ensures the concentration and continuity of container groups in the yard. Our evaluation metrics and multiple experimental scenarios demonstrate that this method significantly enhances yard efficiency and overall port productivity compared to static and other dynamic algorithms.

Introduction

In the context of globalization and containerization, maritime container terminals play a critical role of the global logistics network. Container terminals are critical nodes in the maritime logistics system, specifically designed for loading, unloading, storing, and transshipping containerized cargo. To ensure the seamless transfer of goods between maritime and land-based transportation systems, these terminals utilize advanced handling equipment and state-of-the-art information management systems. With the continuous advancement of equipment, the production efficiency on the quay side of container terminals has significantly improved, shifting the production bottleneck from the quay side to the land side [1,2]. As an essential component of container terminals, the yard is a designated area within the terminal specifically for storing containers. It is typically divided into import yards, export yards, and transshipment yards. Import yards are used to store containers that have just been unloaded from ships, export yards are for containers awaiting loading onto ships, and transshipment yards temporarily store containers that are in transit. Effective management of yard space is crucial for maximizing resource utilization and ensuring the quick retrieval of containers, thereby enhancing the overall operational efficiency of the terminal.

The yard occupancy rate refers to the ratio of the actual storage capacity used to the total capacity in container yards. It is typically expressed as a percentage, reflecting the current degree to which containers are actually stored in the yard. In practice, during the construction phase of a container yard, there is typically a predetermined optimal yard occupancy rate and a maximum yard occupancy rate [3]. Fig. 1 is the cost variation of yard operations with yard occupancy rate. As observed from Fig.1, as the yard occupancy rate increases, the cost of yard operations gradually rises when it has not yet reached the optimal occupancy rate. However, as the yard occupancy rate continues to increase, the cost of yard operations experiences a rapid escalation. As the yard occupancy rate approaches the maximum yard occupancy rate, the cost increase becomes more gradual. The underlying cause of this phenomenon is that once the yard occupancy surpasses its optimal threshold, resource and operational conflicts within the yard intensify significantly. This leads to a scarcity of available resources and subsequent production bottlenecks, which hinder the overall efficiency of yard operations.Fig. 1Cost variation of yard operations with yard occupancy rate.Fig. 1

In yard management, resources are primarily categorized into two key elements: storage space resources and operational equipment resources. Storage space resources refer to specific areas and stacking structures used for container storage, which require effective planning and utilization to maximize storage density and accessibility. Operational equipment resources include yard cranes, container trucks, and other mechanical devices responsible for container loading, unloading, shifting, and transportation, directly impacting yard operational efficiency and service capacity.

Lower stacking densities typically indicate relatively abundant storage space and equipment resources in the yard, capable of meeting the operational demands of quay cranes and external trucks. However, as container terminal throughput rapidly increases, yard management under high stacking densities faces numerous challenges. Under such conditions, traditional methods of yard space allocation often fail to efficiently utilize resources, leading to decreased productivity and increased operational conflicts, thereby affecting overall terminal operational efficiency.

The container terminal yard is a dynamic and discrete logistics system [4]. Yard conditions change continuously over time, making yard space allocation [5,6] a dynamic issue [7]. When addressing yard space allocation, it is essential to take a dynamic perspective into account. Allocating space for container groups should consider not only the current yard status but also anticipate the future operational states. Additionally, a holistic view is necessary. Container groups entering the yard vary in terms of resource quality, container quantity, and turnaround time. Therefore, it is crucial to allocate yard slot resources from advantageous zones to container groups that are better suited for maximizing resource utilization. The goal for a terminal is to achieve the highest possible productivity with the least cost under limited resources.

Woo et al. [8] was one of the first to formally introduce the space reservation model, which is distinct from yard planning [9], yard templates [10], and sub-block models [11]. The space reservation model is more flexible and adaptable, with objectives that differ from the other three models. The space reservation model primarily focuses on container groups, which typically refer to a set of containers with similar attributes that can be stacked together without causing container flips. For example, containers with the same vessel, size, type, discharge port, and related attributes are considered a container group. In contrast, the other three models are primarily used for broad categorizations such as shipping lines, services, vessels, or ports.

In this paper, addressing the spatial allocation problem in container terminal yards, a novel yard space allocation method is proposed, along with a dynamic yard space reservation algorithm based on a reward-penalty mechanism. This method aims to maximize yard service capacity and optimize yard space resource utilization under any yard occupancy rate. Literature review

Many researchers and research teams have previously investigated the Yard Space Allocation Problem (YSAP) and put forth various strategies and methods to address it. Based on the nature of the problems studied and the approaches used in the literature, this paper categorizes the literature into two broad categories: static method literature and dynamic method literature, for the sake of simplicity.

Static method literature

This section will provide an overview of the key findings and approaches in the static SAP domain. Most of the research on static problems primarily explores scenarios within a static environment, where the impact of environmental changes is not considered during the research process. Kim et al. [12] studied the spatial allocation of import containers, establishing a cost model with the objectives of optimal storage space and the optimal number of cranes for decision-making. Chen et al. [10] developed two heuristic methods and proposed a hybrid heuristic approach. Extensive experimentation indicated that the newly introduced hybrid method demonstrated significant efficacy. Lee et al. [11] primarily focused on yard management in transshipment hub ports. The authors established a mixed-integer programming model with the goals of minimizing the number of yard cranes and minimizing space utilization, which was solved using CPLEX. Given the scale and complexity of this model, two heuristic algorithms were also developed. Ambrosino et al. [13] introduced a hybrid 0/1 linear programming model and a heuristic method to define storage rules, aiming to maximize the reduction of space utilization in export yards.

There also have been a variety of works that addressed the challenge of yard space allocation in container terminals under uncertain conditions. Zhen [14] examined the problem of yard space allocation in the context of an uncertain maritime market. The author introduced a mathematical model and developed a metaheuristic approach to tackle real-world scenarios, especially in large-scale settings. Zhen et al. [15] extended their research to multi-period yard layout planning in container terminals. The authors proposed a multi-period yard layout planning model aimed at minimizing transportation costs and addressing yard traffic congestion issues. The authors also presented solution methods based on local branching and particle swarm optimization. Yang et al. [16] focused on finite spatial and mechanical resources in the yard and established a stochastic optimization model. Given the complexity of the model, a two-stage decomposition strategy-based harmony search algorithm was designed to solve the proposed model.

A few works have been published on determining the yard space allocation problem by combining it with the Container Slot Allocation Problem (CSAP). Chen et al. [17] and Yu et al. [18] both proposed two-stage methods aimed at balancing workload within container zones and minimizing the distance between container zones and berths. Chen's work [17] focused on yard space allocation for export containers at seaports. In the first stage, they utilized a mixed-integer programming model to determine container yard areas (in terms of yard slots). In the second stage, a mixed-sequence stacking algorithm was employed to determine the precise storage locations for each container. Yu [18] introduced a berth-sharing strategy and constructed a two-stage mathematical model based on this strategy. In the first stage, the target berths were determined with the objective of workload balancing and minimizing transportation distances. In the second stage, container slots were selected based on the results from the first stage, with the goal of minimizing yard crane movements.

Furthermore, some researchers have integrated data analytics-related methods into yard space allocation. Maldonado et al. [19] presented a Decision Support System (DSS) based on data analysis and mathematical models. This DSS was designed to optimize the allocation of storage space for import containers at ports.

In prior research, many researchers and their teams have proposed various methods to address the yard space allocation problem. These approaches encompass rule-based strategies, exact solution methods, and heuristic algorithms, among others. However, these studies often had limitations, as they typically focused on specific aspects of the problem and often assumed the existence of abundant resources without considering resource scarcity scenarios. Furthermore, container terminals operate in dynamic environments, yet the aforementioned researchers commonly treated them as static environments and did not incorporate the dynamics of yard operations.

Dynamic method literature

The dynamic nature of yard space allocation in container terminals has led many researchers to approach the problem from a dynamic perspective. Zhang et al. [7] were among the pioneers in introducing a rolling-horizon approach to tackle this challenge. The authors divided time into segments, and within each segment, the problem was decomposed into two stages. The first stage focused on balancing the workload in each container zone, while the second stage aimed to minimize the transportation distance between blocks and berths. Woo et al. [8] addressed the yard space allocation problem for export containers by introducing the concepts of space demand and space reservation. Their study aimed to minimize the number of reserved spaces and proposed four strategies for space reservation, along with a forecasting method to predict the space demand for export containers. Zhen [20] implemented a real-time Decision Support System (DSS) for dynamic yard space allocation at the sub-block level, considering uncertain factors during ship loading and unloading. The author also introduced evaluation metrics such as yard utilization, yard crane (YC) utilization, loading speed, and fitness to assess the effectiveness of the DSS's space reservation. Wang et al. [21] transformed the problem into a three-dimensional yard allocation problem with a time dimension (3DYAPT). This was modeled as an integer linear programming problem. Wang developed a dynamic shape adjustment and placement algorithm based on simulated annealing (SA-DSAP) to address the 3DYAPT.

There are a few dynamic methods have been published for determining the Container Slot Allocation Problem (CSAP). Park et al. [22] and Park et al. [23] introduced online search algorithms. Park T.'s algorithm [22] dynamically adjusts and optimizes stacking strategies by continuously generating variants of overlay strategies and evaluating their performance in practical applications. Park, H. J [23]. proposed a data-driven dynamic stacking strategy consisting of two modules: the first module generates a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) specific to each container group for weight classification, while the second module implements the data-driven dynamic stacking strategy as an online algorithm to determine real-time storage locations for incoming containers. Both dynamic and online algorithms presented by these researchers effectively address the problem of container stacking allocation in specific scenarios.

These aforementioned researchers have approached the container yard space allocation problem from a dynamic perspective and have successfully applied dynamic algorithms to practical operations, achieving significant results. However, their focus has been primarily on the inbound process of containers, aiming to balance the workload associated with container arrivals. They did not consider other yard operations and overlooked the overall production capacity of the yard. Additionally, these studies did not address issues related to resource scarcity and resource conflicts.

Summary

In summary, the preceding yard space allocation methods and strategies have exhibited certain limitations, particularly when operating under high-density conditions. Static methods, in particular, have shown disadvantages in these scenarios. These approaches have not fully leveraged the advantageous resources within the yard, notably those proximate to the berth. Moreover, the methodologies described have failed to adequately account for conflicts in container operations that may arise within the same blocks involving different container groups, as well as potential conflicts in container outbound operations in the future. Additionally, these strategies have not made optimal use of dynamic information derived from the container arrival curve to facilitate the allocation of positions for incoming container groups. The cumulative impact of these constraints results in suboptimal and inefficient yard space allocation. Furthermore, the majority of the aforementioned research studies have not taken into consideration the challenges related to resource scarcity. Additionally, they have not comprehensively addressed the overarching efficiency of yard operations as a whole. Problem description

Container terminals feature two main yard layouts [24]: vertical alongside (VA) and parallel alongside (PA). These layouts typically correspond to two distinct operational modes: end-loading/unloading (ELU) and side-loading/unloading (SLU) modes [25]. In VA yards, the ELU mode is prevalent, where containers are transported to the transfer zone (TZ) [26] at the ends of the yard by terminal trucks or Automated Guided Vehicles (AGVs) before being handled by yard cranes to reach their designated slots. In contrast, PA yards allow terminal trucks or AGVs to directly access the yard and perform loading/unloading operations at the target bays called transfer point (TP) [27], which is referred to as the SLU mode within the yard. This study focuses on the SLU operational mode in the context of the PA (horizontal) yard layout, as illustrated in the following Fig. 2.

Typically, the yard is divided into various blocks based on the type of containers stored, including the transshipment block, export block, and import block, as depicted in Fig. 2 above. Each block comprises multiple bays, and each bay is further divided into stacks and tiers, as shown in Fig. 3 below. At the shoreside, container handling is carried out by quay cranes (QC), involving the unloading from or loading onto vessels and terminal trucks. Within the yard, the transfer of containers between terminal trucks and the yard is managed by yard cranes, while horizontal transportation between QCs and YCs is facilitated by terminal trucks.Fig. 2The PA (horizontal) yard layout of the container terminal.Fig. 2Fig. 3An illustration of a block in the stacking yard.Fig. 3

Manifestation of operational conflicts

Certainly, a container terminal yard operates as a dynamic system where continuous production activities are in progress at all times, resulting in ever-changing yard conditions. There are four primary production activities within the yard: loading operations, unloading operations, gate-in operations, and gate-out operations. Loading and unloading operations involve horizontal transportation performed by terminal trucks within the terminal. In contrast, gate-in and gate-out operations pertain to terminal truck activities outside the terminal boundaries. Loading and gate-out operations are characterized by the removal of containers from the yard while unloading and gate-in operations involve the introduction of containers into the yard from external sources.

The described yard operations entail two primary forms of conflicts:

Intra-Group Container Conflicts: These conflicts arise when containers from the same group need to enter the yard and may encounter operational conflicts with containers from different groups already in the yard. These conflicts can occur at two different time points. The first occurs at the current moment when containers from the same group need to enter, potentially conflicting with containers from different groups that are already being processed or about to be processed. The second time point is in the future when containers from the same group are departing. If their departure times align with those of different group containers already in the yard or are close in timing, this can also lead to operational conflicts.

Inter-Group Container Conflicts: These conflicts involve containers from different groups that need to enter the yard. Similar to intra-group conflicts, inter-group conflicts can occur at two distinct time points. The first is at the current moment when containers from different groups enter the same yard area, leading to potential operational conflicts. The second time point occurs in the future when containers from different groups are departing. At this time, they may also generate operational conflicts when their departure times align or are in close proximity to each other.

From the port's perspective, ship operations, particularly the loading operation, are of paramount importance, and they are inherently complex. Nonetheless, prior research studies [17,18,7] have predominantly focused on achieving an equitable distribution of operations during the entry phase while often disregarding the outbound operations. Currently, the majority of Chinese ports grapple with challenges stemming from spatial resource inadequacy. With the surge in container volumes, conventional static methods have become inadequate to fulfill the existing demands. Furthermore, equipment resources also face issues of insufficiency. According to statistical data, the ratio between equipment resources and spatial resources in most ports is approximately 1:1.2 or 1:1.3, as illustrated in Fig. 2. This implies that, in the majority of cases, a single piece of equipment must be responsible for the operations in two different blocks. Consequently, achieving operational equilibrium in the yard necessitates a comprehensive consideration of operational conflicts during both the entry and departure phases, recognizing that balancing entry operations is just one facet of the conflict resolution endeavor.

Distance between blocks and berths

Once the construction of the container yard within a container terminal is completed, the distances between any given block b and any berth β are established. Therefore, the varying lengths of these distances entail different horizontal transportation distances for internal container transfers. For the container yard, the closer a container block is to the berths, the more advantageous it is, as exemplified by the central transshipment container block depicted in Fig. 2. The level of activity within these advantageous blocks to some extent reflects the yard's efficiency and cost structure. Greater production operations in these advantageous blocks result in lower horizontal transportation costs. Additionally, the shorter distances to the berths enable higher efficiency for quayside cranes during loading and unloading processes.

However, the spatial resources within these advantageous blocks are limited, and it is impractical to stack all containers in these blocks. To maximize the utilization of spatial resources in the advantageous blocks, containers destined for these blocks should have the shortest dwell times within the entire yard. Moreover, the higher the number of containers of the same type within these advantageous blocks, the greater the utilization of spatial resources. This optimizes the use of the advantageous blocks.

Summary

This paper addresses the container yard space allocation problem from the three perspectives discussed above. Firstly, it alleviates yard operation conflicts, reducing the probability of operational conflicts, and consequently minimizing waiting times and enhancing the overall operational efficiency of the yard. Secondly, it departs from traditional container area classification by prioritizing the usage of advantageous container areas based on the container's storage time. Lastly, it enhances the utilization of yard space resources by minimizing the waste of bay positions and increasing their efficiency of use. Model formulation

Based on the problem description provided in Section 3, this paper has established a mathematical model with the objectives of reducing conflicts in container production operations within the container yard, shortening horizontal container transportation distances, and minimizing spatial resource wastage in the yard. This model is designed to reserve yard locations for container groups that require entry within each time interval Δt. The specific details are described as follows.

Solution approaches

Clearly, reserving yard positions for multiple groups is an NP-hard problem [28]. When the problem reaches a certain scale, the model described above cannot be directly solved using solvers such as CPLEX. In light of this problem and the constructed model, this paper proposes a dynamic reservation algorithm (DRA) based on a reward-penalty mechanism, considering the dynamic nature of the container yard.

Dynamic reservation algorithm

Container terminals are dynamic and complex environments where a plethora of production operations and events evolve over time. Spatial resources within the container yard are subject to dynamic changes as new containers may occupy certain positions, which are subsequently released as containers depart. Equipment resources within the yard also vary over time, with adjustments in equipment positioning being constant. Furthermore, production activities within the yard may shift from one container area to an-other over time.

To address the complexity and real-time demands of such dynamic environments, we propose a dynamic reservation algorithm augmented with a reward-penalty mechanism. This algorithm not only considers the current reservation status but also thoroughly evaluates past and future reservation situations. Specifically, the algorithm makes decisions based on past reservations and the real-time status of the container yard to maximize the utilization of spatial and equipment resources while ensuring the smooth progress of production activities. The core principle of this algorithm design is to balance past, pre-sent, and future demands to adapt to the intricate changes within dynamic environments.

Within each Δt time interval, the DRA reserves positions for every arriving container group. However, due to the dynamic changes in the yard, the reservations of the group in the previous Δt may become invalid in the current t. Therefore, it is necessary to review all previous reservations at the current time t.

Each reservation r can have one of three states: used, unused, or blocked.(18)rstatus={usednot_usedblocked

From Equation (18), status used indicates that the reservation r has been utilized in the next Δt and is available at the current time t. Status not_used indicates that the reservation r has not been utilized in the current time t. Status blocked indicates that the reservation r has been used but is currently unavailable at time t due to certain yard operations. The reservation is temporarily locked to prevent it from going to waste or causing container reshuffling. In the next Δt, the reservation's availability is rechecked, and if it can be used, the lock is removed.

Reserving based on reward-penalty mechanism

The Dynamic Reservation Algorithm (DRA) employs a Reward-Penalty Mechanism (RPM) for location reservations. The algorithm calculates a reward-penalty value for each bay in a yard area, formulated as follows:(19)rt=conflictt+distanceCostt+spaceCostt+μ∙rtpre+ν∙rtlaterIn this equation, rt represents the reward-penalty value for the selection of bay bay in block b for container group i at time t. The term conflictt denotes the reward-penalty associated with conflicts during the selection of bay bay in yard at time t. The distanceCostt accounts for the reward-penalty linked to the distance between the chosen bay in block b and the container group's loading/unloading berth β at time t. Furthermore, spaceCostt reflects the reward-penalty concerning the availability of space in the chosen bay bay in block b at time t. Additionally, the algorithm encourages container group i to exhibit concentration and continuity within the block by introducing reward values, rtpre for the prior time t, and rtlater to consider the subsequent selection of the same block at time t later. The parameters μ and ν serve as discount factors, assuming values between 0 and 1.

The design of conflict reward-penalty

The design of conflict reward-penalty factors in the proposed model can be categorized into two types of conflicts. The first type conflict1 relates to reservation of container group i while considering the current containers stored in the block b. The reward-penalty for this type of conflict is defined by the following Equation (20):(20)conflict1={−100，ifτb≥10−20，if10>τb>020，ifτb=0andfutureconflictsexist100，ifτb=0andnoconflictsexist

The second type conflict2 encompasses conflicts arising when container group i reserves bay bay belonged to block b while another container group also plans reservations within the same block b. The reward-penalty for this type of conflict is determined using Equation (21):(21)conflict2={−50，ifcurrentconflictsexist−20，iffutureconflictsexist0，ifnoconflictsexist

The total conflict reward-penalty conflictt at time t is then computed by summing the values for conflict1 and conflict2 as shown in Equation (22):(22)conflictt=conflict1+conflict2

This results in the reward-penalty value for conflicts at time t when container group i reserves bay bay within block b.

The design of distance reward-penalty

The design of the reward-penalty system for distance cost, as outlined in Equation (23), is structured as follows:(23)distanceCostt={20−γb∙βγβ‾×20，ifγb∙β<γmax−M，ifγb∙β≥γmaxIn this equation, γβ‾ represents the mean distance between berth β and all blocks. The parameter M assumes an exceptionally large positive value. The reward component is positively assigned when the distance between block b and berth β is less than the average distance, while it takes on a negative value for distances exceeding this average. Moreover, when the distance surpasses the maximum threshold γmax , the reward becomes infinitely negative.

The design of space reward-penalty

The design of the reward-penalty system for space cost, as expressed in Equation (24), is structured as follows:(24)spaceCostt={−θbay−αiθbay×10，ifθbay>αi0，ifθbay≤αi

The space cost's reward-penalty values are divided into two segments. In the first segment, when θbay>αi, a negative feedback is given as space wastage occurs due to bay reservations. In the second segment, when θbay≤αi, the reward-penalty value for space cost is set to zero.

The design of time-series reward-penalty

The time-series reward-penalty design is an essential aspect, particularly in the context of yard operations governed by a SLU container handling mode. In scenarios where machinery and equipment resources are constrained, the concentration of containers belonging to the same container group within a block is of paramount importance to optimize operations. Hence, to ensure the coherence and continuity of container stacking for the same container group, the algorithm incorporates time-series reward-penalty values to regulate stacking concentration.

Time-series reward-penalty values encompass two categories. The first category pertains to the past reward-penalty value, specifically indicating whether the same container group had prior reservations within the same block, as articulated in Equation (25). The second category relates to future reward-penalty values, signifying the likelihood of the same container group making reservations in the current block at time t+Δt, as represented in Equation (26).(25)rtpre=φn∙λ0∙rt0+φn−1∙λ1∙rt1+⋯+φ1∙λtn−1∙rtn−1(26)rtlater={θbay′−αit+Δtθbay′×10，ifθbay′≥αit+Δt−10，ifθbay′<αit+Δt,∀bay′∈Bayb

Here, λ is a binary variable that reflects whether the block of the container reservations at some previous time t as the current time (t) are the same, assigning a value of 1 if they are, and 0 otherwise. The φ factor is the discount coefficient.

The past reward-penalty value builds on the concept of rewarding past reservations within the same block, thereby amplifying the reward-penalty value based on the number of times the same container group was previously reserved in the same block. On the other hand, future reward-penalty values take into account the prospects of making reservations in the current block at a subsequent time t+Δt. If the number of available positions in the current yard area exceeds the quantity of container groups expected to enter in the next time interval, the likelihood of future reservations in the current yard area is higher, resulting in a correspondingly higher reward value.

Certainly, due to the finite nature of the container yard's spatial resources, it becomes tenable to derive a reward value, designated as rbay, for each individual bay at time t. Subsequently, container group i optimizes its reservation strategy by selecting the bay with the highest associated reward value, as encapsulated in Equation (27):(27)max{Ri}

Algorithmic supplementation

DRA ensures that every container group is assigned a reservation in a bay, regardless of its quality. In practical scenarios, each arriving container must be allocated a location within the yard. Therefore, a reservation is mandatory. Furthermore, even if a suboptimal bay is reserved, there is no immediate concern, as it will be reevaluated in the subsequent time interval t+Δt, as elaborated in Section 5.4. With the dynamic changes within the yard, a rational bay will eventually be reserved.

In instances when occupancy rate of the yard is relatively high, there may not be sufficient locations to accommodate all the incoming container groups for reservations. Therefore, a classification system based on priority and urgency is essential. This classification prioritizes container groups with higher priority and greater urgency for reservations, ensuring that the yard's best resources are allocated to these particular container groups.

The primary reason behind the observed phenomena is that when Δt exceeds 300s and continues to increase, the dynamism of reservations decreases. When containers enter the yard and the reserved location in the block is currently occupied by containers from other groups or unforeseen circumstances, making the reserved location temporarily unavailable, it may lead to the utilization of non-reserved locations to maintain operational efficiency, preventing container trucks from waiting. This leads to a noticeable increase in Nobu. Subsequently, the increase in Nobu. further reduces Auobu. With Δt values smaller than or equal to 300s, the variations in Nobu. and Auobu. are minimal, making them negligible.

Therefore, it can be deduced from the above graphs and analysis that the optimal value for Δt is 300s. This value ensures a balance between the dynamism of reservations and computational resource efficiency.

The observed phenomena are attributed to differences in the focus of the static method and DPA. Generally, static strategies prioritize yard space utilization without considering real-time production operations within the yard. DPA, on the other hand, emphasizes real-time yard production while considering space utilization when it doesn't compromise production requirements. In situations with low yard occupancy rates, both strategies can be applied effectively. However, as the yard occupancy rate increases, especially at high rates, both strategies exhibit their respective shortcomings and fail to meet the requirements of yard production operations under high occupancy rates.

The dynamic reservation algorithm proposed in this paper, based on a reward-penalty mechanism, demonstrates good performance regardless of the yard occupancy rate. DRA effectively avoids conflicts among yard production operations through the conflict reward-penalty mechanism. It optimizes the utilization of advantageous resources within yard through the distance cost reward-penalty mechanism. Additionally, DRA enhances yard space utilization through the space cost reward-penalty mechanism and the time sequence reward-penalty mechanism.

Hence, the results of the experiments and the analysis indicate that the proposed dynamic reservation algorithm based on the reward-penalty mechanism effectively addresses yard space allocation problems. It further enhances yard operations, contributing to increased competitiveness of the port.

Conclusion

This paper introduced a novel strategy and approach to address the container terminal yard space allocation problem. Our experimental results demonstrate that the dynamic reservation algorithm, based on a reward-penalty mechanism, outperforms static algorithms and other dynamic algorithms. This approach combines the advantages of both approaches, leading to significant improvements in yard space resource utilization, reduction of production conflicts within the yard, lower production operation costs, and enhanced overall yard production efficiency. Our research stands out from existing approaches due to its unique combination of dynamic reservation and reward-penalty mechanisms. Unlike static algorithms that fail to adapt to changing conditions and other dynamic algorithms that lack the precision of our reward-penalty mechanism, our approach achieves a balance that maximizes efficiency and adaptability.

Despite the advancements presented in this paper, several avenues for future research remain. The container volume prediction and container slot allocation steps, while simplified here due to space constraints, are crucial components within our comprehensive framework. Future research should delve deeper into optimizing these sub-algorithms to enhance overall performance. Additionally, further exploration of alternative reward-penalty mechanisms and refinement of the dynamic reservation algorithm could yield improvements in the efficiency and adaptability of yard space allocation in container terminals.
