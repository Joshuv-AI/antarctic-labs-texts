T cells in ITP: Focus on T follicular helper cells and cytotoxic T cells
Authors: Sylvain Audia, Nichola Cooper
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryImmune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is an autoimmune disease where premature destruction of platelets as well as inhibition of platelet production leads to thrombocytopenia and associated bleeding. It has long been considered a disease primarily caused by B cells, but the role of T lymphocytes in its pathogenesis is now better understood and deserves elucidation. Two types of T cells will be discussed: (1) splenic T follicular helper cells (TFH) that participate in differentiation of B cells within germinal centres (GC) and stimulate the production of antiplatelet antibodies, thus supporting the humoral autoimmune response; and (2) antibody‐independent mechanisms of action of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTL) that may directly participate in platelet destruction as well as inhibit their production by targeting megakaryocytes. To date, most novel therapies target antibody‐mediated disease, but targeting either TFH or CTL may provide new therapeutic opportunities.Keywords: cytotoxic T cells, ITP, T cells, T follicular helper cells

T FOLLICULAR HELPER CELLS

T follicular helper cells (TFH) support the humoral response within the germinal centre (GC) of lymphoid organs, driving B‐cell proliferation, differentiation and immunoglobulin class switching recombination through a contact‐dependent mechanism involving CD40/CD40L and interleukin (IL)‐21. 1 Early differentiation of TFH from naive T cells depends on antigen‐presenting dendritic cells, ICOS (inducible T‐cell co‐stimulator)/ICOS ligand and IL‐6, while B cells are required for later stages. 2 Activated TFH upregulate their major transcription factor Bcl6 (B‐cell lymphoma 6) and overexpress CXCR5 (CXC motif chemokine receptor 5), the receptor of CXCL13 (CXC motif chemokine ligand 13). CXCL13 and IL‐21, both produced by TFH, stimulate TFH and B cells. Counter‐regulation of TFH through PD‐1 (programmed cell death receptor 1)/PD‐L1 decreases ICOS expression, IL‐21 and IL‐4 production. TFH are identified as CD3+CD4+CXCR5+ICOS+PD‐1+ cells in lymphoid organs. Most of the circulating TFH (cTFH, CD3+CD4+CXCR5+) are resting memory TFH (CXCR5+ICOS−PD1+CD45RO+), while activated (CXCR5+ICOS+PD1+++) are rare but provide better help for B cells. 2

With physiological role in anti‐infectious and antitumoural responses, TFH also participate in allergic and autoimmune diseases. 2 In ITP, we observed increased frequencies of circulating and splenic TFH, the latter being correlated with that of GC B cells and plasma cells. In vitro, the differentiation of B cells and antiplatelet antibody production depended on CD40 ligation and IL‐21. 3

Interestingly, pretreatment with rituximab reduces splenic TFH cells along with IL‐21 and CXCL13 expression, which is replicated in vitro by B‐cell depletion, illustrating the requirement for B cells for their survival. 4 cTFH and serum CXCL13 levels also diminished after rituximab, but their baseline levels do not predict therapeutic response. 4 Splenic TFH expansion has been associated with response to splenectomy, supporting their role in ITP pathogenesis. 5

TFH participate in long‐lived plasma cell differentiation and increase antibody affinity during the vaccination process, 2 which has not been investigated in ITP. In mice, after B‐cell depletion, splenic TFH persist with a different phenotype (Bcl6lowCXCR5lowPD‐1low), while these markers are re‐expressed after B‐cell reconstitution. Whether similar mechanisms occur in human and to which extent TFH contribute to the relapse of ITP after B‐cell repletion needs to be determined.

Although therapeutic inhibition of the CD40/CD40L axis or IL‐21 showed inconsistent results in autoimmune diseases, TFH remain a potential therapeutic target, with the development of new strategies such as GC B cells and TFH dual targeting by anti‐CXCR5 antibody (NCT05070845). In mice, BAFF (B‐cell‐activating factor) participates in TFH differentiation through BAFF receptor suggesting that the therapeutic targets, which are assessed in ITP (NCT05338190, NCT05885555), might also affect TFH. Immunosuppressants such as tacrolimus and sirolimus could also be of interest, as they reduce TFH differentiation and effects on B cells, in vitro. 6

CYTOTOXIC T CELLS

Several studies described a role for cytotoxic CD8+ T cells (CTL) in ITP. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 CTL are activated through their unique T cell receptor (TCR) that binds to the antigen peptide–MHC class I complex, with co‐stimulation from dendritic cells. A subset of CTL, T effector memory CD8+ T cells (TEMRA cells, highly cytotoxic with increased expression of perforin, granzyme A and B, IFN‐γ and TNF‐α), is increased in patients with refractory ITP. 7 Moreover, expanded T‐cell clones persist in these patients over many years, expanding at times of low platelet count, implicating them in disease pathogenesis. Other groups have described increased cytotoxic gene expression, 8 , 9 as well as increased Fas‐ligand expression in CTL in ITP. 10 Coculture of CTL from ITP patients with platelets leads to platelet lysis. 8 , 10 Similarly, ex vivo culture of CTL from patients with refractory ITP with autologous platelets results in degranulation of CTL, and platelet activation and destruction as supported by overexpression of CD62P and binding of annexin V, thus indicating that CTL directly participate in platelet destruction. 7

Cytotoxic T lymphocytes have also been reported in the bone marrow of patients with ITP, 9 , 12 including activated T cells expressing Fas, and surface integrin VLA‐4 and CX3CR1. 9 Whether CTL directly target megakaryocytes in the bone marrow for destruction has not been properly tested. In one study, the presence of CTL in the bone marrow was associated with increased megakaryocyte count as well as reduced platelet production; the mechanism for this remains unclear. 12 A mouse model of ITP has defined CTL‐mediated thrombocytopenia, 13 as well as CTL activity in the bone marrow, with megakaryocytes actively presenting platelet antigens to MHC class I restricted CTL. 14 Further studies are needed to understand whether CTL can directly interact with megakaryocytes to cause functional inhibition.

Currently, studies of CTL in ITP remain limited, and we do not yet know whether CTL could be biomarkers to help direct T‐cell‐mediated treatments. CTL are more prominent in patients refractory to standard ITP treatments such as rituximab, 7 and large granular lymphocytes (CD3+CD16+CD57+ cells, a type of CTL) have also been described in a small cohort of ITP patients with reduced responses to rituximab. Other studies have shown increased CTL in non‐responders to treatment, and Audia et al. described increased CTL in the spleens of 18 patients refractory to rituximab, 15 suggesting that alternative treatments are needed for these patients. However, while consistent, these studies are small and retrospective, limiting interpretation.

T‐cell‐directed treatment is of interest in ITP. Mycophenolate mofetil showed efficacy in patients with refractory disease and reduced relapses when used in newly diagnosed ITP. 16 Sirolimus, azathioprine, ciclosporin and cyclophosphamide have also been used in ITP with demonstrated efficacy. Baricitinib, a JAK inhibitor, shows promise and is currently in clinical trial (NCT05446831). However, many of these treatments are poorly tolerated, and long‐term immunosuppression predisposes to increased infections as well as secondary malignancies. A possible alternative is cytokine‐mediated modulation of CTL, with a report that IL‐27 inhibits CTL in ITP.

CONCLUSION

In ITP, splenic TFH, via IL‐21 secretion and CD40/CD40L interaction, participate in the differentiation and proliferation of autoreactive B cells and thus in the production of antiplatelet antibodies. In addition, platelet‐specific CTL appear to drive direct platelet destruction in patients who are refractory to standard treatments such as rituximab. The emerging dual roles of T cells in the pathogenesis of ITP, both fuelling humoral disease as well as directly targeting platelets, challenge the current therapeutic landscape in ITP and provide new targets for patients with refractory disease. Large prospective clinical studies that target T cells, either in persistent disease, to prevent evolution to chronic disease or to specifically target CTL in refractory disease are now needed.
