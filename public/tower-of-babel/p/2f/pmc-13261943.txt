Antileukemic therapies for older adults with AML ineligible for conventional therapy: systematic review and meta-analysis∗
Authors: Maria Jose Oliveros, Saifur R Chowdhury, Yetiani Roldan, Sara Ibrahim, Gonzalo Bravo-Soto, Charles E Foucar, Gurleen Bhogal, Barbara Burgos-Mansilla, Mobina Bagherianlemraski, Hamed Movahed, Ruvistay Gutierrez-Arias, Tanin Khorrami Taj, Yaping Chang, Seyed Kaveh Hadeiy, Rachel Couban, Roberta Demichelis-Gomez, Luca Malcovati, Kah Poh Loh, Uwe Platzbecker, Linda Gilberto, John Treitz, Maria R Baer, Bernard L Marini, Ryan Mattison, Mikkael A Sekeres, Romina Brignardello-Petersen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOlder adults with newly diagnosed acute myeloid leukemia (AML) are often ineligible for conventional “7+3” induction chemotherapy. Despite recent drug approvals, treatment outcomes remain poor in this population. We conducted an updated systematic review to inform the 2025 American Society of Hematology (ASH) AML update guidelines in older adults. This review compared the efficacy and safety of low-dose cytarabine (LDAC), azacitidine (AZA), 5- and 10-day decitabine (DEC), and gemtuzumab ozogamicin, alone or combined with drugs such as venetoclax (VEN), in older adults with AML ineligible for conventional chemotherapy. We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and nonrandomized studies (NRSs) of adults aged ≥55 years with AML, and synthesized evidence on mortality, remission, quality of life (QoL), functional status, and severe toxicity. We applied GRADE (grading of recommendations assessment, development, and evaluation) to assess the certainty of evidence. We included 47 studies (30 RCTs and 17 NRSs). AZA or LDAC combined with VEN probably reduces mortality and improves remission and QoL. AZA plus isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1) inhibitors may reduce 1-year mortality and improve remission and survival in patients with IDH1-mutated AML. Compared with DEC alone, combining DEC with other agents showed inconsistent effects with mostly low certainty of conclusions. VEN combinations showed promising effects on mortality and remission but lacked data on QoL and functional status. In older adults with AML ineligible for conventional therapy, evidence suggests that hypomethylating agents or LDAC combined with VEN likely improves survival and remission outcomes. Treatment decisions should consider patient goals and functional status. These findings informed 8 recommendations in updated ASH-AML guidelines.

Introduction

Treatment of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) in older adults remains a challenge. Despite advances in disease understanding and the introduction of novel therapies, outcomes in this population remain poor, largely due to comorbidities; adverse disease biology; and limited tolerance to, and efficacy of, intensive or conventional treatment.1,2 Five-year relative survival remains low in older adults, ranging from 21.0% in those aged 60 to 74 years, to 5.6% in those aged 75 to 84 years, and just 2.6% among patients aged ≥85 years.3

In recent years, the number of approved agents for treating AML has rapidly expanded, with 13 approvals since 2017.4 The expansion of treatments beyond monotherapies with hypomethylating agents (HMAs) or low-dose cytarabine (LDAC), to targeted drugs and combination therapies has increased the treatment options available for older adults with AML. The choice of treatment is informed by patient characteristics, including comorbidities, age, and performance status, as well as disease characteristics, including cytogenetic and molecular risk stratification and the presence of targetable mutations.2 However, considerable variability remains in the treatment of older adults, with no clear consensus in certain subgroups.

Many older adults diagnosed with AML are considered ineligible for conventional chemotherapy. However, for selected older patients who are medically fit and have favorable-risk AML (as defined by European Leukemia Network 2022),5 conventional chemotherapy may be considered as a potentially curative option. Nonetheless, this approach carries substantial risks, primarily due to treatment-related morbidity and mortality, often resulting from infections associated with chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression.2 In patients not eligible for, or declining, conventional chemotherapy, HMAs or LDAC, alone or in combination, have redefined standard care.6 Based on the findings of the VIALE-A trial, HMA combined with venetoclax (VEN) has become the preferred approach in this setting.7 Additionally, the therapeutic landscape has expanded with the investigation of targeted therapies.8 Combinations of HMAs with isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1) or IDH2 inhibitors, such as ivosidenib (IVO) or enasidenib (ENA), are being evaluated in patients with the corresponding mutations to enhance treatment specificity and improve clinical outcomes through molecularly informed strategies.

In this scenario, clinicians must often weigh efficacy against tolerability, particularly when considering emerging combinations and novel agents.2 Our previous systematic review on this topic9 informed the 2020 American Society of Hematology (ASH) guidelines, for older adults with newly diagnosed AML who were candidates for antileukemic therapy but not for conventional intensive treatment.6 As part of the guideline update process, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to inform 2 recommendation questions that resulted in 8 recommendations.10 This review compares the effectiveness and safety of LDAC, azacitidine (AZA), 5-day decitabine (5d-DEC), 10-day decitabine (10d-DEC), and gemtuzumab ozogamicin (GO), used either as monotherapy or in combination with drugs such as VEN, IVO, or ENA, in patients who are not candidates for conventional induction therapy.

Methods

We updated the systematic review that informed the 2020 ASH guidelines on treating newly diagnosed AML in older adults.9 We did not register a protocol, but followed prespecified ASH methodological standards for evidence synthesis11 and developed the review question and eligibility criteria based on the ASH guideline panel’s recommendation questions. We report this review according to the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses guidelines12 (supplemental Appendix 1).

Eligibility criteria

We included randomized clinical trials (RCTs) that enrolled older adults (age of ≥55 years) with newly diagnosed AML, considered appropriate for antileukemic therapy but not for conventional induction and postremission therapy. Eligible studies compared any of the following treatments against another, as monotherapy or in combination with any secondary agent: LDAC, AZA, 5d-DEC, 10d-DEC, and GO. Anticipating low-certainty evidence from RCTs, and due to their clinical relevance, we also included comparative nonrandomized studies (NRSs). We included full-text publications and studies with at least 10 participants in each arm. Outcomes included mortality/survival, quality of life (QoL), functional status, recurrence or duration of response, morphologic complete remission (CR), severe toxicity, and burden on caregivers. For severe toxicities, we prioritized specific outcomes in consultation with ASH clinical experts (see supplemental Appendix 2).

Details about these criteria are available in supplemental Appendix 3.

Search strategy

We conducted an umbrella search encompassing this and another 3 recommendation questions addressed in the guideline. We searched MEDLINE (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), and the Cochrane Library for studies published from 2019 (5 months before the last date of search of the original systematic review) to 16 February 2024. In November 2024, we also consulted the guideline panelist experts to identify additional studies published during the review period or after the search. The full search strategy is provided in supplemental Appendix 4.

Selection process

Pairs of reviewers each involving a junior and a senior member of the team, independently screened titles and abstracts using Laser AI software (https://laser.ai). A third reviewer resolved disagreements. Full-text screening followed the same process.

After identifying new eligible studies, we combined them with those from the original systematic review, removed duplicates, and established the final set of included studies.

Data collection and RoB assessment

After training and calibration exercises, 1 reviewer extracted the data and assessed risk of bias (RoB), and a senior reviewer verified the information. We used a pilot-tested Microsoft Excel form to collect study characteristics, patient demographics, interventions, and outcomes.

For RCTs, we extracted the number of events and total participants for dichotomous outcomes. We also extracted hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) when reported. For NRSs, we extracted HRs, risk ratios (RRs), or odds ratios with 95% CIs, prioritizing adjusted over unadjusted estimates. For continuous outcomes, we extracted measures of central tendency and dispersion. We collected outcome data at 1 month, 12 months, and the longest follow-up, when reported.

We assessed RoB in RCTs using a modified Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool with 4 rating levels: definitely low, probably low, probably high, or definitely high RoB.13 For NRSs, we used the ROBINS-I tool, considering age, comorbidities, and performance status as key confounders.14 See supplemental Appendix 5 for the manuals used to assess RoB. Two reviewers resolved disagreements through discussion, and, when needed, a third reviewer adjudicated.

Analyses

We grouped interventions based on the primary agent (AZA, LDAC, 5d-DEC, and 10d-DEC) for monotherapies, and on the primary and secondary agent (VEN, ENA, IVO, or other therapies) for combination therapies.

We attempted to perform a network meta-analysis, but the networks lacked sufficient evidence to produce informative indirect estimates. Thus, when appropriate, we conducted pairwise meta-analyses using a random-effects model with inverse variance weighting, estimating between-study variance using the DerSimonian-Laird method. All analyses were conducted using Review Manager, version 5.4 (The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark).

For dichotomous outcomes, we calculated RRs or HRs, as appropriate, and their 95% CI. To estimate absolute effects, we calculated a common baseline risk for each reference intervention. We identified all study arms in which patients received this intervention and used the mean of the event rates across these arms to generate a single baseline risk, which we used to estimate absolute effect estimates for all comparisons within the table. For continuous outcomes, we planned to use mean differences with standard deviation but did not find any such data eligible for meta-analysis.

We planned subgroup analyses by age (≥70 vs <70 years) to explore heterogeneity, but due to insufficient data, we could not conduct these analyses. We did not plan sensitivity analyses.

Certainty of evidence and presentation of results

We assessed the certainty of evidence using grading of recommendations assessment, development, and evaluation (GRADE).15 We rated the certainty across 5 domains: RoB, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.16 We set our target of certainty at a nonzero effect, but when the point estimate was between the null and the minimally importance difference, we shifted our target to rate certainty in a trivial effect. We used a single minimally importance difference of 10 per 1000 for absolute effects and considered the optimal information size when applicable.17 We planned to explore publication bias through funnel plots if the meta-analysis included ≥10 studies.18

Because we had multiple comparisons, using the interventions most addressed in the studies as a reference, we created tables that compared several interventions with the reference. These tables contained the key information from traditional summary of findings tables,19 including outcome definition, effect estimates, certainty ratings, and explanations for the ratings.

Discussion

Despite recent advances in drug development, AML in older adults remains a challenging disease to manage, starting with the very first conversation regarding treatment options, patient preferences, goals, and aggressiveness of therapy.2 For many older adults with AML, the potential benefits of traditional induction and postremission therapy are offset by devastating side effects, rendering these drugs intolerable.66

We synthesized evidence on 11 treatment comparisons involving 3 main agents: AZA, LDAC, and DEC, as monotherapy, or in combination with drugs such as VEN, IVO, or ENA. This systematic review informed 8 recommendations in the ASH guideline update addressing treatment of older adults with newly diagnosed AML ineligible for conventional therapy.10

Compared with AZA, AZA plus VEN reduces mortality rate, probably reduces 1-year mortality, and probably has little or no effect on 30-day mortality. It probably increases CR and QoL and may reduce severe toxicity, although its effect on functional status is very uncertain. AZA combinations with IVO or ENA may reduce 1-year mortality and may increase CR and reduce toxicity; however, we found no evidence regarding QoL and functional status. LDAC may increase mortality over time and 1-year mortality; it probably results in lower CR but may increase toxicity.

Compared with LDAC, LDAC plus VEN probably reduces mortality rate and may reduce 30-day mortality. It may improve QoL, functional status, and CR, and may have little or no effect on toxicity. LDAC plus other agents probably reduce mortality rate and probably increase CR but may have little or no effect on 1-year mortality and may increase 30-day mortality and toxicity.

Compared with 5d-DEC, 5d-DEC combined with other agents may increase mortality rate, 1-year and 30-day mortality, and CR, and may have little or no effect on toxicity. 10d-DEC may increase 30-day mortality and toxicity, and its effects on 1-year mortality, CR, and recurrence are very uncertain.

Compared with 10d-DEC, 10d-DEC combined with other agents probably increases 30-day mortality, may increase mortality rate, and may have little or no effect on 1-year mortality. The combination probably decreases sepsis. No studies reported QoL or functional status.

The certainty of the evidence varied widely across outcomes and comparisons. Certainty was generally higher for mortality and CR, and lower for outcomes such as relapse, QoL, and functional status. RoB was a key factor contributing to lower certainty. Among the RCTs, concerns were most frequent in the randomization process and deviations from intended interventions. Missing outcome data and selective reporting were common for relapse and toxicity. NRSs contributed only low or very low certainty evidence, due to most being judged as having critical RoB.

Due to the large number of combination regimens, we grouped diverse interventions under the label “others” to allow for synthesis. It was not possible to classify these combinations into broader therapeutic categories, such as targeted therapies or pathway-specific inhibitors, due to the variability in drug class, mechanism of action, and treatment intent. This variation limits the interpretability of pooled estimates and contributes to the uncertainty of the evidence in these comparisons.

The studies included patients aged ≥55 years across all prognostic categories. However, the reporting of outcomes did not allow for extraction of subgroup-specific data by risk or by age. Although study-level analyses did not show substantial differences between subgroups, further research is needed to better understand potential variations in treatment effects.

The strengths of this review include its transparent and methodologically rigorous approach. We followed ASH methodological standards. The review focused on comparisons of high clinical relevance and on outcomes considered important by patients and clinicians. A recent network meta-analysis addressed similar questions but was limited by network sparsity, with few head-to-head comparisons beyond LDAC-based regimens, and it did not assess the certainty of evidence.67 In contrast, we present evidence from multiple direct comparisons and applied the GRADE approach systematically across outcomes and interventions.

We predefined and documented all methods as part of the guideline process. Although a single reviewer performed data extraction and RoB assessments, a senior reviewer independently verified all steps. This approach is consistent with accepted standards for guideline-driven reviews.68

This review provides evidence to support decisions about a range of treatment options for older adults with newly diagnosed AML who are candidates for antileukemic therapy but not for conventional induction and postremission treatment. In clinical settings, treatment availability and patient characteristics may influence decision-making. The ASH AML 2025 guidelines provide an in-depth discussion of the relative desirable and undesirable consequences of the alternatives. The guideline panel issued 8 conditional recommendations.

Future studies should focus on outcomes that matter most to patients and are critical for decision-making, such as QoL, functional status, and caregiver burden, which are rarely reported. There is also a need for analyses that compare VEN-based combinations with targeted therapies, and dual-agent regimens with emerging triplet combinations. In addition, studies should include subgroup analyses by age and prognostic risk to better understand how treatment effects may vary across different patient groups.

Conclusions

Among older adults with newly diagnosed AML who are not candidates for conventional induction and postremission therapy, combinations with VEN may improve mortality and remission outcomes compared with monotherapies, although the certainty of the evidence is variable. Similar benefits may be observed with AZA in combination with IVO or ENA in patients with IDH1 or IDH2 mutations, although evidence remains limited.

These findings underscore the need for treatment decisions tailored to each patient’s clinical context, functional status, and personal goals. The updated ASH guidelines provide a framework to support these decisions, enabling clinicians to match treatment strategies with patient values and available therapeutic options.
