The effect of plasma donation frequency on total serum protein, immunoglobulin G and donor safety: A non‐inferiority randomized controlled trial
Authors: Morten Haugen, Lise Sofie Haug Nissen‐Meyer, Tor A. Strand, Karin Magnussen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesThe safety of varying plasma donation frequencies remains unclear. This non‐inferiority randomized controlled trial investigated the effect of plasma donation frequency on total serum protein (TSP), immunoglobulin G (IgG) concentrations, additional biomarkers, adverse events (AEs) and psychological distress.Materials and MethodsIn this trial, 120 male donors were randomized into three groups: high‐frequency plasma donors (HFPDs, three times every 2 weeks), regular‐frequency plasma donors (RFPDs, once every 2 weeks) and a control group (whole blood donation every 3 months). Blood samples were collected biweekly from baseline until the last donation in Week 16 and 4 weeks after the last donation.ResultsHFPDs completed median (range) 21.5 (1–24), RFPDs 8 (1–8) and controls 2 (1–2) donations. HFPDs had lower concentrations of TSP and IgG compared to controls, with a mean difference (95% confidence interval [CI]) of −5.5 g/L (−7.4, −3.6) and −2.8 g/L (−3.8, −1.8), respectively. Within‐group analysis revealed significant reductions, which increased with the frequency of donations, in TSP, IgG, IgG subclasses, immunoglobulin M (IgM), immunoglobulin A (IgA), ferritin and haemoglobin. Many of the biomarkers required more than 4 weeks to return to baseline levels. Only mild AEs were reported, and plasma donation frequency had no effect on psychological distress.ConclusionHigh‐ and regular‐frequency plasmapheresis substantially reduces concentrations of TSP, IgG and other biomarkers, with greater reductions at higher donation frequencies. Further research is needed to assess the long‐term health implications of frequent plasma donation.

INTRODUCTION

Plasma‐derived medicinal products (PDMPs) are essential for treating immune deficiencies, bleeding disorders, infections and inflammatory diseases [1]. While new indications for immunoglobulins are emerging, many lack robust evidence [2]; this contributes to the growing demand for plasma.

Currently, 60%–70% of the global plasma is collected in the United States, and Europe relies on the United States for 30%–40% of its supply [3, 4]. To achieve strategic independence, Europe must expand plasma collection through voluntary, non‐remunerated donations, aligning with the Substances of Human Origin (SoHO) Regulation and the position of the European Blood Alliance (EBA) [5, 6, 7].

Plasma donation frequency varies, ranging from 12 to 60 donations annually in Europe and up to 104 in the United States [8]. US donors may donate plasma twice weekly, with a minimum 48‐h donation interval [9]. The 20th edition of the ‘Guide for the Preparation, Use, and Quality Assurance of Blood Components’ from the European Directorate for the Quality of Medicines & Health Care (EDQM) recommended a 96‐h interval with a maximum of 33 donations annually [10]. The 21st edition revised this to a 1‐week interval [11], while the 22nd edition [12], published after this trial, recommended a standard 2‐week interval. However, shorter intervals of 1–2 weeks are permitted, provided additional monitoring is conducted, such as regular assessment for adverse events (AEs) and measurement of immunoglobulin G (IgG) concentration every sixth donation.

The health effects of high‐frequency plasma donations remain unclear. Concerns include AEs, reduced bone mineral density, iron deficiency and declines in plasma proteins, especially immunoglobulins, potentially increasing infection risk [8, 13, 14]. Most studies on donation frequency are observational, and controlled trials establishing safe upper limits are lacking [15].

The German SIPLA cohort study [16] followed 3783 donors who underwent up to 60 plasma donations annually over 3 years. Despite lacking a control group and a 75.6% dropout rate, including 12.4% due to low IgG and 2.0% due to low total serum protein (TSP) concentrations, the researchers concluded that up to 60 annual donations of 850 mL may be safe with regular protein monitoring. Laub et al. [17] found significantly lower TSP, albumin, IgG and immunoglobulin M (IgM) in plasma pools from paid, high‐frequency US donors compared to unpaid European or US whole blood or plasma donors. Both SIPLA [16] and Laub et al. [17] are observational studies, and therefore, they provide lower evidence than randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Ciszewski et al. [18], in a non‐randomized experimental design, also reported reduced TSP and IgG levels in weekly donors versus biweekly donors and controls.

The first RCT on plasmapheresis frequency [19] found clinically relevant IgG and ferritin reductions after 3 months of donations twice weekly. IgG, immunoglobulin A (IgA) and IgM levels also declined in donors donating three times a month or twice weekly. Few mild AEs were reported. The authors suggested a potential impact on the immune system, as indicated by reduced IgG levels. However, the study lacked a biweekly donation group.

A recent scoping review confirmed the need for more experimental studies to establish safe limits for plasma donation frequency [15].

This non‐inferiority RCT aims to investigate the effect of plasma donation frequency on TSP and IgG concentrations. Secondary aims include additional biomarkers, AEs, dropout rates and reasons, psychological distress and biomarker recovery 4 weeks after the last donation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The trial protocol has been described previously [20]. This paper presents the primary objective, TSP and IgG concentrations and secondary objectives, including other plasma proteins, haematological biomarkers, psychological distress, AEs and dropout rates and reasons.

Participants

Male blood and plasma donors from 18 to 64 years, with at least one prior plasma donation, were screened. The inclusion criteria were adequate haemoglobin (Hb), TSP and IgG concentrations, as per national and European guidelines [11, 21], and an estimated blood volume (EBV) of >4500 mL, calculated using the International Council for Standardization in Haematology (ICSH) formula [11, 22]. Donors with more than two repeated haematocrit measurements >50% were excluded to prevent prolonged procedures and complications. Participants were randomized 1:1:1 in blocks of three and six to donate for 16 weeks as: ‘High‐frequency plasma donors’ (HFPDs): plasma three times every 2 weeks (24 donations), ‘Regular‐frequency plasma donors’ (RFPDs): plasma once every 2 weeks (eight donations) or the control group: whole blood every 3 months. The randomization list was computer‐generated by an independent researcher. Allocation was concealed until enrolment. Donors refrained from donations for at least 2 months before the study. Written informed consent was obtained.

The study was conducted from January 2022 to July 2024 at four sites at the Blood Centre, Innlandet Hospital Trust, Norway. It was approved by the Regional Committee for Medical Research Ethics South East Norway (2021/238929/REC Southeast A), aligned with the Declaration of Helsinki, and was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05179200).

Plasma donations

Donations involved 720 mL of plasma with anticoagulant (AC) using the Fresenius Kabi Aurora Plasmapheresis machine without saline rinse‐back. A 4% sodium citrate solution (100:6 whole blood to AC ratio) was used. Plasma volume, excluding AC (~650 mL assuming 44% haematocrit), aligned with the Norwegian guidelines [21] and did not exceed 16% of the EBV, as recommended by the EDQM [10].

Data collection

Blood analyses

Samples were collected biweekly from the first (W1) through the last donation (W16), as well as 2 (W18) and 4 weeks (W20) post‐donation. HFPDs completed two additional donations after RFPDs had completed their final donation. Therefore, W16–W20 samples in HFPDs were collected approximately 1 week after the corresponding samples in RFPDs were collected. For consistency, all samples were labelled W16, W18 and W20.

In the plasma donation groups, nine tubes (38.5 mL) were collected every 2 weeks, and six tubes (22.5 mL) at subsequent donations. The tubes were centrifuged at 2200 × g for 10 min at room temperature and analysed within 24 h using standard procedures.

Biomarkers included: TSP, IgG, IgG subclasses 1–4, IgM, IgA, ferritin, Hb, albumin, transferrin (Trf), C‐reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte volume fraction (EVF), erythrocytes, leucocytes, platelets, mean corpuscular haemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (MCHC), neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils and basophils. Male reference values were applied.

Adverse events

AEs were monitored and recorded by technicians. Post‐visit AEs were self‐reported. Events were categorized and graded using international standards [23] and the ‘Severity grading tool for Blood donor AEs’ [24]. Calcium was provided for citrate reactions.

Psychological distress

The Hopkins Symptoms Checklist 25 (HSCL‐25) assessed symptoms of anxiety and depression in the past 2 weeks, 1 week before (W0) and 1 week after (W17) the donation period. The 25 questions are graded on a scale from 1 (‘Not at all’) to 4 (‘Extremely’), and a total mean score cut‐off of 1.75 indicates psychological distress [25, 26].

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated using Sealed Envelope [27], accounting for a 20% dropout rate. Based on local data, the standard deviations (SD) of TSP and IgG were estimated at 4 and 2 g/L, respectively. A sample size of 40 per group ensured that there would be 80% confidence that the lower limit of a one‐sided 95% confidence interval (CI) (or, equivalently, a 90% two‐sided CI) would be above the predefined non‐inferiority margins [20].

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Biomarker differences (W1 vs. W16, W18, W20) were calculated, and generalized linear mixed‐effects models, with group, time and interaction as fixed effects and participant ID as the random effect, were used to account for repeated measurements. An autoregressive covariance structure was applied. Residual normality was assessed with quantile‐quantile plots.

The total number of AEs and the number per 50 donations were reported. The time to low TSP (<60 g/L) or IgG (<6.0 g/L) was illustrated using Kaplan–Meier curves.

HSCL‐25 scores were analysed using linear regression. Proportions above the 1.75 cut‐off at W17 were compared using logistic regression.

For values below quantification limits (IgA, CRP, IgM), imputation was performed using half the lower cut‐off value to enable analysis and reduce bias [28].

Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, and CIs were calculated at the two‐sided 95% level. Normality was assessed using histograms and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data are presented as mean (SD), n (%) or median (interquartile range, IQR). Baseline values are consistently reported as median (IQR) or n (%). Group comparisons are presented as mean or median difference with 95% CIs.

Analysis populations

Both intention‐to‐treat (ITT) and per‐protocol (PP) analyses were conducted. The ITT population included all randomized participants. The PP population included only participants who adhered strictly to the intervention, that is, completing ≥87.5% of planned donations (≥21 for HFPD and ≥7 for RFPDs; see Figure 1), thus reflecting the intervention's worst‐case scenario effects [29].

Flowchart of participants by study group through each trial stage, including the reasons for exclusion, dropout and the main reason for not completing the intervention per protocol. IgG, immunoglobulin G; TSP, total serum protein.

Non‐inferiority analysis

Non‐inferiority was assessed according to CONSORT guidelines [30], comparing group differences in TSP and IgG, using margins from mixed‐effects models. The predefined non‐inferiority margins of −3.0 g/L for TSP and −1.5 g/L for IgG [20] were considered clinically meaningful based on expert consensus. Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons [31].

RESULTS

Of 249 donors screened (Figure 1), 120 were randomized into the HFPD, RFPD and control groups. Following randomization, six HFPDs (15%) and four RFPDs (10%) dropped out due to protocol deviations or withdrawal. Exclusion and dropout reasons are presented in the flowchart. The ITT analysis included 34 HFPDs, 40 RFPDs and 36 controls. Due to incomplete adherence (<87.5% of planned donations), 15 HFPDs and 7 RFPDs were excluded from the PP analysis, leaving 19 HFPDs, 33 RFPDs and 36 controls. Full protocol adherence was achieved by 2 (5.9%) HFPDs and 22 (55%) RFPDs.

The median age was 47.5 years; most donors worked full‐time (Tables 1 and S1). Plasma donation history varied from first‐time to repeat donors, with up to 76 donations. Baseline median TSP and IgG concentrations were 71 g/L and 10.0–10.7 g/L, respectively. Other biomarkers were within reference ranges.

Secondary outcomes at W16

Significant between‐group reductions at W16 were observed in IgG1, IgG2, IgM, ferritin and Hb, with greater declines in HFPDs (Table 2 and Figure 4). Within groups, declines from W1 to W16 were observed in all IgG subclasses, IgM and ferritin in HFPDs and RFPDs, as well as in IgA, albumin, Trf and Hb among HFPDs. No between‐group differences were found for IgG3, IgG4, IgA, albumin, Trf or CRP. Haematological biomarkers were largely unaffected except for EVF and erythrocytes (Table S2).

Immunoglobulin concentrations by study group and week during the donation period.

Recovery of biomarkers after the donation period

At 4 weeks post‐donation (W20), TSP; IgG; IgG subclasses 1, 2, and 4; IgM; IgA; and ferritin remained significantly reduced in HFPDs or RFPDs compared to baseline (Figure 2, Tables 2 and S3).

TSP or IgG below the threshold

IgG < 6.0 g/L was detected 24 times in nine HFPDs (26%) and 7 times in one RFPD (2.5%). These HFPDs all had baseline IgG < 10.0 g/L; two were <8.0 g/L. The low IgG in RFPDs was observed in a donor with a baseline concentration of 6.2 g/L. TSP < 60 g/L occurred 13 times in six HFPDs (18%) and once in one RFPD (2.5%). Hb < 13.0 g/dL was measured in three HFPDs and two RFPDs. Controls showed similar reductions after whole blood donation.

Donors with two consecutive low measurements were deferred for at least 2 weeks. Among HFPDs, four (11.8%) and two (5.9%) were deferred for 2–6 weeks due to low IgG and TSP, respectively. One RFPD (2.5%) was deferred for 8 weeks due to low IgG. One HFPD was deferred due to low Hb. Kaplan–Meier curves showed a significantly higher risk of TSP < 60 g/L and/or IgG < 6.0 g/L with increasing donation frequency (Figure 5).

Kaplan–Meier curve showing time to low concentrations of total serum protein (TSP) (<60 g/L) and/or immunoglobulin G (IgG) (<6.0 g/L).

Adverse events

All AEs were Grade 1 (Table S4). Per 50 donations, HFPDs and RFPDs experienced 0.67 and 1.19 haematomas, 0.15 and 0.68 vasovagal reactions without loss of consciousness, 0.30 and 0.85 citrate reactions, 0.15 and 0.51 infiltrations and 2.23 and 2.03 failed return of red cells, respectively.

Psychological distress

The HSCL‐25 scores showed no significant group differences. Proportions above the 1.75 cut‐off also did not differ significantly (Table S5).

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the effect of plasma donation frequency on plasma proteins, essential biomarkers, AEs and psychological distress over 16 weeks. Donating plasma three times every 2 weeks resulted in significantly lower TSP and IgG concentrations and was inferior to whole blood donation every 3 months. Significant reductions in TSP, IgG, IgG subclasses, ferritin and Hb were observed both between and within groups. Most biomarkers did not return to baseline 4 weeks after the last donation. Only Grade 1 AEs were reported, and no significant differences in psychological distress were found.

Most studies on plasma donation frequency are observational or lack control groups, which limits their validity due to the healthy donor effect and dropout‐related bias [16, 18, 32]. The first RCT by Mortier et al. compared male donors who donated plasma once a month (low frequency, LF), three times a month (high‐frequency, HF) and twice a week (very high‐frequency, VHF) with a placebo group over 3 months [19]. They reported clinically relevant IgG and ferritin reductions in the HF group, with few mild AEs. In their VHF group, IgG declined by 38% (9.23–5.73 g/L), which is comparable to the 24% reduction in our HFPDs (10.4–7.9 g/L). However, we temporarily deferred donors with IgG < 6.0 g/L. Their study had smaller groups (n = 15–16) and a shorter donation period (84 days versus 112 days) and included all participants in the analysis regardless of missed donations, equivalent to our ITT approach.

Both RCTs demonstrate substantial reductions in IgG among high‐frequency donors. However, as Mortier et al. lacked a biweekly donation group, our study addresses this knowledge gap by showing that biweekly donation—the current standard frequency recommended by the latest EDQM guidelines [12]—also results in significant IgG reductions. Mild hypogammaglobulinaemia may increase the risk of infection, as IgG levels ≤6.8 g/L have been associated with an increased incidence of respiratory infections and antibiotic use in otherwise healthy individuals [33, 34]. The new EDQM guidelines [12] recommend IgG testing at least every sixth donation for intervals between 1 and 2 weeks. In our HFPDs, IgG was monitored every third donation, yet 26% experienced IgG < 6.0 g/L. Although donors continued donating until two consecutive IgG < 6.0 g/L, and the donation frequency exceeded weekly, our findings suggest that more frequent IgG monitoring may be necessary to ensure donor safety.

Mortier et al. [19] also observed reductions in ferritin (50.2–20.1 μg/L) in their VHF group, comparable to our HFPDs (median 58–30 μg/L). Repeated whole blood sampling and the absence of a saline rinse‐back in our study may partly explain these reductions.

The SIPLA study by Shculzki et al. [16] also observed IgG level declines with frequent donation, generally within acceptable limits. They suggested stabilization due to physiological adaptation, although 75% dropped out; 15%–20% were excluded for low IgG, TSP or Hb levels. Similarly, our finding of stabilization of TSP and IgG concentration may reflect temporary deferrals of donors with low values or a selection effect favouring donors with higher baseline levels who better tolerate high‐frequency donations.

To our knowledge, this is the first study assessing the effect of plasma donation frequency on psychological distress. No impact on psychological distress was found; however, the study may have been underpowered, the questionnaire may not have been suitable for donor populations, or self‐report bias is also possible, especially in unblinded participants [35].

Strengths include randomization and ITT analysis, which reduce selection bias from noncompliance and dropouts [36]. The ITT analysis typically yields conservative effect estimates versus the PP analysis, and relevant differences were observed between these in the non‐inferiority analysis. The inclusion of active donors as controls also helped mitigate the healthy donor effect [35, 37].

Limitations include focusing on biomarkers without an established correlation to long‐term health outcomes. The 16‐week duration was insufficient to assess the long‐term effects, and the trial was not powered for clinical endpoints. Whole blood donation in the controls may have minimized between‐group differences, resulting in conservative estimates. Despite randomization, the participants may represent a subset of experienced, motivated donors, introducing potential selection bias and the healthy donor effect. Another limitation is the use of fixed donation volumes instead of adjusting for individual EBV. Lack of blinding may have influenced subjective outcomes such as psychological distress or AEs. Passive AE monitoring may have led to underreporting, and unclear differentiation between haematomas and infiltrations complicates interpretation. Additionally, the HFPD samples between W16 and W20 were collected approximately a week later than in RFPDs, possibly overestimating the HFPD effects. Finally, restricting the study to men limits generalizability; thus, similar research in female donors is needed.

While recent studies, including ours, demonstrate significant biochemical changes in frequent plasma donors, evidence on donor safety and long‐term health effects of biweekly or more frequent plasma donation remains limited. Until more evidence emerges, plasma donation policies should follow precautionary principles to protect donor health and ensure a sustainable plasma supply. This does not preclude PDMP availability [38], as plasma collections can be increased by adjusting donation volume, safely extending the donation frequency or expanding the donor pool.

In conclusion, high‐frequency plasma donation causes greater reductions in TSP and IgG than whole blood donation. Both HFPDs and RFPDs are at risk of clinically relevant decreases in TSP, IgG, other immunoglobulins and ferritin. Long‐term prospective studies are needed to assess the health implications of frequent plasma donation.
