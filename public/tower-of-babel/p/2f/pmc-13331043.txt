Introduction

Transformation in healthcare

Citizens depend on high-quality healthcare; however, significant disparities in the quality of care persist across health services and hospitals. This occurs despite the implementation of quality management systems, accreditation procedures and regulations, as well as the inherent motivation and efforts of healthcare professionals to provide optimal care.1

Given the highly complex nature of the healthcare sector, it is essential to account for the various contexts surrounding organisational changes. This is particularly critical given the increasing demand within the sector, rising costs and the ongoing need to maintain operational stability.2 In this regard, optimising resources while considering the existing service structure is crucial. Enhancing quality not only streamlines service processes but also improves the delivery of care between providers and patients.3

Furthermore, the COVID-19 pandemic underscored the urgent need for health services and hospitals to develop the capacity to learn and evolve rapidly to improve care quality.1 4 5 However, uncertainty remains regarding how to effectively assess the capacity of health services and hospitals to learn and improve.1

Knowledge management and organisational learning

Healthcare services and hospitals are complex organisations; therefore, it is essential that knowledge management be employed as a strategic resource to develop competent professionals capable of performing their tasks effectively,6 thereby directly impacting the quality of care. Consequently, investing in human capital fosters a sense of value, promotes personal development and enhances the overall quality of services provided.7

Nevertheless, the concomitant presence of substantial disparities in quality, the recognition that the quality of care is strongly influenced by relational and organisational factors and the inherent motivation of health professionals highlight the importance of learning and improvement processes within health services.1

In this context, the term organisational learning (OL) refers to an institution’s ability to continuously reshape its knowledge base over time without losing its organisational memory, thus fostering constant change and adaptation within services.8 From this perspective, OL is considered a process of continuous improvement in both practices and the knowledge acquired over time. It is postulated that OL serves as a strategy that employs essential techniques and processes to transform knowledge into skills.9 By converting knowledge into practice, a service can undergo a transformation, which remains an inherently challenging process.

The concept of OL was pioneered by Peter Senge, who identified five disciplines that characterise the core practices for building learning organisations: personal mastery, mental models, shared vision, team learning and systems thinking.10

OL in healthcare

Learning within healthcare services and hospitals plays a crucial role in addressing challenges and ultimately improving performance.1 11 OL and continuous improvement are mutually reinforcing; through repeated cycles of learning and enhancement, knowledge and experiences are consistently refined, leading to the generation of new information. Furthermore, institutional learning capacity is essential to facilitate collective OL.12 13 Similarly, the continuous advancement of the healthcare field necessitates the presence of robust improvement capacity.14

From this perspective, OL serves as a tool that guides healthcare services and hospitals towards systematising knowledge and activities.7 It integrates into the organisational culture and is linked to the active process of knowledge acquisition, taking shape through the institution’s implicit memory within the strategic framework of its values, vision and mission.

It is important to note that the literature reveals a scarcity of studies analysing OL models in the healthcare field,15 16 including their effects on clinical practice.17 A preliminary search of MEDLINE, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews and Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Evidence Synthesis was conducted, and no current or ongoing systematic or scoping reviews were found regarding the implementation of OL models in health services and hospitals.

Therefore, considering the challenges faced by the healthcare sector regarding the quality of care,1 18 it is essential to identify and map OL models within these environments.

Methods

The scoping review protocol has been registered with the Open Science Framework (OSF) at https://DOI.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/GH6JZ, in accordance with the JBI guidelines.19 The review will adhere to the framework originally proposed by Arksey and O'Malley20 and subsequently refined by Levac et al.21 The protocol was developed following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines.22

The process for conducting this scoping review will consist of the following six stages: (1) formulating the research question; (2) identifying pertinent studies; (3) selecting eligible studies; (4) mapping the data; (5) synthesising, summarising and reporting the findings and (6) integrating expert consultation.

Step 2: identifying pertinent studies

Major electronic databases will be searched, including PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, Scopus, Web of Science, LILACS (Literatura Latino-Americana e do Caribe em Ciências da Saúde), CINAHL, PsycINFO and Cochrane. The reference lists of all eligible articles will be manually screened to identify additional relevant citations, ensuring a comprehensive search. Furthermore, a search for grey literature will be conducted via Google Scholar (for manuals, reports, documents, books and conference proceedings) and OpenGrey.eu.

Search strategy

Step 3: selecting eligible studies

Three reviewers (MTdA, ABBG, and BSL) will independently screen the search results based on titles and abstracts. Duplicate studies will be removed. The same reviewers will then perform a full-text review to determine whether the studies meet the predefined inclusion criteria. Discrepancies will be resolved through discussion or by consulting two other reviewers (KSdM and RLAdV). Finally, the study selection process will be summarised using a PRISMA flow diagram (figure 1).

Inclusion criteria

To identify relevant publications, the following inclusion criteria will be applied: original research articles, narrative reviews, systematic reviews, observational studies or other studies pertinent to the subject. Exclusion criteria

Studies will be excluded based on the following criteria: case reports, case series, studies not conducted in healthcare settings and incomplete studies. While duplicate studies will be removed during the screening process, they do not constitute a thematic exclusion criterion.

Step 4: mapping the data

A standardised electronic form will be used for data extraction, to be completed independently by two reviewers (ABBG and BSL). The extracted data will then be converted into a format compatible with Microsoft Excel, creating a database for the subsequent text-mining phase of the review. The data collection instrument will be validated by two experienced researchers (KSdM and RLAdV).

The following information will be mapped by the reviewers: study identification (title, author, year and country) and data related to the research questions (study objectives, methodological design, OL model, healthcare setting, continuing education strategies, and associated benefits and impacts) (table 5).

Step 5: synthesising, summarising and reporting the findings

A descriptive analysis of the mapped studies will be conducted using the electronic framework developed for data extraction and other relevant information. The content of the included studies will be categorised according to the review objectives as follows: (1) OL models; (2) continuing health education strategies and (3) benefits and impacts. The results will be summarised to align with the research purpose.

The level of evidence for the studies included will be assessed using the JBI Levels of Evidence, developed by the JBI Levels of Evidence and Grades of Recommendation Working Party.23

Step 6: integrating expert consultation

Expert consultation provides opportunities for stakeholder and consumer involvement, allowing them to suggest additional references and offer insights beyond those identified in the literature. This step will be incorporated to obtain key feedback and integrate knowledge that may not be accessible through academic publications, such as emerging trends and relevant historical or contextual perspectives. Although the literature reflects variability regarding the timing and extent of expert engagement, this consultation will be undertaken in a structured manner to enhance the methodological rigour, relevance and completeness of the review.1921

Patient and public involvement statement

This is a protocol for a scoping review; the research will be conducted based on a wide and comprehensive literature search from relevant databases; the individual patient data will not be included.

Ethical approval

Approval from the research ethics committee is not required due to the study’s public domain nature.

Ethics and dissemination

The findings are expected to be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal and presented at a scientific conference.
