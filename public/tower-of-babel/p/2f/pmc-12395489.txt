Introduction

Osteosarcoma is a malignant mesenchymal tumor characterized by osteoid production, commonly arising in the long bones. Extraosseous osteosarcoma (EOO), occurring in soft tissues without bone involvement, is rare, and primary renal EOO is exceptionally uncommon, with fewer than 50 reported cases [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Its clinical and imaging features are often nonspecific, leading to diagnostic challenges. Patients may present with abdominal discomfort, hematuria, or be diagnosed incidentally. Imaging may show a renal mass with calcifications, mimicking other malignancies such as sarcomatoid renal cell carcinoma (RCC) [[4], [5], [6], [7]].

Definitive diagnosis relies on histopathology, confirming malignant osteoid and excluding bone-origin tumors. Immunohistochemistry supports the diagnosis by identifying mesenchymal and osteoblastic markers [6,[8], [9], [10]]. Given its aggressive behavior and poor prognosis, early diagnosis and complete resection are critical. Surgery remains the cornerstone of treatment for localized disease. Immunotherapy has rapidly evolved as a promising strategy in controlling the progression of aggressive malignancies [11]. We present a rare case of primary renal EOO and review the literature to improve recognition and management of this entity. This work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [12].

Case presentation

A 56-year-old male with a family history of cancer (father: lung cancer; brother: gastric cancer) presented asymptomatically. He had no history of smoking, chronic illness, or prior surgeries. A routine ultrasound incidentally revealed a left renal mass. On exam, mild left costovertebral angle tenderness was noted. Laboratory tests, including alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and urinalysis, were normal. Contrast-enhanced CT showed a 4 × 5 × 5 cm heterogeneously enhancing mid-pole left renal mass with ill-defined borders, scattered calcifications, and central hypoenhancement. The interface with the left psoas was indistinct, raising suspicion for local invasion (cT4), though no lymphadenopathy was seen (Fig. 1). Bone scan was negative, and renal scintigraphy showed GFRs of 31 and 58 mL/min/1.73 m2 for the left and right kidneys, respectively.

CT images of the left renal tumor. (A) Axial plane and (B) sagittal plane showing a heterogeneously enhancing mass and scattered calcification within the tumor (red arrow).

After multidisciplinary discussion, surgery was chosen due to imaging features suggestive of non-RCC pathology with an experienced surgical team proficient in laparoscopic surgery. One month post diagnosis, laparoscopic radical nephrectomy was performed by an experienced team at a high-volume Urology center in Vietnam, removing the kidney, perinephric fat, psoas fascia, allowing for an objective assessment of potential local invasion. Gross pathology showed a kidney measuring 11 × 6 × 5 cm, contained a 4 × 6 × 5 cm white-brown tumor with hemorrhagic necrosis and ill-defined borders invading perinephric and renal sinus fat. Intraoperatively, no invasion of the psoas muscle was observed, and this was confirmed by negative surgical margins. Estimated blood loss was 50 mL, with no intraoperative or postoperative complications recorded, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day three.

Histological evaluation of the peripelvic lymph node revealed no evidence of tumor involvement. Histologically, hematoxylin and eosin staining demonstrated osteoid and chondroid matrix components, with diffuse proliferation of epithelioid and spindled tumor cells exhibiting marked atypia, hyperchromatic nuclei, frequent mitoses, and multinucleated osteoclast-like giant cells. Immunohistochemical staining revealed positivity for Vimentin and SATB2, and negativity for CK7, CK20, GATA3, p63, and PAX8 (Fig. 2). These findings confirmed the diagnosis of osteoblastic primary renal osteosarcoma, staged as pT3aN0M0.

(B) The tumor displays diffuse proliferation of epithelioid cells, spindled cells of varying sizes, large hyperchromatic nuclei, and marked atypia, interspersed with multinucleated giant cells resembling osteoclasts, set within an osteoid matrix. (C) Neoplastic cells resembling chondrocytes on a chondroid matrix. (D) Immunohistochemical staining reveals negativity for CK20, CK7, GATA3, p63, and PAX8. (E) Immunohistochemical staining is positive for Vimentin. (F) Immunohistochemical staining is positive for SATB2.

The patient declined adjuvant treatment. At six months after surgery, physical examination was unremarkable, but serum creatinine had increased to 112 μmol/L. Follow-up CT revealed multiple osseous lesions involving the L1 vertebral body, sacrum, and left iliac bone, which were not present on the preoperative bone scan—suggesting metastatic disease rather than local recurrence. The patient was referred to medical oncology for consideration of multimodal therapy, and scheduled for imaging follow-up every 3–6 months.

Discussion

Clear cell RCC is the most prevalent among primary renal malignancies, whereas renal sarcoma accounts for approximately 1 % [8]. Osteosarcoma is a subset of primary renal sarcoma, an exceptionally rare malignant mesenchymal tumor. The disease affects both sexes equally, typically between ages 29–80, and has an unclear pathogenesis [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Primary renal osteosarcoma subtype is exceedingly aggressive with a distant metastasis rate of 32 % at the time of diagnosis and an overall survival of approximately 10 months [13]. Common clinical symptoms include flank pain, gross hematuria, weight loss, and a palpable mass in the lumbar region [4,5]. Serum ALP has been suggested for diagnosis, monitoring, and prognosis, but its role remains debated due to inconsistent elevation in metastatic cases [14]. On CT, renal osteosarcoma appears as a heterogeneous mass with irregular borders, necrosis, hemorrhage, or calcification within the lesion [6,7]. The “sunburst” sign is considered a characteristic radiologic feature [13]. Moreover, CT is useful in evaluating the degree of invasion and metastasis, as well as in excluding sarcomas metastasis from other organs to the kidney [1].

The diagnosis of primary renal osteosarcoma relies on histopathology and immunohistochemistry. The pathologic features are characterized by a mixture of osteoblastic, chondroblastic, and fibroblastic cells, resulting in the presence of both bone and cartilage in the tumor [9]. Sarcomatoid RCC with ossification is rare, and identifying carcinoma components is key to distinguishing it from primary renal osteosarcoma. Among renal osteosarcoma variants, the pleomorphic type is most common, while osteoblastic and chondroblastic types are less frequent [1,15]. In this case, given the tumor's location near the renal hilum and its predominant extrarenal component on imaging and gross pathology, differential diagnoses—including retroperitoneal dedifferentiated liposarcoma with heterologous osteosarcomatous differentiation and sarcomatoid carcinoma with heterologous elements, possibly of urothelial origin—were carefully considered. Extensive sampling of the tumor and adjacent mucosa was performed, as accurate diagnosis may require thorough tissue evaluation or even total tumor embedding to exclude differential diagnoses [2,3]. The tumor cells demonstrate expression of osteoblastic differentiation markers, including Vimentin, CD56, Smooth Muscle Actin (SMA), S-100 protein and SATB-2, while epithelial markers are absent, supporting the diagnosis of primary renal osteosarcoma. Vimentin and SMA highlight the mesenchymal origin of the tumor cells, while SATB-2, a specific marker of osteoblastic differentiation, confirms the osteogenic nature of the tumor [6,[8], [9], [10]]. Although typically linked to neuroendocrine differentiation, CD56 can also be expressed in mesenchymal tumors such as osteosarcoma [1]. The combined immunohistochemical profile is crucial for distinguishing primary renal osteosarcoma from other differential diagnoses [4].

Surgery is the primary treatment method with the goal of complete tumor resection and negative surgical margins. Surgical monotherapy should be considered for patients with tumors confined to the organ [15]. Adjuvant therapies, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted treatments with PD-1 antibodies have been reported [1,3]. Adjuvant platinum-based chemotherapy is an effective treatment that improves overall survival, progression-free survival and recommended treatment for extraosseous osteosarcoma, but clinical evidence for primary renal extraosseous osteosarcoma is limited due to the rarity of the condition [16]. PD-1 inhibitor-based immunotherapy is currently being evaluated for osteosarcoma treatment and has shown preliminary efficacy [17]. However, there's a lack of evidence to support its use in extraskeletal sarcomas. There's a growing trend towards combining multiple therapeutic modalities, such as surgery, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy or surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, which appears to yield improved treatment outcomes [13,16]. This patient was incidentally diagnosed with primary renal extraosseous osteosarcoma at a localized stage and was treated with surgery. However, the disease progression was notably aggressive, with distant metastases appearing within six months of surgery. This is markedly faster than the typical progression reported in the literature, which often occurs beyond 12 months postoperatively [4,15].

Combining imaging findings that raised suspicion for a rare malignancy with immunohistochemical analysis allowed accurate histological diagnosis. However, due to the limited available evidence, there has yet to be a significant improvement in the prognosis of patients with primary renal extraosseous osteosarcoma. This case, discovered incidentally in an otherwise asymptomatic patient, underscores the critical role of regular health checkups and general screening tools such as abdominal ultrasonography. Early detection—even in rare malignancies—can enable timely intervention and may potentially alter clinical outcomes. Artificial intelligence (AI) offers promising solutions for the early diagnosis and personalized treatment of rare tumors like renal extraosseous osteosarcoma. Models such as AlphaFold have advanced drug discovery, while AI-driven imaging tools enhance genomic prediction and therapeutic planning, potentially improving diagnostic accuracy and guiding targeted interventions [18,19].

Conclusion

In summary, primary renal osteosarcoma is an exceptionally rare and aggressive neoplasm, with early diagnosis posing significant challenges due to its nonspecific clinical presentation. Immunohistochemical analysis is crucial for definitive diagnosis. The combination of radical surgical resection with negative margins and adjuvant chemotherapy may improve therapeutic outcomes. More case series are needed to evaluate the diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis of this disease.
