Investigation the implementation of evidence-based protocols for cannulation in Arteriovenous Fistula: a mixed methods study
Authors: Lulu Hu, Hongyan Niu, Cong Liang, Xiuming Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe cannulation technique employed by nurses significantly impacts arteriovenous fistula (AVF) patency rates. To ensure optimal outcomes, nurses must strictly adhere to evidence-based protocols and maintain up-to-date knowledge of best practices in cannulation methods.MethodsA sequential mixed-methods study was conducted. In May 2024, convenience-sampled nurses from haemodialysis units in Jiangsu Provence, China, completed an evidence-based questionnaire on AVF cannulation practices and associated factors. Subsequently, purposively sampled haemodialysis nurses and nurse managers participated in semi-structured interviews. Quantitative data were analysed using descriptive statistics, and qualitative data were analysed thematically.ResultsA total of 489 nurses completed the survey, and 10 nurses participated in semi-structured interviews. Quantitative findings revealed that while 89.16% of departments had cannulation plans, only 65.44% had corresponding diagrams and just 62.78% of these included both vessel direction and cannulation sites. Regarding cannulation techniques (multiple selections allowed), although rope ladder puncture (RL) was the most commonly used technique (96.93%), nearly half of the respondents still employed area puncture (AP) (44.38%), despite guideline recommendations against it. Multivariate regression analysis showed that the availability of ultrasound-guided cannulation was negatively associated with the use of AP or buttonhole puncture (BH) (P < 0.05). Higher evidence-based practice (EBP) competency scores (P = 0.006) and years of dialysis experience (P = 0.001) were positively associated with the use of BH. In contrast, the role of head nurse (P = 0.001), the establishment of a vascular access team (P = 0.03) were all negatively associated with the use of BH. Qualitative analysis revealed three key themes: (1) Insufficient implementation of cannulation plans in clinical practice, (2) lack of standardized competency assessment for nurses, and (3) weak EBP awareness and implementation capacity. These findings suggest that the persistent use of suboptimal techniques stems not from a lack of guidelines, but from systemic issues including skill mismatches, insufficient monitoring, and limited EBP integration.ConclusionsThis mixed-methods study reveals a critical gap between evidence-based guidelines and the clinical reality of vascular access cannulation in hemodialysis settings. While quantitative findings indicate suboptimal adherence to recommended techniques, qualitative insights elucidate that this is not merely a knowledge deficit but is deeply rooted in fragmented responsibility, inadequate competency assessment systems, and weak organizational support for EBP. Therefore, improving patient safety and cannulation outcomes requires moving beyond simple guideline dissemination to implement multi-level interventions. We recommend establishing structured skill validation programs and specialized vascular access teams to foster a culture of accountability and ensure the translation of best evidence into routine clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-05025-6.

Background

Hemodialysis requires vascular access, with the arteriovenous fistula (AVF) being the most common and effective option [1]. AVF patency and function can be influenced by factors such as anatomy, comorbidities, age, creation method, daily care, and cannulation technique [2]. An AVF used for hemodialysis is typically cannulated with two needles three times a week, totaling at least 312 cannulations annually. The cannulation technique affects both long-term patency and complication outcomes [3]. Nurses play a key role in selecting the cannulation technique and providing daily care, which significantly impacts AVF patency [4].

Clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) aim to delegate evidence analysis to information management professionals, link practice with scientific knowledge, and enhance patient care quality [5]. There are evidences on how to perform cannulation and which technique to choose in arteriovenous fistula [2, 6, 7]. Five cannulation techniques have been described in the literature: rope ladder puncture (RL), area puncture (AP), buttonhole puncture with blunt needles (BHb), buttonhole puncture with sharp needles (BHs) [8] and multiple single cannulation technique(MuST) [9].

Each has its advantages and disadvantages. Guidelines recommend RL or BH for AVF, and BH preferred when the vessel is too short to provide sufficient length, and RL for arteriovenous graft (AVG). American, Australian, and some European guidelines recommend RL as the primary technique due to fewer infections, though BH may be considered for short cannulation segments [7, 10]. The execution of RL lacks a clear definition, possibly due to limited research and the reliance on experience-based knowledge for local recommendations. It varies slightly across units, leading to potential misunderstandings, from individual nurses to local policies [11]. Nurses described a method similar to RL as involving new, systematically distributed cannulation sites across the AVF, while others compared it to BHs, rotating between multiple sites. This technique is called MuST in Portugal [12]. BH is frequently selected first. It should also be possible for every nurse on the team to cannulate every patient without having to alter their method. To avoid re-cannulation and the trampoline effect, buttonhole is divided into sharp and blunt needle techniques. New or less experienced nurses tend to prefer buttonhole with sharp needles, as it eliminates the need for site selection, allowing them to focus on needling [13]. AP is discouraged due to its association with complications like stenosis, bleeding, and aneurysms [14]. Nurses’ recommendations and area guidelines both said that AP should be avoided since it causes aneurysms [15]. Although RL was said to reduce problems and prolong patency, nurses also noted that it was more difficult to execute and that there was a higher risk of infiltration. AP is the most common technique in Brasil [16], RL in Australia [17] and the U.S [18]., and BHb in Sweden [4]. In Beijing, China, a survey of 543 hemodialysis nurses found that 96.5% used RL, 27.3% used BH, and 26.9% used AP for AVF; for AVG, 93.9% used RL, 13.5% used BH, and 19.9% used AP [19].

There is evidence that nurses adjust the cannulation technique based on patient needs. For example, if a technique causes pain or fear of needles, the nurse will try to adjust the technique. Nurses also described how patients are only verbally informed about the different cannulation techniques. To increase patient participation, written materials in the patient’s language, such as those for hemodialysis patients [20], could be provided. Another key aspect of good cannulation practice, agreed upon by dialysis units, is continuity between nurses. This means that knowledge of the individual AVF through physical examination and descriptions of AVF and cannulations in the medical record is fundamental for the given care. Continuity is particularly important when using BH [4], as nurses must insert the needle at the same direction and angle to ensure less pain and a smoother procedure for the patient [21].

Nurses can further standardize cannulation procedures by documenting vascular access information, including storing images of cannulation plans and recording cannulation directions and angles [22]. Ultrasound-guided cannulation, as a visualization tool, can display the position and depth of blood vessels as well as the needle tip, thereby increasing cannulation success rate—particularly in cases involving difficult or complex vascular access. Ultrasound has been shown to significantly improve cannulation success rate [23]. Prevention of infection is critically important for AVFs and AVGs. Key measures identified in the evidence summary include ensuring that the patient’s arm is clean and dry prior to puncture, and that nurses perform the procedure using strict aseptic technique. These practices play an essential role in reducing the risk of infection and are strongly recommended in current clinical guidelines [24].

There are gaps between clinical practice implementation and best evidence [2–4, 25]. The adherence to these guidelines varies, with different hospitals and countries favoring different methods [19, 26, 27].

Methods

Study design

This study utilized an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design to achieve a comprehensive understanding of vascular access cannulation practices. The design was operationalized in two rigorously connected phases to specifically investigate the implementation fidelity of evidence-based recommendations and the underlying causes of non-adherence. Phase 1: Quantitative Assessment of Adherence and Gap Identification. Initial phase involved a cross-sectional survey targeting hemodialysis nurses. This phase was designed to measure the level of adherence to evidence-based recommendations outlined in the evidence summary and clinical guidelines. Specifically, we quantified the prevalence of specific techniques—such as RL + AP versus RL only to objectively identify and map the discrepancies between recommended practice and actual clinical behavior. These identified gaps formed the empirical basis for the subsequent qualitative inquiry. Phase 2: Qualitative Exploration of Underlying Reasons. Building directly upon the quantitative findings, the second phase employed semi-structured interviews. Purposive sampling was used to recruit nurses who represented varying degrees of adherence identified in Phase 1. The interview guide was specifically tailored to explore the contextual, systemic, and cognitive barriers explaining why the non-adherence observed in the survey occurred. Integration and Analysis: The integration of data occurred at the interpretation stage. We employed a connecting strategy where qualitative themes were explicitly mapped onto the quantitative compliance gaps. For instance, statistical associations (e.g., low use of a technique) were juxtaposed with interview narratives explaining the barriers to that technique. This process transformed isolated statistical findings into a cohesive, multidimensional explanation of the current practice landscape. The reporting of this study adhered to the GRAMMS checklist.

Participants

Participants were nurses working in hemodialysis centers in Jiangsu Province in China. Participants were eligible for inclusion if they met all of the following criteria: Employed as a nurse in a hemodialysis center (blood purification center) at the time of the study. Currently working in the center (i.e., not on leave, sick leave, or absent for other reasons). Held a valid registered nurse license. Had obtained the specialized qualification certificate for hemodialysis nursing. Only nurses fulfilling all four conditions were invited to participate in the survey. The survey was distributed by the Blood Purification Nursing Professional Committee of Jiangsu Province Nursing Association through a description and link in their online newsletters. Additionally, snowball sampling was used, with the lead author sharing the survey link with relevant contacts, who then forwarded it to colleagues across the province.

Qualitative data were collected through semi-structured interviews with nurses working in hemodialysis centers. Participants were recruited using purposive sampling to ensure diversity in clinical settings, years of experience, and professional roles. Interviews were conducted until the analysis indicated that the range of perspectives relevant to the research question had been comprehensively captured and no substantially new thematic insights were arising. This iterative sampling approach ensured informational adequacy while adhering to Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis methodology [28, 29].

The quantitative data were collected using Question Star (Wenjuanxing, Changsha Ranxing Information Technology Co., Ltd., China) by a self-developed questionnaire comprising three sections. The first section recorded general information about the participating nurses, including years of hemodialysis nursing experience, professional rank, type and grade of hospital, and relevant characteristics of the dialysis center. The second section evaluated current practices related to cannulation procedures. Items in this section were developed based on a Chinese evidence summary, which synthesized recommendations from multiple sources, including both national and international clinical practice guidelines, clinical decision support systems, systematic reviews, and expert consensus statements [24]. Based on the evidence summary, the practice section was organized into four main domains, each represented by a set of specific items: (a) Choice of cannulation technique—Items in this domain asked respondents to indicate which cannulation technique they routinely used in clinical practice, with response options covering RL, AP, BH, and MuST. This was intended to measure adherence to evidence-based recommendations regarding technique selection. (b) Pre-puncture assessment—This domain included questions about the steps taken prior to performing a puncture, such as skin preparation, site selection, and evaluation of vascular access condition. The items were formulated to reflect the procedural requirements specified in the source guidelines and evidence summary. (c) Personnel qualifications for cannulation procedures—Questions here collected information on the professional background, training, certification, and experience of staff members responsible for cannulation procedures. This aimed to determine whether the task was performed by appropriately qualified individuals as recommended in the evidence sources. (d) Complications related to cannulation—The final domain investigated the occurrence and types of complications associated with cannulation, such as bleeding, hematoma, infection, and vascular damage. Respondents were asked to report the frequency of these events and the management approaches used. This was included to evaluate the impact of current practice patterns on patient safety outcomes. Each item was initially drafted in accordance with the recommendations extracted from the evidence summary. Responses were in dichotomous or multiple choice response formats. The third section assessed nurses’ knowledge or attitudes toward evidence-based practice (EBP) for cannulation. The Developing Evidence-Based Practice questionnaire (DEBPQ), constructed by Gerrish [30] and translated into Chinese by Yang [31], was used. The 49-item DEBPQ assesses five domains: ‘Practice knowledge bases’, ‘Barriers to finding and reviewing evidence’, ‘Barriers to changing practice on the basis of evidence’, ‘Support to change practice’ and ‘EBP skills’, with five-point Likert scales. Subsequently, the draft questionnaire was reviewed by a multidisciplinary panel of clinical experts, including nephrologists, dialysis nurses, and methodologists, who assessed content validity, clarity, and relevance. Minor revisions were made based on expert feedback to ensure that all items accurately captured the intended aspects of clinical practice. The finalized questionnaire was pilot-tested among five nurses (not on the research team) in the authors’ unit to confirm feasibility and comprehensibility before full-scale administration.

The qualitative data were collected through individual, face-to-face or telephone semi-structured interviews. Interviews were conducted in a quiet room free from interruptions, and each session was limited to a maximum duration of 30 min. Telephone interviewing allows access to participants who might be unable to attend in-person due to workload constraints or distance, and it can encourage openness by providing a sense of anonymity. Developed from the questionnaire, the interview guide aimed to explore underlying reasons for key statistical trends, with each theme mapped to a corresponding quantitative construct. The guide comprised open-ended questions designed to elicit detailed descriptions and reflections from participants. Main topics addressed in the interviews included: (a) Current cannulation procedures in the dialysis center—Participants were asked to describe how cannulation procedures were regulated in their hospital, with particular attention to protocols for challenging cases such as new or difficult vascular accesses. Questions explored step-by-step workflows, decision points, and any adaptations made in complex scenarios. (b) Determination of cannulation plans for patients—Participants were asked how patient-specific cannulation plans were formulated in their setting, including criteria considered, sources of information consulted, and the healthcare professionals responsible for making these decisions. This aimed to clarify the division of responsibility and the influence of clinical judgment versus standardized protocols. (c) Participants were asked to describe how they incorporate best available evidence into their clinical cannulation practice. Specifically, they were prompted to discuss situations where recommended evidence was not applied in their unit, such as: failure to use ultrasound guidance; absence of a planned cannulation protocol for patients; repeated use of AP without rotation; or the use of needle-tip to remove the scab of BH that differ from the guideline-recommended approach. For each instance, they were asked to explain the reasons why these EBPs were not implemented. (d) Assessment of nurses’ cannulation competence—Participants were questioned about existing mechanisms for evaluating nurses’ proficiency in performing cannulation procedures. Topics covered included the existence of formal competency assessments, performance indicators, frequency of evaluation, and any training or certification requirements linked to procedural competence. The interview guide was pilot-tested with two nurses to assess clarity and relevance, and minor adjustments were made before full-scale data collection. All interviews were audio-recorded with consent, transcribed verbatim, and imported into NVivo software for coding and thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach.

Data analysis

Quantitative survey data were entered into SPSS (IBM Corp., version 25.0) and analyzed descriptively using frequency/percent and mean/SD (or median/IQR for non-normal data). Multivariable logistic regression was used to determine the factors influencing cannulation technique selection. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Data analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework [28, 29]. First, audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and checked for accuracy. Transcripts were read repeatedly to enhance familiarity with the data (familiarization). Initial codes were generated inductively by systematically identifying features of the data that appeared relevant to the research aims (coding). These codes were collated into potential themes, which were reviewed and refined by comparing them against the dataset (theme development). Themes were defined and named to accurately capture their essence (theme definition and naming). Finally, the thematic map was reviewed to ensure coherence with both the coded extracts and the overall narrative of the data (producing the report). To ensure data consistency, the data were entered into an Excel spreadsheet by researchers experienced in qualitative analysis. Each interview was independently color-coded and categorized by the researchers, then collaboratively reviewed to group similar content and identify recurring themes, ensuring intercoder reliability. Direct quotes from participants were incorporated to enhance validity [32]. Throughout this process, NVivo software was used to assist data management and retrieval. This systematic procedure ensured that the final themes were firmly grounded in participant accounts and reflected the complexity of translating evidence into clinical practice. All interviews were conducted by the first author, and the transcription of audio recordings. The entire qualitative data analysis process including coding, theme development, and interpretation were carried out collaboratively by the first and second authors, following Braun and Clarke’s framework.

Table 3 summarizes the development and management of cannulation plan among the participating nurses. Although the majority of respondents (89.16%, n = 436) reported that their departments had cannulation plans, the availability of cannulation diagram was considerably lower (65.44%, n = 320), indicating a gap between policy and practical implementation tools. Among units with diagrams, electronic integration into the information system (61.96%, n = 303) was more common than paper-based format (38.04%, n = 186). However, only 62.78% (n = 307) of diagrams included information on both vessel direction and cannulation site, suggesting that even when diagrams exist, their clinical utility may be limited.

Regarding responsibility for cannulation planning, access nurses were the most frequently involved (64.62%, n = 316), followed by first-puncturing nurses (52.76%, n = 258) and access doctors (46.22%, n = 226). Patient involvement in plan development was minimal (4.70%, n = 23), reflecting a predominantly provider-driven approach to cannulation planning.

In terms of technique selection philosophy, a majority of nurses (63.39%, n = 310) favored tailoring methods to individual patient conditions, while 36.61% (n = 179) preferred a standardized approach. This split suggests ongoing tension between individualized care and protocol-driven practice in cannulation management.

Development and Management of Cannulation Plan

Regarding cannulation techniques used in clinical practice, RL was the most commonly reported method (96.93%, n = 474), while BH and AP were used by less than half of the respondents (45.60% and 44.38%, respectively), indicating that RL remains the dominant approach in clinical practice.

With respect to scab removal in BH, the syringe needle bevel was the most commonly used method (63.39%, n = 310), followed by sterile ophthalmic forceps (20.04%, n = 98) and a scab-removal device on the needle cap (16.56%, n = 81).

When selecting a cannulation technique, nurses prioritized clinical factors over patient-centered considerations. The most frequently cited determinants were procedural difficulty (87.93%, n = 430) and anticipated success rate (84.87%, n = 415). In contrast, patient preferences (51.94%, n = 254) were less commonly considered, suggesting a potential gap in patient-centered care.

When facing a difficult cannulation site or a lack of confidence in their own skills, the majority of nurses (76.08%, n = 372) sought assistance from skilled colleagues to adhere to the original plan. However, a notable proportion deviated from the plan by selecting alternative RL sites (11.45%, n = 56) or converting to AP (5.52%, n = 27) to ensure success. Only a small minority strictly followed the original plan despite anticipated failure (4.91%, n = 24) or used ultrasound guidance (2.04%, n = 10), highlighting a reliance on experiential knowledge rather than technology-assisted approaches when facing difficulty. Table 4 summarizes clinical cannulation practices and related factors.

The survey showed that the majority of nurses reported performing physical examination before cannulation (94.27%, n = 461) and advising patients to conduct home-based self-examination (96.73%, n = 473). Ultrasound assessment of fistula maturity prior to cannulation was conducted by 80.37% (n = 393) of respondents, and regular ultrasound follow-up was recommended by 83.84% (n = 410).

Nurses’ DEBPQ scores

The total mean score for EBP competence was 3.38 (SD = 0.62) on a 5-point scale, indicating a moderate level of self-reported EBP competence among participants (Table 6). Among the subscales, the highest mean score was observed for sources of knowledge (M = 3.78, SD = 0.64), followed by barriers to finding and reviewing evidence (M = 3.47, SD = 0.82), barriers to changing practice (M = 3.46, SD = 0.81), and support for changing practice (M = 3.44, SD = 0.85). The lowest mean score was found for EBP skill levels (M = 2.76, SD = 0.89), suggesting that nurses perceived their practical skills in EBP as relatively under developed.

Factors influencing cannulation technique selection

As shown in Table 7 (Variable assignment is detailed in Supplementary File 3), when comparing nurses who used both RL and AP versus those who used RL only, the availability of ultrasound-guided cannulation was significantly associated with a lower likelihood of using AP (P = 0.001), indicating that nurses equipped with ultrasound guidance were less likely to employ AP.

Several factors were significantly associated with the choice of RL and BH versus RL only. Higher scores on the DEBPQ were associated with an increased likelihood of using BH (P = 0.006). Years of dialysis experience were positively associated with the choice of BH (β = 0.128, P = 0.001). Nurses with more experience had higher odds of adopting this technique (OR = 1.056, 95% CI: 1.011–1.105). Conversely, head nurses was associated with a lower likelihood of BH (P = 0.001), suggesting that nurses in leadership roles may prefer RL over BH. Similarly, the establishment of a vascular access team was associated with reduced odds of BH (P = 0.03). The availability of ultrasound-guided cannulation also showed a significant negative association with BH (P = 0.025).

In the analysis comparing RL, AP, and BH versus RL only, the provision of training in cannulation skills was significantly associated with technique selection (P = 0.011), indicating that nurses who received training were more likely to adopt a range of cannulation techniques.

Ten nurses were interviewed (Supplementary File 4), and the analysis identified three key themes. These themes, described below with illustrative quotations, relate to: (1) AVF cannulation practices; (2) implementation processes; and (3) factors influencing cannulation implementation.

Despite the clinical assignment of challenging fistula cannulation to designated personnel, the criteria for this designation remain ambiguous. There is a notable tendency among nursing staff to not explore evidence-based alternative cannulation methods to address procedural difficulties, reflecting a relatively weak awareness and application of EBP. This is further compounded by a general lack of an EBP-oriented learning environment within the department, characterized by insufficient training on relevant knowledge. Consequently, clinical nurses often lack proficiency in core EBP skills, such as literature retrieval, critical appraisal of evidence, and the translation of evidence into practice.

Qualitative evidence serves to complement the quantitative findings

This study, by comparing “how cannulation is performed” with “why the cannulation protocol is not followed”, reveals a critical gap between clinical practice and evidence-based protocols. Quantitative data show that current practices significantly deviate from ideal standards. Qualitative analysis uncovers the underlying reasons for this discrepancy: it stems not from nurses’ ignorance or rejection of best practices, but primarily from uneven skill levels, a lack of standardized solutions for complex cases, and clinical constraints. These findings indicate that the practice gap is driven mainly by systemic and situational barriers, not individual preference. Therefore, we recommend bridging this gap through structured interventions such as establishing clear competency frameworks, implementing targeted skill training, and providing evidence-based tools for complex situations​ to translate theoretical protocols into sustainable clinical practice.

Discussion

This study reveal that nurses’ EBP competency, the presence of a vascular access team, cannulation skills training and assessment, and ultrasound guidance availability are key factors influencing cannulation technique selection. These results are consistent with those of Fan et al. [19], who similarly identified the importance of structured training and team-based approaches. We emphasize that vascular access work should be grounded in specialized access nurses or teams. Previous research has also highlighted the role of vascular access coordinators in improving AVF care [33]. Taken together, these findings underscore that effective collaboration between vascular access nurses, physicians, and coordinators is essential for evaluating, planning, and managing challenging or new fistulas [34].

The availability of ultrasound equipment is negatively correlated with nurses’ choice of AP over RL. Ultrasound-guided cannulation motivates nurses to prefer RL, as it addresses technical challenges in accessing difficult AVFs. Traditionally, experienced nurses performed blind cannulation; however, the high failure rate and patient distress from repeated attempts can lead to frustration and erode trust in caregivers [35, 36]. Ultrasound enables real-time needle visualization, improving single-attempt cannulation success and preventing complications [37]. Therefore, in hospitals with ultrasound equipment, nurses are more likely to choose RL, even for challenging cases, rather than resorting to AP.

This survey found that 217 (44.38%) nurses used AP, despite guidelines recommending against it due to complications like bleeding, stenosis, and pseudoaneurysm [38]. This research shows that 98 nurses (20.0%) used all three techniques, with trained nurses more likely to tailor techniques to individual patient conditions. Each cannulation technique has distinct advantages and disadvantages. Previous studies have shown that different techniques can lead to differences in cannulation success rates, patient-reported pain levels, and the incidence of cannulation-related complications [39]. Notably, BH, though often preferred, has been linked to intimal hyperplasia and infection [40–42]. While RL is the most endorsed method, its implementation varies due to challenges such as difficult access, patient pain and fear, and variability in nurses’ skills, attitudes, and biases. Effective implementation, regardless of the plan’s quality, remains a key consideration in decision-making. Therefore, cannulation technique selection should consider the patient’s vascular condition, hygiene and self-care abilities, as well as the healthcare team’s experience and patient preferences [43]. To enhance standardized cannulation practices and nurse skills, regular education and training on vascular access techniques should be conducted, with ongoing evaluation and retraining in various cannulation techniques [44].

Nurses with higher EBP capabilities are more likely to discover, implementation, and evaluate new cannulation techniques in clinical settings. Conversely, low self-efficacy in technical skills can drive nurses to rely on familiar techniques like RL—perceived as “safer” despite evidence favoring alternatives. However, this limited technical confidence is not merely an individual deficit; it may also reflect a workplace culture that lacks the adequate support or training for EBP implementation. Nurses with greater seniority possess more extensive clinical experience and skills, which enhance their cannulation techniques and foster greater patient trust, thereby facilitating the successful application of BH. In contrast, head nurses—who bear ultimate responsibility for unit-level quality indicators such as infection and complication rates—may favor RL due to its lower infection risk and greater suitability for team-wide standardization. A management perspective focused on risk mitigation and team uniformity favors techniques that are easy to standardize and less reliant on individual skill, making them more practical for departments with diverse skill levels.

The survey identified the overall prevalence of specific cannulation practices and quantified the extent to which they aligned with best evidence. Despite the availability of cannulation plans and diagrams, the diagrams lacked essential information on vessel direction and cannulation sites and were not readily accessible, resulting in a gap between guidelines and clinical practice and limited practical usefulness. Of particular concern is the persistent use of AP, despite its well-documented association with vascular damage and complications. Nurses may resort to AP when facing difficulty, yet lack the support or resources to adopt safer alternatives. The extremely low rate of ultrasound use in difficult cannulation situations further underscores this point. When nurses encounter challenging accesses, they overwhelmingly rely on experienced colleagues rather than technology-assisted approaches, reflecting a deeply ingrained culture of experience-dependent decision-making rather than technology-enabled precision. Using the syringe needle bevel for scab removal in BH—a practice inconsistent with guideline recommendations—highlights a further gap between knowledge and practice.

Thematic analysis of the interview data uncovered several recurrent factors—such as limited continuing education opportunities, institutional protocols, time constraints, and ingrained habits—that contributed to the observed gaps. This integrated approach allowed us to formulate conclusions that reflect both the magnitude of the issue (from the quantitative data) and the mechanisms sustaining it (from the qualitative data). It also highlights that improving evidence-based cannulation practice cannot rely solely on policy dissemination; rather, it requires targeted interventions addressing the specific barriers identified in this study. The synthesis of both data types thus provides a more actionable and nuanced understanding of the current situation than either method could achieve independently.

This study has several limitations. Most notably, the statistical model did not account for key patient-centered variables, such as patients’ understanding of cannulation procedures, their specific demands or preferences regarding treatment, and their knowledge of fistula maintenance. Additionally, the quantitative data relied on self-report, which may be subject to social desirability bias. The qualitative portion included only 10 participants, which may not fully capture diverse perspectives. The non-random selection of hemodialysis facilities may limit representativeness. Future research should employ larger, multi-center samples and incorporate objective observation measures to validate these findings.

Conclusion

This mixed-methods study provides a comprehensive understanding of the current status and underlying determinants of vascular access cannulation practices among hemodialysis nurses. The quantitative findings reveal a significant gap between theoretical guidelines and clinical reality: despite widespread awareness of cannulation plans, the actual implementation of supporting tools and adherence to recommended techniques remain suboptimal. Notably, the persistent use of the inappropriate AP by nearly half of the respondents, even in the presence of established guidelines, highlights a critical patient safety concern.

The integration of qualitative data offers a deeper explanation for these discrepancies. The identified themes—fragmented responsibility, lack of standardized competency assessment, and weak EBP implementation capacity—suggest that the barriers to optimal cannulation are not merely informational but are deeply rooted in systemic and organizational deficiencies. The quantitative association between lower EBP competency scores and the use of advanced techniques further supports this interpretation.

Our findings underscore that improving cannulation outcomes requires more than simply disseminating guidelines. Effective interventions must adopt a multi-level approach that bridges individual capability and organizational support. This includes establishing structured skill validation programs and promoting specialized vascular access teams to ensure systemic accountability. Equally important is the cultivation of a revised work structure that facilitates the sharing of knowledge, education, and training, enabling newer nurses to learn from experienced colleagues within an evidence-based framework. Ultimately, fostering a workplace culture that actively supports EBP is essential to enhance both patient safety and clinical outcomes.
