Escalating diabetes prevalence in pakistan: a qualitative study of endocrinologists’ perspectives on contributing factors, management barriers, and strategies
Authors: Muhammad Ebad Khan, Matti Ullah, Muhammad Usman Khan, Malaika Nasir, Irum Khan, Nayab Tariq, Owais Qarni, Zainab Ishtiaq, Azra Batool, Mahnoor Ajmal, Aqsa Abbasi, Alishba Bibi, Tanveer Afsar Malik, Sidra Hafeez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPakistan has one of the highest diabetes prevalence rates globally, yet limited research exists on endocrinologists’ perspectives regarding drivers, barriers, and solutions.MethodsThis qualitative study conducted semi-structured interviews with 21 endocrinologists across Islamabad, Rawalpindi, and Peshawar from March to April 2026. Purposive sampling was used to recruit participants. Inductive thematic analysis following Braun & Clarke’s framework identified four main themes and nineteen subthemes.ResultsEndocrinologists perceived a drastic rise in diabetes prevalence, with most estimating that one in three adults is now affected (based on their clinical observations) and reported a 50% increase in cases over 2–3 years and early onset type 2 diabetes in patients as young as 17–20 years. Urban–rural disparities were evident: urban areas have higher prevalence driven by sedentary lifestyles and unhealthy diets, while rural populations present with advanced disease and limited access to care. Factors contributing to diabetes prevalence included unhealthy dietary habits, sedentary lifestyle, obesity (with one participant estimating 52% central obesity), genetic predisposition, and stress/sleep disturbances. Major barriers comprised poor treatment adherence, financial constraints (insulin unaffordable for many), low health literacy (with most participants estimating approximately 98% of patients lacking basic knowledge), cultural misconceptions (insulin phobia, traditional remedies), medication shortages, and healthcare system limitations (2–3 min per patient). Strategies identified were patient education (with one participant estimating 80% of disease management), early screening, primordial prevention from childhood, government support for affordable medications, and the pharmacist as an underutilized diabetes educator.ConclusionPakistan faces an accelerating diabetes epidemic with a concerning shift to younger populations. Multi-level interventions addressing financial barriers, health literacy, cultural beliefs, systemic constraints, and expanding the pharmacist’s role as educator are urgently needed.Clinical trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12902-026-02425-5.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes (T2DM) is the most common and has become the leading one in developing nations [1]. Currently, diabetes is one of the most prevalent chronic conditions, impacting approximately 10.5% of adults worldwide, and is one of the most significant contributors to morbidity and mortality. The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) diabetes Atlas has released estimates for the prevalence of diabetes for the year 2021, presenting data at the global, regional, and national levels, as well as projections for the year 2045 [2]. Global health spending on diabetes in 2010 was at least USD 376 billion (ID 418 billion), and in 2030, it will be USD 490 billion (ID561 billion) [3]. Over the next twenty years, the region of South Asia is predicted to see an increase in diabetes cases by approximately twenty to thirty five% [4]. Currently, 6.9% of people in Pakistan have diabetes. That number is expected to grow to 15% in 2040, which would put Pakistan 3rd in the world for diabetes prevalence [5].

Diabetes is frequently referred to as a “silent epidemic” because it is usually asymptomatic in the initial stages and is particularly abundant in the presence of obesity in adults, occurring up to 90%. Complications of diabetes such as microvascular and macrovascular issues. Microvascular issues include retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy while on the other side, cardiovascular disease is an example of a macrovascular problem [6]. In Pakistan, one key barrier to effective diabetes management is inadequate diagnosis as 26.9% of affected individuals remain undiagnosed [7]. The highest proportion of undiagnosed diabetes alongside genetic vulnerability, sedentary lifestyle, diet rich in fat and sugar, rapid urban expansion, and unequal distribution of inadequate rural healthcare, has made the condition alarmingly widespread throughout the nation. Pakistan has the highest prevalence of diabetes in the entire Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region [8]. Diabetes can significantly affect someone’s quality of life, and it often comes with serious mental health issues, including anxiety, depression, social withdrawal, and a distorted body image [9]. In the U.S., 36.7% of adults with metabolic syndrome also have diabetes [10].

Numerous studies demonstrate the significant knowledge gap among people, especially those living with diabetes, about the intricacies of their condition with estimates ranging between 46% and 70%. 50% of adults with type 2 diabetes are undiagnosed and unaware that they have the disease [11, 12]. Common barriers to self-care include financial constraints, physical limitations, susceptibility to palatable foods, forgetfulness, and demanding work schedules, while risky behaviors such as smoking, excessive sedentary time, and poor dietary choices remain highly prevalent among adults [13, 14]. It is also well documented that risk factors for non-communicable diseases among adults, such as high tobacco use, physical inactivity, and poor diets, exist [15]. Out of the different types of diabetes, type 2 diabetes is the most prevalent. It’s mainly due to the increased rate of obesity (a BMI of 35 or more) and lack of physical activity. In developing nations, physical inactivity and poor dietary habits are significant contributors. Furthermore, blood pressure, cholesterol, and processed food consumption are also significant factors [16]. As a result, the need for focused, region-specific prevention strategies for high diabetes burden countries becomes critical [17].

Most research studies regarding diabetes prevalence concentrate on metrics: number of cases, numbers by geography, and numbers by age. There is a scarcity of research that consults with healthcare professionals, particularly endocrinologists, regarding what they believe to be the real contributory factors on the ground. Prevention strategies often miss the mark without qualitative insight. No extensive qualitative studies in Pakistan as well as internationally examine endocrinologists’ views regarding the causes of diabetes prevalence. Most studies are clinical audits or quantitative surveys. They miss out on the realities faced by physicians who contend with this barrage of patients daily and without that evidence, policymakers can’t design targeted, cost-effective prevention programs. The main objective of this study is to analyze diabetes prevalence patient profiles in Pakistan to explain contributing factors (obesity, genetics, poor diet, inactivity, socioeconomic pressures), and present management challenges (poor education, resource gaps, delayed diagnosis, cost constraints) and discuss clinical modifications considering local resource constraints and suggest potential solutions (early screening, affordable drugs, pharmacist educator, adherence to guidelines that are internationally accepted.

Methodology

Study design and setting

We employed a qualitative descriptive approach, conducting in-depth face-to-face interviews guided by a semi-structured interview protocol. Participants were selected through purposive sampling, intentionally recruiting endocrinologists across diverse clinical settings and geographic locations to capture information-rich cases aligned with our research objectives. We held face-to-face, semi-structured interviews at participants’ workplaces in Islamabad, Rawalpindi, and Peshawar from March 2026 to April 2026. The interview guide is available as Supplementary file S1, and adherent to COREQ checklist is provided as Supplementary file S2.

Participants

We recruited endocrinologists based on criteria set by the lead researcher. Endocrinologists were selected as the sole participant group due to their primary role in managing complex diabetes, combining clinical and epidemiological expertise with high-volume frontline experience. Their metabolic training uniquely positions them to offer integrated perspectives on contributory factors, barriers, and solutions. Although primary care physicians also manage diabetes, endocrinologists handle the most challenging cases and broader complications, making them ideal qualitative informants. Demographic information (age, gender, qualifications, practice setting, and years of experience) was recorded during interviews. Experience was defined as years since completion of the highest qualification (FCPS or postgraduate training) in endocrinology or related specialty. Categories were standardized for consistency. Of the 30 contacted, 21 agreed to take part; the other nine declined due to scheduling issues or lack of interest.

To establish content validity, the interview guide was evaluated by a panel of experts and a psychologist, who provided insights to optimize its effectiveness for data collection. The interview guide was piloted with two endocrinologists who met the inclusion criteria. Pilot interviews assessed question clarity, flow, and duration, leading to minor wording refinement and the addition of probing prompts. Data from pilot interviews were not included in the final analysis. We ceased data collection when thematic saturation was achieved operationalized as the stage where successive interviews yielded no novel codes or thematic insights. Saturation was first observed after 19 interviews; two additional interviews confirmed redundancy, leading us to conclude data collection at 21 participants. Saturation was initially achieved after 19 interviews; two further interviews confirmed that no new information arose, and data collection was therefore concluded at 21 interviews [20].Two researchers conducted interviews: MN (Pharm D, experienced in qualitative research) and MEK (M. Phil Scholar in Pharmacy Practice, trained in qualitative methods). Neither had any prior relationship with the participants; they made it clear they were independent academics so that people would feel comfortable speaking openly. Both were trained in semi-structured qualitative interviewing and completed two pilot interviews each to fine-tune their approach before the actual data collection. With participants’ permission, they recorded the interviews and jotted down observations. Each interview lasted about 20–25 min on average. To keep the analysis consistent and rigorous, MUK and MEK independently coded the data, and field notes were maintained to capture richer insights.

Data analysis

The methodological approach for this study employed a qualitative descriptive design with semi-structured interviews and thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework [21]. Similar qualitative methodologies have been used in prior physician-focused diabetes research by C. Lynne et al. [22] and H. Alzahra et al. [23]. We replicated the following procedural components from these earlier studies: (1) the qualitative descriptive design with semi-structured interviews, (2) purposive sampling for participant recruitment, and (3) thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s framework. However, modifications were made to tailor the methodology to the specific objectives of the present study: (1) the interview guide was developed specifically to explore endocrinologists’ perspectives on prevalence drivers, barriers, and strategies (Table 1), (2) the inclusion criteria were refined to focus on endocrinologists with a minimum of two years of clinical experience in diabetes care, (3) the study was conducted across three cities (Islamabad, Rawalpindi, and Peshawar) rather than a single site, and (4) the analysis included independent double-coding with adjudication procedures adapted for this study. Interviews were conducted in a combination of Urdu and English, reflecting the natural language preferences of the participants. While endocrinologists in Pakistan are trained in English, many expressed themselves more freely and with greater nuance when using Urdu for culturally embedded concepts. This bilingual approach facilitated richer, more authentic narratives. Two bilingual team members (MUK and MEK) performed forward translation and back-translation of interview materials. Discrepancies were resolved through joint discussion and, when needed, adjudication by a third reviewer (MU) to preserve conceptual equivalence. Interviews were transcribed verbatim, and the entire team reviewed recordings multiple times to achieve data familiarization. We adopted an inductive analytic strategy guided by Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework, allowing themes to emerge directly from participant narratives without imposing pre-existing theoretical constructions. Two independent coders (MUK and MEK) conducted line-by-line open coding on each transcript, generating descriptive codes grounded in participants’ own language. Coding disagreements between the two independent coders (MUK and MEK) were resolved through iterative consensus-building discussions. When discrepancies arose, both coders revisited the original transcripts and engaged in reflexive dialogue to reach agreement. In cases where consensus could not be achieved through discussion, a third reviewer (MU) was consulted as an adjudicator to provide a final decision. This process ensured that the final thematic structure accurately reflected participant narratives while maintaining inter-coder reliability [21]. Two coders (MUK and MEK) independently read each transcript line by line, extracting descriptive codes rooted in what participants said. They grouped these codes into possible subthemes based on recurring patterns. Through ongoing discussion and consensus, they refined and merged the subthemes into broader themes that captured the main patterns across the entire dataset. The final themes are presented in the Results section.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from Hamdard University’s Ethical Review Committee (ERC-FOP-13-03, March 10, 2026). Participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time. The research involved human participants and followed the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality was protected by anonymizing the data (using codes E-01 to E-21). All participants gave informed consent for publication. For this qualitative interview study, we used verbally informed consent, as approved by the ethics committee. Participants agreed that their anonymized responses and relevant background details (including indirect identifiers like those in Table 2) could be published. We assured them of confidentiality and anonymity. No direct personal information (names, specific locations, or contact details) appears in this manuscript.

Epidemic shift and changing demographics

Drastic increase in prevalence

Participants reported a drastic rise in diabetes prevalence in Pakistan over the past 2–10 years, with most participants estimating that rates were now reaching approximately 30% of the population (one in three individuals) or approximately 34.5 million cases. They noted a 50% increase in just 2–3 years, patient volumes more than doubling over a decade, and that every other clinic patient is now diabetic, with a steady day-to-day upward trend and no sign of decline.Current estimates indicate increase in diabetes prevalence with rates now reaching approximately 30% of the population. This indicates that one out of every three individuals in the population are now diabetic. Endocrinologists consistently observed a marked shift toward earlier age of onset for diabetes, now frequently diagnosing type 2 diabetes in patients as young as 17–20 years, including teenagers and children. Urban–rural disparity

Participants highlighted a clear urban-rural disparity: rural populations have lower diabetes prevalence due to physically active lifestyles, but they present with more advanced diseases, poorer medication adherence, lower health literacy, and severely limited access to diagnostics and specialized care. Conversely, urban areas show higher prevalence driven by sedentary jobs and unhealthy diets, yet urban patients demonstrate better disease awareness and treatment consistency. Multifactorial drivers of rising prevalence

Unhealthy dietary habits

Clinicians identified unhealthy dietary habits as a primary driver of rising diabetes prevalence. They documented high consumption of bakery products, processed foods, fast food, refined flour, sugary drinks, packaged juices, and fried snacks. Overeating, large portion sizes, and a deeply ingrained culture of consuming excessive sugar in daily food, sweets, mithai, and sugary desserts were also highlighted as main contributing factors leading toward prevalence of diabetes.Having fast food and unhealthy diets for years, they find it exceedingly difficult to abandon these habits abruptly. Discussion

The following discussion interprets participants’ clinical observations and perceptions in the context of existing literature, recognizing that these represent endocrinologists’ frontline experiences rather than population-level epidemiological measurements.

Pakistan has one of the world’s highest diabetes burdens, with estimates indicating that prevalence is rapidly increasing and the age of onset shifting alarmingly toward younger populations. While the most recent IDF estimates indicate a prevalence of 6.9% [24], participants in our study perceived a much higher current burden, estimating that approximately one in three adults is now affected. This discrepancy may reflect either the rapid evolution of the epidemic beyond official estimates, the clinical reality that endocrinologists see the highest-risk patients, or a combination of both factors. Participants in our study reported a marked rise in prevalence over 2–3 years, patient volumes more than doubling in a decade, and type 2 diabetes now frequently diagnosed in individuals as young as 17–20 years. These trends align with national data showing diabetes increased from 5.2 million in 2000 to 33 million in 2021 [19], representing a six-fold increase in two decades—a trajectory that far exceeds global averages. This rapid escalation is consistent with the International Diabetes Federation’s projections, which identified Pakistan as having one of the fastest-growing diabetic populations worldwide [25].

A clear urban-rural disparity was reported by participants from their clinical experience, which warrants careful interpretation given the urban-centric nature of this study. Urban areas have higher prevalence driven by sedentary jobs and unhealthy diets but better awareness and treatment consistency. Conversely, rural populations present with more advanced diseases, poor adherence, low health literacy, and limited access to diagnostics. This aligns with other studies on Urban-Rural disparity, who reported rural barriers including high costs, poor infrastructure, and restricted female mobility [26] and with Dina et al. (2025), who noted that rural patients face delayed diagnosis and higher complication rates [19]. Early-onset diabetes in Pakistan is attributed to genetic susceptibility, family history, and high sugar consumption, alongside broader South Asian predisposition [27, 28], with rising pediatric cases confirmed [29]. Rural populations have lower prevalence due to active lifestyles but present with advanced disease, poor adherence, low health literacy, and limited access to diagnostics. Urban areas have higher prevalence driven by sedentary jobs and unhealthy diets but better awareness and treatment consistency. This is consistent with other review study that reported rural barriers include high costs, poor infrastructure, restricted female mobility, and fatalistic beliefs [30]. Conversely, Urban areas have better detection, but rural patients face delayed diagnosis and higher complication rates at first presentation [19].

The specialists reported multiple multifactorial drivers based on their clinical observations: Unhealthy dietary habits (excessive sugar, processed foods, large portion sizes), sedentary lifestyles, unprecedented obesity levels (with one participant estimating 52% central obesity), genetic predisposition, and chronic stress with poor sleep. A national nutrition review similarly links refined carbohydrates to diabetes burden [31], and prolonged desk work to normalized inactivity [32]. The PREVENT-CARD Registry confirms high general (71.2%) and central obesity (68.2%) in Pakistan [33]. Genetic studies show inherited susceptibility (insulin deficiency, lipodystrophy pathways) leads to earlier onset and poorer response [34]. Local data also report 82% poor sleep quality among diabetic patients, emphasizing its role in progression [35].

Major barriers to optimal diabetes management reported by endocrinologists include poor treatment adherence and denial of diagnosis, financial constraints limiting access to insulin, low health literacy (with most participants estimating approximately 98% lacking basic knowledge), persistent unhealthy dietary behaviors, inconsistent availability of medications and diagnostics, and deep-rooted cultural misconceptions such as insulin phobia and reliance on traditional remedies. Systemic issues — high patient volumes with only 2–3 min consultation time — further hinder care. National studies report non-adherence rates of 48–59.9%, driven by forgetfulness, unaffordability, and discontinuation after symptom improvement [36, 37]. 42% of patients cite cost as the main obstacle, with average annual diabetes-related expenses of 55,113 PKR [38]. Recent price deregulation increased median insulin prices by 31.87%, disproportionately affecting poor households [39]. Another study reported that across all educational levels, participants reported difficulty adhering to dietary restrictions in social contexts, especially at weddings and communal gatherings where refusing food is culturally unacceptable [40] and misconceptions that diet alone can manage diabetes or that a single blood test rules out the condition [41]. Cultural misconceptions including insulin phobia and preference for traditional remedies are well-documented in South Asian populations [42]. Systemic barriers reflect Pakistan’s doctor-to-patient ratio, which remains below international standards [43, 44].

Strategies identified by participants center on patient education (with several participants estimating this could address up to 80% of disease management), early screening, primordial prevention from childhood, government support for affordable medications, and expanding the pharmacist’s role as a diabetes educator. A Pakistani RCT showed structured diabetes educator-assisted counseling significantly improved HbA1c and medication adherence [45]. The Discovering Diabetes initiative (2024) screened 948,103 individuals and prevented complications in 452,170 cases [46]. Locally produced generic GLP-1/GIP therapies are improving real-world access and outcomes [47]. Pharmacist-led interventions in Lahore community pharmacies significantly improved diabetes knowledge, self-management, and HbA1c [48].

Strength and limitations of study

This study offers rich, frontline qualitative insights from 21 practicing endocrinologists across three Pakistani cities, using rigorous inductive thematic analysis, bilingual translation, independent double coding, and COREQ reporting to enhance trustworthiness. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the findings are limited to endocrinologists’ perspectives and may not reflect views of other healthcare providers (e.g., primary care physicians, diabetologists, or family medicine practitioners) or patients themselves. Second, the numerical estimates reported by participants—such as the perception that one in three adults is diabetic, 52% central obesity, 98% lack of knowledge, and 80% disease management through education—are clinical impressions and participant estimates, not objectively measured epidemiological data. These figures should be interpreted as perceptions that reflect endocrinologists’ frontline experiences, rather than as verified population-level statistics. Third, the study was confined to urban centers (Islamabad, Rawalpindi, and Peshawar), which may not fully represent the perspectives of endocrinologists practicing in remote rural areas or the unique challenges faced in those settings. While the inclusion of endocrinologists who treat patients from rural backgrounds provides some indirect insights, direct data from rural healthcare providers would strengthen the understanding of rural-specific barriers and solutions. Future research should consider including endocrinologists and other healthcare providers from rural regions to capture a more comprehensive national perspective. Fourth, the short interview duration (20–25 min) and possible social desirability bias are also acknowledged, though thematic saturation was achieved and confidentiality was assured. Finally, the qualitative design of this study prioritizes depth and contextual understanding over statistical generalizability; therefore, the findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and informative for policy development rather than definitive prevalence estimates.

Conclusion

This qualitative study provides frontline insights from practicing endocrinologists across Pakistan, who perceived a diabetes epidemic of alarming proportions. Our findings indicate that endocrinologists observed widespread prevalence with a striking shift toward early-onset type 2 diabetes in their clinical practice. Unhealthy dietary habits, sedentary lifestyles, unprecedented obesity levels, genetic predisposition, and modern stressors are the primary drivers. However, the most formidable challenges are deep-rooted barriers including financial constraints, low health literacy, cultural misconceptions, medication shortages, and systemic healthcare limitations. Endocrinologists unanimously identified patient education as the cornerstone of care and highlighted the pharmacist as a crucial yet underutilized diabetes educator. Addressing this crisis requires a multi-level, equity-oriented strategy integrating primordial prevention, public awareness, health system strengthening, and expanded roles for pharmacists, particularly in rural and underserved areas. These findings reflect endocrinologists’ clinical experiences and perceptions, which provide valuable qualitative insights, but should not be interpreted as nationally representative epidemiological estimates.
