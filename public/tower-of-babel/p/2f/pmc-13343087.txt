Understanding the impact of the follow-up period on the association between excess body weight, and cardiovascular events and mortality: a prospective cohort study of the UK Biobank
Authors: Jufen Zhang, Henry Oluwasefunmi Savage, Jason N Dungu, Rudolph Schutte, Barbara Pierscionek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveStudies have examined the association between excess body weight and risk of cardiovascular (CV) outcomes in the general population. However, the impact of the follow-up period of excess body weight on the association between excess weight and CV outcomes is less investigated. We sought to investigate this using the UK Biobank data.Study designPopulation-based prospective cohort study.SettingThe UK.ParticipantsAdults aged between 40 and 69 years were recruited between 2006 and 2010. The study included 469 162 participants; the median (IQR) age was 57 (50–63) years, and 56.3% of the participants were female.ExposuresBody mass index, age, gender, ethnicity, education, smoking status (never, previous and current), alcohol status (never, previous and current), systolic blood pressure, sleep duration, walking pace, overall health rating (excellent, good, fair and poor), dietary patterns (such as red and processed meat consumption, and intake of fish, fruit, poultry and vegetables) and self-reported medical history (such as diabetes, chest pain/discomfort and falls).OutcomesCV outcomes, including CV events such as cerebrovascular disease, ischaemic heart disease, heart failure and CV death.ResultsOf the 469 162 participants without CV disease at baseline, 56.3% were female. The mean age was 57 (SD: 9.6) years. During the median follow-up of 13.7 (IQR: 12.9–14.4) years, 27 305 participants developed a CV outcome, including 6698 who had a CV death. Overall, the strength of association between obesity and CV outcomes increased gradually as the follow-up period increased compared with normal weight. A significant association between obesity and the first CV event was observed after 4 years follow-up period (HR (95% CI) 1.14 (1.02 to 1.26)). The association of obesity with CV death was found after 10 years of follow-up, and there was an increasing strength of the association between 10 years (1.11 (1.01 to 1.22)) and 15 years (1.21 (1.13 to 1.29)) follow-up periods. No association was found between being overweight and CV death at any follow-up. Both overweight and obesity in men were associated with CV death observed before 8 years, while obese women were found to be associated with CV events and CV death after 8 years follow-up period.ConclusionsThis large observation study shows that obesity could lead to CV death as early as 10 years follow-up period, while individuals who were obese were more likely to have their first CV event after 4 years follow-up. No association between being overweight and CV death was observed in any follow-up time. Further studies are needed to evaluate the impact of the follow-up period on associations between overweight/obesity and CV outcomes. Screening or early intervention for obesity can be highly valuable for preventing and managing CV outcomes in a primary care setting.Keywords: Cardiovascular Disease, Death, Obesity, Observational Study, Body Mass Index

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a leading cause of death globally.1 2 Studies have revealed that excess body weight is a contributing factor towards adverse cardiovascular (CV) outcomes,3 4 and in particular, obesity is associated with the risk of developing heart failure (HF), coronary heart disease and death from CV causes (CV death).5

Cohort studies with prospective and retrospective designs are often used to investigate the impact of excess body weight (overweight and obesity), comorbidities and biomarkers on CV outcomes, such as CV events and CV death. In a prospective design, participants are enrolled, the related information is collected and they are followed up for a period during which the CV outcomes are recorded. Subsequently, the association of excess body weight with CV outcomes is statistically analysed based on data collected at baseline during enrolment. In a retrospective design, baseline data and CV outcomes are extracted from electronic databases or medical records based on specific study criteria, and a follow-up period is determined by the available data. The associations are then assessed using statistical modelling. More recently, researchers have begun applying a trajectory approach using trajectories of body mass index (BMI) over time (eg, the course of BMI changes over time) as a measure of excess weight for predicting the risk of CV outcomes.6,8 This provides a more robust assessment to explore the associations. However, many studies still rely on cohort data without body weight measured repeatedly over time; baseline body weight becomes only a measure available in the analysis.

There are a number of factors that could influence the association between excess body weight and CV outcomes. Previous research has basically centred on the selection of potential covariates and confounders and statistical methods to explore the association. Statistical models used to evaluate the association are mainly from models that take into account the events during the follow-up period (eg, logistic regression) and the time to the event (eg, Cox regression). However, the choice of a specific predictive period (prediction of event time from baseline data) is quite often based on the data availability in the study, rather than on a consideration a priori as a part of study design. Baseline excess body weight associated with short-term and long-term risks of CV outcomes has been widely reported,9,11 but the follow-up period used in these studies varies considerably. This possibly explains the heterogenous results of strengths of association between excess body weight and CV outcome.12

There are limited data in the literature regarding the relationship between duration of follow-up period (prediction time to event) from baseline measurement of excess body weight and its impact on the association between excess weight and risk of CV outcomes in the general population. A better understanding of this is important for managing weight efficiently and may be useful to risk stratify patients. Using the UK Biobank data, we aim to evaluate how the follow-up period from baseline affects the relationship between baseline excess body weight and CV outcomes, including CV events, such as cerebrovascular disease, ischaemic heart disease (IHD), HF and CV death. We also investigate the associations in women and men and different age groups.

Methods

Study population

The UK Biobank13 is a large general population-based prospective cohort study conducted from 22 assessment centres across the UK and taken between 2006 and 2010 for over half a million adults aged between 40 and 69 years. The data include detailed information on participant demographics, lifestyle, physical activity and medical history. More details of the UK Biobank procedures and assessments can be found online (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/ukb/). In this study, participants with CV disease, such as angina, myocardial infarction and stroke, were excluded.

Excess body weight

Weights (kg) and heights (m) were measured during the initial visit to the assessment centre. BMI is defined as the body weight divided by the square of the body height and is expressed in units of kg/m2. Excess body weight is defined as a BMI≥25 kg/m2. Obesity and overweight are defined as a BMI≥30 kg/m2 and 25–29 kg/m2, respectively, based on the WHO definitions (https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/obesity-and-overweight).

Cardiovascular disease

CVD at baseline is defined as the participants who reported a previous myocardial infarction, history of angina, stroke or hypertension. The CVD data were collected via touchscreen questionnaire available on the UK Biobank website (http://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/showcase/docs/TouchscreenQuestionsMainFinal.pdf). The questionnaire has been shown to have reasonable validity and reliability.14 Each participant was asked questions, such as ‘Has a doctor ever told you that you have had any of the following conditions?’ A response could be ‘Heart attack’, which was coded as 1; ‘Angina’, coded as 2; ‘Stroke’, coded as 3; ‘High blood pressure’, coded as 4; ‘None of the above’‚ coded as −7 and ‘Prefer not to answer’‚ coded as −3.

Other variables

The other variables (online supplemental table 1) in the study were also collected via touch-screen questionnaire when participants attended the recruitment centre. These variables include age, gender, ethnicity, education, smoking status (never, previous and current), alcohol status (never, previous and current), systolic blood pressure, sleep duration, walking pace, overall health rating (excellent, good, fair and poor), dietary patterns (such as red and processed meat consumption, and intakes of fish, fruit, poultry and vegetables) and self-reported medical history (such as diabetes, chest pain/discomfort and falls). In this study, it was assumed that participants’ dietary habits were stable during the study period.

Outcomes

The main study outcomes were CV outcomes, including CV events, such as cerebrovascular disease, IHD and HF, and CV death during the study follow-up period. The CV events were recorded from hospital admission data. The cause of death was defined according to the 10th edition of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10), and CV death was coded as I00–I99 to correspond to CV diseases. Cerebrovascular disease was coded as I60–I69, HF coded as I50 and IHD coded as I20–I25. The study period for participants was the date of their first attendance at the recruitment centre up until 30 December 2022, the date of death or the date of the first relevant CV event. The date of the outcome was taken from hospital admissions data and from national death registries.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were described by BMI groups of normal weight (BMI≤25 kg/m2), overweight (BMI between 25 and 29 kg/m2), obesity (BMI≥30 kg/m2), CV events (cerebrovascular disease, IHD or HF) and CV death. Continuous variables were expressed as mean with SD or median with IQR and counts (%) for categorical data. Multivariable Cox proportional hazard regression models were used to assess the association between BMI groups and the time to CV death and first CV hospitalisation event. The multivariable models were adjusted for age, sex, BMI, ethnicity, education, systolic blood pressure, sleep duration, walking pace, smoking status, alcohol status, red and processed meat consumption frequencies, poultry intake, fruit intake, fish intake, vegetable intake, diabetes, falls, chest pain/discomfort and overall health to minimise potential confounding bias and increase the precision of estimates. These covariates were determined based on the literature review, team knowledge, clinical relevance and availability in the dataset prior to the exposure and outcome. The results were presented as HRs with 95% CIs.

To explore the possible pattern of the associations between excess body weight and CV outcomes at varying durations of follow-up, we conducted the analyses starting at the first year of follow-up (follow-up time of 1 year) and then repeated this analysis with one year follow-up increments until the maximum follow-up of 15 years from baseline. Subgroup analyses were performed by age groups (age>60 or age≤60 years) and sex. The subgroup analysis was performed at a follow-up less than and equal to the median follow-up of 8 years and greater than 8 years and the total follow-up duration of 15 years.

In this analysis, we excluded variables with a large number of missing values, such as physical activity. BMI was a key variable used in the study, and we excluded participants whose records were missing. For outcome variables, such as CV events and CV deaths, we used the information if available. Median imputation was used to impute the missing values for continuous variables with missing values less than 5%, as they were not normally distributed. The differences between the participants with and without the missing values were similar (online supplemental table 2). Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata 18 statistical software (Stata Corporation, College Station, Texas). The level of significance was set at alpha=0.05 with two tails.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of the 499 264 participants with BMI in the dataset (3107 were excluded due to missing BMI data), 30 102 participants with CV disease at baseline were excluded. The study included 469 162 participants in total.

In this study, the median (IQR) age was 57 (50-63) years, and 56.3% of the participants were female. Compared with normal-weight participants, overweight or obese participants had higher average waist and hip circumferences. Those in the obesity group had a higher proportion of diabetes (10%), a history of chest pain or discomfort (18%) and more than one fall (9%) and were more likely to be previous smokers (38%) and slightly less likely to consume alcohol currently. They had a higher average of red meat and processed meat intake and a higher overall poor health rate. The average fruit intake was higher in participants of normal weight. A greater proportion of participants with normal weight had higher education (40%) reported a brisk walking pace and had an overall excellent/good health rate. The proportion of men was higher in participants in the overweight group (online supplemental table 1).

Discussion

The findings from this cohort study of 469 162 participants in the general population suggest that the follow-up period (predicting time) significantly affects the association between excess body weight and risk of CV events and CV death. We observed that compared with normal weight, the significant associations between obesity and CV death were stronger as the length of follow-up increased after 10 years, and a significant association of obesity with CV events was found after 4 years of follow-up. A consistent association was observed between overweight and CV events after an 8-year follow-up period, and no association was observed between overweight and CV death at any length of follow-up. It appears that there was an association between overweight and CV events in men before or after the 8-year follow-up period, but it was not found in women. Men with obesity had an earlier risk of CV death (before 8 years of follow-up).

Several studies have reported on the association between excess body weight and CV outcomes in the general population at various follow-up periods. One study conducted in two towns in the west of Scotland included 15 402 participants aged 45–64 years with a long-term follow-up of 20 years. They demonstrated that baseline obesity was associated with an increased risk of CV death or CV hospital admission compared with normal weight.14 A systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of 14 prospective studies demonstrated the association between BMI (per 5 kg/m2) and sudden cardiac death with a duration of follow-up greater than 10 years (RR (95% CIs) 1.12 (1.02 to 1.23) for 10–15 years, 1.32 (1.17 to 1.5) for 12–20 years and 1.28 (1.08 to 1.51) for over 20 years).12 The findings of these studies are consistent with the present study, which shows that obese participants had a significantly higher risk of CV death after a 10-year follow-up period. This suggests that participants with obesity are likely to have CV mortality after 10 years of being obese. Long-term obesity could lead to damage to cardiac structure and harm the nature of metabolic function. In addition, obesity triggers immediate low systemic inflammation, which leads to insulin resistance and tissue damage. In the long run, these could lead to life-threatening situations. Furthermore, we observed that participants with obesity had CV events as early as 4 years after baseline measurements. Similar results are reported from a systematic review and meta-analysis study including 28 studies with 647 388 participants, which showed positive associations between BMI (per 5 kg/m2 increment) and HF incidence (HR (95% CIs) 1.32 (1.22 to 1.44) for 5–10 years, 1.33 (1.24 to 1.43) for 10–15 years, 1.46 (1.33 to 1.61) for 15–20 years and 1.7 (1.39 to 2.08) for over 20 years).15 Another study including 7921 employees of the Electricity Generating Authority in Thailand during 1997–2009 also showed that BMI≥23 kg/m2 was associated with CV events mediated indirectly through risk factors of diabetes and hypertension during an average of 17.2 (SD: 5.5) years of follow-up.16 In this study, we did not find a significant association between overweight and CV death, which may indicate unmeasured confounding due to the nature of observational studies. However, although excess adipose tissue causes heart structure change, increased inflammation and metabolic stress, overweight may not be severe enough to cause CV death. A previous study found that being overweight was associated with short-term elevations in the risk of atrial fibrillation in the Women’s Health Study.9 A higher BMI was also found to be significantly associated with a short-term CVD event in the elderly.10

We observed that there was a stronger association between CV outcomes and obesity in men than in women, and it looks like the association was found earlier in men than in women. This may be due to inherent physiological and behavioural sex differences. compared with women, obese men are more prone to gaining deep belly fat, resulting in an accelerated onset of hypertension and dyslipidaemia sooner, which could lead to an earlier CV outcome. In addition, obese men are less likely to have a healthy diet and use primary care services compared with women.17 Recent research findings supported the associations found in women and men in this study.18,21

The association between excess body weight and CV outcomes has been studied extensively. However, few studies have explored the possible effect of duration of follow-up on associations of predictors (such as excess weight) with clinical outcomes. Ljungquist et al21 studied how the identification of potential factors of survival may be dependent on the length of the predictive period by using a logistic regression model with 5 years, 10 years, 15 years and 20 years of follow-ups after baseline. They found that the length of the predictive period was important in identifying determinants of survival. The effect of the duration of follow-up in mortality analysis studied by Meinow et al22 indicated that the longer you study average risk, insignificant results may obscure significant effects during a part of the follow-up. Very recent studies investigated how the follow-up period from baseline affects the association of baseline vitamin D with adverse outcomes. They showed that the follow-up period significantly affects the determination of relative risk for stroke and Major Adverse Cardiovascular Event(MACE) using baseline 25(OH)D concentration.23 24

In the present study, we observed that a higher value HR of obesity compared with normal weight was found at the first year of follow-up for CV outcomes, including CV events and CV death, and HR gradually declined until about 8 years of follow-up (figures 1,2). Notably, most of these HRs were not significant and had wider 95% CI, particularly for CV death. This seems to indicate that the relationship between baseline excess body weight and CV outcome is weakened with increasing periods of follow-up, which might be due to an ‘apparent effect’; the longer the follow-up period, the lower the association. It is, however, noted that the associations between obesity and CV outcomes increased as the length of follow-up after 8 years and were most pronounced in analyses based on long-term follow-up data (figures 1,2). These patterns are consistent with and support the hypothesis that a prolonged period of obesity increases the risk of CV death. Further studies are needed to fully elucidate the causal relationships.

In this study, we mainly focused on the predictive period for studying the associations. It is difficult to reconcile the current study findings with other studies due to possible differences in study populations, medical conditions and the number of variables used in our models. The major strength of the study is the use of a large prospective cohort UK Biobank dataset based on a large middle-aged population with a long follow-up period, which allows us to study the pattern of relationships between excess weight and CV outcomes with varying follow-up durations. In addition, the models were adjusted for a number of lifestyle variables, such as smoking status, alcohol consumption, sleep duration, physical exercise and dietary intake. Furthermore, the findings of our study provide additional insights to the literature about the impact of how follow-up duration of excess body weight affects the associations.

This study analysed observational data from the UK Biobank, which, like any observational study, has certain limitations. First, BMI does not differentiate between lean muscle and fat mass, and the possibility of residual confounding by the typical fluctuations in BMI during follow-up exists. Second, dietary intake variables used in the models were obtained from a questionnaire, and the assumption was made that dietary habits were relatively stable over time, which may be subject to bias. We recognise that dietary habits and body weight can vary, especially in obese participants who may be trying to lose weight as well as in participants who may develop subclinical disease that leads to a reduction in weight (intentionally or due to illness); this could potentially explain the complex temporal patterns. The long study period coincides with improvements in risk factor management and CV therapies, which may modify associations between adiposity and outcomes. Further investigations are needed to evaluate the impact of the follow-up period on associations between overweight/obesity and CV outcomes. Third, competing risks were not addressed due to limited data. In addition, there were potential confounding biases, which can compromise the validity of our conclusions. However, in the multivariable regression analysis the potential confounders, such as age and smoking/alcohol use, were included as covariates in the models, and other biases, such as survivor bias (early events removing high-risk individuals) and weight changes causing misclassification over time. In this analysis, those who had BMI<18.5 were included in normal weight; however, the numbers in this group were small. We studied CV events as the first CV hospitalisation and did not study each component individually due to the limited events. Furthermore, there may be a ‘healthy volunteer bias’, which may limit some external validity as the UK Biobank participants are considered to be healthier, wealthier and less ethnically diverse than the general UK population.

In conclusion, this prospective cohort study suggests the length of follow-up period influences the strength of the association between excess body weight and CV outcomes. The association between obesity and CV events was observed much earlier than the association between obesity and CV death. Regardless, the impact of obesity on CV outcomes is significant, and efforts to normalise body weight in the general population are supported by this study. Screening or early intervention for obesity can be highly valuable for preventing and managing CV outcomes in a primary care setting.
