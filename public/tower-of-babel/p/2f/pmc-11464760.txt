Study on the performance temporal variation of rainwater harvesting systems based on the dynamic change of rainfall pattern and domestic water demands
Authors: Zhonghui Liu, Weilun Chen, Jukui Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRainwater harvesting systems (RWHs) in buildings are benefits in alleviating urban water scarcity. However, climate change and changes in building water demand affect the expected performance of RWHs over their life cycle. Previous studies have only focused on the adaptability of RWHs to climate change without considering the changes in building water demand. To address this issue, this study explores the impact of combined changes in precipitation patterns and domestic water demand on the water-saving efficiency of RWHs over their 25-year life cycle based on historical observations. Three cities in Japan were selected as case studies. The results indicate that during 25-year life cycle of RWHs, precipitation in different cities in Japan shows an increasing trend, while domestic water demand shows a decreasing trend. Such trend resulted in a change of -2.90–4.02% in the water-saving efficiency of the established RWHs and a change of -2.90–3.82% in the theoretical optimal water-saving efficiency, and these effects were greater in RWHs in buildings with high non-potable water demands. Therefore, RWHs in buildings with low non-potable water demand can appropriately reduce the rainwater tanks, while RWHs in buildings with high non-potable water demand must increase the rainwater tank size to eliminate this adverse effect. Furthermore, both reductions in domestic water demand and increases in precipitation have a positive effect on the water-saving efficiency of RWHs. However, when both domestic water demand and precipitation increase or decrease simultaneously, the change in water-saving efficiency of RWHs is related to the non-potable water demand of the building. This study can provide data support and theoretical evidence for the further implementation of RWHs under climate change, and can help stakeholders to optimize and update RWHs to ensure system feasibility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75843-x.Keywords: Rainwater harvesting systems; domestic water demands, Climate changes, Water-saving efficiencySubject terms: Climate change, Climate-change impacts, Sustainability

Introduction

Rainwater harvesting systems (RWHs) are widely used in buildings to replace some of the non-potable water demand due to their simple equipment construction, low installation cost, and wide range of rainwater sources. This is because water used for purposes such as toilet flushing and irrigation in buildings does not require high quality potable water as a supply source1,2. This part of the non-potable water demand using relatively clean rainwater to meet can effectively alleviate the shortage of urban water resources. RWHs have achieved remarkable water-saving efficiency and economic benefits in developing countries3–5. In recent years, RWHs have been proven to have significant advantages in energy saving and CO2 emission reduction6–8, restoring urban ecology9,10, and controlling stormwater runoff at the source11,12, and have become an important implementation tool for urban low-impact development (LID) and sponge city construction. However, unlike stable reclaimed water reuse, the water-saving efficiency of RWHs is influenced by multiple factors such as climate conditions13,14, the catchment area of the building15, and the scale of water demand16. Ignoring these influencing factors and making incorrect assessments, designs and use of RWHs can affect their environmental and economic benefits, thereby affecting their feasibility17. Therefore, it is essential to study the influence of these factors on the water-saving efficiency trend of RWHs.

Previous studies have generally reported the mechanisms by which RWHs are influenced by climatic conditions. Rahman, Snook18 investigated the water-saving efficiency of 159 RWHs in the Greater Sydney region of Australia. It was found that due to significant differences in precipitation characteristics across the Greater Sydney region, the range of variation in water-saving efficiency of RWHs were from 31 to 93%. Ali, Zhang19 compared the feasibility of RWHs in five climatic zones in Pakistan and concluded that properly designed RWHs can be economically viable in humid subtropical, warm semiarid, and Mediterranean continental climates, while in cold semiarid and warm desert regions, the investment cost ratio of using RWHs is less than 1.0. Chapa, Krauss20 during the development of a generic design model for RWHs, it was found that due to differences in hydro-logical features in different regions, four sets of design curves need to be developed to achieve a universal design for RWHs in Ecuador. In recent years, many scholars have turned their attention to the impact of future climate change on the water-saving efficiency of RWHs, because global warming will bring about changes in precipitation characteristics that can have a serious impact on the water-saving efficiency of RWHs already in use over their next 10–20 years of life cycle. Zhang, Zhang21 found that the future water-saving efficiency of RWHs in Urumqi, China will increase by 23.1–44.9%, while the future water-saving efficiency of RWHs in Chengdu and Beijing will decrease by 1.1-24% due to the increase and decrease of future precipitation variability. This situation has also been confirmed by Alamdari, Sample22, the author concluded that water supply reliability for different RWHs in the U.S. ranges from − 17.9% to + 22% due to different climate change trends in different regions in the future from 2041 to 2068. Thus, changes in precipitation patterns due to climate change have made it impossible to continue using RWHs in some areas where they have proven to be viable23.

The impact of the water demand on the water-saving efficiency of RWHs is equally important. Shadmehri Toosi, Danesh13 believed that the selection of the optimal rainwater tank for RWHs depends more on the level of water demand than on the precipitation. However, previous studies on the influence of water demand on the water-saving efficiency of RWHs only focused on exploring the impact of different water demand scales on the feasibility of RWHs. Lani, Syafiuddin24 argued that in Malaysia, the water-saving efficiency of RWHs in large commercial buildings with greater water demand is only 7% higher than in small commercial buildings, but the economic feasibility of the former is much higher than the latter. However, Campos Cardoso, Cavalcante Blanco3 came to the opposite conclusion, they concluded that in Brazil, the use of RWHs in buildings with larger rainwater catchment areas and water demand is not economically feasible, and that only public buildings with low demand and with climatic conditions favorable to rainwater harvesting and with small coverage areas have a better potential for water conservation. However, these authors’ research conclusions are limited to studying the feasibility of RWHs by selecting cases with different levels of water demand, ignoring changes in precipitation caused by climate change, and the fact that water demand in buildings may also change over their life cycle with the use of RWHs. Chen, Gao25 discovered that changes in water demand may affect the water-saving efficiency of RWHs, therefore, the study explored the water-saving efficiency of RWHs in buildings with seasonal and non-seasonal water usage patterns. The author concluded that when the water demand pattern matches the precipitation pattern, such as both are single-peak precipitation and the peak times are similar, using RWHs can achieve the same water-saving efficiency. However, the author was also limited to exploring only the seasonal variation of water demand within one year and ignored the inter-annual variation of water demand. When a city or region begins to popularize RWHs, the use of RWHs will lead to an increase in the level of water demand by residents due to the availability of more water. However, the promotion of urban water-saving policies is not only about promoting RWHs alone but also includes multiple measures such as rainwater harvesting, wastewater reuse, water-saving appliances and other aspects. In particular, the promotion of water-saving appliances, unlike RWHs and reclaimed water reuse, which are indirect substitutes for non-potable water demand, can directly reduce the building potable water demand and non-potable water demand. Therefore, the long-term promotion of water-saving appliances, accompanied by measures such as rising awareness of water conservation among residents, can lead to a direct decline in water demand over the life cycle of using RWHs, thus affecting the water-saving efficiency of RWHs. However, few studies explored the effect of changes in water demand, or even simultaneous changes in precipitation and water demand, on the water-saving efficiency of RWHs.

To dress this issue, this study aims to explore the spatial and temporal trends of RWH performance during its life cycles under the combined impacts of precipitation and building water demand changes to enhance the robustness and adaptability of RWHs in response to dynamic changes in the objective environment and to ensure that such systems can achieve expected performance during life cycles, which helps stakeholders to implement RWHs with more reliability and propose upgrading solutions. Three cities in Japan, Akita, Tokyo, and Fukuoka, were selected as the study subjects, analyzed the precipitation variation in different cities from 1980 to 2018, explored the trend of water demand in different cities from 1995 to 2019, and the water balance model was used as a simulation tool. The 1980–2018 precipitation data and 1995–2019 water demand data were split into different 15-year daily-resolution precipitation data and 1-year daily-resolution building water demand data as inputs to water balance model to explore the trend of water-saving efficiency of RWHs over their 25-year life cycle under the synergistic changes of rainfall and water demand. The simulation process covers buildings with low, medium, and high non-potable water demand levels for identifying the combined impact trends of precipitation and water demand on the water-saving efficiency of RWHs. This study can provide data support for the design and optimization of RWHs throughout their life cycle, as well as theoretical guidance for future urban rainwater harvesting and utilization policies.

Materials and methods

Description of RWHs

A typical RWH is shown in Fig. 1. RWHs are mainly composed of capture roof, first flushing device or treatment device and rainwater tank. Rainwater is collected through the capture roof and then processed through a first flushing device or treatment device to remove large suspended particles and pollutants from the rainwater, which is finally stored in a rainwater tank for non-potable reuse. The first flushing device can significantly improve the water quality of collected rainwater by discharging 1–2 mm initial high-concentration precipitation runoff into sewers26. However, this approach reduces the available of precipitation, thereby decreasing the water-saving efficiency of RWHs. One alternative solution is to install a treatment device before or after the rainwater tank, usually a floating type filter, which uses fillers of different densities to absorb and purify impurities in the rainwater, without sacrificing a portion of the precipitation in order to achieve full utilization of rainwater27. In this study, the treatment device was selected as the rainwater treatment unit for RWHs, considering that climate change may have an impact on the initial precipitation runoff volume discarded by the first flushing device. Excess rainwater overflows from the rainwater tank to the lower drains. When available rainwater is insufficient to meet the non-potable water demand of a building, a separate line from the main water plant is connected directly to the rainwater tank to supplement the RWH with potable water.

Study area

This study chooses Japan as a case study. Japan is a country with abundant precipitation, with an annual average precipitation of around 1800 mm. The precipitation pattern throughout the year shows a unimodal tendency, with the peak precipitation concentrated from June to September40. According to the Kӧppen-Gieger classification map as shown in Fig. 2, Japan consists of four main climatic zones with warm and humid climatic conditions.

The Kӧppen-Gieger classification of Japan.

However, due to uneven population distribution, uneven terrain, and many short and fast-flowing rivers in Japan, the per capita water storage resources are only 3400 m³/year, which is less than half of the world average, making it a country with a serious water shortage. In particular, the Kanto coastal region, Kitakyushu region and Tokai region are facing serious water shortages41. Since 1980, Japan has vigorously implemented policies such as RWHs, reclaimed water reuse, and water-saving appliances, which have significantly alleviated the phenomenon of water scarcity in Japanese cities. Therefore, this study selected Tokyo, Fukuoka, and Akita as representative cities of the Kanto coastal region, the Kitakyushu region, and the Tokai region, respectively, to analyze the impact of changes in precipitation and water demand on the water-saving efficiency of RWHs. The geographical locations of the three cities are indicated in Fig. 2, and detailed information on the cities is presented in Table 1.

Data sources

The per capita water demand of three cities in the research area from 1995 to 2019 was selected for input into the YAS algorithm to analyze the impact of changes in demand on the water-saving efficiency of RWHs41.

Geraldi and Ghisi42 and Zhang, Jing36 concluded that using 10- to15-year daily steps of precipitation series for water balance models can obtained results with similar accuracy as using a 30-year hourly precipitation series. This study selected a 15-year daily precipitation series as the precipitation parameter input for YAS for simulating the water-saving efficiency of RWHs in three cities from 1995 to 2019 over a total of 25 years. Therefore, the daily precipitation data for the three cities from 1980 to 2018 were obtained from the Japan Meteorological Agency40. The annual precipitation in Tokyo, Fukuoka and Akita were 1547.372 mm, 1686.000 mm and 1722.731 mm, respectively.

The data from 1980 to 2018 were split into 25 sets of 15-year precipitation series from 1980 to 1994, 1981–1995, 1982–1996 …… and 2004–2018, and marked them as the first group, second group, third group, …… twenty-fifth group respectively, used to simulate the water-saving efficiency of RWHs under different water demand scenarios in three cities from 1995 to 2019. For example, the first group of precipitation series, 1980–1994, was used to simulate the water-saving efficiency of RWHs in three cities in 1995, and the second group of precipitation series, 1981–1995, was used to simulate the water-saving efficiency of RWHs in three cities in 1996.

Chen, Gao25found that seasonal variations in non-potable water demand have little effect on the performance of RWHs. Therefore, we chose the per capita water demand in Fukuoka, Tokyo, and Akita as the non-potable water demand input for 1995–2019. Meanwhile, we assume that this per capita water demand remains constant over the year. The per capita water demand in these three cities were obtained from the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism41. In each of these three cities in Japan, a typical residential building with a family of three was selected as a simulation case, which has a roof catchment area of 100 square meters. And assume that the non-potable water demand accounts for 20% (low demand), 30% (medium demand), 40% (high demand) and 50% (high demand) of the total water demand of residential buildings33,43.

Therefore, the aim of this study is to analyze the dynamic trends of the water-saving efficiency of RWHs over a 25-year life cycle from 1995 to 2019 under the joint variation of precipitation and domestic water demand, when the building non-potable water demand is 20-50%, respectively (Fig. 3). The water-saving efficiency of RWHs for each year was determined with domestic water demand data for that year and precipitation data for the previous 15 years. For example, the water-saving efficiency of RWHs in 1995 was modelled from 1995 domestic water demand data and 150-year precipitation data from 1980 to 1994. The quantification process of changing trends in precipitation, domestic water demand, and water-saving efficiency of RWHs please refers to Sect. 2.5–2.7.

Results

Trends analysis of precipitation patterns

Figure 4 illustrates the changing trends in the statistical characteristics of the 25 sets of 15-year daily-resolved precipitation data used to assess the water-saving efficiency of the RWHs in each year during 25-year life cycles in Akita, Tokyo, and Fukuoka. As can be seen from Fig. 4, the changing trend of annual precipitation in the first 13 sets of 15-year precipitation data, with the starting years from 1980 to 1992, in Akita was relatively smooth, decreasing slightly from 1688.63 mm to 1632.07 mm. Subsequently, the annual precipitation of the last 12 sets of 15-year precipitation data, with starting years from 1993 to 2004, in Akita showed a significant upward trend, rising to 1759.23 mm. Inversely, the changing trend of annual precipitation from the 15-year precipitation data with the starting years of 1980 to that with the starting years of 1982 in Fukuoka showed a significant downward trend, ranging from 1690.83 mm to 1598.60 mm. This is due to the significant extreme rainfall event that occurred in Fukuoka in 1980, with 2976.50 mm of precipitation throughout a year. Subsequently, the annual precipitation of 15-year precipitation data showed a steady then upward trend, with the increasing to 1702.60 mm in 2004–2018 precipitation data. The annual precipitation of 15-year precipitation data in Tokyo showed a significant upward trend, rising significantly from 1502.07 mm to 1635.40 mm.

The influence of precipitation and water demand on water-saving efficiency

From Figs. 4 and 7, it can be seen that the changes of precipitation and per captia domestic water demand of all three case cities under the 25-year life cycles of RWHs show the same trend. Therefore, this section only explores the effect of relative changes in 15-year precipitation data and domestic water demand data on the water-saving efficiency of RWHs in buildings with 20-50% non-potable water demands, whereas urban differences in such effects will not be discussed.

Figure 9 shows the changes in the water-saving efficiency under the optimal rainwater tank of established RWHs over the next 25-year life cycle. And for buildings with non-potable water demand, the changes in precipitation and domestic water demand had significant impacts on the water-saving performance of RWHs. For example, in the three cities, the relative changes in 15-year precipitation data and domestic water demand ranged from − 5.45 to 3.43% and from − 4.43 to 3.29%, respectively, while the relative variation in the water-saving efficiency under the same tank size of RWHs in buildings with 20%, 30%, 40%, and 50% non-potable water demand ranged from − 0.61 to 0.97%, from − 1.51 to 2.63%, from − 2.19 to 3.34%, and from − 2.90 to 4.02%, respectively.

Figure 10 shows the changes in the optimal water-saving efficiency of established RWHs over 25-year life cycle. Overall, the change trends in the optimal water-saving efficiency of RWHs are similar to Fig. 9, with a small impact on RWHs in buildings with low non-potable water demand levels (20%) and a large impact on RWHs in buildings with high non-potable water demand levels (50%), ranging from − 1.82 to 1.05%, -1.34–4.04%, -2.19–4.30%, and − 2.90–3.82%, respectively. Additionally, in buildings with low non-potable water demand and medium non-potable water demand, RWHs will need to downsize the rainwater tanks in order to re-achieve optimal water-saving efficiency. Conversely, in buildings with high non-potable water demand, RWHs will have to enlarge the rainwater tank to regain optimal water-saving efficiency. In Tokyo, for example, for buildings with 20% non-potable water demand, the rainwater tank size of RWHs should be reduced from 12 m3 to 7 m3 in order to cope with the combined changes in precipitation and domestic water demand; for buildings with 30% non-potable water demand, the rainwater tank size of RWHs should be reduced from 13 m3 to 11 m3; for buildings with 40% and 50% non-potable water demand, the rainwater tank size of RWHs should be expanded from 11 m3 to 14 m3 and from 10 m3 to 13 m3, respectively, in order to re-achieve the optimum water-saving efficiency.

Compared Fig. 9 with Fig. 10 the water-saving efficiency of RWHs tends to increase linearly with the rise of precipitation variation, and decreases linearly with the rise of domestic water demand. For the optimal water-saving efficiency of RWHs, the optimal water-saving efficiency of RWHs showed a significant downward trend when the domestic water demand in buildings showed an upward trend change and the precipitation pattern showed a downward trend change; when the domestic water demand in buildings showed a downward trend change and the precipitation pattern showed an upward trend change, the optimal water-saving efficiency of RWHs showed a significant upward trend. However, when the domestic water demand in buildings and precipitation pattern together show an increasing trend, the optimal water-saving efficiency of RWHs in buildings with different non-potable water demands shows a slight increasing trend. When the domestic water demand in buildings and precipitation pattern together show a decreasing trend, the optimal water-saving efficiency of RWHs in 30% non-potable water demand buildings shows a decreasing trend, while the optimal water-saving efficiency of RWHs for 40% and 50% non-potable water demand buildings shows a significant increasing trend. However, the effect of synergistic reduction in precipitation pattern and domestic water demand on the changing trend in water-saving efficiency of RWH under the optimal rainwater tank size in 30% non-potable demand buildings is positive.

Discussion

The aim of this study is to investigate the changing trends in the impact of combined changes in precipitation patterns and domestic water demand on the water-saving efficiency of RWHs over their life cycle. Zhang, Zhang21 noted that due to the intensification of global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, the average precipitation in the last 20 years has undergone different levels of increase and decrease compared to the historical periods. Such changes can significantly affect the water-saving efficiency of RWHs in the context of climate change39,45. In order to avoid differences in evaluation results caused by differences in statistical characteristics between one-year rainfall time series46, this study quantifies the trend of the impact of changes in precipitation patterns on the simulation results of RWHs by quantifying the temporal trends in the statistical characteristics of the 15-year historical precipitation data for different starting years. It is shown that in the southern, central, and northern regions of Japan, which are Fukuoka, Tokyo, and Akita, respectively, the recent annual precipitation all show a significant upward trend due to climate change. The result is consistent with the study of Zhang, Zhang14. The authors found that the annual precipitation in different regions of China showed increasing trends in different degrees from 2020 to 2050. However, this obtained result is different from the finding of Alamdari, Sample22. They concluded that the annual precipitation in the central and southern regions of the U.S. showed different increasing and decreasing trends from 2041 to 2068. This is attributed to the different time scales of the study. Climate change patterns due to different GHG emission scenarios are expected to have a greater impact on existing rainfall patterns on longer time scales, such as 50 years and 100 years47,48. However, the values of long-term forecasts of future precipitation indicate that the frequency of precipitation is decreasing, although the change in future precipitation is on an upward trend, which includes drought events and continuous precipitation duration in advance of precipitation, and the precipitation intensity of a single precipitation event is on an upward trend49. This will result in variations in water-saving efficiency and reliability of RWHs during different seasons50. Therefore, it cannot be assumed that an increasing trend in available rainfall will have a positive impact on the water-saving efficiency of RWHs simply by the increasing trend of annual precipitation. The changing trend in the water-saving efficiency of RWHs affected by precipitation patterns will be determined from the water balance process by means of long-term continuous simulation.

Domestic water demand is another one of the key factors affecting the water-saving efficiency of RWHs51. Changes in domestic water demand are more difficult to predict than changes in precipitation caused by multiple interacting environmental physical, chemical, and biological processes14. In this study, by simulating the changing trend of per captia daily domestic water demand over the 25-year life cycle of RWHs implemented in 1995 in Japan, we found that even in regions with severe water scarcity, domestic water demand is on a decreasing trend with the gradual spread of the implementation of RWHs. This finding is inconsistent with the finding of Kisakye and Van der Bruggen52 They suggest that when RWHs are installed in buildings, domestic water demand behavior becomes less frugal as the available water resources shift from a single source of potable water provided by the main water plants to portable water provided by main water plants and non-potable water provided by rainwater that is captured from buildings, which will result in an increasing trend of domestic water demands. However, our findings indicate that as urban water conservation policies continue to spread and advance, more and more water-saving devices are installed, symbolizing the increasing awareness of water conservation among inhabitants, which ultimately leads to a decrease in domestic water demand and total urban water consumption53. However, this decreasing trend affects the water-saving efficiency of RWHs in their life cycles, which ultimately affects the economic benefits of such systems. Furthermore, for main water plants, lower domestic water demands mean lower water revenue, which creates a financial impact on the main water plants to balance the potable water treatment costs and maintain operating expenses. Therefore, the popularity of RWHs and the implementation of water conservation policies form a cooperative game with the changing behavior of domestic water demand54. Future research should focus on the equilibrium of this game relationship to develop and implement optimal water conservation strategies in cities.

Both changes in precipitation and domestic water demand are important for the water-saving efficiency of RWHs. The increase or decrease in precipitation and domestic water demand resulted in four changes in the water-saving efficiency of RWHs. Firstly, based on the previously discussed, the scenario of precipitation rises and domestic water demand decrease results in an increasing trend in the water-saving efficiency of RWHs. This scenario represents the future implementation of RWHs. Climate change will lead to an increasing trend in precipitation in regions such as warm monsoon climate areas and when urban water conservation policies in these regions grows a certain scale, the RWHs and water-saving devices will be widely used and awareness of water-saving will increase, which will eventually lead to a decrease in domestic water demand to increase the water-saving efficiency of RWHs19,52. Conversely, when precipitation decreases and water demand rises, it represents an region of severe water shortage such as Brazil, where with the newly popularized water-saving policies, water-saving measures such as RWHs can provide non-potable and potable water to residential buildings so that they have more water available, for example, during the dry season when residents have more water for toilet flushing and irrigation. Therefore, in this scenario, there is a significant upward trend in water consumption and water demand of buildings in the short term51. However, these arid regions may be negatively impacted by climate change21. Therefore, the water-saving efficiency of RWHs in such regions may show a decreasing trend over their life cycle. Furthermore, the simultaneous rise in precipitation and water demand represents a scenario where RWHs are being promoted in regions where precipitation is expected to increase in the future due to climate change and where water-saving measures are just becoming widespread. The water-saving efficiency of RWHs in these regions will tend to increase as water-saving policies begin to spread. Interestingly, in regions where both precipitation and domestic water demand decrease, the water-saving efficiency of RWHs in buildings with low non-potable demand shows a decreasing trend, while the water-saving efficiency of RWHs in buildings with high non-potable demand shows an increasing trend. This can be attributed to the fact that small-sized RWHs are more sensitive to the negative impact of precipitation changes, and this sensitivity is stronger than the positive impact on domestic water demand changes. This is because the small-sized RWHs have smaller tanks and are limited in the amount of rainwater they can harvested during rainfall events. More frequent short and intense precipitation caused by climate change results in small-sized RWHs to discharge large amounts of stormwater overflow during rainfall events. On the contrary, large-sized RWHs are more robust to changes in precipitation frequency and therefore better able to cope with the negative impacts of changing precipitation patterns on the water-saving efficiency of RWHs under climate change.

Since different regional precipitation patterns can significantly affect the results of continuous simulations for the water-saving efficiency of RWHs, variations in precipitation and domestic water demands may have different effects on the water-saving efficiency of RWHs in buildings in regions with different rainfall patterns55. However, the changing trends of water-saving efficiency of RWHs are similar in regions with the same precipitation pattern. Therefore, the results obtained in this study can be used not only in the Japanese cities but also in regions with the same type of precipitation patterns. In regions with other precipitation patterns, the obtained results can be further optimized and improved by adding new local cases through the proposed methodology for enhancing the generalizability of the conclusions and the reliability of RWHs in different regions.

The limitation of this study is that because we do not expect to introduce the impacts of extraneous factors such as differences in GHG emission conditions and uncertainty in future domestic water demand conditions on the final results, the historical precipitation data and historical domestic water demand data are selected to analyze the changing trends in the water-saving efficiency of RWHs instead of using a common methodology used to explore the performance of RWHs under climate change, which using the predicted precipitation data provided by global climate model for analysis. Future studies should focus on different GHG emission scenarios and the dynamic projections of domestic water demands to comprehensively assess the joint impact of changing trends in precipitation and domestic water demand on the water-saving efficiency of RWHs.

Conclusion

Climate change-induced variability in precipitation and uncertainty in domestic water demand prevented RWHs from achieving their expected performance over their life cycle, affecting the feasibility of such systems. To address this issue, this study explores the changing trends in the water-saving efficiency of RWHs over their 25-year life cycle that affected by changes in precipitation and domestic water demand, thereby providing data foundation and theoretical support for improving the robustness of RWHs to dynamic changes in complex environments. Historical precipitation data from 1980 to 2018 and historical domestic water demand data from 1995 to 2019 in three different cities of Japan were selected for continuous simulation of RWHs in buildings with different non-potable water demands in order to quantify the characteristics of changes in the water-saving efficiency of such systems over a 25-year life cycle. The main performance conclusions are as follows:

Over the 25-year life cycle of RWH operation in these Japanese regions, precipitation patterns in Japan show a significant upward trend and domestic water demand shows a significant downward trend with the impact of climate change and the gradual implementation of water conservation policies. Under this changing trend, the water-saving efficiency of the established RWHs under the optimal tank varies from − 2.90 to 4.02%. The water-saving efficiency of RWHs in buildings with low non-potable water demand and with small rainwater tanks is more influenced by the synergy of precipitation and domestic water demand, while the water-saving efficiency of RWHs in buildings with high non-potable water demand and with larger rainwater tanks is less influenced by the synergy of precipitation and domestic water demand. Furthermore, the impact of changes in precipitation and domestic water demand on the theoretical optimal water-saving efficiency of RWHs ranged from − 2.90 to 3.82%. As a result, RWHs in 20% and 30% non-potable water demand buildings must be downsized in the rainwater tanks and in 40% and 50% non-potable water demand buildings must be upgraded to bring the RWHs back to their theoretical optimal water saving efficiency.

On the other hand, in terms of the relationship between the water-saving efficiency of RWHs and the dynamics of precipitation and domestic water demand, both a reduction in domestic water demand and an increase in precipitation positively affect the water-saving efficiency of RWHs and vice versa. However, the optimal water-saving efficiency of RWHs showed an increasing trend when both domestic water demand and precipitation increased, whereas the optimal water-saving efficiency of RWHs showed an increasing trend only in buildings with high non-potable water demand when both domestic water demand and precipitation decreased.

Future studies should further explore the adaptive capacity of RWHs to cope with complex changing environments based on different GHG emission scenarios, dynamic projections of domestic water demand in buildings, and the feasibility of RWHs in regions with different precipitation patterns.
