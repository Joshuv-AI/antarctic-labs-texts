Microbiomes of 2024’s Periodical Cicada Brood XIII Vary By Species and Location
Authors: Annalee Price, Swe Yein Aung Mog, Jean Dubach, Craig Billington, Peter Larsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe 17-year Periodical cicadas (Magicicada spp.) are long-lived insects that emerge in mass synchronized events after 17 years underground. Their survival and ecological success depend heavily on their microbiomes, which include obligate bacterial symbionts essential for nutrient acquisition, as well as occasional pathogens such as the behavior-altering fungus Massospora cicadina. While the periodical cicada lifecycle is well studied, little is known about how cicada microbiomes vary across species and environments during a single emergence event. During the 2024 emergence of Brood XIII in northern Illinois, 17-year cicadas were sampled from four ecologically distinct forest preserves. Cicadas were identified by species and sex; their microbiomes were assayed using 16S rRNA gene sequencing and tested for the presence of the fungal pathogen M. cicadina DNA in asymptomatic individuals. Sampling sites were characterized by plant community composition, historical disturbance, and potential presence of the antifungal compound juglone. Microbiome composition differed significantly by cicada species and site, but not by sex. The obligate symbionts Hodgkinia cicadicola and Sulcia muelleri dominated microbiome profiles, though other bacteria—including Pantoea agglomerans, a potential pheromone producer—were variably abundant. Cicada species distributions were non-random across sites and correlated with local plant diversity. M. cicadina DNA was detected in 23% of otherwise asymptomatic cicadas, with infection rates varying by location and negatively correlated with microbiome diversity. This study highlights complex interactions between cicada species, their microbial communities, and environmental variables such as plant diversity, soil chemistry, and land use history. Background

Seventeen-year periodical cicadas (Magicicada septendecim) are large flying insects with striking patterns, wings like stained glass windows, distinctive loud droning songs in late summer, and long lifecycles that result in a sudden emergence of millions of individuals into their environment [1]. Cicadas also are creatures of multiple superlatives. When periodical cicadas emerge en masse to molt at long last from the spring soil after spending 17 years below ground [2, 3], they are the longest-lived insect known. For their brief, above-ground lives, the huge swarm of newly emerged insects can become one of an ecosystem’s dominant forms of animal biomass [4]. Their mating song makes them among the loudest creatures on the planet, each cicada singing at a volume roughly equivalent to a roaring chainsaw [5]. After mating, periodical cicadas lay their eggs on the undersides of tree leaves and the newly hatched grubs fall to the ground where they burrow into the soil, beginning the 17-year cycle anew, feeding on tree roots and engaging in their final, highest-ranked biological trait: cicadas are planet earth’s champion urinators, utilizing a velocity jet system to eject the water as they sift out nutrients from a thin soup of tree root phloem [6]. This extraordinary life cycle is the periodical cicada’s survival strategy as their massive co-emergence outpaces any predator’s capacity to consume them [7]. Their long lifecycle, with an underground sleep of a prime-numbered period of years, makes it difficult for potential predators to synchronize with the periodical cicada’s emergence and specialize in or anticipate the infrequent bounty of swarms of big, slow-moving insects [8].

Microbiomes play a crucial role in many insects and their relationships with their environment. Insect microbiomes can enable them to utilize resources they would not otherwise be able to exploit, enhance their resistance to pesticides, or evade pathogens [9]. Their microbiome communities can be directly inherited, acquired through interaction with their environment, or stochastically shaped by a sequence of random events and encounters [10]. Species identity and specific environment can shape an insect’s microbiome, possibly modulating its fitness for a given context [11].

A significant component of periodical cicada biology that makes the cicada’s extraordinary lifecycle possible is the cicada’s microbiomes [9, 12, 13]. Periodical cicadas harbor two highly specialized, obligate symbiotic bacteria species, Candidatus Hodgkinia cicadicola (H. cicadicola) and Candidatus Sulcia muelleri (S. muelleri), that enable them to convert their diet of dilute sugar water in plant root phloem into the foodstuffs necessary for survival. To greatly simplify the complex dynamics between host and symbiote metabolomes, the bacterium H. cicadicola is adept at synthesizing vitamins, while S. muelleri possesses the necessary metabolism for producing many essential amino acids [14, 15]. Periodical cicadas have specialized fatty body organs for harboring these requisite symbionts and to ensure that the next generation of cicadas has been adequately inoculated with the bacterial partners needed to survive their long subterranean sleep [16]. The periodical cicada’s microbiome also is where the only organism that has evolved to specialize in taking advantage of the cicada’s unique lifecycle prowls. The fungal pathogen, Massospora cicadina, exclusively infects periodical cicada, lying in wait for years as patient spores among the soil until its singular target emerges once again [17, 18]. Once this fungus infects a host, the fungus consumes the cicada’s hindquarters and the fungus manipulates its host’s extended phenotype by exuding the amphetamine cathinone and the psychedelic psilocybin, altering the host’s mating behaviors in a way that helps spread the infection and cast fungal spores about the site of the next generation’s emergence, even as the cicada’s abdomen is being consumed by mycelia [19].

The 13- and 17-year periodical cicadas are grouped into Broods, in which a brood is defined by the geographic region and the calendar year that the cicadas are expected to emerge. Each brood may be composed of multiple cicada species that are synchronized to co-emerge. Brood XII occupies northern Illinois in the United States, as well as parts of Indiana, Iowa, Wisconsin, and Michigan [8, 20]. These mass emergences allow for the unique opportunity to examine the dynamics between the ecosystem and the local community on a microbial level [21]. Additionally, the extended residence of a brood in a specific geographical location provides an opportunity to study host-microbe interactions, given the nature of microbes to adapt to their surroundings; common effects of the environment on microbial variation are the reduction or increase of phenotype variability [22, 23]. In this study, advantage was taken of the 2024 emergence of 17-year cicada Brood XIII in northern Illinois to sample cicadas from multiple, environmentally distinct sites, assay their microbiomes by 16S rRNA, and test for the presence of M. cicadina DNA. Three species of 17-year periodical cicada in Brood XIII emerged simultaneously: Magicicada cassini, M. decim, and M. decula (hereafter referred to as cassini, decim, and decula, respectively). Four study sites were selected in west suburban Chicago, each site having a unique ecological history, level of historical disturbance, and distribution and diversity of tree species. Our goal was to determine the effects of cicada species, sex, location history, area tree diversity, and infection with M. cicadina on cicada microbiome community compositions. Both species and location were discovered to affect the microbiome community and the frequency at which M. cicadina DNA is found in otherwise outwardly asymptomatic individuals. A network of biological interactions links the macro-scale environmental conditions to the micro-scale of bacterial communities via variations in cicada species.

Materials and Methods

Collection Locations

Four locations in the area west of the City of Chicago were selected for sampling (Fig. 1). These nature preserves, managed by the Forest Preserves of Cook County (FPCC), have well-characterized plant species distributions and have historical site information available from Public Land Survey data [24]. Since cicadas generally do not travel more than half a mile from their place of emergence [25], sites are unlikely to contain individual cicadas that emerged from one of the other selected study sites. All sites are managed by the FPCC but not necessarily under active management at the site of collection. While the sites may contain a variety of environmental types, the following descriptions apply to the specific region from which cicadas were collected.

Selected study sites with Brood XIII 17-year cicada emergence. Four forest preserves, managed by the Forest Preserves of Cook County, were selected in the western suburbs of Chicago

Brookfield Woods

Brookfield Woods is the least disturbed site of those sampled. Brookfield Woods was prairie in a 1938 aerial image survey with widely spaced trees, but likely is transitioning to woodland via savanna. The location currently is described as a savanna.

Miller Meadow

Miller Meadow is the most highly disturbed site of those considered. Aerial images from 1938 indicate that the area was an agricultural field. The site was under construction from the mid-1990s until 2006 for a Metropolitan Water Reclamation District Tunnel and Reservoir Plan (MWRD TARP) project. The soil at this site was replaced, and it was revegetated in 2007. The area currently is described as Eurasian meadow transitioning to wet-mesic prairie.

National Grove

National Grove is a location that transitioned between prairie and timber. This preserve was fully acquired by the FPCC in 1925. Prior to becoming a part of the Forest Preserves, it had been a recreation park with concrete dance pavilions and other features. The area is currently described as woodland and unassociated woody growth.

Westchester Woods

This site was prairie according to 1938 aerial images and was used as a goat farm prior to the FPCC’s acquisition and for some period afterward. The area currently is described as woodland and unassociated woody growth.

Plant Community Sampling

Leaves from the trees where cicadas were sampled were collected. Tree species present at cicada sample sites were identified by their collected leaves [26], and the tree species identification was verified against lists of tree species known to be present in the region of the collection sites. Alpha diversity for collection sites was calculated as H’ using the Shannon-Weiner Index [27].

Cicada Sampling Methods

On June 10, 2024, 15 cicadas were collected at each site, disregarding any individuals that showed outward signs of M. cicadina infection, for a total of 60 potential samples. All samples were collected using nitrile gloves; cicadas were euthanized in a 50-ml collection tube containing an ethanol-soaked tissue.

Upon collection, the cicadas were identified by their sex and species subgroup. Female cicadas have a pointed ovipositor at the bottom of their abdomen, while male cicadas have a blunt abdomen [17]. For the identification of cicada species, decula have a pattern of narrow orange stripes on their abdomens and decim have broad orange stripes and orange spots between eyes and wings, while cassini have no stripes. Alpha diversity for cicada populations was calculated as H’ using the Shannon-Weiner Index [27].

Dissection and DNA Extraction

The dissection procedures followed those from Ungurcanu’s 1972 manuscript [28]. The abdomen was separated from the thorax using a scalpel. Upon pulling away the thorax, clean forceps were used to collect the internal organs of the abdomen. All internal tissue was placed into 1.5-mL microcentrifuge tubes. QIAGEN DNeasy® Blood and Tissue Kits were used for DNA extractions, using recommended manufacturer protocols for DNA extraction from tissue, with an overnight incubation with lysis buffer in a 56 °C water bath (QIAgen, 2023). DNA concentrations were measured using Qubit DNA High Sensitivity assay (Invitrogen, 2024).

Microbiome Community Analysis

A subset of 36 samples was selected from the set of 60 samples for microbiome analysis. Within the subgroups, the approximate ratio of species was maintained for each site, with roughly equal numbers of males and females considered. Nine samples from each site with successful DNA extraction were selected for the 16S library. In addition to the cicada samples, a control (ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community DNA Standard 2000 ng) was added to the library set. Microbiome community composition was determined using QIAseq® 16S/ITS Panel Kit, using V3/V4 variable regions, and following recommended manufacturer protocols (QIAGEN, 2021). For DNA sequencing libraries, all samples were diluted to 1 ng/mL in accordance with the QIAseq® 16S/ITS Panel Kit, following the manufacturer’s protocol (QIAgen, 2021). Each final library, alongside the control, was measured for the resulting DNA concentration using Qubit DNA (High Sensitivity). Combined, normalized libraries were sequenced using Illumina MiSeq with a V3 600 bp flowcell. Sequence data was analyzed for quality, and microbiome community compositions were determined using CLC Genomics Workbench 23. All populations were normalized to sum to a total of 100, and the rare taxa, defined as present at less than 5% of total average microbiome abundance, were eliminated from analysis. Alpha diversity for collection sites was calculated as H’ using the Shannon-Weiner Index [27]. Differential abundance of bacterial species was calculated as a 2-Factor ANOVA with factors for location and species, at a significance of FDR corrected p-value < 0.05. Differential bacterial abundance as a function of host sex was determined using a t-test with a p-value threshold < 0.05.

Detection of Fungal Pathogen, M. cicadina

The presence of the fungal pathogen M. cicadina was assayed using PCR amplification of characteristic stretches of 18S (SSU) and 28S (LSU) ribosomal gene regions as previously described by Short et al. [29]. PCR primers used were NS24 (AAACCTTGTTACGACTTTTA) with nssu1088R (CGGCACCTTACGAGAAATCA) and LR5 (ATCCTGAGGGAAACTTC) with LR0R (GTACCCGCTGAACTTAAGC). PCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 51.1 °C for 45 s, and elongation at 72 °C for 90 s, with final elongation at 72 °C for 5 min. M. cicadina DNA was considered to be present in samples if bands for both NS24/nssu1088R and LR5/LR0R primers, with product sizes of ~650 bp and ~950 bp, respectively, were present as visualized by agarose gel electrophoresis.

Correlations Between Site Features

The Pearson correlation coefficient was calculated between all datatypes (species diversity, percent cicada infected by M. cicadina, percent of population by species, juglone presence, and relative disturbance) grouped by site. For “disturbance” and “juglone,” the data were transformed into ranked integer values for calculating correlations.

Results and Discussion

Plant Communities

Specific species found at each site are listed in Table 1, and alpha diversities of plant communities are summarized in Table 2, and the H’ alpha-diversities are found in Table 3. Fourteen tree species were confirmed to border sample collection sites, with Buckthorn found to be present at all locations, while eight tree species were unique to a single location. By alpha diversity, Westchester Woods was the most diverse location, and Miller Meadow and Brookfield Woods tied for the least diverse sites. A ubiquitous species found in these communities is Buckthorn, which is an invasive species in Illinois [30]. Two plants known to synthesize juglone are Black Walnut, found at National Grove, and, to a lesser extent, Mockernut Hickory, found at Westchester Woods [31, 32]. Juglone is a toxic compound that inhibits the growth of other plants, exhibits antifungal properties, and is toxic to some insects [33, 34].

Cicada Species

The distribution of cicada species by site is summarized in Table 2. Decula was the most commonly collected cicada species. The distribution of cicada species is not random by location. National Grove has fewer cassini and more decim than expected by random chance. Brookfield Woods has more cassini and fewer decim. Westchester has more decula than expected by chance. There is no significant enrichment or depletion for cicada species at the Miller Meadow location.

Correlations between population features are summarized in Table 3. The abundance of decula at a site is negatively correlated with the abundance of decim. A positive correlation exists between the alpha diversity of tree species and the alpha diversity of cicada species by location, indicating that a more diverse tree population supports a more diverse community of cicada. Notably, the abundance of decula is strongly negatively correlated with sites with the juglone-producing tree species, Black Walnut and Mockernut Hickory. Decim is strongly, positively correlated with these tree species. This suggests that the presence of juglone is significant to cicada distributions and that either species of cicada has variable tolerances for the toxic compound, or else juglone influences some species-specific competitor, pathogen, or preferred food of cicada. While cicadas are known to lay eggs in Black Walnut tree twigs [35], no specific information is available on whether or how susceptible cicadas are to juglone.

Cicada Microbiome

The detected cicada microbiome populations are summarized in Fig. 2 and can be found in tabular format in Supplemental Data 1. A total of 88 bacterial species were identified across all samples by 16S rRNA gene, variable regions V3/V4 microbiome analysis. Of those, 99% were identified at least at the taxonomic level of Family, 92% to Genus, and 80% at the level of Species. On average, 88% of the microbiome populations were comprised of the obligate symbionts H. cicadicola and S. muelleri. While there were significant differences in the abundance of H. cicadicola and S. muelleri by 2-factor ANOVA, there were also significant (t-test p-value < 0.05) differences in the ratio between H. cicadicola and S. muelleri when grouped by species. Cassini has a significantly (p-value < 0.05) higher average H. cicadicola to S. muelleri log2 ratio (1.14) than either decim (0.18) or decula (0.38) (Fig. 2).

For the following most abundant bacterial species, 8% of the average microbiome populations were comprised of the bacteria Lonsdalea iberica, Chitinophaga ginsengisegetis, Pantoea agglomerans, and Pantoea identified at the level of Genus. The next two most common bacteria in the cicada microbiome after the obligate symbionts are Lonsdalea iberica, a tree pathogen, and Chitinophaga ginsengisegetis, a soil-associated bacterium [36]. These bacteria that make up a significant proportion of the cicada microbiome outside of the obligate symbionts could be derived from the cicada’s diet and environment. Pantoea agglomerans, the next most abundant non-obligate symbiont [37], is perhaps more likely to be specific to the cicada microbiome rather than simply derived from its diet or environment. P. agglomerans also is found in the microbiomes of locusts, where it is a symbiote that facilitates the biosynthesis of the pheromone that synchronizes swarming behavior [38]. While cicada swarming behavior is more typically described as a function of sonic signaling and chorusing centers [5, 39], the presence of P. agglomerans opens the intriguing possibility that there is a pheromone component to cicada swarming as well.

The remaining 82 microbiome bacterial species accounted for the outstanding 4% of the total microbiome population, of which no individual bacterial species accounted for more than 0.5% of the microbiome population on average.

Alpha diversity, calculated as Shannon’s Diversity Index H’, for the microbiome community, when considered by location, was highest for National Grove and lowest for Brookfield Woods. When grouped by cicada species, decim had the highest alpha diversity for microbiome communities, and cassini had the lowest. Alpha diversity values are summarized in Table 2.

No significant differences were found in the microbiome community as a function of the sex of the cicada. For 2-factor ANOVA, there were 14 significantly differentially abundant bacteria species by cicada species, including obligate symbiont H. cicadicola, Dietzia cinnamea, Microbispora rosea, Chitinophaga polysaccharea, Cloacibacterium, Enterococcus (Genus), Roseburia (Uncultured), Candidatus Liberibacter, Daeguia caeni, Bradyrhizobium sp. J.UKR5.1, Bacterium CK5-15, Uncultured Tepidimonas sp, Pseudomonas sp., and Uncultured gamma proteobacterium. Seven bacterial species, including H. cicadicola, were significantly differentially abundant by location (Micrococcaceae (Family), Chitinophaga ginsengisegetis, Candidatus Sulcia muelleri, Uncultured bacterium, Erwinia sp. C17(2011), and Uncultured Enterobacter sp.). No bacterial species were differentially abundant as a function of the interaction of cicada species and location except H. cicadicola.

M. cicadina Detection

The detection of M. cicadina DNA by location and species is summarized in Table 3, and the initial data and images of agarose gels can be found in Supplemental Data X. DNA of the pathogenic fungi M. cicadina was found in seven out of 30 (23%) cicadas that did not otherwise show outward signs of fungal infection. There is no significant enrichment or depletion for infected individuals as a function of either sex or cicada species. There is enrichment by sample site, with Miller Meadow having fewer infected individual cicadas and Westchester Woods having more infected cicadas than would be expected by chance (Table 2).

There was no significant effect, by t-test, for the presence of M. cicadina on the relative abundance of any single bacterium member of the microbiome community. However, there is a strong negative correlation between the alpha diversity of cicada microbiomes and the percentage of M. cicadina infections by location (Table 3), indicating that increased diversity of cicada microbiomes is associated with decreased frequency of fungal infections. A negative correlation was found between a history of increasing disturbance at a site and the detected frequency of fungal infections.

Conclusions

An analysis of a sampling of Brood XIII’s 2024 cicada emergence identifies correlations between the environment, the distribution of cicada species, and the cicada’s microbiomes and frequency of fungal infections, suggesting a network of biological interactions that links the macro-environmental parameters to the micro-environment of bacterial species within the cicada.

In this analysis, cicada species decula and decim are differentially abundant by location, potentially signifying a preference for different conditions. Cicada microbiomes vary by cicada species and location, reflecting differences in cicada’s chosen environmental niche and the native microbiomes of their environment and food. In our data, the ratio of obligate symbionts varies as a function of species, further suggesting that the vast swarms of three simultaneously emerging species of cicada may be utilizing slightly different environmental niches. The presence of P. agglomerans, as one of the more abundant and universally present bacteria in the cicada microbiome, is suggestive that cicadas may be using pheromones to communicate with one another in addition to their characteristic singing and visual displays. The detection of Candidatus Liberibacter, commonly associated as a major pathogen of citrus that is usually transmitted by psyllids [40, 41], was perhaps a little alarming. Identification of the specific 16S rRNA reads used to make this identification, however, indicated that the actual sequence is more closely aligned with Liberibacter crescens [42], a non-pathogenic and lab culture-able species (BLAST E-value 0.0, 98% identity). We propose that the presence of this Liberibacter in the cicada microbiome is similarly of a non-pathogenic variety.

The frequency of observed, asymptomatic fungal infections is a function of location and not species. Increased fungal infection frequency is associated with lower diversity microbiomes. However, it cannot be determined from this data if fungal infections depress microbiome diversity or if perhaps low microbiome diversity invites fungal infection. Detection of fungal DNA in the guts of cicadas may be indicative of sublethal fungal infections in the adult population. The negative correlation between the history of disturbance at a site and detected fungal infections suggests that M. cicadina fares better at sites where it is allowed to lurk in relative peace in the soil while it waits for the next emergence of periodical cicada.

There are potential weaknesses of the study, mainly a function of taking a very small sample of the many millions of Brood XIII cicadas that emerged in 2024. Our observations and correlations might be true for the subset of cicadas collected here, but our samples might not capture all of the underlying patterns for all cicada populations of Brood XIII. Sample collection was also performed in a relatively small area of each site, which would prevent us from detecting spatial variability within a site as well as between sites. Samples were collected from adult cicadas, which might not directly reflect the bacterial populations and their dynamics of cicada nymph microbiomes. While the potential presence of P. agglomerans entertains the intriguing possibility that cicadas may be using microbiome-mediated pheromones to communicate, 16S rRNA gene sequence alone is insufficient to identify the species with absolute certainty or to conclude that the specific metabolism necessary for pheromone synthesis is present in the microbiome. Additional analysis is necessary to pursue this hypothesis. Both disturbance and the presence of trees known to synthesize juglone were inferred to have an effect on cicada populations and the frequency of M. cicadina infection. The actual presence of juglone in the soils, however, was not validated by direct chemical assay. A single, ranked integer value cannot capture the complex histories for these sites, and additional research will be needed to quantify which features that derive from disturbance have an effect on cicada populations.

We look forward to the opportunity to test these hypotheses and others when cicada Brood XIII emerges once again in 2041. Meanwhile, multiple other periodical cicada broods will emerge across North America, providing opportunities to further study the interactions between cicada species, location, and microbiomes.
