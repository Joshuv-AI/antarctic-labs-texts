Impact of early intensive GDMT on LVEF recovery and ICD decision making in de novo HFrEF
Authors: Diogo Rosa Ferreira, Daniel Inácio Cazeiro, Joana Brito, Rafael Santos, Joana Rigueira, Doroteia Silva, Nuno Lousada, Fausto Pinto, Dulce Brito, João Agostinho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsImplantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (ICD) implantation is recommended in patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤ 35% after 3 months of optimized medical therapy (OMT). Whether recent advances in guideline‐directed medical therapy (GDMT), including angiotensin receptor‐neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI) and sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) alter the timing of ICD implantation remains uncertain.MethodsIn this single‐centre, prospective cohort study, 106 patients with newly diagnosed HFrEF (mean age 63 ± 13 years; 25% women; 53% non‐ischaemic aetiology) and baseline LVEF ≤35% were enrolled between 2019 and 2022. Echocardiographic assessments were performed at baseline, 3 months and 12 months to evaluate LVEF improvement. The primary endpoint was LVEF recovery >35% between 90 days and 1 year.ResultsBaseline mean LVEF was 27%. At 3 months, mean LVEF increased to 37% (P < 0.001), and 58% of patients achieved LVEF >35%. These patients showed further improvement to a median LVEF of 45% at 12 months. Among those with LVEF ≤35% at 3 months (n = 44), only eight patients (18%) recovered by 12 months, six of whom received cardiac resynchronization therapy. The rapid initiation and optimization of GDMT, particularly ARNI and SGLT2i, was associated with early LVEF improvement.ConclusionsEarly and intensive GDMT optimization resulted in significant LVEF improvement within the first 3 months post‐diagnosis for most patients. Those who failed to recover by this point exhibited limited improvement by 1 year. These findings suggest that the conventional 3 month window for ICD decision making remains appropriate, despite advancements in heart failure therapy.

Introduction

Guidelines recommend implanting an implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (ICD) in symptomatic heart failure (HF) patients with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of ≤35% after 3 months of optimized medical therapy (OMT). 1 However, this recommendation predates the widespread use of angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI) and sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), both of which have demonstrated sustained benefits beyond 3 months. 2 , 3

The Heart Failure Optimization (HF‐OPT) study indicated that continuous optimization of guideline‐recommended medical therapy (GDMT) for at least 180 days in patients with HFrEF was associated with additional LVEF improvement to >35%. 4 Notably, 46% of enrolled patients with persistently low LVEF at Day 90 showed LVEF improvement to >35% by Day 180, significantly increasing the total rate of improvement to 68% [95% confidence interval (CI) 63%–72%] by that time.

The HF‐OPT study suggested that the evaluation for ICD implantation should not occur before Day 180 after diagnosis and could even be considered for extension up to 1 year post‐diagnosis. It is important to note that in the HF‐OPT trial, SGLT2i were not included as part of standard care, and ARNIs were not yet widely adopted, with only 25% of patients receiving ARNI at baseline.

Given the significant changes in the therapeutic landscape due to the incorporation of comprehensive optimized medical therapies, it is pertinent to question whether the prescription of SGLT2i and ARNI as first‐line therapies could lead to earlier and greater improvements in LVEF or, conversely, contribute to sustained left ventricular reverse remodelling beyond 3 months.

Methods

This study conformed to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. We conducted a single‐centre, prospective cohort study involving patients diagnosed with HFrEF at the HF outpatient clinic of Santa Maria University Hospital.

We included patients who met the following criteria: (1) a de novo diagnosis of HF with left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤ 35% during an index hospitalization for HF; (2) enrolment in a post‐discharge HF management programme designed to promote rapid initiation and up‐titration of guideline‐directed medical therapy (GDMT), with up‐titration visits every 2 weeks and routine follow‐up appointments at 3, 6 and 12 months after discharge, as well as additional visits if clinically indicated; (3) regular assessment of adherence to and tolerance of therapy; and (4) echocardiographic evaluation performed at baseline (Day 0), 3 months and 12 months.

We consistently evaluated echocardiograms using the same standardized protocol within the same echocardiography laboratory. The baseline echocardiographic assessment was performed during hospitalization prior to the initiation of GDMT.

The primary endpoint of the study was the proportion of patients achieving an improvement in LVEF to greater than 35% between baseline and 90 days, as well as between 90 days and 12 months. Secondary endpoints included mortality, hospital admissions and the incidence of implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) shocks during the study period.

We compared LVEF at 3 and 12 months to analyse the primary endpoint. We checked if the lower limit of the 95% CI was above 5%. We used basic statistics to describe patient characteristics, how many improved their LVEF above 35% and event numbers. For continuous data, we used t‐tests or Wilcoxon tests, and for categorical data, we used chi‐square (χ 2) tests.

We defined target doses for GDMT as follows: bisoprolol at a minimum of 10 mg daily or carvedilol at 25 mg twice daily, spironolactone at a minimum of 25 mg daily and sacubitril/valsartan at 97/103 mg twice daily.

Results

Between 2019 and 2022, a total of 154 patients were screened, resulting in the enrolment of 106 participants. Not entering the cohort population was mainly due to a baseline LVEF > 35% (36 patients) and not completing the 12 month echocardiogram (12 patients; Figure 1). The mean age of the patients was 63 years, with females comprising 25% of the cohort. Non‐ischaemic heart disease was identified as the predominant aetiology, accounting for 53% of the cases, compared with 47% with ischaemic heart disease. In our cohort, the distribution of non‐ischaemic aetiologies was as follows: dilated cardiomyopathy (80%), valvular (7%), post‐tachycardiomyopathy (4%), hypertensive (3%), post‐myocarditis (3%), post‐chemotherapy (2%) and amyloidosis (1%). Additionally, 62% of the patients were classified as New York Heart Association (NYHA) Class II at baseline. Concerning baseline HF therapies, 91% of patients were treated with beta‐blockers, 44% were receiving ARNI. Additionally, 42% were on angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEi) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB), 65% of patients were on SGLT2i and 81% were prescribed mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRA). The majority of patients (53%) had the four foundational therapies already initiated at discharge (Table 1).

Of the total 106 patients, all underwent follow‐up echocardiography at 3 months, and 104 completed the echocardiography at 12 months. The baseline mean LVEF was 27%. After 3 months of OMT, the mean LVEF significantly increased to 37% (P < 0.001). Additionally, 58% of the patients showed improvement, achieving an LVEF > 35%. Among those who reached an LVEF above 35% at 3 months, the median LVEF was 43% at that time and further improved to 45% at 12 months. In contrast, the 44 patients who did not achieve an LVEF > 35% at 3 months had a median LVEF of 28% at that time and did not meet the 35% target at 12 months, with a median LVEF of only 30% (Table 3 and Figure 2).

Out of the group of patients with a LVEF of less than 35% at 3 months, only 8 (18%) saw their LVEF improve to over 35% by the 12‐month mark. Of these patients, 6 received a cardiac resynchronization device during that time. In addition, only two patients from the group that already recovered at 3 months exhibited a LVEF ≤ 35% at 1 year follow‐up.

Over the course of the study, two patients died, both after 3 months of follow‐up. One patient died from a non‐cardiovascular cause and had an LVEF greater than 35% at the 3 month mark. The other patient, with an LVEF ≤ 35%, was classified as having a cardiovascular‐related death (not related to arrhythmia). During the follow‐up period, there were four hospital admissions due to decompensated HF. Additionally, 37 patients received ICD implants, with two appropriate shocks and one inappropriate shock recorded during follow‐up.

Discussion

Prior to this study, the HF‐OPT study was the only prospective study examining improvements in LVEF beyond 90 days in patients with a de novo diagnosis of HFrEF undergoing continuous optimization of medical therapy. The HF‐OPT study revealed that 46% of patients with an LVEF of ≤35% improved to >35% by Day 180, and in a subset of 392 patients, this improvement rose to 77% by Day 360. Time is crucial for LVEF recovery in HFrEF, influencing ICD implantation decisions. Currently, for patients that maintain LVEF ≤ 35%, the recommended time for ICD implantation is at 90 days after reaching OMT, but HF‐OPT results suggested that LVEF improvement may take longer, warranting a delay in ICD decisions until patients are stable and medically optimized. It is important to note that in HF‐OPT, GDMT up‐titration was not aggressive, with only 22% of patients on maximum doses of beta‐blockers at the 3 month mark, 45% on MRAs and 33.2% on ACEi, ARB or ARNI. Furthermore, this study was conducted prior to the widespread adoption of ARNIs and the introduction of SGLT2i as standard treatment practices.

Our research contributes further insights into the evolution of LVEF during the first year post‐diagnosis, reflecting the contemporary treatment landscape for HFrEF, which includes widespread use of these two pharmacological classes.

Our findings differ from those of the HF‐OPT study and are summarized as follows: (i) at 3 months, the majority of patients (58%) exhibited an improvement to an LVEF > 35%; (ii) patients who achieved an LVEF > 35% at 3 months continued to improve; (iii) the subgroup of patients who did not recover to an LVEF > 35% at 3 months maintained a median LVEF ≤ 35% at 12 months; (iv) no significant differences were observed in the number of patients achieving the target dosage of GDMT at 3 months compared with 1 year.

The STRONG‐HF trial demonstrated that an intensive treatment strategy featuring prompt up‐titration of GDMT, coupled with close follow‐up after an acute HF admission, led to a reduction in symptoms, enhanced quality of life and lowered the risk of events when compared with standard care. 5 This trial highlighted the importance of optimizing HF therapy quickly with close monitoring, demonstrating that this approach is more effective than previous standard practices that delay drug optimization and patient oversight. In our study, approximately half of the patients were already receiving ARNI, and two‐thirds were on SGLT2i at the time of discharge. Besides, 53% of patients already had all of the four foundational therapies initiated before discharge, and the median time until reaching OMT was only 6.4 weeks. This early and intensive up‐titration of GDMT resulted in a significant proportion of patients achieving the target dose within 3 months. The absence of significant differences in doses between the 3 month and 1 year follow‐ups may suggest that patients were already on maximal tolerated doses by the 3 month point. These findings may explain the differences in LVEF trajectories relative to the HF‐OPT study. Additionally, this study highlights the essential need for regular follow‐up for these patients at specialized HF centres that are skilled in the intensive up‐titration of foundational therapies.

This study further emphasizes the importance of GDMT in left ventricular reverse remodelling. The pattern of LVEF recovery in our cohort—with over half of the patients improving to LVEF > 35% by 3 months and maintaining this improvement at 1 year—points to the emergence of a distinct clinical phenotype increasingly recognized in recent literature: HF with improved ejection fraction (HFimpEF). Previous studies, including large clinical cohorts, have shown that HFimpEF patients have better clinical characteristics, improved exercise capacity and lower cardiovascular risk compared with those with persistently reduced LVEF. Interestingly, the proportion of patients in our study who improved LVEF early—over 50% by 3 months—appears higher than what has been described in earlier work. This may be explained by the intensive treatment strategy, involving rapid initiation and up‐titration of all four GDMT classes, combined with close follow‐up in a specialized HF clinic. 6 , 7

The intensive medical therapy appeared to create two distinct groups depending on whether patients achieved an LVEF >35% at the 3‐month mark. Patients who improved to an LVEF >35% at 3 months continued to enhance their median LVEF up to 1 year. Conversely, those who did not show improvement in LVEF by the 3‐month mark did not experience significant gains by the one‐year follow‐up. Among the 8 patients who had an LVEF ≤ 35% at 3 months and later recovered at 1 year, 6 (67%) received a cardiac resynchronization device, indicating that resynchronization may have been the main driver of LVEF improvement in this subset of patients. Our findings suggest that rapid and intensive GDMT up‐titration using the four foundational therapy classes promotes dramatically quick improvements in LVEF, usually in the first 3 months after reaching OMT. On the other hand, patients who do not respond to early intensive therapy up‐titration by the 3 month mark are unlikely to recover by 1 year. Although ventricular reverse remodelling may not be fully achieved by 3 months, patients who have not experienced significant recovery by this point, even with aggressive foundational therapy, often continue to exhibit LVEF below the 35% threshold.

As a result, they may remain at elevated risk for sudden cardiac death. Notably, in the group of patients who did not recover and received ICD implants, two appropriate shocks were recorded.

This study has several limitations. First, the observational design of the study is susceptible to biases related to participant selection, missing data and outcome measurement. This preliminary, hypothesis‐generating study was conducted in a single tertiary HF centre, a setting that may limit generalisability; future multicentre investigations involving both tertiary and non‐tertiary centres across different countries will be necessary to validate and extend these findings.

An additional limitation of our study is the absence of subgroup analyses based on other established arrhythmic risk markers, such as nonsustained ventricular tachycardia (NSVT), QRS duration > 150 ms or myocardial scar burden identified by cardiac magnetic resonance imaging.

Although cardiac magnetic resonance imaging offers superior diagnostic precision and the ability to detect reversible myocardial injury and quantify late gadolinium enhancement for prognostic purposes, it was not routinely performed in our cohort due to availability and patient‐related limitations; integrating CMR into future multicentre studies could substantially improve phenotyping and risk stratification in HF.

In conclusion, our study suggests that early and intensive up‐titration of GDMT after a de novo HF diagnosis may lead to meaningful improvements in LVEF for a substantial proportion of patients, with more than half achieving an LVEF > 35% by 3 months and maintaining this improvement at 1 year. Patients who did not reach this threshold by 3 months showed limited further recovery, indicating that this time point may be useful for guiding decisions regarding ICD implantation. Further investigation is warranted to confirm these observations and refine timing strategies for device therapy.
