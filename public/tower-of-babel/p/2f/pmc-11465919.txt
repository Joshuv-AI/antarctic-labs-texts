Reduced muscle mass is an important part of Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition criteria in nutritional diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Juan Li, Minjie Jiang, Xin Hua, Hongxia Xu, Muchen Wu, Jing Wu, Songtao Liu, Hanping Shi, Qinghua Meng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition criteria (GLIM) was established to build a global consensus on the diagnostic criteria for malnutrition. The study aimed to assess the prevalence of the malnutrition diagnosed by GLIM criteria for patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and to determine the role of the reduced muscle mass defined by CT scans in the GLIM criteria.MethodsThis cohort research was conducted on adult cirrhotic patients with HCC. The risk of malnutrition was screened by Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS-2002), and malnutrition was diagnosed by GLIM criteria. The third lumbar vertebrae (L3-SMI) were used to represent the muscle mass in GLIM criteria. The variables associated with overall mortality were assessed by multivariate Cox regression analyses.ResultsThe incidence of malnutrition diagnosed by GLIM criteria was 49.7% (179/360) in patients with HCC. If reduced muscle mass was not included in GLIM criteria, the prevalence of malnutrition was 31.7% (114/360). GLIM-defined malnutrition (HR = 1.979, 95%CI 1.019–3.841, P = 0.044) was independently associated with overall mortality in patients with HCC. However, the GLIM-defined malnutrition (without muscle mass) was not associated with overall mortality (HR = 0.863, 95%CI 0.399–1.867, P = 0.709).ConclusionsSkeletal muscle mass is an integral component of the GLIM criteria for patients with HCC. The malnutrition is common in patients with HCC, and malnourishment is associated with higher overall mortality. GLIM criteria are recommended to assess the nutritional status of hospitalized patients with HCC, which is recommended and can be used as the basis for nutritional interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-024-03438-x.

Introduction

Chronic liver disease, including liver cirrhosis and liver cancer, is a major global health burden [1–3]. The most frequent primary liver cancer is HCC and it presents together with cirrhosis in most cases [4]. In 2015 there were 854,000 incident cases of liver cancer making it the 6th leading cause of cancer worldwide [1]. According to the Global Burden of Disease project, 2.2 million patients died due to liver cirrhosis and approximately 1 million patients died due to HCC [1]. In addition, the 5 year survival for cancer from liver and intrahepatic ducts was only 18% according to data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Program [1]. Malnutrition is a clinical syndrome that results from “an imbalance of nutrients that causes measurable adverse effects on tissue/body form or function, and/or clinical outcome” and it is common among patients with tumor worldwide [5, 6]. Malnutrition is also a significant global public health burden, and malnourished patients are more likely to have adverse outcomes [7–16].

The liver acts as an important player in energy metabolism. Various factors such as reduced food intake, malabsorption, hypermetabolic status, bacterial overgrowth, and metabolic disorder increase the risk of malnutrition in patients with HCC [17, 18]. According to different guidelines, the prevalence of malnutrition in patients with liver cirrhosis was ranged from 20 to 60%, depending on the liver disease etiology, stage of the disease, and the diagnostic criteria used [19, 20]. It must be considered that these patients usually develop HCC on the basis of long-term cirrhosis [4]. HCC as a neoplastic entity also add a metabolic challenge on top of that posed by liver cirrhosis. Therefore, patients with HCC may present with severe malnutrition than other cancer. Malnutrition is associated with a higher incidence of complications and mortality in patients with liver cancer [20]. However, the status of malnutrition can be potentially modifiable with nutrition therapy and aggressive intervention can improve the prognosis. It is important to establish a suitable nutritional assessment to determine the influence of nutrition on liver disease especially in HCC [21, 22].

The GLIM criteria, published in 2019, aim to establish a global consensus on diagnostic criteria for malnutrition across different diseases [16, 23, 24]. The GLIM criteria include two steps of risk screening and diagnosis [23]. First, screening to identify nutritional risk status by using any validated screening tool, and second, assessment for diagnosis of malnutrition at least 1 phenotypic criterion (weight loss, low body mass index, reduced muscle mass) and 1 etiologic criterion (reduced food intake/assimilation and disease burden/inflammation) [23]. Skeletal muscle mass is an important part of GLIM criteria, however, less commonly performed in clinical practice, especially in those settings where skilled clinical nutrition practitioners and professional body composition approaches are lacking [25]. Although the original GLIM guidelines tentatively allowed for skeletal muscle function as a proxy or alternative indicator of skeletal muscle mass, the role of muscle function as an indicator of potential proxy for skeletal muscle mass remains controversial [23, 25]. Skeletal muscle index at the third lumbar vertebrae (L3-SMI) could be used as surrogate markers of whole-body skeletal muscle [25]. In patients with HCC, CT scans can often be used as part of routine diagnostic and/or follow-up visits [19]. Therefore, it is important to determine the role of the skeletal muscle mass defined by CT scans in the GLIM criteria. Our main aim was to assess the clinical application of GLIM criteria with or without CT determined reduced muscle mass in patients with HCC and to verify the association between GLIM criteria and adverse clinical outcomes.

Methods

Study population

The study was conducted as a retrospective study included cirrhotic patients with HCC hospitalized between January 1, 2018, and February 28, 2022, at Beijing You-An Hospital Capital Medical University. Adult patients with a clinical diagnosis of HCC and who underwent an abdominal CT examination during hospitalization were eligible. HCC was diagnosed according to the criteria of the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases clinical practice guidelines for HCC [26]. The exclusion criteria included the following: (1) incomplete clinical information; (2) confirmed or strongly suspected diagnosis of malignant tumors other than HCC; (3) who were long-term bedridden; (4) diagnosed with Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome; (5) pregnancy or lactation; (6) chronic renal and/or respiratory insufficiency or severe heart disease or conditions that cause intestinal nutrient absorption disorders. The study was approved by the ethics committee of Beijing You-An Hospital Capital Medical University (No.LL-2020–079 K). As a retrospective study, informed consent was waived by the ethics committee of Beijing You-An Hospital Capital Medical University.

Anthropometric measurements

Height and weight were measured and recorded by nutritionists using standard measurement methods during hospitalization. Body weight was measured on a digital scale with patients being barefoot and wearing only light clothes in the morning [27]. In this research, the dry body weight of patients with fluid retention was calculated and corrected according to the clinical severity of ascites subtracted a percentage of body weight (reducing 5%, 10%, or 15% of the current body weight if mild, moderate, or severe ascites, respectively), and an additional 5% in body weight will be discounted if patients have water retention in the lower limbs [5, 20]. The body mass index (BMI) was calculated as the dry weight in kilograms divided by the height in meters squared (kg/m2) [28].

Assessment of L3-SMI

CT scans were used to scan abdominal CT images and skeletal muscle mass measures was analyzed using electronically stored CT images. Skeletal muscle was identified through Hounsfield unit thresholds (− 29 HU to + 150 HU) as the previous study described [21]. The skeletal muscle mass at the L3 vertebra was calculated by syngo.via Siemens AG software. The L3-SMI was calculated as the muscle area at the level of L3 (cm2) divided by the height in meters squared (m2) [21, 29].

Nutritional diagnosis

The clinical nutritional diagnosis was completed by trained dietitians. The GLIM criteria include two steps. Firstly, we applied the NRS-2002 to screen nutritional risk [23]. The second step is to diagnose malnutrition for patients with nutritional risk [23]. The GLIM criteria include two etiologic criteria (reduced food intake/assimilation and disease burden/inflammation) and three phenotypic criteria (weight loss, low body mass index, reduced muscle mass) [23]. In this study, patients with elevated serum procalcitonin (PCT) were considered to have inflammation [30–32]. Food intake was evaluated by a professional nutrition team, and food intake of less than 50% of the usual intake in the past week, or any reduction in the past two weeks, was considered a reduction in food intake [23]. Weight loss > 5% within the past 6 months or > 10% beyond 6 months was judged to be nonvolitional weight loss [23]. Low BMI based on the optimal cut-off values in Asians (18.5 kg/m2 for patients aged < 70 years and 20 kg/m2 for patients aged>70 years) [33]. Low L3-SMI based on the optimal cut-off values in Asians (< 42 cm2/m2 for males and < 38 cm2/m2 for females) [34]. Patients who meet at least one of the phenotypic criteria and at least one of the etiologic criteria can be diagnosed with malnutrition in this research [23]. In addition, we made a diagnosis of malnutrition that did not include reduced muscle mass. Patients who meet at least one of the phenotypic criteria (low BMI and weight loss) and at least one of the etiologic criteria (reduced food intake and inflammation) can be diagnosed with GLIM-defined malnutrition (without muscle mass). Subjective Global Assessment (SGA) was carried out by trained dietitians according to the criteria of previous studies and defines malnutrition as SGA grade B or C [16, 35].

Data collection

Data collected from the medical records include demographic information, survival information, complications, and lab data. The complications include Hepatic Encephalopathy (HE), spontaneous peritonitis (SBP), and ascites. The lab data include serum albumin (ALB), the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and serum hemoglobin (HB). The Child-Pugh score and Model End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score were calculated using data recorded in medical records system and calculated as previous studies reported [32, 36]. All patients were followed up until May 1, 2023, or until death (from any reason).

Statistical analysis

The measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation if they were normally distributed, and the comparison between the two groups was performed using an independent sample Student’s t-test. The measurement data were expressed as median (interquartile range) if they were not normally distributed, and the comparison between the two groups was performed using a Mann–Whitney nonparametric test. The count data were measured by a Chi-square test. The variables with statistical differences after univariate analysis were included in multivariate analysis. The association between GLIM-defined malnutrition and in-hospital death were analyzed by logistic regression method. We estimated cumulative incidence of mortality with use of the Kaplan-Meier method. Log-rank tests were used to separately compare unadjusted survival curves by malnutrition. Cox regression analysis was also performed to assess the associations between malnutrition and overall survival, and the results are shown as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence interval (CI). The rate of agreement between the GLIM criteria and SGA was investigated by kappa test. Differences in GLIM criteria were compared using subject work characteristics (ROC) analyses with SGA as the reference tool and area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS.27, and a difference of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Prevalence of GLIM-defined malnutrition in HCC

According to NRS-2002, 85.6% (308/360) of patients with HCC had nutritional risk. Nutritional assessment of patients at risk of malnutrition according to GLIM criteria is shown in Table S1. For patients at nutritional risk, 94.8% (292/308) patients meet the etiological criteria (91.2% had low intake, and 68.2% had inflammation), and 63.0% (194/308) patients meet the phenotypic criteria (29.2% had low BMI, 53.9% had reduced muscle mass, and 29.5% had weight loss). Finally, the prevalence of GLIM-defined malnutrition in patients with HCC was 49.7% (179/360). Reduced muscle mass determined by CT scans was present in 90.5% (162/179) patients with GLIM-defined malnutrition, but low BMI and weight loss was present in only 50.3% (90/179) and 45.8% (82/179) respectively in patients with GLIM-defined malnutrition. If we ignore the phenotypic criteria of muscle mass in the diagnoses process, the prevalence of GLIM defined malnutrition was 31.7% (114/360) in HCC (Table S2). The basic characteristics of different nutritional statuses were listed in Table 1. The level of serum ALB (P < 0.001) and serum HB (P < 0.001) was significantly lower in patients with malnutrition, and the level of NLR (P < 0.001) was higher than those without malnutrition. And patients with GLIM defined malnutrition also had higher MELD score (P = 0.003) and Child-Pugh score (P < 0.001). Patients with GLIM-defined malnutrition had a higher prevalence of SBP (P = 0.003) than those without malnutrition.

Cumulative mortality in patients with and without malnutrition

The cumulative mortality in patients with and without malnutrition were shown in Table 2. Under the standard GLIM criteria, the overall survival probability was significantly lower in patients with malnutrition than in patients without malnutrition (P < 0.001; Fig. 1A). The 90-day, 1-year, 2-year, 3-year, and overall mortality was 14.9%, 24.9%, 36.5%, 38.7%, and 40.9% in patients without malnutrition respectively. And the 90-day, 1-year, 2-year, 3-year, and overall mortality was 27.9%, 49.2%, 61.5%, 68.7%, and 77.7% in patients with malnutrition respectively. Similarly, the overall survival probability was significantly lower in patients with GLIM-defined malnutrition (without muscle mass) than in those without malnutrition (P < 0.001; Fig. 1B). The 90-day, 1-year, 2-year, 3-year and overall mortality was 17.9%, 31.7%, 43.9%, 47.6%, and 50.4% in patients without GLIM-defined malnutrition (without muscle mass) respectively. And the 90-day, 1-year, 2-year, 3-year, and overall mortality was 28.9%, 48.2%, 59.6%, 66.7%, and 78.1% in patients with GLIM-defined malnutrition (without muscle mass) respectively.

The risk factors of in-hospital mortality

In total cohort, the in-hospital mortality for HCC was 9.2% (33/360). The demographic and clinical characteristics of survivor and death in patients with HCC were shown in Table 3. Patients who died in hospital had lower serum ALB (P = 0.032) and HB (P < 0.001), and higher level of serum NLR (P < 0.001). And patients died during hospitalization had higher MELD scores (P = 0.004) and Child scores (P < 0.001) compared to survivors. Patients who died during hospitalization also had higher prevalence of GLIM defined malnutrition (P = 0.002) and reduced muscle mass (P = 0.003) than survivor. However, the prevalence of GLIM-defined malnutrition (without muscle mass) was no difference between survivor and death (P = 0.317). After multivariate logistic regression analyses, Child score (OR 1.371, 1.018–1.846; P = 0.037) and reduced muscle mass (OR 2.779, 1.187-6.600; P = 0.019) were independent risk factors for in-hospital mortality. GLIM-defined malnutrition was not independently risk factor for in-hospital mortality after multivariate analysis.

Features associated with overall mortality in univariate and multivariate cox analyses

All patients were followed up until May 1, 2023, or until death (from any reason). During the follow-up, a total of 213 patients died after the nutritional screening, 74 in the group without GLIM-defined malnutrition and 139 with GLIM-defined malnutrition. No matter reduced muscle mass included or not included in the GLIM criteria, GLIM-defined malnutrition was associated with overall mortality after univariate Cox analysis. And other factors associated with overall mortality were shown in Table 4. In the multivariate Cox analysis, only Gender (HR = 0.590, 95%CI 0.418–0.834, P = 0.003), MELD score (HR = 1.042, 95%CI 1.007–1.07 8, P = 0.019), and GLIM-defined malnutrition (HR = 1.979, 95%CI 1.019–3.841, P = 0.044) were independently associated with overall mortality in patients with HCC. In addition, the GLIM-defined malnutrition (without muscle mass) was not associated with overall mortality (HR = 0.863, 95%CI 0.399–1.867, P = 0.709).

Comparison between GLIM criteria and SGA criteria

According to SGA criteria, the prevalence of malnutrition was 80.3% (289/360) in patients with HCC. Under the SGA criteria, the overall survival probability was significantly lower in patients with malnutrition than those without malnutrition (P < 0.040; Fig. 2). The prevalence of malnutrition diagnosed by SGA and GLIM criteria are shown in Table S3 and Table S4. The agreement between SGA and the standard GLIM criteria (kappa=-0.069, P < 0.001) and GLIM criteria (without muscle mass) (kappa=-0.049, P < 0.001) were poor. The ROC curve was used to predict the overall mortality between patients with malnutrition and without malnutrition diagnosed by GLIM criteria, GLIM criteria (without muscle mass) and SGA criteria respectively (Fig. 3). And the area under the ROC of the standard GLIM criteria (0.690, 0.634–0.746, P < 0.001) was higher than that of GLIM criteria (without muscle mass) (0.624, 0.566–0.682, P < 0.001), and SGA (0.552, 0.491–0.613, P = 0.095).

Discussion

The GLIM criteria were published to build a global consensus on the diagnostic criteria for malnutrition. The results of the meta-analysis showed that the GLIM criteria have high accuracy in the diagnosis of malnutrition and are expected to become the gold standard for clinical diagnosis of malnutrition [7]. However, the GLIM framework is not static, and at least 21 possible combinations of parameters can be used to classify a patient as malnourished or not [12]. Therefore, the incidence of malnutrition in chronic liver disease ranged from 0.7 to 65.1% due to the different combinations of the GLIM criteria in previous studies [24, 27, 37–39]. Because the GLIM consensus criteria have only recently been proposed, it is important to validate it in different clinical settings including HCC [12, 23]. In this study, 49.7% patients with HCC were malnutrition diagnosed by GLIM criteria, and GLIM-defined malnutrition was associated with overall mortality in patients with HCC.

It is different from other malignant tumors, HCC usually developed on top of long-lasting cirrhosis [4]. A previous research found cirrhotic patients had higher protein oxidation rate and lower carbohydrate oxidation rate, which contributes to protein energy malnutrition [40]. HCC as a neoplastic entity adds a metabolic challenge on top of that posed by liver cirrhosis. In addition, the presence of inflammation can also lead to malnutrition in HCC patients [41]. Therefore, patients with HCC may have more severe malnutrition than other cancer. A study found the prevalence of malnutrition diagnosed by GLIM criteria was 24% in patients with lung cancer [42]. Another study includes colorectal cancer, gastric cancer, and lung cancer found the prevalence of malnutrition based on GLIM criteria at admission was 38.9% [5]. A meta-analysis noted the prevalence of malnutrition was 44.2% based on GLIM criteria [7, 16].In this research, the incidence of malnutrition diagnosed by GLIM was 49.7% in HCC was higher than other previous studies.

Sarcopenia is an important part of malnutrition and is also a powerful predictor of clinically relevant adverse outcomes [43]. In the last few years, sarcopenia has been the subject of substantial research in patients with uncompensated end-stage liver disease [43]. A meta-analysis conducted in 2021 showed sarcopenia impaired outcome in cirrhotic patients [44]. Therefore, reduced muscle mass also includes in GLIM criteria. In this study, CT-determined low muscle mass was present in 90.5% patients with GLIM-defined malnutrition. For another phenotypic criteria, low BMI was present in 45.8% patients with malnutrition. This indicates that there is a proportion of patients with normal BMI or obesity who also have reduced muscle mass, and they are also malnourished. Especially with the increasing prevalence of obesity and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, sarcopenia obesity is observed in a significant number of patients with liver disease, and obesity does not preclude malnutrition [20, 45–47]. In this cohort, 49.7% were diagnosed with GLIM defined malnutrition but only 31.7% patients were diagnosed with malnutrition when reduced muscle mass was not included in the GLIM criteria. Patients with chronic liver disease often have fluid retention such as ascites and edema. Although the dry weight of patients with fluid retention was calculated and corrected according to the clinical severity of ascites, the assessment of the degree of ascites is highly subjective and may be a bias in the estimation of dry weight. Therefore, low skeletal muscle mass is an essential component of GLIM, which can improve the detection rate of malnutrition in patients with HCC.

Mortality is often used to test the efficacy of malnutrition as a predictive indicator [48–50]. In other diseases, malnutrition by GLIM criteria is associated with substantially worse clinical outcomes [7, 51]. Although malnutrition diagnosed by GLIM criteria was not associated with in-hospital death after multivariate analyses in this study, reduced muscle mass was also an independent risk factor for in-hospital mortality after multivariate analyses. After multivariate cox analyses, malnutrition diagnosed by GLIM criteria is a significant indicator of overall survival rate in patients with HCC. However, when muscle mass was not included in GLIM criteria, malnutrition was not associated with overall mortality after multifactorial analysis. From these results we found that skeletal muscle mass plays important role in nutritional diagnoses and clinical outcome prediction. As an important part of malnutrition, sarcopenia was associated with adverse outcomes in patients with liver cirrhosis and HCC [21, 44]. Although muscle mass plays an essential role in patients with HCC, there is no agreement on how to precisely measure and define reduced muscle mass in the clinical setting [23]. The muscle mass measured by CT image analysis has the advantages of being objective, quantifiable, accurate, and non-invasive, and is a good indicator of protein malnutrition and sarcopenia than other evaluation tools including anthropometry, dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, and bioelectrical impedance analysis, and it is a relatively better protein-malnourishment and sarcopenia indicator [16, 21]. Therefore, L3-SMI was recommended as the best method to quantify the consistency and reproducibility of muscle mass in patients with HCC [6].

The GLIM criteria validation needs to be compared with other nutritional assessment tools [5]. The SGA is a globally available tool for assessing malnutrition and is recognized as the best tool for assessing malnutrition in the hospital setting [35, 52]. However, SGA had low concordance with other methods of assessing nutritional status (total lymphocyte count, upper arm muscle circumference, triceps skinfold thickness, subscapularis skinfold thickness, BMI and grip strength measurements) [20, 53]. The concordance between SGA and the GLIM criteria were also weak in this research. Altered dietary intake, gastrointestinal symptoms, and ascites are important components of the SGA criteria and are very common in patients with HCC, which may explain the higher incidence of malnutrition described by the SGA in this study [16]. Thus, objective measurements of skeletal muscle can improve the diagnosis of malnutrition in patients with HCC and help monitor changes in their nutritional status over time [38]. In this research, GLIM criteria were also better than SGA criteria in predicting overall mortality in HCC. Therefore, the GLIM criteria may be more suitable than the SGA criteria for the assessment of malnutrition in patients with HCC.

Some limitations of this study should be discussed when considering the results. Firstly, this research is limited by a retrospective single center designed and further multi-regional validation research is needed. Secondly, the disease-free survival (DFS) was not included in the study, and whether malnutrition can affect DFS in these patients requires continued research. Third, a control group of cirrhotic patients with no HCC was not include in this study. Finally, the optimal thresholds for distinguishing malnutrition severity with low muscle mass remains unknown, the severity of malnutrition was not graded in this study. Further research is needed in the future to determine the optimal threshold for severe loss of muscle mass.

Conclusion

Reduced muscle mass assessed by CT-scans is an important part of GLIM consensus criteria in cirrhotic patients with HCC. The prevalence of GLIM defined malnutrition in patients with HCC is high. Malnutrition diagnosed by GLIM criteria containing CT-determined low muscle mass was shown to adequately predict overall mortality in patients with HCC.
