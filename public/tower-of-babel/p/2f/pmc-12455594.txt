Engineering of BZ transposase and transposon donor vector for enhanced efficiency and safety in gene delivery applications
Authors: Saisai Wang, Pingjing Zhang, Yan Sun, Yuan Fang, Pei Wang, Meiqi Shao, Ningning Zhang, Shasha Shi, Xin Chen, Haixia Gao, Jingbo Cheng, Bo Gao, Tao Liu, Qijun Qian, Chengyi Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransposons, as non-viral vectors, provide an efficient and secure method for stable gene delivery and have been successfully applied in human gene therapies. The engineering of transposase has significantly improved the efficiency of various transposon systems, including chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell engineering. In this study, multiple engineering strategies were implemented to enhance the efficiency and safety of the Baize (BZ) transposon system, which was derived from the ZB (the wild-type BZ, BZwt) and has been proven to be an effective tool for genetic manipulation in vertebrates. Through designed engineering and combinatorial mutagenesis in vitro, several hyperactive BZ transposase variants with higher transposition activity, cargo capacity, and integration safety were developed. At optimal activity levels, BZ325 surpassed BZwt by ∼1.2-fold and 2.3-fold at 500 ng and 10 ng dosages of donor plasmids, respectively. Furthermore, reducing the size of BZ donor vector backbone significantly increased CAR-T modification efficiency without compromising its function. Notably, BZ325, BZ326, and especially BZ327 exhibited significantly higher CAR-T engineering rates and CAR expression levels than BZwt. Overall, the engineering of the BZ transposon system significantly enhanced its transposition activity, cargo capacity, and safety, providing a compelling tool for gene transfer applications and emphasizing its potential in gene therapy.

Introduction

Advancements in gene therapy are pivotal for developing novel treatments for genetic disorders and constitute an actively researched domain [1]. Transposons, DNA sequences capable of changing their positions within the genome, have attracted significant attention as potential gene delivery tools, offering an alternative to viral vectors [2, 3]. Viral vectors, currently the principal medium for gene delivery in therapy, can successfully integrate new genes into host cells; however, they are associated with potential complications such as immune reactions and insertional mutagenesis [4, 5]. Transposons are being actively explored as non-viral gene delivery vectors due to their favorable safety profile in therapeutic applications. These naturally occurring mobile genetic elements are engineered into efficient DNA transfer vehicles that demonstrate minimal cellular toxicity and reduced immunogenicity compared to viral vector systems. Early transposon systems, such as Sleeping Beauty (SB) and PiggyBac (PB), have demonstrated promising outcomes in preclinical trials, especially in CAR-T gene therapy [6, 7]. Despite their potential, the efficiency of gene delivery via transposons lags slightly behind or is on par with that of viral vectors, prompting ongoing research focused on refining their safety and efficiency to pave the way for their widespread clinical application [8, 9].

Advanced molecular engineering techniques, such as directed evolution and rational protein design, have been employed to derive transposase variants exhibiting increased activity and elevated specificity. These engineered enzymes demonstrate significantly reduced off-target effects, effectively minimizing risks of insertional mutagenesis in oncogenic loci or disruption of endogenous gene regulatory elements [10, 11].

This research has successfully developed hyperactive transposases demonstrating dramatically enhanced integration efficiency. Notably, the engineered SB transposase variant SB100X shows >100-fold greater activity than the original SB transposase [12]. Similarly, the hyperactive PB transposase exhibits a 17-fold increase in integration efficiency compared to its wild-type counterpart [13]. Further optimization of the PiggyBac system was achieved through strategic modifications including: (i) N-terminal deletion, (ii) addition of a second C-terminal transposase domain, (iii) implementation of symmetric transposon ends, and (iv) removal of internal domain sequences within the 3′ terminal repeat region of the donor vector. These structural refinements collectively generated an optimal PiggyBac transposon system with maximized performance characteristics [14, 15]. Similar enhancements have been achieved with the TcBuster transposon from the hAT superfamily [16]. Moreover, altering three critical amino acids (H187, P247, and K248) in the SB100X transposase, which are crucial for target DNA selection, has markedly altered the target site preferences and the genome-wide integration profile. Mutants such as H187V, P247R, and K248R have been shown to redirect transposon integration away from genes, thus demonstrating improved safety profiles for integration into the human genome [17].

Moreover, the refinement and downsizing of transposon donor vectors are paramount goals in gene therapy and genetic engineering, intended to reduce toxicity and improve gene transfer efficiency. Vector size reduction technologies such as Tiniplasmid, Nanoplasmid, and Minicircle DNA have been explored [18–20]. Tiniplasmid and Nanoplasmid, derived from plasmid DNA, preserve vital replication and selection elements, excluding superfluous DNA to limit the backbone size to ∼500 bp. Conversely, Minicircle DNA produces small, circular DNA molecules lacking bacterial plasmid scaffolds essential for replication and antibiotic resistance, which often provoke immunogenicity and toxicity. These structures are produced through a recombination process within a parent plasmid that isolates the gene of interest from bacterial components, including only regulatory sequences and the gene itself. This streamlined composition improves in vivo safety, prolongs expression, and markedly reduces vector backbone size to ∼300 bp, potentially enhancing transfection efficiency and diminishing immune responses [21]. These advances are applied across gene therapy, vaccine development, and fundamental research, with notable benefits in CAR-T therapy, such as increased transfection efficiency, decreased toxicity, extended expression, and attenuated immune response in connection with SB transposon system [21, 22]. Nonetheless, crafting and cultivating these simplified vectors demand sophisticated techniques and entail technical complexities, particularly in manufacturing Minicircle DNA, where specific control over enzymatic and purification processes is crucial to eliminate extraneous sequences and preserve the gene of interest’s purity.

In this study, the BZ transposon, a member of the Tc1/Mariner superfamily extracted from the zebrafish genome and noted for its considerable activity, exhibits both structural similarity and high homology with the SB transposase [23, 24]. We have engineered various mutations into this element to generate hyperactive mutants with improved safety and efficiency as gene delivery vectors. Specifically, we have identified a collection of BZ transposase mutants with enhanced functionality by integrating multiple mutations into a single sequence, thereby creating several hyperactive BZ variants. Our analysis extended to assessing the impact of these hyperactive variants on the genome-wide integration profile. Additionally, we designed a compact donor plasmid with a reduced backbone size of ∼800 bp, termed Miniplasmid, to further explore the application potential of the BZ transposon in conjunction with CAR-T cell modification. The integration of hyperactive BZ transposase variants with the Miniplasmid transposon donor advances the versatility of BZ transposon systems in mammalian genetics and gene therapy applications.

Materials and methods

Donor plasmid modifications on BZ transposition activity

For the TSD-TIR modifications of donor plasmids, we constructed neomycin resistance gene expression cassettes (PGK-Neo-polyA) and fluorescence gene expression cassettes (LoxP-GFP-polyA). These cassettes were flanked by one or two terminal inverted repeats (TIRs) and varied in target site duplications (TSDs) from one to four TA sequences. Specifically, the PGK-Neo cassettes were designed with configurations such as BZwt+1TA-1TIR-1TA-1TIR, BZwt+1TA-1TIR, BZwt+2TA-1TIR, BZwt+2TA-2TIR, BZwt+4TA-1TIR, and BZwt+4TA-2TIR. The purpose of these constructs was to assess the effect of TSD-TIR modifications on BZ transposition activity, as depicted in Supplementary Fig. S2A.

To evaluate the cargo capacity of the BZ transposon, various donor plasmids containing different-sized fragments were constructed. λ-phage genome fragments of varying lengths (λ1, λ2, λ3: 1.5, 3.5, and 6.9 kb) were inserted downstream of PGK-Neo-polyA and upstream of the 3′ BZTIR. To standardize the total donor plasmid lengths, additional λ-phage genome fragments of 3.5, 5.5, and 6.9 kb were incorporated outside the BZTIRs, serving as external stuffer fragments. The different cargo vectors, with lengths of 1.6, 3.1, 5.1, and 8.5 kb, were designated as pBZ-PGK-Neo-1.6, pBZ-PGK-Neo-3.1, pBZ-PGK-Neo-5.1, and pBZ-PGK-Neo-8.5, were used to assess the cargo efficiency of BZwt and BZ325. The transposase plasmids, driven by CAG promoters and constructed according to previous reports [23, 25], were labeled pCAG-BZwt and pCAG-BZ325. Additionally, pBZ-JWW1 (8810 bp) and pSB-JWW1 (8991 bp) were constructed using PCR to amplify the ORFs flanked by BZTIRs and SBTIRs, based on the JWW-1 human chimeric antibody vector (Addgene, #66748). These constructs included an expression cassette for a human chimeric IgG1 HPV16 L2-specific neutralizing antibody. The lengths of pBZ-JWW1 and pSB-JWW1 within the TIRs were ∼8.1 kb. To further optimize the donor plasmid, we compared the efficiency of two vector frameworks. The first, pBZ/STDP-CAR, contains a BCMA-CAR expression cassette within the BZ 5′ and 3′ TIRs, along with ∼1600 bp of additional sequences, including a resistance gene and an ori sequence. The second, pBZ/MDP-CAR, also contains the same BCMA-CAR expression cassette and TIRs, but features a different framework. Based on the pCpGfree vector (Invivogene, USA), this construction utilizes the smallest resistance gene, BleoR, and the R6K γ origin, totaling ∼700 bp [18]. The schematic representations are provided in Supplementary Fig. S4C.

Plasmids used for transposase engineering

The original BZ transposase sequence was codon-optimized for mammalian cell transposition assays using the Integrated DNA Technologies database and designated as BZwt (https://login.idtdna.com/CodonOpt). All mutants were constructed based on BZwt, while the ZIP optimization assay was performed on BZ206. Additionally, ZIP motifs from the human CEBPα (TQQKVLELTSDNDRLRKRVEQLSRELDTLRGG), CREB3 (LQNKVQRLEEQNLSLLDQLRKLQAMVIEI), CREPT (KDVLSEKEKKLEEYKQKLARV), and TEF transcription factors (LKENQITIRAAFLEKENTALRTEVAELRKEVGKCKT) [26–28] were appended to the N-terminal ends of BZ206 transposase, yielding BZ206CEBPα, BZ206CREPT, BZ206TEF, and BZ206CREB3, respectively.

Single-site BZ mutants were developed by conducting a conservation analysis of previously collected transposase sequences [23, 24] and homologous proteins identified within the InterPro database (IPR007889 and IPR018586). Additionally, some mutants were designed to ascertain specific characteristics based on their similarity to SB100X transposase, such as F188 and K249. The alignment between BZwt and SB100X was generated using CLUSTALW embedded in BioEdit software and subsequently employed to highlight secondary structures of the BZwt transposase with ESPript3.0 [29]. Moreover, based on the amino acid sequence, the interaction structure of BZ transposase and BZTIR was predicted by AlphaFold 3 (https://www.alphafoldserver.com) [30], which facilitated the identification of surface residues likely to interact with DNA. Collectively, these residues, derived from conservation and structural surface analysis, were designated as our target mutation sites. To develop robust BZ variants, multiple-point mutants were designed based on the single mutation results. Mutant variants were generated by PCR amplification of the desired single mutation point, followed by the construction of these fragments into a vector using the Mut Express® II Fast Mutagenesis Kit V2 (Vazyme, China).

Transposase coding sequences and their variants were integrated into plasmid backbones, controlled by either the CAG or EF1α promoter, and terminated with a polyA signal to construct the helper plasmids. The highly active SB (SB100X) and PB (hyPB) transposon systems were similarly engineered for validation purposes.

Transposase messenger RNAs

The transposase messenger RNAs (mRNAs) were synthesized following an established protocol and used for both transposition assays and CAR-T cell modification [31, 32]. To enhance mRNA stability, a CAG Trimer RNA cap structure analog (m7(G(5′)ppp(5′)(2′OMeA)pG), Vazyme, GMP4118PC) and N1-methyl pseudouridine (N1-MepUTP; Hongen Biotech, R5-027) were used. Prior to in vitro transcription (IVT), plasmid templates encoding transposase were linearized with NheI (BestEnzyme, EG15552S) and purified using multimodal chromatography with Capto Core 700 (Cytiva, 17548103). The concentration was then adjusted using tangential flow filtration (TFF) with a KR2i system (Repligen) and a 30 kDa hollow fiber membrane (Repligen, D02-E030-05-N). IVT was conducted at 37°C for 4 h with 200 μg of linearized plasmid per 10 ml of reaction mixture. Following IVT, plasmid templates were removed using DNase I (Vazyme, GMP4104PC), and the solution was buffer-exchanged for the capping reaction using TFF with a 100 kDa hollow fiber membrane (Repligen, D02-E100-05-N). The capping reaction employed the Vaccinia virus capping enzyme (Novoprotein, GMP-M062-M010) and 2′-O-methyltransferase (Novoprotein, GMP-M072-M010) in the presence of S-adenosylmethionine (SAM; Novoprotein, GMP-S062-M005). The resulting mRNAs were purified using Oligo(dT)25 affinity chromatography columns (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A47384) and the ÄKTA pilot 600S chromatography system (Cytiva). Their quality was verified using the Fragment Analyzer 5200 (Agilent Technologies). The overall yield was 8–10 mg per ml of reaction volume.

Cell culturing

HeLa and HepG2 cells were maintained in DMEM (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium, Corning #10-013-CVR) supplemented with 10% FBS (Fetal Bovine Serum, Gibco #10091148) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco #15140122). Similarly, CHO-K1 cells were cultured in F12K medium (Gibco #21127022) supplemented with 10% FBS. Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated using Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation from whole blood obtained from healthy donors. The PBMCs were cryogenically preserved at a density of 2 × 107 cells/ml using CELLSAVING (NCM Biotech #C40100) until needed. Human T cells were cultured in AIM-V medium (Gibco #0870112DK) supplemented with 2% FBS and 100 U/ml IL-2 (Quangang Pharmaceutical #S200220004), maintaining a cell density between 5 × 105 and 1 × 106 cells/ml. T cell activation was performed using T Cell TransACT (Miltenyi Biotec #130-111-160) at a 1:1 ratio of T cells to beads. Target tumor cells, including H929 and Raji, were cultured in RPMI-1640 (Gibco #11875093) supplemented with 10% FBS. All cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2.

Transposition assay in CHO-K1 via GFP detection

Transposition activities of BZ mutants were conducted in CHO-K1 cells by nucleofection using the 2B-Nucleofector Reagent (Lonza, #VVPA-1002) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, the CHO-K1 cells were co-transfected with a donor plasmid containing a GFP expression cassette bordered by BZ TIRs and a helper plasmid at a 1:1 ratio (2 μg + 2 μg). A control group was transfected with only the donor plasmid. The cells were then cultured for 2 weeks to assess the stability of GFP expression. GFP expression was analyzed via fluorescence microscopy (OLYMPUS IX73) and flow cytometry (Cytek Northern Lights #NL1213), with passaging every 3 days.

Transposition assay in HeLa cells by the resistance gene selection

To detect the transposition activities of BZ mutants, a dual-plasmid system was utilized, adhering to methodologies established in previous research [25, 33]. This system comprised a donor plasmid containing a neomycin resistance gene (Neo) expression cassette flanked by BZTIR (pBZ-PGK-Neo) and a helper plasmid encoding the transposase (pLoxP-BZ). Specifically, 3 × 105 HeLa cells were seeded into six-well plates 1 day prior to transfection to achieve ∼80% confluence on the day of transfection. The HeLa cells were co-transfected with the donor and helper plasmids (500 ng each) using FuGENE® HD Transfection Reagent (Promega, USA) at a 3:1 reagent-to-plasmid ratio. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, the cells were trypsinized, and 1% of the transfected cells were replated onto 10 cm plates. The cells were then cultured in selective medium containing 600 μg/ml G418 (Gibco #10131027) for 2 weeks to facilitate drug-resistant colony formation, thereby enabling evaluation of transposition efficiency. The G418-resistant colonies were stained with Giemsa reagent (Solarbio, #G1010) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and counted using ImageJ.

Overproduction inhibition assay of BZ mutants

To compare the inhibition of the overproduction inhibition (OPI) phenomenon in the SB100X, hyPB, BZwt, and BZ325 transposon systems, transposition activity was tested at both high and low donor plasmid dosages using the previously described method. For the high-dose group, 500 ng of donor plasmids were co-transfected with 0, 50, 500, and 1000 ng of helper plasmids into HeLa cells cultured in six-well plates. For the low-dose group, 10 ng of donor plasmids were co-transfected with 0, 5, 50, and 1000 ng of helper plasmids into HeLa cells. To standardize the total DNA dosage, an empty pCAG vector was included in each transfection. After 48 h post-transfection, the cells were trypsinized and replated onto 10 cm plates, seeding 10% of transfected cells for 10 ng donor plasmid and 1% for 500 ng donor plasmid. The cells were subsequently selected in a 600 μg/ml G418 medium. Following a 2-week selection period, G418-resistant colonies were stained with Giemsa and quantified using ImageJ.

Cargo capacity assay of BZ mutants

In HeLa cells, the cargo capacity of BZ325 and BZwt was assessed using the aforementioned transposition method. A total of 500 ng of donor plasmids containing 1.6, 3.1, 5.1, and 8.5 kb fragments and 500 ng of helper plasmids (BZ325 and BZwt) were co-transfected into HeLa cells to evaluate the cargo capacity of various BZ transposition systems.

To further investigate cargo capacity, an ∼8.1 kb sequence between BZTIRs, including pBZ-JWW1 and pSB-JWW1, was co-transfected into CHO-K1 cells with helper mRNA (BZ325, BZ326, BZ327, BZwt, and SB100X) using 2B-Nucleofector Reagent (Lonza #VVPA-1002). Briefly, 1.5 × 106 cells were collected to electroporate the transposon DNA and transposase mRNA (4 μg + 20 μg). JWW1 expression levels were subsequently quantified using flow cytometry. The BD Cytofix/Cytoperm™ Plus Fixation/Permeabilization Kit (with BD GolgiStop™ protein transport inhibitor containing monensin) and PE mouse anti-human IgG (BD Biosciences, USA) were used to detect the positive rate according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were then cultured to assess JWW1 expression levels at 6 and 14 days, which were analyzed via fluorescence microscopy (OLYMPUS IX73) and flow cytometry (Cytek Northern Lights #NL1213).

Transposition assay and cell phenotype assay in T cells

T cells were isolated from human PBMCs by using CliniMACS® CD8 Reagent (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) and CliniMACS® CD4 Reagent (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany). Subsequently, electroporation of human primary T cells was performed 2 days post-stimulation to evaluate the transposition activities of BZ mutants using a Lonza 4D electroporation system, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Initially, 7 × 106 T cells were nucleofected using the corresponding kit.

To compare the standard donor plasmid (STDP) and minimized donor plasmid (MDP) formats and dosages, mixtures were formed by combining either the pBZ/STDP-CAR or pBZ/MDP-CAR transposon vector with BZ327 mRNA. Four groups were used to compare the plasmid formats and mRNA dosages: BZ327STDP + 10 μg, BZ327STDP + 20 μg, BZ327MDP + 10 μg, and BZ327MDP + 20 μg. Similarly, the BCMA-CAR modification assay and the BZ transposase optimization assay used comparable methods, with the transposon plasmid and transposase mRNA electrotransfected at a ratio of 5 μg : 20 μg. Electroporation was conducted using program F1-115 of the Lonza 4D electroporation system. After electroporation, cells were promptly resupplied with medium and transferred from the cuvettes to six-well plates for culture in the aforementioned standard medium.

CAR expression levels were quantified using flow cytometry. Initially, T cells were washed and incubated with specific antibodies on ice for 30 min. After two additional washes, the samples were processed using either a Cytek Northern Lights (model NL1213) or a CytoFLEX Flow Cytometer (Beckman Coulter), and the data were analyzed using FlowJo_V10 or Kaluza v2.1 software. Specific antibodies used included BD Pharmingen™ PE Streptavidin (BD Biosciences, USA) and Biotinylated Human BCMA/TNFRSF17 Trimer Protein (BCM-HM417B) (KACTU Bio, China) for BCMA-CAR detection at 6 and 13 days post-electrotransfection. Meanwhile, T cell phenotypes were detected using Anti-human CD3 PE-Cyn5/CD4 PE/CD8 FITC Cocktail (Biolgend, USA), PC7 Anti-Human CD197 (CCR7) (Biolgend, USA) and Brilliant Violet 421 Anti-Human CD45RA (Biolegend, USA) at 9 days post eletrotransfection.

Cytotoxicity assay and cytokine assay in T cells

To assess the function and safety of CAR-T cells, cytotoxicity assay and cytokine assay were performed. Specifically, cytotoxicity assay protocol were described as below: (i) Tumor cell plating: Viable Raji-C61-luc (BCMA+) and H929-luc cells were resuspended in AIM-V medium with 10% FBS (2 × 104 cells/100 μl). The suspension was inoculated into 96-well flat white plates (100 μl/ well). Edge wells were discarded and filled with 150 μl Dulbecco's Phosphate-Buffered Saline (DPBS) to eliminate edge effects. (ii) CAR-T cell inoculation: Day 9 CAR-T cells were centrifuged, resuspended in AIM-V with 10% FBS, and diluted to target concentrations. Effector-to-target (E:T) ratios (4:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2) were prepared. CAR-T suspensions (50 ul) were added to tumor cell wells. (iii) Incubation: Plates were co-culture for 24 h, maintaining 5% CO2 and 37°C during this period. (iv) Relative cytolysis measurement: Supernatant was removed by inverting and shaking plates. DPBS (80 μl) and One-Glo™ Luciferase Assay System reagent (80 μl) were added per well. Plates were shaken at 300 rpm for 5 min (protected from light). Chemiluminescence was immediately read using a multilabel microplate detector. Finally, relative killing rates were calculated after data export.

Cytokine assay protocol [according to BD Cytometric Bead Array (CBA) Human Th1/Th2 Cytokine Kit, BD Biosciences, USA] was described as follows: (i) Supernatant collection: Supernatant (≥100 μl) from the cytotoxicity assay (E:T = 1:2 co-culture with H929-luc for 24 h) was collected. (ii) Sample dilution: Supernatant (10 μl) was transferred to a 1.5 ml tube and diluted with 40 μl 1 × Assay Diluent; (iii) Capture Bead preparation: Total Human Cytokine Capture Beads required = (Number of samples × 50 μl)/6 (Reagents A1–A6). Beads were mixed and aliquoted (50 μL) into 1.5 ml tubes; (iv) Detection reagent addition: Human Th1/Th2-ⅡPE Detection Reagent (50 μl) was added to each tube and incubated at room temperature for 3 h; (v) Wash and analysis: Wash Buffer (1 ml) was added. Tubes were centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min, supernatant discarded. Pellets were resuspended in 300 μl Wash Buffer. Samples were analyzed by flow cytometry using FCAP Arrayv3 software.

Insertion site analysis

The insertion site libraries of the BZ transposon in HepG2 cells for Illumina sequencing were prepared as described in a previous study [23]. Briefly, HepG2 genomic DNA was isolated from resistant colonies transfected with either BZ donor plasmid (pBZ-PGK-Neo) along with a corresponding helper plasmid (pLoxP-BZ325/326/327/303/206). After 48 h transfection, the cells were then selected in 800 μg/ml G418 medium for 14 days. Approximately 15 000 resistant colonies from each group were harvested and processed for genomic DNA extraction using the TIANamp Genomic DNA kit (TIANGEN #4992254).

Subsequently, 1.5 μg of HepG2 DNA was sheared to a median size of 500 ± 50 bp using a Covaris M220 instrument and purified with Ampure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter, Inc.). Biotinylated primers specific to the BZ transposon carrier (Shanghai Diyue Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) were utilized for primer extension PCR with Phanta Max Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.). The amplification products were purified using Ampure XP magnetic beads, and the target DNA fragments were captured with Dynabeads™ M-280 Streptavidin magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The captured DNA was ligated with adapters containing barcode sequences using BioSci T4 DNA Ligase (Shanghai Dacobio Technology Co., Ltd.), ensuring that each sample had a unique barcode for differentiation.

After ligation, the product underwent nested PCR. The biotinylated PCR products were captured, enriched, and washed with magnetic beads. Half of the eluate was used as the template for a second nested PCR with Champagne Taq DNA Polymerase (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.) to attach the second set of barcodes and specific adapters. Agarose gel electrophoresis was employed for quality control of the PCR library. The remaining PCR products were purified and adjusted to a concentration of 50 ng/sample using Qubit 4.0 (Thermo Fisher Technology Co., Ltd.). The mixed library was further assessed for quality with TapeStation 4150 (Agilent Technologies, Inc) and Qubit 4.0. Finally, the samples were diluted to 10 pM with EB buffer (Shanghai Yuan Ye Biological Technology Co., Ltd.) and sequenced on a MiSeq sequencer (Illumina, Inc.). All primers used are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

To ensure high-quality insertion sites, we applied a rigorous data preprocessing protocol, referenced from a previous study. High-quality reads were aligned to the hg38 human genome reference using the BWA algorithm (version 0.7.17-r1188) to pinpoint insertion locations. To characterize the features and genomic safe harbors (GSHs) of insertion sites, we referred to and combined data from previous studies, including BZwt (or ZB), SB100X, and SB100X-H187V [17, 23]. GSHs were identified based on five stringent criteria: (i) minimum 50 kb distance from the 5′ end of any protein-coding gene; (ii) minimum 300 kb separation from known cancer-associated genes; (iii) minimum 300 kb distance from microRNA loci; (iv) location outside any transcription unit; and (iv) exclusion from ultraconserved genomic regions. These parameters were established to ensure minimal risk of insertional mutagenesis and disruption of critical genetic elements [34, 35]. The coordinates of genic features were downloaded from the UCSC Table Browser, and the cancer gene catalog was retrieved from the Bushman lab repository. The Genomation package was used to analyze the representation of insertion sites across different genic categories [17]. The frequency of insertion sites of SB, BZ, and their variants was compared with 100 000 computationally generated random loci in the human genome to assess their distribution.

Insertion copy number of Neo gene

Genomic DNA from HepG2 cells transfected with the BZ transposon system was extracted to determine the insertion copy number of the neomycin gene using digital droplet PCR (ddPCR). Briefly, 200 ng of genomic DNA was digested with 20 units of DpnI enzyme (New England Biolabs) in a 30 μl reaction volume to eliminate non-integrated plasmid DNA at 37°C overnight. The reaction mixture was then fragmented using CviQI for 4 h at 25°C. The samples underwent PCR amplification with probes and specific primers for the neomycin gene and the single-copy RPP30 gene to measure the genome count. The PCR reactions were conducted in a final volume of 20 μl with 10 ng of fragmented DNA using the ddPCR Supermix for Probes (No dUTP) master mix (Bio-Rad), along with 900 nM primers and 250 nM probes. PCR droplets were generated using the QX200 system (Bio-Rad). The thermal cycling program consisted of 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 94°C for 30 s and 60°C for 1 min, with a final step at 98°C for 10 min. Fluorescent droplets were ultimately counted using the QX200 Droplet Reader (Bio-Rad), and genomic copy numbers were calculated using QuantaSoft Software (Bio-Rad).

Results

Screening of highly active BZ variants by evaluating the conservation of both BZ and SB transposases

The BZ and SB transposon systems share a common structural organization, and their transposases exhibit a close phylogenetic relationship [23, 24]. Consequently, mutants of the BZ transposase (Supplementary Table S1) were engineered based on an alignment of BZwt and SB100X transposases (Fig. 1A) and subsequently screened for hyperactive variants using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), utilizing both donor and helper vectors (Supplementary Fig. S1A). A subset of BZ mutants demonstrated enhanced transposition activity (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, these mutants were combined (Supplementary Table S1) and further assessed for transposition activity by FACS. Most combinations revealed augmented transposition efficiency, although some showed reduced activity (Fig. 1C). Significantly, the Q71R\H110R mutant demonstrated a marked upsurge in transposition activity and GFP expression level (Fig. 1C). The same phenomenon was assessed by FACS in CHO-K1 and PBMC cells (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. S1A) and confirmed by G418 screening in HeLa cells (Fig. 1E) in binary transposition assays employing donor and helper plasmids (Supplementary Fig. S1B).

Additionally, the BZ transposon’s TIRs (201 bp) and TSDs (TA), which were essential for transposition activity, were modified to evaluate their effects on the transposition function [36]. Six TSD and TIR variant combinations within donor vectors were designed, constructed (Supplementary Fig. S2A), and analyzed for transposition activity using binary transposition assays. The data indicated that increasing the number of TSDs and TIRs did not enhance activity, while donor vectors with the configurations 1TA1ITIR–1TA1TIR or 2TA2TIR displayed decrease transposition activity (Supplementary Fig. S2B and C). Hence, the donor plasmid with 1TA-1TIR was used in subsequent assays.

Efforts to engineer BZ transposase for more efficient and safer integration

Leveraging the hyperactive Q71R\H110R variant, three strategies were implemented to enhance the efficiency and safety of the BZ transposon system in mammalian cells. First, we focused on the KTPLLN motif within the linker region of BZ transposase that bridges the DNA-binding and catalytic domains. This motif shows high conservation among Tc1 transposases (Fig. 2A) and is structurally analogous to the WVPHEL motif central to the function of mariner transposases [37], as evidenced by the critical role of WVPHEL in transpososome cleavage processes [38]. Saturation mutagenesis of the W, V, E, and L residues in Mos1 transposase identified several hyperactive variants [39]. Targeted saturation mutagenesis of the R120 and N125 residues in BZ transposase’s KTPLLN motif and subsequent transposition assays identified two variants (N125H and N125Y) that significantly increased transposition activity, as determined by G418 selection (Fig. 2B). Secondly, inspired by work from Miskey et al. [17], we modified key residues within the DDE domain known for interacting with target DNA and influencing integration profiles, to evaluate changes in target site preferences and genome-wide integration in BZ transposase. The alignment of Mos1, SB100X, and BZwt was generated by CLUSTALW and then used to highlight secondary structures of the Mos1 transposase (PDB ID:4U7B) with ESPript 3.0 [29] (top of Fig. 2C). Subsequently, the superposition of the BZ prediction model (purple) with the SB TCC (target capture complex, green, PDB ID: 5CR4) or the Mos1 STC (strand transfer complex, pink, PDB ID:5HOO) showed that F188 in BZ aligns with R186 in Mos1, and K249 in BZ aligns with K248 in SB (bottom of Fig. 2C). Moreover, mutagenesis of conserved residues F188 and K249, analogous to H187 and K248 in SB100X, was performed (Fig. 2C). Variants designed by saturation mutagenesis were analyzed for transposition activity and integration patterns, revealing six mutations that maintained (F188E, F188K, F188M, F188P, F188V) or enhanced (F188G) transposition activity, while others showed reduced efficiency (top of Fig. 2D). Notably, all mutants from K249 showed a significant decrease trend, demonstrating that K249 was a crucial site in the transposition progress (bottom of Fig. 2D). Thirdly, we investigated the impact of fusing leucine zipper (ZIP) motifs from different basic ZIP transcription factors, which constitute an important class of eukaryotic DNA-binding proteins, where dimerization occur by way of coiled-coil regions [26], to the N-terminus of Q71R\H110R transposase. Four variants were created using ZIP motifs derived from CEBPα, CREB3, CREPT, and TEF transcription factors, resulting in Q71R\H110RCEBPα, Q71R\H110RCREPT, Q71R\H110RTEF, and Q71R\H110RCREB3, respectively (Fig. 2E). Transposition assays indicated that Q71R\H110RCEBPα and Q71R\H110RCREB3 significantly outperformed Q71R\H110R variant, whereas Q71R\H110RCREPT and Q71R\H110RTEF showed minimal enhancements (Fig. 2F and Supplementary Fig. S3A). Ultimately, new combined variants were synthesized, leading to markedly improved transposition activities in most combinations relative to Q71R\H110R (named BZ206), particularly with variants CEBPO-Q71R\H110R\N125Y, CEBPO-Q71R\H110R\N125Y\F188K, and CEBPO-Q71R\H110R\N125Y\F188G which named BZ325, BZ326, and BZ327 standing out as the top performers (Fig. 2G and Supplementary Fig. S3B and C). All sites of combined mutants were listed in Supplementary Fig. S3B.

Altered target site preferences of F188K and F188G transposase mutants

Mutations at three critical amino acid positions in the SB100X transposase—particularly H187 and K248—have been shown to significantly influence its target site preferences and genome-wide insertion patterns, implicating these residues in DNA interaction [17]. In our study, we found that substituting phenylalanine at position 188 (F188), the equivalent of H187 in SB100X, similarly altered the target site selection behavior of the BZ transposase. SeqLogo analysis of BZ wild-type (BZwt) and non-F188-mutant variants (BZ303 and BZ325) revealed moderate conservation of adenine at the −3 position and thymine at the +3 position within the consensus sequence, with conservation scores ranging from 0.5 to 0.6 bits. In contrast, F188-mutant variants (BZ326 and BZ327) exhibited markedly increased conservation at these positions (up to ∼1.0 bit), mirroring the trend observed in SB100X engineering (SB100X: ∼0.5 bit; SB100X-H187V: ∼1.5 bit; Fig. 3A). Additionally, the frequency of transposon insertions into the preferred octamer sequence “ATATATAT” notably shifted upon mutating F188 in BZ transposases. While the BZwt, BZ303, and SB100X target the sequence at about a 2% frequency, and BZ325 at ∼3%, the F188 mutants demonstrated substantially higher integration frequencies—around 10% for BZ326, 14% for BZ327, and 19% for SB100X-H187V (Fig. 3A). This suggests that mutants at F188 site altered target site preferences. The mutants BZ303, BZ325, BZ326, and BZ327 exhibited distinct genome-wide distribution profiles compared to BZwt. Data from Fig. 3B and Supplementary Files S3–S11 indicated a pronounced decrease in insertional events within genes, introns, and exons, particularly with the BZ326 and BZ327 mutants. Although the integration frequencies by BZ326 and BZ327 within these genomic regions were found to be only marginally different from those of BZ325. BZ327 exhibit significant decrease in cancer genes. Moreover, our analysis showed that the three BZ mutants, BZ325, BZ326, and BZ327 had a significant elevation in insertion frequency into GSHs compared to BZwt (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. S4A) with P-value < 0.001, meeting all five GSH criteria outlined in the methods section [17]. The frequency of integrations into GSHs among these three mutants BZ325, BZ326, and BZ327 remained relatively consistent (∼22%, Supplementary Fig. S4B).

Significant improvements in the transposition efficiency by BZ transposase engineering

To accurately assess the transposition efficiency of the engineered BZ transposases, we examined the phenomenon of OPI in BZ wild-type (BZwt) and BZ325, alongside comparisons with SB100X and hyPB. OPI, a behavior commonly observed in various cut-and-paste DNA transposons, was also evident in BZwt and BZ325 in HeLa cells, exposed to high (500 ng) and low (10 ng) concentrations of donor plasmids. Notably, the response patterns of BZ transposases closely paralleled those of SB100X transposases. At both plasmid concentrations, all transposases except hyPB reached their activity apex using 50 ng of the transposase plasmid. HyPB transposase, however, peaked at a markedly lower dosage, requiring just 5 ng (Fig. 4A and B). Modification of the BZ transposase significantly enhanced its transposition activity, with BZ325 outperforming BZwt at both donor plasmid dosages. At optimum activity levels, BZ325 surpassed BZwt by ∼1.2-fold and 2.3-fold at the 500 and 10 ng dosages, respectively (Fig. 4C). Additionally, BZ325 demonstrated ∼30% higher transposition activity compared to SB100X at the high plasmid dose, though it was 5% less active than hyPB (Fig. 4A). Conversely, at the low dose, BZ325’s activity was on par with SB100X and hyPB when at their peak efficiency (Fig. 4D). BZ325 and BZ327 insertion copy numbers averaged ∼17 and 15 per HeLa cell genome, respectively. These figures exceed those observed for BZwt (around 8), SB100X (roughly 12), and hyPB (∼9) (Fig. 4E), while similar excision footprints of fewer than 3 bp (CA/TG) were recorded for BZwt and BZ mutants (Fig. 4F).

Significant improvement of cargo capacity by BZ transposase engineering

The cargo capacities of BZwt and BZ325 were compared to those of PS and cgSpy. All four transposon systems utilized the same backbones for the donor and helper plasmids. However, there were variations in the transposase encoding sequences of the helper plasmids and the TIRs derived from different transposon systems (as depicted in Fig. 5A). Our findings indicated that the cargo capacity of the engineered BZ transposase was significantly improved. In the case of BZ325, the transposition activity dropped to 75%, 51%, and 24% as the insertion size increased to 3.1, 5.1, and 8.5 kb, respectively. BZwt exhibited a similar trend, with transposition activity dropping to 48%, 31%, and 16% in the same size ranges (Fig. 5B and C). On average, the transposition efficiency of BZ325 decreased by ∼11% for each kilobase (kb) increase in insertion size, while BZwt, PS, and cgSpy transposases experienced a drop of ∼12%. Meanwhile, a ∼14% decrease in hyPB transposase activity was observed for each kilobase pair increase in transposon length (Fig. 5D and E, and Supplementary Fig. S4D and E). In terms of cargo capacity, BZ325 transposase generally outperformed BZwt, PS, hyPB, and cgSpy transposases as the insertion size increased to 3.1, 5.1, and 8.5 kb, respectively, with the exception of cgSpy, which exhibited minimal decrease in transposition activity (down to 96%) as the insertion size increased to 3.1 kb (Fig. 5D and E).

To further evaluate the impact of large insertion size on the transposition efficiency of BZ mutants, we used a BZ donor vector, containing a 8.1 kb transgene, which includes the JWW1 human chimeric antibody expression box within TIRs (Fig. 5F). The same design was utilized for the SB transposon system, and a parallel comparison was performed. Generally, the integration rates of BZwt, which were measured on the 6th and 14th days using a flow cytometry assay, as described in the methods, were found to be similar to SB100X (∼5% integration rate). However, the integration rates of BZ325 and BZ326 measured on the 6th day were significantly higher than those of BZwt and SB100X (Fig. 5G). Simultaneously, the integration rates of BZ mutants (BZ326 and BZ327) measured on the 14th day were significantly higher than those of BZwt and on par with SB100X in CHO-K1 cells (Fig. 5G).

The efficiency of CAR-T cell modification significantly improved by reducing the size of the BZ donor plasmid

Previous studies suggested that reducing the donor vector size of SB transposon can improve the efficiency of CAR-T modification (named as minicircle donor plasmid, SB MC) [21]. Here we applied a similar technique decreasing the length of vector backbone (named as mini-donor plasmid, MDP) to evaluate its application potential for CAR-T generation mediated with BZ transposon system. The MDP, ∼700 bp in vector backbone with BleoR and R6K γ origin from pCpGfree vector [18], was developed and tested in mammal cells for efficient gene transfer and expression. The design of standard-donor plasmid (STDP), used as a control (∼1.6 kb in vector backbone), and the MDP of BZ transposon are depicted in Supplementary Fig. S4C. T cells isolated from PBMCs were co-transfected with either pBZ/STDP-CAR (5 μg) or pBZ/MDP-CAR (5 μg), along with BZ engineered transposase (BZ327) mRNA (10 or 20 μg). Subsequently, CAR modification efficacy, CAR-T cell proliferation, and CAR-T cell viability were assessed by employing flow cytometry. We found that decreasing the size of donor plasmid can significantly improve the BZ-transposon-mediated CAR-T modification efficiency. Particularly, at high dosage (20 μg) of transposase mRNA, the efficiency of CAR-T cells generated by BZ327MDP was significantly higher than those generated by BZ327STDP at both D6 and D13 post transfection (P< 0.05), while the difference between them was minimal at the low dosage (10 μg) of transposase mRNA (Fig. 6A). Moreover, CAR-T cell expansion and viability showed no significant differences between BZ327MDP and BZ327STDP at either 10 or 20 µg transposase doses, indicating that plasmid formats (STDP versus MDP) and transposase dosages did not affect CAR-T cell proliferation or viability (Fig. 6B and C).

Discussion

Cut-and-paste DNA transposons, notably as gene delivery agents, offer significant promise for gene therapy due to their ease of manufacture, reduced cytotoxicity, and larger cargo capacity in comparison to viral vectors. It is crucial to identify novel DNA transposons to expand the array of gene delivery tools. Highly active transposons such as PB, SB, ZB, PS, Tcbuster, and cgSpy have been discovered and evaluated for their gene transfer efficiencies in both gene therapy and transgenesis [23, 25, 33, 40–42]. Concurrently, refining these discovered DNA transposons— including PB, SB, and Tcbuster—and advancing them into more sophisticated, secure gene manipulation tools is imperative [10, 12, 13, 16]. This is to achieve integration efficiency comparable to, but safer than, viral vectors. The BZ transposon system, derived from zebrafish (Danio rerio), has demonstrated significant potential for transgenesis, mutagenesis, and human gene therapy applications due to its robust transpositional activity and relatively random genomic integration profile. However, comparative analyses reveal that BZ exhibits a modest but consistent integration bias toward genic regions, including gene bodies, enhancers, active transcriptional start sites, and open chromatin domains, when contrasted with the SB100X transposon system [23]. Engineering enhancements in the current study have thus been implemented to amplify its efficiency, cargo capacity, and safety for application in human gene therapy.

Transposase engineering toward more safety in gene therapy

The endogenous mechanism of transposons, commonly known as jumping genes, has been adapted for genetic engineering to facilitate gene insertion into chromosomes. Nonetheless, in human therapeutic applications, indiscriminate insertion by transposases poses significant risks, including genomic instability, oncogene activation, or disruption of endogenous genes. Consequently, developing a reliable and controlled integration system is imperative for clinical use. Two primary approaches have been considered to refine transposases for safer genomic integration in human cells. The first involves engineering transposases to target specific DNA sequences. Modification of the transposase’s DNA-binding domain enables recognition of particular genomic sites, which could mitigate negative effects of gene insertion. Utilizing directed evolution, supported by computational modeling, can lead to transposase variants with specific attributes. Studies of SB100X and retroviral integrases have identified crucial residues—namely H187, P247, and K248—that may influence targeted integration through proximity with target DNA [17]. By altering these residues, the prevalence of SB integrases in noncoding regions has increased, significantly enhancing insertion frequency at GSHs. The present study identified two analogous positions, F188 and K249, in the BZ transposase, which may interact similarly with target DNA. Mutants F188K in BZ326 and F188G in BZ327 demonstrated enhanced transposition activity with distinct target site preferences and integration profiles when compared to BZ206 and BZwt. This substantiates their involvement in DNA binding and suggests a potential route to augmenting genomic integration safety for gene therapy applications. However, F188 modifications in BZ transposase resulted in a less pronounced shift in target site and integration profile compared to their SB100X counterparts (H187). Nonetheless, further assessment of additional mutants—including F188A, F188D, F188E, F188M, and F188P, which show a marginal reduction in transposition activity—could yield safer integration prospects. Furthermore, a highly soluble variant of Sleeping Beauty transposase, engineered using crystal structural modeling [37], demonstrated increased solubility and stability, thereby offering potential benefits in control and safety within therapeutic genome engineering. This soluble transposase represents a promising protocol that may also be applicable to BZ transposase, meriting future exploration.

The second strategy entails the creation of fusion proteins combining transposases with specific DNA-binding domains from other proteins, such as zinc fingers, TALENs, or Cas proteins, to enhance the accuracy of gene insertion [43, 44]. Such precision could translate to more predictable and innocuous integrations. Both PB and SB transposases have been tethered with catalytically inactive Cas proteins from the CRISPR/Cas system to direct transposon integration [45, 46]. Although these constructs have redirected transposon integration, the efficiency of the intended insertions remains notably low, indicating a need for improvement. An innovative approach has involved engineering a transposase that is incapable of integration but preserves, or even enhances, excision capabilities (named as Exc+/Int− transposase) [47]. When fused with an active Cas protein, this Exc+/Int− transposase variant enabled targeted integration, albeit with limited efficiency and inconsistent performance across different mammalian cell types. Actually, an Exc+/Int−SB transposase (excision active and integration dead) has been obtained by a single amino acid switching, yielded proficient excision activity in stem cells [48], suitable for conjugation with Cas proteins for precise insertion. Applying this straightforward strategy to BZ transposase, due to its similarity to SB, appears promising and is worth pursuing in future investigations.

Significant improvement of transposition activity and cargo capacity of BZ transposon system by engineering

The Tc1 and mariner transposase families, both members of the Tc1/mariner superfamily, exhibit phylogenetic proximity, with BZ and SB transposases classified within the Tc1 family [24]. Transposase engineering strategies, previously demonstrated to be effective within this superfamily, hold potential for application to BZ transposase modifications. The WVPHEL motif, a conserved amino acid sequence integral to the transposase dimer interface within the mariner family, has been identified. Saturation mutagenesis of Mos1’s WVPHEL motif resulted in predominantly hyperactive variants, indicating the functional involvement of linker motif in transpososome assembly and catalytic initiation [39]. A structurally analogous motif, KKPLLS, was recognized in the Tc1 family [37], with the BZ transposase containing the corresponding KTPLLN sequence. Notwithstanding, saturation mutagenesis at positions K120 and N125 in BZ’s KTPLLN motif, equivalent to the W and L in Mos1’s motif, yielded merely two hyperactive variants, which contrasts with outcomes from the mariner family [39] and suggests differential motif functionalities across the Tc1 family.

SB transposase has undergone successful engineering to enhance both efficiency and safety, as exemplified by SB100X, whose transpositional activity surpassed its predecessor by 100-fold [12, 17]. Through conservation analysis of BZ and SB100X transposases, eleven BZ variants with augmented activities were engineered, including a notably hyperactive combined mutant, BZ206. The integration of two ZIPs, proteins known to facilitate leucine zipper transcription factor dimerization [26], with transposases resulted in elevated transposition activity, implying ZIPs’ potential to similarly enhance transposase dimerization. Ultimately, this integrative engineering approach yielded several BZ transposase hyperactive mutants (BZ325, BZ326, and BZ327), exhibiting activities on par with hyPB and SB100X, and advancing both cargo capacity and genomic integration safety.

Application potential of transposase and transposon donor vector engineering in CAR-T modification efficiency

Advanced technologies for decreasing the size of donor vectors, such as Minicircle DNA, have been successfully employed for the genetic modification of CAR-T [49] and CAR-NK [50] cells, human hematopoietic stem cells [51], and for creating transgenic mice [52], with applications extending to clinical trials for CAR-T cell therapy [53]. Utilizing the minicircle-based approach in conjunction with the SB transposon system has evidenced improved transfection efficiency, prolonged stable gene expression, and minimized cellular toxicity, underscoring its considerable advantages and potential in immunotherapy. Our current research corroborates that diminishing the donor plasmid backbone size substantially enhances CAR-T cell modification, proliferation, and viability, with reduced transposase mRNA dosages (20 μg). Moreover, the data indicate that engineered BZ transposases significantly outperform the wild-type counterpart in generating CAR-T cells, with up to a two-fold increase in efficiency. Mutants BZ325, BZ326, and BZ327 likewise show markedly improved CAR gene expression levels relative to the wild type, signifying substantial advancements in transposon system efficiency through meticulous vector and enzyme engineering.
