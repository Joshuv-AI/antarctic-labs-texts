Arthroscopic management of spinoglenoid notch cysts: with and without labral lesions
Authors: Binyang Meng, Zi Zhang, Wenhe Li, Qi Wang, Jiangang Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSuprascapular neuropathy caused by spinoglenoid notch cysts (SGNCs) is a rare condition with unclear etiology, typically presenting with significant shoulder pain and weakness. This case report describes two patients who underwent arthroscopic decompression and cyst excision: one with a labral lesion and the other with an isolated cyst. The patient with labral involvement was treated via the labral repair approach, while the patient without labral pathology underwent a direct decompression of the cyst through the subacromial space. Both patients experienced progressive pain relief and functional recovery during follow-up. This study highlights the safety and effectiveness of arthroscopic management for SGNCs and recommends a tailored surgical approach depending on the presence or absence of labral lesions.Keywords: Ganglion cyst, Spinoglenoid notch, SLAP lesion, Isolated cyst, Cyst decompression, Arthroscopic approach

Introduction

Spinoglenoid Notch Cyst (SGNC) is a relatively rare condition [1]and its pathogenesis remains unclear. The spinoglenoid notch is a small depression on the posterior surface of the scapula through which the suprascapular nerve passes to innervate the infraspinatus muscle [2]. It is widely considered that SGNC is associated with superior labral anterior-posterior (SLAP) lesions [3, 4]. Paralabral cysts resulting from labral tears can extend into the spinoglenoid notch, compressing the suprascapular nerve and leading to infraspinatus dysfunction and atrophy, which manifest as posterior shoulder pain, weakness, and limited function.

Diagnosing SGNC can be challenging, as its physical signs are nonspecific and easily mistaken for other causes of shoulder pain, such as rotator cuff injuries, capsular disorders, or joint diseases [5]. Its clinical presentation is influenced by the size and location of the cyst [6]and there is some correlation with whether the suprascapular nerve is compressed. Isolated SGNCs without nerve involvement may present only with vague posterior shoulder pain. However, when the cyst compresses the nerve and causes infraspinatus atrophy or external rotation weakness, it becomes both diagnostically and therapeutically significant.

It is commonly reported that SGNC cases involve concurrent SLAP lesions, for which arthroscopic labral repair and cyst decompression are the standard treatment. In contrast, there is limited literature on the management of isolated SGNCs without labral pathology. This report presents two cases: one of SGNC with a SLAP lesion, managed with arthroscopic posterior capsular approach and labral repair; and the other an isolated SGNC, treated via a subacromial approach. These cases aim to provide practical insights for selecting the appropriate arthroscopic approach based on labral involvement.

Clinical evaluation

A 35-year-old male was admitted due to “right shoulder pain and weakness for more than 2 months.“Physical examination: Mild muscle atrophy was noted in the right infraspinatus fossa, with mild tenderness. The Jobe test was negative, 0°internal rotation resistance test was negative, 0° external rotation resistance test was positive, 0°abduction resistance test was negative, O’Brien test was negative, and shoulder external rotation strength was grade 4.

MRI findings

The right shoulder showed a spinoglenoid notch cyst.

Surgical Procedure: Arthroscopy was performed through the posterior portal to explore the shoulder joint. Mild hyperemia of the long head of the biceps tendon was observed, with an intact insertion and complete tendon tissue around the biceps tendon sheath. The subscapularis tendon insertion was intact, and no damage was found to the superior glenohumeral ligament. The superior labrum, anterior labrum, and anteroinferior labrum were all intact. The posterior labrum showed mild fraying without obvious tearing, and radiofrequency ablation was performed for smoothing. The arthroscope was then moved to the subacromial space, with the posterior portal used as the viewing portal, and an anterolateral portal was added. A shaver was used to debride the subacromial bursa, exposing the scapular spine. After clearing the local soft tissues, the cyst wall was gradually exposed along the scapular spine. Upon incision of the cyst wall, serosanguineous fluid was observed. The cyst wall tissue was debrided with a shaver, gradually exposing the spinoglenoid notch, where good pulsation of the suprascapular artery was noted.

Postoperative outcome

Shoulder abduction and external rotation strength gradually recovered to grade 5. At the 6-month follow-up, the infraspinatus muscle appeared full, shoulder joint mobility returned to normal, and stability was good. The Constant-Murley score for shoulder function improved from 85 preoperatively to 100 postoperatively, and the visual analog scale (VAS) for pain decreased from 4 to 0. MRI showed complete resolution of the spinoglenoid notch cyst.

Clinical evaluation

A 34-year-old male, military personnel, experienced left shoulder pain following a training injury, affecting his training for two years. Physical examination: No significant muscle atrophy around the left shoulder joint was observed. Tenderness was noted in the anterior joint space of the left shoulder. Range of motion for shoulder elevation and abduction was normal. The Jobe test was positive, 0° internal rotation resistance test was negative, 0° external rotation resistance test was positive, 0° abduction resistance test was positive, and the O’Brien test was positive. Shoulder abduction and external rotation strength were graded as 4.

MRI findings

The left shoulder showed a SLAP lesion with SGNC formation.

Surgical procedure: Arthroscopy was first used to explore the intra-articular structures. A superior labral tear approximately 1 cm in length was observed, classified as a SLAP type II lesion. Mild hyperemia was noted in the long head of the biceps tendon, with intact tendon tissue around the biceps tendon sheath. The subscapularis tendon insertion was intact, with no damage to the superior glenohumeral ligament. Both the anterior and posterior labrum were intact, and no significant abnormalities were found in the joint capsule. The glenoid and humeral head cartilage were also intact.After separating approximately 1 cm medially from the superior posterior labrum, the cyst wall was exposed. Upon incision of the cyst wall, serosanguineous fluid was observed, and the cyst wall tissue was debrided using a shaver, with the medial boundary reaching the scapular spine. A probe was used to explore the spinoglenoid notch, confirming good pulsation of the suprascapular artery. Two 2.9 mm suture anchors were implanted at the superior glenoid tubercle attachment site. The suture tails were passed through the torn labral tissue, and knots were tied to secure the superior labrum. The superior labrum was confirmed to be stable upon inspection, and the procedure was concluded.

Postoperative outcome

Shoulder abduction and external rotation strength gradually recovered to grade 5. At the 6-month follow-up, the infraspinatus muscle appeared full, shoulder joint mobility returned to normal, and stability was good. The Constant-Murley score for shoulder function improved from 80 preoperatively to 100 postoperatively, and the visual analog scale (VAS) for pain decreased from 5 to 0. MRI showed complete resolution of the SGNC, and the SLAP lesion had healed well.

Discussion

Pathophysiology

The exact etiology of SGNC remains unclear, but it is often associated with SLAP lesions. Studies suggest that a superior labral tear in the shoulder joint can create a one-way valve, allowing joint fluid to form a paralabral cyst, which then extends along the soft tissues into the spinoglenoid notch, eventually resulting in SGNC [3, 4, 7]. When the cyst compresses the suprascapular nerve, it can produce typical clinical symptoms, including vague shoulder pain, infraspinatus atrophy, and weakness in external rotation [8]. In the absence of suprascapular nerve compression, symptoms are usually nonspecific, and diagnosing SGNC without magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or ultrasound (US) is challenging [1, 7].

Treatment controversies

There is currently controversy regarding the treatment of asymptomatic SGNC. Some patients with SGNC do not exhibit symptoms of suprascapular nerve compression or SLAP lesions, and they do not experience weakened external rotation of the shoulder, presenting only with nonspecific shoulder pain. Imaging often shows no signs of fat infiltration or infraspinatus atrophy, and regardless of cyst size, continued observation is considered a treatment option [9]. As SGNC compresses the nerve at the spinoglenoid notch, it is considered a space-occupying lesion, and non-surgical treatments have shown poor outcomes [2]. For patients with SGNC who do not respond to conservative treatment, surgery should be considered. Surgical options include radiologically-guided aspiration, open surgical excision, and arthroscopic excision. Percutaneous cyst aspiration is not the preferred treatment, as studies have reported a recurrence rate of 48% and a primary failure rate of 18% two years after aspiration [8]. Open surgery allows for complete excision of the cyst but is more invasive and cannot address concomitant intra-articular pathologies, such as labral tears. Arthroscopic surgery is less invasive and allows for the simultaneous treatment of both the cyst and associated labral lesions [5, 8]. Both arthroscopic and open surgical procedures have been shown to significantly improve symptoms of nerve compression [8, 10, 11]; however, arthroscopic surgery is technically demanding and highly dependent on the surgeon’s experience. In cases where the cyst is not adequately identified or treated during arthroscopy, patients may require open surgery due to persistent symptoms. With the advancement of minimally invasive techniques and shoulder arthroscopy, many open procedures have been replaced by arthroscopic surgery, which has shown satisfactory results [1, 11].

Arthroscopic repair of SLAP lesions to stop the one-way valve mechanism is currently the primary etiological treatment for SGNC. However, there is no consensus on whether cyst decompression should be performed in cases of SGNC associated with SLAP lesions. Schroder CP et al. [12]argue that cyst decompression carries a potential risk of damaging the suprascapular nerve and that only labral repair should be performed. By repairing the SLAP lesion and eliminating the source of the cyst fluid, the cyst is expected to gradually resolve on its own without the need for additional decompression. Hashiguchi et al. [13]suggest that SGNC compresses the suprascapular nerve, and without cyst decompression, the patient may experience adverse outcomes due to nerve damage. WANG et al. [14] believe that in cases where only labral repair is performed without cyst wall excision, blunt dissection of the cyst into the joint allows the cyst fluid to drain into the joint cavity, which may carry a risk of cyst recurrence.

Currently, the most commonly chosen surgical approach is labral repair combined with cyst decompression. While some reports suggest no significant difference in outcomes between labral repair alone and combined repair with decompression [6, 15]we emphasize that in cases with active nerve symptoms, combined treatment is more reliable. When performing labral repair combined with cyst decompression, attention must be paid to avoiding vascular and nerve damage during surgery [16]. As the suprascapular nerve passes near the spinoglenoid notch, it lies closer to the glenohumeral joint, at a distance of 18–21 mm from the glenoid rim [7]. It is recommended that during cyst decompression surgery, cyst dissection be performed only within 1 cm of the glenoid rim of the glenohumeral joint. Based on our clinical experience, during arthroscopic removal of the cyst wall, dissection should be stopped promptly upon observing arterial pulsation. This approach can effectively avoid damage to the suprascapular nerve.

Surgical approach selection

Multiple studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between SGNC and SLAP lesions; however, not all SGNC cases are associated with SLAP lesions. In our two cases, one involved SGNC associated with a labral lesion, while the other was an isolated SGNC without labral involvement. Arthroscopic approaches for cyst excision include the capsulotomy approach through the superior posterior joint capsule and the subacromial approach. The capsulotomy approach requires creating a large defect in the joint capsule to obtain sufficient operative space and optimal visualization of the SGNC. The subacromial approach provides more surgical space and better visualization of the cyst, but it does not allow for addressing the SLAP lesion.

After research and discussion, we believe that for SGNC associated with a SLAP lesion, the capsulotomy approach through the superior posterior joint capsule is more appropriate. This approach allows for cyst decompression by draining the cyst fluid through the communication point of the lesion, while simultaneously repairing the damaged labral structure during surgery. In cases of isolated SGNC, the choice of surgical approach can be based on the surgeon’s intraoperative experience and familiarity with the techniques. However, the subacromial approach provides better intraoperative visualization for cyst decompression and helps avoid damaging the intact joint capsule and labral structures.

Clinical outcomes

Our cases support this position: both patients achieved full pain relief and functional recovery. These results highlight the importance of surgical strategy tailored to lesion type, with an emphasis on addressing both structural damage and nerve decompression when indicated.

Conclusion

In conclusion, arthroscopic management of spinoglenoid notch cysts should be tailored according to the presence or absence of associated labral lesions. For SGNCs accompanied by SLAP lesions, a posterior capsular approach enabling simultaneous labral repair and cyst decompression is recommended. For isolated SGNCs without labral involvement, the subacromial approach provides a safer and more accessible option for effective cyst excision. Our findings support the adoption of individualized arthroscopic strategies to optimize clinical outcomes and minimize nerve-related complications.
