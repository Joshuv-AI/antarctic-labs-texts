Coverage engineering by shell-number-controlled nanoconfinement enables nitric oxide electroreduction in the ppm regime
Authors: Shanyuhan Jin, Daliang Xu, Peiwen Xu, Kangwei Yin, Jiaxin Du, Han Chen, Jinling Fan, Heng Liang, Zhongbiao Wu, Xuanhao Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectrochemical nitric oxide reduction (NORR) under industrially relevant dilute feeds is fundamentally limited by insufficient interfacial NO coverage and rapid loss of reactive intermediates. Here we introduce quantifiable nanoconfinement as a coverage-regulation strategy for NORR in the ppm regime. Hollow multishelled Cu2O nanoreactors are constructed as a model system, in which shell number defines confinement depth and progressively strengthens confinement by narrowing inter-shell cavities. Under a 1000 ppm NO feed, the three-shell catalyst achieves a Faradaic efficiency of 97.9% and a single-pass NO conversion of 97.3%, which is competitive with systems operating under NO-rich conditions. Finite-element simulations quantitatively show that multishell confinement nonlinearly amplifies intermediate enrichment, increasing local *H and *NH concentrations by over an order of magnitude relative to the single-shell structure while also enhancing local NO coverage. Coverage-dependent density functional theory and transition-state calculations reveal that the confinement-modulated coverage environment reshapes the NORR kinetic and thermodynamic landscape by rebalancing initial *NO hydrogenation and NH3 desorption, defining a favorable coverage window that balances intermediate activation and product release. These findings establish nanoconfinement-enabled coverage engineering for electrocatalysis under dilute feeds.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Pollution remediation, Electrocatalysis

Introduction

Nitric oxide (NO) is a major atmospheric pollutant emitted from fossil-fuel combustion and a wide range of industrial processes1,2, contributing to photochemical smog, acid rain, and severe health hazards3,4. Conventional NO abatement relies predominantly on selective catalytic reduction (SCR), in which NO is converted into N2 using NH3 or urea at elevated temperatures (300–400 °C)5,6. Despite its maturity, SCR suffers from intrinsic drawbacks, including ammonia slip, formation of corrosive ammonium salts, and substantial energy input7,8. In parallel, industrial ammonia synthesis remains dominated by the Haber–Bosch process, which operates under harsh conditions and accounts for a significant fraction of global CO2 emissions9,10. These challenges have motivated increasing interest in alternative strategies that simultaneously remove NO and valorize nitrogen species under mild conditions.

Electrochemical nitric oxide reduction reaction (NORR) has emerged as a promising pathway to convert NO into value-added ammonia (NH3) at ambient temperature and pressure, offering the possibility of coupling pollutant mitigation with nitrogen resource recovery11,12. Over the past few years, substantial progress has been achieved in catalyst development for NORR, with reported systems demonstrating high Faradaic efficiency (FE) and NH3 selectivity13–15. However, most of these studies have been conducted under NO-rich conditions, typically using pure NO or feeds with concentrations ≥1 vol%16,17. Such conditions differ markedly from real exhaust streams, where NO is usually present at ppm levels (100–1000 ppm)18. Under these dilute feeds, NORR faces a fundamentally different challenge: insufficient surface coverage of NO and its derived intermediates, which severely limits reaction rates and exacerbates competition from the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER)19.

At low NO concentrations, the governing factor of NORR activity shifts from intrinsic catalytic activity to the ability of a catalyst to sustain an adequate local NO coverage. Traditional catalyst optimization strategies, such as electronic modulation20, lattice strain21, or defect engineering22,23, primarily enhance adsorption strength or activation barriers at isolated active sites. While effective under NO-rich conditions, these approaches cannot compensate for the collapse of reactant coverage under ppm-level feeds, where mass transport and reactant residence time become dominant limitations. Consequently, achieving efficient NORR under industrially relevant dilute NO conditions requires a fundamentally different design principle that addresses coverage regulation rather than site activity alone.

Nanoconfinement provides a powerful strategy to modulate local reaction environments beyond conventional surface engineering. Confined nanospaces can enrich reactants, prolong residence times, thereby enhancing local coverage even when the bulk feed concentration is low24,25. Such effects have been demonstrated in electrochemical CO2 or nitrate reduction reactions26,27, where confinement-induced enrichment significantly alters reaction kinetics and selectivity. Importantly, confinement not only influences reactant concentration but also governs the accumulation and fate of surface-bound intermediates, enabling reaction pathways that are otherwise inaccessible under open-interface conditions28,29.

However, despite these advances, the deliberate use of nanoconfinement to regulate the local reaction microenvironment in NORR remains relatively underexplored. In particular, under ppm-level NO feeds, it remains unclear how a confined architecture can sustain local NO availability, retain reactive intermediates, and regulate surface coverage to overcome the coverage collapse caused by dilute reactant supply. Moreover, although hollow and porous architectures have been widely used to enhance mass transport and active-site exposure30, the quantitative relationship between confinement geometry, local coverage regulation, and NORR performance has not been systematically clarified.

Among confined architectures, hollow multishelled structures (HoMSs) provide a distinctive platform for quantitative confinement engineering31. Traditional porous materials typically regulate confinement strength by adjusting pore size, pore distribution, or tortuosity32. Unlike single-shell hollow particles or conventional porous materials, HoMSs comprise multiple concentric shells separated by internal voids33. This architecture retains the advantages of porous materials while introducing an additional discrete structural parameter, namely the shell number, which further governs confinement depth and diffusion pathways. Increasing the shell number can lengthen transport routes for reactants and intermediates, thereby prolonging residence time and enabling stepwise regulation of local coverage. Recent studies have identified shell-number-dependent confinement as an effective handle for manipulating the spatiotemporal distribution of reactive species in gas-involving catalytic reactions34,35.

To test whether such shell-number-controlled nanoconfinement can overcome the coverage limitations of NORR under realistic dilute feeds, we selected Cu2O hollow multishelled structures as a model system. Cu2O is a well-established NORR catalyst with high activity and NH3 selectivity, making it a suitable platform for isolating confinement effects from changes in catalytic composition. By systematically increasing the shell number from one to three, a series of nanoreactors with progressively strengthened confinement was constructed (Fig. 1). When evaluated under a dilute NO feed of 1000 ppm in a flow-cell configuration, the three-shell Cu2O HoMSs achieve an NH3 yield rate of 2675.7 μg h−1 mgcat−1, a maximum FE of 97.9%, and a single-pass NO conversion of up to 97.3%, showing performance that is competitive with reported systems operating at higher NO concentrations.

Schematic illustration of the Ostwald-ripening-assisted construction of Cu2O hollow multishelled nanoreactors with tunable shell numbers and their role in regulating the local reaction microenvironment for electrocatalytic NORR.

Mechanistically, we reveal that the multishell architecture functions as a coverage regulator. Critically, rather than treating the shell number merely as a morphological descriptor, we show that adding shells narrows inter-shell cavities and couples path-lengthening with void-volume contraction to nonlinearly amplify confinement strength, an effect difficult to realize in conventional single-shell or porous architectures. Enhanced nanoconfinement enriches local NO concentration, sustains a larger steady-state population of N-containing intermediates, and increases the availability of surface hydrogen species. These confinement effects reshape the coverage-dependent NORR energetics by rebalancing the relative kinetic and thermodynamic limitations associated with initial *NO hydrogenation and NH3 desorption. These results point to shell-number-controlled nanoconfinement as an effective route for addressing coverage limitations in gas electroreduction under dilute feeds.

Results

Shell-number-programmed construction of Cu2O hollow multishelled nanoreactors

To establish a structural platform for confinement-driven coverage regulation, Cu2O HoMSs with tunable shell numbers were synthesized via a liquid-phase reduction coupled with a sequential Ostwald ripening process36,37. By precisely controlling the reductant dosage and reaction time, single-, double-, and triple-shell Cu2O HoMSs were obtained, hereafter denoted as 1-shell, 2-shell, and 3-shell Cu2O HoMSs. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images reveal a clear, shell-number-dependent evolution of the hollow multishelled architecture (Fig. 2a–c and Figs. S1–2). All samples exhibit uniform spherical morphologies, while distinct concentric shells and inter-shell voids are observed for the 2-shell and 3-shell structures. Statistical analysis of TEM images (Table S1) shows that the innermost shell remains relatively thick (~160 nm) in both 2-shell and 3-shell HoMSs, whereas the newly formed outer shells progressively thin with successive ripening cycles. Concomitantly, the inter-shell cavities shrink markedly as additional shells are introduced: the outermost cavity decreases from 63.7 ± 4.5 nm in the 2-shell structure to 23.3 ± 1.3 nm in the 3-shell counterpart. This systematic cavity narrowing provides a structural signature of progressively strengthened nanoconfinement with increasing shell number. Such structural evolution originates from a sequential Ostwald ripening process37. Initially formed Cu2O nanospheres undergo partial dissolution and redeposition upon additional reductant injection, leading to recrystallization on the outer surface and the formation of new shells. Repeated ripening cycles progressively generate multiple shells separated by confined voids, yielding a hierarchical nanoreactor architecture in which reactant diffusion pathways and residence times can be discretely regulated by the shell number.

High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images (Figs. S3–5) show clear lattice fringes with spacings of ~0.246–0.248 nm, corresponding to the Cu2O(111) planes38, regardless of shell number. Selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns (Fig. S6) display well-defined diffraction rings indexed to cubic Cu2O, confirming the polycrystalline nature of the shells39. High-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) images and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mappings (Fig. 2d–f) further verify the hollow multishell structures. Distinct shell–void contrasts are clearly visible for the 2-shell and 3-shell HoMSs, while elemental line scanning profiles (Fig. 2g–i) reveal homogeneous Cu and O distributions with characteristic intensity valleys at the particle center, confirming the hollow interior. The Cu line-scan profiles exhibit a series of pronounced intensity maxima, with the number of peaks consistent with the designed shell number. Each maximum, together with its adjacent minimum, corresponds to a Cu2O shell and the intervening void, respectively. Accordingly, the peaks are denoted as Shell1, Shell2, and Shell3 from the center outward. This peak–valley sequence, in good agreement with the intended multishell architecture, further corroborates the successful synthesis of Cu2O HoMSs with tunable shell numbers.

To ensure that later observed catalytic performance differences originate from architectural confinement rather than changes in chemical composition or active-site density, the physicochemical properties of the Cu2O HoMSs were systematically examined. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of all Cu2O HoMSs match well with the cubic Cu2O phase (PDF#65-3288)40, with no detectable impurity phases (Fig. S7). The identical diffraction features across different shell numbers indicate that the crystal structure remains invariant upon multishell formation. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis further confirms the chemical consistency of the catalysts (Figs. S8–9). The Cu 2p spectra of all Cu2O HoMSs display characteristic Cu+ features at ~932.5 eV and ~952.3 eV (Fig. S10a)41. Given the intrinsic overlap between Cu+ and Cu0 in the Cu 2p region, Cu LMM Auger spectra were analyzed, revealing a dominant Auger peak at 916.8 eV characteristic of Cu⁺ for all samples (Fig. S10b)42. These results indicate that Cu remains predominantly in the Cu2O state and that the oxidation state is unaffected by shell number.

Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis shows a gradual increase in specific surface area from 26.6 m2 g−1 (1-shell) to 34.1 m2 g−1 (3-shell) (Table S2), reflecting the geometric contribution of additional shells. Importantly, the later electrochemical tests were conducted under identical geometric electrode area and catalyst loading. The electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) values are comparable among the three catalysts, suggesting that surface area cannot account for the catalytic trends. N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms exhibit type IV behavior with H3 hysteresis loops (Fig. S11), indicative of slit-shaped mesopores formed by aggregated Cu2O nanocrystallites43. The pore size distributions (2–10 nm) are comparable across all samples, ensuring similar accessibility for NO diffusion. Together, these results establish that the Cu2O HoMSs possess nearly identical chemical states, crystallographic features, and electrochemically accessible surface areas, allowing the effect of shell-number-dependent nanoconfinement to be isolated as the primary variable governing catalytic behavior.

Confinement-enabled enhancement of NORR under ppm-level NO feeds

The electrocatalytic performance of the Cu2O HoMSs for NORR was evaluated in a flow-cell configuration under an industrially relevant NO concentration of 1000 ppm. To further benchmark the contribution of nanoconfinement, commercial Cu2O particles were introduced as a non-confined control sample. This sample had the same primary chemical composition as Cu2O HoMSs but lacked the deliberately designed hollow multishell architecture. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves recorded under Ar and NO atmospheres show a pronounced current enhancement in the presence of NO for all catalysts, confirming active NO electroreduction (Fig. 3a). Notably, the NO-induced current enhancement follows the order 3-shell >2-shell >1-shell, indicating a strong dependence of NORR activity on shell number.

Quantitative product analysis reveals a clear confinement-dependent enhancement in NH3 formation. Across the investigated potential range (−0.25 to −0.65 V vs. RHE), commercial Cu2O delivered much lower NH3 yield rates than all Cu2O HoMSs (Fig. 3b). Notably, even the 1-shell Cu2O HoMSs outperform commercial Cu2O. This result highlights the structural contribution of the hollow multishell architecture. In addition, among the Cu2O HoMSs, the NH3 yield rate increased systematically with shell number. The amount of produced NH3 was primarily quantified using the indophenol blue UV–vis spectrophotometric method (Fig. S12). To further verify the accuracy of NH3 quantification, ion chromatography (IC) was employed as an independent validation method (Fig. S13)44. The closely consistent yield rates obtained from the two methods confirmed the accuracy and reliability of the quantitative results in this study. The 3-shell Cu₂O HoMSs deliver a maximum FENH3 of 97.9% at −0.25 V and achieve an NH3 yield rate of 2675.7 μg h−1 mgcat−1 at −0.65 V. In contrast, the 1-shell counterpart exhibits significantly lower NH3 yields under identical conditions.

Furthermore, the multi-shell configuration led to a higher NO conversion. At a NO flow rate of 30 mL min−1, the NO conversion increased from 47.6 ± 0.1% of 1-shell to 56.0 ± 2.7% of 3-shell (Fig. S14), suggesting that the multishell architecture facilitates more efficient NO utilization under identical operating conditions. To examine whether further increasing the shell number could improve the NORR performance, 4-shell Cu2O HoMSs were additionally synthesized (Fig. S15). TEM analysis confirmed the formation of a well-defined four-shell hollow architecture, with the outermost inter-shell cavity further narrowed to 17.9 ± 1.0 nm, suggesting a more confined inter-shell space. Electrocatalytic measurements showed that the 4-shell Cu2O HoMSs delivered a higher NH3 yield at relatively mild potentials (Fig. S16). However, when the applied potential was shifted more negatively, the NH3 yield of the 4-shell Cu2O HoMSs became comparable to, or even slightly lower than, that of the 3-shell Cu2O HoMSs. This non-monotonic trend may be associated with excessive confinement arising from the further narrowed inter-shell cavities, which could partially offset the benefit of local reactant or intermediate accumulation by imposing additional limitations on reactant diffusion, active hydrogen supply, and product release29. Therefore, the 3-shell Cu2O HoMSs represent the optimal architecture among the examined samples, indicating that shell-number-induced nanoconfinement has an optimal window rather than increasing indefinitely with shell number.

Gas-phase analysis confirms that H2 is the only detectable gaseous byproduct, with no observable N2 or N2O signals. Colorimetric analysis further excludes the formation of N2H4 or NH2OH (Figs. S17–20), indicating high nitrogen selectivity toward NH3. Nevertheless, at more negative potentials, the FE for H2 continuously increased, accompanied by a decrease in NH3 FE (Fig. 3c and Figs. S21–22). This product-distribution trend suggests that the decline in NH3 selectivity at high cathodic bias was mainly attributed to intensified HER competition45,46. Control experiments conducted under Ar, open-circuit conditions, or using bare carbon paper electrodes yield only trace amounts of NH3 (Fig. 3d), confirming that the observed NH3 originates exclusively from electrochemical NO reduction on the Cu2O HoMSs. Electrochemical double-layer capacitance (Cdl) measurements reveal nearly identical values for all samples (Fig. S23), indicating comparable electrochemically active surface areas. This result further supports that the enhanced NORR activity arises primarily from confinement effects rather than increased active-site density.

To elucidate how confinement influences NO utilization, the gas residence time in the flow cell was systematically tuned by varying the NO flow rate. At a flow rate of 5 mL min−1, corresponding to a residence time of 1.44 s, the 3-shell Cu2O HoMSs achieve a single-pass NO conversion of 97.3% (Fig. 3e). Fitting the conversion–residence time relationship yields an apparent reaction rate constant of 2.78 s−1 (Fig. S24), highlighting the rapid kinetics enabled by the confined architecture. Notably, even at higher gas flow rates, a high NO conversion can be maintained by increasing the reactor volume to prolong the gas residence time. In a larger flow channel (Fig. S25), extending the residence time in this manner still affords an NO conversion of 80.0% at a flow rate of 30 mL min−1, underscoring the robustness and scalability of the multishell nanoreactor under practical operating conditions. Furthermore, the 3-shell Cu2O HoMSs exhibit consistently high NH3 production across a wide range of NO concentrations (99.9%, 10%, 1%, and 1000 ppm) (Fig. 3f).

To place the performance under dilute NO feeds into a broader context, a comparative analysis of NH3 yield and NO conversion rates with previously reported NORR systems was conducted (Fig. S26 and Tables S3–4). Notably, under a NO concentration of 1000 ppm, the present 3-shell Cu2O HoMSs achieve NH3 yield and NO conversion metrics that are competitive with those of many catalysts evaluated at substantially higher NO concentrations (typically ≥ 1 vol%). This performance suggests that efficient NO conversion can be achieved at 1000 ppm, without the need to elevate the feed concentration to NO-rich levels commonly used in prior studies. Importantly, this result demonstrates the feasibility of NO conversion at ppm levels and suggests a route toward resource utilization without relying on typical NO-enrichment steps.

The durability of the 3-shell Cu2O HoMSs was evaluated through cycling and long-term electrolysis tests. After five consecutive cycles at −0.45 V (vs. RHE), the NH3 yield decreases by only 13.9%, while the FE remains high (Fig. 3g). A 30-h continuous electrolysis test was performed to evaluate the operational stability of the 3-shell Cu2O HoMSs. As shown in Fig. 3h, the current density remained stable with minimal fluctuation throughout the electrolysis. Meanwhile, the FENH3 was consistently maintained above 90%, indicating that the stable current response was accompanied by sustained NH3 production and high product selectivity. Post-reaction TEM images confirm that the multishell architecture remains intact after prolonged NORR operation (Fig. S27). XPS and Cu LMM Auger spectra indicate that Cu remains predominantly in the Cu+ state, with minor Cu2+ signals attributed to surface oxidation upon air exposure (Fig. S28)47. ICP-MS analysis reveals minimal Cu dissolution (6.16%) after long-term electrolysis (Table S5), further confirming catalyst stability. To assess tolerance toward common exhaust impurities, co-feeding experiments were performed. The presence of 10% CO2 causes negligible change in NH3 yield, whereas 300 ppm SO2 leads to a moderate decrease in activity (Fig. S29), consistent with competitive adsorption effects. These results demonstrate that the multishell Cu2O HoMSs maintain robust performance under realistic exhaust compositions.

To further evaluate the behavior of the catalyst under more demanding operating conditions, an additional galvanostatic test was conducted at 100 mA cm−2 under the same 1000 ppm NO feed (Fig. S30). During the 30 h operation, the single-pass NO conversion gradually decreased with prolonged electrolysis. Interestingly, partial recovery of NO conversion was observed after catholyte replacement, during which the catholyte flow channel was emptied and NO was continuously supplied. This behavior suggests that the performance decay was more likely associated with dynamic gas–liquid–solid interfacial changes, such as possible flooding of the electrode. More broadly, these results highlight the current challenge in achieving high current densities for low-concentration NO electroreduction: under dilute NO feeds, the attainable NORR current is intrinsically constrained by the limited NO molar flux, while simply increasing the gas flow rate would shorten the residence time and compromise single-pass NO conversion. Therefore, sustained high-current operation for ppm-level NO electroreduction is not only determined by the intrinsic catalyst activity, but also requires reactor-level optimization to balance NO delivery, gas residence time, water management, and gas–liquid–solid interfacial stability. Future strategies, including enlarged effective electrode areas, optimized gas-flow fields, improved water management, and stacked or modular flow-cell configurations, will be needed to match the target current with sufficient NO delivery while maintaining high single-pass NO conversion.

Confinement effects of Cu2O HoMSs on reaction intermediates

To elucidate the NORR pathway and clarify how the confined nanoreactor architecture influences reaction intermediates, attenuated total reflectance surface-enhanced infrared absorption spectroscopy (ATR-SEIRAS) measurements were performed. Spectra were collected for Cu2O HoMSs with one, two, and three shells under open-circuit potential (OCP) and at applied potentials ranging from −0.2 to −1.0 V vs. RHE (Fig. 4a–c). A broad absorption band located at 3100–3450 cm−1 was assigned to the stretching vibrations of *HNO and *OH species20,48. Meanwhile, gradually intensified bands at 1378–1400 cm−1 and 1627–1661 cm−1 were attributed to the accumulation of NH4+ and *OH species, respectively49,50. Across all three Cu2O HoMSs, three characteristic vibrational features were consistently observed: N–H stretching of *NH (~1175–1185 cm−1), –NH2 wagging (1260–1279 cm−1), and –H–N–H bending (~1485 cm−1) associated with *NH2 species20,51,52. The invariance of intermediate peak positions across different shell numbers further indicates that varying the shell architecture does not alter the intrinsic reaction mechanism.

To further elucidate how the shell-confined architecture influences intermediate behavior, the ATR-SEIRAS responses of the three Cu2O HoMS catalysts were compared in detail. Considering both spectral distinguishability and assignment reliability, the *NH vibrational band was selected as a representative marker for N-containing intermediates. Distinct shell-number-dependent evolutions of *NH and NH4+ bands were observed. For the 1-shell Cu2O HoMSs, both *NH and NH4+ bands increase only modestly over the investigated potential range. In contrast, the 2-shell Cu2O HoMSs exhibit an evident attenuation of intermediate-related *NH signals accompanied by an increase in the NH4+ band intensity. For the 3-shell Cu2O HoMSs, the *NH band varies only slightly with potential, whereas the NH4+ band increases substantially as the potential becomes more negative. As summarized in Fig. 4d, the 3-shell Cu2O HoMSs display the highest *NH peak intensity, followed by the 2-shell and 1-shell counterparts.

Notably, although NH3 formation consumes NH-containing intermediates, the 3-shell catalyst still exhibits the strongest steady-state *NH signal. This indicates that multishell confinement sustains a larger interfacial reservoir of hydrogenated N intermediates near the active surfaces, rather than merely promoting their transient formation. Such a persistent intermediate pool is consistent with confinement-assisted retention within the inter-shell cavities and provides spectroscopic evidence for progressively strengthened confinement with increasing shell number. In addition, the NH4+ band shows a slight blue shift from 1378 to 1400 cm−1 as the shell number increases (Fig. 4e), suggesting weakened interaction of NH4+ with the catalyst surface, which may facilitate product release and contribute to the higher NH3 production on multishelled architectures.

Considering that NORR proceeds via multiple proton-coupled electron transfer steps involving successive hydrogenation of N-containing species, the availability of surface hydrogen (*H) is also critical. To probe *H-related species, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements were conducted using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) as a radical trapping agent (Fig. 4f)53. Under identical conditions, the *H signal intensity increases with shell number, with the 3-shell Cu2O HoMSs exhibiting the strongest signal, followed by the 2-shell and 1-shell samples. This trend suggests that the multishell structure enhances surface-accessible hydrogen species, thereby facilitating continuous hydrogenation steps. Notably, the shell-number-dependent *H signal is consistent with both the *NH signal intensity and the observed NH3 production rates. Collectively, these results indicate that although the multishell confinement does not change the intrinsic NORR pathway, it significantly enriches N-containing intermediates and *H species at the reaction interface, thereby promoting efficient NH3 formation.

Finite-element simulations of confinement-driven coverage enrichment

Given the nearly identical Cdl values for all catalysts (Fig. S23), the performance differences among Cu2O HoMSs with different shell numbers are unlikely to arise from variations in electrochemically accessible surface area. Instead, these differences point toward an architectural confinement effect. Together with the in situ ATR-SEIRAS and EPR results, we hypothesized that multishell nanoconfinement primarily modulates the transport, enrichment, and retention of reactants and key intermediates within the nanoreactor. To evaluate the transport behavior of Cu2O HoMSs with different shell numbers, EIS measurements were conducted under Ar and 1000 ppm NO atmospheres (Fig. S31). In the EIS spectra, the high-frequency semicircle is generally associated with charge-transfer resistance at the electrode/electrolyte interface, whereas the low-frequency region reflects the diffusion and transport behavior of electroactive species54. The low-frequency responses under Ar indicate that increasing shell number modulated the intrinsic mass-transport behavior of Cu2O HoMSs and introduced a more pronounced diffusion-related impedance contribution. Under NO, the smallest semicircle of the 3-shell Cu2O HoMSs suggests the lowest charge-transfer resistance and the fastest NO-related reaction kinetics. In addition, comparison of the low-frequency region showed that NO introduction also changed the diffusion-related impedance response, suggesting facilitated diffusion and transport of NO-related electroactive species under reaction conditions. This electrochemical evidence supports that the multishell architecture modulates mass transport and reaction-related interfacial processes.

To further test this hypothesis and elucidate how the multishell architecture impacts the NORR process, finite element method (FEM) simulations were performed to resolve coupled mass-transport and surface-reaction behaviors. Geometric models of 1-shell, 2-shell, and 3-shell Cu2O HoMSs were reconstructed based on TEM observations. To allow inter-shell mass exchange, identical pore openings were introduced on each shell according to BJH pore-size distribution analysis. In the simulations, NO was continuously supplied at 1000 ppm and allowed to diffuse and adsorb within the nanoreactor, yielding spatial concentration fields.

We analyzed the spatial distribution of NO to evaluate the confinement-induced reactant enrichment. As shown in Fig. 5a–c, the introduction of additional shells leads to progressively enhanced NO accumulation within the internal cavities. For the 1-shell nanoreactor, NO is mainly concentrated near the catalyst surface, and the cavity NO concentration remains close to the 1000 ppm feed level, indicating limited enrichment. In contrast, the 2-shell and especially the 3-shell architectures generate pronounced concentration gradients and substantially higher NO levels within the confined interior. The 3-shell HoMSs exhibit the highest local NO concentration in the cavity region (Fig. 5c), demonstrating an effective enrichment effect under industrially relevant dilute NO feeds.

To further probe how nanoconfinement influences key intermediates, a simplified reaction network was implemented in the FEM simulations. In this model, NO entering the HoMSs adsorbs on shell surfaces and is hydrogenated to an N-containing intermediate (represented as *NH). The *NH intermediate is further converted to NH3 through a simplified downstream hydrogenation step, while surface *H is continuously generated via water electrolysis. The generated NH3 is treated as a diffusible product and continuously removed through the outlet boundary, thereby avoiding artificial product accumulation within the nanoreactor. Surface-bound *H and *NH can either be retained within the nanoreactor due to shell-induced confinement or diffuse outward through pores and be lost to the external region. The simulation results show that the local concentrations of both *H (Fig. 5d–f) and *NH (Fig. 5g–i) increase systematically with shell number, consistent with enhanced intermediate retention in multishell architectures.

To further quantify the confinement effect, the local concentrations at the center of the outermost inter-shell cavity in each model were extracted and normalized to the cavity-center concentration of the 1-shell HoMS. As shown in Fig. 5j, the NO concentrations in the 2-shell and 3-shell architectures are 1.06 and 1.24 times that of the 1-shell structure, respectively. In comparison, the enrichment of key intermediates is more pronounced: the local *H concentration increases by 15.99- and 59.35-fold, whereas the *NH concentration rises by 7.51- and 18.62-fold in the 2-shell and 3-shell HoMS, respectively. Notably, this intermediate enrichment does not scale linearly with shell number. Instead, the much larger increase from the 2-shell to the 3-shell architecture suggests a non-linear amplification of confinement strength, arising from the coupled effects of shell-number increase and progressive narrowing of the inter-shell cavities. To verify that this nonlinear enrichment trend is not caused by a specific pore-parameter setting, we further performed sensitivity analyses by varying the pore diameter and porosity (Fig. S32). The results show that these parameter variations do not alter the shell-number-dependent enrichment trend, further supporting the robustness of the simulated confinement effect.

In addition to the pore-parameter sensitivity analysis, we further examined whether the simulated intermediate-enrichment trend depends on the kinetic parameters used in the simplified reaction–transport model. Specifically, sensitivity analyses were performed on the rate constants k1 and k2, corresponding to the NO-to-*NH conversion step (NO + *H → *NH) and the subsequent *NH-to-NH3 conversion step (*NH + *H → NH3), respectively. As shown in Fig. S33, although the absolute *NH concentration varies with the selected kinetic parameters, the shell-number-dependent enrichment trend remains unchanged, with the 3-shell Cu2O HoMSs consistently showing the strongest *NH accumulation. This result further indicates that the simulated intermediate retention and enrichment are mainly governed by the multishell geometry rather than by a specific choice of kinetic parameters.

Based on these results, we propose a mechanistic picture for confinement-enhanced NORR in multishelled nanoreactors (Fig. 5k). As additional shells are introduced, each new shell may introduce an extra diffusion barrier while further compressing the confined cavity space. Consequently, the overall confinement is not simply an additive effect of shell number, but a nonlinearly amplified outcome of coupled mass-transport regulation and local microenvironment reconstruction. Under such conditions, NO tends to be enriched in the outer inter-shell cavity, where spatial restriction is strongest. Meanwhile, the multishell architecture may promote the accumulation of reactive intermediates and prolong the residence of species such as *NH and *H within the confined voids, suppressing their rapid loss to the bulk electrolyte. The extended residence time may increase the probability of productive *NO–*H encounters and successive hydrogenation steps, thereby contributing NH3 formation.

Coverage-dependent energetics revealed by DFT calculations

The FEM simulations indicate that multishell architectures increase the local NO concentration near the Cu2O surface. In electrocatalysis, an increased local concentration near the catalytic surface has been used to rationalize enhanced adsorbate coverage. By analogy, the FEM-derived NO enrichment suggests a more NO-rich interfacial environment under multishell confinement, providing a concentration-level basis for considering higher-*NO-coverage surface states. However, the FEM model does not directly evaluate the coverage-dependent reaction energetics. Therefore, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were further performed using representative *NO coverage models to assess how NO enrichment influences the thermodynamics and kinetic barriers of NORR. Accordingly, Cu2O(111) models with low and high *NO coverages of 1/16 and 9/16 monolayers (ML) were constructed to represent NO-poor and NO-enriched surface conditions, corresponding to one and nine NO adsorbates per 16 surface Cu sites, respectively. The 1/16 ML model describes an isolated-*NO limit, whereas the 9/16 ML model captures a high-coverage state while retaining available surface sites for subsequent hydrogenation.

Free energy diagrams derived from experimentally observed intermediates show that increasing *NO coverage raises the free energy change of the first hydrogenation step (*NO → *HNO) from 0.45 to 0.62 eV (Fig. 6a). This trend suggests that dense *NO coverage introduces competition for surface electrons, which may make initial hydrogenation thermodynamically less favorable. Bader charge and charge-density-difference analyses (Fig. S34) suggest that at low coverage, the *HNO intermediate gains 0.11 e⁻ from the Cu surface, weakening the N–O bond and promoting activation. At high coverage, electron donation decreases to 0.06 e⁻ due to electronic sharing among multiple *NO species, consistent with the increased thermodynamic difficulty of the first hydrogenation step.

To assess the overall thermodynamic profile, we evaluated all elementary steps with positive free energy changes along the NORR pathway, including *NO → *HNO, *HNO → *HNOH, and NH3 desorption. Although low coverage favors early hydrogenation steps, NH3 desorption exhibits the largest uphill free energy change (ΔG = 1.15 eV), arising from strong adsorption at unsaturated Cu sites with a short Cu–N bond length of 1.92 Å. In contrast, at high *NO coverage, the NH3 desorption free energy decreases sharply to −0.07 eV, accompanied by an elongated Cu–N bond length of 2.17 Å, indicating significantly weakened adsorption and facile product release.

To further clarify the coverage-dependent energetic trend, additional Cu2O(111) models with intermediate *NO coverages (4/16, 5/16, 8/16, and 12/16 ML) were constructed. As shown in Fig. 6b, both *HNO formation energies and NH3 desorption energies exhibit pronounced non-monotonic dependence on *NO coverage. When coverage increases from 1/16 to 4/16 ML, *HNO formation becomes slightly more favorable, whereas NH3 desorption remains largely unfavorable. Once coverage exceeds 4/16 ML, NH3 desorption energy decreases sharply, indicating substantially weakened product adsorption. Meanwhile, the *HNO formation energy exhibits a U-shaped dependence on *NO coverage, decreasing again at higher coverages. Notably, the 9/16 ML condition gives the lowest *HNO formation energy and the lowest NH3 desorption energy within the high-coverage regime.

While the coverage-dependent *HNO formation and NH3 desorption energies reveal the thermodynamic response of key elementary steps to surface coverage, they do not directly describe the kinetic difficulty of the hydrogenation process. Therefore, to further evaluate the kinetic influence of *NO coverage, transition-state calculations were performed for the representative initial hydrogenation step from *NO to *HNO under different *NO coverages (Figs. S35–36). The estimated kinetic barrier increases with increasing *NO coverage, indicating that dense adsorbate environments make the initial hydrogenation step more kinetically demanding. Thus, higher *NO coverage facilitates NH3 desorption thermodynamically, but can simultaneously impose a greater kinetic constraint on initial *NO hydrogenation.

Although the transition-state calculations indicate that high *NO coverage makes the initial *NO hydrogenation step more kinetically demanding, the apparent NORR performance in confined nanoreactors is not governed solely by the intrinsic kinetic difficulty of an isolated elementary step. FEM simulations show that multishell HoMSs concurrently enrich *NO- and *H-related species within the confined void space, increasing their local availability and spatial co-presence. Such enrichment may increase the probability of productive *NO–*H encounters and thereby contribute to the apparent reaction flux through the initial hydrogenation step. Therefore, the enhanced local availability of reactive species may partly compensate for the increased kinetic difficulty of the initial hydrogenation step, while the high-coverage environment simultaneously facilitates NH3 desorption by weakening NH3 adsorption.

Together with the FEM results, these DFT calculations suggest that multishell Cu2O architectures regulate the local coverage environment and associated NORR energetics. This coverage enhancement reduces the thermodynamic difficulty of NH3 desorption, while dense adsorbate environments make the initial *NO hydrogenation step more kinetically demanding. Therefore, nanoconfinement-induced coverage enhancement does not simply promote all elementary steps, but rebalances the relative kinetic and thermodynamic limitations between initial *NO hydrogenation and NH3 desorption. This coverage-regulated balance helps rationalize the experimentally observed shell-number-dependent NORR performance of the multishell Cu2O nanoreactors under industrially relevant dilute NO feed conditions.

Discussion

In conclusion, we show that the key bottleneck of NORR under dilute feeds, i.e., insufficient interfacial NO coverage and rapid intermediate loss, can be overcome by nanoconfinement-enabled coverage engineering. Shell-number-programmed Cu2O hollow multishelled nanoreactors progressively strengthen confinement by narrowing inter-shell cavities, enriching NO and retaining reactive intermediates to sustain high steady-state coverage in the ppm regime. In this system, the shell number is not merely a morphological descriptor, but serves as a structural parameter that correlates with cavity contraction, diffusion-path lengthening, and confinement depth. Consequently, the three-shell catalyst achieves efficient NH3 synthesis with high FE and near-complete single-pass NO conversion. Combined in situ ATR-SEIRAS, EPR, FEM simulations, transition-state calculations, and coverage-dependent DFT analysis reveal that multishell confinement does not alter the intrinsic NORR pathway, but reshapes the coverage-dependent energetics by rebalancing the relative kinetic and thermodynamic limitations between initial *NO hydrogenation and NH3 desorption. This work positions quantifiable nanoconfinement as a transferable design principle for gas-involving electrocatalysis under dilute feeds, with implications for realistic exhaust purification and nitrogen valorization.

Methods

Reagents and materials

All the reagents are analytical-grade and directly used without further purification. Copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate (CuSO4·5H2O, ≥99%), hydrazine hydrate aqueous solution (N2H4·H2O, 85.0% of volume fraction), ethanol (C2H6O, 99.7%), and potassium chloride (KCl, 99%) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP, MW = 55000, AR) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Cuprous oxide (Cu2O, ≥99%), potassium sulfate (K2SO4, 99%), potassium hydroxide (KOH, ≥95%), sodium nitroprusside dihydrate (C5H4FeN6Na2O3·H2O, ≥99.98%), sodium hypochlorite (NaClO), phenyl hydroxide (C6H5OH) were obtained from Aladdin. Hydrochloric acid (HCl), ethyl iminobenzoate hydrochloride (C9H12ClNO), sodium acetate (CH3COONa), acetic acid (CH3COOH) and ammonium iron(III) sulfate dodecahydrate (NH4Fe(SO4)2·12H2O) were purchased from Macklin. Ammonium chloride standard solution (NH4Cl, 1 M), hydroxylamine standard solution (NH2OH, 2 M) and hydrazine standard solution (1000 μg/mL in 1.0 M HCl) were purchased from Shenzhen Fulin Co., Ltd.

Synthesis of catalysts

The Cu2O hollow multishell structures (HoMSs) were synthesized following a previously reported Ostwald-ripening protocol with minor modifications36,37. In a typical synthesis, 300 mg of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) was dissolved in 30 mL of an aqueous 0.01 M CuSO4 solution under magnetic stirring at 300 rpm. Subsequently, 10 μL N2H4·H2O was injected into the solution, during which the color changed from blue to green and then to orange, indicating the formation of Cu2O colloids. The mixture was stirred for an additional 30 min at 300 rpm to obtain the single-shell Cu2O HoMSs. To grow the second shell, another 10 μL N2H4·H2O solution was added to the reaction mixture, followed by continuous stirring for 30 min to trigger Ostwald ripening. The third shell was produced by adding 15 μL N2H4·H2O solution and stirring for an additional 30 min. After the formation of 3-shell Cu2O HoMSs, an additional 17 μL N2H4·H2O was added to the suspension, followed by continuous stirring at 300 rpm for another 30 min to complete the growth of the fourth shell. The resulting suspension was immediately centrifuged at 8000 rpm, washed three times with deionized water and ethanol alternately, and collected as the final Cu2O HoMSs. The products were stored as ethanol dispersions and vacuum-dried prior to use.

Preparation of working electrodes

3 mg of catalyst powder was dispersed in a mixed solvent consisting of 950 μL of ethanol and 50 μL Nafion solution (5 wt%, DUPONT D520), followed by ultrasonication until a homogeneous catalyst ink was obtained. Then, 200 μL of the catalyst ink was uniformly drop-cast onto a 1.5 × 1.5 cm2 hydrophobic carbon paper (CP, TORAY YLS-30T) and dried at room temperature (25 °C). The catalyst loading was maintained at approximately 0.27 mg cm−2 for all electrochemical measurements.

Catalyst characterization

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images were obtained using a TALOS F200X G2 microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected on a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation over a 2θ range of 5–90° at a scan rate of 10° min−1. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed on a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha spectrometer. The specific surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution were determined using a static nitrogen adsorption instrument (JW-BK132F). Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) analysis was conducted on an Agilent 7800 (MS) system.

Electrocatalytic experiments

All electrochemical measurements were carried out on a CHI660F electrochemical workstation (Chenghua Inc., Shanghai, China). Unless otherwise specified, the electrocatalytic reduction experiments were conducted in a flow-cell reactor with a channel size of 1 × 1 cm2, equipped with a three-electrode system. A Pt mesh (1.5 × 1.5 cm2) and a Hg/HgO reference electrode (filled with 1 M KOH) served as the counter and reference electrodes, respectively. The Hg/HgO reference electrode used in this study was checked against a newly purchased Hg/HgO reference electrode. Both electrodes were immersed in the same electrolyte, and the open-circuit potential difference between them was measured. The working electrode was the catalyst-coated hydrophobic carbon paper. The cathodic and anodic compartments were separated by a proton-conductive membrane (PEM, Nafion 117, thickness: 183 μm). The effective membrane area was 1 or 4 cm2, depending on the flow-channel size used. Prior to use, the Nafion 117 membrane was pretreated by treating in H2O2 (5 wt%) solution at 80 °C for 60 min, followed by rinsing in deionized water for 30 min. The membrane was then boiled in H2SO4 (5 wt%) at 80 °C for 60 min and finally rinsed again in deionized water for 30 min. For the scale-up experiment, the channel size of the flow-cell reactor was increased to 2 × 2 cm2, and the Pt mesh size was changed to 2.5 × 2.5 cm2, while the other configurations remained unchanged.

During the reduction experiments, 40 mL of 0.1 M K2SO4 (pH = 6.9 ± 0.1) and 40 mL of 0.1 M KCl (pH = 6.9 ± 0.1) were used as the catholyte and anolyte, respectively. The electrolytes were circulated through the flow cell at a rate of 20 mL min−1 using a peristaltic pump (BT100-2J). The electrolyte was prepared by dissolving K2SO4 or KCl in deionized water to obtain 0.1 M K2SO4 or 0.1 M KCl solution, respectively. The electrolyte was freshly prepared before each electrochemical measurement and temporarily stored in a 1 L reagent bottle at room temperature (25 °C) before use. Prior to each electrochemical measurement, the electrolyte solutions were purged with high-purity Ar (99.999%) at a flow rate of 50 mL min−1 for 30 min, followed by saturation with NO/Ar mixed gases (0.1 vol.%) for an additional 30 min to remove residual impurities completely.

All electrochemical measurements were performed without iR correction. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was performed at a scan rate of 100 mV s−1. The chronoamperometry (i-t curve) were carried out at various applied potentials with continuous stirring at 300 rpm to evaluate the catalytic activity. Electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) measurement

The ECSA measurements were conducted in an H-cell configuration. The working electrode, reference electrode, and proton-conductive membrane were the same as those used in the flow-cell measurements, while a Pt plate (1 × 1 cm2) was used as the counter electrode. A total of 40 mL of 0.1 M K2SO4 and 40 mL of 0.1 M KCl were used as the catholyte and anolyte, respectively. The ECSA was evaluated by recording cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves in the non-Faradaic potential region. CV curves were recorded at scan rates of 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 mV s−1. The electrochemical double-layer capacitance (Cdl) was extracted from the linear fit of the capacitive current (Δj = ja − jc, where ja and jc are the anodic and cathodic current densities, respectively) plotted as a function of scan rate. Cdl was determined from the slope of this plot which corresponds to 2 × Cdl.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurement

The EIS measurements were conducted in a flow-cell configuration, and the cell configuration was the same as that used for the reduction experiments. EIS measurements were performed at open-circuit potential with an AC amplitude of 5 mV over a frequency range from 106 to 0.1 Hz at 25 °C under Ar or 1000 ppm NO atmosphere.

Determination of ammonia (NH3)

The NH3 produced during electrolysis was quantified through spectrophotometric measurements based on the indophenol blue method. The reagent solutions were prepared as follows: (A) 0.15 M phenol and 0.47 mM potassium nitroprusside, (B) 0.13 M NaOH and 0.002 M NaClO solution. The 2.5 mL of solution A and 2.5 mL of solution B were added to 1 mL of the diluted electrolyzed solution. The mixture was then heated at 37 °C for 30 min in a water bath. Absorbance was measured by a UV-2600 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan) at a wavelength of 631.5 nm. Ion chromatography (IC) was further used to independently verify the NH3 yield. The electrolyte samples were analyzed using a Thermo Scientific AQUION ion chromatography system equipped with an IonPac CG12A guard column (4 × 50 mm, 4 μm) and an IonPac CS12A analytical column (4 × 250 mm, 4 μm). The injection volume was 25 μL. NH4+ was detected by suppressed conductivity detection using 20 mM methanesulfonic acid as the eluent at a flow rate of 1 mL min−1. The separation was performed under isocratic elution, with a total run time of 14 min.

Determination of gas products

The quantitative analysis of H2, N2, and N2O was conducted by an online gas chromatograph (GC-9660) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD). A baseline correction was carried out using Ar injection under identical GC conditions to quantify and eliminate air-related background interference.

In-situ ATR-SEIRAS measurement

Potential-dependent infrared spectra were obtained using a Nicolet iS50 Fourier-transform infrared spectrometer coupled with an electrochemical reflectance accessory and a liquid-nitrogen-cooled MCT detector. The infrared beam was directed at an incidence angle of 60°. A custom-built two-compartment, three-electrode cell was employed. Consistent with the electrolyte configuration used in the electrochemical measurements, the cathodic and anodic compartments each contained 10 mL of 0.1 M K2SO4 and 0.1 M KCl, respectively. The cell consisted of a catalyst-coated Si crystal as the working electrode, a Pt foil (1 × 1 cm2) as the counter electrode, and an Hg/HgO reference electrode. The working electrode was prepared by applying 200 μL of catalyst ink (3 mg mL−1) onto the Si crystal, followed by drying under ambient conditions. Before each measurement, the catholyte was purged with Ar for 30 min and subsequently with NO for another 30 min. The MCT detector was precooled with liquid nitrogen for at least 30 min to stabilize the signal. A background spectrum was acquired under open-circuit conditions, after which spectra were collected at the designated applied potentials.

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurement

EPR spectrum was conducted on a Bruker A300 spectrometer. Active hydrogen produced during the electrolysis process was trapped using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) in a 100 mL electrolyte solution containing 20 mM DMPO.

Finite element method (FEM) simulations

FEM simulations were performed using COMSOL Multiphysics. A 2D square computational domain (1600 nm × 1600 nm) was employed to describe species transport within the local diffusion field. The geometric models of single-, double-, and triple-shell Cu2O catalysts were reconstructed based on TEM observations, where the white regions represent the Cu2O shells and the remaining regions correspond to the internal cavities. Identical pore openings were introduced on each shell to allow inter-shell mass exchange. In the simplified 2D FEM model, the pore openings were uniformly distributed along each shell boundary. For the main simulations shown in Fig. 5, the pore diameter was set to 50 nm, and 6, 8, and 12 pore openings were introduced in the 1-, 2-, and 3-shell models, respectively. This pore-opening setting corresponds to the medium-porosity model used in the sensitivity analysis. To evaluate the influence of pore-related parameters, additional sensitivity analyses were performed by varying the pore diameter from 10 to 90 nm and the pore-opening density from low to high porosity levels. In the pore-size sensitivity analysis, pore diameters of 10, 30, 50, 70, and 90 nm were examined. In the pore-density sensitivity analysis, the low-porosity model contained 5, 7, and 10 pores in the 1-, 2-, and 3-shell models, respectively; the medium-porosity model contained 6, 8, and 12 pores, respectively; and the high-porosity model contained 7, 9, and 14 pores, respectively.

The “Laminar Flow” module was used to describe the fluid flow, providing the velocity field for species transport. A normal inflow velocity with an average value of 0.001 m s−1 was defined at the inlet, while the outlet was maintained at zero static pressure with suppressed backflow. Periodic flow conditions were applied to the transverse boundaries, and all other solid boundaries were designated as no-slip walls. Mass transport of all species was described using the “Transport of Diluted Species” module. At the inlet, a fixed average concentration of NO was applied (1000 ppm at 298 K and 1 atm based on the ideal gas law), while the outlet concentrations for all species were set to zero to simulate continuous species removal. The initial bulk concentration of H2O was specified as 5.54 × 104 mol m−3. The diffusion coefficients were summarized in Table S6. The “Chemistry” module was used to define the NORR reactions. Three species, NO, intermediate species (NH assumed) and NH3 were defined as feedstock, intermediate and product, respectively. Surface *H was supplied via H2O electrolysis, and all species were allowed to adsorb on the nanoreactor surfaces. Six elementary steps were considered, including surface adsorption-desorption equilibrium reactions of NO, NH, and *H, and three irreversible reactions (NO → NH, NH → NH3 and H2O → *H). The “Surface Reactions” module was performed to define the catalyst surface where the reactions take place. The local species concentrations calculated from the transport module were directly fed into the kinetic expressions. For the “Surface Reactions” module, the initial surface concentration of all adsorbed species on the catalyst surface was specified as 0 mol m−3. Unless otherwise specified, all simulation parameters were kept identical across the three catalyst models.

Simulations were conducted at 298 K and 101325 Pa. The velocity field from the fluid flow and the concentration field from the mass transport and reaction were explicitly linked via the built-in multiphysics coupling interface of “Reactive Flow, Diluted Species”, thereby establishing a fully coupled reaction-transport system. It should be noted that the FEM model provides a simplified representation of mass transport and surface reactions in the multishell nanoreactors. Therefore, the FEM results are used to support mechanistic interpretation rather than to provide a complete quantitative description of the catalytic system.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations

In this study, all of DFT calculations were performed in Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP)56,57 alongside the Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE)58. Exchange and correlation energies were derived using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional59. Van der Waals (vdW) corrections were incorporated through the application of Grimme’s DFT-D3 method60. Inner electrons were modeled using projector augmented wave (PAW) pseudopotentials61,62, setting a cutoff energy at 400 eV.

The Brillouin zone sampling was executed via a Γ-centered k-point mesh, generated employing the Monkhorst-Pack method. Geometry optimizations proceeded until the force on each atom diminished below the convergence threshold of 0.03 eV/Å, ensuring energy convergence within 10−6 eV. The DFT + U method was employed for Cu2O to correct the electron self-interaction, with the U value set to 3.6 eV. Transition states were identified using the CI-NEB and DIMER methods. The transition-state structures were converged until the residual forces were below 0.06 eV/Å, with a tighter electronic energy convergence criterion of 10−7 eV.
