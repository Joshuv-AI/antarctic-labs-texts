Intensive home treatment: an exploratory study on when patients in psychiatric crises benefit
Authors: Leo Jan Klunder, Dorien Spanhaak, Oswald Jan Nicolaas Bloemen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntensive Home Treatment (IHT) is an acute intensive outpatient treatment intended to replace or shorten clinical admission for patients in psychiatric crisis. Recent studies have demonstrated that IHT is noninferior to clinical admission in several patient groups. However, it remains unclear which patient characteristics, if any, predict the degree of reduction in crisis severity after IHT. Furthermore, the effectiveness of IHT in reducing crisis severity when used to shorten clinical admissions remains unclear.AimsTo elucidate what variables predict the course of crisis severity in IHT.MethodWe conducted an exploratory prospective naturalistic study. We recruited 120 participants referred for IHT. A list of putative predicting variables was constructed on the basis of interviews with IHT staff and a patient and support system council. To measure crisis severity, symptom severity questionnaires (SQ-48; symptom questionnaire 48) were administered at the beginning and after 3 and 6 weeks of IHT.ResultsNone of the putative predicting variables predicted the course of crisis severity during IHT. Crisis severity decreased, the mean SQ-48 decreased from 84 to 46 (Cohen’s d 1.25, 95% CI 0.66–1.81). Participants with bipolar or psychotic disorders reported less severe baseline symptoms but improved equally to other participants. In participants receiving IHT after admission, no clear improvement was observed.ConclusionsCrisis severity decreased during IHT, but baseline participant characteristics did not predict outcome of IHT. The lower baseline crisis severity in participants with bipolar or psychotic disorder suggests that clinicians have a different referral pattern to IHT for patients with these diagnoses. IHT did not further improve crisis severity if initiated after clinical admission. This suggests that both the need for IHT as aftercare and its timing should be critically evaluated by clinicians.Keywords: Crisis resolution, Outpatient care, Naturalistic, Transdiagnostic

Background

Intensive home treatment (IHT) is an acute intensive outpatient treatment aimed at stabilizing patients with an acute psychiatric crisis in their home environment, thereby preventing or shortening hospital admissions. IHT may involve up to two home visits per day, providing both clinical care and crisis management. In recent years, IHT teams have become widespread, particularly in countries and regions such as the Netherlands, the United Kingdom, Norway and Catalonia, and have significantly influenced the management of psychiatric crises [1].

Research on the efficacy of IHT has increased and has focused mostly on hospitalization as an endpoint (summarized in a Cochrane review in 2015) [2]. Recent randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that compared with traditional inpatient treatment, IHT can effectively and safely reduce the duration of hospital admissions [3, 4]. Compared with hospitalized patients, patients who received IHT had lower use of hospital-based (and other) psychiatric services within 1 year following IHT and did not differ in quality of life, psychosocial functioning, symptom severity or recovery [5]. Martín-Blanco et al. recently showed that previous hospitalizations and higher baseline clinical severity predicted hospital admission during IHT. Overall, the published percentages of patients requiring hospitalization during IHT treatment range from 4.6 to 36.6% [1].

Hospitalization rates in these studies serve as a proxy for better patient outcomes. However, the decision to admit an IHT patient to the hospital may be influenced by factors other than psychiatric crisis or symptom severity. For example, patient-specific factors (preference, caregiver burnout), referrer-specific factors (earlier successful hospital referrals), or organization-specific factors (availability of beds in the area) were included. Evidence is lacking on which patient characteristics predict improvements in patients with psychiatric crisis. One study reported that there was no convincing evidence that clinical or environmental factors predict the effect of IHT versus treatment as usual (hospitalization or outpatient care) after 26 weeks of follow-up [6]. Another study examined predicting factors, but focused on a subacute population for whom a referral-to-start time of two weeks or more was considered acceptable [7]. However, no study has specifically addressed the acute phase of IHT and which patients benefit in terms of functioning and symptom severity. As IHT is an acute intervention aimed at decreasing crisis within the first few weeks, this knowledge is important for clinicians. Furthermore, patients commonly also receive IHT following hospitalization to enable discharge or shorten the hospital stay. There is no evidence in the literature about the efficacy of IHT after hospitalization or the characteristics of patients referred with this indication.

The current exploratory study aims to bridge these knowledge gaps by investigating variables predictive of successful IHT outcomes during psychiatric crises. We hypothesize that identifiable patient-related or clinical variables influence the effectiveness of IHT interventions, thereby providing valuable guidance for optimizing future crisis management strategies.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed as an exploratory prospective naturalistic study, with the aim of improving our ability to predict the effect of IHT as a treatment modality.

We selected possible predictive variables through iterative consultation of patient council members, family council members, and IHT-treated nursing staff. We presented a list of variables, discussed them, and amended them after every consultation.

We performed the study from 2018 to 2022 in an urbanized region in the Netherlands with smaller cities in the vicinity of Amsterdam and Utrecht with approximately 250,000 inhabitants (Gooi-en Vecht area). The intensive home treatment teams provide highly frequent crisis treatment with up to two home visits per day. Patients are referred to IHT through three pathways, each aimed at reducing psychiatric crisis while serving as an alternative to clinical admission. Patients were referred (I) following crisis resolution intake after initial referral by a general practitioner, (II) at the point when another treating mental health professional would have otherwise initiated clinical admission or deemed the crisis severe enough to necessitate admission within days, or (III) when a clinical ward psychiatrist determined that early discharge with IHT support was a viable option. The alternative available treatment was clinical admission to either a high-intensity care clinic or an open-door clinic. IHT and clinical staff work closely together to discharge or admit patients as soon as possible.

The IHT team operates 7 days a week, from 9:00–23:00. The team consists of a psychiatrist, a resident physician, and multiple nurses. During night hours, a crisis service team consisting of a psychiatrist, a resident, and a social-psychiatric nurse was available. This crisis service team is physically and operationally integrated with the IHT team. In the Netherlands, a nationwide model fidelity audit has been performed [8] to determine adherence to the IHT model. Our team had a score of 135 out of 195, which is a midrange score. For most subjects, the team had high model fidelity, with notable exceptions for the absence of a cotreating psychologist and no standardized procedure of referral before and after clinical admission. The standard duration of treatment for IHT was 6 weeks, but the duration of treatment could be shorter or longer depending on necessity. The treatment period was defined as starting at intake and ending when the IHT contact frequency decreased to 2 per week or less.

Recruitment of participants

In the period from May 2018 to March 2021, we approached all patients who met the inclusion criteria for informed consent for participation in this study, except for the months March–April 2020, in which the IHT team readjusted during the first of three COVID-19-related lockdowns in the Netherlands. A power calculation indicated that a minimum sample size of 91 participants was required to detect an effect size of 0.20 with a power of 0.8 and an alpha of 0.05. To account for an estimated 20% drop-out rate, the target sample size was increased to 120.

Participants were recruited for the study if they lived in the Gooi-en Vecht area, had an indication for IHT after specialized intake, were 18 years or older and were able to provide consent. Participants were excluded if there was a safety risk that could only be averted by admission to a clinical ward or inability to speak and/or write Dutch. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Ethical approval

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Assessment Committee (METC) of the Maastricht University Medical Centre, with the decision document 2018 − 0373, as it was considered to not be subject to the Wet Medisch-Wetenschappelijk Onderzoek (Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act) given the lack of patient burden and not utilizing patient material. The study protocol was designed to comply with the Code Goed Gedrag (Code of good behavior regarding patient data).

Informed consent

Potentially eligible participants were informed about the possibility of participating in this study at the time of assessment for IHT. The participants were informed that failure to participate would not affect the care they received and that they could withdraw their consent at any time. Written consent was obtained from all participants.

Procedures for data collection

We collected data at baseline and at 3 and 6 weeks post-baseline. The main outcome was crisis severity, and the secondary outcomes were clinical admission, mandatory care and suicide. Staff measured crisis severity via the Symptom Questionnaire-48 (SQ-48) [9, 10]) at every interview. The SQ-48 is a 48-item self-report measure spanning seven psychopathology domains and two functioning domains (Depression, Anxiety, Somatization, Agoraphobia, Aggression, Cognitive problems, Social Phobia, Work functioning, and Vitality). The subscales’ Cronbach alpha coefficients range from 0.78 to 0.98. The SQ-48 correlates well with several other instruments, including those for overall psychopathology severity. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, and higher total scores reflect greater symptom severity. The SQ-48 was developed for comprehensive symptom screening and routine outcome monitoring in mental health care and is available in the public domain [9]. For pragmatic reasons, we used a total score of this multidimensional questionnaire. No questionnaire has been specifically validated for crisis severity, but we believe that the combination of psychopathology and functioning of the SQ-48 offers a pragmatic proxy of crisis.

All demographic characteristics and predictive factors were assessed at baseline. Sex and age were recorded on the basis of the patient’s passport or identity card. The primary diagnosis was derived from the DSM-5 classification documented in the patient file. The indication for IHT was categorized as either a primary intervention or clinical aftercare. Drug or alcohol abuse was considered present if the patient met at least one of the following criteria: a DSM-5 substance use disorder diagnosis, anamnestic evidence of recent use, or a positive drug test. Household composition was classified as a number of people living with the participant. The presence of a good friend was assessed through a yes/no interview question. Educational attainment was categorized from no formal education beyond primary school to the completion of higher education. The estimated degree of cooperation was rated on a scale from 1 to 10 by IHT staff. A history of IHT, as well as clinical and involuntary admissions, was quantified on the basis of the number of documented occurrences in the patient file.

Statistical analysis

Multilevel modeling was used to account for the nested structure of the data, with repeated measurements across timepoint (level 1) nested within patients (level 2) and the SQ-48 score as the dependent variable. Level 2 included the individual-level variables mentioned above. All baseline variables, timepoints, and their interaction terms (i.e., variable × timepoint) were initially included as fixed effects.

Model reduction was based on significance thresholds, with nonsignificant main effects and interaction terms excluded from the final model. Missing data were handled via pairwise deletion. If any variables were found to have significant main effects or significant interaction terms were found to be significant, before-after two-tailed paired t tests were conducted to estimate effect sizes. The assumptions of normality (assessed via Shapiro–Wilk tests, visual inspection of QQ plots, and skewness and kurtosis checks) and homogeneity of variance were met for all timepoints for the main outcome, including the last time point, although the remaining sample sizes were small.

Given our interest in the effect of clinical admission before IHT, we compared the admitted and not admitted groups before and after IHT, regardless of whether this was a main or interaction effect in the previous stage. Pearson’s chi-square test was used to assess the secondary outcomes (no suicides or mandatory care occurred). Drop-out analysis was conducted by using t tests for continuous data and chi-square tests for ordinal data to compare participants who dropped out and those who did not. The data were analyzed via SPSS 28, and graphs were generated via SPSS 28.

Results

We recruited 120 participants from May 2018 to June 2021 (Table 1). All participants provided written informed consent. For the SQ-48 questionnaire, we obtained 103 responses at baseline, 74 responses at t = 3 weeks, and 21 responses at t = 6 weeks. This decrease was primarily due to participants transitioning from IHT to regular outpatient care.

Drop-out analyses were conducted with drop-out status at the three-week point and drop-out status at the 6-week point as the dependent variables. Descriptive statistics

The participants were 80 women (66.7%) and 40 men (33.3%). The average age was 43.9 years (SD 15.8, range 18–77, missing 1, 0.8%). The participants were diagnosed with a variety of psychiatric disorders, with a relatively large fraction suffering from depression (44.2%). Among the participants, 68 (56.7%) lived in a single-person household. Nineteen (15.8%) had no or lower education, 40 (33.3%) had middle-level education (at least 12 years), 29 (24.2%) had completed higher education, and data were missing for 3 participants (2.5%) (Table 1).

We assessed the reason for referral to IHT. Forty-six participants (38.3%) were referred for ‘IHT as a primary intervention’ (i.e., without clinical admission), and 74 participants (61.7%) were referred for ‘shortening clinical admission or aftercare after clinical admission’.

Main outcome measures

At intake, participants reported an average SQ-48 score of 82, indicating high levels of psychological distress. Over time, the scores decreased substantially, with average scores of 61 at three weeks and 46 at six weeks, reflecting a reduction in crisis severity. This change over time was statistically significant (p < 0.001), and the effect size for the reduction from baseline to six weeks was large (paired t-test Cohen’s d = 1.25; 95% CI: 0.66–1.81), suggesting meaningful clinical improvement (Fig. 1).

We then proceeded with the main objective; to assess which variables predict the clinical course, we assessed all selected potential predictive variables for interactions with crisis severity over time. None of the variables showed significant interactions with time or with changes in crisis severity. Thus, we found no variable that predicted changes in crisis severity during the study period.

Since clinical admission likely reduces crisis severity and diagnosis may influence the likelihood of admission, we aimed to determine whether prior clinical admission before IHT could explain the lower crisis severity in patients with bipolar disorder or primary psychotic disorder. While participants with bipolar disorder or primary psychotic disorder were admitted more often before starting IHT (67% vs. 26%), subgroup analysis controlling for whether participants were admitted or not showed that, regardless of whether they were admitted, baseline crisis severity was lower in participants with bipolar disorder or primary psychotic disorder (p < 0,05, in both admitted or not subgroup t tests).

Although none of the variables significantly predicted the course of crisis severity, we found no improvement in the group of participants referred for IHT as clinical aftercare, as opposed to having found improvement in the group of participants referred for IHT as a primary intervention. The participants referred for IHT as clinical aftercare had a lower level of crisis severity at baseline. This level was comparable to the post-IHT intervention levels in both groups (participants clinically admitted before IHT and participants not clinically admitted before IHT) (Fig. 3). The group with IHT as aftercare did not significantly improve (p=-0.26 in a before-after t-test)).

Secondary outcome measurement

There were no suicides or suicide attempts during the study period. There were 8 clinical admissions during the study period. Five of these patients had a diagnosis of depression, but depression was not significantly associated with clinical admission (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.30). Moreover, no other variable predicted clinical admission during IHT (data not shown).

Discussion

Using an exploratory, naturalistic study, we assessed the course of crisis severity in participants with psychiatric crises who were treated by IHT and the possible variables influencing the course of crisis severity. The literature lacks adequate investigation of variables predicting IHT success, particularly during the acute treatment period that defines IHT (5). In our study, the crisis severity of participants decreased during IHT, in line with earlier research on IHT effectiveness [3, 4, 7, 11, 12]. Among the putative predictive variables, none predicted the course of crisis severity. Participants with bipolar and psychotic disorders showed lower crisis severity at all time points but improved at rates comparable to other participants. We found no significant improvement in participants referred to IHT as clinical aftercare, although we found no statistically significant difference in the course of crisis severity compared to IHT as a primary intervention. This is in line with findings from the field of IHT in adolescent psychiatry, published during the inclusion phase of our study, in which all putative predictors were found to have no effect on the course of crisis severity [13]. This is also consistent with a recently published observational study of a subacute IHT population, which concluded that IHT is effective for a broad spectrum of psychiatric patients [7]. Thus, our data provide no evidence that IHT should be limited to a specific population and that there appears to be no reason for preferentially offering this treatment modality to specific patients. Particularly in the context of the established noninferiority of IHT compared to clinical admissions [1, 3–5, 11, 14], the lack of apparent contraindications is a positive finding for the crisis resolution field.

In this study, we defined a psychiatric crisis as a severe decline in mental well-being that disrupts a person’s capacity to care for themselves or to function in society. Assessing the severity of psychiatric crises is inherently challenging, as it involves evaluating a complex and often subjective experience that is not fully captured by symptom-based measures, biological indicators, or diagnostic classifications. Many researchers have studied the number of clinical admission days or societal costs, which could serve as proxies of crisis severity [3–5, 11, 12, 14]. However, these approaches do not capture crisis severity directly as decisions for admission may be influenced by other factors than individual patient crisis severity. In contrast, our study employed a more direct method in which an interview-administered questionnaire was used. This approach aims to capture both the subjective and objective aspects of crisis severity at the patient level. However, we acknowledge that any measurement tool is limited and that a single score cannot fully represent the level of distress or the precise care needs of each individual. Despite these limitations, we believe that changes in crisis severity over time are meaningful and can be captured with repeated interviews.

Support system

Earlier qualitative research indicated that social support is considered highly important for IHT effectiveness and feasibility by caregivers and members of the support system [15]. Furthermore, recent studies on IHT referrals have shown that the presence of family support is a predictor of referral to IHT when there is a choice between clinical admission and IHT [16, 17]. Therefore, we expected the presence, involvement, and connection with a support system to have a positive effect on the decrease in crisis severity. However, in our data, there was no relationship between household size, support system size (including absence thereof), the presence of any good friend and crisis severity and a decrease in crisis severity. A possible explanation is that those participants who were selected during intake were correctly believed to not need social support for a good IHT intervention. However, early drop-out was associated with reporting not having a good friend, and as the mechanism remains unclear, it cannot be ruled out that this might have influenced our findings. Our results nevertheless show that crisis severity was ameliorated during IHT, even for patients without a support system.

Severe psychiatric conditions

Compared with other participants, participants with a diagnosis of bipolar or primary psychotic disorders had a similar reduction in crisis severity during the IHT intervention. These findings suggest that IHT is equally effective for severe or more chronic disorders. This contrasts with a recent study where having a psychiatric history predicted less improvement in well-being and symptom severity [7]. One possible explanation for this difference is that psychiatric history is a broader concept than diagnosis alone, which was not separately considered. Another explanation is that the study focused on a subacute population (mean referral-to-start time of 15 ± 52.9 days), whereas we studied a group in acute crisis in whom psychiatric history might be less predictive of severity. Counterintuitively, we found that participants with a diagnosis of bipolar or primary psychotic disorders had lower crisis severity at baseline in our sample. Participants with these diagnoses were admitted more often, and since admission likely reduces crisis severity this might explain part of this finding. However, admission cannot fully explain this effect, as crisis severity was lower in participants with these diagnoses regardless of being in the IHT as aftercare or IHT as the primary intervention subgroup. We believe that the most likely explanation for the observed lower crisis severity is referral bias by staff members. Staff members may perceive a crisis as more severe in participants diagnosed with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder. This perception could have influenced referral decisions at three time points resulting in a lower measured severity. First, these participants could be referred to IHT at a lower crisis severity, increasing the number of participants with a low severity in the primary intervention subgroup. Second, participants with high crisis severity and these diagnoses may have been referred for clinical admission more often, reducing the number of participants with a high severity in that same primary intervention subgroup. Third, participants with these diagnoses who were admitted could be referred to IHT only when their crisis severity was lower, leading to lower severity in the aftercare subgroup. Future studies should obtain data from clinical admission to further clarify these issues. Nevertheless, adding to recent data showing favorable results in patients with more severe symptoms or previous hospitalizations as well [1], we found no reason in the data to be more reluctant to use IHT in patients with more severe mental illness.

Secondary outcomes

None of the participants in the study population committed suicide during the study period. This is within the margin of expectations given the best-known estimation of suicide incidence during IHT at 14.6 per 10 000 [18, 19]. When this rate was applied to our sample (n = 120), the expected number of suicides was 0.18. Among our participants, 8 (6.7%) were admitted to a clinic. This number is the same percentage reported in the Martín-Blanco cohort (6.7%), and it is relatively low compared with that reported in the Cornelissen and Stulz studies [1, 3, 4]. In these studies, clinical admissions ranged from 62 to 99%, which is at least in part due to their designs. In their designs (using Zelen randomization), participants were first randomized to care as usual or IHT and then often clinically admitted (for at least a day) before the start of IHT and informed consent and recruitment [3, 17].

IHT as aftercare

An interesting finding in this study was the outcome of IHT as clinical aftercare. The role of IHT as aftercare is to enable discharge and resocialization when otherwise not possible, thus leading to a shorter clinical admission. In contrast, IHT as a primary intervention focuses on crisis stabilization within and with the help of the support system. In our study, IHT as a primary intervention resulted in significantly lower psychiatric crisis severity. In contrast, participants who were referred for IHT intervention after clinical admission did not show amelioration of psychiatric crisis. We believe that this lack of effect is most likely a lack of further effect and that after clinical admission, no further improvement is expected. This interpretation aligns with recent studies [3] in which the effects of clinical admission and IHT intervention on crisis severity were roughly equal. The lack of additional improvement and the lower baseline severity after clinical admission suggest that patients may be hospitalized longer than necessary. Patients might be discharged after a shorter admission, might not need clinical admission at all or might not need IHT after admission.

Limitations

An important limitation inherent to our exploratory design is the lack of a control group that would allow us to possibly attribute the amelioration of crisis to the intervention. Furthermore, we cannot control for the effects of the duration of clinical admission or crisis severity at the start of clinical admission, as we did not collect data on these effects. Another important limitation is the lack of follow-up, especially at six weeks. Although no variables were associated with 6-week drop-out, the low follow-up may have influenced our results, potentially increasing the risk of type II errors. Our data, which were obtained in a single-center study, are not necessarily representative of regions other than where the data were collected. IHT teams are always positioned within a regional context of other available forms of acute and intensive care, leading to regionally varying forms. Nevertheless, we believe that the mechanisms of how and when IHT is beneficial do not significantly differ and can be generalized.

The fact that none of the baseline variables predict the course of crisis severity needs to be interpreted with caution. For the main outcome, the SQ-48 total score, it is important to interpret this multidimensional composite with caution, for example since changes in different subscales may balance out, yielding the same overall score. However, given the SQ-48’s good subscale correlations with other severity measures, we consider it likely that lower total scores reflect reduced crisis severity at the group level. Regarding potentially predictive variables, we cannot be certain that we have not omitted a predicting variable. However, by involving staff, patients and support systems in the variable selection process, we believe that the risk is minimized, especially within the context of trying to find clinically useful variables. There are two occasions where selection bias may have influenced our results. First, when participants were referred for IHT, as detailed earlier in this discussion, and second, when the IHT indication was given after assessment by the IHT staff. Given the exploratory naturalistic nature of our studies, the included population consisted of participants deemed suitable for IHT intervention at referral. Therefore, the composition of the population and treatment outcome is, at least in part, a reflection of the quality and nature of the referral (by referrers) and subsequent assessment (by IHT staff). The referral behavior observed in this naturalistic study likely reflects a real-world tendency to refer patients with severe mental illnesses even when their symptom severity is relatively low. Therefore, in practice, our findings are valuable for making optimal decisions after referral to the IHT. The strengths of this study include the baseline inclusion of 120 patients in this acute setting, its prospective design and direct assessment of patient crisis severity, rather than more societal or contextual outcomes such as admission or healthcare costs.

Conclusions

No baseline participant characteristics predict the outcome of IHT, whereas crisis severity decreases during IHT. The participants with and without social support improved comparably. Participants with more severe conditions, such as psychotic and bipolar disorders, had lower baseline crisis severity but improved comparably to other participants. We believe our findings respectively suggest a referral bias for patients with these characteristics, while there is no evidence that they benefit less.

IHT did not further improve crisis severity if initiated after clinical admission. This suggests that both the need for IHT as aftercare and its timing should be critically evaluated by clinicians.
