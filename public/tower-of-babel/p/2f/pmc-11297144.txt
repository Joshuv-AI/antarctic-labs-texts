Operational application of Fengyun geostationary meteorological satellites to cloud observation products
Authors: Mingbin Du, Shuang Luo, Jun Shi, Wei Guo, Jiating Zhang, Hao Gu, Wen Gu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Cloud products from geostationary satellites are the main alternative to surface synoptic cloud observations (SYNOP), and have become the baseline products for the development and construction of the Quality Management System (QMS) of integrated meteorological observation in China. This study addresses the needs of the China Meteorological Administration (CMA) for such an operational reform, and it is carried out using real-time observations obtained from the Fengyun-2E (FY-2E) geostationary satellite in 2012 to derive cloud total amount and classification using two different methods. Compared to surface SYNOP observations, the cloud total amount estimated by FY-2E is generally significantly lower (about 30% lower on average). The cloud classification resulting from the two methods used in this study is also significantly different from the classification obtained from surface observations (difference between 22 and 32%). The difference is smaller for the classification method, which uses additional auxiliary temperature profiles.

Introduction

Clouds play an important role in the Earth system. The average global cloud coverage is estimated to be about 65–70%1–4 and, hence, clouds have a significant impact on the energy balance of the Earth atmospheric system, because they reflect shortwave infrared radiation, while absorbing and emitting longwave infrared radiation5–7. Clouds can drive changes in weather and climate on multiple spatiotemporal scales8–10, which is of great significance for weather forecasting, climate change, cloud physics, weather modification, aviation, and other fields. Cloud amount and cloud type can be obtained through conventional observations from the surface. However, such observations are limited both temporally and geographically, and subject to several other factors. Observations from the surface are dominated by SYNOP observations (i.e., human made or produced by automatic stations using ceilometers), although complemented by ground-based radar/lidar networks11,12. The SYNOP observations of cloud from surface are affected by a certain degree of subjectivity, they are less reliable under certain atmospheric conditions (e.g., significant aerosols load, presence of fog, etc.), limited by the spatial distribution of stations, observation time, meteorological conditions (winds etc.) and other factors, making it impossible to obtain continuous and full coverage of cloud information13–15. Radar/lidar observations provide the most reliable cloud information (especially for cloud classification) and a good temporal sampling (of the order of minutes), but the limitation of the spatial coverage persists.

Satellite cloud images have the advantages of wide coverage, strong temporal continuity, and high spatial resolution, making them the only feasible way to observe clouds globally. The cloud products from surface meteorological stations are predominantly obtained by a visual method of in-situ results, based on the relatively subjective definition and understanding of clouds. Clouds observation is based on cloud shape, structure, cloud block size, brightness, color, movement speed, and local experience, which can easily lead to significant subjective errors. Satellites, with their wide spatial coverage and high temporal frequency, have become the main means of cloud observations, replacing traditional visual surface observations. In particular, geostationary meteorological satellites have the ability to continuously observe the temporal changes of clouds with wide coverage and high frequency (temporal resolution finer than 5 min), allowing to monitor the rapid changes of clouds in space and time16–19. Satellite cloud products are obtained by using fully automated algorithms, based on state-of-the-art technology and retrieval methods (optimal estimation, neural networks, etc.)20–23, while also saving human resources for manual/visual observations and improving operational efficiency. The precision and objectivity of data are usually enhanced by the use of auxiliary surface and atmospheric information (surface albedo, land cover type, NWP forecast, etc.). At the same time, responding to the quest for high-quality satellite data and applications, accurate cloud total amount and cloud classification products can effectively improve the knowledge of cloud height and microphysical parameters (phase, particle radii, optical thickness, etc.), which allows for improved retrieval of atmospheric temperature profiles, tracking of cloud winds, improvements of the numerical model parameterization scheme and forecast results17–19,24.

Automation of manual surface meteorological observation is promoted nationwide by the China Meteorological Administration (CMA) since 2010. The manual observation of clouds is gradually decreasing, making the corresponding cloud information more and more scarce. In 2013, according to the “Opinions of the China Meteorological Administration on the reform and development of comprehensive meteorological services at county levels”25, programs related to manual observations of cloud amount and classification from national meteorological stations were officially canceled. Following the modernization of meteorological developments and the construction of the Quality Management System (QMS) of integrated meteorological observation in China, cloud observation mainly relies on automated observation equipment. Among them, geostationary satellite observations are the main source of cloud data, and surface cloud observations become a supplement (i.e., for validation) to satellite observations. With the use of satellite cloud detection methods, cloud observations in China have made significant progress in terms of spatio-temporal coverage and resolution. The objective of this study is to review these progresses and apply two systematic methods to retrieve the cloud amount and classification, respectively. The retrieved products are validated with ground-based visual observations. This study is intended to provide a reference for the application of satellite cloud products in China.

Methods and data collocations

Both these methods present advantages and disadvantages, and their combined used in this study ensure a better constraint on the uncertainty on the derived CTA value. The first method (CTA1) is somewhat simpler than the second method (CTA2) in terms of calculations but cannot accurately reflect the cloud cover within the 2 × 2 pixels unit (i.e., at pixel level). The CTA2 method estimates the cloud cover for each pixel but requires the use of the radiative transfer theory and, hence, strong assumptions on the clouds/atmosphere system (e.g., single cloud, plane-parallel atmosphere, etc.), which may introduce systematic errors. Moreover, the accuracy of both methods is affected by the quality of the used cloud mask product13,34.

Cloud classification methods

The cloud classification of traditional SYNOP observations in China is carried out according to the specifications of World Meteorological Organization (WMO)35,36, and the clouds are classified into 10 categories, which are cumulonimbus (Cb), cumulus (Cu), stratus (St), stratocumulus (Sc), altocumulus (Ac), nimbostratus (Ns), altostratus (As), cirrostratus (Cs), cirrocumulus (Cc), cirrus (Ci), with subdivisions up to 29 sub-categories. The research on the standards for satellite cloud classification has also been advanced, in recent years, but no unified standard has been reached. The most used standard is that established by the International Satellite Cloud Climatology Project (ISCCP)37–41. The ISCCP mainly classifies clouds into 9 main categories, including low, middle and high-level clouds. The schemes of the SYNOP and ISCCP are shown in Fig. 2. The pressure and temperature corresponding to cloud height of the main categories of the SYNOP and ISCCP are based on the temperature profile of the U.S. standard atmosphere 1976 as an empirical illustration42. Clouds are generally encountered over a range of altitudes varying from sea level to the top of the troposphere, and the approximate heights of each cloud category are also preliminarily estimated. The ISCCP mainly classifies clouds based on the cloud-top pressures (obtained from the top temperature and the atmospheric temperature profile) and the cloud optical thicknesses. It is difficult to perform precise classification at nighttime, considering that the accurate retrieval of optical thickness is a challenge. Meanwhile, it is also difficult to establish an optical thickness retrieval model for FY-2E/S-VIIRS in practical applications.Figure 2CLC based on (a) WMO35,36 and (b) ISCCP46,47 schemes (the red curve represents the cloud top temperature at the corresponding height, while the brown, yellow and light-yellow areas represent low, middle and high-level clouds, respectively).

In this study, CLC methods are mainly based on the characteristics of spectral channels, and supporting daily operational application efficiently and conveniently. The cloud types are reduced to six groups plus the clear sky, hence seven categories: clear sky (Clr), cumulonimbus (Cb), stratocumulus/altocumulus (Sc/Ac), altostratus/nimbostratus (As/Ns), cirrostratus (Cs), cirrus densus (Cid), and mixed pixels (Mix), mainly due to technological limitations. Two methods are considered: the uniform pixel method (CLC1) and the temperature profile method (CLC2).

The uniform pixel method (CLC1) uses the cluster analysis with multi-channel satellite data, performing cloud detection (clear/cloudy) and cloud height assignment, with classifying pixels into three types: surface (or clear) pixels, medium low cloud pixels, and high cloud pixels. Then, the high cloud pixels are further classified into As, Ns, Cs, Cid and Cb. Through characteristics analysis of mean and SD while preprocessing pixels, representative characteristics of various types are identified for uniform pixels of clouds within segments (typically 32 × 32 pixels coverage each segment). And then cluster analysis is performed on CLC33,43–45, as shown in Fig. 3.Figure 3Flow diagram of the uniform pixel method (CLC1).

Flow diagram of the uniform pixel method (CLC1).

The temperature profile method (CLC2), as shown in Fig. 4, assumes that the atmosphere is horizontally homogeneous and in local thermal equilibrium, ignoring the scattering effect of the atmospheric gases. The radiation received by the satellite from the atmosphere and underlying surfaces (from land/sea surface or from the cloud top) is, under these assumptions, a simple function of wavelength and temperature. When the observation zenith angle of the satellite is small, the cloud top can be approximated as a blackbody, so the cloud height can be identified by interpolating the temperature profile of isobaric surfaces46–48. The cloud top height (CTH) can be derived preliminarily with interpolating environmental variables, such as temperature and water vapor profiles, which include the use of observations and parameters from numerical weather prediction (NWP) models49,50, e.g., European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECWMF). In practical application, conventional radiosonde of the last 3 days is used to calculate the temperature thresholds for cloud height assignment to ensure real-time operation. The radiosonde is analysis objectively according to the corresponding pixels using with method of Cressman, considering the low resolution of the station data. Fengyun-4 has greatly improved the quality of atmospheric temperature profile products, thanks to the measurements of the Advanced Geosynchronous Radiation Imager (AGRI) and the Geostationary Interferometric Infrared Sounder (GIIRS)51,52.Figure 4Flow diagram of the temperature profile method (CLC2).

Flow diagram of the temperature profile method (CLC2).

In addition, the use of these instruments provides a better constraint on the cloud type, because the effective height of a radiation depends on the channel used to observe it; this allows to categorize different types of clouds. Using the relationship between the water vapor channel and the infrared channel from satellite data to categorize different types of clouds is a common approach in meteorological analysis. The slope of the scatter plot between the water vapor channel and the infrared channel can provide valuable information for cloud classification based on to their distinct characteristics33,48,53. However, it’s important to note that the identification of the slope pattern and its implications for cloud classification can be complex and influenced by various factors, such as the cloud phase (water/ice), altitude, and the specifications of the satellite instruments used. For accurate cloud classification, it is often beneficial to combine this analysis with other data sources or techniques.

Results

The areas with higher CTA are located in the Yangtze River basin and its southern region, with cloud cover exceeding 50%. The central and western regions show larger CTA than the eastern region. Validation of CLC Products

Figure 8 shows the comparison between the cloud classification obtained using satellite observations (CLC1 and CLC2) and the classification obtained from surface observations. Both CLC1 and CLC2 are significantly different than the CLC values determined via surface observations. The mean difference (Fig. 8, left panel) is about 22% for CLC1 and about 30% for CLC2. This comparison statistics does not show any significant difference between day and night. The comparison per each cloud type (Fig. 5, right panel) shows that the higher identification accuracy for stratocumulus and altocumulus (up to 70%), and the lower accuracy (below10%) for cirrostratus and cirrus densus. The stratocumulus and altocumulus clouds are generally easier to classify due to their distinct characteristics and their emission in IR channels. These clouds are low to mid-level, and often have a more uniform structure and are less influenced by the complex optical properties of the atmosphere compared to higher or thinner clouds like cirrus. High-level clouds, on the other hand, are composed of ice crystals. They are often thin and can have complex shapes, which makes them more challenging to classify accurately using only IR channels36,46,47. FY-2E/S-VISSR has only 5 spectral channels, and the correlation between the infrared channels is high. Some cloud categories exceed the recognition ability of the Fengyun 2 series stationary satellites, and there are certain difficulties and accuracy limitations in CLC with them. To improve the classification accuracy of cirrus clouds, it is very beneficial to use multispectral data with more channels and advanced algorithms, e.g., artificial intelligence, which can be trained to recognize complex patterns in cloud data, improving classification accuracy38,39,56.Figure 8Error statistics of the CLC with 2 methods in daily observation time of manual station.

Summary and discussion

CTA and CLC are products of great significance for monitoring and early warning of natural disasters such as rainstorm, severe convection, typhoon, dense fog, etc. The distribution and change in CTA and CLC are also important indicators for atmospheric circulation studies, and the primary factors to be investigated in climate change research. The CTA and CLC satellite products derived from satellite observations present technical limitations that must be addressed and possibly overcome to allow a systematic application of these satellite products. This study was conducted in response to the needs of CMA to upgrade and improve CTA and CLC estimates, i.e., replacing surface observations with the use of operational satellite products (cloud mask, temperature profiles, radiance in thermal channels, etc.). Our study shows that the CTA estimated using FY-2E satellite data is generally significantly lower that the values estimated from surface observations, especially when the radiation estimation method is used (about 30% lower on average). Moreover, the satellite-based CLC significantly differs from the classification based on the SYNOP observations. The satellite CLC results estimated from both methods used in this study are 22–32% accurate with respect to SYNOP observations; the higher accuracy (~ 70%) is found for stratocumulus clouds and altocumulus clouds, while the lowest (~ 10%) for cirrus clouds.

In the field of remote sensing, the use of geostationary meteorological satellites has been explored for operational application of the cloud observation tasks, as they can provide advantages such as wider perspectives and coverage. The comparison of geostationary satellite cloud observation products to SYNOP observations is analyzed, highlighting the potential for satellite data to complement or replace traditional cloud observations of which CTA and CLC are reported in visual surface observations. The satellite CTA results from various methods have shown generally significantly lower performance compared to the SYNOP observations. This may be due to the fact that satellite observations may not be able to fully capture all cloud layers under certain conditions, especially when the cloud layers are thin or the cloud structures are complex. The current satellite CLC products also show significant differences for high-level clouds, which are composed of ice crystals. These clouds present unique challenges for several reasons, which contain low emissivity and complex shapes of clouds, limited spectral information of instruments. Additionally, the observation characteristics of satellite and SYNOP have a significant impact on the comparisons. Overall, this study suggests that while geostationary satellites cloud observation is essential for operational applications, inaccuracies in current methods persist. Efforts to address challenges like improving satellite instruments and products retrieving methods are ongoing to enhance the accuracy of satellite cloud observation. The use of multi-source data has become essential in satellite observations in recent years. On one hand, the incorporation of auxiliary information in satellite observation methods is indeed essential and provides numerous benefits that enhance the understanding and modeling of the cloud. On the other, integrated space and ground-based cloud observations can be achieved with construction of automatic ground-based instruments (i.e., sky imager, radar/lidar), which can also be used to replace traditional visual surface cloud observations. The satellite cloud observations can be improved through calibration and verification of space and ground-based data.

The use of auxiliary information from atmospheric temperature profiles has improved the results of satellite CLC to some extent, by better estimating cloud heights44,48,49,57,58. The Fengyun 4 satellites are carrying the AGRI instrument equipped with 14 channels, while only five bands are available on the Fengyun 2 series. The advantage of using the advanced Fengyun 4 geostationary satellites is evident for the improvement of CLC, and relies into the capability to distinguish liquid water clouds, supercooled water clouds, thick ice clouds, thin ice clouds, multi-layer ice clouds, and mixed clouds. Thus, this study provides a solid basis for the China’s meteorological industry to develop and achieve a “precise cloud monitoring”, by focusing on developing remote sensing space-based instruments with extended spectral capability and automated cloud detection and classification methods (e.g., artificial intelligence), moving gradually away from traditional services based on surface observations.
