RRP9 Promotes Esophageal Squamous Cell Carcinoma Progression through E2F1 Transcriptional Regulation of CDK1
Authors: Gang He, Yilong Wan, Yanbo Zhu, Bo Peng, Shengxiang Shao, Xinyi Zou, Zhenyu Han, Jiaxi Li, Sheng Ju, Xin Tong, Jun Zhao, Hansi Liang, Liuqing Zhang, Jiashi Xiong, Dong Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEsophageal cancer is a major disease that seriously threatens human health. Ribosomal RNA biogenesis is implicated in tumorigenesis, while the small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs) are responsible for post‐transcriptional modifications of ribosomal RNAs during biogenesis, which are identified as potential markers of various cancers. The clinical significance, biological behavior, and potential molecular mechanism of the nucleolar small nuclear ribonucleoprotein RRP9 in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), which is a major pathological type of esophageal cancer, are investigated in this study. It is found that RRP9 is abnormally highly expressed in ESCC tissues and is closely associated with poor prognosis. Furthermore, it is found that RRP9 could regulate the cell cycle protein‐dependent kinase CDK1 to promote ESCC progression in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, RRP9 promotes ESCC progression through enhancing the E2F1‐mediated transcriptional regulation of CDK1. Collectively, the results defined RRP9 as an important tumor driver in ESCC that acted by activating oncogenic signaling by the E2F1‐CDK1 pathway, and suggested RRP9 as a candidate therapeutic target for ESCC.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer is one of the most common malignant tumors of the digestive tract, ranking sixth in the global cancer death rate.[ 1 ] The geographical differences in the incidence of Esophageal cancer are obvious. In developed countries such as Europe and the United States, Esophageal adenocarcinoma is the most common, while in African and Asian countries, Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) is the most common. In China, ESCC is the main pathological type of esophageal cancer, accounting for 86.3%. Although there are multiple types of esophageal cancer, the clinical symptoms are relatively similar.[ 2 ] However, the early clinical symptoms of ESCC are not significant, and most patients have been in the middle and late stages at the time of treatment, and even accompanied by remote metastasis. At present, radical surgery, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy are the main treatment methods for ESCC, and new treatment modes such as targeted therapy or immunotherapy can be considered for advanced patients. Even so, the 5‐year survival rate for patients with advanced esophageal cancer is only ≈20%. Therefore, the need for more sensitive markers for early diagnostic and more effective antitumor targets has become increasingly urgent for the diagnosis and treatment of ESCC.

The development of ESCC is a continuous process involving multiple steps and is extremely complex. Tumor development is indeed related to genes, in which abnormal expression of noncoding RNA directly affects tumor development. The small nucleolar RNA (snoRNA) is a kind of small noncoding RNA widely distributed in the nucleolus of eukaryotic cells. It binds with specific proteins to form nucleolar small ribonucleoprotein (snoRNP) to play an important role in the modification, processing, and folding of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) precursors. Moreover, it was found that the abnormal expression of snoRNA and snoRNP was closely related to the occurrence and development of various tumors. The SNORA21 and SNO‐RA42 could promote the progression of colorectal cancer, and be used as a biomarker for predicting the prognosis of colorectal cancer.[ 3 , 4 ] Nucleolar small ribonucleoprotein RRP12 was highly expressed in colorectal cancer, and the highly expressed RRP12 promoted the proliferation of colorectal cancer cells.[ 5 ] Ubiquitin ligase Smurf1 catalyzed the diacylation of nucleolar small ribonucleoprotein RRP9 to promote the development of intestinal cancer.[ 6 ] Similarly, we also found that RRP9 was abnormally highly expressed in esophageal cancer tissues and was closely associated with poor prognosis in patients.

The nucleolar small ribonucleoprotein RRP9, also known as U3‐55K, has been identified as a core subunit of the U3 snoRNP complex.[ 7 ] RRP9 protein, a member of the WD repeating protein family, is 475 amino acids in size and consists of an N‐terminal region and a WD repeating domain.[ 8 ] In eukaryotic cells, U3 and RRP9 proteins are essential for nucleolysis of pre‐rRNA. Specifically, the RRP9 protein is associated with U3, a complex that is critical for the process complex to produce 18S rRNA.[ 9 , 10 ] In addition, deletion of the C‐terminal WD repeat domain of RRP9 leads to loss of association with U3 RNA and loss of nucleolar localization of RRP9, indicating that protein‐protein interaction contributes to RRP9's localization and RNA binding.[ 11 ] In this study, although we had shown that RRP9 was strongly associated with poor prognosis in patients with ESCC, the biological function of RRP9 in ESCC cells and the underlying molecular mechanism were still unclear. Here, we experimentally validated the promotive effects of RRP9 during tumor progression in the ESCC model. We further revealed a previously unknown signaling axis that the interaction between RRP9 and E2F1 directly modulates CDK1 transcription in ESCC cells.

Results

RRP9 Expression Correlates to Poor Prognosis of ESCC Patients

It has been proved that small ribonucleoprotein RRP9 plays an important role in the occurrence and development of colorectal cancer and pancreatic cancer.[ 6 , 12 ] Similarly, we also found that RRP9 showed abnormally high expression in ESCC tumor tissues compared with normal tissues (Figure 1A,B), and its expression level was positively correlated with tumor stage in collected 50 ESCC biopsies (Figure 1C; Table S5, Supporting Information). Among all cases, ESCC patients with high expression of RRP9 have the poorer overall survival compared with those with low RRP9 expression (Figure 1D). To explore the role of RRP9 in cancer, we analyzed the expression of RRP9 in some tumor models, including head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC), tumor colon (liver metastasis), and Pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), from TCGA and other public databases (Figure 1E; Figure S1A, Supporting Information). Among some tumor types, higher RRP9 expression predicted poorer overall survival (Figure 1E). In addition, we found that abnormal expression of RRP9 was significantly correlated with T cell infiltration and lymph node metastasis (Figure 1F; Table S5, Supporting Information). Besides, we assessed the RNA expression level of RRP9 in five ESCC cell lines. All cell lines harbored RRP9 RNA at a detected level (Figure S1B, Supporting Information). Taken together, RRP9 is associated with poor prognosis in ESCC.

RRP9 Maintains the Malignant Behaviors of ESCC Cells

To directly test the putative tumor‐promotion functions of RRP9, we used a lentiviral vector to stably knock down human RRP9 in the ESCC cell lines with high RRP9 expression levels. In the ESCC cell line Eca‐109, RRP9 knockdown by three different shRNAs resulted in a significant decrease in RRP9 expression, especially the shRNA No.3 has the highest knock down efficiency (Figure S2A, Supporting Information). RRP9 knockdown by shRNA induced a significant decrease in RRP9 expression in ESCC cells Eca‐109 and TE‐1 (Figure 2A,B), and reduced cell proliferation compared to those transfected with scrambled shRNA (Figure 2C; Figure S2B, Supporting Information). Similarly, the flow cytometry for apoptosis analysis revealed that RRP9‐knockdown could impair the growth and promote the apoptosis of tumor cells (Figure 2D; Figure S2C, Supporting Information). Consistently, the cell cycle analysis indicated that RRP9‐knockdown in both Eca‐109 and TE‐1 cells led to a substantial increase in the G1 and G2 phases, accompanied by a decrease in the S phase (Figure 2E; Figure S2D, Supporting Information). In addition, cells with knockdown RRP9 expression exhibited an impaired ability of migration and invasion in two ESCC cell lines (Figure 2F,G; Figure S2E,F, Supporting Information). Moreover, we established subcutaneous xenografts of Eca‐109 cells stably expressing an shRNA targeting RRP9 or a scramble control, respectively, in nude mice. As a result, RRP9‐knockdown resulted in a significant reduction in tumor size and weight (Figure 2H; Figure S2G,H, Supporting Information). Collectively, our findings suggested that RRP9‐knockdown impaired ESCC cell growth and survival in vitro and in vivo.

RRP9 Regulates the Progress of ESCC via CDK1

To explore RRP9‐dependent gene regulation, we used the RNA sequencing data of esophageal cancer samples in the TCGA database for GSEA analysis, and divided the samples into high‐expression group and low‐expression group according to the median expression of gene RRP9. BioCarta analysis showed that DEGs were enriched in the EFP pathway, mitochondrial pathway, G2 pathway, cell cycle pathway, and several other tumor‐associated pathways (Figure 3A). Pathway Interaction Database (PID) assay also suggested that DEG was mainly concentrated in cell cycle‐related pathways, such as the PLK1 pathway and E2F pathway (Figure 3B). Moreover, the GSEA analysis revealed that the G2 pathway, cell cycle pathway, PLK1 pathway, and E2F pathway‐related genes were significantly enriched in both the RRP9‐positively expressed group (Figure 3C). Furthermore, down‐regulation of cell cycle‐related genes CCNL1, ESCO2, SGOL2, CDK1, and SMC2 was identified in RRP9‐knockdown cells by qPCR and WB analysis (Figure 3D,E). Especially, there was a significant positive correlation between the expression levels of RRP9 and CDK1 in esophageal cancer (Figure 3F). In a word, the CDK1 was significantly down‐regulated in RRP9‐knockdown cells.

RRP9/CDK1 Axis Sustains ESCC Cells Growth and Survival

We next investigated the underlying mechanism of RRP9‐induced ESCC cells growth. Our previous studies have found that CDK1 was a key downstream gene regulated by RRP9 in ESCC. Therefore, we knocked down CDK1 after overexpression of RRP9 to observe the regulatory effects of the RRP9‐CDK1 regulatory axis on ESCC cells growth (Figure S3A,B, Supporting Information). Importantly, silencing CDK1 significantly reduced cell proliferation in RRP9‐overexpressing cells (Figure 4A). Consistently, RRP9 overexpression inhibited the apoptosis of ESCC cell Eca‐109, and this inhibition was abolished after silencing CDK1 (Figure 4C; Figure S3C, Supporting Information). Similar findings were observed in the migration and invasion assay of ESCC cell Eca‐109 (Figure 4B,D,E). Furthermore, the tumor formation of the human Eca‐109 cells emerged after 4 weeks, accelerated tumorgenicity and larger tumor size were observed in the RRP9‐overexpressing group, while delayed tumorgenicity and smaller tumor size were observed in the CDK1‐knockdown group (Figure 5A–C; Figure S4, Supporting Information). Similarly, primary tumors from the RRP9 overexpressing group had many Ki67‐positive cells, and subsequent down‐regulation of CDK1 expression led to a sharp decline in Ki67‐positive tumor cells (Figure 5D). Together, our data demonstrated that RRP9 regulated ESCC cell growth and survival in vitro and in vivo via CDK1.

RRP9 Interacts with E2F1, the Upstream Regulator of CDK1, to Promote Tumor Progression

In the process of gene transcription regulation, transcription factors that bind to the promoter region of the gene often play an important regulatory role.[ 13 ] At the same time, it has been found that E2F1 could bind to the promoter region of CDK1.[ 14 , 15 ] To further confirm the binding of E2F1 in the CDK1 promoter in ESCC cells, we performed a ChIP‐qPCR analysis to locate the E2F1‐binding site on the CDK1 promoter in Eca‐109 cells. As a result, we found that E2F1 proteins were enriched in the CDK1 promoter region (Figure 6A). More importantly, it had been confirmed that RRP9 interacted with E2F1 by IP (Figure 6B). Moreover, the overexpression of RRP9 significantly promoted the binding of E2F1 with the CDK1 promoter in ESCC cells (Figure 6C). More importantly, we conducted a CHIP‐re‐CHIP experiment in the ECA109 cells overexpressing RRP9 that we previously constructed. The results showed that E2F1 and RRP9 were significantly enriched in the promoter region of the CDK1(Figure 6D). To further verify whether the interaction between RRP9 and E2F1 promoted the transcription of CDK1, we constructed different luciferase reporter vectors containing RRP9 or E2F1 together with wild CDK1 or mutant CDK1 promoter motif, and then detected the effects of RRP9 and E2F1 on luciferase activity of these constructs by performing a luciferase assay. As a result, we found that the mutant CDK1 promoter motif inhibited RRP9/E2F1‐mediated reporter gene expression (Figure 6E). In addition, in the case of high expression of RRP9, once the expression of E2F1 is downregulated, the expression level of CDK1 also decreases accordingly (Figure S5, Supporting Information). These results together suggested that RRP9 could interact with E2F1 and promote the transcription of CDK1 by enhancing the binding of E2F1 to its promoter (Figure 6F).

Discussion

Ribosomal RNA biogenesis has been implicated in tumorigenesis, while snoRNAs are responsible for post‐transcriptional modifications of ribosomal RNAs during biogenesis. In this study, we revealed the roles of the nucleolar small nuclear ribonucleoprotein RRP9 in tumor progression of ESCC. Furthermore, our in vitro and in vivo experiments validated the promoting effect of RRP9 on the ESCC model. At last, we uncovered that RRP9 interacted with E2F1, the upstream regulator of CDK1, to promote tumor progression.

Ribosome biogenesis is a highly regulated process that involves multiple steps and factors.[ 16 ] Alterations in the regulation of ribosome biogenesis can lead to an imbalance in protein synthesis, which is a hallmark of cancer cells.[ 17 ] Changes in RRP9 expression or function could potentially disrupt ribosome biogenesis, leading to increased protein synthesis and potentially contributing to uncontrolled cell growth, a characteristic of tumor cells.[ 12 ] In our study, high RRP9 expression correlated with poor patient prognosis and lower survival rates, suggesting that RRP9 could be a potential marker for ESCC. Moreover, knockdown of RRP9 restrained the progression of ESCC by inhibiting proliferation, migration, invasion, and enhancing apoptosis in vitro. It was further confirmed that the downregulation of RRP9 inhibited tumor formation in vivo. Collectively, we elucidated the promoting role of RRP9 in ESCC.

The underlying molecular mechanism of RRP9 involvement in the regulation of ESCC remains to be elucidated. In this context, we identified that CDK1 was a key mediator for RRP9 in promoting tumor progression in ESCC via IPA. CDK1 is a key regulator of the cell cycle and plays a critical role in cell division. It is involved in controlling the progression of cells from the G2 phase to the M phase of the cell cycle, where cell division takes place.[ 18 ] Dysregulation of CDK1 can have significant implications for cell proliferation and, potentially, the development of cancer.[ 19 , 20 , 21 ] Similarly, we confirmed that silencing CDK1 significantly reduced cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in RRP9‐overexpressing ESCC cells. Moreover, the tumor formation of the human Eca‐109 cells emerged after 4 weeks, accelerated tumorgenicity and larger tumor size were observed in the RRP9‐overexpressing group, while delayed tumorgenicity and smaller tumor size were observed in the CDK1‐knockdown group. In addition, the expression levels and activity of CDK1 in adrenocortical carcinoma can serve as a prognostic marker and a therapeutic target.[ 21 ] Here, we indicated that high expression of both RRP9 and CDK1 was correlated with poor patient prognosis. Currently, the inhibitors of CDK1 and related kinases have been developed and studied in clinical trials.[ 22 ] Taken together, it suggested that RRP9 intervention combined with CDK1 inhibitors could better inhibit tumor progression.

RRP9, as a key protein involved in ribosomal RNA (rRNA) processing, mainly functions to regulate the cleavage and modification of rRNA precursors, affecting ribosome biosynthesis. Nucleolar stress typically disrupts ribosomal RNA synthesis, impairing ribosome biogenesis. RRP9 deficiency or inhibition triggered nucleolar stress, leading to nucleolar protein translocation and release of free ribosomal proteins. These proteins bound and inhibited MDM2, stabilizing p53 to induce tumor cell apoptosis.[ 23 ] Esophageal cancer, particularly adenocarcinoma, was classified as a TP53‐mutant tumor, with ≈50% of cases harboring TP53 mutations. However, nucleolar stress regulates cell cycle and apoptosis not only through p53‐dependent pathways but also via p53‐independent mechanisms. The transcription factor E2F1 played a critical role in DNA damage and nucleolar stress independently of p53.[ 24 ] The previous study reported that E2F transcription factor 1 (E2F1) transcription activated CDK1.[ 15 ] Consistently, our data demonstrated that RRP9 could transcriptionally activate CDK1 by interacting with E2F1 in ESCC cells. The binding of RRP9 to E2F1 may induce conformational changes in the E2F1 protein, thereby enhancing its ability to bind to the CDK1 promoter and activate transcriptional activity. Additionally, RRP9 could act as a scaffold protein to co‐localize E2F1 with other transcriptional co‐factors at the CDK1 promoter region, forming a transcriptional microenvironment. The elevated local concentration may further promote transcriptional activation through a phase separation mechanism. Furthermore, there was a significant positive correlation between the expression levels of RRP9 and CDK1 in esophageal cancer. Of course, there must be a variety of other transcription factors in the promoter site of CDK1 to regulate the transduction of other signal pathways, which require in‐depth exploration in further research. Collectively, RRP9 could interact with E2F1 and promote the transcription of CDK1 by enhancing the binding of E2F1 to its promoter in ESCC cells.

Overall, we demonstrated that RRP9 was highly expressed and associated with poor survival of ESCC. RRP9 could bind to E2F1, altering the transcriptional activity of CDK1, and promoting ESCC progression (Figure 6E). Our studies expanded on the previously reported observations on the function of RRP9 and added novel mechanistic insights, and might contribute to the development of a therapeutic strategy for clinical ESCC treatment.

Experimental Section

Patients and Clinical Samples

The expression level of RRP9 was measured using the immunohistochemical assay in 50 patients with ESCC who underwent the operation and were confirmed by pathology at the First Affiliated Hospital Affiliated to Soochow University between 2018 and 2019. The clinicopathologic characteristics of all patients were described in Table S1 (Supporting Information). This study was approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of the First Hospital Affiliated to Soochow University (No.2019313), and all patients provided informed consent. Research involving human samples research had been performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cell Lines

The ESCC cell lines Eca‐109 and TE‐1 were purchased from the Procell Life Science&Technology Co., Ltd (RRID: CVCL_6898). The cell lines have been tested and authenticated, which verifies the authenticity of the cells by STR profiling during the experiment. In addition, the cell lines were cultured according to the supplier's instructions at 37 °C with a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2, and examined free of mycoplasma regularly.

Inducible RRP9 Overexpression, Downregulation, CDK1 Downregulation, or E2F1 Downregulation

ESCC cell Eca‐109 and TE‐1 were transfected with the lentiviral vectors encoding the human RRP9 gene, while the empty vector (EV) was used as a negative control. All the lentiviruses used in this study were provided by Genechem (Shanghai, P.R.C). Meanwhile, downregulation of RRP9 in ESCC cells was constructed using a shRNA technique (Genechem, Shanghai, P.R.C). Three shRNAs targeting specific human RRP9 sites and a negative control shRNA (shCtrl) are shown in Table S2 (Supporting Information). Furthermore, knockdown of CDK1 in ESCC cells stably expressing RRP9 or a negative control was reconstructed via the shRNA targeting specific human CDK1 sites (Table S2, Supporting Information). In addition, knockdown of E2F1 in ESCC cells expressing RRP9 or a negative control was reconstructed via the siRNA targeting specific human E2F1 sites (Table S2, Supporting Information). Finally, the overexpression or downregulation of RRP9 and the downregulation of CDK1 were validated by Quantitative reverse transcription‐polymerase chain reaction (qRT‐PCR) and western blot analysis.

RNA Extraction, Reverse Transcription, and qRT‐PCR

Total RNA was isolated by using the RNeasy Mini kit (QIAGEN). Then the first‐strand cDNA was synthesized by using the HiScript III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis kit (#R312, Vazyme, Nanjing, P.R.C). The qRT‐PCR was performed by using the ChamQb SYBR qPCR Master Mix kit (#Q311, Vazyme, Nanjing, P.R.C). Data were collected and analyzed with a CFX Connect Real‐Time PCR Detection System instrument (Roche LightCycler480 II, CA, USA). The GAPDH was utilized as a housekeeping gene, and the 2−ΔΔCt method was applied to calculate relative gene expression values. In addition, the primer sequences were listed in Table S3 (Supporting Information).

Western Blotting (WB) Analysis

Western blot was conducted as described previously.[ 25 ] Briefly, proteins extracted from the cells were resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) and then transferred to polyvinylidenedifluoride (PVDF) membranes (0.45 µm, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA), and then incubated with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer. The following primary antibodies were used: mouse anti‐RRP9 (Novus Biologicals, USA), rabbit anti‐CCNL1 (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), rabbit anti‐ESCO2 (Biorbyt), rabbit anti‐SGOL2 (Bioss), mouse anti‐CDK1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, MA, USA), rabbit anti‐SMC2 (Abclonal), rabbit anti‐GAPDH (Sigma–Aldrich). Horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugated secondary antibody (Anti‐rabbit IgG, HRP‐linked Antibody # A0208; Anti‐mouse IgG, HRP‐linked Antibody # A0216, Beyotime) was used.

Cell Proliferation Assay

ESCC cells were plated onto 96‐well plates at a density of 2.5 × 103 per well. After that, the growth of the cells was analyzed with the Celigo Image Cytometer (Nexcelom, USA) for five consecutive days.

Colony Formation Assay

Briefly, 500 cells were seeded into 6‐well plates. After culturing for 10 days, colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and then stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 30 min. Finally, the numbers of colonies were counted and analyzed.

Apoptosis and Cell Cycle Assay

Cell apoptosis was examined by using an annexin V‐fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)/propidium iodide (PI) kit (Miltenyi Biotec). For cell cycle analysis, ESCC cells were harvested and fixed in ice‐cold 70% ethanol overnight at 4°C. After fixing, cells were incubated with propidium iodide/RNase A staining solution (Sigma–Aldrich) at room temperature for 20 min. Furthermore, the labeled cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (Beckman Gallios, Germany), and cell cycle distribution was analyzed using FlowJo v10 software (Tree Star, USA).

Cell Migration and Invasion Assays

The Transwell migration, Matrigel invasion, and wound healing scratch assays were used to determine migration and invasion of the indicated cells. The detailed experimental protocol was based on a previously published reference.[ 26 ] For the cell migration and invasion assays, 1 × 105 cells were seeded on the top chambers of the Transwell coculture system (Corning, NY, USA). For the wound healing scratch assay, a scratch was generated when the cell growth reached 80% density. After that, cells were washed with PBS to remove detached cells. At last, images were captured on fluorescent microscopy (Nikon Eclipse Ti, Tokyo, Japan) at the respective time points (8 and 48 h).

Immunohistochemical Staining (IHC)

Paraffin‐embedded primary ESCC tumor tissues or xenografts from nude mice were sliced into 4‐µm thicknesses. Furthermore, histological sections were separately incubated with specific antibodies for RRP9 (1:100, Novus Biologicals, USA) and Ki67 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology) overnight at 4 °C. The immunodetection was performed on the following day using DAB (Dako, Carpinteria, CA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Subsequently, the samples were stratified into low expression (typically scored 0–4) and high expression (typically scored 5–12) groups based on the Immunoreactive Score (IRS). Finally, immunostaining scores were evaluated as described previously.[ 27 ]

Animal Xenograft Tumor

The six‐week‐old male athymic BALB/c nude mice were purchased from the Shanghai Experimental Animal Center of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, P.R.C.) and maintained under defined conditions at the Animal Experiment Center of Soochow University. To establish xenograft tumors, 5 × 106 Eca‐109 cells stably transduced with scramble shRNA or shRNA targeting RRP9 were suspended in 100 µL of phosphate‐buffered saline and Matrigel (BD Biosciences) at a ratio of 1:1, and then delivered via subcutaneous injections into BALB/c nude mice. For the function of the RRP9/CDK1 axis in tumor formation, 5 × 106 Eca‐109 cells overexpressing RRP9 or downregulated CDK1 were implanted into nude mice via subcutaneous injections. The size of subcutaneous tumors was measured every week, and the volume was calculated using the standard modified formula Volume (mm3) = (length × height2) × π/6. Then, tumor formation was observed for the following 1 month. After that, anesthesia was induced at a concentration of 3–5% isoflurane to cause rapid loss of consciousness, followed by cervical dislocation. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Soochow University (No.2019313).

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP) Assay

Chromatin was isolated from 1 × 107 Eca‐109/EV and Eca‐109/RRP9 cells. As described previously,[ 13 ] chromatin was crosslinked with formaldehyde (1%), disrupted, and immunoprecipitated with magnetic protein G dynabeads using 2 µg of the specific antibodies anti‐E2F1. Immunoprecipitated chromatin was unbound and reverse crosslinked using elution buffer at 65 °C. The amount of immunoprecipitated DNA for each specific antibody was quantified by quantitative PCR. Values of retrieved DNA were related to the amount of input DNA to quantify the efficiency of the immunoprecipitation. Negative controls included the same samples incubated with rabbit IgG as the primary antibody. In addition, the primer sequences of CDK1 were described previously.[ 28 ]

Luciferase Assay

The full‐length cDNA of human RRP9, E2F1, and CDK1 genes was constructed by General Biosystems (Anhui, China). The CDK1 promoter fragment was amplified from human genomic DNA with appropriate sets of primers. Mutations of the consensus E2F1‐binding site were synthetic. PCR and synthetic products were cloned into the luciferase vector pGL4‐basic. All constructs were verified by sequencing.

As described previously,[ 13 , 29 ] 293T cells were transfected with RRP9 or E2F1 together with the CDK1 reporter or pGL4‐basic luciferase reporter and Renilla luciferase plasmid using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The transfected cells were cultured for 48h. Cells were lysed and assayed with the Dual Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology). Each experiment was repeated three independent times.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) Analysis

Cells were collected in cell lysis buffer supplemented with the protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime Biotechnology). The cell lysates were immunoprecipitated with mouse anti‐E2F1 antibody (1:50, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, MA, USA) overnight, followed by incubation with Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (Thermo Fisher, USA) for 2 h. Finally, immunoprecipitated protein complexes were detected for E2F1(1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) and RRP9(1:3000, Invitrogen, USA) using WB.

Bioinformatic Analysis

The esophageal cancer and normal tissue samples from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) were analyzed. The datasets analyzed during the current study were available in the TCGA database TCGA‐ESCA project, [https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/projects/TCGA‐ESCA], and the standardized and differential analysis methods were used to analyze the differences of TPM data using the R Wilcoxon test. Using the median expression value of RRP9 in all samples of esophageal cancer as the threshold, the samples were divided into a high‐expression group and a low‐expression group. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the RRP9 high expression group and the RRP9 low expression group were shown (NP < 0.05 and FDR < 0.25) in Table S4 (Supporting Information). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the R/Bioconductor package clusterProfiler[ 30 ] with Hallmarks gene sets (version 7.1) from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB).

Statistical Analysis

All observations were confirmed by at least three independent experiments. The statistical values were measured using SPSS 20.0 software (Chicago, IL, USA) or GraphPad Prism 9.0. Specifically, the T‐test (for parametric data) and the Mann–Whitney U‐test (for nonparametric data) were used to determine statistical significance in comparison between the two groups. Meanwhile, the one‐way ANOVA test (for parametric data) or Kruskal–Wallis test (for nonparametric data) was used for multiple comparisons. In addition, the Spearman rank correlation test and Kaplan–Meier method were separately employed for correlation analyses and survival curves. Here, data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), n≥3. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
