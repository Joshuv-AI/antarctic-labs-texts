Quantum geometry in hexagonal circuit QED lattice with triple leg stripline resonators
Authors: Dongmin Kim, Jun-Won Rhim, Kyungsun Moon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, we investigate various geometric aspects of a photonic hexagonal lattice made of triple-leg stripline resonators (TSRs) in a circuit QED system. The inherent two-fold degenerate spatial modes of the TSR act as two distinct orbitals in our 2D lattice system. Remarkably the energy spectra of the system exhibits the dispersive quadratic band-touching to the top and bottom flat bands. Our analysis reveals how the system harnesses destructive interference to establish flat bands via stabilized compact localized states (CLSs). We further explore the real-space topology corresponding to the flat bands by finding proper non-contractible loop states (NLSs). Additionally, in a zigzag-structured hexagonal lattice, we demonstrate the induction of topological flat edge modes at zero energy by analyzing the Zak phase. We also elucidate the quantum geometric origin of other dispersive edge bands that arise from the singular point of the flat band.

Introduction

In condensed matter physics, flat band systems have received significant attention because various many-body phases are expected to occur due to the extremely suppressed Kinetic energy. Indeed, phenomena such as ferromagnetism1–4, superconductivity5–8 and Wigner crystallization9–11 are extensively studied within these systems. Not only observable in materials with Kagome-type structures12–18 or twisted bilayer graphene19–23, flat bands can also be engineered in artificial atomic systems, as represented photonic lattices24–30.

Photonic systems, especially those like circuit quantum electrodynamics (QED), offer enhanced control over parameters compared to fermionic systems, paving the way for the study of diverse and complex physical phenomena. Circuit QED system has showcased the ability to achieve strong coupling between superconducting qubits and microwave resonators31–34. This capability is not only pivotal in the development of gate-based quantum computers35–37 but also in creating photonic lattices that are ideal for quantum simulations38–41. The controllable parameters like hopping strengths allow for the realization of flat bands in intricate lattice structures, such as Kagome38,42–44 and Lieb45,46 lattices, through capacitively coupled superconducting microwave resonators.

In the paper, we explore the photonic hexagonal lattice using triple-leg stripline resonators (TSRs). A general superconducting resonator in a circuit QED system is a linear resonator with a non-degenerate mode. However, a TSR has two-fold degenerate microwave photon modes(s, p)47. Namely, one can study phenomena originating from the multi-orbital character of a system by using a circuit QED system consisting of TSRs.

The distinctive design of TSR, featuring three legs, enables the construction of a photonic hexagonal lattice akin to graphene, as illustrated in Fig. 1(a). Note that our system is distinguished from graphene in the sense that it possesses two orbitals per site. Electrons in graphene also have spin, which can be thought of as another degree of freedom in each unit cell. However the spin-orbit coupling in graphene is too weak to exhibit the multi-orbital characteristics. There exist other 2D Kagome-lattice materials which use this extra degree of freedom to obtain interesting physics (e.g. spin-valley qubits in GaAS) as well as an experiment using a honeycomb lattice made of coupled micropillars with four orbitals per unit cell in the A and B sublattice48–50.

We describe the lattice by formulating a tight-binding Hamiltonian where each TSR is capacitively coupled to its neighboring resonators. This led to an energy band structure featuring Dirac cones, one of the representative properties of graphene, alongside two flat bands above and below the Dirac points. In our analysis, by fine-tuning hopping strength, compact localized states (CLSs), which are eigenstates of the flat bands51,52, are stabilized by destructive interference in the hexagonal lattice structure. The CLS is a highly localized eigenstate of a flat band, with nonzero amplitudes confined to a finite region and strictly zero elsewhere. We note that these two flat bands belong to the singular flat band, whose Bloch wave function possesses a singularity in momentum space53. The singularity occurs at the band-touching point of the flat band, which makes the CLSs incomplete because the Bloch wave functions corresponding to the touching point do not participate in constructing the CLSs. Hence, some extended states exhibiting real-space topology, called the non-contractible loop state (NLSs), should be complemented to span the flat bands completely51. While the lattice structure can be interpreted as a toroidal geometry under periodic boundary conditions, the NLSs manifest as stripe-shaped modes that wrap around the entire torus along either the toroidal or poloidal directions. Interestingly, the singular band-crossing of the flat band is geometrically characterized by the quantum distance, which quantifies the resemblance between two quantum states by a positive number between 0 and 154–58. We show explicitly that we can examine various bulk-boundary correspondences in our TSR hexagonal lattice system. By considering finite-width TSR systems, we find two types of edge modes originating from the geometric properties of the bulk, such as the Zak phase and quantum distance. The Zak phase is a topological invariant of a one-dimensional system, determined by integrating the Berry connection over the entire Brillouin zone. When the system preserves reflection symmetry, the Zak phase of a band is quantized to either zero or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi$$\end{document}. Notably, if the total Zak phase of the occupied bands is an odd multiple of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi$$\end{document}, an edge mode is guaranteed to appear in the gap just above the topmost occupied band59. Additionally, another form of bulk-boundary correspondence arises from the quantum distance. In a singular flat band, even in the absence of a gap between it and a touching parabolic band, a dispersive edge band can still emerge between them. In this case, the maximum quantum distance around the singular point dictates the effective mass of the edge band55. We theoretically demonstrate the controllability of these edge modes by varying coupling strength to superconducting qubits60. Regarding the detection scheme, we theoretically demonstrate how coupling superconducting qubits to the edge TSRs enables energy-resolved detection of the edge states. This coupling shifts the frequencies of edge modes depending on the qubit states, allowing the dispersive and flat edge states to be identified and modulated in a controllable manner. This frequency shift also manifests as a pronounced reflection dip in input-output measurements at the edge port, providing an entirely microwave-based method to detect the edge modes. Such a mechanism provides a feasible and experimentally implementable way to observe the topological and geometric features unique to our TSR-based system.

Flat band analysis

For the flat band analysis, we apply the periodic gauge for the Bloch Hamiltonian of Eq. 3. We analyze the localized nature of the flat bands by finding compact localized states (CLSs). By normalizing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{v}_\pm (\textbf{k})$$\end{document}, one can obtain the normalization factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N(\textbf{k})$$\end{document}, which becomes singular at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{k}=0$$\end{document}6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} N(\textbf{k})={1\over \sqrt{8(3-\cos {k_1} - \cos {k_2}-\cos {(k_1-k_2)})}}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Hence we obtain an eigenvector discontinuous at the band-crossing point. These flat bands are called the singular flat band53. In this case, the singularity prevents N CLSs from forming a complete set for each flat band. To span the whole flat band, several extended states, called the non-contractible loop states (NLSs), should be complemented. The NLSs are also the eigenstates of the flat bands and are illustrated in Fig. 3(c) and (d). These are topological states in the sense that they cannot be smoothly deformed to CLSs on the torus geometry reflecting on the conventional periodic boundary condition.

Geometric aspect: edge state

First, we show that the flat edge modes at zero energy are induced topologically by calculating the Zak phase, which is defined by7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \gamma _n(k_x) = i\int _{-\frac{2\pi }{\sqrt{3}}}^\frac{2\pi }{\sqrt{3}} dk_y \textbf{v}_n^\dag (\textbf{k})\cdot \partial _{k_y}\textbf{v}_n(\textbf{k}), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where n is the band index. If the Zak phase is quantized, one can apply the bulk-boundary correspondence to predict edge modes. We consider an effective 1D system for a given \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_x$$\end{document}, which is gapped except at the Dirac point(\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_x=\pm 2\pi /3$$\end{document}). There exist an odd number of edge modes in the gap if the total Zak phase, obtained by adding all the Zak phases of the bands below the gap, is an odd multiple of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi$$\end{document}59. To investigate the zero-energy edge modes, we calculate the Zak phase of the two lowest bands. The Zak phase of the lowest flat band is evaluated to be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-2\pi$$\end{document} for all nonzero \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_x$$\end{document} because \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{v}_n^\dag (\textbf{k})\cdot \partial _{k_y}\textbf{v}_n(\textbf{k}) = i\sqrt{3}/2$$\end{document} in this case. On the other hand, the Zak phase of the second lowest band is calculated to be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma _n=\pi$$\end{document} for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-2\pi /3<k_x<2\pi /3$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma _n=0$$\end{document} otherwise because \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{v}_n^\dag (\textbf{k})\cdot \partial _{k_y}\textbf{v}_n(\textbf{k}) = -i\sqrt{3}\cos \frac{k_x}{2}(2\cos \frac{k_x}{2} + \cos \frac{\sqrt{3}k_y}{2})/(6+4\cos k_x + 8\cos \frac{k_x}{2}\cos \frac{\sqrt{3}k_y}{2})$$\end{document}. Second, we will explain the origin of other dispersive edge bands stemming from the singular points of the flat bands at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{k}=0$$\end{document}. The Zak phase of the lowest flat band, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\gamma _n=-2\pi$$\end{document}, cannot explain the existence of the edge bands right above the flat band. However, there exists another kind of bulk-boundary correspondence originating from different geometric notion, the quantum distance55,56. The quantum distance between two Bloch eigenstates with momenta \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{k}_1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{k}_2$$\end{document} is defined as8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} d^2(\textbf{k}_1,\textbf{k}_2) = 1- |\textbf{v}^\dag (\textbf{k}_1)\cdot \textbf{v}(\textbf{k}_2) |^2, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}which measures the resemblance between two quantum states. Therefore, one can extract the bulk geometric quantity such as the maximum quantum distance from the effective mass \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m_0/d^2_\textrm{max}$$\end{document} of edge dispersion around the band-crossing point. As illustrated in Fig. 4(b), two edge bands are connected to the upper and lower flat bands of the ribbon geometry in the hexagonal TSR system, highlighted by red dashed circles. Lateral qubit coupling to finite strip edges

Microwave input-output spectroscopy of qubit-controlled edge modes

Input-output reflection spectroscopy, a standard technique in circuit-QED lattices37,61,62, also serves as a theoretical framework for probing the edge modes in our TSR model. We consider a coherent drive applied to the s-mode of the upper-left TSR, with dissipation incorporated through internal and external Lindblad terms in the master-equation formalism. The full dispersive-frame formulation, including the non-Hermitian matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M(\omega _{\textrm{in}})$$\end{document} and its steady-state solution, is presented in Supplementary Information S4. Here we summarize only the resulting reflection coefficient, given by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_{11}(\omega ) = 1 - \sqrt{\kappa _{\textrm{ext}}}\,a^{\textrm{ss}}_{1s}/E$$\end{document}. The principal reflection dip in Fig. 6 aligns precisely with the qubit-shifted edge-state energy derived in the preceding subsection, exhibiting the expected shift \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta E=\tfrac{3}{4}\chi \sigma _{z}$$\end{document} at the maximally localized point (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k=0$$\end{document}). Consequently, the very same edge modes can be resolved either through the qubit’s frequency shift or through their microwave reflection signature, providing two complementary and experimentally compatible routes for detecting the topological boundary states of the TSR lattice.

Conclusion

In this work, we have demonstrated a novel approach to photonic lattice structures through the integration of TSRs in a circuit QED system. Our study successfully emulates a graphene-like hexagonal lattice, revealing distinct orbital behaviors and energy spectra. The intricate balance between the two-fold degenerate modes and hopping strength, dictated by the structural characteristics of TSRs, has led to an intriguing energy spectrum where we have both massless and infinitely massive particles. While the hexagonal lattice structure has been deemed unsuitable for destructive interference, the presence of multi-orbital characteristics in the hexagonal lattice enabled the emergence of CLS and a stabilized flat band. The entire band structure exhibits two distinct types of band crossings: the Dirac point and the quadratic band-touching of the flat band, which are geometrically characterized by the quantized Berry phase and the maximum quantum distance, respectively. Furthermore, our investigation into the ribbon geometry of the TSR system provided insights into the bulk-boundary correspondence and the dispersion of edge states. The coupling to qubits demonstrated the controllability of edge-localized states, showcasing the system’s potential for diverse photonic band structures and exploring geometric aspects. We further propose, at the theoretical level, two experimentally compatible schemes—dispersive qubit read-out and input-output reflection spectroscopy—that independently detect and characterize the qubit-controlled edge modes. We believe that one can explore more diverse types of photonic structures as well as the inherent geometric aspects utilizing resonators with multi-leg designs, similar to TSR.
