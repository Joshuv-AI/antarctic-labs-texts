Imaging findings in Fontan-associated liver disease: Results of a single center study
Authors: Sara Lewis, Renata Mazurek, Gabriel Nemzow, Kali Hopkins, Isabel Fiel, Alice Chan, Marcelo Facciuto, Bachir Taouli, Thomas D Schiano, Ali Zaidi, Lauren Grinspan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFontan-associated liver disease (FALD) is a consequence of Fontan circulation causing liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, portal hypertension, and potentially hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), with imaging essential to evaluation.ObjectivesTo describe the cross-sectional imaging features of hepatic morphology and focal liver lesions (FLLs) in patients with FALD.MethodsThis retrospective single-center study included patients post-Fontan procedure (10/2016-9/2022) who underwent CT or MRI and non-targeted liver biopsy for fibrosis staging. Two observers in consensus assessed CT/MRI for imaging findings of cirrhosis, portal hypertension, and FLL (>0.8 cm) detection and characterization, including size, enhancement pattern, and MRI characteristics. A composite reference standard (pathology, multidisciplinary tumor board, imaging characteristics, or FLL stability) informed FLL diagnosis. Biopsies were staged using the Congestive Hepatic Fibrosis Score (CHFS) [range, 0(no fibrosis)-4 (cirrhosis)]. Associations between variables were analyzed using logistic regression and Fisher's exact tests.ResultsResults from 41 patients [26M/15F, mean age = 29.9y, MRI, n = 24/CT, n = 17] are presented. CHFS were 1(n = 4)/2(n = 19)/3(n = 14)/4(n = 4). Imaging signs of cirrhosis were common: liver surface nodularity (n = 31) and volume redistribution (caudate lobe hypertrophy, n = 35). Varices and ascites were observed in n = 18 and n = 16 patients. 62 FLL were identified in 15 patients (mean size = 1.5 ± 0.7 cm). Diagnoses included benign-appearing enhancing lesions (n = 52 lesions/10 patients), indeterminate (n = 5 lesions/4 patients), HCC (n = 4 lesions/3 patients), and sclerosing hemangioma (n = 1). All HCC cases had CHFS = 3. No association between laboratory, CHFS, and imaging findings of cirrhosis was found (p-values>0.12).ConclusionsImaging findings of cirrhosis are discordant with fibrosis stage in FALD. Enhancing FLLs, including HCC, are common and frequently observed in noncirrhotic liver.Keywords: Fontan-associated liver disease, Computed tomography, Magnetic resonance imaging, Fibrosis, Cirrhosis, Hepatocellular carcinoma

Introduction

The Fontan operation is the final surgery in the staged palliation of single ventricle congenital heart disease in which the systemic venous blood is directly routed to the pulmonary arteries and bypasses the heart [1]. Fontan-associated liver disease (FALD) arises as a consequence of Fontan circulation and includes a spectrum of hepatic abnormalities that can include liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, portal hypertension, and potentially hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [2]. The duration of time since the Fontan procedure is the most consistent factor associated with the manifestations of FALD, with most patients remaining asymptomatic until the 3rd decade after Fontan completion [2]. Patients with advanced liver fibrosis may remain in a compensated state, yet outcomes are poor once cirrhosis and portal hypertension develop, with reported 1- and 5-year survival rates of 57% and 35%, respectively [2,3].

Morphologic changes of liver fibrosis and cirrhosis are observed on cross-sectional imaging, including ultrasound (US), computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). On US, heterogeneous echotexture or surface nodularity are reported in 67% and correlate with time duration after Fontan [4]. On CT and MRI, abnormal parenchymal enhancement is reported in 63% of patients [5]. A previous study reported discordance between morphologic findings of liver disease and liver function tests (82% of cases had normal liver function tests) despite the presence of parenchymal changes of fibrosis and cirrhosis on imaging [5]. Focal liver lesions (FLLs) are very common in adult patients who have undergone a Fontan procedure, with lesions present in up to 50% and increasing in prevalence with greater time after Fontan palliation [6]. Most liver lesions are arterially hyperenhancing at cross-sectional imaging and are believed to represent benign lesions such as regenerative nodules or focal nodular hyperplasia–like (FNH-like) lesions, developing due to the altered arterial flow in the liver [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. However, HCC has been reported to develop and most commonly arises in the context of advanced liver fibrosis or cirrhosis [6,10]. Outcomes following a diagnosis of HCC in this population are poor, with a 1-year survival rate of only 50%. At diagnosis, up to 50% of patients with HCC are asymptomatic and approximately 25% demonstrate normal α-fetoprotein (AFP) levels [2]. Considering these clinical challenges and implications for patients, imaging surveillance for HCC in asymptomatic Fontan patients is critical, even though the most effective imaging modality and surveillance frequency have yet to be determined [11].

The purpose of this study was to describe the CT and MRI imaging findings of liver morphology and portal hypertension observed in patients with FALD, as well as the prevalence and appearance of post-Fontan enhancing liver lesions, including HCC.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective HIPAA-compliant single-center study was approved by our institutional review board with a waiver of informed consent (STUDY-24-00680). The Mount Sinai Hospital Adult Congenital Heart Disease program database was queried from 10/2016-9/2022, for all patients who underwent Fontan palliation. Inclusion criteria were: (a) patients with Fontan procedures who underwent multiphase contrast-enhanced CT or MRI; and (b) right heart catheterization with non-targeted transcaval hepatic biopsy and portal pressure measurement (as proximal as possible to imaging; median = 152 days, IQR = 358 days; <3 months, n = 16, 3–6 months, n = 7, >6 months, n = 18). Exclusion criteria were: (a) no imaging or (b) the biopsy obtained at outside institutions without congestive hepatopathy fibrosis scoring. All patients were clinically optimized and in stable condition at the time of congenital cardiac catheterization with biopsy and at imaging, including those who underwent catheterization during a hospital admission (n = 7). Clinical indications for liver biopsy included evaluation of abnormal imaging findings, assessment of hepatic fibrosis, elevated LFTs or transplant evaluation, based on a published series from our institution [12].

Clinical and demographic data were recorded, including age (at biopsy), sex, race/ethnicity, cardiac diagnosis, type of Fontan, time since Fontan procedure, other etiologies of liver disease, portal pressures at transvenous liver biopsy, and subsequent treatment for patients diagnosed with HCC. Laboratory values collected included albumin, total bilirubin, direct bilirubin, aspartate transaminase, alanine aminotransferase, international normalized ratio (INR), platelets and AFP. Non-invasive scoring systems for assessing liver fibrosis, FIB-4 and APRI scores, were calculated [13,14]. Patient's medications during the study period were recorded.

Cross-sectional imaging acquisition

Multiphasic multidetector CT and/or MRI were performed using a variety of platforms using standard clinical protocols. On CT, these included GE Healthcare and Siemens Healthineers scanners (n = 17). Several multichannel MRI systems were used for scanning, including 1.5T (Magnetom Avanto, Aera, Sonata, and Symphony, Siemens Healthineers; and Signa HD, HDxt, Optima 450w, GE Healthcare) (n = 18) or 3T (Skyra, Siemens Healthineers, 750, GE Healthcare) (n = 6) platforms. The sequences and acquisition parameters varied slightly between different imaging platforms, however arterial phase (AP) images were obtained 20–40s after iodinated (CT) or gadolinium-based (MRI) intravenous contrast administration and portal venous phase (PVP) images were obtained 60–100s after contrast administration. Twenty MRI exams were performed with a liver specific gadolinium based contrast agent (gadoxetic acid, Eovist/Primovist, Bayer Healthcare); in these cases, equilibrium (EP)/transitional phase (TP) images were obtained after 3–6min of contrast administration, and hepatobiliary phase (HBP) images were obtained 10 to 20 min after contrast administration. Extracellular contrast agent gadobutrol (Gadavist, Bayer Healthcare) was used in the remaining 4 cases. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) was acquired (b-values 50, 800), with apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps generated automatically by the scanner.

Qualitative imaging analysis

The qualitative analysis of the liver parenchyma and FLLs was performed by two abdominal radiologists in consensus (Observer 1, with 12 years of experience; Observer 2, with 1 year of experience). The observers were aware of the diagnosis of FALD, but unaware of clinical and pathologic information.

Liver morphologic assessment: The observers assessed the following imaging features, defined previously, as either present, absent or equivocal, as follows: cirrhosis, liver surface nodularity, liver blunting, left lobe hypertrophy, right lobe atrophy, caudate lobe hypertrophy, periportal space enlargement, hepatic steatosis, hepatic iron deposition, portal vein patency, and hepatic vein patency [15]. The diameter of the suprahepatic IVC was measured on axial imaging. The spleen length was measured on coronal imaging. The presence or absence of abdominal ascites and varices were recorded. Staging of the severity of liver disease was also performed according to the following criteria: Normal (no cirrhosis or portal hypertension); Indeterminate fibrosis (morphologic changes in the liver, no portal hypertension); Compensated (cirrhosis and portal hypertension); Decompensated (cirrhosis and portal hypertension with ascites). A composite portal hypertension score (score range, 0-9) was also calculated based on the number of variceal sites (score, 0-3), the volume of abdominal ascites (score, 0-3), and the maximal craniocaudal diameter of the spleen (score, 0-3), according to published criteria [16].

Liver lesion assessment: Each CT and MRI was evaluated by the readers in consensus for the presence or absence of FLL. When present, the number and location (right lobe, left lobe, caudate lobe, central or peripheral) of FLL were recorded and the diameter was measured on the sequence they were best visualized on. All lesions ≥0.8 cm in size, based on a clinically used measurement threshold for biopsy feasibility, were counted and analyzed for per lesion imaging characteristics. Lesion hyperintensity/isointensity/hypotensity were recorded on AP, PVP, EP/TP and HBP for MRI (for those patients who underwent gadotextate MRI) and hyperdensity/isodensity/hypodensity for CT relative to the background liver parenchyma. For patients who underwent MRI, the appearance on DWI/ADC and T2-weighted imaging (hyperintense/isointense/hypointense) was recorded. Cross-sectional imaging performed after the index imaging study was evaluated by the two observers to assess lesion stability.

Reference standard for FLL diagnosis

A composite reference standard included histopathology, multidisciplinary tumor board (MDTB) adjudication, imaging characteristics, or lesion stability over time between 10/2016-7/2024, and categorized into the following diagnoses: benign-appearing enhancing lesions (consisting of focal nodular hyperplasia-like lesions, regenerative and dysplastic nodules), as reported previously [8,[17], [18], [19]], HCC, indeterminate lesions, and sclerosing hemangioma. Indications for MDTB adjudication or biopsy include the presence of lesion arterial phase hyperenhancement and washout. There is no universally accepted single time interval for a FLL to be rendered benign, although LI-RADSv2018 uses stability of at least 2 years as an ancillary feature of benignity [20].

Pathologic assessment

Liver biopsy was performed via a transvenous approach during the cardiac catheterization, most commonly through femoral (or internal jugular) venous access, using a biopsy system advanced into the hepatic vein to obtain 2 to 4 core tissue samples. The procedure was performed alongside hemodynamic measurements, such as the hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) measurements, with patients monitored post-procedure for complications as per our practice standard and published previously [12]. Specimen adequacy was assessed according to standard of care and previously described [12]. Liver biopsies scored according to the Congestive Hepatic Fibrosis Scoring (CHFS) system, which has shown reproducibility among pathologists and correlation with clinical and laboratory markers of congestive hepatopathy [21]. The category definitions are as follows: score 0: no significant fibrosis; score 1: central zone fibrosis only; score 2A: centrizonal fibrosis and portal fibrosis with accentuation of central zone fibrosis; score 2B: centrizonal and portal fibrosis with accentuation of portal zone fibrosis; score 3: bridging fibrosis; and score 4: cirrhosis [21].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the study population and results. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation and range or as median with IQR for normally or non-normally distributed data, respectively. Logistic regression analyses and Fisher's exact tests were conducted to evaluate associations between clinical, imaging, and pathologic variables in the study population. Regression analyses were used to assess continuous and categorical variable relationships. Fisher's exact tests were employed for categorical variables, including dichotomized clinical measures to evaluate associations where appropriate. Significance was defined by p-values less than 0.05 and supported by odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Anatomic imaging findings were compared across groups, with select thresholds applied based on clinical relevance (e.g., Fontan pressure dichotomized at ≥12 mmHg vs. <12 mmHg). Analysis was conducted using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA) and R (Version 4.5.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) software.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 41 patients with FALD (M/F 26/15, mean age 29.9 ± 6.2y, range, 20-47y) comprised our study population (Fig. 1). Characteristics of the study population are summarized in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1. One patient with HCC presented with a reported span of heavy alcohol use. Overall, alcohol use (regular/frequent n = 6 (FLL, n = 5); rare/occasional/social n = 11 (FLL, n = 1)) or BMI extremes (<18 kg/m2, n = 2, >30 kg/m2, n = 4) were prevalent in a minority of patients and were inconsistent in clinical characteristics and pathological/imaging results. Other etiologies of liver disease were absent in the study population. At baseline, most patients were on antiplatelet or anticoagulation therapies (aspirin, n = 22 (54%), direct oral anticoagulant, n = 10 (24%), warfarin, n = 4 (10%)), with other medications individualized based on clinical assessment (loop diuretics, n = 14 (34%), beta-blockers, n = 14 (34%), mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, n = 11 (27%), angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors, n = 8 (20%), phosphodiesterase 5 inhibitors, n = 4 (10%), digoxin, n = 2 (5%) amiodarone, n = 1 (2%). The liver biopsy results demonstrated the following pathologic stages: stage 1 (n = 4, 9%), stage 2A (n = 18, 42%), stage 2B (n = 1, 2%), stage 3 (n = 14, 34%), and stage 4 (n = 4, 10%).

Imaging evaluation

Seventeen patients underwent contrast enhanced CT, 20 patients with gadoxetate-enhanced MRI, and 4 with gadobutrol (nonspecific extracellular agent)-enhanced MRI.

Liver morphologic assessment

The results of the consensus evaluation of the liver morphology, sequela of portal hypertension, and severity of liver disease are presented in Table 2. Most of the patient population demonstrated morphologic findings associated with liver disease, especially volume redistribution within the liver (i.e., caudate lobe and left lobe hypertrophy), liver surface nodularity, and frank liver cirrhosis (Fig. 2). All patients demonstrated patency of the portal and hepatic venous systems. The mean suprahepatic IVC diameter was 2.3 ± 0.4 cm (range, 1.7-3.5 cm). Varices, ascites, and splenomegaly were observed in less than half of the study population for each feature. One patient with heterotaxy syndrome had two spleens, whose measurements were within the normal distribution and did not significantly impact findings. The mean portal hypertension score was 1.5 ± 1.6 (range, 0-6).

FLL detection and diagnosis

A total of 62 FLL ≥0.8 cm in size were identified in 15/41 (36.6%) patients. Eleven patients with lesions underwent MRI (8 gadotextate-enhanced MRI and 3 gadavist-enhanced MRI) and 4 had contrast enhanced CT. Mean lesion size was 1.5 ± 0.7 cm (range 0.8-4.5 cm). Eight patients had multiple lesions (range, 2-29 lesions). The lesion diagnoses were as follows: benign appearing enhancing liver lesions (n = 52 lesions in 10 patients), indeterminate (n = 5 lesions in four patients), HCC (n = 4 lesions in three patients), and sclerosing hemangioma (n = 1). The diagnosis of HCC was established by imaging criteria and MDTB in three patients and at pathology in one patient (Fig. 3). The diagnosis of sclerosing hemangioma was established by biopsy. The diagnosis of benign-appearing enhancing liver lesion was established by biopsy in one patient (biopsy revealed FNH), and by imaging criteria and/or stability over time in the remaining nine patients (median duration of stability was 783 days and the IQR was 465.5-948.5 days). The lesions classified as indeterminate were based on the nodule not meeting imaging criteria for a specific diagnosis, with stability of two lesions in two patients with the length of 556.5 ± 79.5 days, range, 477-636 days. One patient with HCC also had separate two indeterminate liver lesions, which were included in the subsequent radioembolization treatment zone. One patient with an indeterminate lesion underwent liver transplant, with no lesion identified at explant pathology.

Imaging findings of liver lesions in 15 patients are summarized in Table 3, with characteristics stratified based on lesion diagnosis summarized in Table 4. Most lesions were small and showed arterial phase hyperenhancement (APHE). Among those who had gadoxetate-enhanced MRI, nearly all lesions were hyperintense on hepatobiliary phase imaging. None demonstrated restricted diffusion. Of the four HCCs in three patients, three (75%) had APHE, and all showed portal venous washout and a capsule. Association between clinical, pathologic, and imaging data

No association was found for laboratory measures of fibrosis (APRI and FIB-4 scores) and morphologic imaging findings of cirrhosis (all p-values >0.16). A strong linear association was observed between FIB-4 and APRI scores (R2 = 0.79, adjusted R2 = 0.79, p < 0.0001). Fontan pressure measurement ≥12 mmHg was associated with right hepatic lobe atrophy (p = 0.004). Furthermore, there was no association between pathologic CHFS stage and morphologic imaging findings of cirrhosis (all p-values >0.12). The presence of liver lesions, including HCC, was also not associated with morphologic imaging findings of cirrhosis (all p-values >0.21). There was no association between pathological features or liver imaging findings based on longer time from Fontan to biopsy.

Discussion

Improved long-term survival after Fontan procedure has underscored the importance of early detection of FALD and its complications. We report a comprehensive single center experience in patients with FALD demonstrating that a significant proportion of patients exhibit CT or MRI findings of cirrhosis, despite discordant pathological findings, underscoring the limitations of the application of imaging criteria that were developed for other etiologies of liver disease, as well as limitations of pathologic assessment due to tissue sampling error. We also show that small arterially enhancing FLLs are highly prevalent in this population, including HCC.

Liver fibrosis is an expected and progressive result of Fontan physiology, with distinct histopathologic characteristics compared to other chronic liver diseases [22]. Chronic venous congestion from elevated central venous pressures leads to sinusoidal dilatation and impaired hepatic perfusion [23]. The resultant increased hepatic afterload correlates with increased liver stiffness, altered vascular flow patterns, and fibrotic histological changes [24]. Long-standing ventricular dysfunction promotes collagen deposition, fibrotic banding, and architectural distortion, particularly in perivenular and portal regions [23]. The lack of hepatocellular injury, inflammation and necrosis differentiates FALD from other causes of liver disease [25,26]. Cirrhotic imaging features were commonly observed in our study, notably surface nodularity, caudate hypertrophy, and volume redistribution, aligning with prior CT/MRI findings: reticular pattern (63%), nodularity (26%), and caudate hypertrophy (11%) [5]. Future larger prospective studies are warranted to determine if patterns of volume redistribution are distinct in FALD.

The disconnect between imaging-based cirrhosis, pathologic fibrosis staging, and laboratory markers in our cohort was noteworthy and consistent with prior studies [6,27]. Although 66% of patients demonstrated cirrhotic features on CT/MRI, only 10% had biopsy-confirmed cirrhosis, and median FIB-4 (0.9) and APRI (0.4) scores were low, which agrees with a prior study showing that the majority of patients (82%, 40/49) with FALD had normal serum liver function tests, despite the presence of hepatic parenchymal changes on imaging [4]. There are two potential explanations for these discordances. First, the degree of liver injury and distribution of fibrosis in the liver parenchyma is heterogeneous, with reports indicating that transjugular liver biopsies may underestimate severity in up to 40% of patients undergoing liver transplant [1,28]. Histopathology can range from mild sinusoidal dilation and/or congestion to extensive hepatocyte atrophy, sinusoidal fibrosis, and bridging fibrosis within a single sample, complicating CHFS assignment. Liver biopsies for fibrosis assessment performed during cardiac catheterization are typically not image-targeted, and therefore may not capture areas of greatest disease burden identified on cross-sectional imaging. Our findings support the need for improved strategies to enhance detection and accurate staging of liver fibrosis while maintaining a systematic and safe approach. Although percutaneous or transhepatic biopsy allows for more targeted sampling of areas of concern, these approaches may carry higher procedural risk such as hematoma formation compared with transvenous techniques [29]. Secondly, published imaging characteristics of liver fibrosis and cirrhosis are not necessarily applicable to the FALD population. For example, liver surface nodularity on ultrasound correlated with length of time from Fontan completion, not cirrhosis, in a study of 55 patients [1]. However, patients with higher CHFS scores in our study generally demonstrated cirrhosis on imaging. Identifying accurate imaging traits of cirrhosis in this population is critical, as the pathologic diagnosis of cirrhosis does result in a change in patient management, including establishing care with a hepatologist, the initiation of HCC surveillance, and more close monitoring of liver function tests [12].

Typical patterns of portal hypertension are also not observed in this population. Varices and ascites were noted in many patients, whereas splenomegaly was not. FALD results in postsinusoidal portal hypertension where sequelae of portal hypertension can be seen despite a normal hepatic venous pressure gradient and can be associated with major adverse events. It is uncertain if the manifestations of portal hypertension are due to progressive liver disease or are a result of systemic venous congestion and circulatory failure [2]. In FALD, many varices are actually shunts that form related to the procedure, which are rarely significant, and the presence of ascites could also be cardiac in nature, rather than related to the liver disease. One interesting observation was that elevated Fontan pressures were associated with right hepatic lobe atrophy. The right hepatic lobe might be more susceptible to altered hemodynamics in the Fontan circulation, and the prolonged congestion may progress to segmentally greater degrees of fibrosis and volume loss. As such, the pathophysiologic changes in liver volume redistribution related to portosystemic pressures should be studied in a larger series.

An important finding in our study is the high prevalence of FLLs (37%), which agrees with prior studies (up to 47.7%) on CT or MRI [4,6,8,19]. The majority of FLLs in our study demonstrated benign imaging characteristics, such as arterially enhancing lesions, which post-mortem and biopsy studies have shown to represent benign regenerative nodules and FNH-like lesions [8,18,27,30,31]. The term FNH-like lesion is used in FALD, as FNHs arise in normal liver parenchyma [17]. Of note, a few patients had many liver lesions, including one patient with 29 arterially enhancing FLLs, which contributed disproportionately to the overall lesion count and may partially account for the high prevalence observed in our cohort.

Hypervascular FLLs require careful scrutiny, especially those with portal venous washout, as HCC does occur in the Fontan population, although reported prevalence varies widely (1.3–15%) [6,32,33]. Imaging diagnosis of HCC is challenging, as Liver Imaging Reporting and Data System (LI-RADS) and American Association of the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) endorsed criteria (arterial phase hyperenhancement, washout, and capsule) [34], may be absent or misleading in the FALD setting. The elevated central venous pressures in the Fontan liver can cause growth of all hypervascular FLLs and result in the absence of portal venous phase washout [7,8]. Variable hepatic venous outflow may also contribute to atypical imaging findings in FLLs.

Not all FLLs meeting HCC imaging criteria are malignant. In a multicenter study of 130 patients, eight (6.1%) patients had LI-RADS 4 or 5 lesions, but only two (1.3%) patients had biopsy-confirmed HCC (1.3%) [6]. Similar reports have been published elsewhere [30], including in our study the case of a biopsy-proven sclerosing hemangioma mimicking HCC at imaging. Accordingly, LI-RADS or AASLD criteria are not endorsed for FLL characterization in FALD, and the diagnosis of HCC should therefore be confirmed by biopsy or adjudicated by MDTB if biopsy is not feasible [6,34]. For indeterminate FLLs, MDTB discussion, biopsy, or short-term surveillance may be appropriate. Gadoxetate-enhanced MRI with hepatobiliary phase imaging may aid in lesion characterization, as well as longitudinal follow-up [7,8].

Limitations

We recognize several limitations to our study, including the retrospective design and single-center experience, which may limit generalizability. In addition, given the inherent limitations of cross-sectional imaging, including the mix of modalities used in this study and imaging interpretation, FLL classifications may have been influenced by the lack of histologic confirmation in all cases. The MDTB at our center is comprised of experts in each discipline. We did not evaluate inter-observer differences in FLL classification. We also acknowledge that in some patients, multiple lesions were analyzed, and therefore lesion-level observations cannot be assumed to be statistically independent. All liver biopsy and pressure measurements were obtained during cardiac catheterization; the procedural measurement conditions (i.e., sedation, ventilation, end-expiration, fenestration status) are beyond the scope of this manuscript, however no trend was apparent based on anesthesia status, which was predominantly monitored anesthesia care (MAC). We recognize that clinical context with sample size may have limited outcomes observed. Although a six-month interval between imaging and histopathologic assessment was not achieved in all subjects, patients remained clinically stable and medically optimized during this period, making substantial changes in underlying liver imaging and histopathology unlikely; nevertheless, this variability in timing represents an important limitation of our study. The challenge in assessing CHFS scores at pathology may be overcome by performing quantitative measurement of collagen proportionate area to assess the percent liver fibrosis. Nonetheless, our findings highlight the necessity for a multidisciplinary approach and protocol standardization to integrate cross-sectional imaging, pathology findings, and clinical data for optimized clinical decision making and patient management and should be hypothesis generating for future studies. In fact, recently published guidelines for the management of adults with congenital heart disease (ACHD) and Fontan circulation now endorse screening for liver disease including at least annual imaging and laboratory evaluation, including alpha-fetoprotein, and at least 1 consultation with a hepatologist [35].

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study reinforces the high prevalence of liver morphological changes in patients with FALD, along with a disconnect between liver morphology, pathologic liver fibrosis, and biochemical evidence of liver dysfunction, along with a very high prevalence of arterially enhancing FLLs including HCC. The risk of HCC in this population and the overlap in imaging appearance with benign lesions underscore the importance of continued efforts to optimize surveillance protocols to improve early detection and outcomes in patients with FALD. In this context, the newly endorsed 2025 ACHD recommendations for patients with Fontan circulation emphasize the role of annual liver imaging and laboratory evaluation to screen for both FALD progression and HCC. Further prospective studies are needed to establish the most reliable surveillance protocols and improve early detection and long-term outcomes in this growing patient population.

Clinical perspective

Cross-sectional imaging and histopathologic findings of liver cirrhosis are discordant, likely due to early morphologic changes in cardiac disease without presence of cirrhosis, as well as tissue undersampling at biopsy. A high prevalence of enhancing FLLs, including HCC, occurs in patients with FALD even in the absence of histopathologic cirrhosis.
