Health‐Related Quality of Life in Adolescents and Young Adults With an Uncertain or Poor Cancer Prognosis: Quantitative Baseline Results of the CORD‐AYA Study
Authors: Milou J P Reuvers, Helle‐Brit Fiebrich‐Westra, Johan A F Koekkoek, Jacqueline M Tromp, Hans M Westgeest, Mathilde C M Kouwenhoven, Karin J Beelen, Mathijs P Hendriks, Arjan J Verschoor, Maarten M J Wijnenga, Marleen Kok, Adrianus J de Langen, Sofie Wilgenhof, Monique E M M Bos, Suzanne E J Kaal, Janine Nuver, Rhodé Bijlsma, Roy Lalisang, Annet W Coumou, Mariette J Agterof, Irene E G van Hellemond, Baukje Flameling, Winette T A van der Graaf, Olga Husson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThree subgroups of adolescent and young adult patients (AYAs; 18–39 at diagnosis) with an uncertain or poor cancer prognosis (UPCP—those diagnosed with advanced disease, who will likely die prematurely) were previously identified: those receiving traditional treatments (chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy), newer treatments (immunotherapy or targeted therapy), and patients with glioma. This study examines differences in health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) among these subgroups, and comparisons between AYAs with a UPCP, recently diagnosed AYAs, and a healthy control group.MethodsData were collected from two AYA cohorts: CORD‐AYA (n = 155), including AYAs with a UPCP any moment post‐diagnosis, and COMPRAYA (n = 549), including AYAs within 6 months after diagnosis. A healthy control group, matched by age and sex, was also included (n = 430). All completed the EORTC QLQ‐C30.ResultsNo significant HRQoL differences emerged between the traditional versus new treatment group. However, patients with glioma reported significantly lower cognitive functioning compared to patients on newer therapies. AYAs with a UPCP scored lower across all HRQoL scales than the control group. Despite being further along in their disease trajectory, UPCP patients reported HRQoL levels comparable to newly diagnosed patients. AYAs with a UPCP experienced higher financial burden but reported better global quality of life than COMPRAYA patients.ConclusionsNo significant differences in HRQoL were found between treatment groups, but patients with glioma should be considered a distinct subgroup as they have shown reduced cognitive functioning, necessitating specialized care. Individualized care is crucial, as a lack of personalized support may lead to long‐term functional impairment, especially among UPCP patients.Keywords: adolescents and young adults, cancer, glioma, health‐related quality of life, uncertain or poor prognosis

Introduction

Adolescents and young adults (AYAs—between 18 and 39 years old at initial diagnosis) are a unique subgroup in oncology due to their distinct epidemiology, tumor biology, medical, psychological, social and developmental challenges [1, 2, 3, 4]. Research has shown that cancer has a significant negative impact on their health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) [5], which is described as a multidimensional concept referring to a patient's subjective perception on the impact of disease and treatment on their physical, psychological and social life [6]. Multiple factors are related to a lower HRQoL among AYA patients, for example a high symptom burden [7], unmet care needs [8], maladaptive coping strategies and difficulty keeping up with social demands [9]. Furthermore, disease stage may play a role, considering an increased psychological and symptomatic impact during advanced cancer [5, 10].

In high income countries, the relative 5‐year survival rate among AYA patients is approximately 85%. However, this also means that a subset of AYAs face an uncertain or poor cancer prognosis (UPCP)—those diagnosed with metastatic disease or who have no options for curative treatment (e.g., low‐grade glioma)—who will presumably die from their disease, though not in the short term [11, 12]. Burgers et al. developed the UPCP definition in collaboration with five AYAs with a UPCP and healthcare providers (HCPs). In our earlier INVAYA study, they also defined three subgroups of AYAs with a UPCP based on treatment and diagnosis [11]. The first group consisted of patients receiving traditional treatment, including surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and other local treatments. These patients receive multiple lines of treatment and may experience various side effects. The second group included patients who, sometimes alongside traditional treatment, receive relatively newer treatments such as immunotherapy, cell therapy, and targeted therapy. However, there is, currently, limited information on how these emerging treatments affect the individual prognosis, which late effects may develop, and the impact of adverse events at a young age [11]. The third group consists of patients with either low‐grade or high‐grade glioma, with a life expectancy of at least 12 months (hereafter glioma group). They are considered a separate subgroup as, although their disease is incurable, they may still live for five to 15 years after diagnosis. These patients may maintain a stable quality of life for an extended period of time before experiencing symptomatic disease progression or treatment‐related side effects. However, many patients with glioma face neurocognitive impairment from the tumor or antitumor treatment, such as resection, focal irradiation or chemotherapy. This can significantly affect their functioning in daily life [11, 13, 14]. Importantly, these subgroups were created in 2020 by Burgers et al. [11], and it is not well known whether the first two subgroups are clearly distinct within the current therapeutic landscape, as these treatments have become part of the standard of care for various tumor types. This is partly due to the more frequent use of combination therapies, increased personalization of treatment, and the growing use of immunotherapy and targeted therapy. Our previous research among these different therapy subgroups showed no significant differences among subscales of problems and needs (activities of daily living (ADL), physical symptoms, role activities, financial and administrative issues, social issues, psychological issues, spiritual issues, autonomy, informational needs) between the new and traditional treatment groups, which may call for altering the definition of these subgroups. However, patients with glioma did report differences in physical and psychological issues compared to the first two subgroups [15].

Our prior qualitative and quantitative research has demonstrated that cancer affects multiple life domains among AYA patients with a UPCP, including physical, psychological, social, role and spirituality functioning [15, 16]. Many patients report feelings of loneliness, diminished self‐worth, anticipatory grief, and persistent reminders of their UPCP, all while grappling with an uncertain and altered future perspective. Furthermore, they face reduced autonomy, increased financial toxicity, and negative emotions like fear [16]. AYAs with a UPCP differ from other AYA patients due to their uncertain life expectancy, which complicates planning for the future, decision‐making around key milestones in life, and can lead to anticipatory grief. These patients may live for several years with an unpredictable disease trajectory, and our current healthcare system is often inadequately equipped to address this [17]. Importantly, many of the challenges faced by AYA patients with a UPCP impact their HRQoL. Despite this, there is a lack of quantitative HRQoL data specific to this group of patients, limiting a comprehensive understanding of the disease's unique impact on their daily lives. Addressing this gap is crucial for developing patient‐centered care that is tailored to their specific needs and challenges [18]. It can also enhance shared decision‐making between patients and healthcare providers (HCP). Additionally, this data will help HCP to allocate resources, provide targeted support, and empower patients to understand their symptoms, ultimately fostering open communication with their healthcare team [19].

This study aimed to examine the HRQoL scores among AYA patients with a UPCP, as measured with the EORTC Core Quality of Life questionnaire (EORTC QLQ‐C30). Additionally, it compared HRQoL‐scores between the three subgroups of AYA patients with a UPCP: those receiving traditional treatments, new treatments, and patients with glioma, with the aim to assess potential differences and determine whether the current definition of AYA patients needs revision in light of evolving treatments. Finally, this study compared HRQoL scores of AYA patients with a UPCP to those of an age‐ and sex matched control group, to put their scores into perspective, and to those of AYA patients within 6 months after initial diagnosis (COMPRAYA patients). Recently diagnosed patients are known to experience a temporary decline in HRQoL, mostly improving again over time. However, it remains unclear whether AYA patients with a UPCP—the majority further alone in their disease trajectory—follow a similar recovery [5].

Methods

This analysis is part of the CORD‐AYA study (Balancing on a tightrope—living with an uncertain or poor cancer prognosis as an adolescent or young adult). This observational, longitudinal questionnaire study focuses on AYA patients with a UPCP to examine patterns and prevalence of suffering, challenges, and meaningful living, and identifies factors associated with these outcomes. Participants complete questionnaires every 3 months over a 2‐year period, examining challenges, anxiety, depression, hope, life urgency, coping, and social support. This article examines the baseline results and uses two reference groups: COMPRAYA patients and an age‐ and sex matched control group (Figure 1). This study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross‐sectional studies.

Overview of the different groups within this study.

CORD‐AYA Patients

AYA patients with a UPCP are identified as “those with advanced cancer for which there is no reasonable hope of cure, meaning they are likely to die from cancer, but not at the short term.” This definition acknowledges that emerging therapies do not allow us to state with certainty that curation is unattainable in the long term. Patients were eligible if they were facing advanced cancer at study participation (e.g., stage IV disease at diagnosis, metastasized disease later after diagnosis or glioma) between the ages of 18 and 39, had an (estimated) life expectancy of at least 12 months, and were not older than 45 at the time of participation.

Furthermore, AYAs with a UPCP are categorized into three groups for subgroup analysis: traditional, new and glioma. Patients are assigned to the traditional treatment group if they indicated they were receiving chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or hormone therapy. They are included in the new group if they received immunotherapy, cell therapy or targeted therapy (sometimes in addition to traditional therapies) at any moment before participating in this study. Patients with a brain tumor are included in the glioma group.

Patients could enroll at any time after diagnosis. Exclusion criteria included the insufficient proficiency in Dutch or English, or any cognitive or practical limitations interfering with the completion of the questionnaire.

Procedure CORD‐AYA

Multiple Dutch hospitals participated in this study, including Antoni van Leeuwenhoek—Netherlands Cancer Institute, Amsterdam University Medical Center, Rijnstate Hospital, Erasmus University Medical Center, Amphia Hospital, Northwest Clinics, Reinier de Graaf Hospital, Leiden University Medical Center, Isala Hospital and Haaglanden Medical Center. Patients were invited to participate by the study coordinator (MR), either via selection from the electronic patient record (EPR) or referral by their treating physician. Furthermore, recruitment also occurred via word‐of‐mouth, including social media and patient organizations. The final decision to invite patients was based on their treating physician's assessment of eligibility. Participants were recruited between June 2023 and September 2024. Eligible patients received an e‐mail with study information and an informed consent form. If no response was received within 1–2 weeks, a reminder e‐mail was sent. When a patient still did not respond, the study coordinator followed‐up with a phone call or a final e‐mail. Once informed consent was obtained, patients could complete the questionnaire either on paper or online via Castor EDC. The Institutional Reviewing Board of the Antoni van Leeuwenhoek‐ Netherlands Cancer Institute (IRBd22‐344) approved the study.

COMPRAYA Patients

COMPRAYA is an ongoing prospective cohort study, aiming to examine the prevalence of impaired health outcomes over time [20]. Thirteen Dutch hospitals are participating in this study. Participants are diagnosed with cancer between 18 and 39 years old and are invited to participate within 6 months after initial diagnosis. Patients with both early and advanced disease can be included, meaning that there might be an overlap in CORD‐AYA and COMPRAYA patients. Participants are asked to, among other questionnaires, complete the EORTC QLQ‐C30 yearly over the course of 10 years. For this analysis, we have used data from the baseline assessment.

Age‐ and Sex‐Matched Norm Group

Normative population data was obtained from the PROFILES (Patient Reported Outcomes Following Initial treatment and Long‐term Evaluation of Survivorship) database [21]. This is an online database with information from cancer patients, survivors, as well as a control group from the general population, who complete a basic questionnaire each year to determine the reference standard. For this study, we used information from healthy individuals, who were matched on age and sex.

Measurements

Sociodemographic and Clinical Characteristics (CORD‐AYA)

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were self‐reported by patients. They provided information regarding their year of birth, partner status, living situation, educational level, work status, age at diagnosis, cancer type, cancer treatment, and the aim of their treatment. Patients also indicated any additional support they had received (e.g., psychological support, informal or alternative care).

Quality of Life (CORD‐AYA, COMPRAYA, Control Group)

Patients completed the EORTC QLQ‐C30. This is a validated cancer‐specific measurement, including five functioning scales (physical, role, emotional, cognitive, and social), and nine items assessing various symptoms (fatigue, nausea and vomiting, pain, dyspnea, insomnia, appetite loss, constipation, diarrhea, and financial difficulties). These items are all scored on a 4‐point Likert scale, ranging from “not at all” to “very much”. Furthermore, there is a global quality of life scale, consisting of two items that are scored on a 7‐point Likert scale (“very poor” to “very much”). Higher scores on the functional and quality of life scales indicate better functioning. Higher scores on the symptom items indicates higher symptom burden. Raw scores were linear transformed to standardize them, resulting in a score from 0 to 100 [22]. The psychometric values of the EORTC QLQ‐C30 were proven sufficient [23].

Data Analysis

Categorical data were described as frequencies and percentages, continuous data were described as mean and standard deviation (SD). Kruskal‐Wallis tests were used to compare mean HRQoL scores between the three subgroups of AYA patients with a UPCP, and between different patient groups. To account for multiple testing, adjusted p‐values were utilized. Data was only used from patients completing all items. Two‐sided p‐values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics (IBM Corporation, version 30.0, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

In total, 317 patients were invited to participate in the CORD‐AYA study, with 155 patients agreeing to participate and completing the questionnaire (Table 1). This resulted in a response rate of 48.9%. AYAs with a UPCP had a mean age of 31.2 (± 5.5) years at diagnosis. Among AYA patients with UPCP, there were 26 patients in the traditional group (mean age at diagnosis 32.8 (± 4.9)), 75 patients in the new group (mean age at diagnosis 30.9 (± 5.3)) and 54 glioma patients (mean age at diagnosis 29.8 (± 6.4)). 54.7% identified themselves as female. Most common tumor types were gliomas (34.8%) and breast cancer (17.4%), and more than half of the patients were 1 to 5 years post‐diagnosis.

COMPRAYA patients (n = 549) had a mean age of 31.4 (± 5.4) at diagnosis. 60.1% identified as female, and the majority was on sick‐leave at the time of completing the questionnaire. Breast cancer (27.1%) and testicular cancer (17.7%) were the most common tumor types among COMPRAYA participants. AYA patients with a UPCP score significantly lower on all functioning scales compared to control group (Table 3). Furthermore, they report a higher burden for all symptom scales. AYA patients with a UPCP do not differ significantly from the COMPRAYA patients on the functioning scales (Table 4), even though many of them are multiple years after initial diagnosis. They also have a similar symptom burden compared to COMPRAYA patients. However, AYA patients with a UPCP report higher financial burden and a better global quality of life score, which could be due to their prolonged disease trajectory compared to more recently diagnosed patients.

Discussion

This study found no significant differences in HRQoL between subgroups of AYAs with a UPCP receiving traditional versus new treatments, suggesting that treatment modality no longer is a key differentiator in this group. This supports earlier finding that these groups face similar problems and needs [15], and reinforces the notion that they do not need to be considered separately. However, patients with gliomas reported significantly lower cognitive functioning than those in the new treatment group, reflecting a disease‐specific impact consistent with our previous research [13, 15]. This suggests that diagnosis and disease characteristics may play a more defining role in HRQoL than treatment type. Compared to a control group, AYAs with a UPCP reported lower scores on all functioning and symptom scales. They show comparable HRQoL scores to COMPRAYA patients, but do have a higher financial burden. Importantly, most UPCP patients were multiple years after diagnosis, suggesting that the UPCP may lead to long‐term reduction in HRQoL. In contrast, a study among AYA cancer survivors showed higher HRQoL 5–20 years after diagnosis compared to our CORD‐AYA patients. This indicates that among non‐UPCP patients, HRQoL typically improves over time after diagnosis [5, 24]. Interestingly, AYAs with a UPCP reported higher global quality of life than COMPRAYA patients, possibly due to the high number of patients with glioma included in the CORD‐AYA sample, or an adaptive process among patients with a UPCP, whereby they come to value their remaining capabilities despite their multidimensional challenges.

Among AYA patients with a UPCP, scores on financial difficulties, emotional functioning, fatigue, nausea, diarrhea and cognitive functioning were above the threshold for clinical importance, indicating a need for support across these domains [25]. However, these factors might be interrelated, making it difficult to implement a single, effective HRQoL intervention. For example, reduced cognitive functioning can lead to lower scores on social and role functioning, as difficulties in communication, memory and concentration, word‐finding problems and changes in personality are common [13, 26, 27]. Furthermore, financial challenges may limit social activities, and contribute to psychological symptoms like distress [28]. Given the interconnection between these factors, the fact that multiple aspects of HRQoL could be affected, and the often‐unclear causal relationships, providing adequate support is not straightforward and requires a longitudinal, multidimensional approach. Due to the heterogeneity of AYAs with a UPCP, it is difficult to establish clear guidelines for supportive care. This heterogeneity is also reflected in the large standard deviations found across several HRQoL domains, suggesting that individual impact vary widely. Therefore, interpreting scores may not fully capture the personal impact of the disease, underlining the importance of individualized assessments when determining supportive care needs. Additionally, our subgroup analysis indicates that the impact might vary depending on the tumor type, as patients with glioma face different challenges. Therefore, it is crucial to raise awareness and support HCP in identifying AYAs with a UPCP as important first steps [29, 30].

Implications

In clinical practice, supportive care needs will vary, given the multifaceted nature of HRQoL. Therefore, they should be assessed individually and regularly due to the variable disease course of AYAs with a UPCP [30]. Effective HRQoL interventions for patients with advanced disease require a focus on multiple care needs, a combination with peer support, and be delivered in multiple sessions to ensure long‐term impact [31]. Targeting coping is critical, as denial, substance use, and detachment are linked lower HRQoL outcomes in patients with advanced disease [32]. Interventions aimed at managing uncertainty—particularly relevant for patients with a UPCP—have shown to enhance HRQoL [32]. This aligns with findings from other chronic and life‐limiting illnesses, such as heart failure [33] and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [34], where illness‐related uncertainty contributed to reduced HRQoL. Due to the prolonged disease trajectory and need for supportive care, involving informal caregivers is necessary. However, reduced HRQoL among patients is linked to higher caregiver burden. Therefore, it is essential to provide additional support their informal caregivers, using psycho‐education, peer support or psychological help [35].

Palliative and supportive care are most effective when unmet needs are identified early in the disease trajectory, to ensure support can be directed when necessary [36, 37]. Often, the advance care planning model is used, but among AYAs with a UPCP timely palliative care might be more fitting. This model ensures that support is directed toward patients who will benefit most, avoiding unnecessary strain on the healthcare system. This approach supports effective resource allocation, ensuring that those who are unlikely to benefit or are not open to supportive care are not burdened [38]. Criteria have to be adjusted to the specific situation of AYA patients and a UPCP, with emphasis on autonomy, regaining normality, and making future plans. Cognitive impairment, particularly given the number of patients with glioma in this group, must be taken into account [39]. Research suggests that machine learning could help to identify at‐risk patients from their medical records by analyzing specific criteria, enabling physicians to target those most likely to experience problems [40].

Limitations

This study included a large and heterogeneous sample of AYAs with a UPCP, serving as an initial step in examining the impact of the disease and prognosis on HRQoL among this patient population. Additionally, the comparison to other subgroups provided valuable context, allowing for a better understanding of the specific domains most affected in these patients. However, the study is subject to participation bias, as patients with severe physical or psychological issues may have decided not to participate or their physicians advised against their inclusion for similar reasons. Furthermore, the sample depends on the patients included by the physician, and due to this small group of patients, there was no possibility for targeted sampling. In addition, patients who were not sufficiently proficient in speaking Dutch or English were unable to participate. Furthermore, not all aspects that contribute significantly to an AYA's HRQoL were included, for example sexual functioning or fertility, due to the limited content of the questionnaire. This may have resulted in an underestimation of the impact of cancer in the life of an AYA patient. This report includes a cross‐sectional measure of quality of life, and for patients with an unpredictable disease trajectory, this could indicate that they feel better or worse at the time of completing the questionnaire and answer accordingly. Therefore, we are currently conducting a longitudinal questionnaire study to gain more insight into the challenges, experiences, and meaningful living over time. Furthermore, it could provide information on patients at‐risk for problems, to ensure that these patients receive timely and tailored care.

Conclusion

The impact of a UPCP on HRQoL in AYA patients is substantial and comparable to that of recently diagnosed AYA patients, underscoring the serious and lasting burden of the disease. Our findings show no significant HRQoL differences between AYAs with a UPCP undergoing traditional or new treatments, suggesting that the current classification no longer aligns with the current therapeutic landscape. As such, future research should move beyond this distinction. However, patients with glioma require specific healthcare, focusing on cognitive functioning. The HRQoL impairment in AYAs with a UPCP—despite many being further along in their disease trajectory—highlights the need for early, ongoing, and individualized assessment of supportive care needs. Given the multifaceted nature of HRQoL and the changing disease trajectory of patients with a UPCP, timely and tailored interventions are essential to effectively address one's unique challenges and changing preferences over time.
