Combined intracavitary, systemic antifungal and surgical treatment of Chronic Pulmonary Aspergillosis: experience from an immunocompromised patient
Authors: Anne Haglund, Jelena Stankovic, Thomas Decker Christensen, Lise Kristensen, Karen Rokkedal Lausch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We present a complex case of Chronic Pulmonary Aspergillosis (CPA) caused by Aspergillus fumigatus in a pre-existing lung cavity in an immunocompromised patient with granulomatosis with polyangiitis. Initial treatment with voriconazole was limited due to adverse effects and emerging azole resistance.Aspergillus Subsequent therapy with intravenous and intracavitary amphotericin B was also poorly tolerated and clinically insufficient. The patient underwent left-sided thoracotomy with decortication. As a novel approach, the patient recieved Post-surgical instillation of liposomal amphotericin B directly into the resected cavity, combined with systemic antifungal therapy. Subsequently the patient recovered with long-term remission. This case highlights the potential role of post-operative intracavitary antifungal instillation as an adjunctive strategy in refractory CPA, particularly when conventional medical therapy fails.2012 Elsevier Ltd. Introduction

Chronic Pulmonary Aspergillosis (CPA) is a progressive fungal infection, most commonly caused by Aspergillus fumigatus, that arises within pre-existing pulmonary cavities. It is particularly relevant in immunocompromised individuals, including those with tuberculosis, pulmonary malignancies, structural lung disease, or—as in this case—granulomatosis with polyangiitis [1,2]. Diagnosis is frequently delayed due to nonspecific symptoms such as chronic cough, low-grade fever, and hemoptysis; many patients remain asymptomatic until the disease advances [3].

Immunosuppressed patients are at increased risk of invasive aspergillosis, which complicates the clinical course and management of CPA([4]). Diagnosis requires integration of clinical, radiographic, and microbiological data [3,5]. CPA is defined by persistent symptoms and radiographic abnormalities for more than three months, along with serologic or microbiologic evidence of Aspergillus infection [[5], [6], [7]].

Management typically involves prolonged systemic antifungal therapy, although complete eradication is rarely achieved [5]. Surgical resection may offer definitive treatment but is often contraindicated in immunocompromised patients due to poor functional status and increased perioperative risk [2].

This case illustrates a diagnostically and therapeutically challenging presentation of CPA in a patient with granulomatosis with polyangiitis and a pre-existing pulmonary cavity colonized by A. fumigatus.

Case

A 74-year-old Caucasian male, receiving immunosuppressive therapy with 7.5 mg/day prednisolone and rituximab for granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA), presented with a relapse of productive cough and fever. This occurred two years after treatment for bacterial lung abscesses, which had left cavitary changes in the lungs. Broad-spectrum antibiotics were administered despite negative cultures, yet no clinical improvement occurred. High-resolution computed tomography (HR-CT) revealed a substantial cavitary lesion in the lower lobe of the left lung, characterized by marked wall thickening, intraluminal material, and contrast enhancement. Targeted antifungal treatment with voriconazole was initiated in accordance with guidelines [5,9,10]. The local thoracic surgeons were consulted on excision options but refrained from operation due to the high-risk procedure. The patient improved with timely voriconazole therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) in level. Dosage was initially 350 mg, two times daily. Dosage was adjusted accordingly to maintain TDM levels. After 4 weeks, voriconazole treatment was paused due to elevated liver enzymes. Liver examinations were normal, and liver enzymes normalized, after which voriconazole treatment was reinitiated and continued for 7.5 months. To maintain a TDM level between 2 and 5, the patient at this time received voriconazole dosage at 150mg x 2. At the end of this period, the patient developed symptoms of polyneuropathy in both legs and subsequently intermittently paused voriconazole. During a treatment pause of 10 weeks, the patients cough increased but the polyneuropathy subsided.

A broncho-alveolar lavage (BAL) was performed, with no growth (day 376 (1 year and 11 days after day 0)). Isavuconazole treatment was initiated, loading dose according to guideline, following 200 mg once daily. TDM measurements insured levels between 2 and 5 mg/l, yet the patient deteriorated [11]. Readmission allowed for escalation to intravenous liposomal amphotericin B treatment (4 mg/kg) and further diagnostic evaluation. The immunosuppressive treatment with rituximab was temporarily withheld and the prednisolone dosage reduced.

On day 404, CT guided saline irrigation of the pulmonary cavity was repeated. PCR analysis of the cavitary fluid using the Aspergillus AsperGenius® assay, (PathoNostics, The Netherlands) was positive for Aspergillus fumigatus. PCR for the most common environmental resistance profiles (TR34/L98H and TR46/Y121F/T289A) was negative. Culture yielded Aspergillus fumigatus sensu stricto (identified according to morphologic criteria and Bruker MALDI-TOF, MIS online database, version 2). Despite liposomal amphotericin B treatment the patient deteriorated, hence caspofungin (50 mg x 1 daily, after a loading dose of 70 mg) was initiated and intracavitary irrigation with liposomal amphotericin B was performed(day 425). The intracavitary irrigation was performed as a CT guided instillation of 50 mg of liposomal amphotericin B mixed into 20mL of 5 % glucose solution through a pleural drain directly into the lung cavity [5]. During the procedure and 2 hours after, the patient was placed horizontally. When raised to vertical levels, the patient started coughing, with yellow fluid running from the nostrils, resembling AmBisome® solution. This indicated communication between the pleural cavity and the bronchial tree. No further irrigation was performed, as the procedure was intolerable for the patient. Intracavitary samples taken during the procedure cultured Aspergillus fumigatus sensitive to azoles and amphotericin B.

As the clinical status continued to deteriorate and no further medical treatment were viable, surgical intervention was re-evaluated. A left-sided thoracotomy with a total decortication was performed on day 439 (1 year and 2 months after initial presentation). A large pleural thickening containing the cavitary changes was removed, while the underlying lung tissue was preserved ((day 439(1 year and 2 months after day 0)) (Picture 2).Picture 2Resected pleural tissue demonstrating marked thickening and cavitary transformation.Picture 2

Irrigation with liposomal amphotericin B through a post surgically installed drain was performed repeatedly two days post-operative along with systemic liposomal amphotericin B administration (300 mg once daily). There were no post-operative complications, including no evidence of bronchopleural fistula. The removed pleural tissue grew Aspergillus fumigatus sensitive to azoles and amphotericin B. Microscopy demonstrated hyphae in the luminal necrosis, though there was no clear distinction as to whether angioinvasion was present.

The patient recovered quickly from surgery, reporting reduced coughing, increased energy, and an improved general condition. Intravenous liposomal amphotericin B was continued for 4 weeks post-operatively, followed by oral posaconazole (300 mg x 2 the first 24 hours, followed by 300 mg x 1 daily). 14 weeks post-surgically the patient developed side-effects to posaconazole, consisting of a rash and itching of the skin and scalp. Treatment was changed to isavuconazole (200 mg x 3 the first 24 hours, followed by 200 mg x 1 daily), for another 7 weeks, and immunosuppressive therapy for his GPA was reinitiated. TDM for isavuconazole was 3 mg/L, target range is 2–5. Isavuconazole was ultimately terminated due to similar side effects and consequently the patient terminated all antifungal treatment (day 632, 175 days after surgery). 10 weeks post-surgically, HR-CT showed regression of the left-sided cavitary changes and complete regression of intraluminal material (Picture 3). The patient remained well and slowly recovered to pre-Aspergillus status, working fulltime and with no signs of relapse 2 year after completed treatment.Picture 3HR-CT ten weeks postoperatively with marked regression of the left-sided cavitary lesions and complete resolution of intraluminal material.Picture 3

Discussion

A complicated course of Aspergillus fumigatus infection in an immunosuppressed patient with a pre-existing lung cavity is presented here. The diagnosis were challenging, as serum Aspergillus galactomannan, Aspergillus IgG and initial BAL examinations were negative. Diagnosis was confirmed via direct puncture of the lung cavity. This highlights the importance of obtaining appropriate samples, particularly for slow-growing moulds.

Another challenge in immunosuppressed cases is to balance immunosuppressive treatment regarding efficacy and safety. In this case, rituximab was temporarily paused and prednisolone reduced. Patients with vasculitis have a high risk of infections; a recent review found a pooled infection prevalence in this group of 54.6 % and a specific risk of Aspergillus infection of 2.2 % [1].

Long-term azole-treatment is often challenging, and in this case, intolerable side effects necessitated pauses in treatment. An insufficient response to azole treatment raised suspicion of antifungal resistance, which was later confirmed through renewed diagnostic workup. The isolates exhibited varying resistance profiles throughout the treatment course, raising concerns about the underlying mechanisms [2,5]. Unfortunately, Genome sequencing was not performed, though it could have added valuable insights. Without sequencing, it remains unclear whether the observed resistance pattern reflects clonal evolution, mixed infection, or reinfection, which limits the understanding of treatment failure and pathogen dynamics. This uncertainty impedes the ability to tailor antifungal therapy effectively and may obscure the true epidemiology of resistance development.

Future cases should prioritize genomic analyses to guide clinical decisions and improve outcomes in complex fungal infections.

Following detection of resistance, liposomal Amphotericin B was initiated; however, the clinical condition worsened. Treatment was intensified with caspofungin along with liposomal amphotericin B irrigation directly into the cavity [13]. Due to bronchial communication, the patient could not tolerate irrigation, which was subsequently discontinued.

As shown in this case, surgical resection may serve as the final and curative intervention for aspergilloma [5]. The surgery revealed pleural thickening, creating a thick capsule around the cavity, presumably impairing penetration of the antifungal drug and possibly explaining the failure of systemic treatment. Prior reports describe intracavitary amphotericin B as well-tolerated in Aspergillus cavities, although rarely curative [13,14]. We found no reports of postsurgical instillation to enhance the cure rate of surgery. Although the patient initially could not tolerate instillation in the cavity, post-operative liposomal amphotericin B instillation was well accepted. Others report successful treatment with liposomal amphotericin B instillation in combination with thermal ablation [15]. The benefit of local antifungal instillation remains unclear, it may be beneficial in areas of poor drug penetration.

Treating mould infections in immunosuppressed patients requires a tailored, multidisciplinary approach, as illustrated here. Therefore, case studies help illuminate novel treatment strategies. Pre- and post-surgical local instillation of liposomal amphotericin B along with surgical removal was here shown to contribute to a successful cure of the patient.
