Structural elucidation of the herpesvirus replication machinery: Cryo-EM insights into helicase–primase complex assembly and mechanisms of antiviral inhibition
Authors: Qiaomei Zhou, Baohu Li, Dazhou Shi, Shenghua Gao, Peng Zhan, Jinfei Yang
Published: 2026

Herpes simplex virus (HSV) establishes lifelong latency and periodically reactivates, causing recurrent disease1. First-line nucleoside analogues like Acyclovir inhibit viral DNA polymerase to block replication, but their efficacy is limited in immunocompromised patients due to drug resistance, frequent recurrences, and inability to eradicate latent reservoirs2. These drugs also require viral thymidine kinase for activation-another vulnerability. Brincidofovir, a lipid-conjugated cidofovir prodrug, inhibits DNA polymerase independently of thymidine kinase and shows broad anti-DNA virus activity; however, polymerase mutations can still confer resistance3.

Consequently, there is a pressing unmet medical need for novel antiviral agents targeting non-overlapping pathways to enable mechanistic synergy and improved long-term viral control. The helicase–primase (HP) complex is essential for the initiation and processive elongation of viral DNA replication, rendering it a high-priority target for novel antiviral therapeutics. Helicase–primase inhibitors (HPIs), including Pritelivir4 (PTV) and its clinical-stage derivative IM-2505, are currently undergoing phase III and I clinical trials, respectively. Notably, Amenamevir (AMNV) has received regulatory approval in Japan for the treatment of HSV and varicella-zoster virus (VZV) infections6.

Recently, four high-resolution structural and mechanistic studies have collectively resolved the architecture of the HSV-1 HP complex and its complexes with inhibitors, establishing a near-atomic framework that illuminates the allosteric inhibition mechanism of HPIs, maps key resistance-associated mutations, and clarifies determinants of viral species selectivity (Fig. 1).

Structural and mechanistic model of the HSV-1 HP complex and allosteric inhibition. (A) Schematic architectures of the HP heterotrimer (left) and the fully assembled replication fork complex (right), highlighting the direct polymerase-helicase coupling mediated by the UL30 FYNPYL motif and UL5 2B/2C domains. (B) The normal catalytic cycle of the HP complex, where spatiotemporal conformational coupling between UL5 (1A–2A domain closure) and UL52 drives processive DNA translocation and RNA primer synthesis. (C) Allosteric inhibition mechanism of HPIs (e.g., Pritelivir, Amenamevir). Inhibitors act as molecular wedges, sterically locking the UL5 motor domains in an open, catalytically incompetent state. (D) Structural basis for clinical resistance and viral species selectivity. The highly conserved UL5 K356 acts as a resistance hotspot (left), while variations in the peripheral UL52 pocket dictate the drug spectrum via steric constraints (right).

The HSV-1 HP complex is a heterotrimeric assembly comprising UL5 helicase, UL52 primase, and UL8 regulatory cofactor, adopting a bilobed architecture in which UL52 occupies the central core, while UL5 and UL8 are positioned at opposing peripheral lobes (Fig. 1A, left). UL5 belongs to the SF1 superfamily of DNA helicases and contains six structurally and functionally delineated domains: an N-terminal domain (NTD), four conserved helicase core domains (1A, 2A, 2B, and 2C), and a C-terminal domain (CTD). ATP hydrolysis and DNA translocation are mechanistically coupled and jointly driven by the coordinated conformational cycling of the two RecA-like motor domains, 1A and 2A. Dong's group7 determined the post-catalytic structure of the HP complex and uncovered pronounced conformational dynamics: rotational movements of UL5's 1A and 2A–2B modules induce reversible opening and closing of the DNA-binding groove. This finding is consistent with a mechanism where helicase unwinding and primer synthesis are spatiotemporally coordinated through allosteric conformational coupling. UL52 engages UL5 through an extensive interface mediated primarily by its NTD and hinge/intermediate domain. Its archaeo-eukaryotic primase (AEP) domain performs the nucleotidyl transfer reactions that are essential for RNA primer synthesis. In contrast, its C-terminal region contains structurally heterogeneous elements, such as a zinc-finger (ZnF) motif, which display reduced local resolution or increased conformational flexibility in the available cryo-EM reconstructions. As the central catalytic scaffold of the complex, UL52 stabilizes the overall architecture through multivalent interactions with both UL5 and UL8. In addition, the AEP-like domain and ZnF domain of UL52 and the exit for UL5 to release single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) are located on the same side of the complex. This conserved topological arrangement provides a structural rationale for the tight functional coordination between DNA unwinding and de novo RNA primer synthesis. Building upon this static structural foundation, Dong's group7 also determined the cryo-EM structure of the HSV-1 HP complex bound to ssDNA, ADP, and Mg2+. This state represents a post-hydrolysis, nucleotide-bound intermediate, thereby providing high-resolution mechanistic insights into its functional cycle. Their analysis revealed that rotational dynamics of UL5's motor domains drive alternating opening and closing of the DNA-binding groove (Fig. 1B); UL52's NTD and hinge region undergo coordinated conformational changes that couple with UL5's translocase activity, while UL8 exhibits a modest counter-rotational displacement. This structural arrangement provides direct experimental support for a tightly coupled, reciprocally coordinated mechanism in which helicase unwinding and primer synthesis are temporally and spatially interwoven. The study further established a comprehensive ssDNA interaction map at near-atomic resolution and validated, via systematic site-directed mutagenesis, that conserved basic residues mediating ssDNA contacts are indispensable for helicase processivity and ATPase activity. Notably, resistance-conferring mutations to PTV and AMNV were mapped precisely to UL5's 2A domain and the region adjacent to the UL5–UL52 interface. These mutations are situated near the ADP-binding site, which further validates the mechanism through which the inhibitors exert their antiviral effects by sharing the binding pocket and modifying the conformation of the ATPase active site.

Structural analyses by Yu's group8 and Tahirov's group9 converge to show that PTV and AMNV bind not at the canonical ATP/DNA active site of UL5, but rather within a well-defined allosteric pocket situated at the UL5–UL52 interface. This pocket is structurally delineated by UL52's α13 and α32 helices, UL5's α17 helix, and the motif IV loop of UL5, with lysine 356 (K356) of UL5 serving as a critical polar anchor that stabilizes ligand binding through specific electrostatic and hydrogen-bonding interactions. HPIs act as ‘molecular wedges’ by inserting into the cleft between the 1A and 2A motor domains (Fig. 1C). This sterically hinders the ATP/GTP-induced domain closure and relative reorientation of 1A and 2A, which are conformational changes essential for nucleotide-dependent DNA translocation. As a result, UL5 is trapped in an open, catalytically incompetent state, and helicase activity is abolished. To further probe the mechanistic basis of inhibition, Tahirov's group9 superimposed the cryo-EM structure of the inhibitor-bound HP complex onto an AlphaFold3-predicted ATP-bound UL5 model, aligning on the 1A domain. This revealed a ∼6.6 Å rigid-body shift of the 2A domain relative to 1A. However, predicted models cannot fully capture nucleotide-induced conformational changes or transient intermediates—so this shift remains a testable hypothesis, not experimental proof. Definitive validation requires high-resolution structures of the ATP-bound or pre-translocation HP complex. Time-resolved cryo-EM and single-molecule studies will further test whether inhibitor binding restricts conformational dynamics.

Yu's group8 not only accurately located the active pocket, but also identified the sites of the “rigid constraints” for inhibitor binding and the key sites for regulating broad-spectrum activity. Structural and multiple-sequence analyses delineated the critical constraint residues within the UL5–UL52 interface pocket: UL5 K356 serves as the central polar anchor, forming essential hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions that orient PTV and AMNV for optimal engagement; K→N substitutions at K356 (or its neighboring K355 in certain contexts) reduce compound potency by 200- to >2000-fold (with intermediate reductions of 8–130-fold observed in partial substitutions), establishing this locus as a major clinical resistance hotspot (Fig. 1D, left). Beyond UL5, primase-domain residues exert potent allosteric control over inhibitor efficacy: UL52 A899 (A906 in HSV-2), positioned at the periphery of the pocket, contributes to PTV selectivity—its A899T substitution correlates with a ∼43-fold loss in PTV potency, and computational modeling indicates steric clash between the threonine side chain and the PTV sulfonamide moiety. In contrast, AMNV's compact, conformationally restrained scaffold avoids this conflict, conferring superior resilience to peripheral mutations and enabling retained activity against diverse α-herpesviruses—including VZV. Complementary cross-species conservation analysis by Abraham's group10 revealed that UL5 K356 is strictly conserved across all three herpesvirus subfamilies (α-, β-, and γ-). In contrast, key UL52 residues, such as F360 and A899, exhibit variable conservation. Specifically, they are partially preserved in α-herpesviruses but largely divergent in β- and γ-herpesviruses. This asymmetric evolutionary pattern, characterized as ‘UL5 conserved/UL52 variable’, offers a persuasive structural and evolutionary justification for the selective antiviral spectrum of PTV and AMNV against α-herpesviruses.

Abraham's group10 has also advanced the mechanistic understanding of replication fork assembly from static structural description to dynamic functional interpretation. Cryo-EM structures demonstrate that HPIs bind specifically within the interdomain groove formed by UL5's 1A and 2A motor domains, stabilizing a catalytically incompetent, open-state conformation that sterically blocks the ATP hydrolysis–dependent domain closure essential for DNA translocation. Complementary single-molecule optical tweezers assays have quantitatively characterized the functional consequences. In the presence of PTV, the wild-type HP complex exhibited a dose-dependent reduction in unwinding velocity and a concomitant increase in the dwell time spent in a kinetically paused state on DNA, directly correlating structural arrest with impaired mechanical progression. Critically, this work reports the first near-atomic resolution structure of the fully assembled herpesviral replication fork complex, comprising the HP holoenzyme, the DNA Pol holoenzyme (UL30–UL42), and bound inhibitor. Within this architecture, a previously uncharacterized protein–protein interface was identified, which is mediated by the highly conserved “FYNPYL” motif in the N-terminal preN domain of UL30 and the 2B/2C subdomains of UL5 (Fig. 1A, right). Functional experiments show that disrupting this interface impairs replication fork assembly and long-chain DNA synthesis, revealing a new structural basis for understanding how the helicase and polymerase cooperate during fork assembly.

Integrating insights from four recent high-resolution structural studies, the mechanistic understanding of the herpesviral HP complex has evolved from subunit-level static architecture to a dynamic framework encompassing replication fork assembly, allosteric regulation, and inhibitor-induced conformational arrest. This integrated structural foundation now enables rational, structure-guided optimization of HPIs and informs strategies to overcome resistance. From a resistance-oriented medicinal chemistry perspective, the K356/K355 hotspot presents both a vulnerability and an opportunity for the design of next-generation HPI. Since current inhibitors rely significantly on the conserved lysine anchor for binding and orientation; to enhance resilience, the ligand-binding energy can be redistributed by strengthening auxiliary interactions with the UL5 α17 helix, motif IV loop, and adjacent UL52-defined subpockets. Compounds that retain active against K356N/K355N variants can be obtained by introducing alternative hydrogen-bonding vectors, water-mediated networks, or hydrophobic/aromatic contacts that are less dependent on the lysine side chain. A second approach targets allosteric pockets outside the canonical UL5–UL52 interface, including cryptic pockets exposed during helicase domain motion, to completely bypass the lysine anchor. Finally, early resistance profiling against K356N, K355N, UL52 A899T/A906T, and combined mutants should guide lead optimization to favor compounds with a higher genetic barrier to resistance. A key challenge is the narrow antiviral spectrum of current HPIs across α-herpesviruses—especially their weak activity against VZV versus HSV, due to divergent peripheral contacts in the inhibitor-binding pocket, notably involving UL52. To broaden coverage, medicinal chemistry should focus on engineering compact, flexible substituents near the UL52 periphery to tolerate sequence variation without compromising binding affinity. The recently discovered direct polymerase–helicase coupling offers a promising “assembly intervention” target: disrupting this interface (e.g., with peptide mimetics or small molecules) could uncouple DNA unwinding from primer synthesis and block processive replication. However, major structural knowledge gaps persist. High-resolution structures are almost exclusively limited to inhibitor-bound states; the apo form, nucleotide-bound conformations, and transient assembly–unwinding intermediates remain unsolved—largely due to conformational heterogeneity and sample instability. As a result, we lack experimentally grounded, quantitative models of the full catalytic conformational cycle and how it coordinates primase activity, lagging-strand initiation, and leading-strand synthesis in space and time. UL8 was long viewed as a structural scaffold maintaining HP complex integrity, but recent cryo-EM data show modest conformational changes during catalysis, suggesting it may regulate helicase translocation, primase activity, and fork progression. Future work must test whether UL8 acts as a checkpoint regulator during fork stalling or drug-induced arrest. Advancing this requires better vitrification, 3D variability analysis, time-resolved cryo-EM, and integrative methods (smFRET, optical tweezers, kinetic assays) to capture functionally critical and transient states. An integrated structure–dynamics–function approach is essential to reconstruct the herpesviral replisome cycle and design next-generation HPIs with broader activity, resistance resilience, and novel mechanisms.

Building on this robust structural biology foundation, artificial intelligence (AI)-driven strategies are poised to play a transformative role in original HPI discovery: AI-assisted cryptic pocket mining can uncover novel, druggable allosteric sites on the HP complex that are inaccessible in static structures11,12, while AI-powered virtual screening enables the rapid identification of novel lead compounds targeting both characterized and newly discovered binding pockets with high potency and selectivity. Moreover, as a sophisticated multi-subunit protein machine essential for viral replication, the herpesviral HP complex is an ideal target for innovative therapeutic strategies beyond traditional small-molecule inhibition, including targeted protein degradation and molecular glue approaches13, 14, 15, 16. These emerging strategies can induce the selective degradation of the HP complex or modulate its protein-protein interactions through allosteric modulation, offering new avenues to develop mechanistically novel anti-herpesviral therapeutics with high efficacy and reduced propensity for drug resistance. In essence, structural biology not only illuminates the molecular basis of herpesviral replication but also serves as a guiding light for the rational design of innovative, next-generation anti-herpesviral drugs, bridging the gap between structural mechanistic insights and translational therapeutic development.
