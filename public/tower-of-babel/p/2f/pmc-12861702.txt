Chinese traditional medicine complex hemorrhoidal analgesic cream promotes wound healing in diabetic rats after anal fistula surgery via Keap1/Nrf2 signaling pathway
Authors: Jian Liu, Zhitao Yin, Xiaojun Tian, Delong Liu, Chunxiao Wang, Hexue Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study to elucidate how hemorrhoidal analgesic cream (HAC), grounded in the traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) ‘elevation and causation’ theory, promotes postoperative wound healing after anal fistula surgery in diabetic rats by modulating the Keap1/Nrf2 signaling pathway. We used high-sugar and high-fat chow combined with streptozotocin (STZ) to induce diabetic rats to establish a postoperative wound model of anal fistula. The rats were divided into a blank control group, model group, wound-only group, experimental group (HAC treatment), and positive control group (recombinant human epidermal growth factor gel). HAC's impact on wound healing was evaluated by glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), serum inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-6), oxidative stress markers (SOD, MDA), and Nrf2 pathway proteins and mRNA. The results showed that the wound healing rate was significantly increased in the HAC group (92.85 ± 3.41 %, P < 0.01), the levels of HbA1c were decreased (P < 0.05), the levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and MDA were decreased (P < 0.01), the activity of SOD was increased (P < 0.05), and the Keap1/Nrf2 signaling pathway was activated. The study demonstrated that HAC could reduce oxidative stress and inflammatory responses by regulating the Keap1/Nrf2 signaling pathway, which validates its scientific value in promoting wound healing after diabetes mellitus (DM) fistula surgery.

Introduction

Anal fistula is a chronic granulomatous tract connecting the anal canal or rectum to perianal skin, presenting with pain, purulent discharge, and itching, and requiring surgical resolution [[1], [2], [3]]. The rising global burden of diabetes mellitus (DM)—over 537 million cases in 2021—exacerbates postoperative healing: DM patients experience 2–3-fold longer wound closure times and a fourfold higher infection risk [[4], [5], [6]]. The pathological mechanism behind this impaired healing is closely related to oxidative stress induced by hyperglycemia. Sustained elevation in blood glucose activates the AGEs-RAGE axis, resulting in excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), inhibiting fibroblast proliferation, suppression of neovascularization, and triggering of the NF-κB-mediated inflammatory cascade. This establishes a vicious cycle of “oxidative stress-inflammation-metabolic dysfunction”, which constitutes the primary biological barrier to wound repair [7]. While conventional therapies—such as debridement, recombinant human epidermal growth factor (rhEGF), and antibiotics—remain the clinical standard, they are limited by high cost, antibiotic resistance, and their focus on single-target pathways [1,3,8].

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has demonstrated unique advantages in the field of chronic wound management [9]. According to TCM theory, anal fistula belongs to the category of ‘anal leakage’, and diabetic wounds are characterized by a root–branch imbalance: lung yin deficiency represents the root, whereas dampness, heat, stasis, and toxins represent the manifestation. The disruption of ‘ascending and descending of qi’ is regarded as the fundamental pathological mechanism underlying both systemic and local imbalances. The theory of ‘elevation and causation’ emphasizes the coordinated movement of qi through ascending, descending, exiting, and entering dynamics among organs, particularly between the lung and large intestine. Impairment of this coordination leads to stagnation of qi and blood, toxin accumulation, and tissue non-healing, forming the theoretical basis for chronic wound persistence [10]. The Yellow Emperor's Classic of Internal Medicine highlights the interdependence of the lungs and large intestine, focusing on the synergy between lung qi propagation and large intestine qi conduction. Modern studies have shown that dysfunction in this lung-intestine axis may contribute to wound chronicity by disturbing intestinal flora, promoting oxidative stress, and altering systemic immune response [11]. Among TCM external therapies, fumigation and topical application based on the ‘elevation and causation’ principle aim to unblock qi, activate circulation, and eliminate pathogenic stasis via transdermal delivery and warming effects [12]. As a classic external treatment formula, hemorrhoidal analgesic cream (HAC) has been shown to significantly reduce wound healing time in clinical treatments, although its molecular impact on oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways remains unclear.

While multiple signaling pathways are involved in wound healing (e.g., PI3K/Akt, MAPK, and Wnt/β-catenin), this work focused on the Keap1/Nrf2 signaling pathway due to its central role in oxidative stress response, immune regulation, and tissue regeneration [13]. Under physiological conditions, Nrf2 is sequestered in the cytoplasm and degraded via ubiquitination by Keap1. Upon oxidative stimulation, Nrf2 translocates to the nucleus, where it binds to antioxidant response elements (AREs) and promotes the transcription of key antioxidant and cytoprotective enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), heme oxygenase 1 (HO-1), and NAD(P)H: quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1) [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. In addition, Nrf2 activation inhibits NF-κB–mediated inflammatory signaling and promotes angiogenesis, supporting tissue repair in diabetic wounds [19,20]. Furthermore, several active compounds in TCM herbs, including flavonoids, terpenoids, and lignans, have been shown to directly interact with Keap1 or modulate Nrf2 nuclear translocation, providing a mechanistic bridge between herbal medicine and molecular oxidative defense [[21], [22], [23]]. However, no study has yet systematically revealed the molecular mechanism of this formula based on the Nrf2 pathway, and there is a lack of evidence linking the theory of ‘elevation and causation’ with modern biological mechanisms [[24], [25], [26]].

Guided by the TCM theory of ‘elevation and causation’, this study aims to investigate the molecular mechanisms by which HAC promotes wound regeneration in a diabetic anal fistula rat model. By focusing on the Keap1/Nrf2 signaling pathway, we seek to establish scientific evidence for HAC's efficacy and to provide an experimental foundation for integrating TCM with modern medicine in the management of chronic diabetic wounds.

Material and methods

Animal rearing and model establishment

Male SPF Sprague–Dawley rats (200–210 g; Liaoning Univ. Traditional Chinese Medicine) were acclimated for 7 days with ad libitum access to food and water in a controlled environment (21–25 °C, 40–60 % humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle). Healthy animals with normal intake, activity, and behavior were selected [27]. Rats were continuously fed a high-fat, high-sugar diet (10 % lard, 1 % cholesterol, 7 % sucrose, 7 % egg yolk powder, 20 g propylthiouracil, 0.5 % bile salts, balance basal feed), followed by a single intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (65 mg/kg) at week 4 [28], Fasting blood glucose ≥16.7 mmol/L on day 7 confirmed successful diabetes induction. All animal experiments involved in this study were conducted in strict accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Liaoning University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (approval number: LZYY231105) and complies with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health (NIH).

Fourteen days after STZ, under ketamine anesthesia, a small perianal circular wound was created. Rats in the wound-only group were dripped with 0.2 ml normal saline after hemostasis. The model group was inoculated with 1 mL fecal suspension. At 48 h after the operation, the feces were purulent, indicating that the model was successfully established.

HAC and experimental groups

HAC contains five kinds of Chinese herbs, including Phryma leptostachya subsp. Asiatica (Hara) Kitamura, Artemisia argyi Levl.et Vant., Nepeta cataria L., Saposhnikovia divaricata (Turcz.) Schischk., and Zanthoxylum bungeanum Maxim., which are medicinal preparations in our hospitals (1:2:1:1:3 herbs mix was decocted twice with water, filtered, and concentrated to a paste. The herbal paste was blended with Vaseline in a ratio of 3:7 to produce the final cream formulation for topical use).

The rats were randomly divided into blank control group, wound-only group, model group, HAC treatment group and positive control group (rhEGF), with 5 rats in each group. Blank group was not subjected to high-sugar and high-fat diet feeding, streptozotocin injections, wound modelling. Wound-only group performed wound modelling and did not perform fecal inoculation. After successful modelling day 1 to day 14, the HAC group received a 5-min application of hemorrhoidal analgesic cream (HAC, 1 mL) followed by sterile gauze dressing to retain the HAC treated wound; the rhEGF group received 0.5 g recombinant human EGF gel (Contains 5 μg rhEGF) retained and covered with gauze dressing [29]. The wound-only group and model groups were given sterile normal saline for external application. The wounds of all groups were washed with sterile normal saline before the dressing change, and the treatment was given once a day (Detailed experimental protocol in Fig. S1).

HbA1c measurement

Blood was collected from orbital veins of rats on day 14, anticoagulated with EDTA, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was detected using a fully automated biochemistry analyzer (Hitachi 7080) with a matching reagent kit (Ningbo Meikang Bio, batch number MK-HbA1c).

Wound healing rate experiment

On days 7 and 14 of the intervention, the wound diameter was measured using a ruler, and the wound was photographed using a Canon EOS 90D digital camera at normalized distance. Subsequently, the wound area was quantified using ImageJ software version 1.53 and the measured wound diameter data. The rate of wound healing was determined using the following formula: Wound Healing Rate (%) = [(Area of Wound at Baseline - Area of Wound at Conclusion of Treatment)/Area of Wound at Baseline] × 100 %.

Histopathological examination of traumas

On the 14th day of the intervention, rats were anesthetized and euthanized. A 5 mm tissue sample was taken from the wound and nearby areas, fixed in 10 % formaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4 μm sections. These were stained with hematoxylin (Sigma, No. H3136) for 5 min and eosin (Sigma, No. E4382) for 2 min, then sealed with neutral gum. Using an Olympus BX53 microscope at 200 × magnification, the density of new capillaries, collagen fiber organization, and inflammatory cell infiltration were assessed in five randomly selected fields per slide.

ELISA

On the 14th day of intervention, the traumatic tissue homogenate (RIPA lysate, Beyotime, item number P0013B) was taken, and the supernatant was centrifuged, malondialdehyde (MDA) content and SOD activity were detected by Nanjing Jianjian Biological Kit (Item No. A003-1, A001-3). TNF-α and IL-6 levels were measured using Lianke Biological ELISA Kits (Item No. EK0526, EK0412) in serum samples collected from the rat tail vein. Absorbance was read at 450 nm using a BioTek Synergy H1.

Western blot analysis

Protein levels of Keap1, Nrf2, HO-1, and NQO1 in wound tissues were evaluated by Western blot on day 14 of treatment. Wound samples were homogenized in lysis buffer containing 1 % protease inhibitor (Beyotime ST506), and total protein was quantified by BCA assay (Thermo 23,225). Thirty micrograms of protein per sample were separated on 10 % SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore ISEQ00010). Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies—Keap1 (Abcam , 1:1000), Nrf2 (CST 12721 S, 1:800), HO-1 (Santa Cruz sc-166,509, 1:500), NQO1 (Abcam, 1:500), and β-actin (Proteintech, item no. 66009-1-Ig, 1:5000)—then with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Proteintech, No. SA00001-2, 1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were developed using ECL chemiluminescence reagent (Millipore WBKLS050), and band intensities were quantified by Image Lab 6.0.

PPI and GO enrichment analysis

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis of key genes associated with the Keap1/Nrf2 signaling pathway was performed using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/cgi/input.pl), followed by Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using SPSS 20.0 software. Comparisons between multiple groups were made using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and two-by-two comparisons between groups were made using the LSD-t test [30]; non-normally distributed data were tested using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant difference, and graphs were plotted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 [31].

Results

HAC effectively promotes postoperative wound healing in diabetic anal fistulae

In the experimental group receiving HAC treatment, the wound healing rate on the 14th day post-intervention was markedly higher compared to the 7th day (Fig. 1A). Specifically, by day 7, the healing rate in the experimental group was 61.20 ± 6.07 %, which represents an approximate 20 % increase (P < 0.05) over the model group's rate of 40.20 ± 10.56 %, and was significantly superior to that of the positive drug group. By day 14, the healing rate in the experimental group further improved to 92.85 ± 3.41 % (P < 0.001), while the positive drug group achieved a rate of 94.11 ± 3.15 % (P < 0.05), indicating comparable efficacy between the two treatments in promoting wound healing (Fig. 1B). Histopathological analysis revealed that, by day 14, the experimental group exhibited a higher density of neovascular capillaries, a denser arrangement of collagen fibers, and reduced inflammatory cell infiltration compared to the model group (Fig. 1C). HAC lowers blood glucose and inhibits oxidative stress and inflammation

Contemporary medical research has established that oxidative stress resulting from hyperglycemia is a key factor in delayed wound healing following anal fistula surgery, alongside oxidative stress and inflammation induced by the wound itself [35,36]. Consequently, on the 14th day of treatment, the experiment was further conducted to investigate the changes in the content of SOD and MDA in the wound tissue (Fig. 2B and C) as well as the serum inflammatory cytokines tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), and the results showed that the experimental group significantly increased the levels of SOD (P < 0.001) and decreased the level of MDA (P < 0.01) and serum TNF-α and IL-6 (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2D and E). These results suggest that HAC not only reduces blood glucose, but also modulates oxidative stress and inflammatory response after diabetic anal fistula surgery.

HAC affects the expression of genes related to the oxidative stress pathway

Nrf2 is a central regulator of redox mechanisms and is closely related to diabetic wound healing, and it plays a key role in inhibiting oxidative stress by regulating the expression of antioxidant enzymes, such as the transporter protein glutathione (GSH) and HO-1, which scavenge intracellular ROS [22].

To further validate the biological connectivity and functional integrity of redox-related targets modulated by HAC, a protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis was performed using STRING with the highest confidence setting (interaction score ≥0.900). The PPI analysis provided important theoretical support for selecting core readouts for downstream experiments. It reinforced the hypothesis that HAC exerts multi-target synergy through interconnected redox mechanisms, which is especially relevant in the context of traditional Chinese compound formulas. For this reason, the results of further experiments by RT-qPCR showed that on the 14th day of the intervention of HAC, the Keap1 mRNA expression in the experimental group was significantly down-regulated (P < 0.001). mRNA expression of Nrf2, HO-1, and NQO1 was significantly up-regulated (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3B). And these genes were significantly enriched in oxidative stress bioprocess (Fig. 3C), further supporting their functional relevance in the antioxidant response initiated by HAC.

Discussion and conclusion

This study integrates the core concept of the traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) theory of ‘elevation and causation’ with molecular biology techniques to systematically explore the mechanisms by which the herbal compound preparation HAC promotes post-surgical wound healing in diabetic anal fistula models. Experimental results demonstrated that HAC exerted multifaceted effects in vivo, including metabolic regulation, anti-inflammation, antioxidative stress, and enhanced wound healing. HbA1c levels were reduced by approximately 50 % compared to the model group, suggesting that HAC effectively improved glucose metabolism under diabetic conditions. This metabolic benefit is preliminarily attributed to bioactive compounds within Phryma leptostachya subsp. And Artemisia argyi, which may modulate insulin sensitivity through phytochemicals such as lignans, sesquiterpenes, and flavonoids [[37], [38], [39]]. At the inflammatory and oxidative stress levels, HAC significantly reduced serum levels of TNF-α and IL-6 by more than 30 %, decreased MDA content in wound tissue by 41.8 %, and enhanced SOD activity by 2.4-fold. These findings provide molecular and physiological evidence supporting the TCM theory that regulating qi dynamics (especially through the lung and intestine) can interrupt the vicious cycle of “oxidative stress–inflammation–metabolic disorder” at the wound microenvironment [40,41]. Mechanistic investigation further confirmed that the wound healing efficacy of HAC is closely associated with the activation of the Keap1/Nrf2 signaling pathway. Specifically, HAC downregulated Keap1 expression while markedly upregulating the transcription factor Nrf2 and its downstream antioxidant and cytoprotective proteins, including HO-1 and NQO1 [42].

The potential role of the complex and diverse herbal components of HAC in regulating the Keap1/Nrf2 axis. Phryma leptostachya subsp. Asiatica (Hara) Kitamura is rich in flavonoids such as luteolin and apigenin, as well as phenylpropanoids, which are known to directly interact with cysteine residues on Keap1, promoting Nrf2 nuclear translocation and subsequent transcriptional activation of ARE-regulated genes [43,44]. Artemisia argyi Levl. et Vant. Contains essential oils (e.g., eucalyptol, camphor), flavonoids (e.g., quercetin), and triterpenes, of which quercetin has been extensively verified as a potent Nrf2 activator through Keap1 modification mechanisms [45,46]. Nepeta cataria L. and Saposhnikovia divaricata (Turcz.) Schischk. Are traditionally paired for their exterior-releasing properties and are rich in volatile oils (e.g., menthone, saposhnikovia chromones). Some evidence suggests that active polysaccharides from Saposhnikovia possess both anti-inflammatory effects and the ability to activate Nrf2 signaling, thereby alleviating oxidative injury and modulating the local immune microenvironment [47,48]. Zanthoxylum bungeanum Maxim. Is characterized by alkylamides (e.g., hydroxy-α-sanshool) and essential oils. Alkylamides and related compounds have shown strong Nrf2-activating potential and NF-κB suppression effects, in addition to promoting local circulation via vasodilation [49,50]. Collectively, the therapeutic benefits of HAC are likely attributable to the synergistic action of multiple phytochemicals—flavonoids, terpenoids, and alkaloids—that activate the Keap1/Nrf2 signaling pathway through various mechanisms, including Keap1 cysteine modification, Nrf2 stabilization, nuclear translocation, and transcriptional enhancement of ARE elements.

Remarkably, the wound healing rate reached 93 % after 14 days of HAC treatment, comparable to that of the positive control (recombinant human epidermal growth factor gel, ∼94 %) but achieved at an ∼80 % lower cost. This underscores the unique advantage of TCM compound formulations in multi-target, cost-effective chronic wound management [51].

Furthermore, this study establishes a conceptual and mechanistic bridge between the TCM theory of ‘elevation and causation’ and the modern biological function of Keap1/Nrf2 signaling pathway activation [52]. HAC significantly enhanced local and systemic antioxidative capacity, reflecting both external (skin-level) and internal (organ-level) regulation of qi dynamics. Nrf2 is widely recognized as a negative regulator of NF-κB-mediated inflammatory signaling. Our data support that HAC upregulates Nrf2-representing the activation of protective qi—while suppressing NF-κB driven inflammation-representing “toxic stagnation”-thereby achieving the TCM therapeutic goal of “harmonizing qi and dispelling toxins” at the molecular level [52,53]. This finding is consistent with previous studies on classical prescriptions such as Huangbai Liniment, which also relies on Nrf2 activation to promote diabetic wound healing, further validating the rationality and scientific value of TCM strategies for chronic wound care [41,54].

From a clinical application perspective, HAC can be administered through two complementary modalities: steam fumigation at 38 °C and topical scrubbing. This dual-mode strategy highlights the integrative advantage of combining traditional Chinese and Western medicine approaches. Fumigation not only provides localized warming but also enhances peripheral circulation and promotes transdermal absorption of the active compounds. In traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), this aligns with the concept of “opening the interstices” and “expelling pathogenic qi” through external therapy. When combined with topical scrubbing, the treatment facilitates effective wound cleansing and promotes debridement, thereby improving the local wound environment. This “fumigation–scrubbing” dual method offers a practical and effective protocol for managing chronic diabetic wounds, especially in resource-constrained clinical settings where access to advanced dressings or high-cost biologics may be limited [55].

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, although the TCM lung–large intestine axis and ‘elevation and causation’ theory suggest a role for the gut microbiota in wound healing, we did not measure gut microbial indicators in this study. Future work incorporating 16 S rRNA sequencing and metabolomics will help elucidate these interactions. Second, while Nrf2 activation was confirmed, we did not directly assess Nrf2 nuclear translocation, which is a critical step in its transcriptional regulation; this omission should be addressed in future studies to more definitively establish the activation status of Nrf2. Third, the potential crosstalk between Nrf2 and other regenerative pathways, such as Wnt/β-catenin and TGF-β1, remains to be explored using conditional knockout models. Finally, the optimal duration of HAC treatment should be refined through pharmacokinetic and biodistribution studies to facilitate personalized therapeutic regimens.

In conclusion, HAC promotes post-surgical wound healing in diabetic rats by modulating the Keap1/Nrf2 signaling pathway, providing both mechanistic insight and empirical support for TCM-based topical therapeutics. This research deepens our modern scientific understanding of the TCM theory of ‘elevation and causation’ and lays an experimental foundation for future development of multi-target, low-cost antioxidant herbal formulations for chronic wound management. Future studies will focus on multi-omics regulation of the lung-gut-skin axis and validate efficacy through multicenter clinical trials to promote the standardization and international application of TCM in chronic wound care.
