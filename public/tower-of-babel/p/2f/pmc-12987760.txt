Social and structural traits influence species gains while resources influence species losses in a native grassland
Authors: Emily M Holden, James F Cahill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultiple factors influence temporal species turnover, including resource requirements and species traits. The standard model in plant ecology is that adding soil nutrients will result in taller communities, reducing understory light levels and leading to species loss via size‐asymmetric competition. However, underlying this model is the notion that competitive dynamics are outcomes of individual species characteristics rather than an emergent trait of the suite of species involved in the interactions. Thus, whether plant social context (identities and interactions of neighbors) impacts competitive outcomes is unclear and potentially overlooked. Using data from a three‐year field study manipulating light and soil resources, we asked how resource manipulations, community diversity, or structural (physiological) and social (interactions with neighbors) traits influenced species turnover. We created co‐occurrence networks to develop novel metrics that capture the prevalence of positive and negative associations for each of 24 species in a native grassland community. We then estimated temporal beta diversity to partition species turnover into gains and losses, testing whether these compositional changes were impacted by resource manipulations or communities' structural or social traits. We found evidence that resources, structural traits, and social traits all impacted aspects of community assembly. Nutrient addition but not reduced light increased species losses, and communities with either high or low specific leaf area (SLA) and root tissue density (RTD) community‐weighted mean (CWM) trait values gained more species. Communities consisting of species forming numerous positive species co‐occurrences gained fewer species throughout the study than communities of species forming fewer positive co‐occurrences. Thus, a species' tendency to form positive co‐occurrences has a functional consequence for community‐level compositional stability. Resource addition increased species losses independently of CWM height, suggesting it was not size‐asymmetric competition for light that resulted in species loss in our study. Together, these results challenge the notion that nutrient‐driven species loss is primarily mediated by size‐asymmetric competition, highlighting the role of species' social interactions in governing community change.Keywords: community stability, diversity‐stability relationships, functional traits, plant networks, root tissue density, species turnover, specific leaf area

INTRODUCTION

Community stability is a prominent topic in ecological research (e.g., MacArthur, 1955; McCann, 2000) and an aspect of long‐term coexistence, though it can also occur at shorter time scales and in response to perturbations (Kéfi et al., 2019). Several frameworks propose different drivers of stability (e.g., Adler et al., 2007; Hubbell, 2001; Tilman, 1988), positing alternate drivers of importance (e.g., competitive ability, resource uptake rates, stochasticity, species traits, and neighbor identity). However, the relative contribution of these drivers to stability remains unclear, and ecologists cannot consistently predict species gain and loss with high confidence (Zurell et al., 2023). There are many potential causes for the lack of clarity on the drivers of compositional stability, which can include processes involving multiple low‐effect‐size drivers and critical biological processes being absent from dominant paradigms. To understand the internal processes promoting community stability, we must know how individual processes contribute to species gain and loss; therefore, ecologists must clarify which drivers influence what process.

Community stability likely results from how individual species interact with local light and nutrient levels, with these interactions influenced by both resource availability and the identities of neighboring species (HilleRisLambers et al., 2012). Together, resource and neighbor contexts determine whether a species persists or is excluded. Community invasibility (the gain of new species) and species loss together represent the two direct mechanisms by which temporal stability is reduced. Within plant communities, resource supply and light availability are commonly identified as key drivers of compositional stability. Three predictions follow from this focus on nutrients and light: (1) increased soil resources cause species loss due to (2) increased plant height, resulting in (3) reduced understory light and subsequent loss of shorter plants (DeMalach et al., 2017; Muehleisen et al., 2023). Indeed, studies frequently find that adding resources to plant communities results in species loss as a subset of species dominates and monopolizes limiting light resources (Borer et al., 2014), and there is evidence that adding light to the understory can “rescue” communities from species loss (Eskelinen et al., 2022; Hautier et al., 2009). However, if compositional stability results from qualities other than resource distributions and use (e.g., species diversity), there should be no consistent relationship between resources and species turnover (Hubbell, 2001).

Species diversity is suggested to increase temporal stability (Valencia et al., 2020; Wagg et al., 2022), acting through both species richness (Ives et al., 2000) and functional diversity (de Bello et al., 2021). Hypothesized mechanisms underlying these relationships include more fulsome use of available niche space and resources, resulting in fewer opportunities for colonization (McCann, 2000). To this end, higher richness leads to more species interactions, generating greater biotic resistance and compositional stability (Smith‐Ramesh et al., 2017). However, there does not appear to be general theories about whether diversity itself impacts rates of species loss after nitrogen addition.

While the above ideas are based upon individual or population‐level properties influencing stability, stability may also be an emergent property of a local community driven by traits of co‐occurring species. Community structural (physiological) traits (i.e., community‐weighted means; CWMs; Lavorel et al., 2008) and their diversity may influence future stability due to traits' mediation of niche requirements (e.g., Fu et al., 2020; Kunstler et al., 2016). To illustrate, if competition for light and soil resources determines species turnover (Martin & Ghalambor, 2023), ecological selection may lead to compositional shifts favoring more competitive species that are taller or have greater specific root lengths (Bennett et al., 2016; DeMalach et al., 2017). Consequently, if traits mediate plant interactions, certain CWM trait values could be associated with reduced species turnover and enhanced community stability (Gerhold et al., 2015; Gross et al., 2017).

Competitive outcomes and plant habitat use can also depend upon the identity of neighbors (Bilas et al., 2021; Cahill et al., 2010). Species differ in their tendency to co‐occur with potential neighbor species, even within a relatively homogenous community, for many reasons (e.g., interactions, niche partitioning; Bimler et al., 2024; Koffel et al., 2021). There is some evidence that small‐scale co‐occurrence patterns infer ecological function, such as invasion resistance or competitive intensity (Bannar‐Martin et al., 2018; Gridzak et al., 2024; Stotz et al., 2020). However, it is unclear whether species' social context (i.e., their neighbors' identities and interactions) or social traits (the direction and strength of neighbor interactions) impacts community stability.

In this paper, we consider the broad question of whether extrinsic resource changes, diversity, or structural and social traits influence species gains and losses in plant communities. We also seek to understand if common characteristics exist among species gained or lost. To do so, we utilize data from a multiyear field experiment in a native grassland. We compared the ability of experimental resource manipulations, locally collected trait data, and novel social metrics to predict species gains, losses, and turnover. Specifically, we ask:

Do resource addition and shading both cause increased species loss, as predicted by the size‐asymmetric light competition hypothesis?

METHODS

Study site

We conducted our study at the University of Alberta Roy Berg Kinsella Research Ranch. The site is a moderately productive, low‐diversity grassland at the northern edge of the Great Plains (53°0833 N, 111.5500 W). The site is dominated by graminoids, including Festuca hallii (Vasey) Piper, Hesperostipa curtiseta (Hitchc.) Barkworth, and Poa pratensis L., but much of its total species diversity is represented by forbs (Brown & Cahill, 2019). Nitrogen and water primarily limit plant growth at Kinsella (Lamb et al., 2007).

Resource manipulations

To determine how resources, diversity, and community traits influence stability, we established 102 1 m × 1 m plots, with each plot subject to one of six combinations of light (−, ambient, +) and nutrient (ambient, +) treatments. We employed a blocked design to account for spatial correlation, with 17 blocks established in a homogenous 50‐ha area within the grassland in May 2021. A schematic showing the layout of plots within blocks is in Appendix S1: Figure S1. At the plot level, we increased light availability by “tying back” standing vegetation outside the 1‐m2 plot by pushing it away from the plot and holding it in place with Vigoro bird netting (Vigoro Corporation, Illinois, USA). New growth was tied back each month of the growing season (May–August). We decreased light availability by placing 1.5 m × 1.5 m × 0.9 m shade structures over the top of plots assigned to the shade treatment (Appendix S1: Figure S2). Structures were constructed of 2.5 cm × 5 cm lumber and covered with shade cloth (Lee Valley Tools, Ottawa, Canada). To add resources, we applied Nutricote 13‐13‐13 slow‐release NPK fertilizer pellets at a rate of 4.48 g/m2 total nitrogen. We used this low fertilization rate to maintain realism relative to the low‐nutrient habitats these species are found in within this region (Lamb et al., 2007). The experiment lasted 3 years, and light and soil resource treatments were reapplied at the start of the growing season in 2022 and 2023. We used resource measures to test for niche segregation among species in our communities as detailed below.

Tests for niche partitioning

Patterns of species co‐occurrence may not only result from plant interactions but could also reflect niche similarities such that species with similar niche requirements will necessarily co‐occur in similar locations even in the absence of any direct plant–plant interactions (Barner et al., 2018, Blanchet et al., 2020). Such an issue would be a significant confound in our study because species co‐occurrences would reflect ranges of tolerance and not meaningful plant associations. To test whether species co‐occurrence patterns were the result of resource requirements, we collected 10‐cm‐deep soil samples in May and August 2021 (i.e., the beginning and end of the first growing season), and quantified plant‐available nitrogen (ammonium [NH4] and nitrate [NO3]) via a colorimetric autoanalyzer following KCl extraction (Bower & Holm‐Hansen, 1980; EPA, 1993). We then calculated the change in nitrogen over the 2021 growing season to quantify the plant‐available nitrogen used by plants. To quantify the amount of light available to plants, we measured photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) above and below the canopy at each plot using an Accu‐Par Light metre (Decagon) and calculated the difference between these measures to find the true light penetration to the understory. For the above canopy PAR measures of shaded plots, we used block‐level above‐canopy mean PAR values of ambient plots. We plotted the proportional cover of the 24 species included in networks along the change in NH4 and NO3 over the 2021 growing season, as well as total light penetration. We then inspected the graphs for evidence of species segregation (Appendix S1: Figure S3). To further test for niche clustering in multidimensional space, we ran a redundancy analysis (RDA) of species proportional abundances constrained by the change in NH4 and total light penetration (Appendix S1: Figure S4). We recognize these are not exhaustive tests for segregation along all possible niche axes, but we focused on these resources because they are limiting at our site and align with our experimental manipulations.

Community composition, productivity, and temporal beta diversity

To quantify species presence and abundance, we identified all vascular plants within a 50 cm × 50 cm focal area in the first and last year of the study, estimating their percent cover. We conducted these surveys during peak biomass periods to ensure maximal species emergence. These data were used to calculate species richness and for subsequent analyses.

Here, we quantify community stability by examining species loss and gain over time, distinct from population‐level stability, which focuses on temporal variation in population sizes (or cover; McCann, 2000). We use temporal beta diversity (Legendre, 2019), which measures dissimilarity in species composition between two time points using the Jaccard index. Values range from 0 (exact similarity) to 1 (no overlap in species between time points). Dissimilarity is the sum of species gains and losses, allowing us to partition compositional changes into gains (species gained relative to shared, gained, and lost species) and losses (species lost relative to shared, gained, and lost species; Legendre, 2019). We calculated temporal beta diversity using the adespatial package (Legendre, 2019), specifically plot‐level species gain and loss.

Characterizing structural traits

To characterize communities' structural traits, we calculated plot‐level CWMs for three commonly measured plant traits. We chose not to use the full suite of traits available for species at this field site (Cahill, 2020) and instead restricted our focus only to those which have been explicitly hypothesized to function in resource acquisition (Freschet et al., 2021; Reich, 2014) and plant interactions (Wang et al., 2010; Yin et al., 2021). We suggest that restricting our set of traits in this manner reduces experiment‐wide type‐I error rates and ensures a hypothesis‐testing framework rather than an inductive description. Trait values came from a database of traits collected at the research site (Cahill, 2020); collection methods are detailed therein. Traits we used included:

Maximum height: The distance from the soil surface to the apex of focal plants. This trait is proposed to function in competition for light (Moles et al., 2009).

Maximum height: The distance from the soil surface to the apex of focal plants. This trait is proposed to function in competition for light (Moles et al., 2009).

Specific leaf area (SLA): The ratio of a plant's leaf surface area to its leaf mass. SLA may reflect a trade‐off between light acquisition and structural integrity or leaf longevity (Wright et al., 2004).

Specific leaf area (SLA): The ratio of a plant's leaf surface area to its leaf mass. SLA may reflect a trade‐off between light acquisition and structural integrity or leaf longevity (Wright et al., 2004).

Root tissue density (RTD): Fine root mass divided by its volume. RTD reflects structural investment in the roots and may impact soil resource acquisition (Freschet et al., 2021; Weigelt et al., 2021).

Root tissue density (RTD): Fine root mass divided by its volume. RTD reflects structural investment in the roots and may impact soil resource acquisition (Freschet et al., 2021; Weigelt et al., 2021).

Traits were combined with abundance estimates to calculate CWMs using package FD (Laliberté et al., 2014). We also calculated functional divergence (FDiv) using package FD. FRic is a measure of functional diversity that captures the spread of a community's functional space (Mouchet et al., 2010) and may be linked to the functional redundancy of community traits (Kang et al., 2015).

Characterizing social traits

Here, we create novel “social metrics” at the species level, based on species co‐occurrences within unmanipulated plots within the field site. We use these metrics as predictors in subsequent compositional stability models, rather than treating co‐occurrence shifts in experimental plots due to treatments as the response variable, which are likely influenced by the treatments. Co‐occurrence patterns can provide evidence of social interactions in many taxa (Gao et al., 2022; Syme et al., 2023), and they can occur due to nonsocial processes such as niche requirements or sampling scales (Blanchet et al., 2020). Here, we do not assume or assert any specific mechanisms generating the co‐occurrence patterns observed. Instead, we focus on the potential outcome of such patterns: a species with many negative co‐occurrences will tend to encounter a narrower subset of species as neighbors than one with many positive co‐occurrences. Thus, the structure of positive and negative co‐occurrences influences which species grow together, even if it does not necessarily alter the number of neighbors. Consequently, the frequency of negative and positive co‐occurrences likely impacts which species grow together.

Using control plot abundance data, we calculated a co‐occurrence matrix using the package co‐occur (Griffith et al., 2016; Appendix S1: Figure S2) and built a probabilistic model for unmanipulated plots, estimating observed and expected co‐occurrences and their probabilities. The co‐occurrence model calculated the observed and expected frequencies of co‐occurrence for all species expected to co‐occur more than once and the probabilities that more extreme values of co‐occurrence could be obtained by chance (Griffith et al., 2016). We were not interested in determining which co‐occurrences are “significant” in their own right; rather, we used co‐occurrence data to indicate whether a given species tended towards positive or negative co‐occurrences with other species. Thus, we employed a lenient inclusion criterion (p < 0.6) to ensure that all species expected to co‐occur more than once were represented in the network. We chose this threshold because it returned the complete set of species for which the model could estimate both observed and expected co‐occurrences (24 species total; Appendix S1: Figure S5, Table S1). Importantly, we did not interpret these associations as statistically supported interactions; their sign was used only to summarize each species' overall tendency to co‐occur more or less frequently with others. Species were nodes, and their associations were edges (Appendix S1: Figure S5).

From predicted co‐occurrences, we created two simple metrics of potential plant sociality, representing the degree to which a species likely grows with other species. We tallied the total number of positive and negative edges for each node separately (i.e., the number of positive and negative associations for each species) in the co‐occurrence network. We call these metrics of positive and negative co‐occurrences “gregariousness” and “reclusiveness,” respectively (Appendix S1: Figure S6). These metrics are absolute and not standardized to 1. We treated gregariousness and reclusiveness like other plant traits, calculating the CWM of each plot by multiplying each species' count of positive and negative interactions by its abundance. The values represent community dominance by social tendencies, paralleling dominance by traits like height.

Characteristics of species lost and gained

In addition to understanding the overall patterns of species loss and gain, we investigated whether species traits influenced their loss, gain, or retention within the community. To do this, for each species, we calculated the proportion of instances it was lost or gained across plots from 2021 to 2023, excluding species in less than 10% of plots in either year. This led us to retain 17 common species, representing 88% of total plant cover in 2021 and 2023 (Appendix S1: Table S2). This ensured sufficient replication while still being able to detect widespread losses or new colonizations.

Statistical analyses

To test whether resource manipulations, diversity, or community traits influence species gains and losses, we fitted linear mixed‐effects models (LMMs) using the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015). Resource treatments (nutrients and light), diversity metrics (richness and FDiv), structural traits (CWM maximum height, SLA, and RTD), and social traits (gregariousness and reclusiveness) were fixed factors in separate models (see Appendix S1: Table S3 for model terms). We included interaction terms among all fixed effects in models to test for context‐dependent relationships. The experimental block was a random effect, and species gains and losses were response variables in separate models (six models total). If interactions were significant, we obtained estimated marginal means for models using the emmeans package (Lenth, 2024). We evaluated model fit by examining residual QQ plots and by calculating marginal and conditional R 2 values using the MuMIn package (Bartoń, 2025). To further clarify the effects of light on species losses, we directly compared light penetration to species losses using an LMM including experimental block as a random effect (Appendix S1: Table S3).

As we examined several predictors of species turnover, we tested for collinearity among model predictors in two ways. First, we calculated Pearson's correlation coefficients for all pairwise combinations of all possible predictors using the Hmisc package (Harrell, 2025), then visualized significant correlations among predictors (Appendix S1: Figure S7). Additionally, we calculated the variance inflation factor (VIF) for each model described above (see Appendix S1: Table S4 for model specifications and VIFs). For resource, diversity, and social and structural trait models, the VIF indicated high collinearity, so we centered the variables and then reassessed VIFs. When variables were centered to avoid collinearity, the centered variables were then used as predictors in LMMs.

To assess which explanatory framework (resource availability, diversity, or trait‐based effects) had the most relative support in predicting species turnover, we calculated corrected Akaike information criterion (AICc) values for LMMs using the AICcmodavg package (Mazerolle, 2025). Separate model sets were evaluated for species gains and losses. We compared AICc, ΔAICc, and log‐likelihood statistics to assess the relative support for each model. We identified the model with the greatest relative support for each response variable as the model with the lowest AICc value. All analyses were conducted in program R (v4.2.1, R Core Development Team, 2024). We fit two generalized linear models (GLMs) using a gamma distribution and inverse‐link function, where the proportion of times a species was gained was the response variable and its total initial abundance and social traits were predictor variables in separate models (Appendix S1: Table S3). We chose this distribution and link function because our response variable was a positive, continuous variable, and this model was the best fit when compared to alternative distributions. We used linear models to model the relationships between the proportion of times a species was gained (response variable) and its structural traits (predictor variable; Appendix S1: Table S3). We also used linear models to model the proportion of times a species was lost (response variable) as a function of its total initial abundance, social traits, and structural traits, all other relationships (predictor variables in three separate models; Appendix S1: Table S3). To identify potential structural or social traits associated with gain or loss, we used the metrics described above. The proportions of times a species was gained and lost became response variables in models where total initial abundance, structural traits (maximum height, SLA, and RTD), and counts of social interactions (positive and negative edges) were fixed factors in separate models (six models total; Appendix S1: Table S3).

RESULTS

We identified 100 vascular plant species across all plots and years. Plot‐level species richness ranged from 4 to 19 in 2021 and from 3 to 19 in 2023. All plots exhibited compositional changes, and 77 of 102 plots (75%) changed in richness (Appendix S1: Table S5). Of these 77 plots, 53 showed a net loss of species, and 24 experienced a net gain of species (Appendix S1: Table S5). We did not find evidence that species sorted according to resource axes (Appendix S1: Figures S3 and S4), suggesting a limited role of niche segregation in creating co‐occurrence patterns within this field site. Our resource manipulations resulted in changes in light environments (Figure 1b,d; Appendix S1: Table S6). Shade treatments significantly reduced light penetration to the understory (Appendix S1: Figure S8, Table S6).

Species gains

Neither fertilizer nor light treatments affected species gains (Figure 1a,b; Appendix S1: Table S7). Rather, community social traits were most predictive of species gains in a community (Table 1). Plots dominated by species of high gregariousness were less likely to gain species than plots with lower CWM‐gregariousness (Figure 2a; Appendix S1: Table S7). In contrast, we found no effect of reclusiveness on species gains (Figure 2b; Appendix S1: Table S7). We found an interactive effect of SLA and RTD on species gains: gains increased in plots with high SLA or RTD values (Appendix S1: Figure S9, Table S7). Though species gains were higher in plots with diverse SLA and RTD values, FDiv did not affect species gains (Figure 3b; Appendix S1: Table S7). Further, species‐rich plots were more likely to gain species than species‐poor plots (Figure 3a).

Species losses

Fertilizer addition increased species losses (Figure 1c; Appendix S1: Table S8), with the resource model receiving the most relative support for being predictive of species loss (Table 1). However, (1) light treatments had no direct effect on species losses (Figure 1d; Appendix S1: Table S8), (2) plots of greater CWM height did not have greater species losses (Figure 4c; Appendix S1: Table S8), and (3) there was no interactive effect of nutrient and light treatments on losses (Appendix S1: Table S8). Further, decreased light penetration to the understory did not increase species losses (χ2 = 0.058, df = 1, p = 0.81; Appendix S1: Figure S10). Neither initial species richness nor FDiv impacted species losses (Figure 4a,b; Appendix S1: Table S8). No social traits significantly affected species losses (Figure 2c,d; Appendix S1: Table S8).

Characteristics of species lost and gained

At the species level, rare and taller species were more likely to be lost from a plot than common or shorter species (Appendix S1: Figure S11, Table S9). No social or structural species traits predicted the probability of a species being gained in a plot (Appendix S1: Table S9).

DISCUSSION

Overall, we found substantial species turnover in this system but limited support for the causes of species gain and loss hypothesized by dominant theories. Different factors influenced species loss and gain: resource addition increased species losses but not species gains, and we found no support for the light limitation hypothesis driving species loss (Figure 1c,d). Species gains were lowest in communities of more gregarious species, suggesting co‐occurrence patterns capture emergent properties of biotic resistance. Contrary to diversity‐stability hypotheses, plots with higher initial species richness gained more species throughout the study. Our results emphasize the need to consider multiple drivers when examining patterns of species turnover and that standard paradigms fail to capture the basic dynamics of community stability.

Resource effects on stability

Species loss commonly seen with nutrient addition (e.g., Muehleisen et al., 2023) can be caused by increased plant growth, deeper shading, and loss of less competitive species in size‐asymmetric competition for light (DeMalach et al., 2017; Grime, 1977). These studies posit that the lack of understory light drives species loss, which they demonstrate by reversing species loss through understory light supplementation (Eskelinen et al., 2022; Hautier et al., 2009). We also found that species losses increased in fertilized treatments, but the direct reduction of light through shading did not significantly increase species loss (Figure 1d; Appendix S1: Table S8) despite decreased understory light penetration (Appendix S1: Table S6). Further, losses were not reduced by light addition (Figure 1d; Appendix S1: Table S8), nor were they related to understory light penetration (Appendix S1: Figure S10). Combined, fertilizer‐induced species loss is likely more complex than simple competitive dynamics in this system (see also Brown & Cahill, 2022; Dickson & Foster, 2011). We suggest alternative mechanisms of niche‐based limits of ammonium toxicity or soil acidification may be at play (Band et al., 2022). This is a low‐nitrogen system (Lamb et al., 2007), and even the low rate of fertilizer applied to plots may have been toxic to native species, leading to reduced performance (Britto & Kronzucker, 2002). As our treatments effectively changed understory light penetration (Appendix S1: Table S6), we suggest nitrogen and light dynamics may not be so tightly linked in low‐nutrient systems (e.g., Brown & Cahill, 2022) compared to the well‐established nutrient‐light‐loss dynamics of high‐fertility areas or historically fertile soils (e.g., Eskelinen et al., 2022; Isbell et al., 2013). We urge caution in asserting light competition as a mechanism of fertilizer‐induced species loss without direct and local evidence.

Diversity effects on stability

In contrast to expectations of the diversity‐stability hypothesis, greater initial species richness and functional diversity did not create temporal compositional stability. Instead, communities with more species at the start of the study gained more species over time (Figure 3a; Appendix S1: Table S7). In other words, increased diversity led to increased colonization, while lower diversity plots had relatively higher levels of biotic resistance. Additionally, initial diversity did not increase or decrease species losses: communities that began with more species to lose did not lose more species (Figure 4a). Diversity likely co‐varies with the actual factors that determine stability, such as synchrony or stress (Cavieres, 2021; Valencia et al., 2020), as evidenced by other examples of mixed relationships between diversity and invasibility or stability (Levine & D'Antonio, 1999).

Trait effects on stability

Structural traits functioned in species turnover: fewer species were gained in communities with intermediate CWM SLA and RTD values (Appendix S1: Figure S9), which could result from both resource‐acquisitive and resource‐conservative plants being present in communities. Diverse resource acquisition strategies could result in greater niche complementarity and fewer available niches for colonizing species (Eisenhauer et al., 2013; Zhu et al., 2015); however, FDiv was unrelated to species turnover (Figures 3b and 4b; Appendix S1: Tables S8 and S9), indicating community invasibility was tied to CWM‐functional trait values and not overall functional diversity. Contrary to the predictions of size‐asymmetric competition (Grime, 1977; Schwinning & Weiner, 1998), we found no relationship between height and species losses (Figure 4c). Further, we found taller species were more likely to be lost over time (Appendix S1: Figure S11c, Table S9), so it is likely the competitive exclusion of smaller plants does not cause richness declines in this system (see also Aarssen et al., 2006; Brown & Cahill, 2019). Overall, we find that structural traits' function in species turnover is less influential than net resource availability or the neighbors' presence in this system (sensu Davis et al., 1998; Table 1). We suggest caution in asserting functional roles of traits in the context of community assembly and biotic interactions without direct tests of species gains and losses within a specific system.

Plant social metrics functioned in species turnover, as more gregarious communities were less likely to gain species (Figure 2a; Appendix S1: Table S7). We found no effect of reclusiveness on species gains, counter to expectation if frequent negative co‐occurrences indicate strong competitors (Grime, 1973). Our results echo the hypothesis that more connected communities are more resistant to invasion (Smith‐Ramesh et al., 2017), and more species associations infer greater stability. These results suggest evidence of relatively closed “modules” of species which exert biotic resistance, with other communities more open to colonization. Alternatively, higher gregariousness and more species associations could reflect greater niche occupancy (Case, 1981), which would also function to reduce colonization.

Co‐occurrence data by themselves can be generated by numerous mechanisms, and accordingly, the causes of the patterns can rarely be linked explicitly to ecological processes (Blanchet et al., 2020). However, we show that the patterns themselves represent a functional trait with impacts on species colonization (Table 1). Thus, we posit that integrating plant interactions into existing theories of community assembly, including the size‐asymmetric light competition hypothesis or functional trait “strategies,” presents an opportunity to enhance our understanding of how species assemblages change. To illustrate, lower light compensation points could be associated with gregariousness in layered canopies, or reclusiveness could be associated with dense, clonal growth. We encourage others to test the role of social traits in coexistence, as the relevant data are likely broadly available in most plant community field studies.

Characteristics of species lost and gained

We found a species' initial abundance to be the greatest predictor of whether it was lost from plots (Appendix S1: Table S9), and common species formed a stable “core” of communities. Common species, by definition, are found in most communities (sensu Gaston & Fuller, 2008), which inherently reduces their likelihood of being gained or lost. Rare species are more vulnerable to extinction due to their smaller population sizes (Gaston, 1994) and were more likely to be gained or lost by plots over time (Appendix S1: Figure S11a,b). Larger initial abundances likely buffered species' responses to external pressures or disturbances (Arnoldi et al., 2019).

Taller plants were more likely to be lost from plots (Appendix S1: Figure S11c), opposing predicted outcomes of size‐asymmetric competition (i.e., shorter understory plants should be lost more frequently). However, this finding is consistent with other findings that short plants are common in natural systems (Aarssen et al., 2006). Our results support others from this system who find that taller plants are not more competitive (Brown & Cahill, 2022), as size‐asymmetric competition predicts smaller plants should lose in light‐limited communities (DeMalach et al., 2016). Together, these results suggest the role of height in turnover is more nuanced than simply competitive suppression.

Overall, we found resources predicted species loss, whereas structural and social traits predicted species gain. Though species losses increased with fertilization, reduced light alone did not increase species loss, suggesting a decoupling of the standard model in this low‐nutrient grassland. Excitingly, we find value in using plant social and structural traits to characterize species turnover and urge others to consider the role of plants' social environment in community stability.
