Concentration-dependent effects of bacterial melanin on new superoxide-producing associates in rat tissues: a rotenone neurotoxic model of parkinson’s disease
Authors: Margarita Danielyan, Kristine Karapetyan, Ruzan Simonyan, Gegham Simonyan, Madlena Babayan, Mikhail Poghosyan, Hasmik Yekmalyan, Maxim Simonyan, Karen Simonyan, Kristina Nebogova
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOxidative stress plays a central role in the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons and is a key factor in the pathogenesis of Parkinson’s disease (PD). Therefore, antioxidant therapy represents a promising therapeutic strategy. This process involves numerous enzymes and signaling pathways that contribute to disease progression. In the present study, bacterial melanin (BM), known for its high biological activity and biostimulatory effects, was applied as a potential antioxidant. We examined quantitative and qualitative alterations in total fractions of biomembrane-associated components derived from rat tissue in a rotenone-induced PD model, under both low and high BM concentrations, along with an evaluation of their antioxidant properties.MethodsPD was induced via intracerebral rotenone injection, and the animals were maintained for 4 weeks. BM was administered twice during the survival period at concentrations of 4.5 mg/ml and 9 mg/ml. To confirm disease progression and evaluate potential therapeutic effects of BM, the Rotarod behavioral test was performed following a 4-week survival period. From the biomembranes of brain, lung, and small intestine tissues, superoxide-generating associates were isolated. Their specific amounts (mg/g) and steady-state concentrations of superoxide (O₂⁻) (M) were determined to assess oxidative stress. Antioxidant activity was quantified using the Coomassie Brilliant Blue (CBB) dye decoloration method.ResultsThe results demonstrated that BM exerted a membrane-stabilizing effect, with the 9 mg/ml dose proving more effective than 4.5 mg/ml. BM modulated membrane formation across the brain, lung, and small intestine by inhibiting the release of newly formed membrane-bound structures (NLP-Nox associates) and regulating the steady-state concentration of O₂⁻.Behavioral tests further confirmed the effectiveness of BM, with animal behavior parameters in the high-concentration group closely resembling those of the control group. It was found that different BM concentrations possess different antioxidant activity: specific activity at 4.5 mg/ml concentration amounted to 1.2 U/mg, while at 9 mg/ml it was 2.9 U/mg.ConclusionsThe results demonstrate BM’s potential as both an antioxidant and membrane-stabilizing agent for PD treatment. The study revealed a concentration-dependent effect of BM’s antioxidant activity in the PD model, with 9 mg/mL showing superior efficacy. Further research and clinical trials are warranted to validate its therapeutic efficacy.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40360-025-00989-x.Keywords: Parkinson’s disease, Bacterial melanin, Superoxide, Associate, Antioxidant activity, Coomassie brilliant blue

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a multifactorial neurodegenerative disorder in the pathogenesis of which oxidative stress plays a significant role [1]. The exact causes of PD remain unclear; however, current evidence suggests that it results from a combination of genetic predisposition and environmental factors [2, 3]. PD is characterized by impaired antioxidant defense, leading to an imbalance between pro-oxidant and antioxidant mechanisms. As highlighted in the review, oxidative stress is a central factor contributing to PD pathogenesis, involving various enzymes and signaling molecules that disrupt neuronal function [4]. According to current research, oxidative stress drives disease progression, and the potential therapeutic benefits of antioxidant supplementation are under investigation. Oxidative stress is particularly critical in dopaminergic neurotoxicity and is considered a major contributor to the progressive degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in PD [4, 5]. Disruption of redox homeostasis in neurons affects multiple cellular processes, ultimately leading to cell death. Evidence of oxidative and nitrative damage has been found in key cellular components of the substantia nigra (SN) in PD patients [6]. Multiple mechanisms contribute to the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in PD, including dopamine metabolism, mitochondrial dysfunction, iron accumulation, neuroinflammation, calcium dysregulation, and aging [7–12]. A deficiency in mitochondrial complex I activity is implicated in the extensive neuronal degeneration characteristic of PD [13]. Functional studies involving mutations in PD-associated genes, such as SNCA, PRKN, PINK1, DJ-1, LRRK2, FBXO7, and ATP13A2, further underscore the role of oxidative stress in disease pathogenesis [14]. In this context, molecules associated with oxidative stress, including DJ-1, coenzyme Q10, uric acid, 8-hydroxy-2’-deoxyguanosine, homocysteine, retinoic acid/carotenes, vitamin E, glutathione peroxidase, superoxide dismutase, xanthine oxidase, and lipid peroxidation products, are being investigated as potential biomarkers for PD. Beyond the classical motor symptoms linked to dopaminergic neuron degeneration, PD is also characterized by a wide range of non-motor symptoms that significantly impair patients’ quality of life and often emerge years before motor manifestations. Growing evidence suggests that the gastrointestinal tract and its neural connections with the central nervous system, particularly via the vagus nerve, may play a key role in PD pathogenesis [15]. The significance of gastrointestinal dysfunction in PD is increasingly recognized [16]. Various gastrointestinal disorders, including infections, dysbiosis, inflammation, motility impairment, dietary factors, and gut-targeted interventions, have been linked to PD onset and progression and may even influence the efficacy of PD pharmacotherapy [17]. Respiratory disturbances are observed in at least one-third of patients in advanced PD stages, leading to reduced quality of life and an increased risk of aspiration-related complications [18]. Degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc) results in increased ventilation both at rest and under specific conditions, while also impairing the body’s response to hypercapnia [19]. Several studies have demonstrated an association between PD and both restrictive and obstructive ventilatory impairments, often accompanied by dyspnea [20, 21]. Based on these findings, our study examined the brain, gut, and lungs of rotenone-intoxicated rats as an experimental model of PD. The concept of oxidative stress as a central mechanism of neuronal damage in PD justifies the exploration of antioxidant-based interventions [22]. Various antioxidants have been investigated as potential therapeutic agents for modulating oxidative stress in PD. Several antioxidant compounds, including creatine, vitamin E, melatonin, and others, have undergone clinical trials, but none have yet demonstrated conclusive efficacy in slowing or reversing neurodegeneration in PD patients [23]. Nevertheless, reducing oxidative stress remains a promising therapeutic strategy. Key antioxidant enzymes, such as NAD(P)H: quinone oxidoreductase (NQO1), regulated by the transcription factor Nrf2, represent attractive molecular targets for disease-modifying therapies [24].

In this study, we employed bacterial melanin (BM) as an antioxidant therapeutic agent, leveraging its antioxidative properties [25, 26]. The high content of paramagnetic centers in melanins enables radical deactivation due to their substantial electron-absorbing capacity. Melanin neutralizes lipid peroxidation products and participates in neurotransmitter processes under various pathological disruptions of neuronal functional structures [26, 27]. It is known for its significant biological activity and biostimulatory effects [28]. At optimal concentrations (4.5–9 mg/mL), BM does not induce microgliosis or exhibit toxic side effects [29, 30]. BM crosses the blood-brain barrier and may enhance the electrical activity of cortical neurons while synchronously modulating neuronal activity in the SN, potentially contributing to its neuroprotective effects [31, 32]. BM administration increases neuronal excitability and facilitates post-stimulation potentiation, indicating that its neuromodulatory effects extend beyond the SNpc to other brain regions involved in motor control and learning [33]. BM is also known for its anti-inflammatory and protective properties, promoting neuronal survival in the SN under toxic conditions and suppressing inflammatory responses while stimulating regenerative processes in neural tissue [34–36]. Animal studies have shown that BM supports neuroprotection by restoring motor function, enhancing vascularization, promoting glial cell proliferation, and inhibiting inflammation and scar formation [26, 37, 38].

Collectively, these findings suggest that BM may exert both protective and reparative effects on brain structures affected by PD, particularly in early disease stages. This scientific rationale supports the selection of BM as a promising candidate for therapeutic application in PD.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the effects of low (4.5 mg/mL) and high (9 mg/mL) concentrations of BM on stabilizing tissue biomembranes in specific organs (brain, small intestine, and lungs) in a rat model of PD to determine its optimal therapeutic dose. Additionally, the study assessed the antioxidant activity of BM.

Materials and methods

The rotenone-induced PD model offers several advantages, particularly its ability to replicate multiple mechanisms involved in PD pathogenesis. These include oxidative stress, formation of neuronal inclusions, and motor impairments resembling those observed in idiopathic PD [39, 40]. These characteristics make it particularly valuable for evaluating neuroprotective agents and compounds with antioxidant properties. In line with this, the present study was conducted using a rotenone-induced PD model in rats, which is widely regarded as one of the most appropriate models, particularly for studies with a survival period of up to 4 weeks [41].

Study design

The experiments were carried out on 24 mature male albino rats (Sprague-Dawley), aged 12–14 months and weighing 220–250 g, sourced from the Experimental Center of the Orbeli Institute of Physiology, National Academy of Sciences of the Republic of Armenia.

The rats were divided into 4 experimental groups, with six animals in each group:

Sham-operated group (Control, C): Received a unilateral intracerebral injection of sterile distilled water and an intraperitoneal injection of isotonic NaCl solution.

Sham-operated group (Control, C): Received a unilateral intracerebral injection of sterile distilled water and an intraperitoneal injection of isotonic NaCl solution.

PD group: Received a unilateral intracerebral injection of rotenone and two intraperitoneal injections of saline over four weeks.

PD group: Received a unilateral intracerebral injection of rotenone and two intraperitoneal injections of saline over four weeks.

PD + low-dose BM (PD + BM 4.5 mg/ml): Received a unilateral intracerebral injection of rotenone and two intraperitoneal injections of BM at a concentration of 4.5 mg/mL over four weeks.

PD + low-dose BM (PD + BM 4.5 mg/ml): Received a unilateral intracerebral injection of rotenone and two intraperitoneal injections of BM at a concentration of 4.5 mg/mL over four weeks.

PD + high-dose BM (PD + BM 9 mg/ml): Received a unilateral intracerebral injection of rotenone and two intraperitoneal injections of BM at a concentration of 9 mg/mL over four weeks.

PD + high-dose BM (PD + BM 9 mg/ml): Received a unilateral intracerebral injection of rotenone and two intraperitoneal injections of BM at a concentration of 9 mg/mL over four weeks.

The minimum sample size of n = 6 per group was selected to comply with the 3Rs principle while maintaining statistical reliability.

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the decision of the Council of European Communities (22 September 2010) [2010/63/EU] and the “ARRIVE” guidelines (Animals in Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments). The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the L. A. Orbeli Institute of Physiology (protocol code: N4, approval date: July 22, 2021).Animals were housed under standard laboratory conditions, including a 12-hour light/dark cycle, a temperature range of 22–24 °C, and relative humidity of 40–60%. They had ad libitum access to a standard pelleted chow (Nutrimix STD-1020) and filtered tap water. The drinking water was routinely tested for contaminants and met the acceptable laboratory standards.

Under urethane anesthesia (1.5 g/kg, intraperitoneally), sterile distilled water and rotenone were intracerebrally administered using a calibrated stereotaxic apparatus. A trepanation was performed from bregma to lambda, followed by dural incision. The rotenone solution (12 µg in 0.5 µl DMSO) was infused into the medial forebrain bundle (AP -2.04 mm; ML ± 1.8 mm; DV + 8.0 mm) over 30 s at a rate of 1 µl/min, according to the stereotaxic atlas [42]. Sterile distilled water was injected in a similar manner (same volume, region, and coordinates). Post-surgery, wounds were treated with Bicillin-5, and wound edges were sutured with catgut and treated with Betadine (10% povidone-iodine solution for topical use, EGIS, Hungary). BM was provided by the Scientific and Production Center “Armbiotechnology” NAS RA. BM injections were administered at two time points: the day following surgery for intracerebral rotenone administration and on the 14th day after surgery. For the two experimental groups, BM was administered intraperitoneally at concentrations of 4.5 mg/ml and 9 mg/ml, corresponding to 10-fold and 20-fold dilutions, respectively, of the original concentration obtained during BM biotechnological production (94 mg/ml) [43]. The BM concentrations were selected based on established melanin concentration ranges known to exhibit biological activity [44]. The volumes of the BM solutions were determined based on the optimal tolerable dose of 0.17 g/kg [29].

Behavioral study

To validate the PD experimental model and assess the pharmacological effects of BM, the Rotarod test was utilized to evaluate motor coordination and balance in rodents [45–48]. In the test, animals are placed on a rotating drum with a textured surface to prevent slipping. The timer starts when the animal begins, and stops when the animal falls, registering the duration the animal remained on the rod. Prior to testing, animals were trained to stay on the rod at 5 rpm for 1 min. All animals must successfully complete this training phase before proceeding to testing. After 30 min of training, a test was conducted, measuring the holding time (speed: 10 rpm).The testing phase consisted of 3 trials, each lasting no more than 300 s, with 10-minute rest periods between trials. Prior to unilateral injections of either sterile distilled water or rotenone, all experimental rats were assessed using the Rotarod test. Following the injections, the animals were monitored for 4 weeks. Following the survival period, animals from the experimental groups (PD, PD + BM 4.5 mg/mL, and PD + BM 9 mg/mL) were subjected to the Rotarod behavioral test.

Biochemical methods

Processing of organ tissues

Experimental animals were euthanized using an intraperitoneal injection of Pentobarbital (400 mg/ml solution) at a dose of 1 ml/kg. Following euthanasia, organs (brain, lungs, and small intestine) were collected post-decapitation. The tissues were rinsed with water to remove contaminants, dried, and weighed. Lung and intestinal samples were mechanically processed by crushing and homogenizing in water (10 g of tissue per 100 ml) using an electric homogenizer set at 1000 rpm for one minute. Brain tissue was manually homogenized using a glass homogenizer to preserve its delicate structure. The resulting homogenates underwent a series of biochemical treatments, including alkaline hydrolysis, incubation at 37 °C, centrifugation, ion exchange chromatography, deionization, heat treatment, a second round of centrifugation, and lyophilization to prepare the samples for further analysis.

Isolation and purification of the isoforms of the total fraction of O2–-producing associates from cell membranes and intracellular formations of the brain, lung, and small intestine of rats

The characterization of isoforms responsible for O₂⁻ production was conducted using Simonyan’s method [49]. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was subjected to ion-exchange chromatography utilizing DEAE cellulose (DE-52) at pH 9.5. Subsequently, gel filtration was performed on a separate Sephadex G-200 column under the same pH conditions. Protein separation was achieved through electrophoresis on polyacrylamide gels, with concentrations of 7% for acidic proteins and 10% for basic proteins (PAGE).

Spectral analysis of NLP-Nox associates

Spectral indices for NLP-Nox associates were analyzed using the Cary 60 spectrophotometer, chosen for its precision in detecting absorbance variations across diverse wavelengths. This method involved identifying characteristic absorption peaks unique to these associates, enabling accurate profiling of their spectral properties.

Determination of NADPH in NLP-Nox isoforms

The quantification of NADPH within the total fraction of NLP-Nox isoforms was conducted using a Cary Eclipse spectrofluorimeter. Fluorescence intensity (F), expressed in relative units, was measured at an emission wavelength of 430 nm following excitation at 340 nm [50].

Determination of the stationary concentration of superoxide radicals presented above

The stationary concentration of superoxide radicals produced by NLP-Nox associates was measured using the adrenaline oxidation method [51]. In this approach, adrenaline reacts with superoxide radicals to form adrenochrome, which has a characteristic optical absorption peak at 500 nm. The concentration of adrenochrome is directly proportional to the amount of superoxide radicals, with one mole of superoxide radical generating one mole of adrenochrome. The molar extinction coefficient of adrenochrome was taken as 750 M⁻¹cm⁻¹. To calculate the stationary concentration (A500/E) of О₂⁻, the optical absorbance at 500 nm was measured. A control experiment was conducted to account for adrenochrome formation due to atmospheric oxygen. Additionally, the specific content of NLP-Nox (mg/g tissue) was determined by weighing the samples after deionization and lyophilization.

Assessment of the antioxidant activity of BM

The antioxidant activity of BM was evaluated using a method adapted from Simonyan et al. [52]. This approach involved measuring the amount of BM needed to achieve a 50% reduction in the discoloration of Coomassie Brilliant Blue (CBB) caused by superoxide radicals (О₂⁻).

CBB, a dye with a characteristic optical absorption peak at 580 nm, exhibits a decrease in this peak after exposure to superoxide radicals. Antioxidant drugs inhibit this discoloration. By analyzing the change in optical absorption intensity in experimental groups, the activity of the antioxidant drug (SOD-mimetic activity) can be determined. O2* is a «hot oxygen» molecule formed by the exothermic splitting of hydrogen peroxide. It is easily reduced by an electron from a hydroxyl group to form superoxide and hydroxyl radicals at pH 7.4. Classic hydroxyl radical scavengers, such as mannitol, alcohol, and sugar, significantly increase the stationary concentration of superoxide.The antioxidant activity of compounds was calculated based on optical spectral indices (units/mg).

Four experimental setups were established:

Control: CBB dye only.

Control: CBB dye only.

Experimental Group 1: CBB+О₂⁻.

Experimental Group 1: CBB+О₂⁻.

Experimental Group 2: CBB+О₂⁻+BM at a concentration of 4.5 mg/ml.

Experimental Group 2: CBB+О₂⁻+BM at a concentration of 4.5 mg/ml.

Experimental Group 3: CBB+О₂⁻+BM at a concentration of 9 mg/ml.

Experimental Group 3: CBB+О₂⁻+BM at a concentration of 9 mg/ml.

In all experimental groups, 96% ethyl alcohol was added to neutralize hydroxyl radicals.

Determination of lipid component in the composition of NLP-Nox

The lipid component of NLP-Nox associates was assessed by quantifying malondialdehyde (MDA), a key product of lipid peroxidation [53].

Reagents and equipment

In this study, the following reagents and equipment were utilized: cellulose DE-52 (Whatman, England), Sephadex G-200 (Pharmacia, Sweden), adrenaline (L-Epinephrine) (#51-43-4, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), cytochrome C (electrophoretically pure protein from bovine heart), Coomassie Brilliant Blue (CBB) (#6104-58-1, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), pentobarbital (Bioveta, PENBITAL Evta), urethane (#U2500, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and rotenone (#557368, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The equipment used included a homogenizer MPW-302 (Poland), a spectrophotometer Cary 60 (USA), a spectrofluorimeter Cary Eclipse (USA), centrifuges (K-70D and K-24 Janetzki (Germany)), an electric homogenizer type MPW-302 (Poland), a manual glass homogenizer, and a lyophilizer LGA 05 (Janetzki).

BM was produced through a biotechnological process utilizing an industrial strain of Bacillus thuringiensis treated with nitrosoguanidine. The resulting BM was characterized as a high-molecular-weight, amorphous, water-soluble polymer with dark brown coloration, composed of quinone, phenolic, and amino acid components. The biosafety of BM was experimentally confirmed through standardized toxicological testing. A complete production technology was developed, including optimized isolation and purification protocols from the culture medium [54].

Statistical analysis

The biochemical data were analyzed using Student-Fisher variance analysis. For behavioral test results, the Bonferroni post hoc test was additionally applied. For pairwise comparisons of group means, t-tests were employed. To control the overall error rate, the significance level for each individual test was divided by the total number of comparisons. Biochemical data were analyzed using p-values and are presented as mean ± SEM (standard error of the mean), while behavioral test results are expressed as mean ± SD (standard deviation). Each experimental group contained 6 animals (n = 6). All statistical analyses were conducted using the online GraphPad tool (version 7, GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Biochemical studies

Isoforms of O₂⁻-producing associates isolated from brain, lung, and small intestine tissues of rats in C, PD, PD + BM 4.5 mg/ml, and PD + BM 9 mg/ml groups were not resolved by PAGE electrophoresis, as they remained at the entry of the gel tubes. This lack of bands corresponding to acidic and basic water-soluble proteins suggests the purity of these associates. Spectroscopic analysis revealed characteristic optical absorption peaks at 280 nm, 412 nm, 530 nm, and 560 nm in the oxidized state. Additionally, absorption peaks at 340 nm and 370 nm were observed, which are indicative of NADPH and FAD, respectively (Fig. 1).

In their oxidized state, the O₂⁻-producing associates exhibit distinct optical absorption peaks at 280 nm, 412 nm, 530 nm, and 560 nm. Furthermore, peaks at 340 nm and 370 nm are observed, which are characteristic of NADPH and FAD, respectively (Fig. 1). The isoforms of these associates from the studied groups were not resolved by PAGE electrophoresis, as they remained at the gel entry points. This absence of bands corresponding to acidic and basic water-soluble proteins suggests that the associates are highly purified.

The specific amounts of total fractions of NLP-Nox associates from brain, lung, and small intestine tissues in the C, PD, and PD + BM (4.5 mg/ml and 9 mg/ml) groups were determined through deionization and vacuum lyophilization, with results recalculated to milligrams per gram of tissue (mg/g). Lyophilized isoforms were stored at -10 °C under aerobic conditions. In the control group of brain tissue, the amount of associate was 80.2 mg/g (Table 1). In the PD group, it increased by 9.8%. After treatment with BM at 4.5 mg/ml, the amount decreased and approached the control level (86.1 mg/g), showing a 2.3% reduction compared to the PD group. With BM at 9.0 mg/ml, the amount decreased further, nearing the control level (82.2 mg/g), a 6.7% decrease compared to the PD group. These results suggest that bacterial melanin has a membrane-stabilizing effect, with the 9 mg/ml concentration being more effective (Table 1).

Behavioral test results: Rotarod test

Behavioral assessments revealed significant differences among the experimental groups. The control group exhibited normal motor function, with a mean score of 53 ± 1.73. In contrast, the PD group showed a significant decrease in motor function, with a mean score of 28 ± 3.00 (p < 0.0002 compared to the control group). The PD + BM 4.5 mg/ml group had a mean score of 40 ± 4.36, which was significantly higher than the PD group (F(1, 25) = 14.29, p < 0.0171, Bonferroni post hoc test). Similarly, the PD + BM 9 mg/ml group exhibited a mean score of 49 ± 3.21 (Fig. 4), also significantly higher than the PD group (F(1, 25) = 22.12, p < 0.0012, Bonferroni post hoc test). Thus, animals treated with BM showed statistically significant improvements in retention time on the rotating rod compared to the PD group, although their retention time remained lower than that of the control animals (p < 0.001). Treatment with BM resulted in concentration-dependent improvements in motor function. Specifically, the retention time of the PD + BM 9 mg/ml group on the rotating rod was significantly greater than that of the PD + BM 4.5 mg/ml group (p < 0.001).

These results indicate a clear improvement in motor function with BM treatment, supporting its efficacy in alleviating motor deficits associated with PD in the rat model.

Discussion

We selected rotenone as an experimental model of PD due to its well-established ability to reproduce key features of the disorder. Rotenone, a natural pesticide, is a potent inhibitor of mitochondrial complex I [55, 56]. As a lipophilic compound, it readily crosses the blood-brain barrier, and chronic low-dose exposure leads to complex I inhibition throughout the rat brain [57]. Despite its systemic effects, rotenone specifically induces oxidative damage in the striatum and selective degeneration of the nigrostriatal dopaminergic pathway, which is associated with motor impairments characteristic of PD [58–60]. One factor contributing to neuronal death may be increased membrane permeability to ions due to lipid peroxidation. The SN is particularly vulnerable to oxidative damage because dopamine metabolism and auto-oxidation generate hydrogen peroxide. Moreover, SN neurons have higher iron levels, which catalyze oxidative reactions, and reduced levels of glutathione, a key antioxidant, relative to other brain regions [61, 62]. The involvement of oxidative stress in neuronal degeneration in PD suggests a therapeutic target within oxidative pathways.Antioxidant therapy plays an important role in the treatment of PD [23]. It is known that melanins serve crucial functions in both neutralizing lipid peroxidation products and influencing neurotransmitter processes in neurons. They act as antioxidants, protecting cells from oxidative stress-induced damage, while also participating in the regulation of neurotransmitter activity, particularly in catecholaminergic neurons. This dual functionality is particularly relevant for various neurological disorders where these processes are impaired [63, 64].

In this study, we utilized BM as an antioxidant therapeutic agent (Fig. 5), based on its well-documented antioxidant and neuroprotective properties [65, 66]. BM appears to modulate neurotransmitter release and synaptic activity, potentially enhancing neuronal communication [67]. Current evidence suggests BM can regulate the critical balance between excitatory and inhibitory neuronal processes essential for normal brain function [33]. In our previous research, we isolated superoxide-producing associates from tissue biomembranes of various organs and measured their specific quantities (in mg/g) and steady-state superoxide concentrations (M), proposing a unique method for assessing oxidative stress. It was found that BM at a concentration of 6 mg/ml exhibits significant membrane-stabilizing effects in a PD model [68]. The current study attempted to evaluate the effects of low and high (compared to the concentration of 6 mg/ml) concentrations of BM on the stabilization of tissue biomembranes in specific organs of rats in a PD model to determine its membrane-stabilizing properties and its most optimal therapeutic concentration, as well as to assess the antioxidant activity of different BM concentrations. The BM concentrations were selected taking into account the values of melanin concentrations exhibiting biological activity [44].

Mechanistic insights into the antioxidant and neuroprotective effects of BM in a rotenone-induced PD model

The characteristic features of the NLP-Nox associate include the content of lipoprotein and NADPH-oxidase (Nox). NLP-Nox associates are enzymatic systems in which NADPH is not free but bound to lipoprotein as part of a complex. These associates also contain FAD. NADPH absorbs light at a wavelength of 340 ± 30 nm and emits fluorescence at a wavelength of 460 ± 50 nm [50]. Due to the presence of NADPH, these associates continuously produce superoxide radicals through the enzymatic pathway. In vitro, the NLP-Nox associate generates O₂⁻ by transferring an electron from NADPH through the Fe(III) bridge (part of Nox) to oxygen, forming a superoxide radical. The amount of O₂⁻-producing associate released from membranes is measured in mg/g. In particular, isoforms of associates from brain, lung, and small intestine biomembranes, each at a concentration of 2 mg/ml, produce superoxide for 10–12 days at room temperature under aerobic conditions. The presence of superoxide is confirmed by the reduction of cytochrome C. Cytochrome C solution is added to the associate solution, and cytochrome C reduction spectra are recorded for 10–12 days. Unlike the classical sodium dithionite reducing agent, which maintains cytochrome C in the reduced form for only 3–4 min, these associates maintain this reduction for 10–12 days. The obtained data indicate that PD leads to a decrease in the stability of biomembranes, as reflected in the obtained indicators. Associates were released from all brain, lung, and intestinal tissue biomembranes in all groups, although to varying degrees. Decreased membrane stability, exacerbated by oxidative stress, is a significant problem in PD. In the control group, oxidative stress also contributes to membrane damage, which in this case is caused by high pH 9.5 (a condition used in the method) [49]. At this high pH, lipid peroxidation of biomembranes occurs [51]. The study confirmed that in PD, biomembranes are damaged not only in rat brain tissue, but also in the lungs and small intestine. The degree of specific concentration of the associate (mg/g) reflects the degree of membrane damage, while the stationary concentration of superoxide radicals (M) produced by this associate indicates its activity. Although the greatest release of superoxides was observed from brain biomembranes as a whole, the lowest stationary concentration of superoxides was also recorded there. This difference is explained by the fact that brain tissue contains the least amount of NADPH compared to the small intestine and lungs, with a concentration of 2 mg/ml in all three cases. NADPH serves as a source of electrons for the formation of superoxide radicals, and its lower concentration in the brain leads to a lower stationary concentration of superoxides [69]. According to the study results, treatment with BM at a concentration of 4.5 mg/ml increased the stationary concentration of superoxides in biomembranes of different rat tissues compared to the PD group, but did not bring the data close enough to the control, whereas treatment with BM at a concentration of 9 mg/ml increased it even more in biomembranes of all studied tissues, compared with the PD group, significantly bringing the indicators closer to the control group data. The obtained data indicate a concentration-dependent membrane-stabilizing effect of BM. At all concentrations, BM has a regulatory effect on membrane formations in the brain, lungs and small intestine, suppressing the release of new membrane-bound thermostable formations (NLP-Nox associates) from these membranes, as well as regulating the stationary concentration of the resulting O₂⁻. Moreover, BM appears to inhibit lipid peroxidation of the NLP-Nox associate, a component of biomembranes, preventing the cleavage of hydrophobic bonds responsible for the enzyme’s connection with the biomembrane. This conclusion is supported by the observed antioxidant activity of BM, with higher efficacy at a concentration of 9 mg/ml (2.9 units/mg) compared to 4.5 mg/ml (1.2 units/mg).

The Rotarod test was used by us to confirm the development of an experimental rotenone model of PD and the expected correction by BM of different concentrations of motor impairments in this PD model. This test is effective for screening the effects of drugs on motor coordination and for studying brain injuries or PD-like diseases in animal models [70–72]. Since a significant limitation of the rotenone model of PD is the variability of behavioral symptoms in animals (only some animals show obvious motor impairments), in our study animals that did not develop behavioral impairments were excluded from further analysis. The results of the Rotarod behavioral test indicate motor impairments in the rotenone-induced PD model and show significant preservation of motor activity in animals receiving BM, especially at a concentration of 9 mg/ml. Although deterioration in strength and endurance was observed compared to control animals, the obtained data confirm the development of PD in the experiment and demonstrate the potential of BM for correcting motor impairments in rats. Thus, BM has proven effective in alleviating motor impairments caused by rotenone injection.

Although the present study provides valuable information about the antioxidant effects of BM in a rotenone-induced PD model in rats, several important limitations must be acknowledged. The lack of histological verification, such as immunohistochemical analysis of dopaminergic neuron survival (e.g., tyrosine hydroxylase staining) and glial activation, precludes a more complete understanding of the underlying changes at the cellular and tissue levels. Addressing these limitations in future studies will be crucial to enhancing the translational significance of BM not only as an antioxidant but also as a neuroprotective agent in PD models. Despite promising results, additional research is needed to fully understand the mechanisms of BM action and its potential clinical applications. Essentially, BM is considered as a potential natural compound with significant antioxidant and neuroprotective properties, requiring further study of its therapeutic potential.

Conclusions

Thus, bacterial melanin is capable of neutralizing free radicals, reducing oxidative damage to neurons. Through decreasing oxidative stress, it stabilizes mitochondrial membrane potential and inhibits cytochrome c release, thereby preventing mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis. Our findings reveal concentration-dependent membrane-stabilizing properties of BM, including inhibition of lipid peroxidation in biomembranes. The 9 mg/mL BM treatment showed significantly greater antioxidant activity compared to the lower concentration. Notably, BM treatment improved behavioral parameters and reduced motor deficits in our rotenone-induced PD model. These positive outcomes highlight BM’s neuroprotective potential against toxin-induced neurodegeneration. Given that oxidative stress represents a fundamental pathological mechanism in neurodegeneration, BM shows promise for developing novel therapeutic approaches for PD. Its unique properties suggest potential applications both as a complementary treatment to existing therapies and as an alternative neuroprotective strategy.
