National Cancer Institute Cancer Treatment Tolerability Consortium: Current status and future directions
Authors: Rachel D Altshuler, Sandra A Mitchell, Diane C St Germain, Brennan Parmelee Streck, Alice P Chen, Lori M Minasian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Short abstractAs cancer treatments evolve, there is a growing need to assess, analyze, and integrate measurements of cancer treatment tolerability. This report describes the rationale, development, and outcomes of the National Cancer Institute Cancer Treatment Tolerability Consortium, and discusses future opportunities for cancer treatment tolerability research.Keywords: cancer treatment, Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), patient‐reported outcomes, Patient‐Reported Outcomes version of the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (PRO‐CTCAE), tolerability, toxicity

BACKGROUND

Understanding cancer treatment tolerability is important in gauging the risks and benefits of a treatment regimen, optimizing the dosing and scheduling of that regimen, and providing information for clinicians to communicate to patients the possible adverse events (AEs) and ways in which those AEs can be mitigated and managed. Several different definitions of treatment tolerability have been proposed. 1 , 2 All these definitions incorporate the degree to which the adverse effects of a treatment regimen will be acceptable to the patient.

In the past, tolerability was characterized as the absence of acute high‐grade AEs that indicated a safety concern. When a regimen has a generally acceptable safety profile, reports of clinical trials have often concluded with comments such as “generally well‐tolerated.” 3 , 4 Safety and tolerability are overlapping but distinct constructs. 4 Safety is the science of detecting, understanding, monitoring, and preventing AEs. Although safety data can inform our understanding of treatment tolerability, it is only one aspect among multiple factors that influence the acceptability of a treatment by a patient (Figure 1).

A treatment regimen can have a good safety profile but not be acceptable to some patients. For example, adjuvant therapy with aromatase inhibitors has a good safety profile but some patients with breast cancer are unable to tolerate the chronic arthralgias, and electively discontinue therapy. 5 , 6 , 7 Currently, there is no standard approach to assessing cancer treatment tolerability as a concept separate from safety, nor an optimal way to integrate these different tolerability measurement approaches, which have had limited study. More work is also needed to identify the best methods to integrate tolerability data with clinical data in the analysis of clinical trial outcomes. To address these knowledge gaps, the National Cancer Institute (NCI) established a consortium to support focused study of this topic area.

In this article, we describe the rationale for the development of the NCI Cancer Treatment Tolerability Consortium, and provide highlights from the funded work. 8 , 9 The articles offered in this special issue provide important insights toward an improved understanding of cancer treatment tolerability.

DEVELOPMENT OF THE NCI CANCER TOLERABILITY CONSORTIUM

The standard lexicon for reporting AEs in cancer clinical trials is the NCI Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE). 10 CTCAE incorporates a severity grading rubric that is used to capture a safety signal requiring medical intervention. These AEs are reported in clinical trial results as the sample proportions experiencing high‐grade, or serious, AEs. This approach provides a rapid means to evaluate a safety profile. A regimen from a trial may have an acceptable safety profile when relatively few high‐grade AEs are seen. However, such AE tables often do not provide sufficient information about low‐grade AEs, which if cumulative or chronic, may affect a patient’s willingness to continue treatment. As a companion to CTCAE and derived from it, the NCI led the development of the Patient‐Reported Outcomes version of the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (PRO‐CTCAE). 11 This measurement system provides a means for patients to provide complementary reporting of their symptomatic AEs. The PRO‐CTCAE item library includes self‐report items evaluating 78 symptomatic toxicities, including their presence, frequency, severity, and/or interference with usual daily activities. The item library includes a range of common and less common symptomatic AEs, including pain, nausea, fatigue, visual disturbances (photopsia and watery eyes), and hot flashes. 11 Together, these two measurement systems provide a comprehensive set of data elements needed to fully understand the AE profile of a given regimen. 12

The AE profile for cytotoxic agents, which typically have a narrow therapeutic window, is dose dependent, and as such, determination of dose‐limiting toxicities and the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) are key components of the study design, particularly in early‐phase trials. However, in the contemporary treatment context that includes targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and, increasingly, combinations of cytotoxic and noncytotoxic agents, the emphasis when evaluating treatment tolerability has shifted toward considerations of dose and schedule optimization to achieve and maintain treatment tolerability over time. Unlike classic cytotoxic agents, noncytotoxic agents may have a wide therapeutic index. 13 , 14 Additionally, the AEs may not appear in the first cycle of therapy but develop over time. With the increased use of targeted therapy and immunotherapy, as well as combinations of cytotoxic and noncytotoxic agents, the MTD may not be the optimal dose‐finding approach, especially when defined in the first cycle of treatment. 15 , 16 , 17 Thus, a comprehensive approach to evaluating tolerability should include safety, patient perception, treatment data, and additional factors (Figure 1). These approaches can strengthen shared decision‐making, and improve the provision of anticipatory guidance that enhances self‐management. 18 , 19

To address the growing need to better characterize tolerability, the NCI released a funding opportunity announcement that called for proposals to develop alternative methods and approaches to understanding treatment tolerability. 8 Investigators needed access to clinical trials that incorporated both CTCAE and PRO‐CTCAE with data from these trials available for secondary analysis. The overall aim of the funding opportunity announcement was to develop analytic methods to better understand treatment tolerability and methods that served to integrate clinician and patient reporting of tolerability with other clinically relevant data. An additional purpose was to create a consortium of the funded research teams to share analytic approaches to determining tolerability and to develop a menu of methods for public use. The funded teams developed approaches to missing data, developed predictive modeling, created a graphical representation of data, and analyzed the impact of baseline symptoms and host factors.

Consortium meetings were held monthly to stimulate the sharing of information and replication of analytic techniques with different data sets. Consortium activities were guided by a steering committee composed of representatives from the research teams, patient advocates, NCI scientific staff, and the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA). Additional members of the steering committee included a representative from the Children’s Oncology Group to enable the bidirectional sharing of information in the development of methods to analyze pediatric tolerability end points. The full list of Tolerability Consortium members can be found in Table S1.

Highlights from the Cancer Treatment Tolerability Consortium teams

The work of the NCI Tolerability Consortium study teams, as outlined in Table 1, offers useful methods for analyzing clinician‐ and patient‐reported toxicity data, which thereby furthers our understanding of treatment tolerability. Consortium analyses resulted in new tools to visualize the prevalence and trajectory of AEs. These methods also include approaches to incorporating baseline measurements and to validate summary measures such as the toxicity index and GP5. Consortium analyses also examined the impact of variables such as age, functional impairment, and allostatic load on the prevalence and trajectory of AEs and elective treatment discontinuation.

Improved methods for measuring and analyzing tolerability, including the work generated by this consortium and reported in this special issue, can aid in risk prediction and the provision of targeted supportive care to improve treatment tolerability and therapeutic response. For example, one group found that the inclusion of PROs as a component of a geriatric assessment was associated with improved treatment tolerability compared to usual care. 31 , 32 Similarly, multiple teams demonstrated that AEs are associated with elective treatment discontinuation, 20 , 23 , 29 and therefore providing targeted supportive care to those at greatest risk for treatment toxicity may reduce discontinuation. The teams also focused on the development of analytic approaches and graphical representations that improve our understanding of treatment tolerability 20 , 21 , 22 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 34 and allow for better risk stratification. 30 , 33 These provide important insights that can be applied to support shared decision‐making and target interventions to mitigate treatment toxicity.

CURRENT WORK AND THIS SPECIAL ISSUE

The work of the Tolerability Consortium reflects the multidimensionality and complementary approaches to characterizing the toxicity profile, including both clinician‐ and patient‐reported AEs, and the value of statistically efficient, interpretable, meaningful approaches to gauging treatment tolerability. This special issue highlights the selected accomplishments of the four funded groups, as well as the contributions of other participants in the consortium, including the FDA and Children’s Oncology Group. The special issue also includes an article from the NCI elaborating the multidimensional aspects of tolerability and the measurements and other indicators used to capture treatment tolerability. 35 The results, presented in full in the accompanying articles, are highlighted here.

Toxicity index

The Cedars‐Sinai/University of California Los Angeles group, which had previously developed the toxicity index to summarize CTCAE data over time, applied the toxicity index to PRO data, instead of presenting only the highest grade or the proportion experiencing each grade. 36 They examined the measurement properties of the toxicity index and its responsiveness to between‐arm differences in treatment tolerability. The group also developed a novel web interface for graphical analysis of toxicity index values with Sankey diagrams. 37

Descriptive analysis of PRO‐CTCAE

The inclusion of PRO‐CTCAE in a cancer clinical trial generates high‐dimensional data, including different symptomatic AEs, multiple attributes, and change over time. Stepwise approaches are needed to create the data set, generate tables and figures, and create, if desired, composite scores for data reduction and mitigation of issues of multiplicity. The article by the Dueck laboratory offers considerations for investigators when implementing PRO‐CTCAE, including perspectives on item selection, missing data, and visualization. 38 They also provide R macros that aid data analysts in their initial descriptive efforts. 39

Validating a summary measure of overall side effect bother

Single‐item measures of the overall impact of side effects provide a brief and interpretable summary measure of treatment tolerability that can complement the toxicity profiles captured by CTCAE and PRO‐CTCAE. GP5 (“I am bothered by the side effects of treatment”) is one example of a single‐item measure that exhibits desirable measurement properties. 28 In this issue, Peipert et al. demonstrate that GP5 scores meaningfully distinguish subgroups experiencing treatment intolerability warranting treatment discontinuation. 40 This finding generalized across a range of disease and treatment contexts, which thereby supports the predictive and analytic validity of this single‐item measure.

Decisional regret

Evidence suggests that poorly tolerated treatments contribute to decisional regret, 41 particularly among older adults, those with advanced disease, and those with more comorbid conditions. The relationship between treatment tolerability as measured by PRO‐CTCAE scores and decisional regret is explored in this issue by Flannery et al. in their study examining the impact of comprehensive geriatric assessment on decisional regret. 42 Their work also highlights the importance of examining the interplay between treatment tolerability and constructs that influence tolerability, such as decisional regret. This work illustrates that tolerability can be telescoped according to the scientific objectives; for example, tolerability can include investigating proximal treatment effects for regulatory decision‐making, or can focus on knowledge generation to optimize supportive care delivery.

Treatment tolerability in pediatric patients

The pediatric PRO‐CTCAE module was in the initial stages of measurement development as the consortium was being constituted. However, the NCI recognized the importance of including representatives from the Children’s Oncology Group in this initiative. At a fundamental level, characterizing tolerability in children has similar challenges to characterizing it in adults. However, some challenges are specific to the population of pediatric patients with cancer. For example, pediatric tolerability considerations must also incorporate the parent/proxy perspective, which poses additional analytic and interpretive complexity. In this issue, Parsons et al. highlight considerations when incorporating the perspectives of the child, parent, and clinician in tolerability research, as well as the progress being made toward inclusion of PROs in pediatric cancer clinical trials and the challenges that remain to be addressed. 43

FDA perspectives

Information about treatment tolerability is an essential component of regulatory decision‐making. Increasingly, tolerability is being informed by the incorporation of patient‐centered outcomes. The NCI and FDA have worked collaboratively to improve the collection, analysis, interpretation, and graphical representation of tolerability data. The FDA has also developed a guidance series for patient‐focused drug development, 44 and has worked to integrate patient‐generated data informing treatment tolerability into the FDA label. In this issue, Bhatnagar et al. describe the evolving FDA perspective on patient‐centered tolerability assessment. 45

CAVEATS AND FUTURE CONSIDERATIONS

Several caveats should be considered when interpreting the findings generated via this consortium and reported in this special issue. The research of the consortium was conducted as secondary analyses with existing data sets from completed clinical trials. A remaining question is whether the retrospective trial data sets that were analyzed by the consortium are representative of the population of patients with cancer. Several important subpopulations are often underrepresented in clinical trials, including older adults, individuals with lower socioeconomic status, and people with disabilities and comorbidities.

Future work is warranted to replicate the analytic methods with data sets that include diverse trial populations and contemporary treatment approaches, such as immunotherapy in combination or sequence with cytotoxic chemotherapy, to ensure that the methods developed by the Tolerability Consortium are broadly generalizable. Replication of these analytic techniques and visualization approaches in future prospective trials will also strengthen their interpretability and utility. All of the studies reported in this special issue examined treatment tolerability in the context of cancer‐directed therapy; however, future research is also needed to explore the applicability of these methods in the context of cancer prevention and interception trials. 46 Finally, with the increasing recognition that treatment tolerability is shaped by many factors, research to improve the characterization and mitigation of treatment toxicity should also consider the effects that physical function, psychosocial well‐being, and health behaviors may have on the tolerability outcomes being studied.

In terms of the impact on cancer clinical trials, we believe that the NCI Tolerability Consortium has stimulated the field to propose more thoughtful, hypothesis‐driven analytic plans in prospective trials to address tolerability. The Tolerability Consortium has also demonstrated the value of capturing and understanding low‐grade toxicities. This work has also encouraged a focus on evaluating the tolerability of multiagent treatment regimens, the role of dose‐optimizing study designs, and the experience of treatment tolerability in understudied subgroups at risk for greater toxicity, including older adults and those with multiple comorbid conditions.
