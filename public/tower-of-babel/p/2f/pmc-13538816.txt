The Upfront Effectiveness of a 6‐Week Continuous Glucose Monitoring Intervention for Non‐Insulin‐Using Individuals With Type 2 Diabetes Is No Longer Detectable at 6–12 Months: An Observational Follow‐Up of a Randomized Trial Cohort
Authors: Jiawei Ryan Zhang, Roseanne O. Yeung, Donna P. Manca, Pratima Singh, Inga Olu‐Jordan, Tawnya Perry, Gulelala Rahim, Evan M. Hagen, Darren Lau
Published: 2026

Introduction

Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) has been shown to improve glycemia in individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D) not using insulin. However, only two trials, to date, have examined effectiveness past 3–6 months in this specific population [1].

We recently reported a randomized trial of an upfront 6‐week CGM intervention compared to enhanced usual care (EC), both paired with virtual diabetes education. At 3 months, we detected a baseline‐adjusted differential glycated haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) improvement of 0.65%, favouring CGM [2]. The extent to which these benefits are sustained past 3–6 months without additional intervention is unclear. In this pre‐specified observational trial cohort follow‐up, we examined the ongoing effect of the upfront 6‐week CGM intervention, compared to enhanced usual care, on HbA1c at 6–12 months among individuals with repeat measurements.

Methods

As reported elsewhere, adults with T2D and HbA1c > 7.0% not on insulin were recruited from December 2022 to February 2024 [2]. Participants were computer randomized 1:1 to CGM or EC. CGM‐allocated individuals received three FreeStyle Libre 2 (Abbott Laboratories Canada, Mississauga ON) sensors covering 6 weeks, with two virtual diabetes educator visits in weeks 1–2 and 5–6. After 6 weeks, there was no formal protocol for continuing CGM, and most individuals discontinued it. The EC arm received the 2 educator visits only. Individuals were followed at 3 months for the primary outcome of baseline‐adjusted change in HbA1c.

Following the conclusion of the study at 3 months, we sent reminders to individuals on a periodic basis to attend visits with their usual providers for HbA1c monitoring. Using Alberta's provincial electronic medical record (EMR), we identified individuals with subsequent HbA1c measurements.

HbA1c measurements after 3 months were categorized as 6 months (91–182 days), 12 months (183–365 days) and most recent (91–540 days). Change from baseline was adjusted for baseline HbA1c using linear regression. Sensitivity analysis was conducted excluding individuals known to have used CGM during the observation period. This study was approved by the University of Alberta Research Ethics Board (Pro00119503); individual consent was obtained to monitor the individual EMRs for HbA1c values.

Results

The original trial randomized 105 individuals, with 86 trial completers at trial at 3 months. Of these, 81 (94% of trial completers, 77% of individuals randomized) had at least one subsequent HbA1c value at 91 days or longer, and are included here. These individuals had an average age of 57.8 years (SD = 12.2; 46% female) and average baseline HbA1c of 8.0% (SD = 1.2%) (85 mmol/mol [SD = 13]) (Table 1), similar to the original trial. In terms of randomization, 43 had been randomized to CGM and 38 to EC arms.

Of the 43 included individuals randomized to CGM, 15 had evidence of off‐protocol CGM use beyond 3 months. We were unable to track whether individuals in the EC arm accessed CGM during the observational follow‐up period.

In terms of crude/unadjusted mean HbA1c in each group, overall, CGM individuals had an average observational follow‐up change of −0.23% (SD 1.27%) (−6 [SD 14] mmol/mol), whereas EC individuals had an average observational follow‐up change of −0.52% (SD 1.70%) (−10 [SD 19] mmol/mol) (median follow‐up 467 days [CGM arm 470 days, EC arm 449 days], n = 81) (Table S1). Notably, very few individuals had HbA1c values within the specific window for 6 months (i.e., 91–182 days) (n = 38, 47%).

Consistent with our full trial results [2], CGM users experienced a higher degree of baseline‐adjusted HbA1c reduction at 3 months (−0.62% [95% CI −1.11 to −0.13]; −6.80 mmol/mol [−12.09 to −1.50]; p = 0.01, coefficients < 0 favour CGM) (Figure 1 and Table S1). This difference was no longer observable at 6 months (0.03% [−0.59 to 0.64]; 0.28 mmol/mol [−6.46 to 7.03]; p = 0.93), 12 months (−0.14% [−0.67 to 0.38]; −1.56 mmol/mol [−7.35 to 4.24]; p = 0.59), and with most recent values (−0.09% [−0.54 to 0.36]; −1.00 mmol/mol [−5.89 to 3.88]; p = 0.68).

Discussion

This observational follow‐up of a randomized trial cohort examined whether the upfront benefits of a 6‐week CGM intervention were sustained at 6–12 months, compared to usual care. By 6 months, we were unable to detect a difference in baseline‐adjusted HbA1c change between CGM and EC.

Glucose‐lowering may be particularly impactful for long‐term glucose control and mitigation of complications to the extent that it is offered and achieved earlier in the trajectory of T2D [3]. We examined the population of individuals with T2D, before progression to the point of requiring insulin. This population, comprising the majority of individuals with T2D [4], is distinct in that the benefit of CGM may be driven more by lifestyle and behaviour changes rather than medication adjustment [5, 6]. While there is increasing evidence that CGM improves short‐term glycemic outcomes in this population [1], the few randomized trials with follow‐up to 6 months do not agree whether there is a sustained effect beyond the initial usage period [7, 8, 9]. One trial suggested a lasting, but attenuated effect, at 12 months, but also included individuals using basal insulin [10]. Ours is the first randomized trial follow‐up cohort tracking exclusively non‐insulin‐using individuals up to 12 months.

Our primary limitation was insufficient statistical power due to low sample size, particularly at the 6‐month outcome window. In terms of our ‘most recent’ HbA1c analysis (median 467 days), we were able to include follow‐up data in 94% of the original trial completers. All the same, due to large confidence intervals, we are unable to conclude confidently that CGM was no longer effective—rather, we conclude that the difference observed at 3 months was no longer observable, and that larger, additional studies are needed. Additionally, at 6 months, the apparent loss of difference may have been driven by an improving HbA1c in the EC arm. We were unable to track concomitant medication changes past 3 months, or other interventions that individuals may have adopted. This includes the possibility of off‐protocol CGM use among EC‐randomized individuals after 3 months, which may account for some apparent diminishment of the 3‐month CGM effect, over time. Post‐trial follow‐up may also have been biased towards more engaged participants.

We found that the observed 3‐month effectiveness of a brief 6‐week CGM intervention for non‐insulin‐using individuals with type 2 diabetes was no longer detectable at 6–12 months. While observational studies suggest that the benefit of CGM may be sustained up to 24 months, many of these studies lacked control groups and examined ongoing CGM users [11]. In contrast, most of our participants were no longer on CGM after the initial 6 weeks intervention. While our sample size was limited, it makes intuitive sense that CGM‐related behaviour modifications may need long‐term reinforcement, which could come in the form of further CGM sensors or other educational ‘refresher’ content [11]. Having said that, the continuous back‐to‐back use of CGM sensors in non‐insulin‐using adults may entail substantial health care costs, as well as alert and data fatigue. Intermittent CGM at 3‐month intervals has been proposed as an alternative [3]. Future studies with more participants and longer follow‐up are needed to determine how best to sustain the upfront benefits of CGM over time.
