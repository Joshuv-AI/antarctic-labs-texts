Patients’ Needs and Expectations for the Application of Prevention of Drug-related Problems Prevention Programs
Authors: Ainhoa Vilarrubias Llopis, Jesús Ruiz-Ramos, Adrián Plaza Díaz, Mireia Puig Campmany, Anna Buisan Álvarez, Caterina Sampol-Mayol, Ana Juanes-Borrego
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:Drug-related problems (DRPs) constitute a relevant health issue with a significant and escalating impact. The objective of this study is to identify the needs and expectations of patients benefiting from secondary prevention programs for DRPs in an emergency department.Methods:A 7-month prospective study was conducted. Three participatory sessions were held with professionals from different healthcare levels, along with individual interviews conducted with patients and their caregivers and healthcare professionals involved. The concepts around which the analysis was centered on the journey maps of each user of a secondary prevention program of DRPs and the problems, needs, and opportunities for improvement in various scenarios, including primary care, hospitals, residences, intermediate care, and community pharmacies.Findings:A total of 40 interviews were conducted to define the scenarios in which the patient can travel in the healthcare environment. Patients identified problems after hospital discharge, expressing the desire to have more time with the healthcare professional to comprehend, and integrate the information about their treatment. Similarly, difficulties arose in resolving doubts because there was no contact person available after discharge. The healthcare settings of primary care and community pharmacy play important roles in instilling confidence in users.Conclusion:Patients engaging in DRP’s prevention programs require a redefined healthcare team to manage their medications. This team should have clearly defined roles among professionals and implement a follow-up and reinforcement program to personalize treatment adjusting medication (medication changes), educating patients through guidelines to enhance patient’s adherence.

INTRODUCTION

Drug-related problems (DRPs) are a significant public health issue in Western countries, ranking as the fourth to sixth leading cause of in-hospital deaths. It is estimated that around 5%–10% of hospital admissions and 21% of readmissions are attributable to medication-related iatrogenic events, which are considered preventable in approximately 69% of cases.[12] In the context of age-related comorbidity, DRPs are associated with high mortality rates, complications, prolonged hospital stays, reduced quality of life, and increased healthcare costs, especially among patients with chronic illnesses.[3] Physiological changes due to aging alter the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of drugs, making the elderly particularly vulnerable to DRPs.[4] Furthermore, increased frailty, dependence, and cognitive impairment in older adults contribute to poor adherence to treatment.[5] Consequently, older patients with higher degrees of frailty are at an increased risk of experiencing DRPs, which, in turn, contribute to and perpetuate their state of frailty.[6]

Over the recent decades, due to population aging, there has been a progressive increase in frailty patients with multiple comorbidities and polypharmacy presenting to emergency departments (EDs).[78] Comorbidity and polypharmacy are known to be associated with increased hospital admissions and healthcare costs.[910] Several studies have also linked high therapeutic complexity to nonadherence to treatment, increased adverse effects, and ED visits.[1112]

A statistically significant reduction in avoidable DRPs-related readmissions would have direct clinical relevance for patient health. In addition, healthcare systems would benefit from the reduced utilization of avoidable resources, potentially reallocating this budget to the prevention or treatment of other health issues.[13] To date, several studies have demonstrated that implementing secondary prevention programs for patients admitted due to DRPs improves the healthcare outcomes by reducing new ED visits and hospital admissions, thus enhancing the sustainability of the healthcare system.[141516] However, the preferences of these program beneficiaries regarding their pharmacotherapy to prevent further episodes have not been adequately explored.

The objective of this study is to identify the needs and expectations of patients enrolled in the secondary prevention programs for DRPs, aiming to understand these needs and identify opportunities for improving self-administration and/or medication management support.

METHODS

A prospective study was conducted over a 7-month period from February to October 2023. The research was led by a multidisciplinary team of professionals from a tertiary hospital in Catalonia (Spain), with 644 beds, that handles approximately 140,000 emergencies per year in a healthcare area serving a population of 410,000 people, of whom 24.1% are aged 65 years or older.

Three participatory sessions were held involving professionals from various disciplines and care settings who engaged with a secondary prevention program for DRPs, leading to consultations at hospital emergency services. Intentional sampling was utilized to select stakeholders, including personnel from nursing homes, primary care, intermediate care, hospitals, and pharmacies. In addition, individual interviews were conducted with patients, professional caregivers, and volunteer caregivers (who received no economic compensation) to engage all users who interact within the system. Participants were eligible if they were over 18 years old and had either experienced an ED visit for a DRP in the past year or were a caregiver for such a patient. Individuals who did not speak Spanish or were unable to provide informed consent were excluded.

The interviews were conducted either online, through home visits, or at the hospital facilities, averaging 40 min per participant. Interview guides were developed based on predefined pathways identified with professionals and project objectives. Information processing involved thematic analysis of all extracted content, focusing on identifying dimensions, grouping needs, and recognizing values considered by users throughout the process.

The sample sizes were selected based on the concept of thematic saturation (i.e., the point at which collecting additional interviews does not yield additional insights), which can vary based on homogeneity or heterogeneity of the study population. Since thematic saturation often occurred within the first 6–12 interviews based on other observational studies,[1718] the study team sought a minimum of 10 interviews from each participant group (patients, relatives, and caregivers).

The analysis focused on the journey maps of each program user within the DRPs secondary prevention of DRPs and identified problems, needs, and opportunities for improvement within each scenario. Described scenarios encompass primary care, hospital care, nursing homes, intermediate care, and community pharmacies.

RESULTS

A total of 40 interviews were conducted, including individual interviews with patients (n = 10), relatives (n = 10), caregivers (n = 15), and volunteers (n = 5). During these interviews, patient journey scenarios were defined, including the Primary Care Circuit, Hospital Circuit, Nursing Home Circuit, and Socio-healthcare Center Circuit.

For each circuit, cross-cutting, complementary, and useful coordination elements were identified [Figure 1]. Digital channels were integrated into each circuit, along with current tools common to each scenario. The functionalities and practical utility for professionals were also determined [Figure 2].

Referral Pathway for Secondary Prevention Programs in Primary Care, Nursing Homes, and Social healthcare Centers

Primary care and hospital services within the comprehensive public healthcare system are recognized for their high level of integration and their ability to edit and update the documents and electronic prescriptions. Services outside public healthcare system, such as nursing homes and intermediate care facilities, were perceived as having less frequent document updates. This could be attributed to limited editing access or information management gaps among professionals.

Two key realities were identified where patients may encounter medication-related issues: acute and episodic events when patients visit EDs and the daily management of medication [Figure 3]. Patients arriving at EDs expressed satisfaction with the professional care and a sense of trust. However, confusion often arises postdischarge, especially among older patients who express a need for more time with professionals to understand and integrate electronic prescriptions, including dosage, medication schedule, and the reasons behind prescriptions. Patients transitioning between the different levels of care expressed uncertainty about whom to contact. Primary care and community pharmacy have emerged as significant roles that instill confidence in users.

Perception of the emergency circuit in reference to drug-related problems

The second identified reality involves daily medication management, where patients demonstrate autonomy and proficiency in handling prescribed medications, often through automated systems, providing them with a sense of security. When cognitive abilities hinder autonomy, caregivers play a crucial role in weekly medication management. Support tools identified for effective monitoring include personalized dosage systems, written schemes, and follow-up calls from the healthcare professionals. Several issues contributing to poor management were identified: integrating new medications into daily routines, leftover medication after treatment cessation leading to inadvertent sharing, and unresolved queries causing uncertainty and affecting medication adherence.

To comprehend and identify all interacting points affecting patients, it is essential to define the current healthcare ecosystem of patients. This helps illustrate the level of interaction and integration of each element and scenario in their experience.

DISCUSSION

This study represents the first exploration of the needs and challenges encountered by patients consulting EDs for DRPs. According to the gathered information, there are challenges within the system to ensure sufficient understanding and follow-up of pharmacological treatment after hospital discharge. Patient journeys within defined circuits will be optimized through alignment and integration among all program users – professionals, patients, and monitoring tools – to ensure program efficiency across stages: detection, prescription, and treatment adherence.

Tools provided to patients for medication regimens do not guarantee information comprehension and are not tailored to the patients’ language. Understanding prescriptions and medication-related issues can improve treatment adherence. Postdischarge patient follow-up, through phone calls, medication management (monitored dosage systems), and reinforced educational information, positively impacts patient adherence. Healthcare entities’ information systems and tools require updates to ensure document sharing, information transfer, and data agility. This is essential for maintaining care continuity and facilitating patient follow-up within the healthcare system.

A recent study conducted in the US population found a general willingness to discontinue unnecessary medications.[21] However, barriers such as time constraints for proper medication review and insufficient evidence guiding physicians on medication discontinuation were identified. Utilizing various healthcare professionals and maintaining an accurate medication list were identified as facilitators for deprescribing.

Patients engaging with secondary prevention programs for DRPs need a redefinition of their healthcare team in terms of medication management and a clear delineation of roles among professionals. Incorporating medication experts or consultants at all levels of care could free up physicians’ time for diagnostic and preventive programs. Limitations

This pilot study had several limitations. First and foremost, it was a small study with a limited number of interviewees. Patients were recruited in a single center and the results obtained should be confirmed in other healthcare systems. Further qualitative studies reaching more in-depth, looking at other perspectives, and in other contexts; and quantitative studies to investigate different causes of DRPs (lack of adherence, overtreatment, prescription errors, etc.). This study is also limited by the failure to collect sociodemographic information from participants, including age, sex, and education level. Consequently, we could not analyze the potential influence of these variables on the study outcomes.

CONCLUSION

Patients engaging in DRP’s prevention programs require a redefined healthcare team to manage their medications. This team should have clearly defined roles among professionals and implement a follow-up and reinforcement program for changes, guidelines, and new medications to enhance adherence.
