A biomimetic nanodisc system selectively activates type I interferons by nonclassical STING pathway for cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Qianwen Mu, Haolan Deng, Shuo Li, Xiaoyu An, Xuan Liu, Yue Xi, Hao Liang, Di Sun, Xiaojun Wang, Xue Liu, Gang Liu, Chao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe epigenetic silencing or remarkably diminished expression of STING in cancer cells, along with the structural and functional impairment of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and Golgi apparatus, represents a unique mechanism of tumor immune escape and poses an important challenge for STING-targeted therapies. Here, we develop a cell membrane-derived nanodisc system (ND-cGAMP-HP; HP, heparin), which is capable of presenting activated STING proteins in their native form by means of cell membrane-directed display and biological self-assembly techniques. It can directly introduce activated STING protein to tumor cells and circumvent the translocation process between the ER and Golgi apparatus, selectively activating the IFN-I signaling pathway without initiating the inflammation-related pathway NF-κB. ND-cGAMP-HP triggers potent cellular immune responses and remodels the tumor immune microenvironment. Moreover, it augments immune memory by promoting the differentiation of TCF1+ stem cell-like T cells. We thus manifest a strategy based on STING therapy that does not depend on the ER and Golgi apparatus pathways to activate the IFN-I pathway, for cancer immunotherapy.Subject terms: Cancer immunotherapy, Protein delivery, Nanoparticles

Introduction

The Stimulator of Interferon Genes (STING) is of critical importance in innate immune responses, as well as in the tumor immune processes and cellular autophagy1–3. Cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAMP), as a second messenger, binds to the ligand-binding domain adaptor protein STING situated on the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) surface, subsequently facilitating the transport of STING from the ER to the Golgi4–7. STING interacts with sulfated glycosaminoglycans (sGAGs) that are synthesized in the Golgi, among which Heparin (HP) is one of the most bioactive sGAGs8–10. The two types of ligands, cGAMP and sGAGs, bind to distinct pockets on opposite sides of the STING protein. Specifically, cGAMP associates with the extracellular ligand-binding domain of STING (residues Y167, R232, and R238/F236), while sGAGs bind to the intracellular linker1 region located between transmembrane domains TM1 and TM2 (residues H42, R45, and H50)8,11,12. This ultimately leads to the activation of downstream type I interferons (IFN-I) and nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) signaling pathway13–16.

Currently, a variety of STING agonists are being explored in clinical and preclinical studies for cancer immunotherapy. For example, cyclic dinucleotide (CDN) agonists such as ADU-S100 and MBL-007, as well as non-CDN small molecule agonists like DMXAA, are undergoing clinical trials aimed at enhancing anti-tumor immune responses through STING pathway activation17,18. Additionally, liposome and nanoparticle delivery systems are widely utilized to improve the stability, targeting efficiency, and bioavailability of STING agonists. These agonists, by modulating the STING signaling pathway, promote the activation of T cells and dendritic cells, thereby enhancing the immune system’s ability to recognize and eliminate tumor cells19,20. Despite the progress in the development of many drug systems that targeted the delivery of STING agonists, challenges still exist in attaining optimal drug loading and activation efficiency. Moreover, a considerable number of studies have reported epigenetic silencing or a substantial reduction in the expression of STING in various cancers, such as colorectal cancer, melanoma, gastric cancer, and lung cancer. This downregulation impairs immune surveillance and weakens the effectiveness of antitumor immune responses21–24. It has been noted that a decrease in CD8+ T cell infiltration within tumors is closely associated with the downregulation of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway25. Additionally, during the advancement of tumor development, the structural integrity and functional activity of the ER and Golgi can be compromised. Such disruption can interfere with the normal activation and functioning of STING. Conventional STING agonists often prove insufficient in their capacity to robustly activate the STING signaling pathway, thereby restricting their therapeutic efficacy26–28.

In this study, we employ a biomimetic membrane display strategy to direct the expression of STING on the cell membrane and construct nanodiscs (NDs) through 22A peptide-mediated self-assembly that mimic lipid bilayer structures. This design preserves the native membrane conformation of STING and spatially exposes its functional domains, enabling the incorporation of two essential ligands into distinct binding pockets to achieve controlled activation. The engineered ND-cGAMP-HP directly delivers activated STING into tumor cells and selectively activates the STING-IRF3-IFN signaling axis independently of the ER and Golgi. This targeted activation reshapes the tumor immune microenvironment and enhances durable antitumor immune responses (Supplementary Fig. 1). This strategy provides a generalizable platform for overcoming STING downregulation and intracellular trafficking barriers in cancer therapy and offers new insights into spatially controlled innate immune activation.

Results

Construction of ND-cGAMP-HP

The expression of the STING gene (TMEM173 gene) is notably reduced in a variety of cancers (Fig. 1a), which poses a major challenge to the effectiveness of STING agonist based immunotherapy strategies. We utilized cell membrane display technology to achieve the overexpression of the STING protein on engineered cells29–31. Flow cytometry and western blot analysis showed that engineered cells had a large amount of STING proteins on the surface of the cell membrane (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 2). We extracted cell membrane components from engineered cells through ultrasound and gradient centrifugation and subsequently assembled nanodiscs with STING protein (STING-NDs) using the 22 A peptide and dimyristoyl phosphatidylcholine (DMPC). NDs, characterized by their small size, disc-like phospholipid bilayer structure32,33, and increased particle surface areas, exhibit enhanced delivery efficiency within the vasculature. These features render nanodiscs an outstanding delivery vehicle, especially for tumors with abundant neovascularization34,35. Subsequently, we incubated STING-NDs with two types of ligands. STING-NDs incorporate two distinct STING activator ligands, cGAMP and HP. This assembly method utilizes the natural binding affinity between ligands and proteins to bind cGAMP and HP to the binding pockets on the opposite sides of the STING protein (Fig. 1c)8,36. We successfully assembled ND-cGAMP-HP loaded with activated STING protein, which are characterized by their disk-shaped morphology with an approximate size of 12 nanometers (Fig. 1d, e and Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). These NDs demonstrated high stability and effective binding capabilities, achieving binding rates of 82% for cGAMP and 75% for HP, and their specific binding sites are located on the opposite side of STING on the NDs (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 3c, d). These NDs remain stable under various temperatures and pH conditions (Supplementary Fig. 3e–g).

Mechanism of ND-cGAMP-HP activation of the STING signaling pathway

We first assessed the safety profile of ND-cGAMP-HP by cell viability assays and hemolysis test, then we evaluated the in vivo safety of ND-cGAMP-HP by measuring various physiological indicators and conducting histological analyses. The results confirmed the superior biocompatibility of ND-cGAMP-HP (Supplementary Fig. 4). We tested ND-cGAMP-HP in human macrophage THP-1 cells, STING-deficient human ovarian cancer ES-2 cells and STING-low expression mouse colon cancer MC38 cells21,37, ND-cGAMP-HP markedly increased IFNβ expression in these cell types, whereas conventional STING agonists like cGAMP showed no significant effect, we also detected the expression level of IFNβ gene by qPCR, and found that ND-cGAMP-HP increased IFNβ gene expression(Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 5). Further validation through western blot analysis confirmed that ND-cGAMP-HP recruits and phosphorylates downstream TBK1 and IRF3. Thereby activating the STING signaling pathway (Fig. 2b).

Interestingly, when evaluating the expression levels of IFNβ and P65 in mouse hepatocarcinoma cells H22, which can normally express STING, we found that ND-cGAMP-HP increased the release of IFNβ in the absence of upregulation of P65, IKKβ, and IKBα phosphorylation (Fig. 2c, d and Supplementary Fig. 6). P65, also known as RelA, is one of five components of the NF-κB light chain enhancer that activates B cells38, indicating that ND-cGAMP-HP selectively activates the IFN-I pathway while avoiding activation of NF-κB. This selectivity is crucial for reducing side effects and improving treatment efficacy, as it can minimize the occurrence of inflammatory reactions (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. 8).

We then examined the intracellular action site of ND-cGAMP-HP by staining the ER and Golgi. The results revealed that ND-cGAMP-HP did not co-localize with ER and Golgi but was distributed in the cytoplasm (Fig. 2e, f). Moreover, we extracted cytoplasmic proteins and confirmed that they did not contain components related to the ER or the Golgi apparatus. Co-immunoprecipitation detection revealed the formation of complexes by ND-cGAMP-HP and TBK1 and IRF3, showing their co-localization in the cytoplasm (Supplementary Fig. 9). These results indicate that ND-cGAMP-HP acts directly in the cytoplasm, recruiting and phosphorylating TBK1 and IRF3 to more efficiently activate STING. We hypothesize that the underlying mechanism may be that the transport of STING from the ER to the Golgi apparatus is a critical step in recruiting downstream signaling complexes, such as TBK1-IRF3 and IKK-NF-κB. When STING is activated through non-canonical localization, it is directly delivered to the cytoplasm or specific subcellular regions (e.g., perinuclear areas), where it may only interact with molecules related to IRF3 activation, such as TBK138,39. The activation of NF-κB requires the recruitment of the IKK complex, and the activation of the IKKβ complex and phosphorylation of IKBα are closely associated with its recruitment to the Golgi membrane. Therefore, bypassing the Golgi may directly prevent the correct localization and activation of IKKβ, inhibiting its signaling39. Furthermore, we assessed the cellular entry capability of ND-cGAMP-HP, and observed that tumor cells more readily uptake ND-cGAMP-HP compared to conventional membrane vesicles (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 10), which is related to the targeting effect mediated by 22 A peptide assembled NDs to the scavenger receptor class B type I (SR-BI), which has high expression on the surface of tumor cells (Supplementary Fig. 11)40,41.

Therapeutic efficacy of ND-cGAMP-HP in early-stage tumors

After conjugating ND-cGAMP-HP with Cy5.5, we administered it via tail vein injection into MC38 tumor-bearing mice. Small animal imaging results reveal that ND-cGAMP-HP was highly enriched at the tumor site and had a longer in vivo half-life of 18.8 h compared with 1.35 h for cGAMP, indicating a more durable therapeutic effect at the lesion site (Fig. 3a, b). We then examined ND-cGAMP-HP distribution in various tissues. While the free Cy5.5 primarily accumulated in the liver, kidneys, and lungs, ND-cGAMP-HP, due to its unique disc-like structure and small size, penetrated the tumor vasculature more effectively, concentrating in the tumor site and lymph nodes (Fig. 3c)42.

The therapeutic efficacy of ND-cGAMP-HP was assessed in the early-stage MC38 tumor model with a tumor size of 100 mm3, which had defective STING protein expression. At this point, we initiated treatment with intravenous injections of ND-cGAMP-HP, administering a total of four doses (Fig. 3d). The results were remarkable, demonstrating that ND-cGAMP-HP significantly inhibited tumor growth over time. Notably, tumors in the ND-cGAMP-HP-treated group showed a marked reduction in size, with some tumors completely regressing, leading to full remission (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 12). This was accompanied by a substantial increase in the survival rate of the treated mice, with no significant changes in body weight observed, indicating good biosafety (Fig. 3f–h). Furthermore, by comparing the antitumor effects of ND-cGAMP-HP with those of clinical-stage drugs (ADU-S100, MK-1454), we found that ND-cGAMP-HP exhibits potent antitumor activity and significantly increases the survival rate of mice (Supplementary Fig. 13).

Additionally, we investigated the in vivo mechanism underlying ND-cGAMP-HP’s response. The findings revealed that ND-cGAMP-HP selectively activated the STING signaling pathway in vivo. This activation led to an increase in the phosphorylation levels of downstream signaling factors TBK1 and IRF3, which are critical components of the STING pathway (Fig. 3i). Furthermore, ND-cGAMP-HP selectively induced the expression of IFNβ without activating the NF-κB signaling pathway. This was evidenced by a significant increase in IFNβ levels, while the levels of both P65 and phosphorylated P65, the marker of NF-κB activation, remained unchanged (Fig. 3i, j), which avoids the risk of inflammatory response (Supplementary Fig. 14)43. These results align with the previous in vitro experiments.

The therapeutic effects of ND-cGAMP-HP in advanced tumors

To explore the therapeutic effects of ND-cGAMP-HP in tumors with normal STING expression and tumors with STING expression defects, as well as its ability to respond to the tumor microenvironment at different stages of progression, we established H22 and MC38, advanced tumor models. H22 cells express STING normally, while MC38 cells have defective STING protein expression.

In the MC38 advanced tumor model, tumors grew to approximately 300 mm3. We administered ND-cGAMP-HP using the same method as in the early stage, with four intravenous doses. The results were promising, showing that ND-cGAMP-HP significantly inhibited tumor growth. The tumors in the ND-cGAMP-HP-treated group experienced considerable shrinkage, leading to a notable improvement in the survival rates of the mice. In contrast, the control groups exhibited less pronounced effects (Fig. 4a–d). Similarly, in the 300 mm3 of the H22 advanced tumor model, ND-cGAMP-HP led to a significant reduction in tumor size, further supporting the efficacy of ND-cGAMP-HP in combating large tumors (Fig. 4e–h). These results indicate that ND-cGAMP-HP can respond to various types or stages of tumors, and its efficacy in STING deficient and STING expressing tumors highlights its versatility and prospects as a therapeutic strategy.

Next, we conducted a long-term treatment experiment to further investigate the immune protective ability of ND-cGAMP-HP on the subjects. After the complete regression of MC38 tumors in mice treated with ND-cGAMP-HP, we reintroduced MC38 tumor cells into the subjects and used a group of untreated mice as a control (Fig. 4i). The results showed that in the untreated group, the tumor grew to approximately 100 mm3 on day 7. In contrast, mice treated with ND-cGAMP-HP showed significant tumor resistance. After 35 days, the tumors in the ND-cGAMP-HP group still did not exist, and the survival rate of the ND-cGAMP-HP group was 100% (Fig. 4j, k). These results suggest the potential of ND-cGAMP-HP to induce sustained immune response and memory and maintain a tumor free state for a longer period of time.

ND-cGAMP-HP reverses tumor microenvironment tolerance mechanism

We further investigated the immune regulatory mechanism of ND-cGAMP-HP, with a focus on its impact on adaptive cellular immune response and innate immune response. ND-cGAMP-HP showed strong T cell responses in both advanced MC38 and H22 tumor models, significantly enhancing the clone of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in lymph nodes and spleen of MC38 and H22 model, as evidenced by flow cytometry results (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. 15 and Supplementary Fig. 16). These findings were corroborated by immunofluorescence assays, which showed a marked increase in the infiltration of CD8+ T cells (Fig. 5b). It is worth noting that ND-cGAMP-HP promotes the differentiation of TCF1+ stem cells like CD8+ T cells (Fig. 5c, d), a rare subset of antigen-specific T cells characterized by their capacity for long-term self-renewal, expansion, and multipotency, which are crucial for sustaining durable anti-tumor responses44. TCF1 plays a critical role in maintaining the formation of stem-like central memory CD8+ T cells. The regulation of TCF1 by STING activation ensures the persistence and potency of these stem-like T cells, thereby contributing to sustained anti-tumor immunity and enhancing the therapeutic potential of immunotherapies45,46. Additionally, ND-cGAMP-HP also elevated the differentiation of effector memory T cells marked by CD44+ expression (Fig. 5e). The increase in memory T cells may explain why, in long-term studies, the ND-cGAMP-HP group showed no signs of tumor growth in re-challenge with the tumor cells.

In addition, ND-cGAMP-HP promoted the activation and differentiation of dendritic cells (DCs) and natural killer (NK) cells, which are crucial for initiating and sustaining anti-tumor immune responses (Fig. 5f–h, Supplementary Fig. 17 and Supplementary Fig. 18a–c). The treatment with ND-cGAMP-HP led to a reduction in regulatory T cells, which are known for their role in suppressing immune responses, thus creating a favorable immune environment for anti-tumor treatment (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Fig. 18d). ND-cGAMP-HP reduces the differentiation and cloning of PD-1 and TIM3 positive T cell subsets, which typically benefit tumors to evade immune surveillance (Fig. 5j, k, Supplementary Fig. 18e, f, Supplementary Fig. 19). To further investigate the exact role of these immune cells in triggering immune effects in tumors, we depleted the relevant immune cells (CD4+ T, CD8+ T, and NK) using antibodies. The results showed that the anti-tumor effect of ND-cGAMP-HP was abolished when CD8+ T cells were depleted. And depletion of CD4+ T cells or NK cells also significantly affects the anti-tumor effect of ND-cGAMP-HP. This indicates that the potent immune response induced by ND-cGAMP-HP is the result of coordinating multiple key immune cell populations, mainly relying on CD8+ T cells, and CD4+ T cells and NK cells also play important roles in this process (Supplementary Fig. 20).

ND-cGAMP-HP triggers immune-mediated immunogenic cell death in tumors

Additionally, we observed that administration of ND-cGAMP-HP in tumor-bearing mice markedly elevated the expression of multiple damage-associated molecular patterns within tumor tissues, including calreticulin (CRT), heat shock proteins (HSPs), adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) (Fig. 6a–c and Supplementary Fig. 21). Immunoblotting and immunofluorescence analyses consistently demonstrated enhanced surface exposure of CRT and increased intracellular HSP expression, while extracellular ATP and HMGB1 release were significantly augmented compared with control groups. These DAMPs are canonical hallmarks of immunogenic cell death (ICD) and are known to facilitate dendritic cell maturation and antigen presentation. The coordinated upregulation of these molecules suggests that ND-cGAMP-HP treatment promotes a pro-immunogenic remodeling of the tumor microenvironment in vivo.

To determine whether ND-cGAMP-HP directly induces cytotoxicity in tumor cells, we performed in vitro assays in the absence of immune cells. ND-cGAMP-HP alone did not significantly reduce tumor cell viability nor directly trigger classical ICD-associated molecular changes (Supplementary Fig. 22), indicating that its antitumor activity is not attributable to intrinsic tumor cell toxicity. Notably, when peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were co-cultured with tumor cells, ND-cGAMP-HP treatment resulted in pronounced immune cell-mediated tumor cell apoptosis (Fig. 6d, e). This process was accompanied by elevated release of HSP70, CRT, and HMGB1 into the supernatant, consistent with immunogenic apoptotic death. Importantly, the addition of the caspase-3 inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK significantly attenuated DAMP release, confirming that the observed ICD was apoptosis-dependent. Collectively, these findings indicate that ND-cGAMP-HP does not directly kill tumor cells but instead potentiates immune cell-mediated immunogenic apoptosis, thereby reversing immune tolerance and coordinately enhancing both innate and adaptive antitumor immune responses.

Discussion

An increasing volume of research highlights the crucial role of the cGAS-STING pathway in tumor immunotherapy. This pathway has become a prominent target for enhancing anti-tumor immune responses47–49. By encapsulating STING agonists within nanoparticles, researchers strive to improve the selective delivery of these drugs to tumor sites, thereby augmenting their therapeutic impact and minimizing off-target effects50–52. However, the unique mechanism of tumor immune escape poses the most significant challenge to STING-targeted therapy, such as epigenetic silencing or significantly reduced expression of STING in cancer cells, as well as structural and functional damage to the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus, making tumors unresponsive to traditional STING-targeted therapy.

This research employs a cell membrane display technology to express STING on the membrane and incorporate it into STING-NDs. These STING-NDs, possessing a lipid bilayer structure analogous to natural membranes, effectively preserve the membrane-associated structure and functionality of STING. The NDs particles adopt a disc-like shape, which enhances their capacity to penetrate the tumor vasculature more efficiently. This structural design not only refines the targeting of the tumor but also optimizes the therapeutic potential of the STING agonists encapsulated within the nanodiscs53. We have engineered a system in which two essential ligands required for STING activation are strategically bound to distinct binding pockets on the STING protein. This design facilitates the activation of STING and ensures its functional activity within the cytoplasm. Notably, the ND-cGAMP-HP system delivers activated STING proteins directly into the cytoplasm and activates the IFN-I pathway through a way independent of the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus, thereby addressing the challenges of tumor STING downregulation and dysfunction of the intracellular membrane system. This approach allows for highly efficient and selective activation of the STING-IRF3-IFN signaling pathway.

We evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of ND-cGAMP-HP in tumors with normal STING expression or tumors with STING expression defects, as well as its ability to respond to the tumor microenvironment at different stages of progression. The outcomes manifested that ND-cGAMP-HP possesses substantial potential for tumor treatment. In long-term follow-up studies, even after re-implanting tumors, ND-cGAMP-HP indicated its sustained therapeutic efficacy. Nevertheless, additional investigations are warranted to assess supplementary biomarkers and treatment outcomes in extended cohorts. Long-term dynamic changes of cytokine profiles remain to be investigated in future extended experiments, which is a limitation of the present work. A comprehensive set of long-term experiments will be critical to validate its effectiveness across a broader spectrum of parameters and clinical scenarios. The success of ND-cGAMP-HP is attributed to its ability to provoke a robust immune response. Specifically, ND-cGAMP-HP significantly enhances the activation of T cells, increases immune cell infiltration into tumors, and reduces the expression of immune-suppressive molecules. Moreover, a critical aspect of ND-cGAMP-HP’s effectiveness is its capacity to substantially elevate the expression of TCF1+, a marker associated with maintaining CD8+ T cell stemness, and to increase the activation of CD44+ effector memory T cells. Notably, although as a novel drug delivery platform, the ND-cGAMP-HP formulation has successfully overcome the challenges in chemistry, manufacturing, and controls, as we have established standard operating procedures for large-scale synthesis and implemented appropriate quality control measures for the nanodiscs.

In summary, we have, for the first time, developed a biomimetic nanodisc system that selectively activates type I interferons by the nonclassical STING pathway for cancer immunotherapy. This approach enhances the tumor immune microenvironment and promotes durable immune responses, corroborating its outstanding potential as an immunotherapeutic agent.

Assembly and characteristics of nanomaterials

We utilized cell membrane display technology to achieve the overexpression of the STING protein on engineering cells. Cells were disrupted using ultrasonic treatment, sonicated for 15 s, followed by a 5 s pause, repeated three times. The lysate was then centrifuged at 3200 × g for 5 min to remove debris. The supernatant was collected and subjected to further centrifugation at 20000 × g for 20 min to clear additional impurities. Final centrifugation at 100,000 × g for 1 h, the precipitate was resuspended in PBS to obtain the cell membrane components. The extracted cell membrane fragments were mixed with 22A peptide (PVLDLFRELLNELLEALKQKLK, synthesized from GenScript) and DMPC (a short-chain lipid that plays a crucial role in stabilizing the outer edge structure, thereby contributing to the overall structural integrity of the nanodiscs, HY-109541, MCE) in a ratio of 1:5:25, respectively. This mixture was allowed to self-assemble into nanodiscs (NDs) at room temperature with gentle rotation for 4–6 h. cGAMP (HY-100564, MCE) and HP (HY-17567, MCE) were then added to the mixture in a 1:5:5 ratio, and the solution was rotated at room temperature for an additional 2 h to form ND-cGAMP-HP. The final ND-cGAMP-HP product was stored at −80 °C for future use.

To assess the particle size distribution, negative staining was performed on the samples, which were then imaged using a Tecnai Spirit transmission electron microscope operating at 200 kV. Zetasizer Nano ZS-90 was used to measure the size and zeta potential of the nanoparticles.

To assess the stability of the ND-cGAMP-HP, we stored them at −80 °C for 45 days and at 4 °C for 8 days, and measured their particle sizes using dynamic light scattering (DLS). Additionally, to evaluate the stability of ND-cGAMP-HP under different pH conditions, we incubated them in media with varying pH values at 37 °C for 24 h, followed by particle size analysis using DLS.

To evaluate small molecule binding efficiency, the ND-cGAMP-HP and MV-cGAMP solutions were dissolved in PBS and centrifuged at 21000 × g for approximately 30 min. The supernatant, containing unbound small molecules, was collected. Residual concentrations of cGAMP and HP in the supernatant were measured using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Cytotoxicity assay

To evaluate the cytotoxicity of ND-cGAMP-HP, the experiment was conducted using AML12 cells, which are mouse normal liver cells known for their robustness and suitability in toxicity testing. The cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well in a 96-well plate and cultured for 24 h to allow for cell attachment and growth under standard conditions. Following this incubation period, the cells were treated with various concentrations of cGAMP and ND-cGAMP-HP for an additional 24 h to assess their impact on cell viability.

To investigate the selective activation of the STING signaling pathway by ND-cGAMP-HP, we co-incubated ND-cGAMP-HP with an NF-κB agonist and MC38 tumor cells for 24 h. The supernatants were then collected and added to a 96-well plate containing PBMCs (1 × 104 cells/well) and AML12 cells (1 × 104 cells/well), followed by a 24 h incubation.

To determine the cytotoxic effects, the CCK-8 assay, a widely used method for cell viability assessment, was employed. The CCK-8 reagent was added to each well according to the manufacturer’s recommended concentration, and the cells were incubated for 1 h. This reagent contains a tetrazolium salt that is reduced by dehydrogenases in viable cells to form a colored formazan dye, which can be quantified by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm (OD450nm) using a microplate reader.

The OD450nm values obtained from the assay were then used to calculate the cell viability percentage for each treatment group, allowing for a comprehensive analysis of the cytotoxicity profile of ND-cGAMP-HP at different concentrations.

Western blot

Cells and tumor tissues were lysed with ultrasonication in RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime Biotech) for 30 min on ice. The lysis buffer contained 1% Phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (ST506-2, Beyotime Biotech) and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (HY-K0022, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ 08852). After lysis, the samples were centrifuged under 13000 × g for 30 min, and the supernatants were denatured at 95 °C for 10 min and loaded onto a 4-20% gradient SurePAGE (M00657, GenScript, Piscataway) along with 4 × SDS loading buffer (150 mM Tris-HCl, 12% SDS, 0.2% bromophenol blue, 25% glycerol, 6% β-mercaptoethanol, pH 7.4). Proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Merck Millipore Ltd, Darmstadt) and blocked in a Protein-free rapid blocking solution (G2052-500ML, Servicebio Biotechnology) for 30 min at room temperature. The membranes were then incubated with the corresponding primary antibodies for 12 h at 4 °C, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. The membranes were visualized using the ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad) with SuperKine West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (BMU102-CN, Abbkine).

Co-immunoprecipitation

ND-cGAMP-HP treated MC38 tumor cells were first slowly lysed on ice using the RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime Biotech), which contained 1% phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (ST506-2, Beyotime Biotech) and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (HY-K0022, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ 08852), and included 1% Triton X-100. The lysate was then subjected to gradient centrifugation, with an initial centrifugation at 400 × g for 15 min to collect the pellet, followed by a high-speed centrifugation at 21000 × g for 30 min to collect the supernatant. This process removed the nuclear and membrane components. The cytoplasmic proteins were then transferred to a new tube, and protein concentration was determined. Western blot analysis was subsequently performed for use in subsequent experiments.

For co-immunoprecipitation, incubate the obtained sample with Anti-STING at 4 °C for 1 h. Next, add protein A/G magnetic beads (88803, Thermo Scientific Pierce) and incubate the mixed sample at 4 °C for another hour. Wash the beads with 150 mM Tris-HCl to remove unbound substances, and repeat this washing step three times to ensure minimal background. After washing, resuspend the beads and elute the target protein by centrifugation. Then, the eluted proteins were analyzed by Western blotting.

qPCR

After incubating the cells with the drug for 12 h, the cells were collected for RNA extraction using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (RC112-01). For the reverse transcription process, 10 pg of RNA was used as the template in a 20 μl reaction volume, utilizing the ABScript Neo RT Master Mix for qPCR with gDNA Remover (RK20433) to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA). The resulting cDNA served as the template for quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis using the BrightCycle Universal SYBR Green qPCR Mix with UDG (RK21219). In a 10 μl reaction mixture, 2 μl of DNA template (10 pg) was combined with 0.4 μl of each primer (10 μM; forward primer: GCCATCAGTCACTTAAACAGC, reverse primer: GAAACTGAAGATCTCCTAGCCT). The qPCR was performed on an Agilent AriaMx Real-Time PCR System to quantify the transcription levels of the IFNβ gene in the cells.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

After incubating adherent cancer cells with the drug for 24 h, the supernatant or cell extract was collected for analysis. The levels of cytokines, specifically IFNβ (Abclonal, RK00420), P65 (Abclonal, RK07076), IL-6 (Abclonal, RK00008), TNF (Abclonal, RK04875), HMGB1(Abclonal, RK06737), CRT (MM-44761M2, Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd), and HSP70 (EM1137, FineTest), were quantified using a mouse ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To further assess the in vivo effects of the drug on cytokine production, tumor fragments were harvested from mice and then co-incubated with the drug for another 24 h. Following this incubation period, the supernatant was again collected, and the concentrations of IFNβ, P65, IL-6, and TNF were measured using ELISA kits.

Flow cytometry

Cells were cultured in a 6-well plate for 24 h, followed by washing with PBS three times. Then, single-cell suspensions were prepared and incubated with antibodies for 30 min at room temperature. The final cells were analyzed with CytoFlex (Beckman).

Lymphatic lymphocytes isolated. Collect lymph node tissue into a culture medium supplemented with serum and antibiotics. Grind the tissue and then filter it through a 70 μm mesh to remove large debris. Centrifuge at 150 × g to collect the cells. The collected cells are then used for flow cytometry analysis.

Splenic lymphocytes isolated. Collect spleen tissue into a culture medium containing serum and antibiotics. Grind the tissue and add 30% v/v red blood cell lysis buffer. Let it stand for 10 min to lyse the red blood cells. Centrifuge at 400 × g to collect the cells. The collected cells are then used for flow cytometry analysis.

Cell Staining and Analysis. Resuspend the collected cells and aliquot an equal volume into EP tubes. Add the flow cytometry antibodies to the cells and incubate in the dark for 30 min. Resuspend the cells in PBS and centrifuge to remove excess dye. Repeat the washing step three times. Analyze the stained cells using flow cytometry (CytoFlex, Beckman).

To evaluate the effect of ND-cGAMP-HP on CRT levels in vivo, we labeled live and dead cells with PI staining and marked CRT using a flow cytometry antibody (Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit, 40302ES60, Yeasen, Shanghai, China). After 15 min of incubation, flow cytometric analysis was performed to assess the changes.

To evaluate the targeting capability of ND-cGAMP-HP, we divided MC38 tumor cells into two groups: one treated with an SR-BI inhibitor and the other without inhibitor pretreatment. After 24 h of co-incubation with the inhibitor, both groups were exposed to DiD-labeled ND-cGAMP-HP for 12 h, followed by flow cytometry analysis (CytoFlex, Beckman) to assess cellular uptake efficiency.

In vivo imaging of ND-cGAMP-HP

After labeling ND-cGAMP-HP with the fluorescent dye Cy5.5, the excess dye was removed by centrifugation to ensure only conjugated ND-cGAMP-HP particles remained. The Cy5.5-labeled ND-cGAMP-HP (containing 1.0 nmol of dye and 10 μg cGAMP per mouse) was then injected via the tail vein into mice. At specified time points:1 h, 12 h, and 24 h post-injection, mice were imaged using the Caliper IVIS Lumina II system to monitor the biodistribution of the dye-labeled ND-cGAMP-HP. Following the imaging, the mice were euthanized, and major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, as well as tumor tissues and lymph nodes, were harvested. These tissues were then subjected to ex vivo imaging on the same instrument to assess the accumulation and localization of the dye.

Pharmacokinetic measurement

After conjugating Cy5.5 to ND-cGAMP-HP, the mixture was centrifuged to remove any excess dye. C57BL/6J mice were then intravenously injected with either free Cy5.5 (1.0 nmol dye per mouse) or ND-cGAMP-HP-Cy5.5 (containing 10 μg cGAMP per mouse) (n = 5–8). At specified time points post-injection (1 min, 1, 2, 4, 6, 22, 44, 66 h), blood samples (50 μl) were collected from the retro-orbital plexus of each mouse. The collected blood samples were centrifuged to separate the plasma, which was then diluted 5-fold in PBS buffer containing 5 mM EDTA. Fluorescence was quantified using a plate reader. To determine the concentration of the samples, a standard curve was prepared using PBS buffer with 5 mM EDTA and 20% (v/v) mouse plasma. The data were expressed as the percentage of injected dose (% ID), with the plasma sample collected at the 1 min time point representing the maximum injected dose (100% ID). Nonlinear regression and two-phase decay modeling were performed using GraphPad Prism software to analyze the pharmacokinetics of ND-cGAMP-HP-Cy5.5 and free-Cy5.5 over time.

Mice tumor models

To establish a subcutaneous tumor model, MC38 colon cancer cells (2 × 106 cells) or H22 liver cancer cells (2 × 106 cells) were implanted subcutaneously into the thighs of C57BL/6 J or BALB/c mice. Tumor growth was monitored using calipers, and tumor volume was calculated as an ellipsoid (0.5 × length × width2). On the 7th day post-implantation for the early-stage tumor model (100 mm3) or the 14th day for the advanced tumor model (300 mm3), mice with similar tumor sizes were randomly assigned to three groups: the PBS control group, the cGAMP group, and the ND-cGAMP-HP treatment group. To compare the therapeutic efficacy of the 22 A peptide and SMA-assembled NDs, we also included an SMA-cGAMP-HP treatment group as an experimental control.

Treatments were administered via tail vein injection (cGAMP 10 μg per mouse). cGAMP (5 mg/ml) and HP (50 mg/ml) were prepared and stored as high-concentration stock solutions. The ND: cGAMP: HP complex was assembled at a mass ratio of 1:5: 5. The dilution factor was calculated based on the amount of each component added, and the concentrations of cGAMP and NDs in the final ND-cGAMP-HP complex were determined accordingly. The working solution was diluted to a final cGAMP concentration of 120 μg/ml. Prior to injection, the solution was centrifuged at 21000 × g for 30 min to remove any unbound compounds by discarding the supernatant, resuspended in an equal volume of physiological saline. A volume of 100 μl of the purified ND-cGAMP-HP solution was then administered via tail vein injection. Based on previous measurements indicating a cGAMP loading efficiency of 82%, each injection contained approximately 21 μg of ND-cGAMP-HP (equivalent to 10 μg of bound cGAMP from an initial 12 μg dose × 82%).

For the early-stage tumor model, injections were performed on days 7, 14, 16, and 18. For the advanced tumor model, injections were given on days 14, 16, 18, and 25. Animals were humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation when tumors reached 1.5 cm in diameter or when moribund signs, including pronounced weight loss or tumor ulceration, were detected.

Immunofluorescence of tumor tissue

Fresh tissue samples were collected and trimmed according to experimental requirements. Rapid OCT embedding was performed within 30 min. Tissue sections were cut at a thickness of 18 μm. Frozen sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, followed by three washes in PBS, each for 5 min. Tissue sections were placed in a retrieval box containing EDTA antigen retrieval buffer (pH 6.0) and subjected to microwave antigen retrieval. The process involved 3 min on high power, a 10 min pause, and 3 min on low power, ensuring the buffer did not evaporate excessively to avoid drying the sections. After natural cooling, the slides were washed in PBS. Excess PBS was removed, and 5% BSA was added for 30 min to block non-specific binding sites. The primary antibody was diluted in PBS to an appropriate concentration. 100 μl of diluted primary antibody was added to each section and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Sections were washed three times in PBS, followed by incubation with diluted Hoechst stain at room temperature for 10 min. Each section was mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium and covered with a coverslip. Fluorescence microscopy was used for observation.

ATP detection assay

After administration, tumor tissues from mice of the same weight were collected under sterile conditions. The tissues were cut into small pieces and washed with ice-cold PBS to remove impurities and blood. The tissues were then homogenized and lysed. The homogenate was centrifuged at 13000 × g for 10–15 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris, and the supernatant was collected. ATP concentration was measured using an ATP Quantification Kit (Biyuntian, Cat. No.: S0027), and the measurement was performed using a fluorescence microplate reader. The ATP concentration in the samples was quantified by comparing the chemiluminescence signals to a standard ATP curve.

Subsequently, 50 μl of the supernatant was transferred to a microplate, and the absorbance (Asample) at 545 nm (OD545nm) was measured using a microplate reader. Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software (Version 10) was used to perform data analysis. Student’s t-test was used to compare the two groups. Multi-groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA. The P-value on the graphs is denoted as, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001. All the values were reported as mean ± s.d. with the indicated sample size. No sample in any representative experiment was excluded from the analysis. All the animal studies were performed after randomization. Experiments were not performed in a blinded fashion.

Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo software (v10). IVIS data was analyzed using Living Image software (v4.5). Confocal images were analyzed by ZEN 3.4.
