Hereditary angioedema attack trends among patients maintained on lanadelumab long-term prophylaxis
Authors: Maeve O’Connor, Joshua Jacobs, Laura Panattoni, Grace Gahlon, Nicole Bariahtaris, Krystal Sing, Daniel Fox, Bob G Schultz, Salomé Juethner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKey words: Hereditary angioedema, lanadelumab, long-term prophylaxis, real-world data, Trio Health, Consortium of Independent Immunology Clinics

Hereditary angioedema (HAE) is a rare inherited disorder characterized by recurrent and unpredictable episodes of tissue swelling that can affect the extremities, face, abdomen, genitals, and the upper airway.1, 2, 3 HAE associated with mutations in the SERPING1 gene leads to deficiency or dysfunction of the C1 inhibitor (C1INH); HAE-C1INH-Type1 or HAE-C1INH-Type2, respectively.4 HAE-C1INH has an estimated prevalence of 1 in 50,000 individuals in the United States.5

The burden of disease associated with HAE is substantial.6 HAE attacks can be painful, disfiguring, potentially life-threatening, and may require a physician visit or hospital treatment.1,2,7 Additionally, patients with HAE have reduced health-related quality of life compared with the general population.8 Daily activities such as school or work are often impacted, and there are issues with depression and anxiety.7, 8, 9 Direct and indirect costs add to the disease burden due to missed work, reduced productivity, and medical expenses.10,11 Finding an appropriate treatment regimen to manage HAE is critical for patients since disease control and treatment satisfaction have been positively correlated with improved quality of life.12 According to the World Allergy Organization/European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (WAO/EAACI) guidelines for HAE management, total disease control should be the goal of HAE treatment to normalize patients’ lives.2 HAE management plans should be individualized to each patient’s needs due to the wide variability in HAE symptoms, response to and tolerance of HAE medications, and factors impacting clinical course and quality of life.3 These treatment plans should be monitored regularly and adjusted based on the patient’s needs, and should include consideration of long-term prophylactic medications to prevent HAE attacks, and use of short-term prophylactic medications before medical procedures or other events known to trigger HAE symptoms.3 Furthermore, effective on-demand medication should continue to be available for all patients, including those with very long attack-free periods, as early intervention is critical in the event of an attack.

To manage HAE attacks, long-term prophylaxis (LTP) is used to reduce attack frequency and severity.2 Lanadelumab is a self-injectable, fully human mAb used for HAE LTP.13,14 Clinical trials support the long-term efficacy and safety of lanadelumab for reducing the frequency of HAE attacks in adults and children, with follow-up ranging from 26 weeks to 33 months.15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20 In 2018, lanadelumab was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for patients with HAE aged 12 years or older, and in 2023, the approval was extended to patients aged 2 years or older, based on the results of the SPRING trial.21 Additionally, lanadelumab is recommended among first-line LTP options for patients by the WAO/EAACI and the US HAE Association Medical Advisory Board.2,3 Of note, lanadelumab is currently the only guideline-recommended LTP for patients aged 2 years to younger than 6 years.22

Long-term observational studies based on data gathered from routine clinical practice provide additional information on treatment outcomes achieved by patients with HAE, and some studies on lanadelumab have indicated incremental improvements in attack prevention after a steady state has been reached.23,24 Detailed real-world data on lanadelumab use are needed to better understand its long-term benefits for patients after each successive year of treatment. To date, real-world data on lanadelumab use for extended durations have come from the INTEGRATED Study with 44 months of follow-up,24 and the EMPOWER Study with 26 and 32 months of follow-up in new and established users, respectively,25 as well as from the Adelphi HAE Disease Specific Program cross-sectional survey, in which patients had a mean of 55 months on lanadelumab treatment.26 However, these studies did not assess individual patient profiles and related changes in outcomes. In the present study, attack outcomes and health care resource utilization were analyzed from a large dataset of adult patients with HAE in the United States to better understand long-term real-world lanadelumab use and patient outcomes over time.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a retrospective cohort study of lanadelumab use among patients with HAE in the United States. Patients were identified in the Consortium of Independent Immunology Clinics (CIIC) HAE database, which includes data from over 150 clinics in more than 30 states and is managed by health care providers who are a part of the CIIC. The database contains curated electronic health records obtained from commercial, Medicare, and Medicaid patients. Eligible patients had a confirmed diagnosis of HAE-C1INH, were aged 18 years or older, and were receiving lanadelumab LTP for at least 6 months.

Individual treatment periods were constructed for HAE-indicated LTP, which included the time covered by medication fills, allowing a gap of at most 90 days. Patients were selected if they had at least 1 treatment period for lanadelumab between August 1, 2018, and December 31, 2023. The first lanadelumab treatment period that persisted for at least 6 months was retained for analysis. For lanadelumab treatment periods initiated before the start of the study, the start dates were considered under follow-up from the study start date (August 1, 2018), provided that the treatment period lasted at least 6 months. Each treatment period was censored at the treatment period end date. Patients were followed until discontinuation, loss to follow-up, or the end of the observation period on April 30, 2024.

Data on outcomes were collected from patients during clinic visits and covered the time since the previous visit. Outcomes were self-reported by the patient since the last visit. The time between each patient’s visit to the clinic was referred to as the “follow-up interval.” Time between visits varied, reflecting real-world behavior; therefore, “follow-up intervals” were not fixed and outcomes were time-adjusted by converting the outcomes to rates per month. A total of 4 follow-up intervals were used to assess longitudinal trends over time. Follow-up intervals were required to have at least 75% of the days covered by the lanadelumab treatment period; any intervals covered by the lanadelumab treatment period for less than 75% were excluded from the analysis. Interval 1 was used as a reference with longitudinal changes in attack rate and the proportion of attack-free patients assessed over intervals 2, 3, and 4 (Fig 1). The primary outcomes assessed for each interval included number of attacks per month, the proportion of attack-free patients, rescue medication use, and attacks requiring treatment in the emergency room or hospitalization. Exploratory outcomes included dosing frequency changes, treatment discontinuation, and reasons for discontinuation.

Study design for analysis within the Trio Health database. Follow-up intervals were included in the analysis if they had at least 75% coverage with lanadelumab.

Statistical analysis

Primary outcomes were assessed for each follow-up interval while the patient was on lanadelumab. Categorical variables were analyzed as proportions and frequencies, whereas continuous variables were analyzed using means, SDs, medians, and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Differences in mean monthly attack rates were analyzed using t-tests, and differences in median monthly attack rates were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. For all statistical testing, interval 1 was used as the comparator for intervals 2, 3, and 4. No adjustments for multiple comparisons were made. Follow-up intervals that reported an HAE attack but not the number of attacks were imputed to derive an attack rate. A multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE)27 procedure was used to impute the censored attack outcomes using complete data from other observations. The proportion of patients with zero attacks during each follow-up interval was compared using a 2-sample z-test assuming independence between the groups, with significance assessed at the 90% confidence level.

Interval attack rates were used to perform a post hoc classification of patients by yearly attack rates. The proportion of patients in each attack rate category (0 attacks per year, >0 to <2 attacks per year, 2 to <4 attacks per year, 4 to <8 attacks per year, and ≥8 attacks per year) and their progression over intervals 2, 3, and 4 was assessed and visualized by using a Sankey diagram created at SankeyMATIC.com.28 Exploratory outcomes were assessed using frequencies and proportions. Patients who were still on lanadelumab when they were lost to follow-up were censored. All statistical analyses were conducted by using R version 4.2.1 in the Komodo virtual environment RStudio integrated development environment.

Results

Patient demographics and disease characteristics

A total of 141 patients were included in the study (Table I), most of whom initiated treatment between 2018 and 2019 when lanadelumab became available in the United States; all 141 patients were included in interval 1. Patients had a mean ± SD age of 41.4 ± 14.6 years at the start of the study period (Table II). Most patients were female (85 of 141 [60.3%]), White (95 of 141 [67.4%]), and lived in the South region of the United States (79 of 141 [56.0%]). Patients had a mean ± SD time since diagnosis of 17.9 ± 13.9 years, and most (123 of 141 [87.2%]) had HAE-C1INH-Type1. Among the 105 patients with baseline data, 2 (1.9%) had received an investigational drug and 13 (12.4%) had received another LTP (7 had received Cinryze [Takeda Manufacturing Austria AG] and 6 had receive Haegarda [CSL Behring GmbH]) prior to starting lanadelumab.

Lanadelumab exposure and persistency

The mean duration on lanadelumab was over 3.5 years (mean ± SD treatment time 1305 ± 567 days; median treatment time 1446 days [IQR = 770-1812 days]). Nearly half of patients (69 of 141 [48.9%]) were receiving lanadelumab for ≥4 years and nearly 25% for ≥5 years (34 of 141 [24.1%]); Table II). The mean ± SD durations of follow-up intervals 1, 2, 3, and 4 were 376 ± 426 days, 267 ± 163 days, 286 ± 154 days, and 283 ± 147 days, respectively. Twenty-one patients were lost to follow-up after interval 1, 24 patients after interval 2 and 16 after interval 3. In all, 9 patients had a confirmed discontinuation of lanadelumab over the 4 intervals, an overall discontinuation rate of 2.1%. Non–mutually exclusive reasons for discontinuation of lanadelumab treatment in these 9 patients were adverse events (n = 4), lack of efficacy (n = 3), other reasons (n = 5), payer or payment issues (n = 1), and unknown (n = 2). There were 51 patients (36.2%) whose dosing interval was extended (eg, to dosing every 4 weeks [Q4W]), with a mean ± SD time from treatment period initiation to time of extended dosing of 505 ± 330 days (median 386 days [IQR = 268-677 days]). Among patients who extended dosing intervals during the observation period, 28.9% extended in interval 1, 37.8% in interval 2, 28.9% in interval 3, and 4.4% in interval 4.

HAE attack rate

The 141 patients had a total of 307 follow-up intervals with fully populated attack data and an additional 130 follow-up intervals with partial outcome data. The mean ± SD monthly attack rates were 0.89 ± 4.27 in the 105 patients who had attack rate data before lanadelumab initiation and 0.21 ± 0.43, 0.17 ± 0.40 (P = .374 vs interval 1), 0.18 ± 0.59 (P = .688 vs interval 1), and 0.16 ± 0.38 (P = .399 vs interval 1) for intervals 1 to 4, respectively (Fig 2). The proportion of patients who were attack-free increased from 40.4% during interval 1 to 52.5% during interval 4 (Table III and Fig 4). At 90% confidence level thresholds, the percentage of patients who were attack-free was significantly higher in interval 3 compared with the first interval (40.4% vs 52.1% [P = .077]) and this significance was maintained in interval 4 (40.4% vs 52.5% [P = .083] [Fig 3]). A total of 25% of the patients (20 of 80) remained attack-free from interval 1 through interval 4.

During interval 1, the proportions of patients in the categories for 0, >0 to <2, 2 to <4, 4 to <8, and ≥8 attacks per year were 40%, 30%, 11%, 13%, and 6%, respectively. In interval 4, the corresponding proportions for each group were 53%, 24%, 13%, 4%, and 8% (Fig 4). Patients from each attack rate category became attack-free in a later interval, including patients in the highest disease activity group (≥8 attacks per year). The overall probabilities of becoming attack-free in a follow-up interval from the categories 0, >0 to <2, 2 to <4, 4 to <8, and ≥8 attacks per year were 24.3%, 32.5%, 22.9%, and 33.3%, respectively. Patients who were attack-free had an 80.3% chance of remaining attack-free in the following interval. Additionally, over half of the patients who were lost to follow-up were attack-free (53.1%). Patients with <4 attacks per year who had a subsequent follow-up interval had a 91.2% probability of maintaining or improving their attack rate in the next follow-up interval. The percentage of patients with <4 attacks per year improved from 82% to 89% from interval 1 to interval 4. Patients with 4 to <8 attacks per year who had a subsequent follow-up interval had a 77.3% probability of moving to an improved attack rate in that interval, whereas the proportion of patients with ≥8 attacks per year remained relatively stable from interval 1 to 4. However, among patients with subsequent follow-up intervals, only 1 remained in the category of ≥8 attacks per year.

Rescue medication, emergency department visits, and hospitalizations

A numerical decrease was observed in the proportion of patients that required rescue medication for attacks across intervals (Fig 5). Few patients required more extensive support during an attack. HAE attacks requiring treatment in the emergency room or hospitalization or both occurred in 2.1% of patients (3 of 141) during interval 1. During intervals 2 to 4, treatment in the emergency room occurred among 0% to 1.3% of patients and hospitalization occurred among 0.8% to 1.3%.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study used data collected by physicians in the CIIC, aggregated in the CIIC/Trio Health HAE database, to assess long-term use and attack rates among patients who had received lanadelumab for at least 6 months. It represents one of the few longitudinal studies of patients receiving lanadelumab LTP published to date to include patients on lanadelumab treatment for more than 4 years,26,29 and, we believe, the first to include patients treated for more than 5 years. Throughout the study, lanadelumab discontinuation rates were low, monthly attack rates remained low and stable, and the percentage of patients who were attack-free increased over time. Although monthly attack rates were reduced compared with pre–lanadelumab baseline, the data set also provided evidence of incremental improvement in disease control over successive years of lanadelumab treatment among patients with a range of baseline attack rate profiles, highlighting the broad effectiveness and durability of lanadelumab LTP. Patients who experienced attack-free intervals were more likely to continue to experience attack-free intervals during long-term lanadelumab treatment. Most patients maintained a similar attack rate throughout all 4 follow-up intervals. However, many patients in the category of 4 to <8 attacks per year in interval 1 ended up in a lower attack rate category by interval 4, and the overall percentage of patients who were attack-free increased. This suggests that patients may continue to experience a decrease in annual attack rates during long-term lanadelumab treatment. The improvement over time was especially noted in some patients with moderately high attack frequencies during interval 1, who became attack-free after longer periods receiving lanadelumab treatment.

Monthly attack rates reported from this analysis align with previously published real-world data on lanadelumab treatment outcomes among patients from the United States with HAE.23,25,26,30 In the Adelphi HAE Disease Specific Program,23 which reported on 51 patients with HAE-C1INH who received lanadelumab for 3 or more years, the proportion of patients experiencing a mean of ≥1 attacks per month decreased from 54.0% pre-lanadelumab to 9.8% at the time of the survey.26 In the prospective, noninterventional multicenter EMPOWER Study, the mean (95% CI) monthly attack rate for 90 patients from the United States and Canada with HAE-C1INH established on lanadelumab LTP was 0.20 (0.1-0.3) for a mean ± SD treatment duration of 907 ± 469 days.25

Overall, data from this CIIC study add to the evidence from real-world practice demonstrating sustained reduction in attack rates and an increase in attack-free patients over time among patients treated with lanadelumab, which was also demonstrated in the phase 3 HELP OLE long-term extension clinical trial.20

Over one-third of patients (36%) in our CIIC study had extended interval lanadelumab dosing, indicating treatment persistence and allowed patients to achieve stable disease control, making them eligible for less frequent dosing. The second part of the US panel–based chart review study analyzed 75 patients with HAE who had their dosing interval extended from every 2 weeks (Q2W).30 Nearly all of the patients who extended did so due to well-controlled disease, with a median (95% CI) time to first dose extension of 6.5 months (5.1-7.4 months). In the EMPOWER Study, patients established on lanadelumab treatment who extended from Q2W to Q4W dosing (15%) maintained a low mean (95% CI) monthly attack rate of 0.01 (0.00-0.02) attacks per month in the Q2W period and 0.02 (0.00-0.05) for the Q4W dosing period.25 Additionally, patients who extended dosing intervals appeared to have a slightly lower mean ± SD body mass index (29.4 ± 6.3 mg/kg2) than patients who remained on Q2W dosing (32.0 ± 8.5 mg/kg2). In the INTEGRATED Study, patients who extended dosing intervals (72.7%) tended to have a lower mean ± SD number of pre-lanadelumab attacks (34.1 ± 31.8 attacks) than patients who remained on Q2W dosing (40.6 ± 36.7 attacks).24 Attack rates before lanadelumab initiation could provide important information regarding patients who may be successful with dosing interval extension.

There are limitations to this study related to issues inherent in the CIIC Health database and the resulting study design. Due to the method of data collection, follow-up times to collect data were not standardized as occurs in clinical trials. As such, interval 1 was longer than the following intervals, allowing for more time when patients could have experienced attacks. If time frames were similar, there may have been reduced variability in the proportion of attack-free patients. The data were also limited by patient reporting, which is subject to recall bias and lacked specific information about events. Missing or censored data on attack counts limited data analysis, which required imputation, thus increasing the risk of bias. The censored attack counts refer to intervals whereby the patient reports that “at least 1 attack” occurred but did not report the specific number of attacks that occurred. As a result, precision in attack counts was lost and imputation (MICE) was used to derive the attack rates. Various assumptions were tested around imputation (eg, assuming that “≥1” was equal to exactly 1 attack), but MICE was most appropriate due to the nature of missingness. Only including patients who received lanadelumab treatment for at least 6 months may have biased selection to patients who were successfully treated with lanadelumab. However, this was needed to ensure adequate follow-up time and an opportunity to assess patients who reduced dosing intervals over time. While the reasons for dosing interval extension of lanadelumab treatment were not captured, it is not unreasonable to expect that the majority were stable on Q2W given the labeled indication that dose interval extension can be considered in patients who are stable and attack-free. The selection of only patients seen at CIIC practices may have biased the patient population to those receiving an elevated standard of care for HAE compared with the general population of patients with HAE. This specialized care setting could lead to lower health care resource utilization, namely hospitalizations and emergency department visits, if the patient’s HAE was managed differently than it would be at a less specialized clinic. Data collection by practitioners at CIIC clinics could also be more reliable, as it was being recorded by physicians experienced with managing patients with HAE. Additionally, future researchers might consider assessing the similarities in outcomes between patients who reduce their lanadelumab dosing frequency and those who do not.

Clinical implications

During up to 5 years of lanadelumab treatment, patients with HAE on lanadelumab for at least 6 months had low attack rates, few discontinued treatment, and attack-free patients increased numerically over time, indicating incremental improvements.

Conclusion

This study using longitudinal real-world data from CIIC clinics includes some of the longest follow-up times for patients on lanadelumab LTP treatment for HAE compiled to date, with most patients initiating treatment between 2018 and 2019, when lanadelumab was first available in the United States. Additionally, this study reports individual changes in patient outcomes over time. Patients on lanadelumab for at least 6 months had a low treatment discontinuation rate, suggesting satisfaction with their LTP therapy. The monthly attack rate was stable from initiation to the end of follow-up (a mean overall duration of 3.5 years), with an increase in the percentage of attack-free patients over time. Many of the patients on lanadelumab continued to experience reductions in attack rates over time, regardless of their attack rate profile at baseline. The study results demonstrate long-term effectiveness and treatment persistence in a real-world setting, and may be an important consideration for US providers and patients when selecting individualized options for the management of HAE with LTP.
