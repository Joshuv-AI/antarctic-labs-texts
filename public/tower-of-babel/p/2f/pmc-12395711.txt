Morphological diversity assessment in date palm (Phoenix dectylifera L.) germplasms grown under hot arid region of India
Authors: Ramkesh Meena, M. K. Chaudhary, P. S. Gurjar, Jagadish Rane, A. K. Singh, Lalu Prasad Yadav, K. L. Kumawat, Dhurendra Singh, B. R. Chaudhary, M. K. Jatav, R. S. Singh, Manzer H. Siddiqui, Saud Alamri, Shahbaz Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDate palm (Phoenix dactylifera L.) is a vital fruit crop cultivated in hot, arid regions due to its economic, nutritional, and ecological significance. Understanding the morphological diversity among different genotypes is crucial for breeding, conservation, and improving yield potential. The current study aimed to assess the phenotypic diversity of 37 date palm genotypes from 27 accessions grown in India's arid climate, focusing on 26 morphological traits.ResultsThe study employed statistical analyses such as ANOVA, correlation analysis, and principal component analysis (PCA) to evaluate trait variability and their contributions to yield improvement. Key traits like leaf length (CH1), leaflet length (CH2), bunch length (CH9), bunch weight (CH15), fruit weight (CH16), and pulp weight (CH20) exhibited significant genetic variation. Correlation analysis revealed strong positive associations between bunch weight (CH15) and key yield components, including yield per plant (CH26), number of bunches per plant (CH7), and fruit traits like weight (CH16), width (CH18), and length (CH17). Additionally, negative correlations were observed, such as stone weight (CH19) with the pulp-to-stone ratio (CH21) and total soluble solids (CH25), highlighting trade-offs in fruit composition. The impact of both genetic and environmental factors on trait expression was demonstrated by PCA, which further separated genotypes such as Medjool (MDL) and Tayer (TYR) based on fruit weight and yield, respectively. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated that PC1, PC2, and PC3 accounted for a significant portion of the total variation, with leaf length, bunch length, fruit weight, and pulp weight being key contributors. Additionally, cluster analysis grouped genotypes into two major clusters, identifying genetically similar accessions such as Gizej and Sakaloti, as well as Binet-A-Isha and Tayer, which could serve as promising breeding material.ConclusionThese findings emphasize the rich morphological diversity within date palm accessions and highlight the potential of key morphological traits in breeding programs aimed at enhancing fruit quality and yield stability under hot-arid conditions.

Introduction

The date palm (Phoenix dactylifera L.), a dioecious and perennial plant, belongs to the Arecaceae family and is one of the oldest fruit crops, cultivated since 6,000 BC along the Tigris and Euphrates rivers in present-day Iraq and later in the Mohenjo-Daro civilization along the Indus River. Date palms are grown in warm dry and semi-arid locations such as South and West Asia, North Africa, sections of Central and South America, Southern Europe, India, and Pakistan. They originated in the desert oases of the Gulf region [1]. Because of its rich nutritional profile, the fruit is considered a vital component for food security and is a significant source of carbohydrates, mostly glucose and fructose [2–5].

Their cylindrical, unbranched stems that can grow up to 30 m in height distinguish date palms. Mature trees can have a crown of 100–120 green leaves, each of which has several pinnae [6]. Depending on the cultivar, leaf position, and environmental conditions, the leaves and their constituent parts—the petiole, leaflets, and spines—display significant variety [7]. Male trees are usually needed for successful pollination, and only female date palms produce fruit. The process of growing date palms for fruit production includes choosing trees according to desired qualities, such as fruit size, quality, and post-harvest attributes, which are crucial for both domestic consumption and international trade [8].

Considering the challenges of genetic erosion, pest infestations, and environmental pressures, date palm genotypes are crucial for advancing morphological diversity studies and promoting genetic conservation and improvement. To evaluate the diversity and classification of date palm cultivars, studies have used both phenotypic data [9–11] and molecular markers [12, 13]. Understanding how different cultivars adapt to various agro-climatic conditions especially in dry locations like the Thar Desert requires an understanding of phenotypic diversity, which includes differences in vegetative and reproductive features. Date palm genetic diversity is now well understood thanks to recent developments in multivariate analytic methods like Principal component analysis (PCA). PCA facilitates the discovery of critical characteristics linked to quality and productivity [14]. Current studies indicate that specific morphological characteristics, such as leaf size and fruit attributes, serve as critical indicators of productivity under harsh environmental conditions [15, 16].

Furthermore, the date palm's economic and nutritional importance is growing worldwide, and it remains a crucial crop in arid and semi-arid countries. Enhancing the production and resilience of this crop, especially in areas with environmental limits, requires ongoing research centred on genetic variety, phenotypic characterisation, and sustainable growing approaches.

Material and methods

The study was conducted for 37 different accessions/cultivars of date palm under the hot arid climatic conditions at ICAR- Central Institute for Arid Horticulture, Bikaner, India (Table 1). The accessions of date palm are being maintained in field repository at ICAR- Central Institute for Arid Horticulture, Bikaner, India. The ICAR-CIAH, Bikaner is a National Active Germplasm Site (NAGS) for arid horticultural crops. The introduced and local materials were collected from various date palm research centers across India, while exotic materials were obtained through ICAR-NBPGR, New Delhi, Government of India. The collected genotypes have been maintained at the National Date Palm Field Repository and the National DUS Centre for Date Palm at ICAR-CIAH, Bikaner. For the morphological study, twenty-six vegetative and reproductive traits were used for the assessment of variation among cultivars (Fig. 1) and germplasm block with fruiting behaviour (Fig. 2; A- E). Data analysis

The experiment was analyzed using a one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) conducted within a randomized block design (RBD), Pairwise comparisons of treatment means were performed using Duncan's Multiple Range Test (MRT) at 5% and 1% significance levels, with critical difference (CD) values reported for each trait. The variance components and coefficients of variation were computed [17]. The heritability in a broad sense and expected genetic advance were determined by using the formula [18]. The coefficient correlation among all possible character combinations at phenotypic and the genotypic level were estimated employing formula [19]. Coefficients of variation were calculated as indicators of variability. RStudio (version 2023.3.0 +) tools were used for Data processing and graphical visualization. Principal component analysis (PCA) and facetted density plots were performed using the most current available versions of the FactoMineR, factoextra, and ggplot2 packages, drawing on fundamental methods by Hotelling [20] and Pearson [21]. Cluster analysis was conducted with the cluster, factoextra, dendextend, and ggplot2 programs following Sokal & Michener [22]. Variance analysis used the variability and agriculture packages, while correlation analysis was conducted with the corrplot package [23].

This suggests that future breeding or genetic research may concentrate on attributes with more variability.

Notably, fruit weight (CH16) emerged as a central trait, showing very strong correlations with pulp weight (CH20, r = 0.990), fruit width (CH18, r = 0.894), pulp thickness (CH24, r = 0.826), and pulp/stone ratio (CH21, r = 0.806). These findings suggest that heavier fruits are generally wider, contain more pulp, and possess a better pulp-to-stone ratio, making them more desirable for both fresh consumption and processing. Similarly, fruit length (CH17) was strongly associated with stone length (CH22, r = 0.896) and pulp weight (CH20, r = 0.713), indicating that larger fruits tend to have larger stones but also improved edible content.

In terms of yield optimization, the number of bunches per plant (CH7) had strong positive correlations with bunch length (CH9, r = 0.618) and overall yield (CH26, r = 0.817), while bunch weight (CH15) showed the strongest correlation with yield (r = 0.923). Other yield-influencing traits included the number of harvested fruits per strand (CH14, r = 0.612) and the number of strands per bunch (CH10), which was moderately correlated with both bunch weight (r = 0.603) and bunch length. These findings highlight that yield is a composite outcome of bunch architecture, fruit set, and fruit development traits.

Additionally, some developmental dynamics were evident; for example, a strong correlation between the number of fruits at the pea stage (CH12) and fruit drop (CH13, r = 0.770) implies the importance of managing early fruit retention to ensure higher harvestable yield. Overall, the matrix underscores the importance of selecting for traits like fruit weight, pulp characteristics, and bunch structure to improve both yield and quality. These correlations can guide breeders in targeting key traits that simultaneously enhance productivity and fruit marketability.

Discussions

Morphological traits and their predictive potential

The analysis of 26 morphological traits across 37 diverse date palm (Phoenix dactylifera L.) accessions in the Thar Desert of India provides critical insights into the utility of leaf traits as predictors of fruit yield and quality. Morphological characterization, particularly of leaf attributes such as leaf length (CH-1), leaflet length (CH-2), and spine presence, has long been a cornerstone for identifying and differentiating date palm cultivars [10, 24, 25]. However, these traits are subject to significant environmental modulation, as noted by Munier [26] which complicates their use as stable predictors. The present study addresses this challenge by quantifying the associations between leaf traits and yield components, leveraging correlation and multivariate analyses to dissect the polygenic nature of yield [2, 3].

Leaf length (CH-1) exhibited a highly significant positive correlation with bunch length (CH-9, r = 0.72, P < 0.01), bunch weight (CH-15, r = 0.68, P < 0.01), leaflet length (CH-2, r = 0.65, P < 0.01), and the number of strands per bunch (CH-10, r = 0.61, P < 0.01). These associations align with the physiological role of leaves as primary photosynthetic organs, where increased leaf surface area enhances carbon assimilation, thereby supporting greater fruit production [27]. Comparable findings in date palms [16] and other crops like bael (Aegle marmelos), ivy gourd (Coccinia grandis) and aonla (Phyllanthus emblica) conserved mechanism across species, where leaf morphology [28–34] suggest a conserved mechanism across species, where leaf morphology directly influences reproductive output. However, the strength of these correlations may vary due to environmental factors such as water availability and temperature extremes in the Thar Desert, which could amplify or suppress trait expression compared to studies in less arid regions [35].

Leaf traits and yield optimization in arid ecosystems

The study’s results underscore that date palm genotypes with longer leaves and larger leaflets are more productive in the Thar Desert’s arid conditions, yielding higher fruit quantities and superior quality. This is evidenced by the positive correlations between leaf size and bunch characteristics, including bunch length (r = 0.72, P < 0.01), bunch weight (r = 0.68, P < 0.01), and strands per bunch (r = 0.61, P < 0.01), corroborated by principal component analysis (PCA). The first principal component (PC1), explaining 38.7% of the variance, was heavily loaded with yield per plant (CH-26, loading = 0.89) and bunch weight (CH-15, loading = 0.82), reinforcing their role in productivity [29]. These findings are consistent with prior research highlighting the direct link between leaf-driven photosynthesis and fruit yield [8, 36, 37].

The superior performance of genotypes with enhanced leaf traits in arid environments may be attributed to their ability to maximize photosynthetic efficiency under limited water availability. Larger leaves increase the photosynthetic surface, potentially compensating for reduced stomatal conductance under drought stress [38]. However, this contrasts with studies in more temperate climates, where excessive leaf size may lead to water loss and reduced yield under high evaporative demand [39]. The Thar Desert’s extreme conditions likely select for genotypes with balanced leaf traits that optimize both water use efficiency and carbon assimilation, a hypothesis warranting further physiological studies.

Thorn and stone traits: balancing defence and resource allocation

Thorn characteristics, such as length (CH-5) and density, play a dual role in date palms by providing defense against herbivores and environmental stress while potentially competing for resources with fruit production [40]. The positive correlation between thorn length and fruit weight (CH-16, r = 0.58, P < 0.05) and pulp weight (CH-20, r = 0.55, P < 0.05) suggests that thorns, as modified leaf structures, may contribute to photosynthetic capacity indirectly [39]. This is a novel finding, as previous studies have primarily emphasized thorns’ protective role rather than their photosynthetic potential [38]. However, excessive thorn development could divert assimilates from fruit production, as suggested by resource allocation trade-offs [41]. Optimizing thorn traits in breeding programs could thus enhance yield by reducing resource competition while maintaining stress tolerance.

Stone size (CH-22) showed positive correlations with fruit length (CH-17, r = 0.62, P < 0.01), fruit weight (r = 0.59, P < 0.01), and pulp weight (r = 0.57, P < 0.01), indicating its influence on fruit morphology and yield components [16]. However, stone size had minimal impact on pulp yield, aligning with findings in other crops where stone traits primarily affect fruit texture rather than edible biomass [42]. The positive association between stone and fruit size may reflect genetic linkage or pleiotropy, where genes controlling stone development also influence fruit growth [8, 29]. These relationships highlight the need for careful selection to balance stone size with pulp yield in cultivar development.

Correlation analysis

Correlation analysis revealed strong positive associations between yield per plant and key traits, including fruit weight (r = 0.78, P < 0.01), fruit width (r = 0.73, P < 0.01), pulp weight (r = 0.76, P < 0.01), pulp-to-stone ratio (r = 0.69, P < 0.01), and bunch weight (r = 0.82, P < 0.01). These findings confirm the polygenic nature of yield and the utility of component traits for indirect selection [43]. The significant correlations align with previous studies on date palm diversity [9, 11, 44, 45], which reported high variability in reproductive traits across cultivars. Notably, negative correlations between yield and total soluble solids (TSS, CH-25, r = −0.53, P < 0.05) and stone weight (r = −0.49, P < 0.05) suggest a trade-off between fruit quantity and quality, where high-yielding genotypes may produce less sweet or smaller-stoned fruits [15].

The strong association between bunch weight and yield (r = 0.82, P < 0.01) positions it as a primary selection criterion, as it integrates multiple reproductive traits. This is supported by Bodian et al. [46, 47] who identified bunch characteristics as reliable indicators of yield potential. The interplay between vegetative and reproductive traits, such as the correlation between leaf length and bunch length (r = 0.72, P < 0.01), further suggests that selection for enhanced vegetative growth could indirectly improve yield. These insights provide a robust framework for breeding programs targeting high-yielding, stress-tolerant cultivars.

Principal component analysis: unravelling phenotypic diversity

PCA elucidated the key traits driving phenotypic variation among the 37 accessions, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 38.7% and 21.4% of the total variance, respectively. PC1 was dominated by yield per plant (loading = 0.89), bunch weight (loading = 0.82), and fruit weight (loading = 0.79), while PC2 was influenced by leaflet length (loading = 0.71) and stalk length (loading = 0.68). Negative loadings for TSS (loading = −0.62) and thorn density (CH-4, loading = −0.58) on PC1 indicate their inverse relationship with high-yielding cultivars [29]. These results align Karim et al. with [48] who used PCA to identify yield-related traits in date palms.

Genotypic differences were evident, with Medjool (MDL) characterized by large fruit weight and length, contrasting with Tayer (TYR), which exhibited reduced bunch and fruit weights under arid conditions [15]. These variations likely reflect genotype-by-environment interactions, where heat and drought stress differentially affect trait expression [28]. PCA thus serves as a powerful tool for identifying traits for targeted breeding, particularly in harsh environments.

Cluster analysis: genetic relationships and breeding implications

Significant genetic difference was found in the hierarchical cluster analysis of 37 date palm genotypes based on 26 physical and phenotypic variables. The genotypes were arranged into two main groups, Cluster I (16 genotypes) and Cluster II (21 genotypes). Subsequent divisions within each core cluster revealed complex patterns of trait similarity that were probably influenced by both environmental adaptation and genotypic background.

Cluster I: low yielding but resource efficient genotypes

Fruit-related measures such as fruit length (FLM), fruit weight (FWM), and fruit width (FMW) were lower in Cluster I, especially sub-cluster Ia (Sawi, Dhakki, Punjab Red), suggesting a propensity for smaller and lighter fruits. Reduced vegetative parameters including leaf length (LL) and total number of leaves (TL) were also shared by these genotypes, which may indicate adaptive flexibility to resource-scarce conditions common in arid regions like the Thar Desert. Such compact morphotypes are indicative of a survival-oriented strategy that prioritizes metabolic efficiency over fruit bulk. This trend is also observed in cultivars of other desert species, such as Ziziphus spp. and Phoenix dactylifera, that have adapted to arid conditions [49, 50].

Sub-cluster Ib genotypes (e.g., Binize, Sakaloti) showed moderate variability with intermediate productivity traits, such as bunch weight (BW) and productivity ratio (PR). This cluster potentially harbors genotypes with balanced growth and yield, suitable for dual-purpose breeding—targeting both environmental resilience and acceptable fruit quality.

Cluster II: high yielding genotypes

In contrast, Cluster II genotypes, especially those in sub-cluster IIa (Medjool, Hayani), exhibited consistently higher values for fruit traits (FLM, FWM, FMW), aligning with their commercial reputation for superior size and taste. These genotypes also performed well in vegetative vigor traits (number of bunches, NB), indicating a synergistic combination of reproductive and vegetative robustness. The co-expression of these traits positions Cluster IIa genotypes as ideal candidates for intensive cultivation under managed inputs, where their full yield potential can be realized.

Sub-cluster IIb (e.g., Shamran, Nagal) revealed more heterogeneity, with certain genotypes showing high kernel weight (KG) but relatively smaller fruit size, indicating divergent selection pressures. This variation may reflect distinct resource allocation strategies—favoring seed development in certain ecotypes, possibly as a reproductive assurance mechanism under terminal drought or heat stress. Such genotypes can be valuable for breeding programs targeting abiotic stress tolerance and seed-to-fruit biomass efficiency.

Vegetative traits like LL, TL, and NB formed a separate cluster, suggesting that vigorous leaf growth is associated with higher reproductive capacity—a finding echoed in earlier studies on date palm and other perennials [9, 11, 46, 47]. Meanwhile, bunch weight (BW) and kernel weight (KG) formed distinct clusters, emphasizing their unique roles in yield dynamics and post-harvest value.

Comparison with PCA

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) complements this clustering by identifying key traits driving variability. PCA would likely highlight fruit traits (FLM, FWM) and vegetative traits (LL, NB) as principal components, as they show the greatest variation across clusters. For example, Cluster IIa’s high fruit trait values would contribute significantly to the first principal component, while Cluster I’s vegetative trait patterns (e.g., smaller LL in Ia) would influence subsequent components, aligning with the heatmap’s clustering patterns.

This analysis underscores the phenotypic diversity among date palm genotypes, with Cluster I favoring resource-efficient traits and Cluster II emphasizing productivity. These insights are critical for breeding programs targeting specific traits, such as fruit quality or environmental resilience, particularly in arid regions like the Thar Desert.

Conclusion

The present study offers important new information about the agronomic potential and genetic variety of date palm genotypes grown in the dry Thar Desert. The results demonstrate that, even in the face of difficult circumstances like saline irrigation, date palm cultivars with long leaves and longer leaflets are extremely prolific and produce fruits of exceptional quality. This is explained by their beneficial effects on important characteristics that are essential for fruit production, such as bunch weight, bunch length, and the number of strands per bunch. Fruit size is favourably correlated with thorn length, although factors relating to leaf shape, including the number of thorns or the distance between thorns, do not directly correspond with productivity. Additionally, the study shows that although stone size affects fruit texture, it has no discernible effect on total fruit or pulp yield. This suggests that other factors, such as vegetative traits and environmental conditions, are more important in determining fruit quality and yield in date palm cultivation.
