Conserved stomatal regulation across winegrape cultivars challenges conclusions regarding hydraulic strategies
Authors: Charles O Obiero, Markus Keller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlants are often classified on a spectrum from isohydric to anisohydric depending on the extent of stomatal regulation of plant water status, but this concept remains controversial. We examined the stomatal behavior of 30 winegrape cultivars with diverse geographic origins and fivefold differences in canopy size. Using controlled irrigation in an arid climate, we imposed soil drydown cycles in each of two years, measured changes in soil moisture, leaf water potential (Ψleaf), and gas exchange, and calculated derived traits that are commonly used to describe plant hydraulic strategies.ResultsWhen measured against relative extractable soil water (θe), all cultivars maintained constant stomatal conductance (gs) as the soil dried from θe > 1 to 0.33, and decreased gs at θe < 0.33. When measured against predawn leaf water potential (Ψpd) as a proxy for soil water potential (Ψsoil), all cultivars began lowering gs at Ψpd ≈ -0.3 MPa (corresponding to θe ≈ 0.33 and Ψsoil ≈ -0.2 MPa) until the stomata were essentially closed at Ψpd < -0.7 MPa. As residual transpiration continued, leaf wilting began at Ψpd < -1.5 MPa. Thus, while the rate of soil drying varied depending on canopy size, stomatal regulation of Ψleaf was broadly conserved across cultivars, with common thresholds for stomatal closure and turgor loss. Leaf area-specific whole-plant hydraulic conductance followed the same patterns as gs but increased further as gs reached a plateau. Cultivar rankings differed for distinct traits used to assess and describe plant hydraulic strategy, and the 30 cultivars did not fall on a continuum from isohydric to anisohydric behavior for any measured trait.ConclusionIn winegrapes, stomatal regulation of Ψleaf is similar across cultivars, but cultivars might variously be classified as isohydric or anisohydric depending on the extent of water stress and the choice of driving variables. The similarity of stomatal behavior across 30 cultivars indicates limited potential for using cultivar choice or targeting gs in breeding as adaptation strategies to drought.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08682-9.Keywords: Isohydricity, Plant hydraulics, Plant water status, Soil moisture, Stomatal control, Water deficit

Introduction

Drought is a major abiotic factor that limits crop productivity by inducing plant water stress, which reduces growth and yield and compromises product quality. As climate change continues, many cropping environments are becoming drier, exacerbating the impacts of drought on agricultural production. Because of its importance to survival and reproduction, plants have evolved adaptive mechanisms to cope with water stress. A central component of drought adaptation lies in the regulation of stomatal control of the leaf transpiration rate. Stomata modulate a delicate balance between water conservation and carbon assimilation [1–3]. Based on how plants regulate stomatal conductance (gs) and, consequently, leaf water potential (Ψleaf) in response to declining soil water potential (Ψsoil), plants have been categorized as either isohydric or anisohydric [4–7]. Isohydric plants are thought to restrict gs early during water stress, thus conserving water and maintaining Ψleaf, while anisohydric plants maintain higher gs to enable gas exchange for photosynthesis at the expense of an increased risk of hydraulic failure. However, evidence supporting a strict dichotomy between isohydric and anisohydric behavior remains inconsistent. Recent research has suggested that physiological responses to water stress lie on a continuum across plant genotypes rather than in two distinct categories [8, 9]. Moreover, tighter stomatal regulation does not always correlate with improved Ψleaf [8–11], and genotypes may adjust their hydraulic strategy in response to varying soil moisture [12, 13]. Such plasticity implies that stomatal behavior might be broadly conserved across genotypes but modulated by soil moisture.

Grapevine (Vitis vinifera L.) cultivars provide an intriguing model for studying drought adaptation due to their genetic diversity and more than 11,000-year domestication history across diverse climates [13–16]. Grapevines have adapted to a wide range of environmental conditions, suggesting variability in stomatal behavior and regulation of Ψleaf under drought. For instance, cultivars with tighter stomatal regulation were reported to be associated with warmer climates, while cultivars with less tight stomatal control were linked to cooler regions [13]. Yet, cultivar behavior often varies across locations and growing seasons [5, 17–24], and some cultivars seemingly switch hydraulic strategies depending on soil moisture [12, 25]. For example, Merlot, Syrah, and Garnacha vines were described as anisohydric at high soil moisture, changing to isohydric as the soil dried [12, 26], although Syrah and Garnacha had previously been classified as anisohydric and isohydric, respectively [5]. In another study, all 17 cultivars investigated—including Syrah and Garnacha—responded anisohydrically (in terms of Ψleaf changes) to water stress despite a continuum of stomatal responses across cultivars [9].

The overall aim of our study was to reassess the classification of winegrape cultivars as isohydric or anisohydric under water stress. We previously described Ψleaf responses to soil drying of a diverse panel of 30 V. vinifera cultivars in a common garden experiment in an arid environment [27]. Here, we focused on the stomatal responses of the same cultivars over two growing seasons and used gs as the metric to assess isohydricity, whereby constant gs under soil drying was interpreted as an anisohydric strategy, and declining gs was interpreted as an isohydric strategy [4–6]. Following Levin et al. [9], we hypothesized that there is a continuum of stomatal responses to soil drying across winegrape cultivars. Our objective was to identify cultivars that could tolerate water stress despite permitting gas exchange to maintain productivity under the increasing drought conditions associated with climate change. We collected nearly 800 coupled measurements of soil moisture (ranging from above field capacity to nearly the permanent wilting point), plant water status, and leaf gas exchange. These data were then used to calculate derived physiological traits that are commonly used to characterize plant hydraulic strategies. Cultivar rankings for these traits were compared to assess whether they convey consistent information regarding hydraulic strategy. This study contributes to a growing body of evidence that challenges traditional classifications of drought response in grapevines. By integrating physiological measurements with environmental data and cultivar-specific traits, it underscores the importance of context in interpreting stomatal behavior and hydraulic regulation. Ultimately, a more nuanced understanding of drought adaptation will inform breeding and management strategies aimed at improving resilience in viticulture under increasingly variable climatic conditions.

Materials and methods

Site and plant material

A common garden experiment was conducted in 2021 and 2022 in a vineyard at Washington State University’s Irrigated Agriculture Research and Extension Center (46°17’49"N; 119°44’07"W; 365 m above sea level) in southeastern Washington, USA. The annual precipitation (< 200 mm) in this region is more than fivefold lower than the evapotranspiration (1040 mm; 20-year average for Roza.2 AgWeatherNet station; https://weather.wsu.edu). The soil is a Warden silt loam with a depth of about 50 to 100 cm above an impermeable hardpan [28]. The vineyard was planted in 2010 at a density of ~ 2000 plants per hectare with 30 own-rooted, virus-tested V. vinifera winegrape cultivars originating from diverse European regions with varied genetic backgrounds and published hydraulic strategies ranging from isohydric to strongly anisohydric (Table 1 in [27]). Triplicate groups of five plants of each cultivar, with each group located in a different row in each of three replicate blocks, were fully irrigated from budbreak to fruit set and then exposed to two successive drydown cycles interrupted by a rewatering event when plants began to show symptoms of severe water stress during early fruit ripening. Details of the vineyard layout, cultural practices, and experimental design are reported in [27].

Data collection

Pruning weight (i.e., weight of all dormant stems or canes removed during winter pruning) was determined on six plants per cultivar (n = 6) to provide a seasonally integrated measure of canopy size after the end of each growing season [27]. During the soil drydown periods, volumetric soil water content (θv) was measured biweekly at depths of 30 cm and 60 cm beneath three plants per cultivar (n = 3), using a neutron probe (Hydroprobe Am/Be model 503DR, CPN International, Concord, CA, USA). For comparison across different soil types, we converted θv to the unitless relative extractable soil water (θe) normalized to permanent wilting point (θpwp = 7% v/v) and field capacity (θfc = 26% v/v) [27]:

In addition, θv was also converted to Ψsoil as described elsewhere [27, 28]. In concert with θv measurements, the transpiration rate (E) and gs were measured on the same plants (n = 3) every 14 days during the first drydown cycle in both years. Measurements were conducted on fully expanded, sun-exposed leaves between the 7th and 15th node from the growing tip of the main shoot, using a portable infrared gas analyzer with a 6.25-cm2 broad leaf chamber and air flow rate of 240 mL min− 1 (LCi-SD, ADC Bioscientific, Hoddesdon, UK). Ambient air was drawn in ~ 2 m above the top of the vine canopy. All gas exchange measurements were conducted between 13:00 h and 15:00 h (Pacific Daylight Time), immediately before and on the same leaves as midday Ψleaf measurements. Data for daily midday ambient temperature (Tmd) and vapor pressure deficit (VPDmd), averaged for the time window during which physiological measurements were taken, were obtained from an on-site weather station (https://weather.wsu.edu). The Ψleaf at predawn (Ψpd) and midday (Ψmd) was measured with a pressure chamber (model 615D, PMS Instrument Company, Albany, OR, USA) as described [27]. Leaf area-specific whole-plant hydraulic conductance (Kplant) was estimated based on the evaporative flux method [29, 30]:

We used our θv-derived Ψsoil to calculate Kplant because Ψpd (the common proxy for Ψsoil) from our dataset underestimated Ψsoil at θe > 0.36 [28], and thus the slope of the regression between Kplant estimated from Ψpd and Kplant estimated from Ψsoil was > 1 (Additional file 1).

To assess common indicators of plant water use and water stress tolerance, we calculated the average maximum gs (gmax), and Ψpd and Ψmd at 50% and 90% stomatal closure (i.e., Ψpd50, Ψmd50, Ψpd90, Ψmd90) for each cultivar as described under Data analysis below. While a higher gmax of a cultivar generally enables higher rates of transpiration and photosynthesis, a higher Ψpd50, Ψmd50, Ψpd90, or Ψmd90 has been interpreted as indicating a more isohydric behavior [13, 31–33]. We also fitted the transition points Θ1 and Θ2 in the Ψpd—gs response curves. According to Knipfer et al. [34], Θ1 marks the boundary between mild and moderate water stress and corresponds to the Ψleaf at which stomatal closure occurs, whereas Θ2 (Ψpd = Ψmd) is the boundary between moderate and severe water stress and corresponds to the Ψleaf at which leaf turgor loss occurs.

Because the gasy of the θe—gs, Ψpd—gs, Ψmd—gs, and Kplant—gs relationships was sometimes overestimated due to the nature of the curves, we used the gasy from the θe—gs relationship as our gmax for uniformity in further calculations.

Results

Seasonal trends of stomatal conductance

The overall shape of the change in gs during soil drying across all 30 winegrape cultivars resembled that of the Ψleaf change [27]. In both years, gs increased during the first two weeks of soil drydown, then declined as the soil water deficit intensified. The decrease in gs occurred earlier in 2021 than in 2022 (when it was also initially more gradual), consistent with the difference between years in the decreases in θe and Ψleaf [27]. In 2021, gs dropped below 0.03 mol H2O m− 2 s− 1 in seven weeks, whereas in 2022, gs only declined to 0.08 mol H2O m− 2 s− 1 over 11 weeks (Fig. 1).

Response of stomatal conductance to soil drying using two definitions of soil moisture

The overall shape of the response of gs to θe across 30 cultivars was the same as that of the response of Ψleaf to θe [27], with a similar threshold of θe ≈ 0.33 above which gs became unresponsive to θe (Fig. 2). Note that θe reached values > 1 because measurements started immediately after irrigation, when the soil was still above field capacity. Despite high variability in gs at any level of θe and some variation in the responses of gs to θe between the two growing seasons, the general response pattern was the same across seasons (Fig. 2A and Additional file 2). Across the 30 cultivars, the average maximum gs (gmax) in the absence of water stress (i.e., the plateau of the θe—gs response curve) was slightly higher in 2022 (0.17 mol H2O m− 2 s− 1) than in 2021 (0.15 mol H2O m− 2 s− 1). However, the decline in gs under soil water stress was steeper in 2022 than in 2021, similar to the pattern observed for Ψpd (see Additional file 7 in [27]). While the relationship between gs and θe did not differ significantly (at 95% CL by Proc nlin Gauss-Newton method, Eq. 4) between Garnacha and Sémillon (Fig. 2B), the average gmax was 18–26% higher in Cabernet Sauvignon, Durif, and Tempranillo compared to Melon (Fig. 2 and Additional file 2). Except for the lower gmax of Melon, these differences in gs were not related to the differences in canopy size among the cultivars (Fig. 2 in [27]), and the gs response to θe did not differ between Garnacha—Sémillon and other cultivars (Additional files 3 and 4).

The average gmax in the response of gs to Ψpd was the same between years (Fig. 3A), between Garnacha and Sémillon (Fig. 3B), and between cultivars differing in canopy size (Fig. 3C) (at 95% CL by Proc nlin Gauss-Newton method, Eq. 5). However, there was a slightly later but steeper decline in gs as Ψpd decreased in 2022 than in 2021 (Fig. 3A and Additional file 5). Moreover, the Ψpd—gs response of the pooled Garnacha—Sémillon data was similar to that of the other cultivars (Additional files 6 and 7). The shape of the Ψpd—gs response curve differed from the Ψsoil—Ψleaf and Ψpd—Ψmd response curves (see Additional files 9 and 15 in [27]), resembling a sigmoid curve (i.e., Gompertz growth function) with three distinct phases (Fig. 3A): A saturation phase at Ψpd > -0.3 MPa (here defined as boundary Θ0, the onset of stomatal closure), where gs remained near gmax; an exponential decay phase at Ψpd between − 0.3 and − 0.7 MPa, where gs declined rapidly with increasing water stress, and a “lag” phase at Ψpd < -0.7 MPa once gs had dropped below 0.05 mol H2O m− 2 s− 1 (gmin). On average, 90% stomatal closure (boundary Θ1 in ref [34]). for the 30 cultivars and the two growing seasons occurred at Ψpd = -0.68 MPa. The variability in gs was generally highest at high Ψpd and progressively decreased as Ψpd declined below approximately − 0.5 MPa. When gs was plotted against Ψmd, a similar overall response was observed, but the values were shifted to the left and were more variable (Additional file 8), indicating Ψmd < Ψpd at any soil moisture level (see also Fig. 8 in [27]) in addition to variability in Ψmd and gs independent of soil moisture.

Stomatal conductance versus transpiration and plant hydraulic conductance

When the data from all 30 cultivars were pooled, the relationship between gs and E across the entire range of soil water content was slightly curvilinear (Fig. 4). The curvilinearity could be explained, in part, by the positive correlations between E, but not gs, and both VPDmd and Tmd, especially at high θe (Additional file 9). Moreover, the variability in E decreased progressively as gs declined and the stomata assumed stricter control of evaporative water loss. But even as gs approached zero, a residual E remained. The association between gs and E was similar across growing seasons (Fig. 4A). All cultivars had similar average gs and E either below or above a θe threshold of 0.33, above which the response of gs to θe plateaued out (Additional file 3); except that Barbera and Malbec had about 38% higher gs than Melon at θe > 0.33 (Table 1). Despite the limited variation among cultivars, the cultivar ranking for the average gs was correlated with that for E at θe > 0.33 (Spearman rank correlation coefficient, rs = 0.43; P = 0.01) and, especially, at θe < 0.33 (rs = 0.77; P < 0.001). Also, at θe < 0.33, the cultivar means for gs correlated strongly with those for E (Pearson correlation coefficient, rp = 0.93; P < 0.001). Consequently, cultivars with lower gs tended to have lower E, especially at low soil moisture. However, there was no clear pattern between cultivar origin (Table 1 in [27]) and the ranking of gs and E; even full siblings (e.g., Auxerrois, Chardonnay, and Melon) were distributed over the entire spectrum.

The relationship between gs and Kplant across 30 cultivars was also curvilinear. The Kplant—gs response curve resembled a Michaelis–Menten function, with gs leveling off as Kplant continued to increase (Fig. 5). The two seasons differed slightly but significantly despite high variability in both gs and Kplant (Fig. 5A and Additional file 10), reflecting the cooler temperature and lower VPD (Fig. 1 in [27]) and the higher θe (Fig. 3 in [27]) early in the 2022 growing season. The relationship between gs and Kplant did not differ between Garnacha and Sémillon (Fig. 5B and Additional file 10) or between cultivars differing in canopy size (Fig. 5C and Additional file 10). Moreover, the response of the pooled Garnacha—Sémillon data was also similar to that of the other cultivars (Additional files 11 and 12).

Leaf water potential at 50% and 90% stomatal closure, and cultivar rankings for different traits

Though both Ψpd and Ψmd at 50% and 90% stomatal closure seemingly varied somewhat among the 30 cultivars, none of this variation reached statistical significance (Additional file 13). Garnacha and Sémillon closed their stomata at similar Ψpd and Ψmd, and the full siblings Aligoté, Auxerrois, Chardonnay, and Melon were spread almost over the entire Ψpd and Ψmd ranges for stomatal closure. Thus, it was not possible to clearly group cultivars as isohydric (i.e., more responsive stomata) or anisohydric (i.e., less responsive stomata) or rank them on a continuum from isohydric to anisohydric, based on Ψpd50, Ψpd90, Ψmd50, or Ψmd90. Nevertheless, the average Ψpd90 across all cultivars was identical (-0.68 ± 0.02 MPa) to boundary Θ1 extracted from the overall Ψpd—gs response curve (Fig. 3).

Spearman’s rank correlation revealed poor relationships between the different traits commonly used to assess the hydraulic strategies of different plant genotypes (Fig. 6). For example, the cultivar ranking for Kplant50 and that for the slope of the Ψpd—Ψmd relationship did not correlate with any other trait. The cultivar ranking for gmax was only related to those for canopy size (rs = 0.43; P = 0.02) and the slope of the θe—Ψpd curve (rs = -0.37; P = 0.04). The cultivar ranks for some traits were correlated (e.g., slopes of θe—Ψpd and Ψsoil—Ψpd, or θe—Ψmd and Ψsoil—Ψmd) because the traits were interdependent (i.e., one trait was derived from another one). Similarly, the ranks for other traits (e.g., Ψpd50, Ψpd90, Ψmd50, and Ψmd90) were significantly correlated, but this was likely because these traits were themselves related. Therefore, the different traits commonly used to describe plant hydraulic strategies conveyed different messages that led to different conclusions. Finally, Pearson correlation analysis showed that the average cultivar gmax was associated with canopy size (rp = 0.44; P = 0.01; Additional file 14), suggesting that cultivars such as Aligoté, Durif, and Melon tended to have small canopies due to their low gs under well-watered conditions, rather than being more sensitive to water stress.

Discussion

Our two-year experiment conducted in an arid climate evaluated within-species variation in the response of winegrapes to water stress, using a panel of 30 V. vinifera cultivars originating from climatically diverse regions [27]. When volumetric soil moisture was normalized to field capacity and permanent wilting point, all 30 cultivars maintained near-constant gs under initial soil drying and dropped gs as the water deficit intensified at θe < 0.33. Therefore, though they varied nearly fivefold in canopy size, leading to variation in the rate of soil water depletion [27], all cultivars had a common θe threshold of 0.33 (roughly corresponding to a Ψsoil of -0.2 MPa, see ref [28]). at which they began to perceive water deficit and close their stomata. When Ψpd was used as a proxy for Ψsoil, gs also varied about its maximum at Ψpd > -0.3 MPa, then dropped as Ψpd declined to -0.7 MPa, remaining virtually unchanged below that threshold. Consequently, using the response of gs to soil drying as the metric for plant isohydricity [4–6, 19] leads to the problematic conclusion that all cultivars are both isohydric (declining gs) and anisohydric (constant gs), depending on the definition of soil moisture and the degree of water deficit. These results support the notion that the shape of plant response functions (gs here and Ψleaf in ref [27]). to soil moisture depends on the choice of θv or Ψsoil as the driving variable [37]. Moreover, the cultivar rankings for traits that are commonly used to assess plant hydraulic behavior (i.e., gmax, Ψpd50, Ψpd90, Ψmd50, Ψmd90, Kplant50, and slopes of θe—Ψleaf, Ψsoil—Ψleaf, and Ψpd—Ψmd curves) were poorly correlated with one another. These findings do not support our initial hypothesis of a continuum across cultivars of stomatal responses to soil drying. Instead, they suggest that using different definitions of soil moisture or measuring different plant response traits leads to contrasting conclusions regarding hydraulic strategies and their variation among different plant genotypes.

Our data led us to add boundary Θ0 to the Ψpd—gs response curve developed earlier [34]. Above Θ0 (Ψpd > -0.3 MPa), gs varied about its maximum, indicating a saturation phase where stomata were fully open and soil moisture was not limiting. Below Θ0, progressive stomatal closure led to increasingly tight control over transpiration as shown by the decline in both E and its variability. The Θ0 also corresponds to the θe threshold of 0.33 (Ψsoil ≈ -0.2 MPa) at which both Ψleaf [27] and gs began to drop in parallel as the soil dried down. Therefore, both measures of water status converged on the same threshold Θ0 marking the onset of stomatal closure during soil drying. Unsurprisingly, and despite differences in how soil moisture was expressed, this threshold resembles the one found for the onset of declining rates of transpiration of woody plant species, including grapevine (in which leaf appearance and expansion also begin to decline at this point), in drying soil [38–40]. Unlike those earlier transpiration results, however, our data also show that even though gs reached a plateau at high soil moisture, the transpiration rate and Kplant continued to increase due to VPD and temperature effects. While higher VPD and higher temperature raise the evaporative demand, we acknowledge that unsaturation (i.e., relative humidity < 100%) in the substomatal cavity at high VPD might lead to underestimation of gs [41]. The curvilinearity in the gs relationship with E and Kplant is consistent with previous reports and implies that cultivars, or genotypes more generally, may differ in their response to VPD and temperature when soil water is not limiting [42–44].

Because Ψpd, Ψmd, and gs of all cultivars followed similar trajectories during soil drying, stomatal closure did not prevent Ψleaf from declining in any cultivar, despite the large differences in canopy size among cultivars [27]. Conversely, no cultivar permitted Ψleaf to drop early to keep its stomata open, which further contradicts predictions of iso-/anisohydry theory in agreement with conclusions reached with different tree species [45]. Knipfer et al. [34] hypothesized that stomatal closure (i.e., gs → 0) caused a sudden change in the slope of the Ψpd—Ψmd curve from steep to gentle or flat (their boundary Θ1, marking the transition from mild to moderate plant water stress). Though their boundary Θ1 (-0.68 MPa) is identical to that identified here, the more gradual change of the Ψpd—Ψmd curve in our study [27] parallels the rather gradual decline of gs with decreasing Ψpd in all cultivars. The θe associated with boundary Θ1 seems to coincide with the threshold for the onset of berry shrinkage in grapevines in drying soil [46–47]. As is the case for cambial growth in the woody organs [48], insufficient water is likely available to sustain berry growth once the stomata close. Moreover, like the woody organs, the berries may also release some stored water to the leaves, though the capacitance of grape berries is very limited: a 1-g berry with 90% water content shrinking 20% loses < 0.2 g water; for a shoot with two 100-berry clusters, that equates to only 36 g of water. Slow but continued residual (cuticular and minimal stomatal, see ref [49]). transpiration following stomatal closure (i.e., gs < 0.05 mol H2O m− 2 s− 1 at Ψpd < -0.7 MPa) caused leaf water status to decline further towards boundary Θ2 (Ψpd = Ψmd ≈ -1.5 MPa) at which turgor loss began to result in leaf wilting and senescence (i.e., yellowing) as shown in [27]. Our boundary Θ2 is slightly lower than that (Ψpd = -1.33 MPa) reported previously [34]. We found no values of Ψpd < -1.5 MPa and contend that Ψpd values substantially below − 1.5 MPa, as often reported in the literature, are incompatible with the notion that Ψpd equilibrates with Ψsoil (see also ref [28]). if Ψsoil = -1.5 MPa is accepted as the permanent wilting point.

While the variation in canopy size among our 30 cultivars did not alter the general Ψpd—gs response pattern, we found a positive correlation between canopy size and maximum gs across cultivars. This suggests that genetic variation in photosynthetic capacity at high soil moisture, rather than differences in the tightness of stomatal control under intensifying water stress, drove the differences in growth capacity among cultivars. Though stronger stomatal regulation of evaporative water loss (i.e., more rapid decrease in gs with declining Ψpd) is thought to be the main reason for stiffer control of Ψleaf in isohydric plants as soil water becomes limiting, our cultivars did not differ in this respect. Consequently, though the overall shape of the Ψpd—gs response curve in our study agrees with previous reports [9, 19, 31, 34], our conclusions regarding hydraulic strategy differ. In earlier studies that used gs to categorize winegrapes as either isohydric or anisohydric, this was primarily based on visual differences in the shape of the θv—gs, Ψsoil—gs, or Ψpd—gs relationships [9, 19, 34, 50–52]. The reported differences and conclusions varied across those studies. Here, we collected measurements in the field and over two growing seasons, maintained plants clean of systemic pathogens, paired gas exchange measurements with measurements of soil moisture and plant water status, and statistically tested the θe—gs, Ψpd—gs, and Kplant—gs relationships. This analysis showed that the physiological responses to soil drying were modulated by the growing season but not by the cultivar.

Although the two growing seasons in our study were different, with the warmer and drier early season in 2021 being associated with lower average maximum gs and a less steep decline in gs with increasing soil water deficit compared to 2022, the θe—gs and θe—Ψleaf [27] response curves were similar. While our study was limited to a single site, the broadly similar results over two growing seasons suggest that environmental variation mostly shifts data points along a common plant response curve among cultivars in addition to causing some variability around that curve. Nevertheless, since the soil at our site was uniform across cultivar replicates, it would be desirable to test cultivar responses across diverse soil conditions in future field trials. Our conclusions may also be somewhat limited by the necessarily limited sample size (n = 3 per cultivar and measurement date), which might have missed some minor differences among cultivars, but they are strengthened by the high total number of measurements across two seasons, permitting robust correlation analysis. Indeed, the cultivar rankings for traits commonly used to describe plant hydraulic strategies were poorly correlated, similar to what has been reported for other species [8]. This confirms that measuring different traits thought to characterize plant behavior under water stress can lead to differing conclusions regarding plant hydraulic strategies.

Our findings have several implications: (1) Stomatal behavior of different winegrape cultivars cannot be neatly categorized into fixed isohydric or anisohydric strategies, nor does it follow a continuum from isohydric to anisohydric behavior across cultivars. The apparent hydraulic strategy depends on which plant response trait is measured and how soil moisture is defined and measured, and over what range. Therefore, growers should be cautious about relying on cultivar labels when making irrigation decisions. A common threshold of θe ≈ 0.33 (Ψsoil ≈ -0.2 MPa) or Ψpd ≈ -0.3 MPa emerged across cultivars as the point where stomata begin to close, providing a practical benchmark for irrigation scheduling to avoid overirrigation. (2) Residual water loss continues below the point of stomatal closure (gs < 0.05 mol H2O m− 2 s− 1 at Ψpd ≈ -0.7 MPa), which will ultimately result in leaf wilting (i.e., turgor loss at Ψpd = Ψmd ≈ -1.5 MPa) and abscission, thus providing another irrigation benchmark to avoid excessive water stress. (3) Because the stomatal response to soil drying seems to be broadly conserved across diverse winegrape cultivars, other traits such as shoot vigor (determining canopy size), rooting depth or architecture, or cuticular properties might be more promising targets for improving drought resilience in breeding programs. (4) Because our findings confirm that conclusions about plant hydraulic strategies can shift depending on the choice of driving and response variables, there is a need for multi-trait approaches and careful consideration of measurement ranges when interpreting physiological data. Taken together, this study encourages a more nuanced view of grapevine water stress responses, one that emphasizes plasticity and context over rigid classifications, and supports flexible, site-specific irrigation strategies in the face of climate change.

Conclusion

Applying controlled soil water deficit to 30 field-grown, own-rooted winegrape cultivars grown side-by-side in an arid climate showed that different cultivars respond similarly to soil water deficit despite large variation in geographic origin and canopy size. Our observations do not support the notion of a continuum of stomatal responses to water stress across cultivars. Instead, they suggest that stomatal closure (onset at Ψpd ≈ -0.3 MPa, completion at Ψpd ≈ -0.7 MPa) may be largely conserved across V. vinifera cultivars despite 11,000 years of selection and breeding in diverse climates. Moreover, while stomatal closure strongly slows evaporative water loss, it cannot prevent it completely, which leads to leaf wilting and abscission (turgor loss at Ψpd ≈ -1.5 MPa) as the soil dries towards the permanent wilting point. Our data also imply that using information about stomatal closure to infer differences in plant hydraulic strategies is problematic, as changes in both gs and Ψleaf depend on whether soil moisture is expressed as volumetric water content or water potential, and on the degree of water stress. Moreover, different commonly used traits to assess plant hydraulic strategies correlate poorly with one another across cultivars, which further undermines classification attempts. Because a cultivar may variously be classified as isohydric or anisohydric depending on variable choice and degree of water stress, physiological models must account for differences in such choices and measurement ranges to avoid misleading conclusions. A key practical implication of our findings is that they support the use of common physiological thresholds across grape cultivars in conjunction with differences in vigor, and hence canopy size, affecting water use for irrigation scheduling. The apparent lack of diversity in stomatal regulation, however, curtails its usefulness for genetic adaptation (i.e., breeding) as a mitigation strategy for intensifying drought episodes.
