From Breakthrough to Access: How Biotechnology and Artificial Intelligence are Reshaping the Scalability and Precision of Cancer Immunotherapy
Authors: Babak Jahangiri, Elahe Asadollahi, Alireza Zomorodipour
Published: 2026

Over the past decade, cancer immunotherapy has transformed oncology, enabling durable responses in previously intractable malignancies. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), adoptive cell therapies, and related strategies have demonstrated the power of harnessing the immune system to eliminate cancer ( 1 , 2 ). However, benefits remain uneven; many patients fail to respond or develop resistance ( 2 - 4 ). The field now stands at a critical inflection point where incremental improvements to existing platforms may yield diminishing returns unless they directly address resistance mechanisms. These include T-cell dysfunction, antigen loss, impaired antigen presentation, and immunosuppressive signaling within the tumor microenvironment (TME) ( 3 , 4 ).

While combination strategies involving anti-angiogenics, oncolytic viruses, microbiome modulation, metabolic inhibitors, or antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) are central to current research, many remain empiric ( 5 ). Without a clear biological rationale, such combinations risk increasing toxicity and cost without meaningful benefit. A more disciplined, hypothesis-driven approach is required. In recent years, advances in biotechnology have established immunotherapy as a cornerstone of cancer care through innovations in protein engineering, gene editing, synthetic biology, nanoparticle delivery, and artificial intelligence (AI)-based design ( 5 , 6 ). Specifically, AI offers opportunities to prioritize effective strategies, identify uncharacterized targets, and enable longitudinal modeling of tumor evolution. This allows for the early prediction of resistance and the guidance of adaptive therapeutic interventions ( 6 , 7 ).

Next-generation cellular therapies represent a major leap forward, powered by biotechnology. While chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapies have shown remarkable success in hematological malignancies, their limited efficacy in solid tumors highlights persistent barriers, including tumor hetero-geneity, immune evasion, and physical constraints such as the dense, desmoplastic stroma that impedes effective T-cell infiltration and persistence ( 3 , 8 ). To address these challenges, advanced cell engineering is being employed, incorporating multi-antigen targeting, activation with logic gates, and resistance to immunosuppressive signals ( 9 , 10 ).

Allogeneic CRISPR-edited cells and in vivo CAR generation platforms may further enhance scalability and accessibility of the therapies ( 1 , 5 ). Alternative platforms, such as natural killer (NK) cells and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), offer advantages in versatility, though challenges regarding persistence, trafficking, and functional durability remain. Increasingly, AI is being applied to optimize receptor design and functional tuning of these engineered cells ( 6 ).

The TME plays a central role in shaping therapeutic response. Immunosuppressive networks comprising regulatory immune cells, inhibitory cytokines, and metabolic constraints limit effective anti-tumor immunity ( 8 ). Remodeling the TME to enhance immune infiltration and reverse immunosuppression is essential. Crucially, TME-targeted strategies must be integrated into immunotherapy design rather than treated as adjuncts ( 8 ). Biotechnological innovations, such as nanoparticle-mediated localized delivery of cytokines, oncolytic viruses, and synthetic biology-designed immune engagers, aim to turn tumors ‘hot’ and maintain this immunologically active state ( 9 , 10 ).

Despite advances in genomic profiling and singlecell technologies enabling detailed characterization of tumor–immune interactions ( 5 ), translating these insights into robust predictive biomarkers remains challenging ( 3 , 8 ). AI will be central to this transition, enabling the integration of high-dimensional datasets to generate predictive models of treatment response and guide individualized therapy selection ( 6 ).

Emerging platforms, including mRNA-based systems, offer flexible approaches for cancer vaccines and immune modulation ( 5 ). Beyond general immune modulation, personalized multi-neoepitope vaccines are emerging as a potent form of individualized immunotherapy, leveraging patient-specific tumor mutations to induce highly targeted cytotoxic T-cell responses ( 11 ). While prolonged manufacturing lead times currently challenge rapid intervention, their greatest potential lies in rational combination with other therapies to bridge this gap and sustain long-term efficacy. More broadly, the convergence of immunology, synthetic biology, and computational tools is enabling increasingly sophisticated immune engineering, underpinning the development of next-generation immunotherapies ( 5 ).

High costs and manufacturing complexity currently hinder the widespread adoption of immunotherapy. Scalable allogeneic (“off-the-shelf”) cell therapies represent a promising strategy to improve accessibility and reduce economic burdens ( 8 , 12 ). Achieving global impact will require coordinated efforts across scientific, clinical, and policy domains.

In conclusion, cancer immunotherapy is at a turning point defined by integration, precision, and strategic prioritization. The challenge is no longer to demonstrate efficacy, but to achieve consistent, durable, and accessible responses. Biotechnology plays a central role in this evolution, with an expanding arsenal ranging from engineered ICIs and TILs to CRISPR-enhanced cell therapies. Progress will be driven by advanced drug delivery systems (lipid- and polymer-based nanoparticles) and synthetic biology approaches for generating ‘smarter’ immune cells. Enhancing immune cell access to tumors and overcoming barriers to effective immune infiltration and function, together with leveraging the convergence of bioengineering and artificial intelligence (AI), will be critical. Ultimately, the future of oncology hinges on our ability to translate high-dimensional biological data into precise, adaptive, and accessible clinical solutions.
