Cross-cultural adaptation and validation of the mindful eating behavior scale in community-dwelling older adults
Authors: Volkan Türkmen, Özge Mengi Çelik, Sena Dilşad Akçakaya, Berna Betül Cihan, Aslı Hilal Güzelalp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMindful eating is a fundamental approach for promoting healthy dietary behaviors and well-being in older age. However, there is currently no validated scale available to assess mindful eating specifically among older adults in the Turkish population. Therefore, a reliable instrument is needed to address this gap and enable accurate assessment of eating awareness in this demographic.AimThe primary aim of this study was to evaluate the validity and reliability of the Turkish version of the Mindful Eating Behavior Scale (MEBS-TR) in adults aged 65 years and older.MethodsThis methodological study included 424 individuals aged 65 and over (61.3% female) with a mean age of 70.87±6.15 years. Data were collected using a Personal Information Form, the MEBS, and the Mediterranean Diet Adherence Screener (MEDAS). The construct validity of the scale was assessed using Minimum Rank Factor Analysis (MRFA) with a polychoric correlation matrix and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) with Mean and Variance Adjusted Maximum Likelihood (MLMV) estimation. Concurrent validity was examined via Spearman's correlation between MEBS-TR and MEDAS scores. For reliability analysis, Cronbach's alpha (α) and McDonald's omega (ω) internal consistency coefficients were calculated, along with test-retest reliability using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC).ResultsThe MRFA results confirmed the original 17-item, four-factor structure (Focused Eating, Hunger and Satiety Cues, Eating with Awareness, and Eating without Distraction), which explained 81.81% of the total variance. CFA demonstrated that this four-factor model had acceptable model fit indices (χ2/df = 2.777, CFI = 0.941, TLI = 0.928, RMSEA = 0.065). The internal consistency coefficients for the total scale were found to be high (Cronbach’s α = 0.861, McDonald’s ω = 0.867). Test-retest reliability, assessed with 150 participants, was found to be excellent (ICC = 0.969). A positive and statistically significant relationship was found between the MEBS-TR total score and the MEDAS total score (rho = .163, p = .001).ConclusionThese findings demonstrate that the MEBS-TR is a valid and reliable instrument for assessing mindful eating behaviors among adults aged 65 years and older in Türkiye.

Introduction

The prevalence of obesity is rapidly increasing today, a situation associated with an elevated risk for cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, musculoskeletal disorders, and certain types of cancer. Although various dietary interventions are recommended for body weight management, mindful eating (ME) has recently garnered increasing interest as an alternative strategy [1]. Concurrently, the number of people aged 60 and over is rapidly growing worldwide, with this population expected to reach 1.4 billion by 2030 [2]. The aging process brings physiological, psychological, and social changes that affect nutritional behaviors. Factors such as reduced sensitivity to physical hunger-satiety signals, distraction, depression, and loneliness during old age can trigger negative eating behaviors. This situation leaves older adults more vulnerable to malnutrition and related diseases [3, 4].

ME is an approach that involves focusing on the moment of eating consciously, in the present, and without judgment. It emphasizes the sensory awareness and experience of food rather than calculations of calories, carbohydrates, or fats [5]. ME enhances an individual's interoceptive awareness—the capacity to perceive internal bodily signals such as hunger and satiety—and helps regulate eating behavior according to these signals [6]. Furthermore, ME behavior reduces individuals' reactivity to environmental triggers (e.g., advertisements, food packaging, or social settings), thereby limiting the tendency for uncontrolled eating driven by external stimuli [6]. Indeed, research has shown that mindfulness practices lead to changes in brain regions associated with emotional regulation, attention, and self-awareness [7, 8]. These findings support that ME is effective not only at the behavioral level but also through brain-based neurobiological processes.

As an intervention, the efficacy of ME is also noteworthy. ME interventions for individuals with eating disorders or obesity have been reported to improve anthropometric data, binge-eating episodes, body image dissatisfaction, eating habits, and quality of life [2]. Meta-analysis studies indicate that ME is not only a practical option for weight loss but also an equivalent alternative to dietary approaches based on restricted energy intake [9]. This is particularly important in light of ongoing discussions focusing on the behavioral aspects of excess body weight, such as impulsivity, lack of awareness of hunger-satiety cues, emotional eating, or eating under stress [10]. Indeed, it has been shown that adding a ME intervention to standard nutritional treatment for obese and overweight individuals over the age of 45 reduced emotional eating [11].

Despite the benefits of this approach, the ability to measure this concept is critical to investigating the effects of ME. The utility of measuring ME significantly expands the knowledge from experimental and longitudinal studies and allows for the objective measurement of performance/success and the validation of ME practices [12]. This measurement need holds special potential for the older adult population, one of the groups at nutritional risk. Mindfulness, or a state of full attention, is seen as a contributing factor to successful aging [13]. Assessing ME behaviors in older adults has the potential to contribute to the prevention of nutritional problems and the promotion of healthy aging in this population [14].

However, as ME is based on internal processes, it is a concept that is difficult to observe directly and is therefore predominantly measured through self-report questionnaires. Although various measurement tools are available for assessing ME in Türkiye, these scales often measure constructs that are intertwined with emotional eating or problematic eating behaviors [15–18]. This makes it difficult to measure awareness in its pure form, especially in older individuals.

In contrast, the Mindful Eating Behavior Scale (MEBS), specifically developed for older adults by Winkens et al. (2018) [19], was designed to overcome the conceptual limitations of previous instruments—such as the Mindful Eating Questionnaire (MEQ)—which frequently conflate mindful eating with external or emotional eating constructs. The scale was specifically designed for and validated in a large cohort of Dutch adults aged 55 and older, ensuring its developmental appropriateness for aging populations. It consists of 17 items distributed across four robust domains: Focused Eating, Hunger and Satiety Cues, Eating with Awareness, and Eating without Distraction. In its initial psychometric evaluation, the MEBS demonstrated strong internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the subscales ranging from 0.70 to 0.89, and its four-factor structure was supported by rigorous factor-analytic methods. Since its inception, the MEBS has gained international recognition as a specialized tool for assessing the attentional components of eating. It has undergone successful cross-cultural adaptations and validations in various languages, including Arabic and Greek, where it consistently maintained its structural integrity and reliability across different community-dwelling populations [20, 21]. While some critiques suggest that the scale focuses primarily on awareness rather than the non-judgmental acceptance facet of mindfulness, its high sensitivity to internal eating cues makes it a superior choice for clinical and research applications in older adults.

Türkiye, as an environment where the Mediterranean diet and deep-rooted eating traditions coexist, offers a significant opportunity for valuable cross-cultural research on eating behaviors [8]. The ability to maintain healthy and moderate eating habits in environments that lead to mindless overeating and obesity is an important way to contribute to the literature. In this context, the primary aim of this study is to perform the validity and reliability analysis of the Turkish form of the MEBS, which measures ME behavior in older adults. The valid and reliable Turkish MEBS form obtained from this study is expected to be an important measurement tool for evaluating the ME behaviors of older adults in Türkiye. Through this tool, it will be possible to lay the groundwork for promoting healthy eating behaviors and developing intervention programs for older adults.

Methods

Study design and methods

This study employed a methodological, cross-sectional design to evaluate the validity and reliability of the Turkish version of the Mindful Eating Behavior Scale (MEBS) in community-dwelling older adults. Eligibility criteria required participants to: (a) be 65 years of age or older, and (b) be able to speak and understand Turkish. Exclusion criteria included being non–community-dwelling or having a diagnosed neurological or psychiatric disorder that could impair cognitive function (e.g., dementia, Alzheimer's disease). The absence of these conditions was determined based on participants' self-reports and medical histories during the initial screening. While a standardized cognitive screening tool (e.g., Mini-Mental State Examination) was not administered, the researchers assessed participants' ability to understand instructions and provide coherent responses during the face-to-face sessions to ensure they could complete the self-administered questionnaires.

A total of 424 community-dwelling older adults residing in Ankara, Türkiye, voluntarily participated in the study. Initially, 25 participants were reached through the researchers’ personal networks; these individuals then invited and referred additional eligible participants to the study via a snowball sampling approach. Data were collected in person at the Faculty of Health Sciences between April 2025 and October 2025. These in-person sessions were conducted to ensure high data quality and to provide assistance with any literacy-related difficulties, given the demographic characteristics of the sample. Participants completed the questionnaires independently in a supervised setting; researchers, who are specialist dietitians in Nutrition and Dietetics, remained present to provide standardized instructions, answer procedural questions, and ensure data quality by checking forms for missing responses (without interpreting item content). Since data were collected during supervised, face-to-face sessions, all questionnaires were checked for completeness on-site, and no missing data were observed in the final sample. Anthropometric measurements were also obtained by the researcher following standard procedures. The total session duration was approximately 30 minutes, and no financial or other incentives were provided for participation. The cross-cultural adaptation of the scale (MEBS) was carried out in accordance with the guidelines proposed by Beaton et al. [22] to determine the reliability of the MEBS, recommendations in the literature regarding sample size for Explanatory Factor Analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) were considered, suggesting a minimum of 5 to 10 participants per item. Accordingly, while at least 85 to 170 participants were required given the scale's 17 items, a higher number was targeted to ensure adequate power for both EFA and CFA, and the final sample included 424 participants.

For the assessment of test–retest reliability, 150 participants were invited to complete the MEBS again [two weeks] after the initial administration. The dataset was randomly split into two subsamples with comparable characteristics: one group (n=212: EFA Count) for exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and the other (n=212: CFA Count) for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Ethical aspect of the research

Ethical approval for the study was granted by the University of Health Sciences Gülhane Scientific Research Ethics Committee, with decision numbered 2025/208. All participants in the study were informed about the research, and those who signed the "Informed Consent Form" were included in the study. All procedures in the study were carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection tools

In the study, data were collected during in-person sessions using a structured survey form consisting of three parts. Participants completed the questionnaires independently (self-administered) in a supervised setting; a trained researcher provided standardized instructions, answered procedural questions, and checked forms for missing responses (without interpreting item content). The first part was the Participant Information Form, which included participants' socio-demographic characteristics (age, gender, marital status, income level, employment status, education level) and their height and weight information used to calculate Body Mass Index (BMI). The second part utilized the MEBS to assess participants’ level of awareness in their eating behaviors, for which validity and reliability analyses were conducted within the scope of the present study. In the third and final part, the Mediterranean Diet Adherence Screener (MEDAS) was used to determine the participants' adherence levels to the Mediterranean diet. Detailed information regarding the scales has been reported in the relevant original publications and has been appropriately cited in the present study. All assessment instruments utilized in this study were validated scales; no non-standardized interview protocols were employed.

Anthropometric measurements

Anthropometric measurements were conducted by the researcher following standard procedures. A Tanita HD 366 scale was used to measure body weight, and a stadiometer with an accuracy of 0.1 cm was used to record height. The formula for calculating body mass index (BMI) is weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). The BMI classifications were as follows: underweight (<18.50 kg/m2), normal (18.50–24.99 kg/m2), overweight (25.00–29.99 kg/m2), and obese (≥30.0 kg/m2) [23].

Mindful eating behavior scale (MEBS)

The Mindful Eating Behavior Scale (MEBS), developed by Winkens et al. (2018), consists of 17 items and 4 subscales (Focused Eating, Hunger and Satiety Cues, Eating with Awareness, and Eating without Distraction) [19]. The scale uses a 5-point Likert-type rating (1=Never, 5=Always), with items 11–17 being reverse-scored. The total score ranges from a minimum of 17 to a maximum of 85, where higher scores indicate greater ME behavior. In the original study, the reported Cronbach's alpha (α) coefficients for the subscales ranged between 0.70 and 0.89.

In the present study, MEBS results are reported using both the total score (calculated as the sum of all 17 items, ranging from 17 to 85) and the mean item score (calculated by dividing the total score by 17, ranging from 1 to 5). This dual reporting approach was adopted to facilitate comparisons with various studies in the international literature. Throughout the manuscript, these two metrics are clearly distinguished and used consistently.

Mediterranean diet adherence screener (MEDAS)

To determine the participants' adherence levels to the Mediterranean diet, the Mediterranean Diet Adherence Screener (MEDAS), developed by Martinez-Gonzalez et al. [24], was used. The validity and reliability study for Türkiye was conducted by Pehlivanoğlu et al. [25]. The scale consists of 14 questions querying the consumption of olive oil, vegetables, fruits, meat, fish, legumes, and other food groups. A total score is calculated based on "Yes" (1 point) or "No" (0 point) answers. Based on the total score, adherence was classified as: <7 points "no adherence," 7–8 points "moderate adherence," and ≥9 points "strict adherence."

Translation and cultural adaptation of the scale

At the outset of the study, permission to use the scale for the validity and reliability study was obtained from its developers via email communication. For the linguistic validation stage, the original English version of the scale was translated into Turkish by three independent experts proficient in English. The researchers synthesized these three translations, creating a unified Turkish form by selecting the most semantically and conceptually appropriate expressions. To check for semantic equivalence, this Turkish form was back-translated into English by an expert with native English proficiency. This back-translation was then compared with the original text to confirm its consistency.

To ensure content validity, opinions were sought from 10 academics specializing in ME behavior. These experts rated the Turkish and original forms on a 4-point scale (1 = major revision needed, 2 = minor revision needed, 3 = appropriate, 4 = very appropriate). The Item-Content Validity Index (I-CVI) for each item and the Scale-Content Validity Index (S-CVI) for the overall scale were calculated using the method proposed by Polit and Beck [26]. Items that received a low congruence score (1 or 2, indicating "revision needed") were revised based on the experts' linguistic and content-related feedback. The calculated I-CVI values for the 17 items ranged from [0.80] to 1.00, and the overall S-CVI/Av was found to be [0.94]. Disagreements among experts were resolved through iterative revisions of the item wordings until a minimum congruence of.80 was achieved for all items, ensuring both semantic and conceptual equivalence. Following the expert review, a pilot study was conducted face-to-face with 10 community-dwelling older adults to evaluate the comprehensibility and cultural relevance of the Turkish form. These cognitive debriefing sessions were carried out by specialist dietitians, who assessed whether the participants understood the items as intended. Since the participants (including those with primary-school education) reported no difficulties in understanding the item content or the 5-point Likert response options, the scale was finalized without further modifications.

Statistical analysis

Prior to analysis, the dataset was screened for missing values. Since data were collected during supervised, face-to-face sessions, all questionnaires were checked for completeness on-site, and no missing data were observed in the final sample (n=424). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation for continuous data, and as frequency (n) and percentage (%) for categorical data. The assumption of normality for continuous data was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Multivariate normality was evaluated using Mardia’s multivariate normality tests.

A correlation-based item analysis, as proposed by Likert, was conducted to evaluate the discriminative power of the 17 items within the MEBS in measuring the intended construct.

The psychometric properties of the scale were assessed through comprehensive validity and reliability analyses. The construct validity of the scale items was examined using both EFA and CFA. For the EFA, the MRFA method was employed for factor extraction using a polychoric correlation matrix to account for the ordinal nature of the Likert-type items. The suitability of the data structure for factor analysis was determined using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Normalized Varimax (Varimax with Kaiser Normalization) was utilized as the factor rotation method, which was justified by the low interfactor correlations (all r <.32). The number of factors to retain was decided based on the Kaiser criterion, parallel analysis, and the minimum average partial (MAP) test. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis was performed to compare the three-factor and four-factor solutions in terms of theoretical coherence and simple structure. Subsequently, CFA was conducted using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) and mean and variance adjusted maximum likelihood (MLMV) estimation methods. MLMV was utilized as a robust correction method to handle potential multivariate non-normality and provide robust standard errors. The model-data fit was evaluated by examining the statistical significance of the factor loadings and multiple goodness-of-fit indices (e.g., x2/df, CFI, TLI, and RMSEA).

To establish concurrent validity, a form of criterion-related validity, the MEDAS was utilized as the reference scale. Spearman's correlation coefficient was employed to assess this concurrent validity [27]. The interpretation of the Spearman correlation coefficients was based on the following cut-off points: 0.00–0.19 as very weak, 0.20–0.39 as weak, 0.40–0.69 as moderate, 0.70–0.89 as high, and 0.90–1.00 as a very high correlation [28]. The reliability of the MEBS was evaluated using the internal consistency coefficient Cronbach’s α and McDonald’s omega (ω), which assesses reliability from a factor-analytic perspective. Test-retest reliability was assessed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC).Data analysis was performed using Jamovi (Ver. 2.7.6.). MRFA was conducted using FACTOR (Ver. 11.05.01), and CFA Analysis was performed using AMOS (Ver. 21.0). Adherence to the MEDAS score and BMI were examined as predictors of mindful eating behavior using multiple linear regression analysis. The dependent variable was specified as the MEBS total score. The assumptions of linear regression (normality, linearity, homoscedasticity, and multicollinearity) were evaluated prior to conducting the analysis. The significance level was set at p<.05 for all statistical decisions.

Study population

The study sample consisted of 424 older adults, with a majority being female and having a primary school education level. The detailed socio-demographic and anthropometric characteristics of the participants are summarized in Table 1.

Validity and reliability study

Data pre-processing

No missing or outlier data were observed in the dataset. The multivariate normality of the MEBS data was investigated using Mardia's multivariate normality tests. The results indicated that the dataset did not meet the assumption of multivariate normality (p<.001) (26).

Item analysis results

The discriminative power of the 17 items in the MEBS was evaluated using the correlation-based item analysis proposed by Likert [29].

The corrected item-total correlation coefficients for the 17 items ranged from.251 to.643, with the exception of Item 17. As these coefficients were positive and greater than.25, they met the criterion for retention in the scale [30]. The item-total correlation coefficient for Item 17 was found to be.107, and the Cronbach's alpha value if this item were deleted was calculated as.867. The deletion of Item 17 resulted in a very small increase of 0.6% in the total scale's Cronbach's alpha, an increase that was deemed not to be a significant improvement in the scale's reliability level. Construct validity stages

Minimum rank factor analysis (MRFA) results

Mardia's Skewness and Kurtosis tests indicated that the dataset did not conform to multivariate normality. Consequently, MRFA was employed as the factor extraction method to examine the factor structure of the MEBS [31–33].

Given that the MEBS is a Likert-type scale that yields ordinal-level measurements, a polychoric correlation matrix was utilized in the MRFA to obtain accurate and robust parameter estimates [34–36].

The sampling adequacy was evaluated using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was used to analyze whether the correlation matrix differed from an identity matrix. The KMO value (0.802) exceeded the 0.80 threshold, indicating an "excellent (Meritorious)" degree of sampling adequacy [37]. According to Bartlett's Test of Sphericity (χ2= 4795.1, df = 136, p <.001), there were sufficient correlations between the data, and the correlation matrix was significantly different from an identity matrix. The results of the KMO and Bartlett's Test of Sphericity indicated that the dataset was adequate for MRFA [29, 30].

To reveal an interpretable and clear factor structure, a rotation method was applied to obtain subscales aligned with the "simple structure" concept proposed by Thurstone [38]. The Normalized Varimax (Varimax with Kaiser Normalization) rotation method was preferred. The Bentler Simplicity statistic index value was found to be 0.936, indicating that the 17-item, 4-dimension model was appropriate for a simple structure [39].

To determine the most appropriate rotation method, the interfactor correlation matrix was analyzed (Table 3). According to Tabachnick and Fidell [27], an orthogonal rotation is preferred when factor correlations are predominantly below the.32 threshold. In the present study, the majority of the correlation coefficients were below this threshold, indicating that the factors are relatively independent (Table 3). Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis was performed to compare the Normalized Varimax (orthogonal) solution with a Direct Oblimin (oblique) solution. The oblique rotation resulted in problematic cross-loadings for Item 5 (.402 and.441) and Item 14 (.444 and.327), where the difference between loadings was less than 10%. To determine the optimal number of factors to retain, several methods were employed. While the most commonly used methods, the Kaiser criterion and the scree test, suggested a 4-factor structure, the parallel analysis developed by Horn [41] also suggested 4 factors. In contrast, the MAP analysis developed by Velicer [42] suggested a 3-factor structure [43–46]. The decision to retain the four-factor model was further supported by the cumulative percentage of variance explained, which reached 81.81%. According to Pett et al. [29], achieving a threshold of 75–80% for maximum variance extracted ensures the practical significance of the factors. While the MAP analysis suggested a three-factor solution, a sensitivity analysis comparing 3- vs. 4-factor solutions revealed that the three-factor model lacked theoretical coherence and exhibited significant cross-loadings for Items 6, 7, and 9, with loading differences of less than 10%. Specifically, in the 3-factor model, items related to physiological signals (Items 8 and 10) clustered with items related to sensory awareness (Items 11–13). Clinically, these items measure fundamentally different constructs; therefore, their convergence into a single dimension is theoretically unjustifiable and lacks clinical meaningfulness. In contrast, the four-factor structure preserved the integrity of these distinct domains, aligning with the original scale's development and demonstrating superior clinical interpretability by adhering more closely to Thurstone's simple structure [38]. The eigenvalue for this four-dimension structure was 11.493 (Total common variance=14.049), explaining 81.81% of the total variance.

Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) Results

A CFA was applied to the MEBS to evaluate the goodness-of-fit of the four-factor structure derived from the MRFA.

The construct validity of the measurement model was assessed using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) parameter estimation method. This method was chosen because the univariate skewness values for the scale items (ranging from −1.612 to -.068) did not exceed the threshold of 2, and the univariate kurtosis values (ranging from −1.141 to 1.802) did not exceed the threshold of 7 [47–51].

Reliability

Internal consistency reliability analysis results

The reliability of the scale was examined using the Cronbach's alpha internal consistency coefficient and McDonald's omega (ω) coefficient, which assesses reliability from a factor-analytic perspective [59].

The Cronbach's alpha coefficient yields accurate results under the assumptions of parallel measurements, multivariate normality, and unidimensional scale structures. According to Guttman (1945), except in cases of parallel and equivalent measurements, the alpha coefficient provides the lower bound of reliability, not the true reliability. Although Cronbach's alpha is the most frequently reported reliability coefficient in research, it often fails to produce adequate and appropriate reliability estimates under many conditions [60–64]. Therefore, in our study, McDonald's omega (ω), which approaches reliability from a CFA perspective, was also calculated.

McDonald's omega (ω) coefficient is also referred to as "construct reliability" [65, 66] and is derived from the CFA method. Discussion

The assessment of ME behaviors in older adults holds significant potential for preventing nutritional issues and promoting healthy aging within this demographic. As ME is an internal process and thus challenging to observe directly, its measurement relies predominantly on self-report questionnaires. While several instruments are available for assessing ME in Türkiye, these tools often measure constructs conflated with emotional or problematic eating behaviors. Furthermore, they were not specifically developed for an older adult demographic. The principal objective of this investigation was to assess the validity and reliability of the Turkish version of the MEBS for the Turkish older adult population aged 65 and over. Our findings confirmed that the scale's 17-item, four-factor structure (Focused Eating, Hunger and Satiety Cues, Eating with Awareness, and Eating without Distraction) holds true for the Turkish version. The results demonstrate that the MEBS-TR is a valid and reliable instrument for measuring ME in this specific population.

In the present study, findings from the EFA and CFA corroborated the original four-factor structure of the MEBS, as developed by Winkens et al. [19]. The CFA model fit indices demonstrated an acceptable level of fit, with χ2/df of 2.777, CFI of 0.941, and RMSEA of 0.065. This is a significant finding, as it suggests that the ME construct and its sub-dimensions (Focused Eating, Hunger and Satiety Cues, Eating with Awareness, and Eating without Distraction) remain conceptually consistent within the Turkish older adult population.

In the reliability analyses, the internal consistency coefficients for the total scale score as well as for the majority of its subscales were consistent with the values reported in the original study, where subscale values ranged from 0.70 to 0.89 (Cronbach’s α = 0.861, McDonald’s ω = 0.867). The high reliability of the 'Hunger and Satiety Cues' (α = 0.888) and 'Eating with Awareness' (α = 0.895) subscales was particularly noteworthy.

However, the reliability coefficients for the 'Eating without Distraction' subscale (α = 0.665, ω = 0.675). Nevertheless, these values remain within the acceptable thresholds reported in the literature. This finding suggests that cultural or cognitive differences may exist in how the older adult population interprets the reverse-scored items (e.g., Items 11–17) contained within this subscale. Similarly, Item 17 exhibited a low item-total correlation (0.107), indicating a weaker contribution to the construct. However, we decided to retain it because its removal did not significantly enhance the total scale reliability (an increase of only 0.6%) and allowed us to preserve the scale's theoretical structure.

In the test-retest reliability analysis, the Intraclass Correlation Coefficients (ICC) obtained for all subscales and the total score from measurements taken two weeks apart were found to be at an 'Excellent' level (ranging from ICC = 0.930 to 0.969). This is a robust finding, demonstrating that the scale yields highly consistent measurements over time.

A significant finding of this investigation was the observed association between ME levels and adherence to the Mediterranean Diet. According to the regression analysis, MEBS scores increased significantly in tandem with Mediterranean Diet adherence. This establishes that greater adherence to the diet is associated with higher levels of ME. In a manner consistent with our findings, a study conducted in Türkiye also identified a positive correlation between MEDAS scores and the dimensions of the MEQ. That study observed that as participants' scores on subscales such as awareness, eating control, and emotional eating rose, their adherence to the Mediterranean diet concurrently increased (p<.05) [72].

As part of the concurrent validity analysis, a positive and statistically significant correlation was identified (rho =.163, p =.001) between the total score of the MEBS-TR and the total score of the MEDAS. This finding is consistent with existing literature, which suggests that ME—representing the process of eating (how one eats)—and diet quality (what is eaten) are interrelated yet fundamentally distinct constructs. Indeed, in a study conducted among Greek adults, Christodoulou et al. [73] reported a finding highly similar to ours. They noted a statistically significant positive correlation of a comparable magnitude (r =.150, p <.05) between the Mindful Eating Scale (MES-16) and the 14-item MEDAS. This parallel suggests that the result obtained in our Turkish older adult population are not isolated but align with patterns observed in other Mediterranean populations, demonstrating that the MEBS-TR structurally captures a similar association. However, it is important to interpret this finding with caution. Although the correlation was statistically significant (p =.001), the magnitude of the relationship (rho =.163) is considered very weak according to standard criteria. Given our relatively large sample size (n=424), even very small effects can reach statistical significance. This does not necessarily imply a strong clinical or practical association. This result reinforces the conceptual distinction between the 'process' of eating (mindful eating) and the 'content' of the diet (diet quality), suggesting that they are related but fundamentally different behavioral domains in older Turkish adults. This weak but significant association is further supported by recent research in the Turkish population. For instance, Ongun Yilmaz et al. [74] highlighted that while mindful eating is linked to diet quality patterns among Turkish individuals, the complexity of these behaviors suggests that they should be evaluated as distinct constructs. This alignment underscores the importance of using population-specific tools like the MEBS-TR to capture these nuances in older adults.

In their study involving 188 Turkish participants, Yıldırım and Cebioglu [72] observed that individuals with high adherence to the Mediterranean diet also had higher MEQ-30 scores and BMI values. However, this difference was not statistically significant (p>.05). The study reported that despite the lack of a significant association between the total scores, the MEDAS score demonstrated a significant positive correlation (p <.05) with specific sub-dimensions of the MEQ-30, namely "disinhibition," "emotional eating," and "eating control". This finding suggests that while the two scales may diverge at the aggregate (total) level, they possess intersections in specific domains, such as the emotional and control components of eating behavior.

On the other hand, other studies in the literature establish stronger links between these two constructs. In the extensive French NutriNet-Santé cohort, which included over 13,000 participants [12], the total ME score was shown to have a clear positive association with adherence to the Mediterranean diet (MEDI-LITE score) (β =.37, p <.001). The authors of that study emphasized that their robust findings diverged from those of smaller-sample studies. Many of those earlier studies had failed to find an association and were conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic. The association found in our study provides valuable data, illustrating the extent to which the sample (an older adult population), the specific mindfulness scale used (MEBS), and cultural factors may influence this relationship. Consequently, the correlation identified with MEDAS confirms that the MEBS demonstrates an expected concurrent validity characteristic.

Adherence to the Mediterranean diet and ME are both health behaviors associated with positive effects on general health and well-being and are linked to lower BMI. However, an individual's strict adherence to the Mediterranean diet in terms of content does not necessarily imply that they consume their food mindfully in terms of process. Conversely, an individual with high mindfulness may exhibit low adherence to the Mediterranean diet, as was the case in the study (68). It is therefore possible to assert that the MEBS, by differing from measures of diet quality, captures the psychological and attentional dimensions of eating behavior and fills an existing gap in this field.

In this study, a statistically significant negative relationship was identified (β = -.104, p =.029) between the total score of the Turkish form of the MEBS and BMI. This finding indicates that higher levels of ME are associated with lower BMI. This conclusion strongly supports both the general literature and the results of the original MEBS development study. The most noteworthy parallel to our findings was observed in the study conducted by Winkens et al. [19], where the MEBS was originally developed. Similar to our study, a negative relationship between ME and BMI was reported in their sample of 1,227 Dutch adults aged 55 and older. Winkens et al. [19] determined that three of the subscales they developed—'Focused Eating' (r = -.10), 'Hunger and Satiety Cues' (r = -.12), and 'Eating with Awareness' (r = -.17)—demonstrated a statistically significant negative correlation with BMI. The fact that our study confirms this negative relationship within a Turkish population of a similar age group is significant evidence that the MEBS-TR captures the expected construct validity and clinical association (i.e., lower awareness, higher BMI).

This inverse relationship is substantiated by other recent studies in the eating behavior domain. For instance, research conducted by Christodoulou et al. [73] found that individuals with a normal BMI demonstrated significantly stronger ME habits compared to those classified as overweight or obese. Similarly, the extensive NutriNet-Santé cohort study [12], which included over 13,000 participants, clearly illuminated this association. It demonstrated that as BMI categories ascended from normal to obese, mean ME scores showed a statistically significant decline (p <.001). Furthermore, a recent study by Andreou et al. [75] involving Greek and Cypriot adults lends additional support to this finding. They identified a significant negative correlation (r = -.25) between the 'Awareness' sub-dimension of ME and BMI. The same study also demonstrated that mindless eating behaviors, such as 'Disinhibition' (loss of control), were positively correlated with higher BMI (r =.30).

Regarding studies conducted in Türkiye, Yıldırım and Cebioglu [72], utilizing a different scale (the MEQ-30), did not identify a statistically significant relationship between the total ME score and BMI (p =.087). However, they noted a trend where scores tended to be lower for individuals classified as overweight or obese. The fact that our study, using the MEBS developed by Winkens et al. [19], successfully established this relationship at a statistically significant level (p =.029) within a Turkish older adult population, indicates that the MEBS-TR is a sensitive instrument for assessing eating behavior and its association with body weight in this demographic.

A recent systematic review by Constâncio et al. [76] emphasized that the psychometric properties of eating behavior scales for older adults are frequently inadequate, identifying the MEBS as one of the few valid instruments developed for this demographic. Consequently, our findings demonstrate that the MEBS-TR is not merely a linguistic translation. It concurrently validates the scale's theoretical foundation within the Turkish older adult population, demonstrating the expected link between higher awareness, internal cues, and a lower BMI. However, adherence to the Mediterranean diet and body mass index together explained only 5.4% of the variance in mindful eating behavior (R2 = 0.054). While both variables showed significant individual associations with mindful eating, the overall explanatory power of the model remained limited.

Mindful eating is a multidimensional construct influenced by various psychological, behavioral, and environmental factors; therefore, it is not expected to be largely explained solely by diet quality and body mass index [77]. Accordingly, this relatively low proportion of explained variance appears conceptually consistent, suggesting that a substantial portion of the variance is driven by unmeasured cognitive and reward-based domains. Indeed, the conceptual framing of mindful eating in older adults may be further enriched by considering the complex interplay between homeostatic appetite regulation and hedonic hunger systems, which represent two interrelated parts of the same brain network [78, 79]. Homeostatic regulation is driven by internal energy deficits and peripheral signals to maintain metabolic balance, whereas hedonic hunger is a reward-driven process that can independently prompt individuals to seek and consume highly palatable foods for pleasure even in the absence of physical hunger [77, 79].

Recent gerontological research in Türkiye supports this distinction, demonstrating a distinct positive relationship between hedonic hunger and abdominal obesity measures in older adult populations [80]. Furthermore, aging itself can induce progressive shifts in how older adults engage with food, where culinary dependency, cognitive changes, or a decline in anticipatory eating pleasure can significantly disrupt the motivational drive to eat [81]. Engaging with these well-established physiological and reward-based hunger frameworks provides a more comprehensive understanding of the psychological drivers behind nutritional choices and successfully contextualizes the complex variance observed in this demographic.

The primary contribution of this study is the introduction of a valid and reliable measurement instrument (MEBS-TR) to the literature, specifically designed to assess ME within a high-risk and specific demographic: individuals aged 65 and over in Türkiye. This scale can be utilized by clinicians, dietitians, and researchers to detect negative eating behaviors frequently observed in older adults, such as emotional eating, distraction, and insensitivity to internal cues. Furthermore, this tool will enable the development of targeted ME-based intervention programs for the older adult population and the scientific measurement of their efficacy.

Our study possesses several strengths, including a rigorous translation and cultural adaptation process adhering to the guidelines proposed by Beaton et al. [22], a sufficient sample size (n=424) for both EFA and CFA analyses, and robust test-retest stability. Additionally, rather than relying on standard linear factor models, employing advanced psychometric estimation techniques specifically tailored for non-normal ordinal data significantly enhanced the structural robustness of the MEBS-TR. This rigorous approach ensures that the captured four-factor structure is exceptionally resilient against estimation biases. Consequently, it provides a more stable framework for cross-cultural comparisons than the original Dutch version[19], or international adaptations such as the Arabic [20] and Greek [21] versions, which relied on conventional continuous indicators.

Nonetheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the sample was recruited using a non-probability (convenience) sampling method, which limits the generalizability of the findings to the entire Turkish older adult population. Second, the self-reported assessment of adherence to the Mediterranean diet may have led to inaccurate responses that could affect the validity of the measurements. Third, although individuals with diagnosed neurological disorders were excluded, no standardized cognitive screening tool was used to assess mild cognitive impairment. Finally, due to the demographic imbalance in the sample, which predominantly consisted of primary-educated and community-dwelling older adults, measurement invariance across key subgroups such as sex and education was not assessed. Therefore, any comparisons between subgroups should be interpreted with caution.

Future studies should consider incorporating such measures to further validate the scale's applicability across different cognitive profiles in older age. While the comprehensibility of the MEBS-TR was confirmed through a pilot study involving 10 older adults, future research could benefit from more extensive qualitative cognitive interviews to capture deeper cultural nuances in eating behaviors. In light of this study's findings, it is recommended that the validity of the MEBS-TR be tested in more diverse samples, such as older adults in residential care, individuals with different chronic conditions (e.g., diabetes, obesity). As a key outcome of this study, the MEBS-TR can now be employed as a primary outcome measure in longitudinal and experimental research designed to evaluate the effectiveness of mindfulness-based eating interventions among older adults.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that the Turkish version of the MEBS (MEBS-TR) is a valid and reliable instrument for assessing mindful eating behaviors in Turkish older adults aged 65 years and over. The scale exhibited satisfactory psychometric properties, indicating robust construct validity, internal consistency, and measurement reliability. The validated MEBS-TR has the potential to contribute significantly to research and practice aimed at promoting healthy eating behaviors, supporting healthy aging, and developing mindful eating–based intervention programs for the older adult population in Türkiye.
