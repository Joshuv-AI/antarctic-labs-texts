Leadership and investment to strengthen child and adolescent mental health in LMICs from the Western Pacific Region
Authors: Manasi Kumar
Published: 2025

Child and adolescent mental health (CAMH) remain an area where skilled workforce, policy advancement, research and integrated models of care continue to lag behind. The saying that ‘when it comes to mental health, all countries are developing countries’ remains quite true. This is particularly stark in the context of child and adolescent mental health field given that over 40% of the world’s population belongs to this demographic and it is estimated that 10–20% of this population may experience significant mental health challenges in their life.1,2 The two scoping reviews on child and adolescent mental health policies, resource allocation and interventions in China and the Western Pacific Region by Luan and Long (2025) provide a contextual, thorough overview of what is the situation on ground and an assessment of where forward going efforts are needed to improve mental well-being of children and young people in these countries.3,4 The socioeconomic, cultural, political and environmental diversity of the countries covered under these reviews imply that a range of unique risk and protective factors determine child and adolescent well-being. In both mainland China and other LMICs from the Western Pacific region, there is an upward trend in the way CAMH problems seem to have increasingly become widespread. The policy environment, evidence-based interventions and public programs to address these problems from prevention-to-care with a focus on strengthening treatments continue to be poorly funded and developed.5,6 Several problem areas have been identified in the reviews presented in this volume and those published recently. A range of interconnected problems have been identified: beginning with addressing care transition from child to adolescent services providing age responsive services, workforce development to prepare clinicians, teachers, parents, peers and community members to understand psychological problems and low intensity prevention and care strategies to addressing widespread trauma associated with intergenerational displacement, violence, and poverty in certain communities and countries with added vulnerabilities such as climate change, remain unaddressed. There are also serious gaps in availability of scalable interventions and their continuous improvement and evaluation within routine settings - an area needing further attention. Without appropriate sociocultural contextualization of the screening, assessment tools and interventions, the programs cannot work and make sense to children and youth in diverse settings. For vulnerable child and youth populations including indigenous populations, other highly minoritized populations of parenting adolescents or those with disabilities and other chronic health conditions, clinical interventions including policy interventions need co-design elements to resonate with their experience and to meet these communities where they are. A framework for enhancing mental health care for children and adolescents

The countries under consideration of the two reviews here cover a diverse range of upper, middle- and low-income country context. Several countries in the Western Pacific region include those under ‘the middle-income trap’7 (as identified by a World Bank report of 2024) where economic advancement is not paving way for holistic investment and forward looking priority setting for the next generation. The rich diversity within China and the efforts towards increasing research outputs and conceptual advancements in CAMH and developmental psychology and psychotherapy research needs to be matched by a faster pace of progress in public policy arena and in development of strong guidelines. These include forward-looking recommendations addressing political, environmental, commercial, social and digital determinants of mental health. In the Western Pacific region, efforts have to be made towards improving policy environment, building health systems and workforce that can meet children and youth needs recognizing the tremendous burden they are living through given massive environmental and socioeconomic perturbations that impact the regions progress.8

The two scoping reviews highlight the mental health burden among children and youth and provide us with new directions and vision for how countries in the Western Pacific region can do better. Strengthening child and adolescent mental health as a core priority of development in each of these countries can accelerate growth, peace and population-well-being. Our future generations will thank us for a timely recalibration of resources and efforts that strengthen population mental health.
