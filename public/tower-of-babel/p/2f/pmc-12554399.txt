Single‐Cell Transcriptomics of Human Pericarotid Lymph Nodes Indicating Stability of Carotid Atherosclerotic Plaques
Authors: Tao Sun, Yanzhao Wu, Guohao Liu, Zhiwei Xue, Mengchen Yu, Bo Mao, Mengtao Han, Xiangjun Liang, Lu Ling, Nanlin You, Longxin Ji, Han Liu, Chenyang Xu, Zhen Qin, Peng Zhao, Donghai Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe link between lymph node (LN) function and atherosclerosis is unclear, especially at the single‐cell level. This study explores the relationship between pericarotid LNs and plaque stability using single‐cell transcriptome analysis.MethodsWe enrolled 106 patients with severe internal carotid artery stenosis undergoing carotid endarterectomy. We recorded baseline clinical data and pericarotid LN size, and analyzed plaque composition to assess its association with enlarged LNs. Single‐cell transcriptome and T/B‐cell receptor sequencing were used to profile pericarotid LNs.ResultsPatients with symptomatic carotid atherosclerosis had more enlarged pericarotid LNs (P<0.001), indicating plaque instability. This was characterized by increased inflammation, greater CD68+ macrophage infiltration (P<0.001), and reduced fibrous content (P<0.001). Single‐cell analysis identified 3 main cellular compartments: lymphocytes, myeloid cells, and stromal cells. Enlarged LNs had significantly more naive B and T cells (P<0.001), suggesting heightened immune response. Macrophages and neutrophils may help in cholesterol transport and degradation, while stromal cells may regulate the immune microenvironment by influencing T‐cell tolerance and lymphocyte trafficking.ConclusionsEnlarged pericarotid LNs are potential biomarkers for plaque instability and recent ischemic events. This study provides a comprehensive single‐cell transcriptomic landscape of human LNs in atherosclerosis.

Atherosclerosis, an intricate inflammatory condition, is closely linked to innate and adaptive immune responses. It is stimulated by the deposition of low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) in the arterial intima, culminating in the formation of lipid‐rich plaques. Progressive plaque growth and rupture can cause thrombosis and vessel occlusion, leading to myocardial infarction and stroke, which are the leading causes of death and disability globally. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 However, the complexity of the systemic immune responses and animal models without spontaneous plaque rupture has limited our understanding of the immune mechanisms involved in this process. 5

Generally, during atherosclerosis development, an immune response to arterial wall inflammation occurs within the intima, media, or adventitia and in secondary lymphatic organs, including the lymph nodes (LNs) or spleen. 6 , 7 Immune cells located in the plaque, circulation, and secondary lymphatic organs influence atherosclerosis. 7 These atherosclerotic innate and adaptive immune responses are primarily due to LDL deposition and modification in the artery wall. 2 , 3 Modified LDL is engulfed by macrophages through scavenger receptors, resulting in foam cell formation, inflammatory cytokine release, and immune cell infiltration. Infiltrating dendritic cells (DCs), which take up antigens in blood vessel walls, present antigens in secondary lymphatic organs to activate B and T cells. 2 In addition to modifying LDL, these antigens that drain through lymphatic vessels and trigger atherosclerotic adaptive immune responses also include lipid, crystalline cholesterol deposition, and cell death–related molecules. 2 , 8 The net positive balance between the recruitment, proliferation, and apoptosis of immune cells and the reverse efflux of cholesterol and dead cells through the lymphatic system contributes to inflammation and plaque growth. 2 , 9 , 10

LNs are essential secondary lymphoid organs with a critical role in the regulation of human health and disease. 11 As the first station of arterial lymphatic drainage, the macroscopic and microscopic changes of LNs may indicate atherosclerosis progression. Centa et al observed enlarged axillary and neck LNs that drain the carotid, as well as an increase in the number of cells and germinal centers in the enlarged LNs, in atherosclerotic mouse models. 8 , 12 In addition, the failed emigration of monocyte‐derived cells from the plaque to the LNs is involved in atherosclerosis initiation and formation. 10 Inhibiting the formation of lymphatic vessels linked to peripheral LNs may aggravate in situ atherosclerosis but prevent transplant atherosclerosis. 13 , 14 These contradictory results suggest an intricate role of LNs in atherosclerosis. Elucidating the LNs’ role in atherosclerosis may help uncover new diagnostic biomarkers and immunotherapeutic targets.

Currently, single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNA‐seq) technology is being used increasingly to understand atherosclerosis. Previous studies have established single‐cell maps of atherosclerotic plaques and circulating blood 5 , 15 ; however, studies on the single‐cell LNs microanatomy in the context of atherosclerosis are lacking. Therefore, we performed scRNA‐seq and single‐cell T/B‐cell receptor sequencing on human pericarotid LNs obtained during carotid endarterectomy. We reported a comprehensive overview of the various pericarotid LNs cell types and aimed to understand and refine the picture of immune response during atherosclerosis.

Methods

Anonymized sequencing data have been made publicly available at the Genome Sequence Archive of the National Genomics Data Center (HRA010389) and can be accessed at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn. Additional data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Patient Selection and Clinical Data Collection

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our hospital (KYLL‐202111‐037‐1). Informed consent was obtained from all the patients before the procedure. Patients undergoing carotid endarterectomy for severe atherosclerotic carotid stenosis (>70% luminal narrowing) in our department between December 2020 and March 2023 were enrolled in this study. All patients were precisely diagnosed, and other pathogeneses were excluded with the use of Doppler ultrasound, computed tomography angiography, magnetic resonance imaging or angiography, and digital subtraction angiography. Symptomatic patients were defined as those who had a history of amaurosis fugax, transient ischemic attack, or ischemic stroke ipsilateral to the extracted plaques within 6 months before the endarterectomy; asymptomatic patients were defined as those without a history of cerebrovascular events. Furthermore, patients with neck tumors, a history of neck radiation, lymphoma, or LN tuberculosis were excluded. During surgery, we recorded the size of the LN around the carotid artery, including the longitudinal and transverse diameters. If there was >1 LN around the carotid artery, the largest node was recorded. Enlarged LNs were defined as those with a transverse diameter of ≥10 mm, measured using a straightedge. Data on venous blood samples and the clinical and demographic characteristics of the participants were collected from the patients before carotid endarterectomy.

Tissue Collection

Plaques were obtained from all the patients and macroscopically evaluated during carotid endarterectomy. Areas exhibiting the greatest plaque area or macroscopic advanced features of atherosclerosis were selected for analysis. The segments were immediately immersion fixed in 4% formalin and subsequently processed for paraffin embedding.

Three nonenlarged and 3 enlarged LNs from 3 asymptomatic and symptomatic patients, respectively, were obtained intraoperatively and immediately transported to the laboratory for digestion and follow‐up tests.

Plaque Pathological Analysis

Hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed according to standard procedures. Picrosirius red staining was used to detect collagen fibers. 3,3′‐Diaminobenzidine–enhanced Perls' Prussian blue staining was used to evaluate the presence of intraplaque hemorrhage.

Quantification of staining, which assessed the extent of plaque features, was documented as the threshold area/lesion area using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health). The evaluation was performed by 2 blinded investigators.

Sample Processing and Isolation of Cells

Briefly, LN samples were cut into ≈1‐ to 2‐mm3 pieces in the RPMI‐1640 medium (Gibco) and enzymatically digested with gentleMACS (Miltenyi) using program 37C_m_TDK_1 on a rotor at 37 °C according to the manufacturer's instructions. The dissociated cells were subsequently passed through a 100‐μm SmartStrainer and centrifuged at 400g for 5 minutes. After the supernatant was removed, the pelleted cells were suspended in a red blood cell lysis buffer (Miltenyi) and incubated on ice for 1 to 2 minutes to lyse red blood cells. After washing twice with 1× PBS (Gibco), the cell pellets were resuspended in sorting buffer (PBS supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum [Gibco]).

Single‐Cell Library Construction and Sequencing

Single‐cell suspensions were stained with antibodies against 7‐AAD (e‐Bioscience) for fluorescence‐activated cell sorting, which was performed using a BD Melody instrument (Becton, Dickinson and Company). Based on fluorescence‐activated cell sorting analysis, single cells were sorted into 1.5‐mL tubes, and cell viability and cell concentration were determined using a Countstar Automated Cell Counter. Cells were loaded at 13200 cells per chip using the 10× Chromium Single Cell V(D)J Reagent kit version 1.1 to ensure optimal cell recovery and representation. A total of 5000 cells were captured in droplets to generate nanoliter‐scale gel emulsion beads. Gel emulsion beads were reverse transcribed in a C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler (Bio‐Rad Laboratories) programmed at 53 °C for 45 minutes, 85 °C for 5 minutes, and held at 4 °C. After reverse transcription and cell barcoding, the emulsions were broken, and cDNA was isolated and purified with Cleanup Mix containing DynaBeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and SPRI select reagent (Beckman), and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification was performed. Next, amplified cDNA was used for 5′ gene expression library construction, followed by PCR amplification. Amplified cDNA was then used for both 5′ gene expression library construction and T/B‐cell receptor (TCR/BCR) V(D)J targeted enrichment performed with the Chromium Single Cell V(D)J Enrichment Kit (10x Genomics), followed by V(D)J library construction. For RNA sequencing library construction, amplified cDNA was fragmented and end repaired, double‐sided size selected, PCR amplified with sample indexing primers, and double‐sided size selected. For TCR/BCR library construction, TCR/BCR transcripts were enriched from amplified cDNA by PCR. Subsequently, enriched PCR product was fragmented and end repaired, size selected, PCR amplified with sample‐indexing primers, and size selected. Libraries prepared according to the manufacturer's user guide were then purified and profiled for quality assessment. Single‐cell RNA and TCR/BCR V(D)J libraries were sequenced by a NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina) with 150 bp paired‐end reads. Raw gene expression matrices were generated for each sample by the Cell Ranger Pipeline version 6.1.2 coupled with human reference version GRCh38‐2020‐A. The output filtered gene expression matrices were analyzed by R software version 4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) with the Seurat package version 4.1.1. The percentage of mitochondrial transcripts for each cell (percent.mt) was calculated and added as metadata to the Seurat object. Cells were filtered before dimensionality reduction (n_genes >200; percent_mito <10%). Expression values were then scaled to 10 000 transcripts per cells and log transformed. Effect of variable (percent.mt) was estimated and regressed out (ScaleData function, model.use = linear), and the scaled and centered residuals were used for dimensionality reduction and clustering.

Single‐Cell RNA Sequencing Data of Normal LNs

Single‐cell RNA sequencing data of normal LNs were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (Series GSE131907). The data set contained single‐cell mRNA expression profiles of 10 normal LNs from patients with lung adenocarcinoma conserving surgery without prior treatment. Harmony was used to correct for batch effects.

Dimensionality Reduction

We used 2000 genes with high cell‐to‐cell variation, which were calculated using the FindVariableFeatures function in Seurat for further dimensionality reduction. The RunPCA function was used to perform dimensionality reduction on linearly transformed, scaled data, using default parameters. Next, the ElbowPlot function was used to identify proper dimensions of each data set.

Cell Clustering and Identification of Marker Genes

After nonlinear dimensional reduction and projection of all cells into 2‐dimensional space by the RunUMAP function, cluster classification analysis was performed by using FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions. Differentially expressed genes in each cluster were identified using the FindAllMarkers function in the Seurat package, which uses a t test to compare gene expression between clusters for statistical robustness. Doublets were identified by searching for cells with substantial and coherent expression profiles from ≥2 cell types.

Reclustering of Major Cell Types

To identify subtypes or cells in different states within a major cell type, we used a 2‐round clustering strategy. First, cells belonging to a cell type were extracted from the count matrix. Then, as was done on the all cells, dimensionality reduction and cell clustering were performed.

Differentially Expressed Gene Identification and Functional Enrichment

Differential gene expression testing was performed using the FindMarkers function in Seurat with parameter “test.use = t.” Enrichment analysis for the functions of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was conducted using clusterProfiler version 4.2.2 R package. “Biological Processes” gene ontology (GO) annotations for all molecules on the surface screen were compiled from org.Hs.eg.db version 3.14.0.

Single‐Cell TCR/BCR Data Analysis

VDJ libraries were processed using the scRepertoire R package version 1.8.0 to filter for productive TCR/BCR chains. We used the combineExpression function to merge the single‐cell RNA data with the TCR/BCR data. The grouping criteria were as follows: TCR clonotypes were defined by identical complementarity‐determining region 3 nucleotide sequences and corresponding VDJC genes, while BCR clonotypes were defined by nucleotide sequences and their associated v‐genes. Clonotypes with >1 cell were classified as expanded, whereas those with only 1 cell were regarded as single. Finally, we used the occupied function to illustrate the composition of different clonotype size levels.

Immunohistochemistry Staining

Paraffin‐embedded carotid plaque samples were serially sectioned and used for immunohistochemistry as previously described. The primary antibodies used in 40 sections from the enlarged group and 66 sections from the nonenlarged group were smooth muscle α‐actin (Catalog No. 14395‐1‐AP, Proteintech; dilution of 1:3000), tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α, Catalog No. 60291‐1‐Ig, Proteintech; dilution of 1:400), and interleukin‐1β (Catalog No. 16806‐1‐AP, Proteintech; dilution of 1:200). The primary antibodies used in 39 sections from the enlarged group and 60 sections from the nonenlarged group were CD68 (ab213363, Abcam; dilution of 1:8000). The primary antibodies used in 35 sections from the enlarged group and 53 sections from the nonenlarged group were CD3 (17617‐1‐AP, Proteintech; dilution of 1:1000). The primary antibodies used in 3 sections from the enlarged group and 3 sections from the nonenlarged group were CD19 (66298‐1‐Ig, Proteintech; dilution of 1:500) and CD1C (28838‐1‐AP, Proteintech; dilution of 1:200). Visualization was performed using Zhiyue WS‐10 digital panoramic scanner, and staining was quantified as the threshold area/lesion area using ImageJ software (ImageJ v1.53). All immunohistochemistry evaluations were performed independently by 2 experienced pathologists, and the quantified results of each tissue slice were finally the average of the two.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Paraffin‐embedded LN samples were serially sectioned and used for immunofluorescence staining according to a previously described protocol. 16 The following primary antibodies were used: C1QA (ab189922, Abcam; dilution of 1:200), anti‐S100A8+S100A9 (ab288715, Abcam; dilution of 1:500), apolipoprotein E (Abcam, ab183597; dilution of 1:500), LDL receptor (Catalog No. 10785‐1‐AP, Proteintech; dilution of 1:200), and oxidation‐modified LDL (ox‐LDL; ab14519, Abcam; dilution of 1:500). Fluorescence was observed using a LEICA lAS X confocal laser scanning microscope.

Statistical Analysis

The clinical data and plaque component analysis data were analyzed using the SPSS software version 25.0 (IBM Corporation). Data are expressed as mean±SD or median (interquartile range), depending on whether they conform to a normal distribution. Continuous data were compared using the unpaired Student's t test, and discrete variables were compared using the χ2 or Fisher's exact test. SPSS software and R software were used for receiver operating characteristic curve analysis, Spearman correlation analysis, binary multivariate logistic regression model analysis, and subsequent visualization. χ2 tests were used to assess differences in cell composition between groups. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05. The statistical tools, methods, and threshold for each sequencing data analysis are explicitly described with the results or detailed in the figure legends or Methods sections.

Results

Presence of Enlarged LNs Around the Carotid Artery in Patients With Symptomatic Carotid Atherosclerosis

Of the 106 participants who enrolled in this study, 42 and 64 patients were included in the symptomatic and nonsymptomatic group, respectively. The baseline clinical, biochemical, and demographic characteristics of the participants in the 2 groups are summarized in Table S1. Notably, enlarged pericarotid LNs were more in symptomatic than asymptomatic patients (27/42 [64.3%] versus 13/64 [20.3%], P<0.001; Table S1). No other clinical, biochemical, or demographic differences were observed between the 2 groups (P>0.05; Table S1).

We performed receiver operating characteristic curve analysis on the obtained longitudinal and transverse diameters data of the pericarotid LNs. We found that both longitudinal and transverse LN diameter had moderate diagnostic values (area under the curve=0.741 and 0.744 respectively; Figure S1).

Enlarged Pericarotid LNs Suggested More Unstable Plaques

The patients were divided into 2 groups based on the presence of enlarged pericarotid LNs, and carotid lesions were assessed histologically (Figure 1). The lesions showed advanced atherosclerosis features in both groups (Table S2). Plaques obtained from patients with enlarged pericarotid LNs exhibited lower amounts of fibrous tissue (P=0.004); however, increased CD68+ macrophage infiltration (median, 5.70% (interquartile range, 3.80%–7.40%) versus median, 3.35% (interquartile range, 2.70%–5.38%); P<0.001; Table S2) and interleukin‐1β content (mean±SD, 33.22±7.46% versus 25.67±8.56%; P<0.001; Table S2). In addition, patients with enlarged pericarotid LNs exhibited a higher incidence of intraplaque hemorrhage, with plaques associated with lower levels of vascular smooth muscles and increased TNF‐α; however, the groups did not show statistically significant differences (Table S2). There was no difference between the 2 groups regarding CD3+ T‐cell infiltration. Furthermore, we examined plaque B‐cell and DC infiltration; however, their immunohistochemical results were negative due to minimal B‐cell and DC infiltration (Figure S2). Therefore, we did not analyze plaque B‐cell and DC infiltration.

Enlarged Pericarotid LNs Were Associated With Plaque Inflammation and Blood Markers of High‐Risk Factors for Atherosclerosis

Logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate the association between plaque components and pericarotid LNs, and the obtained logistic model was statistically significant (χ2=27.303, P<0.001). Two independent variables were included in the model, and every 1% increase in the carotid plaque interleukin‐1β content raised the likelihood of pericarotid enlarged LN by 13.8% in patients; for every 1% increase in CD68+ macrophage content in the carotid plaque, patients had a possibility (29.5%) to develop pericarotid enlarged LNs (Table S3).

Using Spearman correlation analysis, we found that the longitudinal and transverse diameter of LNs were positively correlated with some biomarkers for atherosclerosis, such as homocysteine, creatinine, uric acid, and atherogenic index (total cholesterol—HDL cholesterol [HDL‐C])/HDL‐C); in contrast, they were negatively correlated with the protective factor, HDL‐C for atherosclerosis (Figure S3). Although the association was not statistically significant, this result suggested that the peripheral carotid LNs were a potential marker of atherosclerosis.

Single‐Cell Transcriptome of Pericarotid LNs

The 6 pericarotid LN samples were enzymatically digested to delineate the single‐cell transcription map of pericarotid LNs from patients with carotid atherosclerosis; viable nucleated cells were isolated by fluorescence‐activated cell sorting, and scRNA‐seq was performed. The basic information of enrolled patients and sample characteristics were summarized in Table S4 and Figure S4. Three enlarged LNs were derived from symptomatic patients with large necrotic lipid cores. Subsequently, 3 nonenlarged LNs were obtained from asymptomatic patients with plaque accompanied by calcification, indicating less inflammation in these plaques. 17 Enrolled patients had type V to VII plaques, according to the American Heart Association classification. 18 Following quality control, we analyzed data from 67 652 single cells (Figure S5). According to the uniform manifold approximation and projection map based on canonical gene expression, the cells were divided into lymphocytes, myeloid cells, and stromal cells (SCs) for further analysis (Figure S6A through S6C).

scRNA‐Seq Analysis of Lymphoid Cells in Pericarotid LNs

We applied unbiased clustering on lymphoid cells and manually annotated the cell clusters using the differential expression of established lineage marker genes. According to the annotation results, we discovered that lymphocytes were mainly composed of B, CD4+ T, CD8+ T, and natural killer cells (Figure S6D through S6G). Compared with nonenlarged LNs, the proportion of B cells in enlarged LNs increased (P<0.001), and the proportion of CD4+ T cells decreased (P<0.001) (Figure S6H). This suggested stronger humoral immune activation in enlarged LNs. Thereafter, we conducted more detailed clustering and analysis of B, CD4+ T, and CD8+ T cells.

scRNA‐Seq Analysis of B Cells in Pericarotid LNs

We subdivided the B‐cell population in lymphocytes into 4 primary subpopulations including (Figure 2A–2D and 2G, Figure S7, and Table S5): Naive B cells (expressing CD79A, CD19, and IGHD) functionally enriched in activation and regulation of immune response, memory B cells (expressing CD79A, CD19, and CD27) which play a role in processing and presenting antigens via major histocompatibility class I pathways, germinal center B cells with increased cell division (expressing CD79A, CD19, and Bcl‐6), and plasma cells (expressing SDC1 and CD27), which were functionally enriched in immunoglobulin production, humoral immune response, and complement activation. Enlarged LNs contain a higher proportion (P<0.001) of naive B cells than nonenlarged LNs (Figure 2E). According to the top DEGs and GO, we found that overall B‐cell function was mainly concentrated in antigen processing, presentation, and immune response (Figure 2F). Compared with nonenlarged LNs, B cells in enlarged LNs have more intense ribosome production; in contrast, those in nonenlarged LNs mainly play the role of regulating the intercellular adhesion and activation of lymphocytes (Figure 2H and 2I). Compared with normal LNs, B cells in enlarged LNs expressed more genes involved in the regulation of intercellular adhesion (Figure 2J and 2K).

Single‐Cell BCR Sequencing of Pericarotid LNs

Single‐cell BCR sequencing enables a detailed investigation of B cell clonal expansion and VDJ gene segment usage. BCR clonotypes were identified by their nucleotide sequence and the corresponding V gene, while clonotype size >1 were regarded as expanded clones. We observed an increased proportion (P<0.001) of clonal B cells in enlarged LNs, particularly within the germinal center B‐ and memory B‐cell populations (Figure S8). The immune stimulation experienced in the enlarged LNs is significantly heightened.

scRNA‐Seq Analysis of CD4 + T Cells in Pericarotid LNs

We subdivided the CD4+ T cell population into the following 5 subpopulations (Figure 3A through 3D and 3G; Figure S9; and Table S5): CD4+ naive T cells (expressing CD3D, CD4, TCF7, and LEF1) functionally enriched in detoxification, CD4+ central memory T cells (Tcm; expressing CD3D, CD4, ANXA1 and IL7R) functionally enriched in interleukin‐2 production and foam cell differentiation, CD4+ T follicular helper cells (expressing CD3D, CD4, and PD1) functionally enriched in lymphocyte activation and proliferation, CD4+ regulatory cells (CD4+ regulatory T (Treg) cells, expressing CD3D, CD4, IL2RA and FOXP3) functionally enriched in lymphocyte proliferation and adhesion, and CD4+ proliferative T cells (expressing CD3D, CD4, and MKI67). Enlarged LNs recruited more CD4+ naive T cells than nonenlarged LNs (Figure 3E). Overall, CD4+ T‐cell function was enriched in T‐cell differentiation and activation (Figure 3F). Compared with nonenlarged LNs, CD4+ T cells in enlarged LNs carried out more intense ribosome‐related biological processes; in contrast, CD4+ T cells in nonenlarged LNs primarily aided in regulating other immune cells (Figure 3H and 3I). After removing batch effects, we compared normal LNs with pericarotid LNs. CD4+ T cells within pericarotid LNs demonstrated a pronounced activation phenotype. Multiple critical cytokine regulatory pathways and antigen receptor–mediated signaling pathways were significantly upregulated (Figure 3J, Figure S10A).

Chemokine receptors were expressed on the surface of various immune cells. They usually bind to chemokines to trigger signal transduction, ultimately leading to cell migration, adhesion, or other biological reactions. 19 Therefore, we investigated the expression of chemokine receptor families in CD4+ T cells in pericarotid LNs (Figure 3K). We discovered that the chemokine receptor family was mainly expressed in CD4+ Treg cells and CD4+ proliferating T cells, suggesting that these cells may have migratory properties. There were differences in the migrating ability of the 2 cell types; CD4+ Treg cells have low expression of C‐C chemokine receptor (CCR) 5 and high expression of CCR7. In addition, we found that CD4+ proliferating T cells in pericarotid LNs overexpressed CCR5 and underexpressed CCR7.

scRNA‐Seq Analysis of CD8 + T Cells in Pericarotid LNs

We subdivided CD8+ T‐cell population into the following 4 subpopulations (Figure 4A through 4D and 4G, Figure S11, and Table S5): CD8+ naive T cells (CD8+ naive T cells, expressing CD3D, CD8A, TCF7, and LEF1) enriched in ribosome assembly and protein translation, CD8+ central memory T cells (CD8+ Tcm, expressing CD3D, CD8A, IL7R, and ANXA1) enriched in interleukin‐2 and other cytokines, CD8+ effector memory T cells (Tem cells; expressing CD3D, CD8A, GZMK, and GZMH) which were functionally enriched in cell adhesion regulation, and CD8+ Treg cells (expressing CD3D, CD8A, and CTLA4), which were functionally enriched in lymphocyte activation and regulation. Enlarged LNs contained a greater proportion (P<0.001) of CD8+ naive T cells than nonenlarged LNs (Figure 4E). Overall, CD8+ T‐cell function was enriched in cytotoxic effects and cell death (Figure 4F). Compared with nonenlarged LNs, CD8+ T cells in enlarged LNs perform stronger protein translation; in contrast, CD8+ T cells in nonenlarged LNs play a primary role in other immune cell regulation (Figure 4H and 4I). Compared with normal LNs, the phenotypes of CD8+ T cells in pericarotid LNs exhibit significant enrichment in T‐cell activation pathways and pathways associated with the positive regulation of cytokine signaling (Figure 4J, Figure S12A).

We investigated the expression of chemokine receptor families in CD8+ T cells, given that part of T cells infiltrated in plaques are CD8+ T cells 15 (Figure 4K). We noticed that the chemokine receptor family was mainly expressed in CD8+ Tcm and Tem cells, indicating the migrating potential of these cells. We observed that CD8+ Tcm cells had a high CCR7 and a low CCR5 expression. Simultaneously, we noted that CD8+ Tem cells had a low CCR7 and a high CCR5 expression. In addition, we observed that CCR7 was upregulated in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells of pericarotid LNs compared with normal LNs (Figures 3J and 4J). This may indicate that more T cells were homing and being trapped in the pericarotid LNs of patients with carotid atherosclerosis, which also partially explains the enlarged LNs.

Single‐Cell TCR Sequencing of Pericarotid LNs

Single‐cell TCR sequencing enables comprehensive analysis and visualization of TCR clonotypes, providing insights into the diversity and functional characteristics of the immune system. To rigorously assess T cell clonality, we defined clonal TCR clonotypes as those represented by two or more T cells sharing identical VDJC gene segments and complementarity‐determining region 3 nucleotide sequences. We identified that the predominant clonal T cell populations are primarily distributed within the CD8+ T cells, which are more pronounced in enlarged LNs (Figure 5A–5C and 5G). Compared to non‐enlarged LNs, the clonal expansion of CD8+ T cells in enlarged LNs is primarily concentrated within Tcm and Tem cells (Figure 5D and 5E). However, the most abundant TCR clonotypes, particularly in the complementarity‐determining region 3 region, exhibited significant diversity among different patients (Figure 5F). This observation showed a more active immune response in the enlarged peri‐carotid LNs. The increased clonal expansion of CD8+ T cells, particularly Tcm and Tem subsets, indicated an ongoing antigen‐specific immune surveillance and activation in these regions. Previous studies have demonstrated an association between enlarged LNs and vulnerable plaques and the ability of CD8+ Tcm and Tem cells to migrate. The clonal expansion of CD8+ T cells in LNs surrounding atherosclerotic lesions may influence atherosclerotic plaque stability.

Myeloid cells, including neutrophils, DCs, and macrophages, profoundly affect atherosclerosis occurrence and progression. 20 Therefore, we investigated the single‐cell transcriptome of myeloid cells in LNs. We defined 7 different cell populations, including 4 DC subpopulations, 2 neutrophils, and 1 macrophage population (Figure 6A–6C, Figure S13A, S13C and Table S5). The myeloid cell group's proportions are summarized in Figure S13B. The plasmacytoid DC proportion increased and the DC proportion decreased in the enlarged LNs (P<0.001).

In our study, the DC group was composed of the following 4 subgroups (Figure 6A through 6C and Figure S13): plasmacytoid DCs (expressing LILRA4, ILT7, and RUNX2) which were functionally enriched in toll‐like receptor signaling, conventional DC1 (expressing CLEC9A and CADM1) and conventional DC2 (expressing CLEC10A) mainly playing a role in antigen processing and presentation, and AXL+SIGLEC6+ DC (expressing AXL and SIGLEC6), which may play a regulatory role in cell–cell adhesion. Through DEG and GO enrichment analysis, we discovered that these DCs may cooperate to complete antigen processing and presentation and initiate or regulate immune response.

In atherosclerosis, macrophages can reversely transport cholesterol through lymphatic‐dependent pathways, thereby reducing plaque burden. 9 In addition, macrophages migrating to the draining LNs can present antigens and induce immune responses. 1 Therefore, we studied the macrophage population (expressing CD68 and C1QA). We discovered that these macrophages have elevated gene expression related to cholesterol metabolism and transport, such as ApoE and ApoC (Figure 6D). Through GO functional enrichment analysis, we found that unlike some of the macrophages in the plaque that are involved in the inflammatory process, 15 macrophages in LNs may participate in plasma lipoprotein particle level regulation, high‐density lipoprotein particle remodeling, cholesterol efflux, and cholesterol transport (Figure 6E). Immunofluorescence analysis of LN sections revealed colocalized expressions of C1QA and apolipoprotein E, confirming the presence of macrophages associated with lipid metabolism (Figure 6F and Figure S14A). Therefore, these macrophages may play an important role in translymphatic reverse cholesterol transport and influence plasma lipoprotein levels. Simultaneously, we discovered that these macrophages had an antigen processing and presentation function (Figure 6E), which may trigger an immune response by presenting atherosclerotic antigens in the LNs. In addition, we extracted myeloid cell data and analyzed various chemokine expression. We noted that macrophages primarily expressed multiple chemokines in the myeloid cells of pericarotid LNs (Figure 6G). Therefore, these macrophages cannot function on their own; however, they can play a complex role by influencing the migration of other immune and inflammatory cells.

We identified 2 groups of neutrophils: PLIN2+ (expressing S100A8, S100A9, and PLIN2) and CXCL8+ (expressing S100A8, S100A9, and CXCL8). We observed that CXCL8+ neutrophils have migratory and chemotactic properties (Figure 6H). Notably, we found that PLIN2+ neutrophils overexpressed OLR1, LDLR, LRP1, ACSL, and PLIN2 genes, which were involved in the recognition, internalization, and degradation of ox‐LDL; lipid metabolism; and lipid droplet formation and stabilization 21 , 22 (Figure 6I and 6J). Immunofluorescence in LN sections suggested that the neutrophilic marker S100A8+S100A9 was colocalized with LDL receptor, and surrounding ox‐LDL could be observed (Figure 6K and Figure S14B). LNs are important for reverse cholesterol transport; therefore, cholesterol that is reversely transported through lymph vessels may be degraded by LN neutrophils.

scRNA‐Seq Analysis of Stromal Cells in Pericarotid LNs

SCs demonstrate the structural reticular networks of LNs and promote lymphocyte migration, survival, antigen encounter, and tolerance. 23 Based on marker gene expression, we categorized the SCs into 8 clusters (Figure 7A–7D, Figure S15A and Table S5): TNF+ T‐zone fibroblastic reticular cells (TNF+ TRC, expressing CCL19 and TNF), IL1B+ TRC (expressing CCL19 and IL1B), TUBA4A+ TRC (expressing CCL19 and TUBA4A), blood endothelial cells (expressing CD31 and JAM2), LECs (expressing CD31 and PROX1), perivascular cell (expressing ACTA2, MYL9, and NOTCH3), marginal zone reticular cells (expressing TNFSF11), and follicular DCs (expressing CR2 and CXCL13). The SC group's proportions were summarized in Figure S15B. The perivascular cell proportion increased (P < 0.001) and the TRCs proportion decreased (P<0.001) in the enlarged LNs.

TRCs constitute a rigid 3‐dimensional network allowing DCs to adhere and enhance the probability of successful encounters between activated DCs and cognate T cells. 24 Following LN swelling, TRCs proliferate rapidly to form larger fibroblast networks for accommodating more lymphocytes. 25 In our data, we detected 3 TRC subclusters. GO term analysis suggested that TNF+ TRC was enriched for immune cell differentiation and response to TNF, IL1B+ TRC for antigen processing and presentation, and TUBA4A+ TRC for cytokine production and leukocyte cell–cell adhesion (Figure S15C through S15E). Comparing the 2 groups, TRCs in enlarged LNs significantly expressed genes involved in antigen processing and the presentation of peptide antigens via major histocompatibility class II (Figure 7E through 7G); the processing and presentation of antigen by TRC cells and the slightly increased level in enlarged LNs, can guide T‐cell tolerance to prevent an excessive immune response. 26

LECs constitute a lymphatic network that affects systemic immunity and is important in regulating immune cell transport and homeostasis. 23 GO enrichment analysis suggested that LECs (expressing CD31 and PROX1) were primarily related to the regulation of the inflammatory and coagulation process (Figure 7H). We investigated the expression of the chemokine family genes in SCs, given that SCs such as LECs play an important role in lymphocyte input to LNs and export from LNs. LECs in enlarged LNs express higher chemokine levels than those in nonenlarged LNs (Figure 7I and 7J). This may result in a positive balance between cell interception and output in the LNs. 27 Concurrently, we observed that LECs in enlarged LNs expressed higher adhesion protein gene levels such as P‐selectin and E‐selectin genes, suggesting that they enhanced cell‐to‐cell adhesion (Figure S15F through S15I), which may prevent the lymphocyte exports from the LNs. In conclusion, LEC in enlarged LNs leads to impaired cell output through higher chemokines and adhesion protein expression. First, this will lead to an increase in the number of cells in the LNs. Second, the obstruction of the export of Treg cells and other lymphocytes from LNs will affect atherosclerosis. 28 , 29

The LN blood endothelial cells is primarily responsible for the formation and development of blood vessels (Figure S15J). Blood endothelial cells in nonenlarged LNs are more capable of development and migration and undergo increased angiogenesis. However, the enlarged LN blood vessels have completed development, and blood endothelial cells mainly participate in regulating inflammatory factor production such as interleukin‐1 (Figure S15K).

Additionally, we detected other SC subclusters. perivascular cell express genes involved in extracellular connective tissue development and extracellular matrix construction (Figure S15L). Marginal zone reticular cells express genes involved in cell chemotaxis and migration (Figure S15M). Follicular DCs express genes involved in humoral immune response and lymphocyte migration (Figure S15N).

Discussion

Extensive experimental and clinical evidence has demonstrated that atherosclerosis is an chronic inflammatory disease involving the innate and adaptive immune systems. 1 , 8 , 20 However, secondary lymphatic organs' role in atherosclerosis and the homing, circulation, and activation pathways of related immune cells is under investigation. Some studies have revealed enlargement of LN‐draining lesions in older atherosclerotic mice. Furthermore, lymphatic vessels, which collect in the surrounding LNs and promote transplant arteriosclerosis, are formed in allograft vessels. 13 A recent study revealed that improving Treg cell output from LNs reduces atherosclerosis. 29 However, these studies involved mouse models, which had significant limitations regarding the results application in humans. Ruptured or unstable plaques that are absent in mouse models are more likely to cause clinical ischemia. Current studies have failed to provide a gene expression map describing the periarterial LNs in atherosclerosis, which is critical for understanding localized immune responses and potential therapeutic targets. In this study, we evaluated the association of pericarotid LNs with plaque instability and recent ischemic symptoms. Subsequently, we applied single‐cell RNA sequencing to pericarotid LNs from patients with carotid atherosclerosis to characterize variants of lymphoid, myeloid, and SC subclusters at the single‐cell level.

The peripheral LNs are vital secondary lymphatic organs that initiate adaptive immune responses. 11 Immunogenic substances such as oxidized lipoproteins deposited in the intima is taken up by antigen‐presenting cells and subsequently presented to T cells in draining LNs to induce an adaptive immune response. 8 , 30 Notably, we discovered that the carotid plaques exhibited more severe inflammation in patients with enlarged LNs. Furthermore, inflammatory plaques are more likely to rupture, leading to ischemic symptoms caused by thrombosis and distal embolism. 31 In addition, we confirmed that enlarged pericarotid LNs indicated recent ischemic incidence in patients with carotid atherosclerosis. Meanwhile, we observed that the diameter of LNs was negatively associated with some atherosclerotic risk biomarkers and positively associated with the protective biomarker HDL‐C. This suggested that the changes in the pericarotid LNs may be the result of the combined action of various factors under the condition of atherosclerosis, a systemic disease. However, the complexity and limitations of biochemical markers such as HDL‐C as biomarkers of atherosclerosis must also be considered. 32 Recent studies have found that the quality rather than the quantity of HDL particles is more strongly associated with atherosclerosis. Together, these results suggested that pericarotid LNs could be a potential marker for screening patients with carotid atherosclerosis requiring surgery. Regarding lumen stenosis degree, pericarotid LNs may aid clinicians in determining the degree of inflammation of the carotid plaque, thereby providing a better guide for clinical decision making.

T cells predominate in human plaques compared with the predominating myeloid cells in mouse plaques. 15 B cells primarily reside in the outer membrane of the artery and rarely in plaques. 33 However, these lymphocytes have a profound effect on atherosclerosis. We discovered that enlarged LNs contained a greater proportion of naive B and T cells (P<0.001). This indicates that when the immune response of enlarged LNs is strengthened, more naive lymphocytes are recruited, and they mature under the assistance and stimulation of T follicular helper cells and DCs. 34 The results of single‐cell TCR/BCR sequencing also confirmed that enlarged pericarotid LNs were in a stronger state of immune activation. Meanwhile, according to the results of DEGs and GO analysis, lymphocytes in enlarged LNs have more intense ribosome production, which will assist more protein synthesis; in contrast, lymphocytes in nonenlarged LNs primarily play a regulatory role. These results indicate a strong immune response activation in symptomatic patients with enlarged LNs. Most CD4+ T cells and a small number of CD8+ cells are the main infiltrating immune cells in human plaques. 15 Despite the discovery of the function of the CCR family mediating lymphocyte migration, the specific mechanism of T cells reaching plaques is poorly understood. Previous studies have mainly discovered that T cell migration through LNs to the arterial plaque may be mediated by CCR5, and CCR7 is involved in lymphocyte retention in LNs. 27 , 28 , 35 In our study, we identified 2 cell types in the LNs with the potential to migrate to arterial plaques: CD4+ proliferative T cells and CD8+ Tem cells. Both cell types had high CCR5 and low CCR7 expression. Studies on these 2 cell types could reveal the homing mechanism of lymphocytes in LNs to arterial plaques and the influence of related lymphocytes on atherosclerosis progression. We also found that CD4+ Treg cells have low expression of CCR5 and high expression of CCR7, which may lead to the interception of CD4+ Treg cells in LNs. Inhibiting the export of CD4+ Treg cells from LNs, which play a protective role in atherosclerosis, can aggravate atherosclerosis. 6 , 29 However, these studies were all conducted in mice, and their generalization to humans has some limitations. The function of these chemokine receptors mediating lymphocyte migration in humans is poorly understood.

Studies have demonstrated abnormal clonal proliferation of various T‐cell subtypes in atherosclerosis, especially CD8+ T‐cell clonality, which is associated with disease progression. 36 , 37 Our single‐cell TCR sequencing results suggested greater clonal expansion of CD8+ T cells in enlarged LNs. Previous studies have also found CD8+ T cells show greater clonal expansion than CD4 T+ cells in atherosclerotic plaque. The consistency of this clonal proliferation between plaques and secondary lymphoid organs suggests the deterioration of peripheral T‐cell tolerance and the important role of CD8+ T cells and their clonal expansion in atherosclerotic immunity. In addition, we found that B cells in enlarged LNs exhibited greater clonal expansion than those in nonenlarged LNs. It has been found that autoimmune B‐cell clones are closely related to the development of atherosclerosis. 38 However, it has not been determined whether these clone‐expanded B cells in our study are autoimmune. In conclusion, the design of specific antibodies based on specifically amplified B‐cell and T‐cell clones in plaques may provide a useful tool for revealing the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis and assessing or mitigating the progression of atherosclerosis.

Macrophages can reverse cholesterol transport through lymphatic‐dependent pathways to reduce the plaque burden. 9 In addition, macrophages migrating to draining LNs performed antigen‐presenting functions to induce immune responses. 1 In our study, we observed that macrophages in LNs highly expressed the APOE and APOC genes involved in cholesterol efflux. The APOE gene is vital in cholesterol efflux, especially in cholesterol‐loaded macrophage foam cells and in reverse cholesterol transport. 39 Furthermore, we discovered that the macrophages expressed other genes (CLU and PLTP) involved in cholesterol metabolism. These findings indicated the possibility of macrophages migrating from plaques and participating majorly in reverse cholesterol transport; therefore, enhancing the cholesterol efflux pathways may promote plaque regression. In addition, macrophages were enriched for antigen processing and presentation, suggesting that antigen‐phagocytic macrophages in plaques may be involved in the initiation of atherosclerotic immune responses in LNs. In our study, 2 neutrophil types were observed in the LNs. Notably, we discovered that PLIN2+ neutrophils expressed OLR1, LDLR, and LRP1 involved in lipid endocytosis. In addition, PLIN2+ neutrophils also expressed ACSL1 and PLIN2, which are involved in lipid biosynthesis and the maintenance of lipid storage droplets, respectively. These findings indicate that these neutrophils can engulf LDL and ox‐LDL, undergo lipid metabolism, and eventually form lipid droplets within the cell for lipid storage. These findings suggest that neutrophils may be involved in lipid metabolism, which endocytoses LDL and ox‐LDL and facilitates lipid synthesis and storage as lipid droplets. In a previous single‐cell study of the LNs, researchers discovered that LECs can scavenge modified LDL. Therefore, LNs may play an important role in clearing LDL and ox‐LDL. However, the possibility of the neutrophils migrating to the draining LNs after plaque formation cannot be excluded.

SCs maintain LNs' structure and regulate immune cell trafficking, fluid flow, and LN homeostasis. 23 In our study, we noted that TRCs in enlarged LNs expressed higher major histocompatibility complex II gene levels, which are involved in antigen processing and presentation, than in nonenlarged LNs. We hypothesized that TRCs in enlarged LNs may be more efficiently present tissue‐restricted antigens to enhance T‐cell tolerance and prevent an excessive immune response 40 ; however, the enhanced antigen presentation may indicate an elevated autoimmune response in symptomatic patients. In addition, we discovered that lymphatic endothelial cells in enlarged LNs adhered more strongly to lymphocytes than those in nonenlarged LNs, inhibiting lymphocyte output and resulting in an increased lymphocyte count in the LNs and LN enlargement. Miyazaki et al found that inhibiting of Treg cells on lymphatic endothelial cells aggravated atherosclerosis. 29 Therefore, the inhibition of lymphocyte output from LNs aggravates atherosclerosis in patients with enlarged pericarotid LNs. There are several considerations warranting acknowledgment in this study's design and execution. The number of samples included to construct single‐cell transcription profiles was small. The process by which immune cells migrated between plaques and LNs through the lymphatic and circulatory canals is crucial to the atherosis‐related immune response; however, direct experimental investigation of this phenomenon was not conducted, and was poorly understood. In addition, the lack of control in multiple comparisons is also a limitation of this article.

In conclusion, our data indicated that symptomatic patients with carotid atherosclerosis were more likely to have enlarged peri‐carotid LNs. Therefore, enlarged pericarotid LNs may be associated with more intense plaque inflammation, indicating plaque vulnerability. We presented a comprehensive transcriptional landscape of the pericarotid LNs in symptomatic and nonsymptomatic patients with carotid atherosclerosis. We revealed single‐cell level differences in lymphocyte transcription between enlarged and nonenlarged LNs. We also explored the single‐cell gene expression and function of myeloid and SCs in pericarotid LNs in the context atherosclerosis. By single‐cell TCR/BCR sequencing, we constructed an immune repertoire atlas of pericarotid LNs in patients with carotid atherosclerosis. We found that CD8+ T and B cells showed greater clonal expansion in enlarged pericarotid LNs, indicating the important role of these immune cells and their clonal expansion in atherosclerosis. All in all, we provided an unprecedented view of the suggestive significance and functional remodeling of the pericarotid LNs in atherosclerosis, which helps in refining the roles of LNs in the association of immune response with atherosclerosis pathogenesis and suggests prospective immunotherapy targets. Future studies should focus on validating these findings in larger patient cohorts.
