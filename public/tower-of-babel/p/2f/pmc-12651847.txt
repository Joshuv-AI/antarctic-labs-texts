Roseburia hominis enriched by baicalin reverses the non-response to metformin via upregulating linolenic acid metabolism
Authors: Zenghui Miao, Jianglan Long, Bangrong Huang, Dan Yan, Aiting Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryMetformin is the most commonly used hypoglycemic drug for patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D), but about 30% of patients show non-response potentially linked to gut microbiota imbalance. Although baicalin exhibits potent gut microbiota-modulating activity, its role in reversing metformin non-response remains unclear. Here, we recruited patients with T2D who were non-responders to metformin treatment and collected their fecal samples to construct a humanized mouse model via fecal microbial transplantation. We found that baicalin combined with metformin improved the abnormal glucose tolerance in non-response mice, in which Roseburia hominis was considerably enriched. Mechanically, baicalin combined with metformin activated the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)/acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC)/carnitine palmitoyl transferase 1 (CPT1) pathway, and its enriched R. hominis promoted linolenic acid metabolism, thus reversing the non-response to metformin. Besides, the efficacy of R. hominis in reversing the non-response of metformin was dependent on phospholipase A2 (linolenic acid metabolism key enzyme). Our findings provide feasibility strategies for the metformin treatment of non-responsive patients.

Introduction

Diabetes is one of the fastest-growing global health emergencies of the 21st century, in which type 2 diabetes (T2D) accounts for 90% of diabetes.1 Metformin is recommended as the basic hypoglycemic drug for patients with T2D in the guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of diabetes, and it is also the basic drug in the combination regimen.2 However, it is worth noting that not all patients with T2D benefit from metformin. According to the statistics of clinical data, approximately 30% of patients taking metformin have unsatisfactory curative effects or even no effect.3 In addition, studies have shown that more than 60% of patients with T2D have unsatisfactory or even ineffective results after treatment with metformin for 2–5 years, and the clinical efficacy of metformin decreases with increasing duration of treatment.4,5,6 Because of genetic diversity, the therapeutic effects of metformin differ among individuals, which is considered a potential factor leading to non-response (NR) to metformin.7,8 In addition, the gut microbiota could affect the efficacy of metformin.9 According to previous reports, an imbalance in the gut microbiota reduces the efficacy of metformin and interferes with its action pathway, resulting in the unsatisfactory efficacy of metformin.10 Therefore, a possible solution to the NR of metformin is provided from the perspective of regulating gut microbiota.

Baicalin is the main bioactive component of Scutellaria baicalensis Georgi, exhibiting a range of bioactivities, including anti-inflammatory, anti-bacterial, antioxidant, hepatoprotective, and antiviral effects.11 However, it is difficult to be absorbed by the gastrointestinal tract and has low bioavailability.12 Therefore, baicalin needs to be decomposed or metabolized into free flavonoids or secondary metabolites under the action of the gut microbiota to be absorbed and utilized by the human body.13,14 A study of high-fat diet (HFD)-induced insulin-resistant mice revealed that baicalin treatment significantly regulated the composition of the gut microbiota, thus improving the intestinal microecosystem imbalance and metabolic disorders caused by HFD.15,16 However, whether baicalin can reverse the metformin NR by regulating the gut microbiota is unknown and needs to be further explored.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to explore whether baicalin combined with metformin could reverse the NR to metformin and the underlying mechanism. In our previous research, we successfully constructed a humanized mouse model by fecal transplantation of the gut microbiota from clinical metformin-treated non-responders and reproduced the clinical phenotype of NR to metformin.17 This study mainly evaluated the efficacy of baicalin combined with metformin in this mouse model. The effects of baicalin combined with metformin on the gut microbiota were analyzed, and the potential functional bacteria were screened to explore the mechanism of action. Our study provided a new therapeutic strategy for metformin therapy in non-responsive patients, and baicalin may become a promising drug to assist metformin in the treatment of T2D.

Results

Baicalin combined with metformin reversed the NR to metformin

To investigate the curative efficacy of baicalin combined with metformin, a humanized mouse model was established by fecal microbial transplantation (FMT) in HFD mice treated with mixed antibiotics. The mouse experiment performed is shown in Figure 1A. After 4 weeks of HFD feeding, the area under the curve of the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) of the HFD group mice was higher than that of mice fed with the normal chow diet (NCD) (Figure 1B), which suggested that HFD induction successfully established the insulin resistance model.18 After administration, metformin alone did not significantly ameliorate body weight (Figure 1C) and impaired glucose tolerance (Figure 1D). As expected, similar to the clinical responsiveness of metformin, we found that non-responder recipient mice were resistant to metformin treatment. Thus, we successfully constructed a humanized mouse model by FMT of the gut microbiota from clinical metformin treatment non-responders that reproduced our previous experimental results.17 This provided an experimental system for later research work. After 5 weeks of drug administration, baicalin combined with metformin ameliorated OGTT of the NR mice (Figure 1D). Consistent with OGTT, the combination also ameliorated insulin dysfunction and insulin resistance (Figures 1E and 1F). It was worth noting that baicalin had no effect on OGTT, insulin sensitivity, or insulin resistance (Figures S1A‒S1C). Taken together, these results indicated that baicalin combined with metformin achieved the goal of reversing the NR humanized mouse model.

Combined administration activated the AMPK/ACC/CPT1 pathway

Insulin resistance is often associated with blood lipid disorders.20 Inflammation is a defensive immune response or pathological change in host resistance to damage and the development of disease.21 Therefore, lipid metabolism and inflammation after drug treatment were studied. In contrast to the NR group, the combined administration of baicalin and metformin significantly reduced the concentrations of total cholesterol (TC) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) (Figures 2A‒2C), which indicated that the combined administration could improve blood lipid disorder. Compared with the NCD group, the concentration of the pro-inflammatory factor (interleukin [IL]-6) in the NR group mice was significantly increased, while the concentration of the anti-inflammatory factor (IL-10) was significantly decreased, indicating the presence of inflammation in the NR group mice. Baicalin combined with metformin could inhibit the secretion of IL-6 while promoting the secretion of IL-10 (Figures 2D‒2F), implying that baicalin combined with metformin ameliorated inflammation. The major organs involved in the development of insulin resistance include the liver, pancreas, and adipose tissue.22 Therefore, histological sections of the aforementioned tissues from the various groups were examined after 5 weeks of treatment. AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is a central regulator of energy homeostasis. On the one hand, AMPK can promote the energy production of glucose and fat during catabolism; on the other hand, it can inhibit their anabolism, thus achieving metabolic stability.23 At the same time, the AMPK pathway is also one of the key mechanisms by which metformin plays a hypoglycemic role.24,25 Therefore, we explored the protein expression of AMPK and its phosphorylated form by western blotting. The results showed that compared with the NR group, the metformin group did not promote the expression of AMPK phosphorylation, and baicalin combined with metformin significantly increased AMPK Thr172 phosphorylation (Figure 2H). Importantly, the results of our present investigation also suggested that baicalin combined with metformin could improve lipid metabolism. AMPK plays a central role in controlling lipid metabolism through modulating the downstream acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) and carnitine palmitoyl transferase 1 (CPT1) pathway.26 ACC is the first rate-limiting enzyme in the lipogenesis process, which plays an important role in fatty acid synthesis and fatty acid oxidation.27 CPT1 is the rate-limiting enzyme that transfers the long-chain fatty acyl CoA to the mitochondria for beta oxidation.28 Compared with the NR group, baicalin combined with metformin significantly decreased the expression of ACC protein and increased the expression of CPT1, promoting fatty acid transport into mitochondria for β-oxidation, thereby enhancing lipid catabolism and glucose homeostasis (Figure 2H). In summary, baicalin combined with metformin reversed the metformin NR accompanied by the activation of the AMPK/ACC/CPT1 pathway.

Baicalin combined with metformin enriched Roseburia hominis

Drug treatment for diseases is often accompanied by changes in the gut microbiota.29 To study the effects of baicalin combined with metformin on the gut microbiota, we performed 16S rRNA sequencing on fecal samples. At the species level, the abundance of the gut microbiota of the mice in each group changed significantly after treatment (Figure 3A). According to the Venn diagram, 266 unique operational taxonomic units were shared among the four groups of mice (Figure 3B). Principal coordinate analysis based on beta diversity revealed that baicalin had a significant effect on the gut microbiota composition by promoting phylogenetically unique microbiota different from those in the NCD and NR groups (Figure 3C). The Shannon index and the Chao1 index were ameliorated after treatment, which indicated that baicalin combined with metformin could ameliorate the alpha diversity of the gut microbiota (Figures 3D and 3E). Notably, the species-level proportional abundances, especially R. hominis, Parasutterella excrementihominis, Longicatena caecimuris, and Phascolarctobacterium faecium, were considerably enriched after treatment (Figures 3F and S2). We compared the gut microbiota of the metformin+baicalin group and the metformin group by the function prediction of Picrust2 and found that the regulated gut microbiota might have a regulatory effect on lipid metabolism (Figure 3G). The R. hominis that colonized the NR group likely originated from non-responder patients. Furthermore, metformin monotherapy failed to increase R. hominis abundance in the NR humanized mouse model, but the combination significantly increased the abundance of R. hominis. Therefore, baicalin combined with metformin had a good regulatory effect on R. hominis. In summary, the results indicated that the composition and function of the gut microbiota were significantly altered in response to baicalin combined with metformin treatment and that R. hominis was substantially enriched.

R. hominis administration reversed the NR to metformin

R. hominis is a gram-positive bacterium belonging to Roseburia, one of the key bacteria that degrades dietary fiber xylan in the human gut.30 To investigate the therapeutic effect of R. hominis on metformin unresponsiveness, we still used NR mice as a model (Figure 4A). Interestingly, R. hominis treatment reduced the concentration of 2-h postprandial blood glucose (Figure 4B), implying that R. hominis improved OGTT. Moreover, R. hominis improved insulin dysfunction (Figure 4C) and insulin resistance (Figure 4D). The serum concentrations of TC, LDL-C, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) revealed that R. hominis also improved blood lipid disorders (Figures 4E‒4G). The increased secretion of IL-10 and decreased secretion of IL-1β and IL-6 were associated with the intervention of R. hominis (Figures 4H‒4J), suggesting that R. hominis could improve inflammation. Histological analysis revealed that R. hominis improved hepatic, pancreatic, and epididymal adipose damage, as well as hepatic fat accumulation (Figure 4K). In addition, after R. hominis intervention, the phosphorylated expression of AMPK Thr172 increased, while inhibiting ACC and activating CPT1 (Figure 4L). R. hominis upregulated the expression of phospholipase A2 and fatty acid desaturase 2 to promote linolenic acid metabolism

The potential mechanism of R. hominis affecting host metabolism may be through affecting the secondary metabolites in NR mice. Consequently, it was of interest to know whether treatment alters serum metabolites. To this end, we performed an untargeted metabolomics analysis on serum samples that had been treated with R. hominis. Administration of R. hominis significantly altered a variety of metabolites, including reducing the increase in metabolite concentration induced by NR and increasing the decrease in metabolite concentration caused by NR. As revealed by the principal-component analysis (Figure 5A), the trends of the NCD, NR, and R. hominis groups indicated the presence of systematic metabolic differences among the groups. The pathways altered by R. hominis intervention mainly included lipid metabolism (LA metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, sphingolipid metabolism, and linoleic acid metabolism) and amino acid metabolism (arginine biosynthesis, alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, and lysine degradation), among which LA metabolism was the most prominent metabolic pathway (Figure 5B). The substances we detected located in LA metabolism are phosphatidylcholine, LA, and SDA (Figures 5C‒5E). Compared with the NCD group, the abundance of LA and SDA in the NR group significantly decreased. After R. hominis intervention treatment, the abundance of LA and SDA significantly increased compared to the NR group (Figures 5D and 5E). Querying phosphatidylcholine through the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes database could be metabolized to LA by phospholipase A2 (PLA2), and LA could be metabolized to SDA by fatty acid desaturase 2 (FADS2) (Figure 5F). Therefore, we speculated that the administration of R. hominis regulates the expression of PLA2 and FADS2, thus promoting LA metabolism. Subsequently, we conducted western blot to verify our conjecture. The results showed that the expression of PLA2 and FADS2 increased significantly in the mouse liver after the administration of R. hominis compared to NR mice (Figure 5G). Similarly, baicalin combined with metformin was also the same (Figure S4). In conclusion, the results suggested that R. hominis could promote LA metabolism after intervention.

Darapladib is a potent oral PLA2 inhibitor that reduces PLA2 activity in plasma and in atherosclerotic plaques.31,32 We administered darapladib to the mice for 1 week, which partially inhibited the activity of PLA2 (Figure S5), followed by gavage of R. hominis. At the experimental endpoint, the activity of PLA2 in the mice administered to the darapladib group was significantly reduced (Figure 5H), and the therapeutic effect of R. hominis was impaired (Figures 5I‒5K). Moreover, administration of darapladib did not affect the colonization of R. hominis (Figure S6). Taken together, these results demonstrated that the effect of R. hominis on reversing the NR of metformin was dependent on PLA2-mediated upregulation of the LA metabolic pathway.

Discussion

Metformin has been used in the clinic for more than 60 years, and its clinical value has been recognized by the global medical field.33 As the most commonly used hypoglycemic drug, metformin offers advantages including potent glycemic control, no increased risk of hypoglycemia, multiple potential benefits beyond glycemic regulation, superior cost-effectiveness, and good drug accessibility.34,35 However, more than 30% of patients exhibit poor response to metformin, and gut microbiota dysbiosis has been identified as one of the contributing factors.10 Therefore, regulating the gut microbiota may provide a potential solution for metformin NR.

We constructed a mouse model of metformin NR by FMT.17 On this basis, we verified that baicalin combined with metformin could enrich R. hominis, activate phosphorylation at AMPK Thr172, and promote fatty acid oxidation, thus reversing the NR of metformin. In addition to lipid metabolism, we also performed further validation of the classical signaling pathways involved in glucose metabolism. The results show that baicalin combined with metformin and R. hominis could both inhibit the gluconeogenesis and insulin resistance pathway (Figure S7). In our study, baicalin monotherapy failed to improve the abnormal glucose tolerance and insulin resistance of NR mice. This result contrasts with the conclusions drawn by Minzi Ju et al., who reported that baicalin could improve the blood sugar elevation caused by a long-term HFD.15 Our findings show that the blood sugar of the NR mice has not been significantly improved after 5 weeks of baicalin treatment (Figures S1A‒S1C). Moreover, monotherapy of baicalin in our study also failed to inhibit gluconeogenesis and insulin resistance or promote fatty acid oxidation (Figure S8). This discrepancy could be due to the different model construction methods. To establish humanized mice, we performed pseudo-sterile treatment and FMT on HFD mice, which might cause the microflora difference between HFD mice and NR mice, leading to the differential efficacy of baicalin. However, baicalin could improve abnormal blood lipid metabolism, which aligns with previous research results (Figures S1D‒S1J). When evaluating the regulatory effects on blood lipid metabolism and inflammation, we found that the metformin+baicalin group significantly improved TC and LDL-C levels, suggesting that combined administration could improve lipid metabolism disorders. The significant increase in IL-6 and significant decrease in IL-10 in the NR group indicated that NR induction leads to inflammation in the mouse body. Even though the levels of HDL-C and IL-1β (Figures 2C and 2D) have not changed significantly, it does not affect the conclusion drawn. Similar research results and conclusions have also been reported in other studies.36,37

Roseburia spp. can decompose dietary fiber, especially xylan, and produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) (such as acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid).30 Previous studies have shown that SCFAs can activate the AMPK signaling pathway and inhibit the expression of ACC, thereby alleviating the inhibitory effect on CPT1 and promoting fatty acid oxidation.38,39 In addition to regulating the AMPK pathway, SCFAs can also participate in blood glucose regulation through additional mechanisms, including regulating insulin secretion, improving insulin sensitivity, and promoting intestinal gluconeogenesis.40,41 In our study, the increased abundance of R. hominis was accompanied by improved glucose tolerance, which may be attributed to the combined effects of SCFAs’ direct glucose-regulating actions and their indirect modulation of lipid metabolism.

The main purpose of this study was to highlight the involvement of gut microbiota in the efficacy response of metformin, which does not exclude that other bacteria except R. hominis also have a biological function to reverse metformin NR. According to the experimental results, the abundances of R. hominis, P. excrementihominis, L. caecimuris, and P. faecium increased significantly after baicalin combined with metformin administration (Figures 3F and S2). This initial finding suggested that these bacteria might play important roles in mediating the effects of the combined therapy. It is worth noting that the abundance of R. hominis has not changed in metformin monotherapy, but it has increased significantly after the combination of baicalin. Therefore, this result shows that baicalin has a more obvious regulatory effect on R. hominis than the other three bacteria. From the perspective of biological function, R. hominis has unique advantages. R. hominis plays a positive role in preventing weight gain, improving metabolism, alleviating liver and adipose tissue lesions, and improving gut microbiota and metabolome.42 The biological characteristics and functions of R. hominis make it have more in-depth research value and broad application prospects. It provides important directions for explaining the mechanism of combined administration and optimizing related treatment strategies. In addition, considering the feasibility of the experiment, we finally selected R. hominis as the key therapeutic target and focused on exploring its mechanism in the follow-up study. In our research design, considering that there is currently no method to construct metformin non-responsive model at the cellular level, and the evidence at the animal level is more sufficient than that at the cellular level, we did not conduct relevant validation at the cellular level but conducted the research directly at the animal level. This study revealed that the increase of R. hominis abundance mediated by baicalin could reverse the NR to metformin treatment, and the efficacy of R. hominis depended on the upregulation of LA metabolism mediated by PLA2.

This research proposes a new treatment scheme for the phenomenon of NR to metformin treatment, which has good prospects for clinical transformation and application. In addition, developing this combination as an optimized treatment scheme is in line with current treatment guidelines, which require metformin to be used as the base treatment drug (unless started with metformin or with contraindications to metformin) in combination therapy regimens.43,44 It is worth noting that although we have verified that baicalin combined with metformin could reverse the NR of metformin at the animal level, there are still some challenges before achieving the clinical transformation. On the one hand, this study needs further validation at the clinical level. On the other hand, baicalin capsules are marketed drugs, which use baicalin as its main ingredient, commonly used for adjuvant treatment in cases of acute and chronic hepatitis and persistent hepatitis. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first investigation to assess the effectiveness of combining baicalin in overcoming NR to metformin treatment. Therefore, off-label and ethical issues of baicalin capsules should be addressed before clinical validation. In brief, this study suggested that baicalin reversed the NR to metformin treatment by regulating the gut microbiota, which could provide a reference for further medication.

In conclusion, our study found that combined baicalin and metformin could reverse the metformin NR. R. hominis enriched by the combined administration upregulated the expression of PLA2 and FADS2, thereby promoting LA metabolism. Moreover, the efficacy of R. hominis on reversing the NR of metformin depends on PLA2. We started from the gut microbiota and found that baicalin combined with metformin and its regulated R. hominis could reverse metformin NR, which will provide a new perspective on the treatment of metformin non-responsive patients.

Limitations of the study

This study has preliminarily revealed the mechanism of R. hominis reversing the NR of metformin by regulating the metabolism of LA. However, the identification and functional verification of R. hominis metabolites will help further explore the deep mechanisms underlying the reversal of metformin NR. Conducting research on the biological mechanisms of R. hominis will not only help to clarify the molecular basis of R. hominis regulating host metabolism but also provide a theoretical basis for developing new intervention strategies based on gut microbiota metabolites, which is worthy of further in-depth study. Notably, although this study utilized full-length 16S rRNA sequencing, which provided higher taxonomic resolution compared with shorter hypervariable regions, species-level resolution is not always unambiguous, and some reads may only be reliably classified at the genus level. Although this study found the role of the combination of baicalin and metformin and its enriched R. hominis in reversing the metformin NR, further clinical validation is needed. Moreover, P. excrementihominis, L. caecimuris, P. faecium, and R. hominisregulated metabolites (such as LA and SDA) may also have the potential to reverse the NR to metformin, but it is a pity that their pharmacological effects have not been validated. These are the limitations of this study and the focus of further research.

Experimental model and study participant details

Participant recruiting and sampling

A total of 6 participants were recruited, including 2 females and 4 males. The mean age and glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) of the participants were 47.67 ± 13.37 years and 8.58 ± 1.63%, respectively. The participants were recruited from the Department of Endocrinology, Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University, from March 2023 to December 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows: met the criteria of the American Diabetes Association for type 2 diabetes; metformin monotherapy for 12 weeks and with HbA1c ≥ 7%.3,5,45,46 The exclusion criteria were as follows: pregnancy; severe diabetic complications; severe liver diseases, including chronic refractory hepatitis, cirrhosis, or both hepatitis positive and abnormal liver transaminase; continuous use of antibiotics for more than three days within three months before joining the group; infectious diseases, including tuberculosis and acquired immune deficiency syndrome; and serious organic diseases, including cancer, coronary heart disease, or myocardial infarction. Fecal samples from patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were collected and stored at −80°C. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Board at Beijing Friendship Hospital of Capital Medical University (approval no. 2023-P2-048-01). All participants provided signed written informed consent.

Animals

Five-week-old male C57BL/6J mice (RRID: IMSR_JAX:000664) weighing 16–21 g were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. Mice were fed in a specific pathogen-free environment with a temperature of 20°C–22°C, a relative humidity of 60% ± 5%, and a 12 h light/dark cycle, with free access to diet and water. All animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (approval no. 23–2043) and followed the National Research Council’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

After 1 week of acclimatization, the mice were randomly grouped according to body weight: the NCD group and the HFD group. Mice in the NCD group were fed with a normal chow diet, while mice in the HFD group were fed with a high-fat diet. There were 8 mice in the NCD group and 24 mice in the HFD group. After 4 weeks of high-fat diet feeding, an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was used to evaluate the impaired glucose tolerance. After the model was established, mice in the NCD group continued to be fed with a normal chow diet, while mice in the HFD group continued to be fed with a high-fat diet until the end of the experiment. After that, the mice in the HFD group were given mixed antibiotics by gavage for 1 week. After an interval of two days, fecal microbial transplantation (FMT) was performed on the pseudo-germfree mice for 1 week. The dosage of metformin (Met) and baicalin (BA) was 200 mg/kg/day. After modeling, the mice of the HFD group were divided into the NR, Met, and Met+BA groups according to the OGTT, with 8 mice in each group. To better regulate the gut microbiota, the Met+BA group was given baicalin for 1 week before administration. After 5 weeks of treatment, the feces were collected for gut microbiota analysis.

To evaluate the effects of Roseburia hominis on the NR humanized mouse model, R. hominis (108 CFU, 200 μL) was gavaged to the humanized mouse model daily for 4 weeks. The control groups received 200 μL of sterile water.

In the mice experiment on inhibiting phospholipase A2 (PLA2) activity by administering darapladib, the experiment was still carried out on the NR humanized mouse model. The R. hominis combined with darapladib group received darapladib (10 mg/kg; i.p.) for 1 week before administration, during which other groups were injected with control solvents.47 The drug was subsequently administered continuously for 4 weeks.

Method details

Antibiotic mix (ABX) treatment

The ABX was comprised of ampicillin 100 mg/kg (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), vancomycin 50 mg/kg (Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), neomycin 100 mg/kg (Solarbio), and metronidazole 100 mg/kg (Macklin).48 The mixture was continuously administered for 1 week to remove intestinal symbiotic bacteria.

FMT experiment

As previously reported,49 FMT was performed by thawing fecal pellets from 6 non-responders to metformin (Table S1). The feces were suspended in sterile PBS (1 g feces/10 mL of sterile PBS), and the particles were removed by passing through stainless steel laboratory sieves with a final pore size of 0.25 mm. Each mouse was gavaged with 200 μL every day for 1 week.

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

Roseburia hominis (DSM 16839) was purchased from Ningbo Testobio Co., Ltd. (Ningbo, China). R. hominis was incubated using CMC medium (modified) at 37°C for 10–14 h in an anaerobic incubator. After centrifugation and collection of bacteria, R. hominis was prepared into 109 CFU/mL using sterile water.50

OGTT and insulin tolerance test (ITT)

For the OGTT, the mice that were fasted overnight were administered intragastric glucose (2 g/kg), and then glucose levels were measured at 0, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min in blood samples from the tail tip by a glucometer. The area under the curve (AUC) of blood glucose was calculated to assess tolerance to the glucose load of the mice. ITT was performed by a similar method. Mice that were fasted for 6 h were administered an intraperitoneal injection of 0.75 U/kg insulin, and then glucose levels were measured at 0, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min in blood samples from the tail tip by a glucometer. The AUC of blood glucose was calculated to assess tolerance to insulin load of the mice.

Biochemical, inflammatory factors and histopathological analysis

After the mice were sacrificed, the serum, liver, pancreas, and epididymal adipose were collected for subsequent analysis. Serum samples were used for biochemical and inflammatory factor analysis. The serum concentrations of fasting blood glucose, total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were quantified via kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). The serum concentrations of insulin, interleukin-1β (IL-1β), IL-6, and IL-10 were quantified by ELISA kits from Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd. (Yancheng, China).

The liver, pancreas, and epididymal adipose of the mice were immediately excised and fixed. The fixed pancreas and epididymis fat were used for hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining, and the fixed liver was used for H&E staining and Oil Red O staining as previously reported.51

Measurement of serum Lp-PLA2 activity

As previously reported, serum Lp-PLA2 activity was measured by a PAF Acetylhydrolase Assay Kit from Cayman Chemical.52 The assay uses 2-thio-PAF as the substrate. In brief, 10 μL of plasma was added to 5 μL of 1 mmol/L EGTA and 10 μL of 2 mmol/L 5,5′-dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) in 0.1 mol/L Tris-HCl and then incubated for 30 min at room temperature to allow any free thiols in the sample to react with DTNB. Next, the reactions were initiated by adding a 200 μL substrate solution containing 200 μmol/L 2-thio-PAF. The absorbance was read once every minute at 414 nm. The Lp-PLA2 activity was calculated by the change in absorbance per minute.

Gut microbiota analysis

Isolated DNA was analyzed using 16S rRNA gene sequences to investigate the microbial composition of fecal samples. Nucleic acid extraction was performed using the TGuide S96 Magnetic Soil/Stool DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Cat# DP812). The full-length 16S rRNA gene (covering all hypervariable regions V1-V9) was amplified using universal primers 27F-1492R (primer sequences: Forward [5′-AGRGTTTGATYNTGGCTCAG-3′], Reverse [5′-TASGGHTACCTTGTTASGACTT-3′]). The reaction conditions were as follows: pre-denaturation at 95°C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 50°C for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72°C for 7 min. PCR products that passed the quality inspection were subjected to magnetic bead purification and quantified using a microplate reader. According to the concentration of PCR products, the products were mixed in an equal density ratio, fully mixed, and detected by 1.8% agarose gel electrophoresis, with the main band sequence of 1500 bp as the target band. The target band was recovered using the Monarch DNA Gel Extraction Kit. The 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing of the gut microbiota was conducted by Biomarker (Beijing, China).

The alpha diversity of the gut microbiota was evaluated using the Shannon index and the Chao1 index. To evaluate the beta diversity, principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was visualized. Use Venn diagram to demonstrate shared and unique operational taxonomic units (OTUs) among NCD, NR, Met, and Met+BA groups of mice. To predict the functional profiles of the gut microbiota, PICRUSt analysis was used based on sequencing data, KEGG databases (RRID:SCR_012773). The sequencing data has been deposited into the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under the accession number PRJNA1282467 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra/PRJNA1282467).

Western blot

The mice liver tissue was lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, Beijing, China, R0010) containing PMSF (Solarbio, Beijing, China, P0100) to extract protein. The BCA protein assay kit (KeyGEN BioTECH, Jiangsu, China, KGPBCA) was used to determine the protein concentration. Equal amounts of protein were boiled in SDS-PAGE loading buffer for 15 min, separated on 6% and 10% SDS-PAGE gels, and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010). The membranes were blocked with 5% dry nonfat milk in TBST. These membranes were then incubated at 4°C overnight with primary antibodies against AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) (Proteintech, Cat# 10929-2-AP, RRID:AB_2169568, 1:4,000), insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS1) (Proteintech, Cat# 17509-1-AP, RRID:AB_10596914, 1:1,000), phospho-AMPKα (Thr172) (40H9) (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 2535, RRID:AB_331250, 1:1,000), phospho-IRS-1 (Ser636/639) (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat# 2388, RRID:AB_330339, 1:1,000), phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PCK) (Proteintech, Cat# 16754-1-AP, RRID:AB_2160031, 1:30,000), glucose-6-phosphatase (G6PC) (Proteintech, Cat# 66860-1-Ig, RRID:AB_2882199, 1:5,000), mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) (Proteintech, Cat# 66888-1-Ig, RRID:AB_2882219, 1:30,000), fatty acid desaturase 2 (FADS2) (Proteintech, Cat# 28034-1-AP, RRID:AB_2918142, 1:4,000), phospholipase A2 group (PLA2G) (Proteintech, Cat# 18088-1-AP, RRID:AB_10859777, 1:1,000), acetyl-CoA Carboxylase 1 (ACC1) (Proteintech, Cat# 21923-1-AP, RRID:AB_11042445, 1:4,000), carnitine palmitoyl transferase 1 (CPT1) (Proteintech, Cat# 15184-1-AP, RRID:AB_2084676, 1:50,000), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (Proteintech, Cat# 10494-1-AP, RRID:AB_2263076, 1:20,000), β-tubulin (Proteintech, Cat# 10094-1-AP, RRID:AB_2210695, 1:10,000). The membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies after being washed with tris-buffered saline and Tween. HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Mouse IgG(H + L) (Proteintech, Cat# SA00001-1, RRID: AB_2722565) and HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG(H + L) (Proteintech, Cat# SA00001-2, RRID: AB_2722564), both secondary antibodies were diluted at a ratio of 1:10,000. Finally, the membrane was scanned using the Tanon 4800 multi-automatic chemiluminescence imaging analysis system (Shanghai Tanon Science & Technology Co., Ltd.). Images were analyzed using ImageJ (RRID:SCR_003070) software.

Metabolomics analysis

After thawing the serum samples at 4°C and vortexing for 10 s, 100 μL of serum was mixed with 300 μL of a methanol solution. After vortexing for 60 s, the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4°C for 10 min to precipitate the protein. A 300 μL supernatant was transferred into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube and evaporated under vacuum at low temperature. Subsequently, 100 μL of 50% (v/v) methanol aqueous solution was added to the tube, and the residue was redissolved with ultrasound. The centrifuge tube was then placed in a low-temperature centrifuge and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm and 4°C for 10 min. Finally, 90 μL of supernatant was transferred to an autosampler vial (ANPEL Laboratory Technologies Inc.) for analysis. Furthermore, to evaluate the stability of the system, 20 μL aliquots of serum from each sample were pooled to form the quality control (QC) sample. Pretreatment of the QC samples was performed in parallel to the analytical samples using the same method. In the sample analysis sequence, one QC sample was inserted after every 8 analytical samples, and 2 QC samples were injected consecutively at the start of system equilibration.

A Dionex UltiMate 3000 ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA) was used to separate the metabolites in serum with an injection volume of 6 μL for all samples, employing an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm; Waters) at a column temperature of 50°C. Mobile phase A contained 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (HPLC grade) in aqueous solution, and mobile phase B was methanol (HPLC grade). The gradient elution was as follows with a flow rate of 0.30 mL/min: 0–1.0 min, 2% B; 1.0–5.5 min, 2 to 100% B; 5.5–14.0 min, 100% B; 14.0–14.1 min, 100% B back to 2% B; 14.1–16.0 min, 2% B.

The mass spectrometry data of the metabolites were acquired by Q Exactive (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with electrospray ionization in positive and negative ion modes. The auxiliary gas flow rate was 10 arbitrary units (a.u.). The temperatures of the ion source and capillary were maintained at 300°C and 320°C, respectively. For positive and negative ion modes, the spray voltage and the sheath gas flow rate were set at 3.70 kV and 30 a.u.; and 3.50 kV and 30 a.u., respectively. Both the sheath gas and the auxiliary gas were nitrogen gas; the collision gas was nitrogen with a pressure of 1.5 mTorr. Full scan mass spectra were acquired in positive/negative ion polarity switching mode (thus, positive ion and negative ion spectra were obtained simultaneously) from m/z 150 to 1500 with a resolution of 70,000. The automatic gain control (AGC) target (the number of ions required to fill the C-trap) was set as 1×106 with a maximum injection time of 50 ms. In tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) mode, the precursor ions were fragmented at 10, 30 and 60 eV collision energy, with the AGC target set as 1×105 and a maximum injection time of 50 ms, with the isolation window set as 2.0 m/z unit at a resolution of 17,500 in ddMS2 mode.

The acquired data were processed using Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, 3.0) software, following a sequential workflow: raw data import, peak alignment, peak extraction, and deconvolution steps. Finally, a table containing retention time, mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), and peak intensity was generated. Various adduct ions (e.g., [M + H]+, [M+Na]+) were deconvoluted to obtain individual ion features for subsequent metabolite identification. Additionally, ion features with a coefficient of variation (CV) > 15% in quality control samples were excluded to obtain reliable and reproducible metabolites. Handling of missing values: First, indicators with an excessive number of zero values were directly removed (with a critical threshold of 20%). For missing value imputation, the values were filled with 1/5 of the minimum value. The Human Metabolome Database (HMDB, www.hmdb.ca) and LIPID MAPS (www.lipidmaps.org) were used for primary molecular weight matching to identify metabolites. The mass-to-charge ratio or accurate molecular mass of the detected ions was compared against database entries; the error was limited to 5 ppm.

All data collected in positive and negative ion mode were acquired and processed using Thermo Xcalibur 4.2 and Compound Discoverer 3.3 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The resulting matrix data included the associated retention time, formula, molecular weight, and ion peak area. The data were then exported to the Simca 14.1 software package (Umetrics AB, Umea, Sweden) for multivariate analysis. Prior to formal analysis using Simca 14.1 software, the dataset was subjected to normalization via total ion current normalization to obtain more intuitive and reliable results. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to assess changes in the overall metabolite patterns between groups. To obtain information on metabolites that lead to significant differences, a supervised multidimensional statistical method, namely orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), was further applied to conduct statistical analysis of samples from the NCD and NR groups. In the OPLS-DA model, a metabolite with VIP >1.0 and p < 0.05 was considered to have significance. A permutation test was performed 200 times to assess the risk of overfitting for the OPLS-DA model. To explore the metabolic pathway changes, a KEGG (RRID:SCR_018145) pathway enrichment analysis was performed.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed by GraphPad Prism 10.0 software. The data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The number of animals was exhibited as “n”, which was indicated in figure legends. Statistics were performed by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), one-way ANOVA (or the Kruskal-Wallis test), or Student’s t test (or the Mann-Whitney test), where appropriate. Sample size for each experiment and the statistical tests used are specified in figure legends. Individual data points were only excluded if outliers were detected during the analysis procedure. Statistically significant findings were marked according to the following cutoffs: ∗, p < 0.05; ∗∗, p < 0.01; ∗∗∗, p < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗, p < 0.0001.
