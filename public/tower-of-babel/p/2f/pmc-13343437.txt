A soft perceptive multimodal locomotion robot via double-helix fiber-reinforced artificial muscles
Authors: Yufeng Wang, Chenchen Li, Yulian Peng, Yingao Xu, Jinxing Wang, Zhipeng Wei, Zhengyan Wang, Houping Wu, Hongbo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPneumatic artificial muscle has a high payload-to-weight ratio and rapid response with rich form factors and great design flexibility, making it a promising candidate for muscle-driven bioinspired robots. Here, we propose a perceptive pneumatic artificial muscle (PPAM) with self-length sensing via a seamlessly integrated double-helix conductive fiber coil. The double-helix coil fiber restricts the radial expansion of the silicone tube to achieve axial extension in actuation and measures its own length with a resolution of 10 μm by monitoring its self-inductance. A perceptive multimodal locomotion robot using three PPAMs as a bundle was developed, with crawling, turning, lateral movement, and rolling capabilities. By applying machine learning to analyze the three-channel inductance changes caused by body deformation under different loading conditions, the PPAM-based soft robot can recognize different external stimuli with an average accuracy of 99.28%. It is demonstrated that the soft robot can recognize a deep pit and bypass it automatically, change its gait amplitude to crawl through a low-roof tunnel, and sense its body posture change when a side flip occurs and automatically rolls back to its original position. In summary, the robot can perceive its own state and environment like a biological worm and can actively adjust its locomotion gait/mode to navigate itself through diverse terrains.Keywords: soft perceptive robot, perceptive pneumatic artificial muscle, self-inductance, multimodal locomotion, terrain-adaptive movement

Introduction

During the last decade, soft robotics has emerged as a prominent research field,1,2,3 driven by robotic technologies and material science. Biological muscles have strong ductility4 and elasticity,5 and can sense their own stretching state in real time, benefiting from their special structure and internal proprioception.6,7 Among all actuation technologies, pneumatic artificial muscles have attracted more attention due to their actuation characteristics that closely resemble those of biological muscle.8,9 Owing to the lack of sensing capabilities, the complex behavior of hyperelastic materials10 and the large deformation of soft bodies,11 it is common to encounter large errors in muscle motion prediction based on traditional kinematics models. This imposes significant limitations on the practical use of artificial muscles. Therefore, proprioception is the key to achieving biological muscle-like functionalities in artificial muscles.12

Traditional rigid sensors are incompatible with soft actuators made of silicone rubber. Recent advancements in the development of soft sensors and electronics have provided promising solutions to this challenge.13,14,15 In particular, polymeric functional materials exhibit multi-stimuli responsiveness and, when combined with conductive fillers, they can be engineered into sensors integrated within soft and deformable substrate.16,17 This provides new opportunities for developing soft actuators with built-in self-perception capabilities. In recent years, researchers have proposed sensorized pneumatic actuators via various principles, including resistive,18,19,20,21,22,23 optical,24,25,26,27,28 and capacitive29,30,31 sensors. Notably, Kim et al.32 developed a McKibben-type artificial muscle with a printed conductive composite sensor that was directly formed on the surface of the internal balloon. This provides a new approach for the fabrication of artificial muscles with integrated sensing and actuation. Moreover, in 2020, Legrand et al.33 reported a sensing approach that integrates a pair of conductive wires into the braided sheath of a McKibben artificial muscle to form a capacitive element. However, significant sensing hysteresis (16%) occurs due to the sliding or twisting of the embedded wires along the braided sheath.

Recently, some researchers have explored the inductive deformation sensing method for pneumatic actuators. Felt et al. proposed a “smart braid” for contraction sensing of the McKibben artificial muscle and achieved excellent closed loop feedback control.34,35,36,37 Some researchers have also investigated bellow-type artificial muscles based on the mutual inductance and have achieved good results.38,39 Nevertheless, there is still a gap in the development of artificial muscles with reliable and accurate proprioception capabilities that are adequate for practical applications in “real-world” scenarios.

In this work, inspired by the sensing mechanism of biological muscle spindles40 (Figure 1A) and extending our previous researches,41,42,43 we propose a perceptive pneumatic artificial muscle (PPAM), which consists of a cylindrical silicone tube reinforced by a double-helix conductive fiber (Figure 1B). As the inextensible double-helix conductive fiber restricts its radial expansion, the PPAM elongates when positive pressure is applied to its inner chamber. Furthermore, the double-helix conductive fiber forms a helically shaped inductive coil similar to a mechanical spring, and the equivalent circuit model indicates that the inductance of the coil changes with the length of the PPAM. By measuring the inductance between the two terminals of the coil winding, the length of the PPAM can be monitored accurately in real time. A PPAM bundle-based soft robot can achieve multimodal locomotion, including crawling, turning, lateral movement, and rolling. Owing to their perception and locomotion capabilities, human-computer interactions, environmental perceptions, and autonomous movement adjustment are achieved.

Materials and methods

Fabrication of the PPAMs

As shown in Figure S6, first, two parts of the outer molds were three-dimensional (3D) printed using photopolymer resin (FLGPWH04, Formlabs) via a stereolithography 3D printer (Formlabs Form2). A glass rod was used as an inner support to form the air chamber. The outer molds were assembled and fixed by screw bolts and mounted on a pedestal together with the glass rod. Then, parts A and B of Ecoflex-0050 (Smooth-On, PA) were mixed (1:1 by weight), degassed, poured into the mold slowly, and cured at room temperature. After demolding, a conductive fiber (with a multichannel electrical wire with a Teflon sheath, FF46-2-19/0.04 mm, OD of 0.3 mm) was wound on the outer surface of the silicone tube by carefully following one helical groove (clockwise) from one end to the other and then wound back to the starting point by following the other helical groove (counterclockwise). Finally, a thin layer of uncured silicone was evenly brushed on the surface to fix the conductive fibers, and the airway end was bonded and sealed with a silicone adhesive. Characterization of the PPAMs

In the elongation calibration and cyclic-driven elongation experiments, the PPAM was fixed on a base that was 3D printed via polylactic acid (PLA, RAISE3D, China) via a fused deposition modeling 3D printer (RAISE3D Pro2). A proportional control valve (ITV0010-3BL, SMC, Japan) was used to drive the PPAM to extend at different lengths, and the corresponding inductance data were measured via a circuit board that had an inductance measurement chip LDC1614. The corresponding length data were measured via a laser sensor (HG-C1200-P, Panasonic, Japan). The real-time pressure was monitored with a commercial pressure sensor (ASDXRRX030PGAA5, Honeywell) with an ADC board (NI USB 6218, TX). In the output force characteristic calibration experiment, a commercial load cell (LSB200, Futek) was installed on the base to collect the output force of the PPAM. A LabView-based program was developed for air pressure data and inductance data acquisition, for reading force values from a commercial load cell, and for controlling the proportional control valve for pressure regulation.

Control of the soft locomotion robot

A three-way pneumatic control platform is composed of six solenoid valves (VQ110U-5M-M5, SMC). Every two solenoid valves form a gas pressure loading and releasing control path for one chamber. The real-time control of the solenoid valves was controlled by a driver module with an ADC board (NI USB 6501, TX). A LabView-based program was developed for multichannel gas pressure control.

Results

Working principles and characteristics of the PPAM

As shown in Figure 2A, the PPAM consists of a cylindrical silicone tube and a seamlessly integrated double-helix coil. On the one hand, the double-helix coil restricts the radial expansion of the PPAM, causing it to elongate axially. On the other hand, the double-helix coil senses the deformation of the PPAM in real time. As detailed in Note S1, torsion introduced by the two helical fibers cancel each other, leading to pure elongation (axial extension). As shown in Figure 2B, both finite element analysis (FEA) and images of the actuated PPAM confirm that there is no axial expansion or twisting when the PPAM is pressurized. Design optimization of the PPAM was conducted via finite element simulation and is presented in Note S2. Moreover, an analytical model (detailed in Note S3) was developed to investigate the relationship between applied pressure and muscle elongation by considering both the hyperelastic material properties of silicone rubber and the geometry of the PPAM. As plotted in Figure 2C, the theoretical model gives the same results as the FEA.

To characterize the actuation performance of the PPAM, an experimental setup was built (Figure S7). Figure 2D shows the elongation-pressure curves of the PPAM during the pressurization and depressurization phases under different external loads of 0, 100, 200, and 300 g, respectively. Similar to other pneumatic actuators, the air pressure sensing exhibits a considerable hysteresis of approximately 8% during the extension-contraction cycle. Moreover, external loading pulls the muscle and stretches it longer, and the higher the external loading weight, the longer the muscle extension (1.65 mm/100 g). Therefore, it is challenging to use air pressure to estimate muscle length, as it is considerably affected by the extension-contraction state. The external loading experiments were also performed to evaluate the output force of the PPAM. The results (Figure 2E) indicate that the blocking force increases linearly with the driving pressure, reaching 3.9 N at 51.5 kPa.

As illustrated in Figure 3A, the double-helix coil is composed of two single-helix coils connected in series. One helix coil is clockwise, and the other coil is counterclockwise, ensuring the same current flow direction and positive mutual inductance. The theoretical calculation of the PPAM inductance-to-elongation curve via Equations 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 provides a similar curve as the experimental results (Figures 3B; Note S4). Notably, the experimental results show a smaller length sensing sensitivity than the theoretical curve, which can be attributed to two main reasons: (1) the coil length change in the experiment is smaller than the PPAM’s elongation and (2) there might be some radical expansion of the helical coil when the PPAM is pressurized (the helical coil’s inductance increases with its diameter). Figure 3C shows the experimental results of the inductance-length curves of the PPAM during the pressurization and decompression phases under different external loads of 0, 100, 200, and 300 g, indicating a completely overlapping response with negligible hysteresis of 1.05%. Curve fitting indicates that the inductance-to-elongation relationship matches well with an exponential function (y = 3.856 × e−0.0173x − 3.852), with a length-sensing sensitivity of 0.0667 μH/mm at 0 mm elongation and 0.0281 μH/mm at 50 mm elongation.

To validate the PPAM’s self-length sensing in real time, the PPAM was driven by different air pressures to extend and contract three times at different strokes of 0.8, 3.5, 6.5, and 11 mm. The results (Figure 3D) indicate that inductive sensing can be used to monitor real-time length changes accurately and repeatedly. As shown in Figure 3E, the PPAM was driving by a small pressure to achieve an incremental length change with a step of 0.2 mm. This indicates that the PPAM has the ability to achieve small and precise movements. Figure 3F shows that the PPAM can detect a length change as small as 10 μm, and a small length change can be recognized by a commercial laser sensor (the setup is shown in Figure S8). The experimental results (Figure S9) indicate that the inductance measurement has an extremely low noise of 0.2 nH (peak-to-peak value, using an inductance measurement chip LDC1614 with a sampling rate of 100 Hz), leading to a theoretical length-sensing resolution down to 2.9 μm (at 0 mm elongation state). Moreover, the PPAM was actuated by a high-speed solenoid valve (VQ110U-5M-M5, SMC) to evaluate its response time. The results indicate that the extension and contraction time of the PPAM are 40 and 50 ms, respectively (Figure 3G). Thus, the PPAM can reach a maximum operating frequency of 11 Hz, which is comparable with that of biological muscles.45

Figure 3H shows the inductance variation of the PPAM during 20,000 cycles of extension and contraction between 0 and 14 mm. Notably, there is a small reduction in the inductance signals (means longer PPAM) during the first 50 cycles, and then the inductance response remains stable for the following cycles during the 8-h test. This phenomenon is most likely caused by the viscoelastic relaxation of the silicone material, which leads to a transient change in the mechanical response under repeated actuation. The insets (zoomed-in plots) show the inductance variation of 10 extension-contraction cycles at the beginning and end of the test, indicating no visible degradation or changes in the sensing and actuation characteristics. Further experiments demonstrate that the PPAM can recognize not only active deformation but also passive deformation and is insensitive to temperature variations (Note S5).

In summary, these results reveal that the inductive length sensing of PPAM is highly repeatable, extremely stable, and mechanically durable and is adequate for practical applications with a long operating life. As summarized in Table 1 and compared in Figure 3I, the PPAM has high resolution, low hysteresis, and high durability, substantially surpassing the performance of other sensorized pneumatic artificial muscles.

PPAM bundle-based perceptive robots

As shown in Figure 4A, three PPAMs can be combined and bonded together to form the main body of a soft robot. A miniature circuit board (based on an integrated inductance to digital converter chip LDC1614) was attached to its end for the inductance measurement of the three PPAMs. In this circuit module, each coil was excited in a sequence for inductance measurement; therefore, no crosstalk was introduced by mutual inductance or other interferences between the helical coils. By actuating one or multiple PPAMs in a sequence, the triple-PPAM bundle can be driven to bend in any direction at a specific angle (Figure 4A). As illustrated in Figure 4B, when a PPAM was pressed by an external stimulus, its shape changes, resulting in an inductance change in the helical coil as well. Different degrees of loading (gentle touch or hard press) and loading positions on the soft robot, labeled stimuli 1 to 7 (Figure 4B), lead to different combinations of radial and overall deformation of the PPAMs, resulting in different responses in the three-channel inductance signals. Figure 4C shows the inductance changes of the locomotion robot under these seven different stimuli, with varying patterns of inductance changes among different stimuli.

As detailed in Figures 4D and S13, a machine learning-based data-processing and -recognition framework was used to recognize different stimuli in real time from the inductance signals. To enhance the model’s ability to handle real data, the collected signals were preprocessed. All signals were adjusted to a fixed length via zero padding or truncation and then smoothed via a 15-point moving average filter. The signals were subsequently normalized via the min-max method and offset so that their mean was approximately zero. Feature extraction was then performed on each channel by identifying the maximum and minimum values and recording their temporal order, forming a 6D feature vector. The feature vectors were combined with the corresponding labels to construct the input matrix for model training. The data matrix to be analyzed is divided into a training set, verification set, and test set at a ratio of 4:3:3. The verification set is used to evaluate the performance of the algorithm during the training process. The test set is used to verify the performance of the algorithm on unknown data. Finally, the support vector machine (SVM)46,47 illustrated in Note S6 is used to train on the data and predict unknown stimulus signals. Here, over 470 datasets for each of the 7 different stimuli (over 3,300 datasets in total) were collected. Figure S14 shows a t-SNE-based graph with two dominant nonlinear embedding components as coordinate axes, displaying the distribution of data features of seven different stimuli in low-dimensional space with obvious cluster structures, reflecting good category separability. As shown in Figure 4E, machine learning-based classification prediction achieved an average accuracy of 99.28% on the unknown stimuli for the test set. All seven classes achieved excellent performance, with precision, recall, and F1 scores all exceeding 0.9 (Figure 4F), indicating high reliability and robustness of external stimuli recognition. Furthermore, we adopted a 5-fold cross-validation protocol to further validate the robustness of the model, yielding an average accuracy of 98.49% with a standard deviation of 0.6149%, thereby demonstrating stable performance across folds and reducing the risk of overfitting (Figure S15B). Notably, the data were independently partitioned in each fold. In practical applications, we employed SVM regularization with a box constraint value of 0.1 to further reduce the potential risk of overfitting. Notably, the model’s strong discriminative performance relies on the high resolution, stability, repeatability, and reliability of the inductive deformation-sensing data via the embedded double-helix coil.

Real-time action and interactive stimulus recognition based on this machine learning method can be seen in Video S1. As shown in Figure 4G, five types of tactile stimuli were sequentially applied on the locomotion robot in the experiment, and the results demonstrated that all the stimuli could be recognized correctly. In future work, we plan to further increase the size and diversity of the dataset and explore the perception and interaction with the environment during locomotion.

Multimodal locomotion with self-sensing capabilities

The inchworm in nature has a well-developed muscular system that controls the movement of its body segments through muscles to complete complex body movements. As a trade-off between locomotion modalities and control complexity, the bundle of three PPAMs shown in Figure 4A was used as the multimodal locomotion robot (detailed in Note S7). To demonstrate this ability and investigate the relationship between actuation strategies and locomotion parameters, a multichannel pneumatic control system was developed to regulate the pressures of three PPAMs (Figure S16) as needed. As shown in Figure 5A, when the top PPAM (CH 1) is pressurized, it can achieve an inchworm-like crawling movement, with a maximum velocity of 5 mm/s at the 60° bending angle on the surface. The photograph shows the result of a single crawling motion. When the air pressure of the top PPAM is 68 kPa, its inductance change is 0.8 μH. It should be noted that when one of the PPAMs is actively actuated, the other two undergo passive deformation. The inductance signals accurately reflect these deformations. As shown in Figure 5B, when one of the PPAMs at the bottom (CH 2 or CH 3) is pressurized, it can achieve left/right turning. The crawling speed and turning speed are positively correlated with the bending angle of the locomotion robot, while the turning radius remains nearly unchanged (Figures S20B–S20D).

Illustration, driving pressure, images, and inductance vibration of the self-sensing multimodal locomotion robot undergoing different motion modes

(A) Crawling motion, (B) turning motion, (C) lateral movement, and (D) rolling motion. (E) Photographs of the robot moving laterally on glass plate, wet towel, water at an approximate depth of 5 mm, flat bridge, soil, grass, and rolling on a pebble surface and sand.

Moreover, when the top PPAM (CH 1) and one of the bottom PPAMs (CH 2 or CH 3) are pressurized in a sequence, the robot can achieve lateral movement to the left/right side (Figures 5C and S18). As shown in Figure 5C, first, the bottom PPAM (CH3) is pressurized, both ends of the locomotion robot are lifted to a certain height in that direction. Subsequently, the bottom PPAM (CH3) is depressurized while the top PPAM (CH 1) is pressurized simultaneously, causing the locomotion robot to move laterally in that direction. The lateral speed is positively correlated with the bending angle and gradually saturates as the bending angle increases (Figure S20E).

As illustrated in Figures 5D and S19, when the top PPAM (CH1) and one of the bottom PPAMs (CH2 or CH3) are pressurized and held with a certain phase difference, the robot can roll rapidly on most surfaces. First, the bottom PPAM (CH3) is pressurized, both ends of the locomotion robot are lifted to a certain height. Subsequently, the top PPAM (CH 1) is pressurized, generating a thrust against the ground that causes the body of the locomotion robot to rotate to a certain extent. Then, the bottom PPAM (CH3) and the top PPAM (CH 1) are depressurized, resulting in the rolling of the locomotion robot (Figure 5D). Obviously, based on similar control logic, a locomotion robot can achieve rapid continuous rolling to the left or right. The recorded action of the multimodal motion of the locomotion robot can be seen in Video S2.

Adaptive movement in complex environments

Most crawling organisms in nature have weak vision and do not rely primarily on visual perception; rather, they rely mainly on their body’s mechanoreceptors to perceive physical contact and terrain undulations. Similarly, the locomotion robot has stable and reliable active and passive perception capabilities. Like crawling organisms in nature, it can obtain environmental information and make active adjustments. As shown in Figures 6A, S33, and S34, in a well-lit or dark environment, the locomotion robot moves in a field with a deep pit in the center. At the beginning, the locomotion robot crawls forward normally, and the inductance change is normal. However, when the front parts of the two legs (CH2 and CH3) of the locomotion robot enter a deep pit, the inductance change is abnormal, and it can recognize that there is a deep pit ahead. At this point, the locomotion robot stops crawling and moves in a left lateral movement, with exploratory left lateral movement occurring twice, similar to the exploratory behavior of crawling organisms in nature. Then, the locomotion robot exploratorily detects (exploratory crawling once) whether there is still a deep pit ahead, and the inductance change is still abnormal. This behavior can be repeated multiple times (to avoid excessive repetition, it is shown only once here). Afterward, the locomotion robot moved left laterally multiple times (Figure S34) and made exploratory crawling movements again. At this time, the inductance change was the same as that of crawling on the ground, indicating that the locomotion robot had already moved out of the deep pit range and that the ground was ahead. Afterward, the locomotion robot can smoothly crawl forward, and the change in inductance also shows that the locomotion robot has been moving on the ground. A record of the locomotion robot recognizing and bypassing deep pits through body perception in a dark environment can be seen in Video S4.

Moreover, crawling organisms in nature can adjust their body height through perception and traverse tunnels to reach the other side. As shown in Figures 6B and S35, the locomotion robot crawled forward with a large stride. When it reaches the tunnel entrance, due to the high height of the body bulge, it is resisted, and the inductance is abnormal and accurately reflects that the body is resisted. The locomotion robot can then adjust its body height and move in the tunnel. Similar to crawling creatures, when crawling forward in a tunnel, a locomotion robot can explore and change its body height to perceive whether it is still in the tunnel. When the change in inductance is the same as that of crawling outside the tunnel, this indicates that the locomotion robot has passed through the tunnel. Next, the locomotion robot continues to crawl forward with a large stride. The record of the locomotion robot traversing through tunnels to reach the other side can be seen in Video S5.

In addition, the locomotion robot can recognize its own posture and make active adjustments. As shown in Figure 6C, when the locomotion robot is rolled counterclockwise by an external force, it changes from the original PPAM 2 and PPAM 3 contacting the ground to PPAM 1 and PPAM 3 contacting the ground. The inset (zoomed-in plots) shows that the inductance of PPAM 2 decreases the most, whereas the inductances of PPAM 1 and PPAM 3 remain almost unchanged, thus being able to recognize the current locomotion robot posture. Discussion

In this paper, we propose a PPAM that uses a seamlessly integrated double-helix coil to sense its length by measuring the inductance at the two terminals of the fiber coil. Moreover, the double-helix fiber reinforcement ensures axial elongation of the PPAM when it is pressurized. Like our human muscles, the PPAM can perceive its own length in real time (a response time of 50 ms for both actuation and length sensing), with a high sensing resolution of 10 μm. Rigorous experiments reveal that both the sensing and actuation of PPAM are robust and mechanically durable for practical applications. As analyzed in other inductive sensing works,48,49 PPAM is insensitive to environmental variations such as temperature changes, dust, and water spray. It can achieve the claimed performance in most application scenarios. Notably, nearby ferromagnetic or (metallic) conductive objects can alter the alternative current magnetic field of the inductance measurement, resulting in errors. In practical applications, objects made of ferromagnetic or conductive metal materials should be placed at distances greater than the dimension of the helical coil to avoid interference, or a compensation method should be introduced to correct the results.

A bundle of three PPAMs was bonded together to form an omnidirectional bending robot, referred to as the locomotion robot. The helical coil can detect any deformation caused by either active driving or external loading. Once the robot is pressed, deformation of the three PPAMs occurs, leading to changes in inductance from the three helical coils. The locomotion robot constructed from PPAMs is inherently modular and can be easily expanded or scaled up or down to some extent. With the aid of machine learning algorithms, the mobile robot is capable of accurately recognizing external stimuli. By tuning the driving pressure on one or multiple PPAMs in a sequence, the locomotion robot is capable of crawling, turning, lateral movement, and rolling, showing adaptive mobility in real-world scenarios (on glass, sand, grass, soil, ground, and water). Based on comprehensive perceptual inputs, the locomotion robot can navigate around deep pits in darkness and modulate its body movement amplitude to pass through a tunnel with a low roof. Moreover, the robot can perceive its own posture in real time and autonomously adjust itself when a side flip occurs during locomotion.

Although reliable perception and multimodal locomotion are achieved, the current robot is tethered. It requires tubes connected to the air source (compressed air tank). Also, the response time and pressure control of the current electromagnetic valves are not as stable and precise as the sensing feedback can offer. Piezoelectric proportional valves will be explored to achieve fast and precise closed loop control at a much lower power consumption. In the near future, we aim to develop a fully integrated intelligent locomotion robot using the PPAM framework. By scaling up the robot to two to five times of its current dimension, the control board, battery, and compressed air tank (air pump, or controlled chemical reaction module) can be integrated into the robot’s body, making it untethered. We also explore comprehensive perception algorithms and control strategies to develop a PPAM-driven, untethered soft robot that can autonomously navigate itself in complex environments, interact with and respond to its surroundings, and perform meaningful tasks.
