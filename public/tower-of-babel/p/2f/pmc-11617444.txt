Clinical and Radiographic Evaluation of Zinc Oxide-ozonated Oil, Zinc Oxide-eugenol, Antioxidant Mix, and Probiotic Mix as Obturating Materials in Primary Teeth: An In Vivo Study
Authors: Prachi Arora, Shivani Mathur, Pulkit Arora
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe best space maintainer in both primary and mixed dentition is the primary tooth itself. This is not only because of the clinical crown but also due to the presence of the roots and periodontium that guide the eruption of the permanent tooth. Although over the years many materials have been tried for obturation of pulpectomized primary teeth, no material has been found to fulfill all requirements. The aim of the present study was to evaluate the clinical and radiographic efficiency of zinc oxide-eugenol (ZOE), zinc oxide-ozonated oil, antioxidant mix, and probiotic mix in root canal treatment of primary molars.Materials and methodsOne hundred twenty primary molars of children aged 4–9 years presenting to the department were randomly assigned to one of four groups: obturated with ZOE, zinc oxide-ozonated oil, probiotic mix, or antioxidant mix. All the pulpectomies were carried out by a single operator. Follow-up was done at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months, and treatment success or failure was determined by the evaluation of clinical and radiographic findings.ResultsThere was a statistically significant difference with respect to postoperative pain and swelling among the four groups at the end of the 12-month follow-up period (p < 0.05).ConclusionThe study concludes that among the four different obturating materials, zinc oxide-ozonated oil has shown the most promising results in primary endodontically treated teeth, whereas antioxidant mix was comparable to ZOE with fewer disadvantages and side effects.How to cite this articleArora P, Mathur S, Arora P. Introduction

The primary tooth itself is the best space maintainer in both primary and mixed dentition. This is due to the clinical crown as well as the periodontium and roots, which direct the eruption of the succedaneous tooth. Primary teeth aid in the masticatory process and encourage the development of the maxilla. Therefore, for proper primary dentition development, it is crucial to maintain the integrity of the dentition until the teeth exfoliate themselves.1

Arch space is lost as a result of early extraction of primary teeth with pulpal involvement.

The pulp tissue is rich in cellular and neurological components and has a complicated blood flow.2 Thus, endodontic therapy is considered the best choice for keeping primary teeth in place. In pediatric dentistry, the term “pulpectomy” refers to the process of removing caries and necrotic or inflammatory material from the pulp chamber and root canals, then obturating the canals with resorbable root canal filling material.3

Therefore, the best material for filling root canals in primary teeth should be antibacterial, resorbable concurrently with the tooth root, innocuous to periapical tissues, and capable of forming permanent tooth buds. It should also be radiopaque, quickly fill the canals, adhere to the walls, not shrink, easily resorb if passed beyond the apex, be simple to remove if needed, and not discolor the tooth.4 Additionally, it should not solidify into a hard mass that might impede the eruption of a succedaneous tooth.

These days, calcium hydroxide, pastes based on iodoform, and zinc oxide-eugenol (ZOE) are often utilized materials for basic root canal fillings.

One of the most popular and well-established root canal filling materials for deciduous teeth is ZOE cement.1

Zinc oxide-eugenol for obturation has been shown by several authors to have moderate-to-high success rates in maintaining primary teeth that are chronically infected. Despite its numerous disadvantages, ZOE has also been employed in conjunction with several fixative chemicals, such as formaldehyde, formocresol, paraformaldehyde, and cresol, all of which have inherent cytotoxicity.5

Despite being the gold standard for root canal fillings in deciduous teeth, ZOE has some drawbacks, including slow resorption, irritation to periapical tissues, necrosis of cementum and bone, and changes in the path of the succedaneous tooth when it extrudes past the tooth's apex due to the formation of a hard mass.6

When it comes to the resorption rate, ZOE frequently exhibits a slower pace than the resorption of the primary teeth roots. Nevertheless, it remains one of the most commonly used preparations for primary tooth pulpectomies.4

Ozonated oil has been investigated recently and appears to have potential for usage as an obturating substance for primary teeth. Studies combining ozone with ZOE revealed that the aqueous version of the gas has strong antibacterial properties and virtually minimal in vitro toxicity to oral cells, all without causing drug resistance.7 Due to its intriguing biological properties, including its antibacterial action, debriding impact, ability to stimulate angiogenesis, and strong oxidizing power, ozone has been viewed as promising (Guinesi et al. 2011).5 Due to its excellent antibacterial activity and lack of drug resistance, it is currently being promoted as a potential substitute antiseptic agent in dentistry. It works by releasing oxygen or ozone, which is a perfect delivery system. Ozone's function is caused by breaking the double bonds that lipid molecules’ carbon atoms have when it reacts with oils. This results in oxygenated substances such as polyperoxides and hydroperoxides.

In the quest to search for a material that is biocompatible and enhances the healing process, researchers have recently explored antioxidant systems, which can be enzyme- or nonenzyme-based, functioning cooperatively to shield cells or organs from free radical damage. Removing these free radicals speeds up the healing process and repairs any residual free radical damage. Protease inhibitors are shielded from oxidative damage by antioxidants, which also counteract excess proteases and reactive oxygen species (ROS) that are frequently created by neutrophil buildup in the injured area.8 Excess ROS can destroy fibroblasts and other cells, reducing the suppleness of the tissues. Antioxidants may lessen the likelihood that these negative effects will materialize.

Probiotics have a long history of usage in dentistry. While the halitosis and caries prevention probiotic method has not been well researched for use in endodontic therapy, they have been successfully investigated to help treat periodontal disorders and oral health issues. Probiotics work through a variety of mechanisms, including immune modulation, downregulation of inflammatory responses, synthesis of antimicrobial compounds like organic acids, peroxides, and bacteriocins, production of mucin, inhibition of epithelial invasion by pathogens, stimulation of immunoglobulin A, and competition with other flora, including possible pathogens.9

In the oral cavity, probiotics tend to create a biofilm that protects oral tissues from bacterial pathogens by filling a space that could serve as a future niche for pathogens. Probiotics have already been used in dentistry to treat periodontal disorders, oral candidiasis, and halitosis that are linked to the gut and mouth. They have also been used to prevent dental caries. The majority of species discovered in periodontal pockets are also present in infected root canals. Thus, using probiotics in root canals may prove beneficial in endodontics.4

In order to assess the clinical and radiographic effectiveness of ZOE, zinc oxide-ozonated oil, antioxidant mix, and probiotic mix in the root canal treatment of primary molars, the current study was conducted.

Materials and Methods

With prior consent from the Institutional Ethics Committee, the current study was carried out in the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry, ITS-CDSR, Muradnagar, Ghaziabad, India. There were 120 primary molars in all, from healthy 4- to 9-year-olds. Prior to the procedure starting, each parent or guardian signed an informed consent form. A history of spontaneous pain, tenderness upon percussion, severely decayed primary molars, the lack of a fistula, radiolucency in the furcation or periapical areas, and uncontrollably bleeding within 5 minutes of pulp extraction were among the signs and symptoms of the teeth that were included in the study. Excluded from the study were any teeth that could not be restored, had a fistula, showed excessive movement, gingival swelling, insufficient bone support, or a perforated pulpal floor.

Single-sitting (SS) pulpectomy was the main technique used in the root canal process and was performed by the same operator in every instance. A rubber dam was installed during the root canal procedure after local anesthetic was administered. The coronal and radicular pulp were removed following the opening of the access. Using a pullback action, the canals were prepared with H-files (size 30–35) after the working length was determined.

The following materials were used to obturate the canals:

Group A: Zinc oxide powder with eugenol—Using a cement spatula, zinc oxide powder (Prevest DenPro) and eugenol were combined in a 4:1 ratio on a glass slab.

Group A: Zinc oxide powder with eugenol—Using a cement spatula, zinc oxide powder (Prevest DenPro) and eugenol were combined in a 4:1 ratio on a glass slab.

Group B: Zinc oxide ozonated oil—Using motor-driven lentulospirals, the root canals were filled with newly combined zinc oxide powder and ozonated castor oil (0.007 cc Ozonil).8

Group B: Zinc oxide ozonated oil—Using motor-driven lentulospirals, the root canals were filled with newly combined zinc oxide powder and ozonated castor oil (0.007 cc Ozonil).8

Group C: Antioxidant mix—Oxyfruit 40, an antioxidant mix that is sold commercially, is a type of capsule. It is found inside the capsule as a paste.

Group C: Antioxidant mix—Oxyfruit 40, an antioxidant mix that is sold commercially, is a type of capsule. It is found inside the capsule as a paste.

Group D: Probiotic mix—To create a paste, the probiotic mix was combined with saline.

Group D: Probiotic mix—To create a paste, the probiotic mix was combined with saline.

After that, lentulospirals powered by air were used to slowly transport each of the abovementioned pastes into the tubes. Following this, quickly setting ZOE was poured into the pulp chambers. Stainless steel crowns were used for the final restoration on each tooth. The assessment of clinical and radiographic results was used to determine whether treatment was successful or unsuccessful at intervals of 3, 6, 9, and 12 months.

Radiographic criteria for treatment success included the absence of periapical or interradicular pathology, absence of internal or pathological external root resorption, reduction or no change in the size of interradicular radiolucencies, presence of evidence of bone regeneration, and radiographic continuity of lamina dura. The cases treated were deemed successful when there was no pain and swelling, no tenderness on percussion, absence of sinus or fistula, and mobility, and presence of healthy soft tissue (defined as the absence of swelling, redness, or sinus tract). The Chi-squared test was used for statistical analysis after the clinical and radiological failures were noted. The total outcome of the treatment was recorded for those teeth exhibiting both clinical and radiographic success.

Results

The sample was divided into three age groups: 4–5, 6–7, and 8–9 years. Of the 120 samples, the 4–5 years age group constituted a total of 36 samples, with 11 (males: 10, females: 1) in the ZOE group, 9 (males: 5, females: 4) in the zinc oxide-ozonated oil group, 6 (males: 5, females: 1) in the probiotic mix group, and 10 (males: 5, females: 5) in the antioxidant mix group (Table 1). The 6–7 years age-group comprised a total of 50 samples, with 11 (males: 6, females: 5) in the ZOE group, 14 (males: 10, females: 4) in the zinc oxide-ozonated oil group, 14 (males: 11, females: 3) in the probiotic mix group, and 11 (males: 6, females: 5) in the antioxidant mix group. The 8–9 years age-group consisted of 34 samples, with 8 (males: 6, females: 2) in the ZOE group, 7 (males: 5, females: 2) in the zinc oxide-ozonated oil group, 10 (males: 3, females: 7) in the probiotic mix group, and 9 (males: 5, females: 4) in the antioxidant mix group. There was no statistically significant difference in age and gender distribution between the four groups.

Discussion

While correct biomechanical preparation determines the effectiveness of pulpectomy in a deciduous tooth, the resorbable nature and antibacterial qualities of the filling material dictate the success of root canal therapy in permanent teeth.10

In the past, a number of studies have been conducted to find an efficient filling material whose rate of resorption corresponds with the physiological root resorption of deciduous teeth, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of root canal therapy.10,11

Zinc oxide-eugenol is a commonly utilized obturation material in primary teeth. Despite its high success rate, it does not fulfill all the requirements of an ideal root canal filling substance.12 Specifically, eugenol has been shown to be both neurotoxic and cytotoxic.4,13

Thus, the goal of the study was to assess and compare the effectiveness of four distinct root canal obturating materials in terms of both clinical and radiographic outcomes: zinc oxide powder with eugenol, zinc oxide-ozonated oil, antioxidant mix, and probiotic mix with saline.

The natural eruption of the succedaneous tooth is allowed by the resorption of the root canal filler material, which happens at the same time as the resorption of the deciduous tooth root during exfoliation.1

Furthermore, the substance should be resorbable and nontoxic to the permanent tooth germ and periapical tissues if it is expressed beyond the apex. Extruded ZOE resisted resorption, according to the authors, and took months or even years to resorb.6

Researchers looking for more material were drawn to ozone because of its strong antibacterial activity and biocompatibility with oral cells. Ozonated water, ozonated olive oil, and oxygen/ozone gas are the three main ways that ozone is applied to oral tissue. Olive oil and ozonated water both have the ability to store and release ozone, making them excellent delivery systems.14 To treat dental disease, these application methods can be employed separately or in combination. Ozone reacts with oils by rupturing the double bonds that hold carbon atoms in lipid molecules, resulting in oxygenated substances such as polyperoxides and hydroperoxides, which is what gives them their effect.

Probiotics are acknowledged by the World Health Organization (WHO) as the most significant immune defense mechanism to have in place if bacterial resistance renders the present antibiotics ineffective.

These probiotics work by producing compounds that hinder growth and change the pH of the surrounding environment. They also compete for nutrition, create physical barriers, and boost the immune system. Probiotics have been investigated as a potential treatment for halitosis, periodontal disease, and dental caries prevention. Probiotic therapy, however, has not yet undergone a thorough evaluation for application in endodontic therapy.10

As one tooth was lost before the study's 6-month follow-up, 30 and 29 teeth from the zinc oxide-ozonated oil group (group B) were available for follow-up at 3 and 6 months, respectively. Additionally, 28 and 27 teeth were checked after 9 and 12 months, respectively, because one tooth had to be pulled after 9 months due to clinical failure and another tooth had to be lost before the 12-month checkup. This may be explained by the zinc oxide-ozonated oil mixture's effectiveness.

Due to the loss of two teeth at the 3-month mark and one tooth at the 6-month mark, the probiotic mix group (group C) had 28 and 27 teeth available for follow-up at 3 and 6 months, respectively. Moreover, one tooth was pulled at 9 months and 12 months, leaving 26 and 25 teeth available for follow-up at those times.15

In the antioxidant mix (group D), 27 and 26 teeth were examined at 3 and 6 months because one tooth had been lost to follow-up at that time, and two teeth had been extracted within that time, and one tooth had been extracted within 6 months due to clinical failure. At 9 and 12 months, 25 and 24 teeth were available because one tooth had been lost to follow-up at each time.

Out of the 25 teeth in ZOE (group A) that had discomfort prior to surgery, two teeth reported having pain within 3 months, and one tooth each within 6, 9, and 12 months. ZOE has been proven to be irritating to the periapical tissues and to create a foreign body reaction when it is extruded from the apex, which explains why discomfort was present during the follow-up visit in the group that was given this medication.4,16

This is in contrast to a study by Mani et al.17 that found that, with the exception of one tooth, the ZOE group experienced less discomfort at the 6-month follow-up.

One tooth in group B (zinc oxide-ozonated oil) experienced pain within 3, 6, and 9 months, but none within 12 months. This may be explained by ozone peroxides’ potent antibacterial activity and superior healing capabilities.18

Three teeth in the probiotic mix (group C) reported pain during the 3-month follow-up period, two teeth during the 6-month period, one tooth within the 9-month period, and none during the 12-month period. The findings make sense because Lactobacillus rhamnosus may have an anti-inflammatory impact by inhibiting the growth of Escherichia coli.

Within a follow-up of 3 months, two teeth in the antioxidant mix group (group D) experienced pain, and one tooth each within 6, 9, and 12 months. This is explained by the fact that antioxidants prevent excess proteases and free radicals, which are frequently produced by neutrophil buildup, and shield protease inhibitors from oxidative damage, which lowers adverse events and postoperative discomfort.19

One tooth in each of the ZOE groups (group A) experienced postoperative swelling within 3, 6, and 12 months, respectively. This could be because ZOE lacks antibacterial qualities, which could worsen any remaining root canal infection rather than aid in the repair of damaged tissue. However, research conducted by Mortazavi et al. at different follow-up visits revealed improvement in intraoral edema in the ZOE group after therapy, which is in contradiction to our work.

None of the teeth in the zinc oxide-ozonated oil group reported having intraoral edema. It might have to do with the oil's ability to function for longer periods due to its prolonged contact with the surface.5

Three teeth within 3 months, one tooth within 6 months, and none within 9 or 12 months were found to have intraoral edema following surgery when using a probiotic mix. Probiotics may not be as effective against bacteria as they claim since they replace microorganisms rather than getting rid of them.

Within the antioxidant group, intraoral edema was detected in two teeth within 3 months, three teeth within 6 months, and none within 9 or 12 months. Antioxidants and vitamins A, B, C, and Zn have been employed because they are thought to be crucial in the proliferative, inflammatory, and wound healing phases, as well as in wound remodeling and intraoral edema reduction.

Radiographic evaluation of the groups was performed at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months of follow-up. The probiotic mix group showed the least amount of reduction in the size of postoperative interradicular radiolucency (15%), followed by the antioxidant mix and ZOE groups, and the zinc oxide-ozonated oil group (50%) at the conclusion of the 12-month evaluation period.

The drop in radiolucency in the zinc oxide-ozonated oil group may have been caused by the material's ability to stimulate angiogenesis, which effectively repairs bone.

The removal of ROS in the antioxidant mix group is responsible for the decrease in radiolucency, which may have helped radicular pulp repair.

The outcomes in the probiotic mix group may be attributed to insufficient antibacterial efficacy and decreased bone healing, which would result in a lesser reduction in radiolucency.

Our research supports a study by Chandra et al.18 that examined the effects of zinc oxide-ozonated oil. At six and twelve months, the radiological success rates were 96.6 and 100%, respectively.

To the best of the author's knowledge, this is the first study to assess the radiographic efficacy of probiotic and antioxidant mixes as obturating materials during deciduous tooth pulpectomy. The current study's findings indicate that the clinical success rates for the probiotic and antioxidant mixes are 64 and 83.3%, respectively.

Conclusion

Since ages, ZOE has been considered the gold standard for obturation in deciduous dentition; it does not get resorbed with the physiological resorption of the root, which is indeed the prime requisite for being an ideal obturating material. However, it has several disadvantages, such as causing irritation to periapical tissues and not resorbing if extruded beyond the apex. Several alternatives to ZOE have been tried in this study to overcome its disadvantages.
