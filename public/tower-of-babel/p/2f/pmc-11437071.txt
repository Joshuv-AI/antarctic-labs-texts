Experimental study of the effect of oil polarity on smart waterflooding in carbonate reservoirs
Authors: Mohaddeseh Ahmadi Aghdam, Siavash Riahi, Omid Khani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the influence of oil polarity on interfacial tension (IFT), contact angle, oil recovery, and effluent pH in smart water and Low-salinity water injection. The results indicate that the interaction between the hydration shell of ions and the polar components (PCs) of oil is crucial. Increasing oil polarity enhances the potential for interaction with the hydration shell of ions, leading to reduced IFT, altered wettability, and improved oil recovery; which could be boosted by the contribution of a higher number of anions in the smart water bulk through the enhancement of their interaction with the PCs (especially acidic components) of oil. The study demonstrates that increasing the SO42− concentration in seawater increased oil recovery for oils with higher acid component content, as indicated by total acid number values of 0.87, 0.99, and 1.32 mgKOH/g, the tertiary oil recovery factors for these oils were 61.10%, 69.82%, and 87.09%, respectively. The effluent pH results align with the findings of contact angle and oil recovery, confirming the dominant influence of anions on oil recovery. The interaction between the PCs of oil and the hydration shell of ions is thus highlighted as a critical factor in the observed outcomes.

Introduction

A magnificent amount of the world's crude oil is stored in carbonate reservoirs1,2. However, due to some reservoir properties, a large volume of crude oil is always trapped underground3. Therefore, it is necessary to use enhanced oil recovery methods to increase the recovery from such reservoirs4–6. One of the main enhanced oil recovery methods that has recently attracted a lot of attention is smart waterflooding7. In this technique, the properties of brine are changed in a way that the maximum yield of oil is obtained by changing the ions and/or the salinity of the injected water8. The numerous advantages of this method, along with successful laboratory3, simulation9, and field8 studies have made this method attract a lot of attention and stand out compared to other methods7,10. Smart waterflooding has been able to take advantage of several well-known mechanisms that are classified into two discrete groups according to fluid–fluid and rock-fluid interactions11. Mechanisms related to fluid–fluid interactions are interfacial tension (IFT) reduction12, solution of naphthenic acids13, osmosis14, emulsification15, while the second category includes pH changes16,17, mineral rock dissolution18, fines migration19, multicomponent ionic exchange (MIE)20, electrical double-layer (EDL) expansion21,22, and the salting effect23, to greatly investigate the rock-fluid and fluid–fluid properties. Many researchers believe that wettability alteration is the most important influence of smart water in the study of rock-fluid interactions7; while some studies disprove this statement as in conditions of no wettability changes, smart waterflooding can still increase the amount of oil recovery24–27. Several studies indicate that increasing the hydrophilicity of the environment can increase the amount of oil recovery7,28,29. Nevertheless, other experiments show that hydrophobic environments can also extract crude oil through the continuity of the oil phase on the rock surface3,13,30. Also, fluid–fluid interactions are considered important by many researchers which are directly related to the capillary forces31. Some researchers believe that the highest oil recovery is achieved by decreasing the amount of IFT32–34, others consider the increasing35 trend to be effective, and some literature inferred that the IFT changes are ineffective in oil recovery36,37.

For the ionic interactions of smart water, the continuum models or experimental works are not adequate, thus a literature review of molecular dynamics simulations is useful to gain an atomistic perspective at the pore scale. To eject the leftover oils from the pores, several oil displacing agents have been studied in molecular dynamic simulations, including carbon dioxide38, polymers39, nanoparticles40, surfactants41, smart water42,43, etc. The literature review of molecular dynamics simulations in smart water reveals two mechanisms responsible for wettability alteration: EDL expansion43–53 and MIE42,54–62. EDL expansion is observed to be related to an increase43,50,53, or a decrease48,49 in brine salinity. Compared to divalent ions, monovalent ions dominate EDL expansion as salinity increases44,47,50,51,53–55. These ions can easily interact with water layers and locate near the surface to form EDL44,47,51 or form ionic aggregations in high salinity water55, whereas divalent ions cannot disrupt the hydration layers due to the larger hydration shells that keep them away from the rock44,47,51. Instead, keeping these ions away from calcite allows them to facilitate oil separation by interacting with carboxylates, reducing their attractive interaction with the surface42,49 or modifying the surface charge51. Unlike Ca2+ cations, Mg2+ ions play a crucial role in reducing the tendency of oil components and enhancing detachment time42,49,54,61,62. However, higher Mg2+ concentrations shift oils’ polar molecules to the substrate beyond the EDL, indicating a negative impact on wettability54. Also sulfate ions may change the surface’s wetness to less hydrophobic42 by dislodging carboxylates from the surface through a competitive adsorption process49. The good performance of sulfate ions is well known in cooperation with other cations such as Ca2+52 and Mg2+58. In contrast, some investigations found that raising the concentration of SO42− anions in brines enhances the adsorption of benzoic acid59 and the carboxylate component62 by ion-pairing with cations.

Besides, crude oil type has a vital role in oil recovery, IFT, and the activity of potential determining ions (PDIs) as it is confirmed that oil polarity plays a more important role in fluid–fluid interactions compared to the salinity of the injected fluid63–65. Remesal et al.9 inferred that the ions of injected water have no role in the changes of IFT and this parameter is only dependent on electrostatic forces related to crude oil; while it is inferred the presence of polar components (PCs) of crude oil including resin particles66, asphaltene67 and naphthenic acids68 can extremely disturb the phase balance between fluids and reduce the IFT67,69. Table 1 lists chosen laboratory studies that demonstrate the variety of conditions in which the efficiency of recovery is noticed by changing the oil polarity during smart waterflooding. Several methods were used for altering the polarity of reservoir oil along with other effective parameters such as injection sequences and rock type. According to the results, it can be seen that the composition of oil has a vital role in determining the amount of oil recovery, which is directly related to the total acid number (TAN) and total base number (TBN). Thus, researchers continued their studies on the role of the oil phase and its compounds on the efficiency of smart waterflooding. The majority of studies showed an integrated response of IFT reduction with increasing the oil polarity25,63,64. Divandari et al.70 showed that changes in IFT depend on the absorption of crude oil asphaltene particles at the fluid–fluid interface, which leads to a reduction or enhancement in IFT value of low saline and high saline brines, respectively. The interaction of the acidic and basic components of asphaltene particles with OH− in the anion hydration shell and H+ in the cation hydration shell of smart water, respectively, was further analyzed by Abdi et al. They showed that these interactions are mainly controlled by the hydration energy of ions in water, the number of ions, and the type of PCs of oil71. In another study, the accumulation of polar particles in the fluid–fluid interface resulted in the lowest IFT value at an optimal salinity level of 5000 ppm for NaCl, CaCl2, and KCl salts72. On the other hand, the IFT reduction and solubility of polar particles could also be dependent on the presence of divalent cations of calcium73,74 or magnesium75. Another vital mechanism affecting both fluid–fluid and rock-fluid interfaces is pH changes. This parameter can influence the fluid–fluid interface by altering the composition of injected water by entering naphthenic acids from the oil phase into the aqueous phase16,76,77. Increasing the pH value of injected water at the fluid–fluid interface can also lead to a decrease in the value of IFT16,17,66,78. High pH conditions can enhance the surface activity of asphaltene particles due to the ionization of acidic components in the asphaltenes and reduce the IFT79. Moreover, pH changes in injected water can influence the chemical reactions of PDIs (Ca2+, Mg2+, and SO42−) and crude oil components. Behrang et al. showed that monovalent cations are more effective in acidic and neutral environments compared to divalent cations. They proved that this behavior is reversed in basic environments 66. Also, the fluid–fluid interactions of basic and acidic components of crude oil with injected water show more activity in acidic and alkaline environments, respectively71,80. Further, it is claimed that the activity of some acidic groups of crude oil, such as water-soluble acids can also be intensified in neutral environments which leads to the pH reduction76,77. Additionally, the rock-fluid interface could be affected by pH changes of injected water through the mechanism of mineral rock dissolution16,76,77. Some studies show that the surface absorption of oil polar compounds decreases in alkaline environments16,81, while, other researchers consider acidic environments to be effective in reducing crude oil absorption17,82–85.

Most of the studies conducted in the field of smart waterflooding have primarily focused on altering the composition and salinity of injected water. However, the significance of crude oil polarity in smart waterflooding performance has been confirmed, which has received limited attention72,86. The polarity of crude oil plays a crucial role, as it can influence other mechanisms and its absence can lead to notable changes in fluid–fluid and rock-fluid interfaces34,72,86–88. Moreover, the diverse range of results reported in the literature review indicates a lack of clear consensus, regarding interface modifications, which may be attributed to the impact of crude oil compounds in smart waterflooding. Therefore, conducting a more comprehensive investigation on oil polarity considering the relevant factors is essential to address the observed variations in the application of smart waterflooding techniques. Thus, this study aimed to investigate the impact of oil polarity through the evaluation of various parameters including IFT, contact angle, oil recovery, and effluent pH. In this research, different samples of low-salinity water and smart water were assessed to determine the effect of PDIs. The main focus of this study is to propose a mechanism by examining the interactions between fluids and rocks, to address the inconsistencies in the observed fuzzy behavior and provide more comprehensive explanations to resolve existing uncertainties.

Materials

Salts

The primary objective of this study is to examine the impact of oil polarity and the components of low-salinity and smart water on various processes, including IFT, contact angle, and oil recovery. To achieve this, synthetic brine samples were prepared based on the ion analysis of the Persian Gulf’s seawater. Salts such as NaCl, MgCl2, CaCl2, Na2SO4, NaHCO3, and KCl (obtained from the Merck Company with a purity level exceeding 99%) were utilized in the preparation of brines, including formation water (FW), seawater (SW), low-salinity, and smart water.

Water phase

The studied injection brines are classified as low-salinity and smart water samples, by altering the salinity and ionic composition of SW, respectively. In the design of smart water samples, OLI ScaleChem software was used to respect the total dissolved solids level of the Persian Gulf’s seawater by retaining a charge balance between ions. Selecting and preparing smart brines were done based on another study in the same field98. These samples denoted as SWxCayMgzS, represent modified SWs with varying concentrations of Ca2+, Mg2+, and SO42− ions compared to regular SW. For instance, SW4S indicates four times the concentration of SO42− with constant Mg2+ and Ca2+ levels. SW0Mg0Ca indicates zero concentrations of Mg2+ and Ca2+ while keeping the SO42− concentration constant. Additionally, low-salinity brines were designed regarding the composition of SW. Oil phase

In this work, four various oil samples: one reservoir crude oil (CO) from a southern Iranian carbonated oil reservoir, two extracted during heating the CO sample (Oil5, Oil20), and Normal-Decane as a non-polar oil sample are used. All samples were initially centrifuged and filtered using a 5 mm filter paper to remove solid particles, dissolved gases, and water. The oil polarity was changed by heating CO to retain its original texture. The heating process was performed based on the procedure explained in another study25. The behavior of the oil phase is mainly controlled by the presence of polar groups of crude oil87. Thus, the TAN and TBN were measured for all oil samples to examine the degree of polarity, based on the ASTM D66499 and ASTM D2896100 methods. Experimental procedure

IFT measurement

The pendant drop method considered one of the most accurate methods for measuring IFT, was utilized to measure the IFT between different injection water and oil samples101,102. This parameter was measured following the procedure outlined in the study by Farhadi et al.74. A schematic of the IFT apparatus is illustrated in Fig. 2. To ensure data reproducibility and accuracy, the test was repeated three times. Finally, the average value was reported for all the tested samples.Fig. 2Schematic of IFT measurement apparatus based on pendant drop method.

Schematic of IFT measurement apparatus based on pendant drop method.

Contact angle measurement

Conventional contact angle measurements were performed based on the sessile drop method in surface conditions according to the test procedure proposed by Bahaloo Horeh et al.103. The test was performed on the carbonate polished flat thin sections extracted from the core plugs. A schematic of the thin section’s preparation process is illustrated in Fig. 3, and Fig. 4 shows the initial water-wet and final oil-wet thin sections for the tested oil sample.Fig. 3Preparation steps of reservoir thin sections for conducting contact angle test.Fig. 4Thin sections of carbonate rock, (a) before and (b) after immersion in the tested oil sample.

Preparation steps of reservoir thin sections for conducting contact angle test.

Thin sections of carbonate rock, (a) before and (b) after immersion in the tested oil sample.

After aging the thin sections, a drop of oil was released on the oil-wet thin section in a cubic cell containing the desired injection brine103. The apparent contact angle was then recorded in the stationary condition over time. The test duration was considered to be 48 h as some studies similar to the research field showed that the highest contact angle changes belong to the first 48 h of testing30,104. At each time step, an average contact angle between both sides of the oil droplet was reported. Coreflooding

Coreflooding systems can be used to evaluate the efficiency of smart waterflooding in the enhancement of oil recovery. A schematic of the coreflooding apparatus used in this study is illustrated in Fig. 6. To perform this test the primary characteristics of the selected clean dry core, such as porosity, and bulk volume, were measured. Then, the core was placed in the device and flooded with FW at various injection rates of 0.3, 0.6, and 0.9 cc/min to achieve 100% water saturation. In the next step, flooding with the tested oil sample was started and continued until the initial oil saturation (Soi) and connate water saturation (Scw) were achieved. Next, the saturated core was placed at 80 °C for one month to obtain the initial oil-wetting condition of carbonate surfaces. Accumulation of PCs of oil at the fluid–fluid interface leads to the partitioning interaction13. To assess the effect of oil samples and solution properties on this phenomenon, the pH of brines was measured before and after the core flood, using a benchtop pH meter.

Results and discussion

Effect of oil polarity on the IFT of low-salinity water

Based on the literature, the PCs of crude oil which are active at the surface, have a significant impact on the oil-brine interface by interacting with salts and forming ion complexes25,69,105–111. It has also been confirmed that the trend of decreasing or increasing IFT or the presence of an optimum salinity point for IFT changes depends on the crude oil components63. In other words, the PCs of the oil phase are absorbed by the fluid interface, acting as surfactants, and leading to a reduction in IFT by creating an elastic surface13,25. Table 6 shows the evaluation of IFT for FW, SW, and diluted SW samples with different oils varying in the degree of polarity. The results demonstrate that the lowest IFT for all four oil samples (n-Decane, CO, Oil5, Oil20) is observed with SW. The evaluation of IFT can be explained based on the interaction mechanism between the hydration shell of ions in brine and the PCs of the oil. Abdi et al.71 have previously explained how the acidic and basic components of oil interact with the hydration shell of ions in LSW, leading to the IFT behavior. The presence of various ions in the brine samples, as evaluated in Table 6, results in simultaneous interactions of repulsion and attraction, influencing the formation or non-formation of natural surfactants with acidic and basic oil components in all samples. This repulsion and attraction demonstrate that SW (which has the lower salinity) has a higher potential for reducing IFT compared to FW. However, the dilution of SW reveals that the decrease in salt concentration reduces the interaction potential between low-salinity and the PCs of oil, thereby increasing IFT. On the other hand, increasing oil polarity enhances the interaction between the PCs of oil and the hydration shell of ions in LSW. This increased interaction, along with the formation of more natural surfactants (Table 6), leads to a decrease in IFT as oil polarity increases. The results from Table 6 demonstrate that the optimal salinity, leading to the lowest IFT, does not necessarily follow a consistent downward or upward trend. This behavior can be explained by Abdi et al.'s study on ion hydration and their abundance in the brine solution71. As depicted in Fig. 7, an increase in salinity causes a significant decrease in the distance between water molecules surrounding dissolved salt ions in the bulk aqueous phase. Simultaneously, the number of water molecules associated with each ion also decreases considerably. Since the water molecules surrounding the ions play a crucial role in their interaction with the PCs of oil, an increase in salinity from SW to FW in Table 6 results in a reduction in the number of water molecules and the distance between them within the hydration shell. This reduction, in turn, diminishes the ions' potential for interaction with the PCs of oil. Consequently, LSW exhibits a greater ability to reduce IFT compared to FW. However, the dilution of SW, as observed in Table 6, can also lead to a disruptive behavior that reduces IFT. This behavior is attributed to the number of ions present in the brine solution. When the ions in the brine solution decrease in concentration, their interaction potential with the PCs of oil diminishes due to their decreased abundance. Therefore, according to various studies, the relationship between IFT and increasing salinity has shown a dualistic behavior in the past70,86,112–114. This behavior stems from the interplay between salinity and the number of ions in the brine solution. As depicted in Fig. 7, an increase in salinity reduces the interaction potential with the PCs of oil due to the reduced number and distance between water molecules within the hydration shell. However, a higher concentration of ions will have a greater potential to interact with the PCs of the oil due to the larger number of ions present.Fig. 7The number of molecules in the aqueous phase with measured intervals between ions of NaCl solution in proportion to its concentration 71,115.

Effect of oil polarity and PDIs concentration in smart water on IFT

The interaction between the PCs of the two phases, as indicated by studies by Jian et al.116,117, and Mikami et al.118, reveals the role of asphaltene-water molecule interaction in determining IFT behavior. The behavior of IFT in Fig. 8-a can be similar to the findings of studies by Jian et al.116,117, and Mikami et al.118, with the distinction that, contrary to those studies, the PCs are present in the aqueous phase, while the oil sample is a non-polar organic fluid with very weak polarity. However, the different smart water samples in Fig. 8-a exhibit varying salinity levels and ion compositions, indicating differences in their polarity. This difference in smart water polarity leads to varying interactions with the hydrogens of n-decane across different samples. Consequently, the diverse polarity of the smart water samples results in different IFT values, as depicted by the interaction ratios between brine ions and n-Decane in Fig. 8-a.Fig. 8Effect of oil polarity and PDIs concentration on IFT of smart water/oil at ambient conditions (a) n-Decane (b) CO (c) Oil5 (d) Oil20.

In Fig. 8, the polarity of the oils follows the order n-Decane < CO < Oil5 < Oil20, as determined by the analysis of TAN and TBN in Table 4. The results presented for different smart water samples in Fig. 8 demonstrate that an increase in oil polarity enhances its potential to form natural surfactants at the smart water/oil interface. Consequently, as the oil polarity increases, the IFT decreases across all brine samples. According to the findings presented by Abdi et al.71, the behavior of IFT in smart water is influenced by the interaction mechanism between the PCs of oil and the hydration shell of various ions in smart water. The presence of different PCs (including acidic and basic components) in the oil, as well as the diverse ions in SW, leads to distinct interactions between the PCs of oil and smart water ions. The described interactions are influenced by the hydration energy and abundance of ions. Higher hydration energy and greater ion abundance intensify these interactions. In this regard, the changes in IFT for SO42− and Mg2+ shown in Fig. 8 exhibit the most significant variations compared to changes in their concentrations. This behavior can be attributed to the fact that these two ions, being the strongest anion and cation in SW, respectively, possess a higher interaction potential with the PCs of oil. Consequently, the changes in IFT related to the oil polarity are more pronounced than the changes in the concentration of SO42− and Mg2+ ions.

The increase in the concentration of active ions (Mg2+, Ca2+, SO42−) leads to a competition between the adsorption and desorption of asphaltene at the interface of the two phases. This increase enhances the potential for interaction between the hydration shell of ions and the PCs of the oil. The intensification of interactions I–IV due to the higher concentration of active ions can result in either a decrease or an increase in IFT, as observed in Fig. 8. The opposing forces of adsorption and repulsion between the PCs at the interface are present in all smart water samples, and the polarity of the oil also influences this interaction. Based on the comparison of interactions in Fig. 8 for CO, Oil5, and Oil20, it is observed that doubling the concentration of active ions in SW enhances the attraction of PCs at the interface by intensifying the opposing forces of adsorption and repulsion. This increased absorption results in a lower IFT, indicating that the optimal concentration for reducing IFT is SW2X. Furthermore, the results presented in Fig. 8 demonstrate that the absence of active ions in SW also lowers the IFT. This behavior can be attributed to the interaction of monovalent ions, which are present in much higher numbers compared to active ions. Although the interaction of monovalent ions with the PCs of oil is weaker, their abundance increases the likelihood of interaction. Therefore, despite the weaker interaction, the significant number of monovalent ions affects the behavior of IFT and compensates for the difference in hydration energy between them and active ions.

It has been demonstrated that contact angle changes during smart water flooding are dependent on the dispersion of low saline/smart brine into the high saline water film bounded between rock and oil119. Therefore, contact angle tests need to include the dynamics of ionic transport84,120,121, and imitate real sequences of salinity changes that can take place in a pore space64. To do this, contact angles need to be first measured in high saline water and then replaced with low saline/smart water to measure the contact angle of desired low saline/smart brine122; whereas, conventional contact angle tests include placing an oil droplet on an oil-wet rock sample directly in contact with desired low-saline/smart brine without prior contact of high saline water67,103,104. According to some research, the conventional technique results in deceptive angles because it is unable to represent the ionic transport and dynamics of electrostatic interaction in the film as salinity is altered/reduced123. Indeed, the initial salinity of the thin water film must always remain high to determine the equilibrium contact angle within low salinity circumstances123. Some studies also suggested immersing thin sections in the desired low saline brine and in the absence of crude oil droplet, for 30 min before performing the contact angle test, for better generation of water film between oil and rock33,122; since, the stability and thickness of the water film will identify the rock’s wettability19,124. An unstable or thin water film may cause a significant decrease in diffusion flux; consequently, we observe less change in the rock wettability toward water wetness125. When the thin sections are not exposed to high saline brine prior to the low salinity water, the stable water film cannot form on the rock surface. Hence, the wettability change becomes negligible as observed in Fig. 9. Another reason for the observed results is the temperature dependency of wettability126–128, which is often neglected during conventional contact angle measurements67,103,104. However, in some studies, no discernible growth in the activity of low-salinity brine was observed by raising the temperature84,85.

Effect of oil polarity and smart water on oil recovery

Based on the evaluation of the IFT and contact angle of smart water, the SW2S sample, which exhibited the lowest IFT and the ability to modify the contact angle compared to other samples (excluding the SW4S sample), was examined for tertiary oil recovery assessment. However, the contact angle evaluation indicated that the SW4S sample would yield the best result (higher hydrophilicity). Nevertheless, considering the evaluation of IFT and the need to prevent mineral scale formation, the SW2S sample was deemed the optimal fluid for oil recovery evaluation. SO42− is the strongest anion (in terms of hydration energy) in SW and has been demonstrated in studies by Maghsoudian et al.129, Baradari et al.130, Ghosh et al.131, and Ghosh et al.132 to cause the mineral scale formation. Therefore, determining its optimal concentration is crucial for smart waterflooding. Hence, the SW2S sample was selected for smart water evaluation. To enable a comparison between tertiary recovery and the impact of low-salinity brine and smart water, FW and SW were evaluated as primary and secondary recovery samples, respectively, followed by the SW2S sample as the tertiary recovery sample. Based on the results presented in Fig. 10, an increase in oil polarity leads to higher tertiary oil recovery. Specifically, Oil20 exhibited a recovery factor of approximately 26% higher than CO. These results highlight the significant influence of oil polarity on oil recovery, consistent with studies by Golmohammadi et al.64, and Chávez-Miyauch et al.25. The increase in SO42− ion concentration, coupled with increased oil polarity, has been shown to have a substantial impact on oil recovery. Additional recoveries of 12.06%, 6.53%, and 5.5% are obtained by the combined effect of improving oil polarity with doubling the concentration of SO42− ion as tertiary injection mode for Oil20, Oil5, and CO, respectively. This behavior can be explained by the propensity of SO42− ions to interact with the acidic components of oil. As indicated by the evaluation of IFT and contact angle, the polarity changes of Oil20, compared to CO, primarily affected the acidic components (as evidenced by the TAN analysis in Table 4). Consequently, the interaction between the acidic components of oil and the hydration shell of SO42− ions resulted in higher oil recovery for samples with higher TAN values. The larger increase in TAN for Oil5 and Oil20 compared to CO, along with a higher concentration of anions in the brine bulk (SW, SW2S), underscores the greater impact of anion interactions on the results shown in Fig. 10. The incremental oil recovery in Oil5 can be attributed to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{\rm I}\text{\rm I}$$\end{document}II interaction. In this regard, a lower TBN implies a reduced \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{\rm I}\text{\rm I}$$\end{document}II interaction potential, enhancing the effectiveness of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{\rm I}$$\end{document}I interaction. However, the better performance of Oil20 in comparison with Oil5 highlights the influence of higher TAN and greater polarity of Oil20, as well as increased interaction between PCs of Oil20 and brine anions. Despite the highest recovery factor value of Oil20, it exhibited limited effectiveness in reducing the contact angle. This behavior could be attributed to the formation of an elastic fluid–fluid interface that prevents capillary trapping and snap-off25,64. Additionally, the enhanced continuity of the oil phase on oil-wet surfaces can increase oil recovery through film flow3,13,30. Another reason behind the effective results of coreflooding experiments compared to contact angle tests is the different injection sequences followed during these tests. In coreflooding experiments, after the initial FW flooding of an oil-wet coreplug, the water film of high salinity brine is formed. In the next stages, low saline/smart water is injected into the coreplug to complete the diffusion process of low salinity brine entering the water film; which can increase the amount of oil recovery. In this regard, Farhadi et al.122, proposed to consider fluid injection sequences of contact angle tests aligned with coreflooding experiments. In other words, to simulate the initial corflooding of high saline brine (FW), the contact angle of the oil drop must be measured while it is in contact with desired high saline water (FW). Then, the high saline water must be substituted with low salinity water (SW) to resemble the secondary coreflooding. The same procedure must be followed for the tertiary brine (SW2S). At the end, the equilibrium angle in contact with each brine is taken as the contact angle of each flooding step in coreflooding process122. In this way, changes in the contact angle during and after brine exchange could represent a diffusion-controlled process; that enhances oil recovery by weakening the rock wettability122. The contact angle results showed that the interaction between the hydration shell of ions in smart water and the PCs of oil influenced the contact angle. Anions in smart water, particularly SO42−, had a strong interaction with the acidic components of oil, leading to lower contact angles. The variation in oil polarity affected the contact angle values, but the changes were not significant enough to alter the wettability of carbonates. These little contact angle changes during smart waterflooding depend on low saline brine dispersion into the high saline water film and the effect of temperature which are often overlooked in conventional contact angle measurements. Thus, conventional contact angle tests without the exchange of brines may result in deceptive angles due to the inability to represent ionic transport and electrostatic interaction.

The evaluation of oil recovery showed that the increase in oil polarity, particularly for Oil20, resulted in higher tertiary oil recovery compared to CO. The interaction between SO42− ions and the acidic components of oil played a significant role in oil recovery. The slope of oil recovery changes was higher for Oil20 compared to Oil5 and CO, indicating better separation of oil from the rock surface. The interaction between anions and PCs of oil influenced the results. The evaluation suggests that the interaction between ions and PCs of oil is the primary mechanism for smart water in oil recovery. The evaluation of effluent pH showed that the interaction between cations and PCs of oil is the primary factor in oil recovery during FW injection. The increase in cation concentration in FW leads to a decrease in pH and enhanced oil recovery due to the attraction between cations and basic oil components. The steep slope of pH change for Oil20 can be attributed to the interaction between acidic components of oil with brine anions, resulting in better oil separation from carbonate surfaces. The higher concentration of anions in the smart water produced alongside acidic components of oil contributes to higher pH values in the effluent. The alignment between pH changes and oil recovery emphasizes the significance of interactions between anions and PCs of oil.
