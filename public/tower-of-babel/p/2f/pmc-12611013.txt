Determinants of psychosocial well-being of older adults in Sarawak, Malaysia
Authors: Jeffery Stephen, Sam F. Jiee, Azimah Azman, Md Mizanur Rahman, Rosalia Saimon, Hun C. Gui, Lim S. Hee, Romano Ngui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Malaysia’s rapid demographic shift toward an ageing society poses significant public health challenges, particularly with respect to psychosocial well-being. Sarawak, an ethnically diverse state, faces unique issues related to household dynamics, ageing, and urbanization. This study aimed to describe the sociodemographic characteristics of older adults in urban Sarawak and assess their association with psychosocial well-being.MATERIALS AND METHODS:The cross-sectional study involved 766 older adults aged 60 and above in Kuching and Sibu. Participants were selected using multistage stratified random sampling and were interviewed using a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire. The mental health continuum–short form was utilized to assess their psychosocial well-being, which was classified into poor, moderate, and good categories. Associations with sociodemographic factors were analyzed using Chi-square tests and ordinal logistic regression.RESULTS:Of the 766 respondents, 50.8% were male, and 45.7% were aged 60–64 years. The prevalence of poor, moderate, and good psychosocial well-being was 37.2%, 31.9%, and 30.9%, respectively. Age group and household size were significantly associated with well-being (P < 0.05). Younger respondents (60–64 years) were more likely to report good well-being (odds ratio [OR] = 1.78; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.13–2.81; P = 0.013), while smaller household size reduced the likelihood of poor well-being (OR = 0.55; 95% CI: 0.33–0.91; P = 0.019).CONCLUSION:Recognizing that age and household size influence the psychosocial well-being of older adults in Sarawak presents an opportunity for constructive intervention. Tailoring age-specific programs to the context of urban, multigenerational living can effectively promote healthy ageing. By focusing on family engagement, community support, and access to resources, we can enhance resilience and improve the general standard of living for older adults in these environments.

Introduction

The global ageing population is one of the most significant demographic shifts of the 21st century. By the year 2050, demographic projections indicate that the proportion of the global population aged 60 and above will have doubled from the current figure, surpassing 2 billion individuals.[1] Improved healthcare, rising living standards, and declining fertility rates are driving this demographic shift. It brings both challenges and opportunities, including the need for enhanced geriatric services, supportive infrastructure, and inclusive ageing policies. The strain is particularly evident in developing countries, where the pace of ageing outpaces the capacity of health and social care systems.[2] As life expectancy increases, recognition that well-being in later life must encompass not only physical health but also emotional, psychological, and social dimensions also increases.[3]

In accordance with this shift, the World Health Organization (WHO) proposed the idea of healthy ageing, which is characterized not just by the lack of illness but as the journey of fostering and preserving functional capabilities that promote well-being in later life.[4] Psychosocial well-being, a central aspect of this framework, includes the capacity to regulate emotions, build meaningful relationships, and engage actively in society.[5] There is now increasing acknowledgement that older adults with poor psychosocial well-being are at higher risk of depression, cognitive decline, institutionalization, and premature mortality.[67]

Ageing of the population in Malaysia is progressing at an unprecedented rate. According to national projections, Malaysia will become an aged nation by 2040, with at least 15% of its population aged 60 years and above.[8] This transformation is occurring alongside accelerated urbanisation and shifting family dynamics, particularly in urban and semi-urban settings. Traditional support systems for older persons are weakening, and many older adults face challenges such as social isolation, caregiving strain, and limited access to mental health support.[910] Despite this growing challenge, national surveys and health initiatives have predominantly focused on physical disability, healthcare utilization, and noncommunicable diseases, often overlooking the psychosocial dimensions of healthy ageing.[1112]

Sarawak, Malaysia’s largest and most ethnically diverse state, presents a unique yet under-researched context. With nearly 10% of its population already aged 60 years or older and projections suggesting this figure will reach 15% by 2028, Sarawak is on the frontline of Malaysia’s demographic shift.[13] Urban centers such as Kuching and Sibu are experiencing a dual burden of ageing and urbanization, which has introduced challenges to the provision of equitable, accessible, and culturally appropriate elder care services. Multigenerational living remains common but increasing economic pressures and changing cultural norms have altered traditional caregiving dynamics.[14] Multigenerational living, once protective, now presents mixed outcomes owing to rising intergenerational tensions and reduced autonomy. Moreover, elder-specific health infrastructure and community mental health programs remain limited.[15]

While research in various regions of Malaysia and Southeast Asia has highlighted the influence of factors such as age, gender, income, and marital status on depression or feelings of loneliness in older adults, little use has been made of comprehensive instruments like the mental health continuum, short form (MHC–SF) to assess overall psychosocial well-being.[161718] In addition, existing evidence often treats the older adult population as a homogeneous group, without taking cognizance of important stratifications such as age subgroup (young-old vs. old-old), household composition, and urban-local cultural differences. These gaps limit policymakers’ and practitioners’ capacity to develop targeted, effective interventions.

This study responds to these gaps by examining the sociodemographic determinants of psychosocial well-being of older adults in urban Sarawak. Guided by the WHO age-friendly cities framework, it uses the MHC–SF to explore how variables such as age, gender, income, marital status, education, and household size relate to mental well-being. By contextualizing the findings within the lived realities of older adults in Kuching and Sibu, this research aims to support localized, pragmatic strategies for healthy ageing.

Materials and Methods

This study, conducted over 18 months, from April 2023 to October 2024, utilized a community-based cross-sectional design to investigate the sociodemographic factors affecting the psychosocial well-being of older adults in two major urban areas of Sarawak: Kuching and Sibu. It commenced after approval had been received from the Institutional Review Board. These cities were selected for their demographic diversity, accessibility, and growing proportion of older adults, making them ideal for investigating the intersection of urban living and ageing in East Malaysia. The study population comprised community-dwelling older adults aged 60 years and above residing in selected districts of Kuching and Sibu. The inclusion criteria were the ability to communicate in Malay, English, or a local dialect, the absence of severe cognitive impairment, and consent to participate. Individuals who were bedridden, institutionalized, or diagnosed with dementia were excluded. To achieve a robust and representative sample, a total size of 766 participants was calculated using a single population proportion formula. This estimation was based on a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error, incorporating a projected prevalence rate of 50% for individuals experiencing poor psychosocial well-being. Furthermore, an additional 15% was added to the final sample size to account for potential nonresponses and ensure the reliability of the findings. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board vide Letter No. FME/23/52 dated 23/03/2023, and written informed consent was taken from all the participants in the study.

A multistage stratified sampling method was employed throughout the southern and middle zones of Sarawak [Figure 1]. Initially, administrative divisions in each city were stratified by population density. Subsequently, residential areas within selected divisions were randomly chosen. Finally, eligible older adults from households within these areas were selected using simple random sampling. Proportional allocation ensured representation from both cities. Data was collected via in-person interviews using a standardized questionnaire administered by trained field enumerators. The survey was organized into three main parts. The initial part gathered data on the sociodemographic characteristics of the respondents. The second part assessed psychosocial well-being utilizing the MHC–SF. This tool has been confirmed for use for older populations across different cultural contexts, including those in Southeast Asia.[19] The assessment comprises 11 items that evaluate three areas: emotional well-being (composed of positive emotions and satisfaction with life), psychological well-being (comprising autonomy, personal growth, and self-acceptance), and social well-being (involving social integration, contribution, and coherence). Participants reported the frequency of these feelings they had experienced in the last month, utilizing a 6-point Likert scale that ranged from “never” to “every day.” The second part of the questionnaire assessed psychosocial well-being using the MHC–SF, a validated instrument comprising 14 items that assessed emotional, psychological, and social well-being. Participants rated the frequency of specific feelings during the past month on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“never”) to 5 (“every day”). Each item was scored between 0 and 5, giving a total raw score range of 0–70. According to established scoring methods, participants were classified with poor well-being (low scores in emotional and psychological or social items), moderate well-being, and good well-being (frequent experiences of positive states across all domains). To ensure content validity, the questionnaire was reviewed by a panel of experts in public health, gerontology, and clinical psychology. A pretest involving 30 older adults from a non-participating district was conducted to assess clarity, cultural appropriateness, and internal consistency. The Cronbach’s alpha for the MHC–SF in this study was 0.89, indicating high internal reliability.

Data entry and cleaning were conducted using Microsoft Excel, and analysis was done using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software version 28.0 (SPSS, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics summarized the characteristics of the sample, which included means, standard deviations (SDs), frequencies, and percentages. The relationship between categories of psychosocial well-being (poor, moderate, and good) and independent variables was evaluated using Chi-square tests for categorical data. Variables displaying P values of <0.20 in bivariate analysis were incorporated into an ordinal logistic regression model to calculate adjusted odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for factors influencing psychosocial well-being. The criterion for statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All statistical evaluations were carried out under the guidance of a senior biostatistician to ensure robustness and reproducibility.

Results

A total of 766 older adults participated in the study [Table 1]. The mean age was 67.8 years (SD = 6.1), with 45.7% in the 60–64 age group and 11.5% aged 75 and above. Slightly more than half (50.8%) were male, and the majority (76.1%) were of Chinese and Malay ethnicity. Most participants were married (67.9%), had secondary school education or higher (62.0%), and reported a monthly household income below RM3000 (56.5%). Approximately 65.1% lived in households with fewer than five people. In terms of psychosocial well-being, 37.2% of respondents were classified as having low well-being, 31.9% with moderate, and 30.9% with high [Table 2]. The proportion of high well-being was greater among younger participants (60–64 years) and those living in smaller households [Table 3]. Prevalence differences by gender, income, education, and marital status were not statistically significant in bivariate analysis.

Ordinal logistic regression identified two statistically significant predictors of psychosocial well-being: age group and household size [Table 4]. Respondents aged 60–64 years were significantly more likely to report higher well-being than those aged ≥75 years (OR = 1.78; 95% CI: 1.13–2.81; P = 0.013). Respondents aged 60–64 years had significantly higher odds of reporting good psychosocial well-being than those aged 65–74 years (OR = 1.42; 95% CI: 1.01–2.01; P = 0.045). This finding suggests that even within the younger cohorts of older adults, well-being declines modestly with increasing age. The odds of good well-being were highest among the youngest group (60–64), intermediate in the 65–74 group, and lowest among those aged 75 and above, indicating a stepwise decrease in well-being across advancing age brackets. Similarly, those living in households with fewer than five people had higher odds of good well-being than those in larger households (OR = 1.82; 95% CI: 1.10–3.01; P = 0.019). Other variables, including gender, income level, education, and marital status, did not show statistically significant associations in the adjusted model. The model met the proportional odds assumption and showed good fit, as confirmed by the test of parallel lines (P = 0.37).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that age and household size are key determinants of the psychosocial well-being of older adults in urban Sarawak. The finding that the youngest cohort (60–64 years) had significantly higher odds of better psychosocial well-being compared to those aged 75 and above supports existing literature that mental health often declines with advancing age.[2021] The significant difference in well-being between the 60–64 and 65–74 age groups further underscores the importance of early preventive mental health efforts, as even subtle age progression within older adulthood appears to impact psychosocial outcomes. The “young-old” typically enjoy better physical health, social participation, and autonomy, which protect against psychological distress, loneliness, and cognitive impairment.[22] In contrast, the “oldest-old” are more likely to experience functional limitations, bereavement, or reduced social networks, which can cumulatively erode emotional resilience and overall well-being.[23]

Interestingly, this study did not find significant associations between psychosocial well-being and such variables as education level, household income, or the presence of chronic medical conditions. These null findings may reflect the complex and context-specific nature of psychosocial well-being, particularly in a setting like Sarawak, where strong cultural values, community bonds, and informal support systems can buffer the impact of socioeconomic disadvantage. It is possible that the emotional and social resilience of older adults may moderate the effects of economic or health limitations, reducing their observable influence in regression models. In addition, given the inclusion of only community-dwelling older adults, participants with more severe physical or economic hardship may have been underrepresented, thereby attenuating the association between these variables and mental well-being. These findings suggest that structural indicators like income and education may not universally predict well-being, and that qualitative dimensions such as perceived autonomy, coping ability, and intergenerational support could be more salient in this population.[24] In multigenerational Asian households, while co-residence with family is traditionally regarded as beneficial, this arrangement can sometimes generate care-giving burdens, intergenerational conflict, or loss of autonomy, factors linked to lower psychological well-being.[25] Studies from Indonesia and Thailand have also reported that the quality of family interactions, rather than household size per se, is a stronger predictor of life satisfaction in older adults.[2627]

Notably, other variables such as gender, education, income, marital status, and medical conditions did not influence psychosocial well-being in the ordinal regression model. This is somewhat inconsistent with global findings that link higher education and income with better mental health.[28] However, the Malaysian context may provide unique explanatory factors. Older adults in Malaysia benefit from a range of government-funded safety nets, including access to universal healthcare, targeted cash assistance, and various nongovernmental community support programs.[2930] These schemes may buffer the influence of economic or educational disparities on mental health outcomes. Furthermore, psychosocial well-being may be more closely linked to subjective perceptions of social connectedness, meaning in life, and emotional support rather than absolute material resources.[3132] The absence of significant associations between gender and well-being aligns with some recent regional studies, which suggest that the gender gap in the mental health of the elderly may be narrowing as societal roles evolve.[33] Similarly, medical comorbidities, often linked to poor mental health, did not predict well-being in this study. This may reflect effective chronic disease management, the psychological adaptability of older adults, or the influence of other unmeasured factors such as spirituality or community engagement.

From a public health and policy standpoint, these findings hold important implications. First, ageing policies must be differentiated by age subgroups. Older adults in the 75-plus category appear particularly vulnerable and may benefit from structured psychosocial support interventions, such as befriending programs, senior activity centers, or age-segmented mental health screenings. Second, elder housing policy and urban planning should consider alternatives to conventional multi-generational arrangements. While family cohabitation has cultural and practical appeal, promoting smaller, elder-friendly living environments may enhance autonomy and well-being, particularly in dense urban settings such as Kuching and Sibu.[34] Community health programs should also identify and target “hidden at-risk” individuals, those in large households who may have physical company but are socially or emotionally isolated. Interventions that enhance respect, inclusion, and participation of older adults, elements emphasized in the WHO Age-friendly Cities Framework, are vital for sustaining psychosocial health.[35] These may include intergenerational programs, volunteer opportunities, or access to digital technologies that bridge social distance.[3637]

This study has several strengths. It is one of the few empirical investigations that focused on the psychosocial well-being of older adults in East Malaysia, specifically urban Sarawak, using a validated multidimensional tool, the MHC–SF. The use of a large, community-based sample and rigorous multistage stratified sampling enhances the generalizability of the findings within similar urban settings. Furthermore, the analytical approach, including ordinal logistic regression, allows for robust assessment of associations across ordered levels of well-being. This study makes a significant contribution to the Malaysian ageing literature, particularly for Sarawak, where data on the psychosocial well-being of older adults is limited. Using a validated, multidimensional instrument and a rigorous statistical approach strengthens the credibility of the findings. However, several limitations should be noted. The cross-sectional design limits the ability to infer causation, and the reliance on self-reported data may lead to issues of recall or social desirability bias. Moreover, factors such as loneliness, the quality of family relationships, community involvement, and perceived respect, known to influence well-being, were not assessed and should be included in future models. In addition, the study did not gather detailed information on the marital context of participants, including the number of children and co-residence with offspring, or current marital status (e.g., living together, separated, or widowed but still legally married). These aspects may have an impact on the psychosocial well-being of older adults, particularly in collectivist cultures where family structure, caregiving roles, and spousal companionship are closely linked to emotional and social support.

Future research should utilize a mixed-methods approach to explore the lived experiences that underlie these statistical associations. Qualitative research, such as in-depth interviews and focus group discussions, can provide insight into how older adults in Sarawak perceive “well-being,” that encompasses all aspects of emotional, social, and physical life. In addition, exploring the strategies these individuals employ to navigate intergenerational dynamics within their families will enhance our understanding of their daily lives. Moreover, longitudinal studies are essential for the examination of how changes in household composition, such as family size or living arrangements, interact with ageing trajectories over time. By tracking these variables, researchers will gain valuable insights into the intricate realities of ageing and support systems within this community.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that age and household size significantly predict the psychosocial well-being of older adults in urban Sarawak. Respondents aged 60–64 years and those living in smaller households reported significantly higher levels of well-being. These findings challenge traditional assumptions in collectivist cultures, where larger, multigenerational households are often presumed to provide better social and emotional support. Instead, smaller households may afford older adults greater autonomy, privacy, and psychological space, contributing to improved mental health. The oldest-old group (aged ≥75) appeared particularly vulnerable, suggesting that age-specific psychosocial interventions are needed. The lack of significant associations with gender, income, education, or marital status highlights the need to look beyond traditional demographic categories and consider household dynamics and perceived autonomy as key influences on mental well-being.

In Sarawak, policies must be tailored to reflect the state’s diverse cultural landscape and unique demographic composition. Urban areas such as Sibu may benefit from targeted initiatives that improve access to services and community-based programs. As the population ages, it is essential to regard psychosocial well-being as a vital health outcome alongside physical health. Rather than a one-size-fits-all model, locally grounded interventions should integrate principles of inclusivity, accessibility, and social connection—core aspects of age-supportive environments. Collaborations between local councils, public health departments, and civil society will be crucial to closing service gaps and fostering of community engagement. These actions, tailored to each locality and informed by empirical evidence, are essential to advancing healthy, independent, and dignified ageing. These measures, customized to local needs and grounded in evidence-based insights, are essential for the promotion of healthy ageing and improvement of the quality of life for older adults in Sarawak and beyond.
