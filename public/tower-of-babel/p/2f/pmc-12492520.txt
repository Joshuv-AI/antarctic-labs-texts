Delivering an insecticide-treated net campaign in a complex operating environment: lessons from Anambra state, Nigeria
Authors: Laitan Adeniyi, Kunle Rotimi, Elisabeth G. Chestnutt, Abiola O. Oluwagbemiga, Saliu Ogunmola, Nonso Ndibe, Olusola Oresanya, Julianna Smith, Kolawole Maxwell, Tarekegn A. Abeku
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalaria is one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in Nigeria. Distribution of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) is an important vector control strategy. In 2022, despite security challenges and COVID-19 restrictions, an ITN distribution was implemented in Anambra state. This paper describes the adaptations made to the ITN distribution campaign to ensure the safety of personnel, communities and property during implementation.MethodsFollowing national COVID-19 guidelines, the ITN campaign implemented a single-phase door-to-door distribution strategy. In May 2022, security risk categorization activity was carried out prior to the campaign to determine risk levels in each local government area (LGA) in the state. All LGAs and wards were classified into three risk categories as either high, medium or low. A set of adaptations were made to mitigate risk during the delivery of ITNs in high-risk locations. High risk LGAs were characterized by limited activities with comprehensive coordination involving both security agencies and existing community structures. The security situation in each LGA and ward was monitored during the campaign to ensure the correct set of actions were followed throughout.ResultsAfter conducting risk categorization, 11 out of the 21 LGAs in Anambra state were classified as high security risk. The adapted campaign strategy was implemented across all 11 LGAs. During the campaign 3,850,316 ITNs were delivered to 1,245,548 households across all 21 LGAs in the state. Engaging with community leaders and security agencies during the planning and implementation phases was critical for ensuring the safe delivery of ITNs to households in high-risk areas. Working with distributors from within the community and consistent use of communication technologies also helped to reduce the risk to personnel and supported the monitoring of the security situation and the progress of the distribution within each LGA.ConclusionDespite the insecurity in parts of Anambra state, ITNs were delivered to households in all LGAs. Engaging with existing community structures and appropriate use of technology can help to deliver malaria prevention interventions in security-compromised areas. The lessons from this campaign can help to inform and improve the strategies of future ITN distribution campaigns in complex operating environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05529-w.

Background

Insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) are the most widely used method of malaria vector control globally and are estimated to have averted 450 million malaria cases in Africa between from 2000 and 2015 [1, 2]. Over the last two decades over 2 billion ITNs have been delivered in the Africa region, most of which have been delivered through mass distribution campaigns [3]. Planning and implementing mass campaigns often require complex logistics and a large number of personnel.

Implementing mass ITN distribution campaigns can be especially challenging in settings affected by instability and conflict. Over one billion people globally live in areas affected by conflict or instability, many of which overlap with areas where malaria is endemic [4, 5]. Previous studies have shown that conflict disrupts access to health services [6]. These disruptions also affect access to malaria services and have been observed to lead to rises in malaria cases. For example, during a period of intense conflict in Ethiopia malaria cases increased by 1.3 million in one year [7]. Similarly political and social instability in Myanmar has seen malaria rise from 78,000 cases in 2019 to 584,000 cases in 2022 [7]. Therefore, maintaining access to malaria prevention in areas affected by conflict is especially important.

Nigeria accounts for nearly 27% of global malaria cases and in recent years parts of the country have been experiencing periods of instability [7]. In Anambra state security challenges have affected access to health services in a variety of ways [8]. Stay-at-home orders and fear can prevent people from travelling to health facilities and security concerns can impede the delivery of health interventions and commodities to insecure areas [8].

An ITN distribution campaign was implemented in Anambra state in 2022. During the planning and implementation several adaptations were made to comply with COVID-19 restrictions and to ensure the safety of personnel and property in insecure areas. The adaptations made to comply with COVID-19 protocols included the use of a single-phase door-to-door distribution strategy and are described in detail elsewhere [9]. This paper describes the process of identifying areas with high security risk, implementing adaptations to mitigate security challenges and the lessons from delivering ITNs in a complex operating environment.

Methods

Anambra state and its malaria situation

Anambra state is situated in the southeastern region of Nigeria, bounded by Delta, Imo, Enugu and Kogi states (Fig. 1). The state comprises 21 LGAs and 330 wards. In 2021, when the microplanning of the campaign was conducted, Anambra state had an estimated population of 6,322,000. Design of ITN door-to-door campaign

The Anambra ITN campaign was implemented by Malaria Consortium, in collaboration with the National Malaria Elimination Programme (NMEP), with funding from Open Philanthropy and additional philanthropic funds based on GiveWell’s recommendation. Campaign-related activities started on 8 June 2022 with a state flag off and all activities concluded on 3 September 2022. The ITN distribution was conducted between 7 and 20 August 2022. All campaign activities were carried out in accordance with the NMEP’s Implementation Guide for ITN Mass Campaign in the Context of COVID-19 [12].

Security categorization

Prior to the distribution campaign the security risk level in each of the 21 LGAs in the state was assessed. Malaria Consortium’s Security Framework outlines three levels of risk and the actions to be followed in each situation (Table 2). To assign a risk level, each LGA was visited to gather information from the LGA Malaria Programme Office, locals and security agencies. The frequency, severity and recency of past security incidents were also analysed. In addition, partner liaison security operation incident mapping tools, developed by USAID, were used to triangulate up-to-date information [13]. Threat level indicators

LGAs were categorized as low risk if there were minimal or no disruption to day-to-day activities. A medium level of risk was assigned to LGAs where there were threats of kidnap for ransom, civil unrest and sporadic outbreaks of violence. LGAs with the active presence of armed groups, which posed a continuous risk in those LGAs in addition to the threats characterized for the medium risk LGAs, were categorized as high risk.

The categorization process produced a matrix to inform the specific distribution strategy to be used in each of the LGAs, wards, and communities. In low-risk LGAs (LRLGA) the single-phase door-to-door distribution strategy was carried out without modification. In high-risk LGAs (HRLGA) a new strategy was developed to ensure the safety of staff during the ITN distribution campaign.

Adaptation of the ITN campaign strategy

Following risk categorisation, a Campaign Implementing Team was established. The team included key representatives from security organizations, agencies, and community gatekeepers from state to local level. The guidance provided by these actors played a crucial role throughout the campaign, identifying where adaptations were needed and ensuring security measures were in place to safeguard personnel, material assets and communities.

Recruitment

In the LRLGAs, personnel recruitment followed the selection criteria provided in the National Campaign Guidelines. The criteria included community members who were non-armed personnel, from or resident in the implementation community and had a functional commercial bank account. For the HRLGAs, modifications were made to recruit personnel from a pool of existing community structures and gatekeepers, such as religious and traditional leaders and youth leaders, to comprise distribution teams. The community leaders and gatekeepers were trained as part of the distribution team and participated in the door-to-door delivery of nets from the distribution hubs to households.

Training

In LRLGAs training sessions were organized at different levels within the LGA, with some help at LGA level and others at ward level in clusters. In HRLGAs all training sessions, including orientation sessions for town announcers, were centralized and conducted at LGA level. Orientation sessions for journalists, documentalists, as well as twenty-one civil society organizations were conducted at state level. Adaptations were made to all training sessions to ensure COVID-19 protocols were followed, including ensuring adequate spacing of participants to enable social distancing.

Distribution

The ITN distribution was conducted using a single-phase door-to-door strategy which involved simultaneous household registration and ITN distribution by trained personnel. A description of this distribution method is described in Adeniyi et al. [9]. In LRLGAs registration and distribution activities were carried out by community members. In contrast, in HRLGAs these activities were conducted by community leaders and gatekeepers including religious, traditional and youth leaders.

In both LRLGAs and HRLGAs no activities were carried out on Mondays due to the ‘sit-at-home’ order imposed by a government opposition group across the entire state. In LRLGAs the distribution was carried out over 12 days as outlined in the national guidelines. In HRLGAs the number of distribution days was reduced to 9 days to minimize the exposure of the mobilisation and distribution teams to security risks. Additional personnel were deployed in the HRLGA to ensure a faster distribution within the limited time. Modifications to the daily distribution target were made in LRLGAs and HRLGAs increasing the daily target from 200 ITNs as outline in the National Guidelines to 300 ITNs. However this modification was due to geographical features in the state, which is characterized by high level of urbanisation rather than security risk.

Monitoring and supervision

SurveyCTO was used for supervision and monitoring, and the end-process evaluation [14]. SurveyCTO, is a mobile platform with functionalities that allow the integration of supervisory checklists and enable remote monitoring of the campaign.

Monitoring activities in HRLGAs involved collaboration between community gatekeepers and LGA teams in the affected regions. Local stakeholders provided regular updates on the implementation within the communities. In addition, monitoring teams, including LGA coordinators and information communication and technology (ICT) for development training and technology administrators—campaign personnel who have specialist ICT skills—offered remote and centralized supervision.

Campaign data management and coordination

RedRose, a digital data collection application, was used in both LRLGAs and HRLGAs to collect and transmit household mobilization (registration) and ITN distribution data to supervisors and monitoring teams [15]. RedRose is a platform designed for conducting campaign registration, voucher distribution and supply chain tracking, functionalities which are more suitable for data management and coordination during campaigns. The application also captures location data which enables supervisors to track the campaign remotely, identify any challenges and make adjustments. The application requires an internet connection to sync and upload the data, which can be challenging in remote areas. To overcome this challenge teams also sent a WhatsApp or SMS message to supervisors at the end of each day to report the number of households registered and the ITNs distributed. The information transmitted by WhatsApp or SMS was aggregated but provided supervisors and monitoring teams with data for their daily meetings even if the RedRose platform could not be synced in the field.

Post-campaign monitoring

The end-process monitoring, conducted two days after the completion of the campaign, followed the same process in HRLGAs and LRLGAs. However, strict adherence was placed on the guidelines for HRLGAs which involved remote and centralized monitoring with limited in-person observation of the activities of the distribution teams. Local personnel were used for the post-campaign monitoring in collaboration with the existing structures. The end-process is a quality assurance process to provide rapid information about the quality of mobilization and ITN distribution, and to identify any areas that have not been covered. During the end-process a sample of 8 clusters, communities or settlements are selected for a rapid assessment. A total of 10 randomly selected households are sampled in each cluster.

Outcomes

Security risk categorization

The results of the security review showed that the security risk levels in the state was fluid during the campaign. When the review was conducted 10 LGAs were categorized as low risk and 11 LGAs were categorized as high risk (Fig. 2). Although some wards were categorized as medium risk (Table S1), these were located in LGAs where other wards were categorized as high-risk so the LGA was categorized as high-risk. At the time the assessment was conducted, there was no record of inaccessible wards in any of the 10 LRLGAs. The security categorization step provided a useful basis for activities to continue in HRLGAs as scheduled with the exception of one LGA, Ihiala, where access required travelling through a community categorized as high risk. Household mobilization and distribution

A total of 1593 household mobilization and distribution teams were deployed. A total of 1,245,548 households with a total population of 7,435,476 were registered and received 3,850,316 ITNs containing alpha-cypermethrin and piperonyl butoxide (PBO). The full results of the microplanning, household mobilisation, and distribution are provided in Table S2. The end-process evaluation conducted immediately after the distribution (within 2 days) found 98% of sampled households had been issued with ITNs. Of the households sampled during the end-process evaluation 94% still had the ITNs.

Lessons

Implementing an ITN campaign in a complex operating environment required adaptations to ensure the campaign could go ahead. However, some of the adaptations posed alternative challenges. Several features were also critical to the success of the campaign. The challenges and enablers are outlined below.

Challenges

Training

Adopting a centralized approach for training personnel for ITN distribution in HRLGA mitigated risks but this required participants to travel longer distances to attend the sessions. Renumerating transport costs for those travelling were therefore higher than usual in HRLGAs. The travel time also increased the length of time participants spent away from home and potentially contributed to fatigue due to longer days.

Monitoring

In some security-challenged wards in HRLGAs monitoring teams were reliant on the data submitted by the mobilisation and distribution teams instead of conducting in-person monitoring. The inability to conduct in-person monitoring prevented valuable physical observation to ensure that the mobilization and distribution teams carried out their activities according to plan. The lack of physical presence can also lead to gaps in accountability and adherence to ITN distribution standards.

Enablers

Government engagement

Continued engagement of the state government prior to and during the mass campaign encouraged political buy-in and state ownership. This also fostered the resolution of bottlenecks, such as inadequate capacity of warehouses to store ITNs. Part of the impact of the state ownership was the resource mobilization for some of the social and behavioural change activities spearheaded by the state government. This included the state flag-off and creating awareness through various communication media. This was particularly important for community engagement in the HRLGAs and encouraged ITN uptake across the state.

Collaboration with local stakeholders

The engagement of community structures and gatekeepers to support the distribution in HRLGAs greatly increased community acceptance and reduced the risk to personnel and property. Collaboration with unarmed security personnel within the state also provided a layer of security measures to the campaign team. However, the project still recorded some material losses of digital devices used for data collection, personal belongings of the distribution team, and ITNs.

Working with local community groups improved access to hard-to-reach areas and was critical for gathering reliable up-to-date information on the security situation. This information helped to ensure correct risk categorisation of locations and validate the number of households included in the distribution. The involvement of the grass-root community-based organizations (CBOs) also helped to engage communities and encourage uptake of ITNs.

Dual-approach data reporting

The dual approach of using WhatsApp or SMS messages together with the RedRose platform was beneficial in all LGAs as it helped to overcome issues with connectivity. However, in HRLGAs the use of WhatsApp and SMS platforms also enabled teams to communicate continually with supervisors and monitoring teams to report on the safety and security of teams. These messages were also useful where supervisors and monitoring teams could not enter the field to conduct observation as it allowed images to be sent for visual verification of processes, for example photographs of ITNs arriving at a particular location. In addition, the dual approach improved the safety for the mobilisation and distribution teams as it reduced the importance of syncing with the RedRose platform each day which may have required unnecessary travel, putting them at additional risk.

Discussion

The adaptations made to the 2022 ITN campaign in Anambra state enabled ITNs to be distributed to all LGAs in the state regardless of instability and unrest in some areas. Security risk categorization provided an opportunity for mitigation plans to be developed ahead of the campaign and assign extra resources to reduce risk in HRLGAs. The categorization also meant that additional resources were only allocated where they were needed.

The engagement of local stakeholders was critical for gathering up-to-date information throughout the planning and implementation to ensure LGAs were correctly categorized and adaptations put in place to protect personnel and property. The value of partnering with local and community-based actors for intelligence and service delivery in conflict-affected regions has been documented in several studies [16–21]. Had these adaptations not been made either staff would have been at higher risk of security threats or households in HRLGAs may have been left without access to ITNs.

The use of digital tools for monitoring and supervision of distribution staff from remote safe locations reduced the number of staff in insecure areas while still providing support to troubleshoot any issues. Digital technology also helped to track the whereabouts of those on the ground to monitor their safety. In addition, supervision teams were able to monitor the risk levels and if an area was recategorized they would be aware of which distributors needed to be contacted. Digital technologies have been shown to improve real-time decision-making and been deployed in other complex operating environments to provide services to locations that would otherwise be physically inaccessible [9, 18].

Despite the success of this campaign the adaptations presented some additional challenges, particularly when distributors had to travel further for training. The additional travel hours placed a time burden on distributors and a financial burden on the project. This challenge could be addressed by deploying online trainings, creating smaller training hubs closer to the participants and training more trainers for a highly decentralized ToT model. In addition, reducing the number of days for distribution required adding more staff to distribute ITNs, this also led to higher costs as more distributors had to be trained. Despite increased costs, the delivery of essential interventions in security-compromised areas is critical as conflict-affected communities have been shown to face additional barriers to care-seeking [22].

Security challenges present situations that are complex to operate in complicating both programme delivery and health access in general. Maintaining household access to malaria interventions is particularly important in conflict-affected settings as access to diagnostic and treatment services may be compromised. Although there have been many studies investigating the delivery of health interventions in humanitarian settings, we found comparatively few covering the delivery of health programmes in areas characterized by moderate instability and unrest. These settings present different challenges to humanitarian situations and require appropriately tailored adaptations for sustaining programme delivery.

Conclusion

Insecurity has increased in recent years in Anambra state, in spite of this, the successful delivery of ITNs to households across all LGAs demonstrates that malaria preventions efforts can be achieved in security-prone areas through the leveraging of existing structures, effective collaboration with local stakeholders and securing government buy-in. Adaptations of strategies through deployment of appropriate technologies in periods of instability is essential for ITN campaigns and distribution for malaria prevention and control. The lessons from this campaign can help to inform and improve the strategies of future ITN distribution campaigns in complex operating environments.
