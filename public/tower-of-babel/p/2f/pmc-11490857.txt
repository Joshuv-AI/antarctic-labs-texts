Detection and classification of surface defects on hot-rolled steel using vision transformers
Authors: Vinod Vasan, Naveen Venkatesh Sridharan, Sugumaran Vaithiyanathan, Mohammadreza Aghaei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study proposes a vision transformer to detect visual defects on steel surfaces. The proposed approach utilizes an open-source image dataset to classify steel surface conditions into six fault categories namely, crazing, inclusion, rolled in, pitted surface, scratches and patches. The defect images are first subject to resizing and then fed into a vision transformer subject to different hyperparameter configurations to determine the most optimal setting to render highest classification performance. The performance of the model is evaluated for different hyperparameter configurations, and the most optimal configuration is examined using the associated confusion matrices. It was observed that the proposed model presents a high overall accuracy of 96.39 % for detection and classification of steel surface faults. The study presents a descriptive insight into the vision transformer architecture and in addition, compares the performance of the current model with the results of other approaches suggested for application in literature. Vision transformers can serve as standalone approaches and suitable alternatives to the widely used convolution neural networks (CNNs) by actuating complex defect detection and classification tasks in real-time, enabling efficient and robust condition monitoring of a wide range of defects.Keywords: Deep neural network, Vision transformers, Automated defect identification, Steel surface defects, Non-destructive testing, Non-contact testing

Introduction

Effective metal quality inspection methodologies are crucial for the identification and addressal of critical flaws to prevent the end effects of catastrophic mechanical failures. Defective metals are unsuitable for use in their intended applications due to the deviations from product requirements and the potential hazards they pose to the environment and consumer safety. Steel, being a versatile and essential material for structural purposes, plays a vital role in the economic development of globalized nations. With crude steel production surpassing 1870 million tonnes globally for the year 2019 [1] and 1950 million tons in 2021, the steel industry ranks as the second-largest energy consumer on the global industrial front. Steel is favored for mechanical structural applications due to its desirable properties such as weldability, formability, corrosion resistance, machinability, recyclability, low production cost and comparatively high energy efficiency in extraction.

Structural materials such as steel are not exempt from surface and sub-surface defects due to various factors involved in its production, handling, and operational processes. Surface defects, including crazing, inclusions, and scratches, are commonly observed in steel, and must be detected early on. The increasing stringency of customer requirements for dimensional accuracy reaching micron-level precision necessitates strict adherence to such standards to ensure the long-term operational sustainability on part of both the manufacturers and customers. Minimizing rejections of manufactured components before distribution is crucial to avoid economic disadvantages for production enterprises. Monitoring the quality of manufactured steel allows for the assessment and evaluation of the robustness and refinement of the manufacturing processes within the production facility. Metal defects can arise from inherent issues in the base material or as a result of faulty operations along the production lines. Therefore, quality inspection processes, particularly non-contact and non-destructive testing (NDT) based inspection approaches, are pivotal in ensuring adherence to quality standards, safety parameters, and customer commitments regarding overall product batch quality and quantity. In a highly competitive market with a near-perpetual requirement for raw materials to cater to increasing consumer needs, time is of the essence. Thus, it becomes essential to achieve near-instantaneous results in product quality inspection and promptly execute corrective measures using automated inspection approaches. This in turn minimizes production and distribution lead time which is particularly crucial for widely used materials like steel.

To promote the transition of conventionally employed inspection methodologies into automated strategies, a significant amount of research has been focused on condition monitoring by leveraging the benefits of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in metal inspection techniques. AI-based approaches offer faster and more reliable inspection of metallic raw materials and finished products, overcoming manpower shortages, reducing human errors in classification and enabling the detection of even minute defects. Machine learning algorithms such as Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest and Extreme Learning Machine (ELM), and deep learning models such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers (ViTs) have been employed for metal defect detection and classification. Specifically, deep learning models possess the ability to adapt and classify new input data without the need for human supervision, even after the training phase. Deep learning models offer additional and enhanced decision-making capabilities even when faced with limited availability of data. By leveraging the strengths of deep learning such as its adaptive nature and resemblance to human cognition, the limitations of pure machine learning algorithms can be addressed in the context of defect inspection. Thus, deep learning models are also increasingly adopted in the field of defect inspection, especially in the metallurgical field to enable computationally optimal, faster, diverse and more robust defect classification.

Numerous solutions have been explored and recorded by the scientific community to detect metal defects using machine learning and deep learning approaches as explained herewith. Keesug et al. adopted a line scan camera and linear light source to perform defect detection of rolled steel (POSCO steel rolling strip surface inspection system). This identification and classification of six types of rolled steel strip defects were executed using a multi-class Support Vector Machine algorithm coupled with image processing techniques, thereby resulting in a classification accuracy ranging from 87 % to 94 % [2]. A Process Knowledge-based Multi-class Support Vector Machine (PK-MSVM) classification approach for surface defect detection in hot rolled steel was proposed by Agarwal et al. in 2011 which achieved a minimum error rate of 9.2 % [3]. A framework for the detection of surface defects using Scale Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) and voting strategy with SVM for feature extraction was proposed in 2012 by Suvdaa et al. [4]. Line scan cameras with adaptive local binarization coupled with Discrete Wavelet Transformation (DWT) strategy was advocated by Yun et al. in 2014 to filter out noise from image backgrounds and isolate defects. The performance of this system over 1550 defect images was found to reach 84.7 % as a result of the classification process [5]. In 2016, a genetic algorithm coupled with mathematic morphology-based binarization method for strip steel defect image with non-uniform illumination was explored by Liu et al. [6]. The classification of surface defects in large-scale strip steel image collection was attempted by Hu et al. using a hybrid chromosome genetic algorithm-based approach [7]. Huang et al. examined an intelligent defect detection system for metal products through the use of a GAN architecture in 2018 [8]. The detection of defects in industrial road networks was investigated by Wan et al. via the proposal of an anti-noise dual branch network (ADNet) in 2024 [9].

In 2020, Ryu et al. attained 92.68 % accuracy of classification during the detection of corner crack defects in steel billets using wavelet reconstruction and SVM classifier as part of an examination of adaptive filter parameter selection. This selection was implemented over histogram and morphological features after image segmentation, wavelet transformation (DWT) and wavelet reconstruction followed by enhanced binarization sequentially [10]. The identification of Friction-Stir Weld (FSW) defects on metal surfaces was executed by Praveen et al. in 2021. This involved adopting a fusion of machine vision and classifier-based strategies in which discriminant analysis and nearest neighbor approaches were implemented, resulting in commendably high classification accuracy [11]. Deep learning models such as Single Shot Detector (SSD), Faster-CNN and You Only Look Once (YOLO) were utilized as part of an image-data assessment approach strategy by Lin et al. This was carried out in 2021 as part of a comprehensive combined image-data assessment approach strategy for metal surface defect detection [12]. In 2022, two computationally progressive approaches were presented for prospective applications along the lines of steel and casting defect inspection. Tian et al. proposed the DCC-CenterNet – a rapid detection method for steel surface defects executed over the GC10 DET steel sheet surface defect dataset [13]. The prediction and detection of surface defects on metal castings collected from steel and cast-iron industries was implemented through data driven models developed by Chen et al. [14]. Evidently, machine and deep learning algorithms are indeed prospective implementations well suited for handling numerical data with high accuracy of results for applications that aim at solving diverse challenges [15]. A novel approach encompassing an aggregation network with contextual awareness (CANet) for the detection of surface defects in general was proposed by Wan et al., in 2023 [16].

The application of vision transformers in the detection of metal defects at large and steel surface defects in particular has been gaining attention gradually in recent years. Vision transformers, also known as ViTs, are deep learning models that apply transformer architectures to process visual data. Traditionally, CNNs have been widely used for computer vision tasks, including defect detection in steel surfaces as mentioned above. However, vision transformers offer a more promising alternative by leveraging the self-attention mechanism present in transformers, which allows them to capture global relationships within an image. In the context of steel surface defect detection, vision transformers have shown great potential. They can effectively learn representations from images, enabling the detection of subtle defects and complex patterns on steel surfaces. The self-attention mechanism in vision transformers enables the model to focus on relevant regions of the image, facilitating the identification of defects and distinguishing them from normal regions. The implementation of vision transformers for steel surface defect detection offers several advantages. More specifically, in a Vision Transformer (ViT), A self-attention mechanism is implemented in ViT by dividing an image in patches followed by their embedding into vectors. These embeddings are passed through transformer layers where the multi-head self-attention mechanism computes relationships between all patches by creating Query, Key, and Value matrices. The attention scores, derived from the dot product of Query and Key, determine how much each patch should focus on others. The weighted sum of Values gives a self-attended representation, which is then processed through a feed-forward network. Vision transformers can capture global context information in an image allowing for better understanding of complex defect patterns. Additionally, ViTs can generalize well to unseen defects and adapt to different lighting conditions and viewpoints. The ability of ViT to learn from large-scale datasets also facilitates avenues for transfer learning in which pre-trained models can be fine-tuned on smaller, domain-specific datasets to improve performance. The potential benefits of vision transformers are realized in multiple fields including the biomedical domain for classification of brain tumour [17], retinopathy [18], femur fracture classification [19], COVID-19 screening using radiography [20] and even skin cancer classification via medical vision transformers [21].

Deploying vision transformers in industrial visual inspection domains has been proved feasible by means of a comparative overview with CNN networks performed by Hütten et al. in 2022. This study was suggestive of the capability of ViTs to not only exhibit equivalent performance as that of CNN networks with sparse data but also surpass them in progressively complicated classification tasks [22]. A versatile vision transformer (‘crossformer’) based on cross scale attention was proposed by Wang et al. that aims at compensating for the general drawback of compromising on small scale features of embeddings to lower the resource requirements of self-attention modules. This was actuated through the proposal of a self-bias mechanism to make the popular relative position bias apply to variable sized images as well [23]. In 2022, a culmination of sequential advancements in the adoption of the transformer architecture for use in industry-grade fault diagnosis presented promising paths for real-time application. A Siamese vision transformer for bearing fault diagnosis was developed and examined by He et al. [24]. This approach involved a combination of the Siamese network and vision transformer deployed over the Case Western Reserve University and Paderborn dataset, achieving higher mean classification than Siamese CNNs, Hierarchical Convolutional Autoencoder (HCAE) and Wide Deep Convolutional Neural Networks (WDCNNs). MSFT-YOLO – an improved YOLOv5 model based on transformer structure for steel surface defect detection rendered commendable precision of classification upon examination by Guo et al. [27]. CAS-VSWIN transformer – a Variant-SWIN transformer for surface defect detection was an improved transformer design explored by Guo et al. which strengthened feature transfer between windows and made the framework more capable of serving as a backbone for efficient surface defect detection in industrial environments [28]. Zhang et al. proposed a novel approach modelling rail sample backgrounds in view of paucity of rail defect information in public repositories, rendering an effective inspection model via a memory linked metric learning of anomalies [29]. A comprehensive review of the performance of contemporary machine learning models for the purposes of steel surface defect detection was also undertaken comprehensively by Wen et al., in 2022, bringing out the importance of developing diagnostic solutions for critical material inspection [30].

Lu et al. proposed a vision transformer approach for the detection of defects on rails over rail defect B-Scan image data rendering just over 90 % model accuracy [31]. Preliminary experimental quantifications

The Keras API from the TensorFlow library in Python was utilized to develop the proposed vision transformer model, which was executed on the Google COLAB cloud computing platform using the integrated COLAB cloud graphical processing unit (virtual-GPU). The dataset used for training and testing the model consisted of images depicting various types of surface defects found in hot-rolled steel strips, sourced from the NEU-DET open-source repository. This dataset contains a total of 1800 grayscale images, with 300 samples available for each of the six surface defect categories. To divide the images for training and testing purposes, the 300 sample images within each defect class were further split in a ratio of 0.8:0.2. Thus, 80 % (240 images) were assigned for training, while the remaining 20 % (60 images) were allocated for testing in each class. The proposed Vision Transform model for the detection of defects on hot-rolled steel was trained with data from the ground up.

Experimental procedure

Images from different classes (pitted surface, scratches, rolled-in, patches, inclusion and crazing) are converted to RGB format and resized to a fixed size of 32 × 32 pixels. The data is thereafter split into training and testing sets following which hyperparameters such as learning rate, weight decay, batch size, number of epochs, image size, patch size, projection dimension, number of heads, transformer units, and MLP head units are defined. This is followed by data augmentation and creation of multilayer perceptron (MLP) layers with dropout regularization. Multiple layers of the transformer block are created, which consist of layer normalization, multi-head attention, skip connections, and MLP layers. The final encoded representation is flattened and passed through MLP layers for classification. The model outputs logits, which are passed through a SoftMax activation during training and evaluation. The AdamW optimizer from the TensorFlow Addons library is used, which applies weight decay during optimization. The model is compiled with the sparse categorical cross-entropy loss function and evaluation metrics, such as accuracy and top-5 accuracy. Training is performed using the ′fit′ method, with the training and validation data, batch size, number of epochs, and a checkpoint callback to save the best model weights based on validation accuracy. After training, the model weights with the best validation accuracy are loaded. The model is evaluated on the test set using the ′evaluate′ method, and the accuracy and top-5 accuracy are printed. The predicted labels (′pr′) and true labels (′y_test′) are used to compute the confusion matrix which provides a visual representation of the model performance of classification.

Architecture of vision transformers (ViTs)

Vision transformers (ViTs) have emerged as a notable category of deep learning models in the field of computer vision challenging the dominance of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) in image classification tasks. Rather than relying on CNNs, ViTs propose a different approach inspired by self-attention mechanisms commonly used in natural language processing. Dosovitskiy et al. in 2020 introduced ViT and demonstrated its effectiveness in image classification [49]. The core of a ViT lies in the transformer architecture originally designed for natural language processing which consists of an encoder-decoder framework with self-attention mechanisms. Self-attention enables the model to weigh the importance of different input elements when generating representations. In ViTs, the key component is the transformer encoder. To apply transformers to images, the first step is to convert the two-dimensional spatial input into a one-dimensional sequence of tokens through tokenization. Each token represents a patch of the input image, and the tokens are reshaped into a sequence. To encode spatial information, positional embeddings are added. Additionally, a linear projection is applied to each patch independently to convert them into meaningful representations known as "patch embeddings".

Fig. 4 represents the patch distribution of a sample input steel surface defect image. Positional encodings play a vital role in incorporating the spatial layout information of the image into the tokenized sequence. These capture the relative and absolute positions of the tokens using functions such as sine and cosine with different frequencies. The tokenized sequence is processed by the transformer encoder, which repeatedly applies self-attention and feed-forward neural networks. Each transformer layer consists of a multi-head self-attention mechanism and a position-wise feed-forward network. The self-attention mechanism computes attention weights between each token and all other tokens in the sequence, allowing the model to capture dependencies and relationships among different patches. The attention weights are determined through a weighted sum of three linear projections: query, key, and value. These projections enable the model to compare the similarity between tokens and attend to relevant information, influencing the final output. ViTs present an alternative approach to image classification by leveraging self-attention mechanisms borrowed from natural language processing. They showcase their effectiveness through the encoder-decoder framework of the transformer architecture and the tokenization process. The self-attention mechanism plays a crucial role in ViTs by generating an attention matrix that represents the similarity scores between each pair of tokens. These scores are calculated by taking the dot product between the query and key vectors and scaling the result by the square root of the feature dimension.

Bifurcation of sample input image into smaller patches for model input.

The attention scores undergo a SoftMax operation to normalize them into a probability distribution, ensuring that the weights sum up to 1. The values are then multiplied by the attention weights and summed up to obtain the weighted sum. To enable smooth gradient flow during training, residual connections are introduced around each sub-layer (attention and feed-forward networks) within the transformer encoder. Layer normalization is applied to normalize the intermediate representations. The feed-forward networks in the Transformer Encoder consist of two linear transformations with a non-linear activation function, typically ReLU, in between. These networks introduce a non-linear transformation to the outputs of the self-attention mechanism, enhancing the expressive power of the model. At the top of the transformer encoder, a classification head is typically added to produce the final class predictions. It consists of a linear layer followed by a SoftMax function to compute the class probabilities. ViTs are typically trained using supervised learning with a large, labeled dataset. The model is trained to minimize a suitable loss function such as cross-entropy loss. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) or its variants are commonly used for optimization. To effectively train ViTs, a two-step process is often followed. In the pre-training phase, models are trained on large-scale datasets using self-supervised learning or unsupervised tasks such as image inpainting or colorization. In the fine-tuning phase, the pre-trained models are further fine-tuned on specific downstream tasks using labeled data.

In simpler terms, vision transformers are a powerful alternative to Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for image classification. They adapt the transformer architecture from natural language processing to process images. ViTs start by breaking the input image into patches and converting them into a sequence of tokens. Positional embeddings capture spatial information, and a transformer encoder processes the token sequence. This encoder employs self-attention to analyze relationships between tokens, allowing the model to understand image content. Residual connections and normalization ensure smooth training. Feed-forward networks add non-linearity to enhance model capabilities. A classification head produces final predictions. ViTs are trained through supervised learning, minimizing a loss function with optimization methods like SGD. SGD is used to train a vision transformer because it efficiently handles the large number of parameters in these models and helps in generalization by introducing noise through small random batches during updates. This noise helps the model avoid overfitting and find better minima, making SGD particularly effective for training large-scale models like ViTs. Training involves pre-training on large datasets and fine-tuning on specific tasks with labeled data. ViT offers the following advantages over traditional deep learning and other CNN-based approaches which has motivated its adoption in the present body of work.

(i)Increased Global Context: The self-attention mechanism allows ViTs to capture long-range dependencies in images, enabling them to understand global context better.

Increased Global Context: The self-attention mechanism allows ViTs to capture long-range dependencies in images, enabling them to understand global context better.

(ii)Flexibility: ViTs can process images of arbitrary sizes since they operate on tokenized sequences. This flexibility eliminates the need for fixed input sizes and allows handling both large and small images.

Flexibility: ViTs can process images of arbitrary sizes since they operate on tokenized sequences. This flexibility eliminates the need for fixed input sizes and allows handling both large and small images.

(iii)Translation Invariance: Unlike CNNs, ViTs do not rely on local convolutional filters, which makes them inherently translation-invariant and eliminates the need for pooling operations.

(iv)Cross-Modality Application: The tokenization process allows ViTs to handle various modalities, such as combining images and text, by treating them as different input channels.

The implementation of vision transformers for steel surface defect detection typically involves several steps. First, a dataset of labeled images containing defective steel surfaces is collected. This dataset serves as the training data for the vision transformer model. The images are preprocessed, which may include resizing, normalization, and augmentation techniques to improve the model generalization capabilities. Next, the vision transformer model is constructed using transformer layers. The input images are divided into patches, which are then linearly embedded to generate a sequence of embeddings. These embeddings, along with positional encodings, are fed into the transformer layers, where the self-attention mechanism allows the model to capture global relationships between the patches. During the training phase, the model is optimized using appropriate loss functions, such as binary cross-entropy or focal loss, to differentiate between normal and defective regions. The model learns to classify the images and identify the presence of defects accurately. Training is typically performed on high-performance hardware, such as graphical processing units (GPUs) or tensor processing units (TPUs), to handle the computational complexity of vision transformers. Once the vision transformer model is trained, it can be deployed for defect detection in real-world scenarios. New steel surface images can be fed into the model, and it will analyze the images to identify and localize any defects present. The model predictions can be further post-processed to enhance the interpretability of the results, such as generating bounding boxes around detected defects or generating defect severity scores. Vision transformers have emerged as a promising alternative to CNNs for image classification tasks, leveraging the power of self-attention mechanisms to capture long-range dependencies and global context in images. Principally, vision transformers provide a promising approach for the detection of steel surface defects. Their ability to capture global relationships in images and learn from large-scale datasets makes them well-suited for this task. With further research and advancements, vision transformers do have the potential to become a valuable tool in ensuring the quality and safety of steel materials.

Critical hyperparameters in deep learning networks

In deep learning, hyperparameters are parameters that are set prior to the training of a model that can impact the model learning process. These parameters are not learned from the data but are instead specified by the user or model architect. Hyperparameters define the overall architecture and behavior of the deep learning model including its capacity, complexity, and generalization ability. Some common hyperparameters in deep learning include learning rate, number of layers, number of neurons per layer, activation functions, regularization parameters, batch size and dropout rate. The hyperparameters which are varied for the purpose of optimizing the classification performance of the proposed ViT model is highlighted herewith.

Learning rate

The learning rate (LR) is a scalar value that determines the step size at which the model parameters are updated during the training process. It controls the magnitude of the parameter updates based on the gradients computed using backpropagation. The learning rate is a critical hyperparameter, as it influences the convergence and stability of the training algorithm. A higher learning rate allows for faster convergence but can cause overshooting, leading to unstable training. On the other hand, while a lower value of learning rate ensures stability and avoids overshooting, it may result in slow convergence or trapping at suboptimal solutions.

Batch size

Batch size refers to the number of training examples processed in parallel during a single forward and backward pass of the neural network. In deep learning, training data is divided into mini-batches, and the model parameters are updated based on the average gradients calculated over each batch. The choice of batch size affects the training dynamics and computational efficiency. Smaller batch sizes enable more frequent parameter updates, leading to potentially smoother convergence trajectories. However, smaller batches can introduce noise due to the high variance in the gradient estimates, which may hinder convergence. Larger batch sizes offer more accurate gradient estimates and take better advantage of parallel processing which can speed up training. However, larger batches may converge to flatter minima or require more memory and computational resources.

Patch size

Patch size is primarily used in convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for image processing tasks. It represents the dimensions of the receptive field, which is the local region of the input image that each convolutional kernel examines. The patch size determines the spatial extent of information considered by the network. Larger patch sizes capture more global features and provide a broader context for understanding the input. However, they may result in reduced spatial resolution and the loss of fine-grained details. Smaller patch sizes capture more local and detailed information however may struggle to capture global relationships and large-scale structures. The choice of patch size depends on the characteristics of the dataset and the complexity of the underlying patterns.

Number of heads

The number of heads is a hyperparameter commonly associated with attention mechanisms, notably in the transformer architecture. Attention mechanisms allow the model to focus on different parts of the input sequence when making predictions. The number of heads determines how many parallel attention mechanisms or "viewpoints" the model employs to attend to different aspects of the input. Increasing the number of heads provides more capacity for the model to capture diverse and fine-grained relationships within the data. Each head can learn different patterns or dependencies, enhancing the model ability to capture complex interactions. However, a higher number of heads also increases the computational complexity of the model and may require more memory and training data to generalize effectively.

Number of MLP layers

Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) layers are fully connected layers that transform the input data using non-linear activation functions. The number of MLP layers determines the depth of the neural network. Deeper networks can learn more complex representations by capturing higher-order interactions and hierarchies of features. Each additional layer enables the network to model increasingly abstract and intricate patterns in the data. However, deep models are more prone to overfitting, especially when the training data is limited or when regularization techniques are not appropriately employed. Training deep models can also be computationally expensive due to the increased number of parameters and the need for more extensive computational resources.

Optimizing these hyperparameters is crucial for achieving good performance in deep learning models. Typically, finding the optimal values involves a process of hyperparameter tuning, where different combinations of values are evaluated through techniques like grid search, random search, or more advanced methods such as Bayesian Optimization. Additionally, techniques like learning rate schedules, where the learning rate is adjusted during training, and techniques like batch normalization and regularization can help stabilize and improve the training process. The optimal choice of hyperparameters depends on the specific problem, dataset size, model architecture, and computational resources available.

Conclusion

This experimental study aimed to address the detection of visual faults on steel surfaces, including crazing, inclusion, rolled in, pitted surface, scratches, and patches, by developing and analysing a vision transformer (ViT) model. The ViT model leverages the transformer architecture for effective classification. The selection of the NEU-DET image database for training and testing the model ensured a diverse range of steel surface defect samples. During the experiment, a dataset comprising 1800 greyscale image samples was utilized. The proposed ViT model exhibited exceptional performance, achieving an impressive overall classification accuracy of 96.39 %. This accuracy surpassed the performance of various pre-trained deep learning models and machine learning classifiers, including conventional convolutional neural networks, and existing vision transformer models tailored for the current application under discussion. These results suggest that the ViT model is highly effective in accurately identifying and classifying visual faults on steel surfaces. Notably, the proposed ViT model demonstrated superior performance in terms of accuracy, which indicates its potential for real-world applications. Compared to other models, the ViT model exhibited higher efficiency and required minimal hardware resources. These advantages make it an appealing and cost-effective solution for real-time deployment in industrial settings where real-time diagnosis of steel surface defects is crucial. Furthermore, the findings of this study present an opportunity for integrating the proposed ViT model into existing inspection systems, providing enhanced quality assurance capabilities. This integration could lead to improved detection and timely identification of visual defects in metals used for commercial purposes. The modular nature of the proposed model facilitates its seamless incorporation into existing systems with appropriate graphical processing sequence and displays, thereby contributing to more comprehensive, user-friendly and efficient quality control processes. In conclusion, the proposed vision transformer (ViT) model presents a highly effective solution for the real-time diagnosis of visual faults on hot-rolled steel surfaces. Its commendable performance, efficiency, and cost-effectiveness make it suitable for implementation in industrial settings. By integrating the ViT model into existing inspection systems, companies can enhance their quality assurance processes and enable timely detection of visual defects in metals. The future scope of the proposed model would include the exploration of multiple roadmaps for possibly attaining higher classification performance over a much more diverse, custom dataset for the current application. This would also include provision of functionalities that can extend classification operation of the ViT model over a wider range of defect types under demanding equipment operating conditions upon deployment on the field through use of modular and easy-to-use fault diagnosis systems.
