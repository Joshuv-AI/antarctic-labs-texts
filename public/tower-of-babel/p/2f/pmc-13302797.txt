A modified Sequential Intercept Model for people living with dementia to prevent interactions with the criminal legal system
Authors: Lillian M Morgado, Ana M Tyler, Krystal L Karunungan, Victoria Helmly, Jalayne J Arias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesPeople living with dementia (PLWD) may experience symptoms and behaviors that increase the risk of contact with the criminal legal system as arrestees. This manuscript aims to evaluate the Sequential Intercept Model (SIM) as a mechanism of reducing exposure to the criminal system for people living with dementia.Research Design and MethodsA secondary analysis of two complementary semi-structured interview studies of legal professionals (study 1) and caregivers of persons affected by frontotemporal dementia (study 2). Both studies collected insights and experiences with dementia-related behavior increasing the risk of contact or as the focus of a criminal charge, arrest, or incarceration. We applied the Framework Method to code, chart, and identify overlapping themes between studies. We compared overlapping themes to the Sequential Intercept Model (SIM)< >for applicability to people living with dementia.ResultsThirteen caregivers and fifteen legal practitioners completed interviews across two studies. We observed (1) caregiver interventions and use of pre-crisis resources as key to people living with dementia avoiding criminal involvement< >(2) caregivers may not perceive symptoms and behaviors as posing a criminal risk. The SIM lacks pre-crisis resources to reduce risks for criminal-system interaction for people living with dementia. We propose an adapted model, the Modified Sequential Intercept Model for People Living with Dementia (SIM-D), to integrate “pre-crisis community resources” and “crisis” stages.Discussion and ImplicationsWe propose SIM-D as a tool to bridge research on people living with dementia in the community and the justice system. This research will be central to advancing policy and support resources that can mitigate negative consequences of people living with dementia’s exposure to the criminal system.

Dementia is characterized by diverse cognitive and behavioral symptoms caused by multiple pathologies (e.g., Alzheimer’s disease, Frontotemporal Dementia, vascular dementia) (Hugo & Ganguli, 2014). These symptoms can affect decision making, social behaviors, and impulse control (Liljegren et al., 2015) in ways that could be perceived as criminal. This puts people living with dementia (people living with dementia) at risk for criminal justice involvement, defined in this paper as contact with the criminal legal system through arrest, arraignment, or incarceration. This can happen when dementia-related behaviors lead to socially unacceptable behavior, including unwanted touching or theft. Research on older adults in the criminal justice system has shown that people living with dementia are present and undercounted in the incarcerated population (Baillargeon et al., 2023; Liljegren et al., 2015) and that the criminal justice system is ill-equipped to assist people living with dementia (American Bar Association Commission on Law and Aging, 2022; Arias et al., 2023a). Additionally, 2.5% of incarcerated older adults are diagnosed with dementia prior to their incarceration(Kuffel et al., 2022). People affected by frontotemporal dementia (FTD) are at an increased risk of symptoms that manifest as criminal action (14%) compared to those with other types of dementia (8.5%) (Liljegren et al., 2015). While this risk affects a minority of people living with dementia, criminal justice involvement could lead to negative outcomes (i.e., aggravation of symptoms, loss of housing, isolation). Preventative efforts to reduce the risk of criminal justice involvement for people living with dementia and mitigate individual and societal burdens are ongoing.

Prior work at the intersection of dementia and the criminal legal system has traditionally focused on protecting people living with dementia from becoming victims of crime (e.g., elder abuse (Touza & Prado, 2019), financial exploitation (Yu et al., 2022)). While this is still a critical issue to be resolved, it fails to recognize dementia as a risk factor for criminal justice involvement (Arias et al., 2023b). This necessitates research that expands on existing evidence indicating rates of criminal behavior among people living with dementia (Liljegren et al., 2019) and the prevalence of dementia and other cognitive impairments among older adults who have contact with the criminal legal system (Kuffel et al., 2022). Additionally, prior work has emphasized that the criminal legal system lacks the capacity to manage or support the unique needs of people living with dementia who become justice-involved (Arias et al., 2023b). This work, while important, does not address the factors that could lead to or increase the risk for contact with the criminal legal system or address how people living with dementia navigate the criminal legal system.

In analogous populations of vulnerable people, including juveniles (Heilbrun et al., 2017), people with psychiatric illnesses, and people with autism (Shea et al., 2021), the Sequential Intercept Model (SIM)(The Sequential Intercept Model (SIM), 2020) has provided a model for identifying and implementing solutions (Comartin et al., 2021; Heilbrun et al., 2017). The SIM provides a framework to decriminalize and integrate community-based services for vulnerable populations in the criminal justice system (Heilbrun et al., 2017), a version of which can be seen in Figure 1. It tracks individuals through six intercepts, from Intercept 0 (Community Services) to 5 (Community Corrections) to re-entry into the greater community. Each intercept of the model highlights alternatives to the traditional justice system that reduce criminalization of vulnerable populations. Intercept 0 (Community Services) was added to the original five intercept model in 2017 (Abreu et al., 2017) in recognition of the need to address crises before justice involvement. In this context “community services” refer to services available during a crisis, such as non-emergency crisis hotlines like 988 and 911. The current SIM does not account for pre-crisis factors, though other researchers have suggested adding this (Bonfine et al., 2024).

Sequential Intercept Model (SIM).

To date, there is a gap in the research and scholarship on the SIM’s application to people living with dementia. While the existing SIM may provide insights for people living with dementia with criminal justice involvement, there are critical differences between dementia and these other vulnerable populations. For example, people with psychiatric illnesses can regain competency and return to the community with justice system supports like diversion courts and mental health rehabilitation programs. While some medications can slow the decline of people living with dementia, the disease process cannot be reversed, and it is inevitably fatal.

An adapted version of the SIM could allow researchers and policy makers a method to track and understand the risk of criminal justice involvement for people living with dementia. A preliminary step to adapt the model is understanding the actions and experiences of people living with dementia who experience arrest, detainment, or other contact with the criminal system. Here we report on two complimentary qualitative research studies and propose an adapted version of the SIM to better measure people living with dementia’s risk of contact with the criminal legal system. The investigators focus primarily on people affected by FTD given the increased risk for potentially criminal behavior among this population. For the purpose of this article, we describe “potentially criminal behavior” as behavior that could be perceived as criminal such as reckless driving, harassment, assault, sexual assault, or manslaughter.

Methods

This article describes secondary analysis of two complementary qualitative studies using semi-structured interviews of legal professionals and caregivers of persons affected by FTD. We analyzed the semi-structured interviews using framework analysis to identify themes that emerged across both studies, while considering risks for people living with dementia to encounter the criminal legal system. We compared these findings with the SIM, evaluating alignment and divergence. This provided the foundation to propose a modified version of the model. The “Caregiver Study” used semi-structured interviews of caregivers of people affected by FTD to identify financial, social, and legal consequences of an FTD diagnosis. The Legal Study used semi-structured interviews of legal professionals to understand their experience with people living with dementia who have contact with the criminal legal system. General findings from these studies were published elsewhere (Arias et al., 2023b). Both studies were approved by the University of California San Francisco (UCSF) Institutional Review Board.

Study samples

Investigators used purposeful sampling in both studies to identify participants and ceased recruitment upon meeting data saturation (i.e., no additional themes emerged during interviews) (Marshall, 1996). The two studies recruited separate samples that were not connected. Accordingly, participants from each study were recruited separately and do not represent dyads. As a result, legal professionals and caregivers who enrolled in the study did not describe experiences with the same people living with dementia.

Caregiver study

Investigators recruited caregivers of participants enrolled in a larger parent study that aims to understand the clinical, genetic, imaging, emotional and diagnostic features of FTD. The investigators collaborated with the research team of the parent study to identify potential eligible participants.

Legal study

Investigators recruited legal professionals with criminal justice experience, primarily lawyers, using social media posts (e.g., Facebook and Twitter), identifying contacts within investigators’ professional networks, and implementing snowball sampling.

Data collection

The Caregiver Study and the Legal Study used semi-structured interview guides to collect participants’ insights, perspectives, and experiences with people living with dementia and potentially criminal behavior. Study teams developed the interview guides through a review of the literature and then sought feedback on the guide from field experts. In the Legal Study, investigators also completed two preliminary unstructured interviews with field experts to gauge a preliminary understanding of potential issues and topics that may emerge if people living with dementia have contact with the criminal legal system. Investigators piloted interview guides prior to beginning data collection to assure that questions and prompts elicited rigorous narrative responses that addressed the research question. Legal Study interviews elicited legal practitioners’ experience with and related to people living with dementia who have committed crimes (e.g., “From your experience, if an individual has dementia when he/she commits a crime—how will that be managed within the criminal justice system?”) and recommendations to improve the criminal system for people living with dementia (e.g., “What needs do you have as [legal expert] to better manage individuals with dementia who commit a crime or those who develop dementia while incarcerated?”). Caregiver Study interviews collected participants’ experiences seeking a diagnosis for someone with FTD and the financial, social, and legal consequences of the illness and diagnosis. Through this, interviewers asked about potentially difficult behavior (e.g., “Has your [people living with dementia] ever demonstrated behaviors that have raised specific concerns for you, a family member or a friend?”) and specific concerns regarding criminal liability (e.g., “Have any behaviors raised concerns for civil or criminal liability?”).

Interviews were conducted by study team members (Caregiver Study, J.J.A., K.L.K.; Legal Study, J.J.A., A.M.T.). All interviews were recorded and transcribed for analysis.

Analysis

Two team members (J.J.A., L.M.M.) used the Framework Method (Gale et al., 2013) to individually analyze each set of interviews. This included an inductive review of a subset of transcripts (3–5) to identify themes that were discussed by multiple interview subjects. We used these themes to create a preliminary codebook, which we tested on a different subset of transcripts for applicability. We created a final codebook after discussing and resolving divergences of opinion on coding and the relevancy of specific codes. We used the results of our discussion to create a final codebook to analyze all transcripts. All transcripts were coded using the final codebook to ensure consistency. We resolved questions and disagreements about how specific quotes should be coded through regular discussion to reach consensus.

Once all transcripts were coded, we isolated the coded quotes and organized them into charts based on theme. We then identified areas of consensus and differences in perspectives and experiences reported by participants within each study (lawyers, caregivers) and used these results to evaluate the appropriateness of the SIM for this population.

Model adaption

After coding the two sets of qualitative interviews, we ordered specific themes such as crises (coded as potentially criminal behavior), interventions (coded as reactions to potentially criminal behavior), and resources or lack thereof (all other themes) reported by caregivers and lawyers. We noted which themes mapped onto intercepts of the existing SIM (Comartin et al., 2021; Heilbrun et al., 2017; Shea et al., 2021) and which themes fell outside of the existing SIM. Pre-crisis and immediate post-crisis interventions are described in this paper, while direct interactions with the justice system are described in more detail in a previous paper (Arias et al., 2023b).

Relevant events and resources that occurred before a specific crisis and before Intercept 0 of the SIM were included in this analysis. Results of this analysis are seen in Figure 2. From this mapping, we generated SIM-D, a modified Sequential Intercept Model tailored to the needs of people living with dementia.

Results

Thirteen caregivers and fifteen legal practitioners completed interviews (Tables 1 and 2). Caregivers were mostly white (n = 12), spouses of the people living with dementia (n = 9), and mostly women (n = 9). This is consistent with demographics in the parent study. Legal practitioners were mostly white (n = 12), women (n = 9), and had a range of 5 to 33 years of legal practice. Legal practitioners practiced law in different geographic regions representing 9 different states. Findings from each study are reported elsewhere (Arias et al., 2023b). The investigators identified overlapping complimentary themes of activities of daily living (feeding, dressing, bathing, toileting, and transferring), instrumental activities of daily living, barriers to adequate care, criminal behaviors, financial resources, future planning, placement, public payer systems, and reactions to potentially criminal behaviors. These data supported the process for modifying SIM for people living with dementia. Table 3 provides a detailed overview of these themes.

Legal and caregiver-interviewees reported that caregivers were the primary driver for reducing the risk of criminal-justice involvement for people living with dementia. Caregivers intervened before, during, and after crises to de-escalate situations. For example, interviewees reported that caregivers played an important role in reducing environmental triggers or risks. Pre-crisis resources, including formal caregiving services and pre-crisis community-based resources, may mitigate people living with dementia’s risks for contact with the criminal legal system

Pre-crisis resources are those provided by family members, caregivers, and other members of the community when a people living with dementia is not actively in crisis. For the purposes of this article, we have split these into two classes—formal caregiving services and pre-crisis community-based resources. We define formal caregiving services as paid services to assist people living with dementia with activities of daily living (ADL; bathing, dressing, toileting, eating, transferring, and supervision) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL: meal preparation, transportation, housekeeping, etc.) We define pre-crisis community-based resources as services within the community that are not specific to providing care for people living with dementia but do support them. Interviewees report that pre-crisis resources, including formal care resources and pre-crisis community-based resources, may reduce the risk of contact with the criminal legal system by avoiding crisis points.I think, obviously, all of access to healthcare and elder care in this country would go—starting early before things come to a crisis point would obviously make great sense, but we’ll see, especially with enormously huge group of people aging, and that’s just going to keep happening, right?—Attorney

I think, obviously, all of access to healthcare and elder care in this country would go—starting early before things come to a crisis point would obviously make great sense, but we’ll see, especially with enormously huge group of people aging, and that’s just going to keep happening, right?—Attorney

Legal interviewees explicitly linked lack of access to pre-crisis resources with justice-involvement for people living with dementia. Legal professionals and caregivers’ perspectives diverged on what constitutes “potentially criminal behavior”

Both cohorts attributed potentially criminal behavior as defined in this study (traffic violations, aggression, theft, unwanted sexual advances, etc.) to the people living with dementia’s disease process rather than criminal intent. They differed, however, on what behavior they considered to be “criminal behavior.” Legal experts explicitly described and identified a wide range of behaviors in a criminal context, including violence against caregivers, driving violations, sexual battery, violence against strangers, and theft. Even when discussing similar actions by people living with dementia, caregiver-interviewees were less likely to identify the given behavior as “criminal behavior” even when explicitly asked. All caregivers were asked whether their care recipient had done anything that could be considered criminal. Eight of the interviewees described behavioral symptoms when reporting on presentation of the individual’s illness that could be considered criminal behaviors (including unsafe driving behavior, aggression towards a caregiver, aggression towards a stranger, theft, stalking, public nudity, and sexual harassment). Only 4 of these same caregivers identified the same behavior when explicitly asked about whether they were concerned about potential criminal behaviors.

Interviewees were also concerned about different types of potentially criminal behavior. Model

The SIM-D is based on the understanding that preventing contact with the justice system is one method of reducing negative outcomes for people living with dementia who would otherwise become justice-involved (Arias et al., 2023a). SIM-D allows researchers to analyze dementia care resources through a crime-prevention lens and track people living with dementia’s transition from living in the community through the justice system. We synthesized interview results on these themes and then mapped reported experiences onto the SIM, identified gaps in the model for this population, found intervention points that were not included in the original SIM, and generated the SIM-D.

The SIM-D adds two new levels to the existing SIM and renames one existing intercept (see Figure 3: Sequential Intercept Model for People Living with Dementia). To align with the goal of keeping people living with dementia from encountering the justice system in the first place, we added Intercept −1: Pre-Crisis Community Services and a Crisis Stage. Pre-crisis community services include formal care services, informal care services, and caregivers’ knowledge and training. The inclusion of intercept −1 and the crisis stage account for more specific analysis of resources that avoid or avert specific crises that people living with dementia experience. Intercept 0 has been renamed from “Community Services” to “Post-Crisis Community Services.” This clearly distinguishes the difference between community resources that avoid a crisis entirely (Intercept −1) and those that are engaged immediately after a crisis (Intercept 0).

Modified Sequential Intercept Model for people living with dementia (SIM-D).

This model creates a framework to identify and develop pre-crisis resources that could mitigate risks for contact with the criminal legal system for people living with dementia. Additionally, it provides a structure to support and guide research and decision makers when evaluating whether interventions aimed at mitigating this risk are effective. It allows researchers and policy makers to evaluate the impact of resources that are not traditionally framed as “crime prevention” resources, such as access to public transit’s influence on the amount of traffic violations by people living with dementia or respite care’s impact on rates of violence against caregivers.

Discussion

This study identified overlapping themes across interviews with legal professionals and caregivers that supported adaptation of the SIM to account for unique needs of people living with dementia. Overlapping themes include caregivers’ roles in intervening pre- and post-crisis that could lead to criminal system involvement and the importance of pre-crisis resources. Additionally, this study revealed a dissonance between caregivers’ and legal practitioners’ perception of potentially criminal behavior and the risk it entails. This could be explained partially by their relationship with the people living with dementia who is exhibiting potentially criminal behavior and the interview subjects “own personal expertise.” Legal professionals were asked specifically about their experience with people living with dementia in the criminal legal system. Therefore, these interview subjects were discussing individuals whose behavior was already been identified as potentially criminal. Because of their legal expertise, they better understand what behavior is considered “potentially criminal” than the average person and the consequences of the behavior.

It’s difficult to determine the reason why caregivers specifically did not perceive sexual behaviors and violence towards themselves as potentially criminal, as the original studies did not explicitly ask about this. For the purposes of SIM-D, caregivers may not properly recognize the level of risk these behaviors pose, leading to crises that could be avoided by using respite care or other resources.

In these interviews, caregivers were recognized as the main component keeping people living with dementia from justice involvement. In the absence of other resources, caregivers prevent, de-escalate, and minimize the effects of crises for people living with dementia in their care. Caregivers’ ability to provide for the people living with dementia in their care is constrained by their personal resources. When people living with dementias’ needs exceed what their caregivers can provide, a crisis may occur. Expanding access to pre-crisis resources like transportation support, respite care, and day care for people living with dementia could be vital to keeping them from reaching crisis points and triggering justice involvement. Additional research should be done to quantify the level of access people living with dementia have to these resources and how effective they are at preventing crises that may lead to inappropriate justice involvement.

SIM-D provides a conceptual model to inform future research and policy decisions towards a structure that would assess for, anticipate, and mitigate risk for interaction with the criminal system. The justice system lacks appropriate resources to help people living with dementia in crisis, such as appropriate pre-trial housing, timely cognitive testing, ways to bypass restoring competency, appropriate plea deals, and adequate available social workers. Instead of relying on the justice system to resolve crises for people living with dementia who exhibit potentially criminal activity, the SIM-D shifts the focus to the pre-crisis, crisis, and post-crisis stages and how to resolve issues before they reach the criminal justice system.

There were some limitations to the studies used for this research. Interviewees for both studies were not representative of the demographics for their groups. All of the legal practitioners interviewed were white and female—in comparison, 78% of lawyers in the US identify as white and only 41% identify as female (ABA Center for Bar Leadership, 2025). Meanwhile, all but one of the interviewed caregivers identified as white, when only two-thirds of caregivers to people living with dementia in the United States are white (Alzheimer’s Association, 2025). Due to the small amount of interviewees in both groups and their limited diversity, these studies may have limited transferability to other population groups. Interviews for the caregiver study were conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have informed some responses. Because our analysis was secondary to the two original studies it was limited by the goals and questions of the original studies that they were part of. Additionally, the interview subjects were not dyads. The legal experts and caregivers interviewed were describing their experiences with different sets of people living with dementia, which limits our ability to compare their interpretations of behavior. Interviews with legal experts discussed issues that occurred when caregivers could not help people living with dementia avoid criminal legal involvement. We have described these in a separate paper (Arias et al., 2023b). None of the caregivers interviewed described situations in which they were unable to avoid the criminal-legal system for their people living with dementia. Another limitation of this manuscript was limited information on outcomes for people who do not have access to caregivers. Legal experts from the first study relied heavily on caregivers to identify people living with dementia and caregivers were the interview subjects for second study. It is possible that legal experts interacted with people living with dementia who did not have caregivers, but because they lacked caregivers the legal experts did not identify them as people living with dementia. Despite these limitations, our results provide a meaningful insight to generate an adapted version of the SIM aimed at mitigating risk for criminal system involvement among people living with dementia.

Conclusion

Investigators synthesized interviews from legal practitioners and caregivers from studies on criminal system interaction for people living with dementia. After synthesizing major themes for these interviews, investigators mapped interviewees’ experiences onto the existing SIM to develop SIM-D. SIM-D provides a new tool for researchers to bridge research on people living with dementia and mitigate the risk for interactions with justice, as well as a way for policymakers to understand the utility of non-traditional community resources as tools to mitigate justice involvement for people living with dementia.
