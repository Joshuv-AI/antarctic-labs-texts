Delayed discharges in older adults with mental illness and/or dementia in hospital: a scoping review
Authors: Stephanie Lucchese, Melissa Northwood, Carrie McAiney, Iwona A. Bielska, Shirin Vellani, Mary Buck, Jennifer Harris, Sharon Kaasalainen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDelayed discharge among older adults is rising in hospitals across many countries, placing increased strain on healthcare systems and negatively affecting patients’ emotional and physical well-being due to prolonged hospital stays and uncertainty. Despite this growing issue, limited research has explored the specific impact of delayed discharge on older adults living with a mental illness and/or dementia. This scoping review aims to examine what is currently known about older adults (aged 65 and over) with a diagnosis of dementia and/or mental illness who experience delayed discharge from hospital settings.MethodsThis scoping review followed the five-stage framework outlined by Arksey and O’Malley. Comprehensive literature searches were conducted in Medline, Embase, Emcare, PsycINFO, Web of Science, and CINAHL. Studies were included if they focused on hospitalized older adults (65+) with delayed discharge and a diagnosis of dementia and/or mental illness, including the perspectives of patients, family care partners and healthcare providers. Eleven articles and one grey literature report met the inclusion criteria. Data synthesis was guided by Grembowski’s Conceptual Model of Complexity.ResultsDelayed discharge is a prevalent problem in this population. Patient characteristics, including ages 79 years and older, diagnosis of dementia, cognitive impairment, high levels of care dependency and lack of continuity of care with a primary care physician in the community, were identified as key risk factors for delayed discharge. The most commonly reported cause of delay was the unavailability of appropriate discharge destinations, primarily long-term care homes or adequate support services in the home. Delayed discharge carries significant risks of adverse events for older adults and is distressing to care partners.ConclusionsUpstream approaches to service provisions, such as continuity of care in primary care settings likely helps to reduce delayed discharges. Care practices within the hospital setting, including early family and patient involvement in discharge planning, education and training in dementia and mental health care, and improved collaboration between inpatient and community services are important considerations to address delayed discharge. Further research is needed to address knowledge gaps and inform system-level interventions tailored to this population experiencing vulnerabilities during hospitalization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15105-w.

Introduction

Demographic shifts worldwide are leading to a growing population of older adults [1]. For example, in Canada, adults aged 65 and older are projected to comprise approximately 25% of the total population by 2030 [2]. Similarly, the global population aged 60 years and above is expected to grow from 1.1 billion in 2023 to 1.4 billion by 2030, with the most rapid growth in developing countries [3]. With an increase in age, older adults may experience complex interactions between mental illness, cognitive disorders and chronic conditions [4]. According to the Mental Health Commission of Canada, the prevalence of mental illness among older adults, 65 years and above, is between 17% and 30% and if subclinical anxiety, dementia-related symptoms and depression are factored in, the prevalence can be as high as 40% to 50% [5]. Despite the high prevalence of dementia and mental illness among older adults, the current structure and care processes of the health care system is not effectively meeting the needs of this population [6]. Community-dwelling older adults encounter significant barriers, including limited access to primary care, a lack of specialized services, and insufficient resources in both community and long-term care (LTC) settings [6, 7]. Additionally, the health system is under-resourced and often described as fragmented and difficult to navigate, with limited capacity to address the complex and multifaceted needs of older adults [6]. These challenges exemplify what Grembowski et al. (2014) defines as complexity, which arises from the misalignment between an individual’s health and social needs and the healthcare system’s capacity to effectively address those needs. As a result, the health system remains largely reactive rather than preventative, lacking the infrastructure required to deliver proactive, coordinated care, which can inadvertently contribute to increased hospitalizations [6]. Within this fragmented system, safe and timely discharge from hospital becomes increasingly difficult once acute care treatment is complete, often leading to delayed discharges and placing additional strain on hospital resources [8].

Delayed discharge is a global healthcare issue that highlights system inefficiencies to transition a person to the community and it is often due to lack of integration of upstream approaches to support timely discharges [8, 9]. While there is no universal definition of delayed discharge [10], delayed discharge is consistently defined in the literature when a patient has completed their treatment in hospital and no longer require the intensity of resources in a hospital setting, but remain hospitalized while awaiting transfer to an alternate destination, such as LTC, home with supports, or transitional care units, due to inadequate community resources or their previous residence no longer being suitable to support the level of care that they require [10–14]. In Canada, the clinical designation of Alternate Level of Care (ALC) was introduced to standardize the definition of a patient experiencing a delayed discharge [15]. Operationally ALC and delayed discharge refer to the same phenomenon; the ALC designation provides hospitals with a formal mechanism to track and report patients with a delayed discharge consistently, enabling that high-quality, consistent and accurate data are being captured on all patients with delayed discharge in acute and post-acute hospitals [15]. In other countries such as the United States, England, Scotland and the United Arab Emirates, the phenomenon of ALC is described simply as ‘delayed discharge’ [14, 16]. Based on data from Ontario, 80% of delayed discharges are attributed to older adults, ages 65 years and over [17] with greater risk for designation among those with multiple and interacting health and social conditions [17–21]. For the purposes of this review, the term delayed discharge will be used.

Delayed discharge has significant negative implications for patients, their care partners, and the health care system [8, 9, 17]. At the organizational level, rising rates of delayed discharges signal inefficiencies within the health system, resulting in a substantial financial burden due to the ineffective use of hospital resources and reduced access to timely care for other patients [22]. From the patient perspective, prolonged hospital stays can lead to adverse health outcomes, including functional decline, decreased quality of life, and deterioration in both physical and mental health [9, 22]. A scoping review by Everall et al. (2019), which focused on the experiences of patients and care partners across a range of populations, found that delayed discharges were associated with uncertainty about the illness and treatment, declining health status, and limited involvement in care planning and decision-making [20]. Similarly, a systematic review by Rojas-Garcia et al. (2018) found that delayed discharges contribute to patient complications such as infections, depression, and reduced quality of life. Delayed discharges also have significant implications for the health system, including treatment delays and the cancellation of scheduled procedures, due to the unavailability of beds in surgical or medical wards for patients in Emergency Departments or outpatient settings [23].

Several studies have examined delayed discharges across diverse patient populations to identify characteristics associated with delayed discharge. For instance, a retrospective cohort study by Bai et al. (2019) identified significant predictors of delayed discharge, including advanced age (80 years or older), female sex, a diagnosis of dementia, complications related to diabetes, and patients’ required support from either physiotherapy, occupational therapy or speech language pathology with their activities of daily living (ADLs) [24]. In a study focused on mental health inpatient settings across Ontario that included patients aged 18 years and older, 34% of older adults from the study sample had a delayed discharge [25]. Within the overall sample, schizophrenia, mood disorders and cognitive disorders were more prevalent in the group with delayed discharge compared to those who did not have a delayed discharge [25]. Another Ontario based study examining predictors of delayed discharge in mental health inpatient settings identified impairment on the instrumental ADL scale as one of the strongest predictors of having 30 or more delayed discharge days with odds increasing at each level of impairment [22]. Individuals with the highest levels of impairment were nearly four times more likely (OR = 3.8, 95% CI [2.79, 5.21]) to experience delayed discharge than those with no impairment. Other significant predictors included moderate to severe cognitive impairment (OR 1.6, 95% CI [1.29–2.02] and OR1.90, 95% CI [1.45–2.48]) and middle and older age (OR 2.0, 95% CI [1.62–2.49] and OR 2.99, 95% CI [2.24–3.73]), both of which were associated with progressively higher odds of prolonged delayed discharges [22]. While both studies provided valuable insights into delayed discharges in mental health settings, they did not offer detailed participant characteristics specific to older adults, limiting a deeper understanding of the factors contributing to delayed discharges in this population.

While the body of literature on delayed hospital discharges is expanding [8, 20, 23], a significant gap in the literature remains that specifically examines delayed discharges among older adults with a mental illness and/or dementia given that delayed discharge occurs more frequently in this population. To better understand health system challenges and inform strategies to more effectively support this population when requiring hospitalization, further research is warranted to examine the specific patient characteristics, the level of health and social supports required, and the systemic barriers contributing to prolonged hospital stays among older adults with mental illness and/or dementia. To our knowledge, no reviews have specifically focused on delayed discharges among older adults with mental illness and/or dementia, nor have they addressed the unique challenges and complexities associated with delayed discharges in this population. Therefore, the purpose of this scoping review was to provide a comprehensive overview of the existing research on delayed discharges in these specific patient population, identify gaps in the current literature, and offer insights to inform future policy, practice, and research. To capture the full scope of this topic, we included literature related to both mental illness and dementia and did not exclude studies based on predetermined definitions or diagnostic criteria, allowing for the inclusion of all relevant studies.

Methods

The methodological framework that informed this scoping review was outlined by Arksey and O’Malley (2005), which was further expanded by Levac et al. (2010) [26, 27]. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) was followed to guide the reporting of the scoping review [28]. Arksey and O’Malley’s framework includes six steps, with the last step (consultation with stakeholders) being optional. As such, the first five steps were applied in this scoping review.

Step 1: Identify research questions

The aim for the scoping review was to examine the literature on what is known about older adults with a mental illness and/or dementia experiencing delayed discharges from a hospital setting. This review sought to answer the following four research questions:

Step 2: Identify relevant literature

This scoping review included both scientific and grey literature to obtain comprehensive evidence on what is known about older adults with a mental illness and/or dementia experiencing delayed discharges from a hospital setting. A comprehensive search strategy was informed by the domains of the research questions and developed in consultation with a health sciences librarian from McMaster University in conjunction with the research team. The search began with OVID Medline due to well-developed subject headings (Appendix 1 – Search Strategy) and was adapted for five additional databases: Embase, Emcare, PsycINFO, Web of Science and CINAHL. The searches were limited to the English language. To capture the full scope of the available literature, studies addressing both mental illness and dementia were included, and no exclusions were applied based on predetermined definitions or diagnostic criteria. In addition, reference lists of included studies were reviewed to help identify additional literature for the review. Search results were managed by Endnote 20, a reference manager software and articles were imported into Covidence, a web-based application for screening and extraction [29]. All searches were conducted in September 2022 and updated in September 2024.

A comprehensive grey literature search was conducted using government publications and policy reports, using Google Advanced, Canadian Public Policy Collection, Canadian Electronic Library and Canadian Public Documents, in addition to websites of government and non-for-profit organizations (e.g., Canadian Institute for Health Information, Ontario Hospital Association, Cancer Care Ontario, Ontario Health, Ministry of Long-Term Care, National Health Services) that report on alternate level of care or delayed discharges in hospital settings. Overton database was used to access international policy documents. Details of the grey literature search strategy can be found in Appendix 1.

Step 3: Study selection

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

To understand the scope of the literature that explores delayed discharges amongst older adults with a mental illness and/or dementia, no limit on date of publication was imposed. Studies were eligible for inclusion if they were: (1) primary studies conducted in English; (2) studies involving people 65 years or older; (3) studies focused on delayed discharges or alternate level of care or transitional care; (4) studies focused on the experiences of older adults with delayed discharges and their care partners and healthcare providers; and (5) studies pertaining to a hospital setting. In addition, publications that were not original research studies, including reviews, editorials, protocols and commentaries, were excluded. Although relevant reviews were excluded, their reference lists were manually checked for relevant articles. The grey literature search applied the same inclusion criteria as peer-reviewed studies and excluded documents that did not provide detailed information on the characteristics of older adults with dementia or mental illness experiencing delayed discharge from hospital settings. We excluded studies if information on age (i.e., older adults 65 or greater) or diagnoses of mental illness or dementia were not reported separately in studies that included multiple age cohorts and/or medical diagnoses.

Selection process

Title and abstract screening was independently conducted by two reviewers using the inclusion and exclusion criteria to determine eligible articles for full-text review. Full-text review of the articles was conducted by a minimum of two reviewers from the research team to confirm eligibility. Disagreements between reviewers at each stage were resolved through consensus in regular meetings and/or by a third reviewer. A PRISMA-ScR flow chart was used to report the results of the searches and both screening steps [28].

Step 4: Charting the data

Grembowski et al. (2014) highlights that complexity occurs when there is a large gap between a person’s needs and the health system capacity to support their needs. A data extraction form was created that included variables from the domains of Grembowski’s et al. (2014) model (e.g., person and service delivery) to examine the need-services gap related to older adults with a mental illness and/or dementia experiencing delayed discharge [30]. The form was piloted by two reviewers to ensure clarity of content and consistency in documentation. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and/or by a third reviewer. As charting is considered an iterative process [26, 27], any changes to the data extraction table were made in consultation with the research team before extraction of all articles was complete. The extracted data included first author’s name; publication year; study characteristics (i.e., study design, aim, country, sample size, hospital service admitted to); patient characteristics (i.e., age, gender, diagnoses, living arrangements prior to admission, marital status); characteristics of delayed discharge (i.e., reason for hospital admission, mean length of stay during delayed discharge, reason for delayed discharge); experiences and outcomes with delayed discharge, and barriers and facilitators to discharge.

Step 5: Collating, summarizing and reporting the results

Grembowski et al.’s (2014) framework was used to guide the reporting of data extracted from the studies as it allows for the synthesis of data related to the variables (i.e., person, social support, contextual factors etc.) influencing the complexity in the care of patients with delayed discharges (Fig. 1). In addition, the framework was utilized to describe the patient, health system and other contextual factors that influence the complexity of delayed discharges to delineate the gaps between a person’s needs and the services available to support them. Data was analyzed by providing a narrative synthesis of the results [30].

Results

A total of 35,709 articles were identified through the database search, and duplicates were removed using Covidence. After duplicate removal, 14,712 articles remained for screening of titles and abstracts. After screening, 14,626 articles were excluded leaving 86 articles for full-text review. Following the completion of the full-text review, 11 articles that specifically focused on delayed discharge amongst older adults with a mental illness and/or dementia were included. Although grey literature sources addressed improving dementia care in hospital settings, only those that focused primarily on delayed discharges among patients with dementia and/or mental illness were included in this review. Ultimately, only one document met the inclusion criteria. Details on the screening process are shown in Fig. 2.

Overview of included studies

Tables 1 and 2 provide a summary of the eleven studies and one grey literature report that met the inclusion criteria for this review [16, 18, 31–39]. These studies were published between 1987 [34] and 2022 [36, 39], and were conducted in four countries: England (n = 5), Canada (n = 4), Japan (n = 1), and the United States (n = 1). Most studies were set in urban care environments (n = 9), and two spanned both urban and rural settings. Six studies were conducted in psychiatric inpatient settings [16, 31–33, 37, 38], one study was conducted on a medical inpatient unit [36], while four focused on delayed discharges in a general hospital [18, 34–37, 39]. A range of study designs was employed. The most common study design was retrospective design (n = 5; [18, 32, 33, 35, 39], followed by cohort studies (n = 2; [37, 38], point-prevalence studies (n = 2; [31, 34], one multi-method study [16], and one qualitative study employing an interpretive description methodology [36].

For grey literature, one government document that met the inclusion criteria was published by the National Health Service in London, United Kingdom, and focused on identifying factors contributing to delayed discharges in acute care hospitals for patients with dementia and/or mental illness. The report included hospitalization data from 2018 and conducted a case note analysis review [40].

Proportion of delayed discharge cases that are older adults with mental illness and/or dementia in a hospital setting

Eight studies reported on the proportion of delayed discharges among older adults with mental illness and/or dementia [16, 18, 31, 32, 34, 35, 38, 39]. One study included only older adults with dementia with a delayed discharge [36], while two other studies reported dementia as a factor associated with delayed discharge but did not specify the proportion of patients affected [33, 37]. Reported rates of older adults with dementia with delayed discharge ranged from 44% [18] to 64% [35]. In a retrospective, population-based study of 276,299 individuals using data from four time points (2001/2002, 2005/2006, 2010/2011, and 2015/2016), Aaltonen et al. (2021) found the prevalence of dementia among older adults with delayed discharge ranged from 40% to 48%, compared with 10% to 11% of individuals without dementia.

In studies that included older adults with a mental illness and/or dementia (N = 3), delayed discharges ranged from 24% [31] to 54% [32]. In Hanif et al. (2008), reported that 54% of participants experienced delayed discharge, with 70% of participants with a diagnosis of dementia and 22% with a diagnosis of depression. Similarly, in Koffman et al. (1996), 24% of the participants had a delayed discharge, with 56% had a diagnosis of dementia, 27% with mood disorder and 8% with schizophrenia. In Johnston et al. (1987), 21% of patients screened positive for cognitive impairment, and 60% of this experienced delayed discharge. Grey literature reported that 25% of general hospital beds in England were occupied by people living with dementia [40]. Although the report did not specify the proportion or provide additional information of patients experiencing delayed discharge, the findings highlight a strong association between dementia and delayed discharge in older adult populations. Additionally, none of the studies reported reasons for hospital admission for patients experiencing delayed discharge.

Characteristics of older adults with a mental illness and/or dementia with a delayed discharge

Age

Five studies reported age characteristics of participants with dementia and/or mental illness who experienced delayed discharge [18, 31, 35, 36, 39]. Mean ages ranged from 79 years [31] to 84 years [18]. Two studies provided age range distributions. Lim Fat et al. (2022) reported that 8% of participants were aged 65 to 74 years (n = 6), 38% were aged 75 to 84 years (n = 28), 47% were aged 85 to 94 years (n = 34), and 7% were aged 95 years or older (n = 5). Sakamoto et al. (2022) reported a distribution of 60 to 70 years (n = 1), 71 to 80 years (n = 1), 81 to 90 years (n = 4), and 91 to 100 years (n = 2). The remaining six studies reported age characteristics for the overall study sample but did not disaggregate these findings for participants with delayed discharge [16, 32–34, 37, 38].

Sex and gender

Across the studies, the terms “sex” and “gender” were used inconsistently and often without definition. In most cases, the use of binary terms such as “male” and “female”, and the reliance on administrative or clinical data suggests that biological sex was reported, although this was not explicitly stated. Six studies reported sex or gender-specific data for participants who experienced delayed discharge with dementia and/or mental illness [18, 31, 35, 36, 38, 39], while five additional studies reported sex for the full sample and did not separate findings for those with delayed discharge [16, 32–34, 37]. Among studies that provided disaggregated data, females generally accounted for a higher proportion of delayed discharges, ranging from 58.9% [39] to 71.9% [35]. In Aaltonen et al. (2020), 58% of older adults with dementia and delayed discharge were female. One study reported an equal number of male and female participants with delayed discharge [36].

Ethnicity

Only two studies reported data on the ethnicity of patients who experienced delayed discharge [31, 36]. In Sakamoto et al. (2022), eight participants with dementia were identified as Caucasian (n = 3) and Asian (n = 5). In Koffman et al. (1996), the majority of participants with mental illness and dementia were White (96%), with smaller proportions identified as Black (2.0%), Asian (1%), and Other (1%). No additional information was provided about the individuals categorized as “Other”.

Diagnoses

Among the studies that focused on older adults with dementia [18, 35–37, 39], only two studies provided detailed information on comorbidity and additional medical or psychiatric diagnoses of the participants [35, 39]. In Lim Fat et al. (2022), common diagnoses among individuals with dementia and delayed discharge included dementia with behavioural and psychological symptoms (22%), osteoarthritis (25%), atrial fibrillation (22%), coronary artery disease (18%), and depression (14%). McCloskey et al. (2014) reported that 65% of older adults with dementia had hypertension, 41% had ischaemic heart disease, and 28% had diabetes.

In studies conducted in mental health inpatient settings, diagnoses among older adults with delayed discharge included dementia [31, 32], depression [32], schizophrenia [31], bipolar affective disorder [31, 32], organic illness (not further specified; [32], and personality disorder [31]. Only one study [39] specifically reported on a cohort of patients with dementia and a coexisting mental illness (i.e., depression) who experienced delayed discharge. Tucker et al. (2017) examined delayed discharge among older adults with mental illness or dementia; however, detailed diagnostic information was not provided for those with delayed discharge. The authors only reported that 67% of patients with moderate to high cognitive impairment and 41% of those with moderate to high levels of challenging behaviour experienced delayed discharges of three days or more. Similarly, Johnston et al. (1987) examined a mixed sample of older adults with dementia and/or mental illness and found that within the subset of patients with low scores (less than 8) on the Clifton Assessment Procedures for the Elderly (CAPE), 60% experienced delayed discharge. However, the study did not provide further explanation regarding the relationship between low CAPE scores and discharge delays.

Discharge destination

Six of the eleven studies and the grey literature document reported discharge destinations for older adults with a mental illness and/or dementia who experienced delayed discharge [18, 31, 32, 34, 36, 39, 40] (Refer to Fig. 3). LTC was the most frequently reported discharge destination [18, 31, 32, 34, 36, 39, 40], followed by discharge to home [18, 32], home with supports [18, 36], rehabilitation services [31], residential home [31, 32, 34], and palliative care facility [18], and “other”, no additional details were provided [18, 31]. Cook et al. (2018) also identified ‘waiting for a non-acute National Health Service (NHS) bed as a discharge destination contributing to delays, however, they did not provide a definition. Notably, none of the studies reported patient preferences regarding discharge destination; instead, discharge destinations were presented solely as categorical outcomes without reference to individual choice, values, or goals of care.

Transfers to other medical or psychiatric facilities were also reported as discharge destinations in four studies [18, 31, 32, 34]. Studies that investigated delayed discharges across an entire hospital, discharge destinations included transfers to mental health services [34] and to specialized geriatric services [32, 34]. In acute psychiatric settings within the North and South Thames NHS regions, reported discharge destinations included acute medical wards, specialist psychiatric care, secure inpatient units, and low-level psychiatric care [31]. Across studies, details on the reason for transfer to these discharge facilities were not provided.

Length of delayed discharge

Five studies reported data on the length of delayed discharge among older adults with dementia and/or mental illness [18, 31, 32, 36, 39]. Aaltonen et al. (2020) reported a mean delayed discharge duration of 36.1 days among older adults with dementia. In Lim Fat et al. (2022), 21% of participants with dementia experienced a delay of 15 days or less, 12% experienced a delay of 16 to 30 days, 30% between 31 and 60 days, 10% between 61 and 100 days, 21% between 101 and 200 days, 1% between 201 and 300 days, and 3% experienced delays exceeding 300 days. Sakamoto et al. (2020) reported delays ranging from 0 to 50 days to 201 to 250 days, with one unspecified duration.

Koffman et al. (1996) found that 33.8% of patients with delayed discharge and a diagnosis of dementia remained in hospital for more than three months. However, no further detail was provided regarding the duration of delay for participants with mental illness or the overall mean length of delay in the sample. Hanif et al. (2008) reported delays by discharge destination, ranging from 33 days (sheltered accommodation) to 446 days (Elderly Mental Infirmary Unit). Findings across the studies suggest that discharge destination may influence the duration of delay, however the studies did not explore this relationship further.

Supports prior to hospital admission

Limited information was reported regarding the social, mental health, or medical supports that individuals with delayed discharge received in the community prior to hospitalization. Tucker et al. (2017) was the only study that examined pre-hospital support in some detail. Among individuals without formal social care, 69% experienced a delayed discharge of three days or less, while 31% experienced delays of three or more days. In contrast, among those with formal social care, 54% experienced a delay three days or greater. A similar pattern was observed for mental health supports, with longer delays (62%) more common among those receiving formal support. The study did not report further information examining the potential explanations for these patterns.

Koffman et al. (1996) reported that 96% of patients with delayed discharge had a designated family physician. However, no information was provided on the physician’s role, frequency of engagement or level of involvement in patients’ care. Aaltonen et al. (2021) identified a strong association between primary care use and delayed discharge among older adults with dementia, with odds of delayed discharge increasing by 84% if patients with dementia visited two or more physicians and by 114% if 10 or more physicians were visited. This association was more significant (OR 1.40, 95% CI [1.24, 1.60]) among older adults with dementia than older adults without dementia [18]. No other studies reported pre-hospital support characteristics.

Living status prior to hospitalization

Three studies reported on the living arrangements of persons with dementia and/or mental illness who experienced delayed discharge prior to their hospital admission [31, 38, 39]. Lim Fat et al. (2022) found that, among older adults with dementia with delayed discharge and awaiting LTC placement, most had been living with a partner or family member (56%), followed by residence in a retirement home (21%), living alone (21%), and other unspecified arrangements (3%). Additional living situations reported across the studies included living at home without support [31, 38], living at home with informal care [38], residence in a care home [38], and experiencing homelessness [31]. Some participants’ living arrangements were categorized under “other” without further information provided [38].

Experiences and impacts of delayed discharge from a hospital setting among older adults with a mental illness and/or dementia and their care partners and health care providers

Experiences

One qualitative study reported the experiences of older adults with dementia experiencing delayed discharge in hospital, alongside the perspectives of nurses and family care partners [36]. Sakamoto et al. (2020) identified three themes: living and waiting, distress and hevaiours, and beyond the designation. Patients described the hospital setting as an alternative form of home, where they sought to maintain a sense of routine and normalcy while awaiting an appropriate discharge destination, but reported prolonged inactivity, boredom, and emotional distress, particularly among individuals with advanced dementia. The presence of visible signage used to identify patients with behavioural concerns (e.g., purple markers above beds) was reported to contribute to distress and feelings of shame and stigmatization.

Decline in patient’s physical and functional status was reported by family care partners during prolonged hospital stays, specifically noting deconditioning and reduced mobility [36]. In an effort to preserve the identity and dignity of the patient and help staff ‘look beyond the designation’, care partners frequently shared personal photographs and narratives with the care team to portray who the patient was prior to hospitalization. Nurses reported challenges in engaging patients with dementia in meaningful activities, citing limitations inherent to the acute care setting and the demands of clinical workloads. As a result, patients often at times spent much of their day sitting passively in chairs or remaining in their rooms, with little stimulation or structured activity. Deconditioning was frequently observed by both staff and family care partners, which was noted by nurses that older adults commonly experience physical decline when not mobilized regularly in hospital environments.

Due to systemic pressures within acute care settings, care delivery often required prioritizing efficiency over individualized approaches, which oftentimes resulted in the use of both physical and chemical restraints [36]. Patients reported being physically restricted, often citing the use of devices such as tray tables attached to chairs that limited their ability to move freely. One nurse acknowledged that pharmacological interventions were sometimes used as a “quick fix” to manage responsive behaviours, likely due to heavy workloads and limited time, though this was not explicitly described by the author. Despite these practices, nurses emphasized the importance of addressing patients’ unmet needs, such as hunger, toileting, and pain, wherever possible in order to minimize responsive behaviours, while patients highlighted the importance of respectful assistance with basic care needs and meaningful communication.

Impact of delayed discharge

Two studies examined the impact of delayed discharge [32, 39]. Lim Fat et al. (2022) investigated the iatrogenic consequences of delayed discharge by analyzing the occurrence of adverse events among older adults with dementia with prolonged hospital stays and found that 57% (n = 206) experienced at least one adverse event. Adverse events included: delirium (21%, n = 44), urinary tract infections (15%, n = 31), falls (10%, n = 20), respiratory infections (9%, n = 18), and pressure ulcers and skin or soft tissue infections (each 4%, n = 8). Hanif et al. (2008) explored the financial implications of delayed discharge in England and found delayed discharge amounted to an additional cost of £6,540 per patient over the three-month study duration. This finding highlights the potential health and economic burden associated with prolonged hospital stays for older adults.

Barriers and facilitators to timely discharge for older adults with a mental illness and/or dementia with delayed discharge

Barriers to timely discharge for older adults with mental illness and/or dementia were attributed to discharge destinations challenges. Several studies identified the unavailability of appropriate discharge settings as a key contributing factor [18, 31, 32, 34, 36, 39]. Additional barriers included limited specialist services to support discharge in the community [16, 34] and financial constraints affecting placement decisions [16, 32, 40]. Although the reasons behind financial barriers were not explored in detail, the studies and grey literature reporting this finding were conducted in the United Kingdom, where universal healthcare is available. It may be inferred that financial implications were related to costs associated with private home care or LTC services [16, 32, 40].

Logistical issues such as prolonged wait times for home care or LTC assessments to determine service eligibility and whether the home environment could support the patient’s care needs contributed to delays [18, 31, 32, 39]. Some studies noted delays related to care partner decision-making, refusal of proposed discharge destinations, or lack of early family involvement in discharge planning [16, 32, 40]. Similarly, care partner-related delays were also identified as a barrier to timely discharge in the grey literature for patients with dementia, which specified that the lack of early involvement of family members in discharge planning led to delays due to disagreements over discharge plans or misunderstandings regarding the financial costs associated with discharge destinations, with the assumption that the cost of the LTC home would be funded [40]. In addition, behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia, increased care needs (e.g., mobility or feeding assistance), and refusal of readmission by prior care home were reported as barriers to returning to previous residences [40], although specific details on the specific reason for declining to readmit patients were not reported.

One study estimated that 19% of older adults experiencing a delayed discharge may have been suitable for home discharge if adequate supports were in place such as assistance with daily living, access to professional or specialist care in the home or at day centres, and appropriate housing since 13% of participants experienced homelessness [31] (Fig. 4).

Only one study explicitly identified a facilitator to timely discharge and emphasized the importance of primary care and continuity of care for individuals with dementia [18]. Aaltonen et al. (2021) found that the odds of delayed discharge increased by 84% when individuals with dementia were followed by two or more primary care physicians in the community in the year before admission, compared to those who received continuous care from a single provider. Consistent care from one provider, supported by adequate community-based services, was suggested to improve health outcomes, reduced hospitalizations, decreased healthcare expenditures, and increased patient satisfaction.

The NHS report by Cook et al. (2018), emphasized a key factor in ensuring timely discharge for patients with dementia was ensuring that patients’ medical records include clear and consistent documentation in clinical charts, including records of rehabilitation needs, capacity assessments, and medical progress during hospitalization. The report also emphasized the importance of discharge planning documentation with which there were often delays as nurses lacked sufficient time to document alongside their clinical duties. This had implications to ensure referrals were submitted promptly to support timely discharge for patients to receive support. The authors found that NHS Trusts with dedicated discharge coordinators were more likely to complete the necessary paperwork on time, facilitating smoother transitions to other care facilities [40]. Additionally, collaboration between NHS Trusts and hospital services, particularly when face to face interactions were facilitated, enhanced communication and improved understanding of available services, thereby supporting more effective discharge planning [40].

While the remaining studies (n = 10) did not directly investigate facilitators to timely discharge, they offered a range of recommendations including increasing the availability of specialized community-based services such as psychogeriatric consultation teams [31, 37] and assertive outreach teams [32]; engaging in early discharge planning with families and care teams [32, 33, 37, 38]; and establishing step-down or transitional care units to support patients post-discharge [16, 32, 39]. Other suggestions included implementing hospital-based strategies to maintain patients’ functional abilities and prevent deterioration during hospitalization [35], improving access to home care and LTC for individuals with high levels of need for their activities of daily living and behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia [31, 32, 39], and promoting greater integration between hospital and community services [38]. For instance, Tucker et al. (2017) recommended that hospital staff rotate into community roles to enhance their understanding of local contexts and facilitate knowledge exchange, thereby improving care transitions. Additionally, the McCloskey et al. (2014) study found that 34% of patients with delayed discharge did not receive a diagnosis of dementia until they were admitted to hospital. The authors noted that earlier diagnosis of dementia and the provision of relevant care in the community may reduce delayed discharges by facilitating timely access to specialized services; however, the study did not elaborate on the specific factors linking delayed diagnosis to delayed discharge [35]. This finding was also described in the grey literature, which reported that many patients exhibited clinical signs and symptoms of dementia without having a formal diagnosis [40]. In one example, a patient with a long history of cognitive impairment and a previous resident in three care homes was only formally diagnosed after hospital admission. While the report emphasized the importance of early diagnosis, it did not provide details on how delayed diagnoses contributed to delayed discharge.

Discussion

This study aimed to describe the existing evidence on delayed discharges among older adults with a diagnosis of dementia and/or mental illness in hospital settings. Overall, findings from the studies indicated that delayed discharges were most prevalent among older adults with dementia [18, 31, 32, 39]. Key barriers to timely discharge included the unavailability of appropriate discharge destinations with most patients waiting for LTC home placement, limited availability of specialist services, and waiting for home care supports. The impacts of delayed discharge were linked to iatrogenic factors, increasing a person’s risk of hospital-acquired infections and other adverse events. Care partners and nurses also noted physical decompensation and functional decline during hospital stays. Identified facilitators to timely discharge included access to primary care, early involvement of patients and families in discharge planning, and enhanced collaboration between inpatient and community services.

The findings of this scoping review support Grembowski et al.’s (2014) conceptual model, which emphasizes the role of complexity in the care of individuals with multiple chronic conditions. Although only two studies detailed comorbidities in the review [35, 39], older adults with dementia or mental illness often have multiple chronic conditions and can therefore experience complexity within the health system [22, 30]. Several studies included in the review reported that older adults who experienced delayed discharges commonly encountered a mismatch between their health and social care needs and the services provided within the health system (e.g., unavailability of appropriate discharge destinations, particularly LTC, limited availability of specialist services) and community resources (e.g., delays in the initiation of home care services). The extent of the need–services gap is defined as the disparity between patients’ healthcare needs and the system’s capacity to meet those needs, which directly influences the complexity of care. When adequate supports within the health system are unavailable to meet an individual’s needs, this gap can contribute to delayed hospital discharges.

The information presented in the studies and grey literature report offered only superficial insights into the factors contributing to delayed discharge in this patient population and lacked an in-depth exploration of the underlying cause. Although the concept of delayed discharge is consistently defined in the literature, variations in national policy frameworks may exist, making it difficult to compare findings across studies due to differences in policies and definitions across regions and countries. While the studies in this review did not thoroughly examine the barriers to discharge in depth, a recent report by the Provincial Geriatrics Leadership in Ontario (2025) highlights several systemic challenges for older adults requiring specialized geriatric care. An evidence scan conducted as part of the report indicates that long-standing support programs, such as specialized geriatric and mental health services remain underfunded and have not expanded in proportion to the growing demand. Mental health services for older adults with dementia and/or severe and persistent mental illness are particularly fragmented and difficult to access. Consequently, the limited availability and fragmentation of community-based services places increased pressure on hospitals to address these complex needs, contributing to prolonged hospital stays and delays in discharge [41].

To address delayed discharges within this patient population, upstream approaches need to be adopted while supports in the community need to be strengthened and increased through funding investments. A recent study by Heckman et al. (2024) found that integrated care teams embedded within primary care, combined with case management, improved system navigation and delivered high-quality care, while also reducing hospitalizations among older adults with complex needs. Aaltonen et al. (2021), highlighted the importance of continuity of care in their study and found that older adults who consulted with multiple primary care physicians were at a higher risk of delayed discharge, emphasizing the value of consistent, proactive and coordinated primary care. These findings point to an urgent need to strengthen primary health care systems to offer upstream, preventative solutions for managing complex health needs in older adults. Enhancing the capacity of primary care through integrated care teams and shared care with geriatricians and/or geriatric psychiatrists can support ongoing, specialized management and continuity of care to support older adults living with multiple chronic conditions [6]. In addition, integrating specialized geriatric care and mental health services into primary care helps increase a person’s access to evidence-informed assessment and interventions, particularly in the areas of chronic disease management and deprescribing, while also supporting the provision of ongoing, coordinated care. To effectively support the complex comorbidities among older adults with mental illness and/or dementia, access to community-based geriatric services is essential [31, 37, 38]. The involvement of psychogeriatric assertive community treatment teams, which are multidisciplinary teams that provide intensive case management, treatment and support to individuals with serious mental illness, and integrated care teams, comprising of nurses, geriatricians, geriatric psychiatrists, and an interprofessional team with specialized expertise in geriatrics and system navigation to community support services, have been shown to help support individuals in the community while reducing the need for hospitalizations [42].

Several studies identified LTC homes as a common discharge destination for older adults in the review, which resulted in delayed discharges due to the unavailability of LTC beds. However, no studies examined the rationale behind selecting LTC as the most appropriate discharge destination. It remains unclear whether these decisions were driven by the complexity of the individual’s mental health or dementia related needs or by the limited availability of community-based services to support care at home post-discharge. To better understand the services required by this population, more detailed information regarding their specific care needs is required. Sugiyama et al. (2013) noted that some participants admitted to a psychogeriatric unit could have avoided hospitalization if psychogeriatric consultation or outreach teams had been readily available in the community. Similarly, the Canadian Institute for Health Information (2023) found that one in ten individuals admitted to LTC could have been supported at home with appropriate home care services, emphasizing the ongoing mismatch between patient needs and available healthcare resources [43]. Further research is needed to better understand why individuals with these conditions are more frequently placed in LTC homes. However, for individuals with complex mental health needs and/or advanced dementia, there is a critical need for an increased number of secure LTC homes with memory clinics to support their care and safety within the community.

A notable absence in the included literature was a discussion or examination of practices within the hospital system that contribute to delayed discharge, such as institutional policies, clinical practices, and workforce composition. Only one study addressed institutional policies related to least restraint approaches to care and found that, despite such policies, older adults with dementia were frequently provided with chemical and/or physical restraints (e.g., lap belts, tray tables) during hospitalization, which limited their mobility [36]. Factors such as time constraints and staffing pressures were identified by nurses, which resulted in the use of restrictive practices as a means of managing patient behaviours, using them as a quick and convenient solution rather than as a last resort [36]. Although physical restraints are sometimes perceived as a convenient method for managing behavioural symptoms, empirical evidence suggests they are associated with reduced and adverse effects on physical and cognitive function, potentially infringing on patient’s human rights when used to restrict movement. Adverse events include increased risk for pressure injuries, incontinence and injuries [44, 45]. These negative consequences may lead to functional decline, prolonged hospitalization, and delayed discharge due to deterioration in patient’s physical status, thereby perpetuating a cycle of delayed discharge [45]. The use of physical restraints addresses only the immediate behavioural symptoms rather than the underlying causes that contribute to behavioural escalation. Therefore, a cultural shift within healthcare practice is required to promote the consistent implementation of patient-centered care, particularly in the management of responsive behaviours [45, 46]. Importantly, it remains unclear within the current body of evidence what precipitates the initial hospitalization of older adults with dementia and/or mental illness. Whether admissions are primarily driven by acute medical events, exacerbation of chronic conditions, or care partner burnout is not consistently reported. This gap limits our understanding of how to intervene earlier and more effectively to prevent avoidable hospitalizations to mitigate their impact.

Best practice recommendations for improving care delivery to older adults with dementia emphasize the adoption of person-centered approaches [46]. These approaches involve actively engaging with individuals living with dementia to understand their preferences, values, and interests. Recognizing and validating the individual’s lived reality is essential, as responsive behaviours often represent expressions of unmet needs or alternative forms of communication. Fostering effective and empathetic communication is critical. Furthermore, building trusting and meaningful relationships is a foundational component of high-quality, person-centered care [46]. Similarly, Grembowski et al. (2014) highlight the importance of a person’s value and preference in influencing health care use. These factors influence how individuals perceive their health, what services they seek, and the decisions they make regarding treatment and care transitions. Cultural background, beliefs, and personal goals further inform these values and preferences, affecting whether individuals accept treatment, return home, or move into LTC. None of the reviewed studies reported on patient preferences regarding discharge destinations or goal setting during admission, representing a critical gap in our understanding and potentially missed opportunities to provide more person-centered care. Individual values and preferences, particularly for those experiencing delayed discharges, are important for health care leaders and policy makers to consider when creating policies and translating them to care settings. Some health systems have implemented policies to address hospital capacity issues, such as Ontario’s Bill 7 More Beds, Better Care Act (2022), which authorizes admission to a LTC home without patient consent. Such strategies overlooks the patient’s voice and have been argued to violate a patient’s constitutional right on their liberty of making decisions on where they will live [47]. In a qualitative study by Kuluski et al. (2022), the authors found patients and care partners prioritize safety, affordability of services, and proximity to family when considering what LTC home to transition to [8]. Therefore, to truly align with person-centered care, discharge planning and health policy should prioritize solutions to build capacity within the health system that respect and reflect individuals’ values and preferences.

The composition of a workforce mix is directly tied to care delivery and coordination of care. An understaffed workforce mix can contribute to poor communication, care delays, and fragmented transitions [30]. The Provincial Geriatrics Leadership Ontario has highlighted the importance of strengthening the workforce mix and providing targeted training for staff in hospitals and LTC settings [41]. These strategies are essential to support the effective transition of older adults with dementia and/or mental illness from hospital to community-based care. To implement best practices in dementia care and to effectively support older adults with a mental illness, it is essential to provide continuing education to health care professionals on evidence-based interventions. Training in approaches, such as Gentle Persuasive Approaches (GPA), PIECES (Physical, Intellectual, Emotional, Capabilities, Environment, and Social), crisis intervention and mental health care, are necessary to build capacity within the workforce [36, 41, 48].

In the NHS report by Cook et al. (2018), older adults with dementia exhibiting behavioural and psychological symptoms were denied return to their previous LTC homes due to the facilities’ inability to manage their complex care needs. Although specific details about the nature of the behaviours were not provided, this finding aligns with existing literature that highlights poor access to psychiatric support services and specialized dementia care units in LTC settings as significant barriers to appropriate placement [49, 50]. These challenges are compounded by limited staff training in managing mental illness and dementia. Many LTC homes have limited access to external resources to support residents with responsive behaviours or complex mental health needs. Thus, reinforcing the need to equip LTC staff with specialized training and education in managing these conditions is required to build internal capacity and ensure high-quality, person-centered care for this population. Moreover, staffing levels in LTC settings remain inadequate, despite substantial evidence indicating that higher staffing levels are necessary to ensure safe and effective care [51]. Therefore, enhancing staff education and improving staffing levels in LTC settings can facilitate the successful transition of older adults with mental illness and/or dementia, as LTC homes would be better equipped to manage the complex care needs of this population in their current setting and would be more inclined to accept admission of older adults with mental illness and/or dementia.

Facilitators to ensuring timely discharge and to reduce delayed discharges were identified in the review. These included early discharge planning discussions with an interdisciplinary team, which helped support efficient patient transitions through care pathways [32, 33, 37]. Interdisciplinary collaboration enhanced care delivery and supported smoother transitions, ultimately improving outcomes for older adults. Initiating discharge planning early in the hospital admission allowed healthcare teams to proactively assess patient needs and begin arranging necessary community supports or home placements, rather than waiting until discharge was imminent [52]. Moreover, the active involvement of patients and their care partners was consistently identified as a critical component of effective discharge planning in the literature [13, 52]. Despite recommendations in the studies to include patient and care partners in discharge planning discussions, engagement remains inconsistent in practice [13, 52]. There remains a pressing need to ensure that patients and care partners are meaningfully engaged throughout the care continuum. Consistently involving them in discharge planning and transitions to community-based services is essential for promoting person-centered care and reducing the risk of delayed discharges [9, 53].

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this review is the methodological rigour employed and inclusion of grey literature to explore delayed discharge amongst older adults with a mental illness and/or dementia, which has received limited attention to date. To our knowledge, this is the first review that has explored delayed discharge primarily within this patient population. Although literature was found regarding delayed discharges in older adults with a mental illness and/or dementia, there continues to be limited knowledge available that provides an in-depth exploration of delayed discharges solely within the patient population of older adults with a mental illness or dementia. Therefore, this review contributes to addressing a gap in the existing literature by exploring factors that contribute to delayed discharges among older adults with dementia or mental illness through a comprehensive review of the literature without geographic or year restrictions.

A limitation of the included literature was that the review was restricted to English-language publications, despite not having geographic limitations. Since the aim of the review was to explore what is known about delayed discharge amongst older adults with mental illness and/or dementia, no date restrictions were imposed. However, three studies were published between 1987 and 1996 [31, 33, 34]. There have been significant changes that have occurred in healthcare in the past 30 years regarding policy developments and healthcare program re-design to respond to the increased demands of an aging population. Despite the older publication dates of the studies, the articles provided context to the phenomena of delayed discharge within older adults with a mental illness and dementia and supported that the concept of delayed discharges is a long-standing, international health system challenge. Forward searching was not conducted as the review explored literature published from 1987 to 2024. Furthermore, the limited reporting on multimorbidity and medical diagnoses in the included studies restricts our ability to fully understand the factors contributing to delayed discharge in in older adults with dementia and/or mental illness.

Another limitation found is that minimal studies reported on the ethnicity of participants [16, 31, 36]. Only two studies reported a description of the participants’ ethnicity and participants in these studies were predominantly white (71%, 95%) [16, 31]. Future studies should aim to collect and report data on ethnicity to enhance culturally appropriate care options and determine, from a health equity standpoint, if patients are disproportionately impacted by current healthcare policies and practices. Additionally, while a rigorous search method was employed, it is possible that relevant literature could have been missed, particularly with the grey literature search.

Future directions for research

Further research is warranted to reflect the current realities of the healthcare system and to explore the evolving health needs of older adults, particularly those living with dementia and/or mental illness. It is essential to examine the availability, accessibility, and appropriateness of services designed to support this population, especially in the context of delayed discharges in hospital. The results from the review only scratches the surface of this issue and emphasizes the importance for future research to provide a deeper understanding of the needs-service gap that impacts older adults with a mental illness and/or dementia. This understanding is essential to inform effective interventions aimed at addressing the complex issue of delayed discharges in this patient population. Specifically, research should explore how this need–service gap is shaped by broader social and systemic influences, and how community context affects the health systems capacity to meet the complex care needs of these individuals. Attention must also be given to the development and evaluation of community partnerships that provide integrated and tailored health and social care services. Such partnerships, involving hospitals, community health providers, social services, and other interested parties, have the potential to support smoother transitions from hospital to community settings, serving as a key strategy to mitigate delayed discharges. Strengthening these collaborations could help address the systemic barriers that contribute to delayed discharges, ultimately narrowing the need–service gap and improving continuity of care for this population.

Additionally, research focused on delayed discharges among older adults with mental illness and/or dementia should prioritize the collection and systematic reporting of data, including race, ethnicity, and language background. Obtaining these data is essential for identifying disparities in access to care and for designing culturally appropriate service pathways that support equitable and timely hospital discharge. Incorporating cultural and linguistic considerations into discharge planning not only enhances equity but may also reduce discharge delays by ensuring that post-acute services, such as home care, mental health support, and LTC placements, are responsive to the diverse needs of patients.

Conclusion

Delayed discharges reflect a growing concern highlighting health system inefficiencies to optimize health and social care needs of individuals in the community [17]. With an aging population, the likelihood of experiencing a mental health concern increases with age, as does healthcare utilization [4, 5]. To our knowledge, this is the first review that has explored the concept of delayed discharge amongst older adults with a mental illness and/or dementia. Findings from this review reveal delayed discharges were most common among older adults with dementia and patients with mental health conditions such as schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, with a higher prevalence reported among females. Contributing factors included limited availability of LTC placements, patient developing hospital-acquired complications, physical and functional decline, financial strain on the healthcare system, and insufficient coordination, while timely discharge was facilitated by access to primary care, early family involvement, and improved collaboration between inpatient and community services. To understand the current healthcare challenges regarding the increase in delayed discharges, an epistemological shift must occur and move away from analyzing the phenomena from a single paradigm to one that acknowledges complexity. A coordinated health care response is required to attend to the complex health needs of older adults with a mental illness and/or dementia and to provide integrated care for older adults with complex health and social care needs. Further research is required to explore the impact of delayed discharge on older adults with a mental illness and/or dementia to further understand the health needs of this patient demographic and to explore solutions amongst patients, care partners, health care providers and key decision-makers that are responsive to the care needs of this patient population.
