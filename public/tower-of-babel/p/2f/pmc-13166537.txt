Spontaneous Achilles tendon rupture as the initial presentation of ochronosis: a case report

Introduction

Alkaptonuria (AKU) is a rare autosomal recessive metabolic disorder caused by mutations in the homogentisate 1,2-dioxygenase (HGD) gene, with an estimated incidence of 1 in 250 000–1 000 000 live births worldwide, although higher prevalence is observed in regions such as Slovakia and the Dominican Republic due to founder effects[1–3]. Deficiency of HGD leads to accumulation of homogentisic acid (HGA), a byproduct of tyrosine and phenylalanine metabolism.

Excess HGA undergoes oxidation, generating reactive oxygen species that promote extracellular matrix degeneration, calcification, and apoptosis of chondrocytes and tenocytes[4]. Progressive pigment deposition in connective tissues, termed ochronosis, causes bluish-black discoloration, early-onset spondyloarthropathy, and systemic complications including cardiac valve disease and renal and prostate stones[5].

Musculoskeletal manifestations usually appear in the third to fourth decade of life, with the patellar and Achilles tendons most frequently affected[6]. Spontaneous tendon or ligament rupture occurs in up to 30% of patients, making tendon pathology an important but underrecognized feature of AKU[7]. This case report follows the SCARE 2025 guidelines for surgical case reporting[8].

Case presentation

A 43-year-old male with no known comorbidities presented to the emergency department with sudden-onset sharp pain in the posterior aspect of his right ankle while walking during a routine workday. He described hearing a “snap” at the moment of injury, followed by an inability to push off the foot or bear weight. The pain was severe, tearing in nature, and localized just above the heel. There was no history of trauma, twisting, fall, or previous tendon-related symptoms.

The patient reported chronic lower back pain and stiffness over the past 3 years, typically worse in the mornings. He also noted long-standing episodes of dark-colored urine (Fig. 1), particularly after exertion, which had never been investigated. On examination, there was visible swelling and tenderness over the right Achilles tendon. A palpable gap was noted approximately 4–5 cm above the calcaneal insertion, and the Thompson test was positive, confirming Achilles tendon rupture. Additionally, the patient had bluish-black pigmentation of the sclerae and ear pinnae and brownish discoloration of the teeth (Fig. 2). A family history revealed a niece with a similar history of chronic dark urine, raising suspicion for a hereditary metabolic disorder (Table 1). Surgical repair was performed under spinal anesthesia with the patient in the prone position. A posteromedial longitudinal incision was made over the right Achilles tendon. Intraoperative findings confirmed a complete rupture at the calcaneal insertion. The tendon ends were thickened, friable, and showed dark black pigmentation (Fig. 3), consistent with ochronotic degeneration. After debridement of the affected tissue, primary repair was undertaken as a bone-to-end repair. An intraosseous tunnel was created in the calcaneus to pass No. 2 PDS sutures, followed by insertion of a 5 mm anchor to ensure stable fixation. A modified Krackow suture was applied proximally to reinforce the tendon. The final tendon gap measured less than 4 cm, allowing for direct repair without the need for tendon transfer. The ankle was splinted in plantarflexion, and the patient was instructed to remain non-weight-bearing with crutch-assisted ambulation. Histopathological examination revealed ochronotic pigment deposition within tendon tissue. The findings were characterized by dense pigmented HGA deposits, fragmentation of collagen fibers, and chronic inflammatory cell infiltration, which are highly suggestive of AKU.

No intraoperative complications were encountered. Postoperatively, the patient developed a superficial wound infection, which was successfully managed conservatively with a course of antibiotics and repeated local dressings. The patient and his family were subsequently referred for genetic counseling and a complete metabolic workup. Weight-bearing and formal physiotherapy were initiated 2 months postoperatively. The patient was followed for a total of 4 months, at which point he demonstrated satisfactory functional recovery and had successfully returned to his daily life activities.

The patient expressed full satisfaction with the surgical outcome and the overall care he received. By his final follow-up at 4 months, he was very pleased to have fully recovered his ankle mobility and successfully returned to his normal daily activities without pain.

Discussion

This case highlights a rare presentation of AKU through spontaneous Achilles tendon rupture, emphasizing the disease’s significant musculoskeletal impact and the diagnostic challenges it poses. AKU results from the accumulation of HGA in connective tissues, leading to progressive tendon fragility, cartilage degeneration, and spontaneous tendon ruptures. These pathological effects are largely mediated by oxidative stress and apoptosis, which cause cartilage fragmentation, synovial inflammation, and subsequent joint deterioration. Symptoms of AKU typically manifest clinically in the fourth or fifth decade of life, often after years of silent biochemical changes[6].

Differential diagnoses for spontaneous Achilles tendon rupture include corticosteroid-induced tendinopathy, fluoroquinolone-associated rupture, systemic connective tissue disorders (e.g., Marfan syndrome and Ehlers–Danlos syndrome), inflammatory arthropathies such as rheumatoid arthritis and gout, and metabolic disorders including ochronosis. The absence of medication use, characteristic ochronotic pigmentation, and histopathological confirmation established the diagnosis in this case[7].

AKU is frequently diagnosed intraoperatively, as early clinical symptoms are minimal or nonspecific. Dark urine, which results from HGA excretion, is usually present in childhood but often overlooked, leading to delayed diagnosis until adulthood when musculoskeletal symptoms become more pronounced[9]. Despite this relatively late clinical presentation, AKU causes rapid and progressive connective tissue degeneration, resulting in significant joint damage, tendon ruptures, and even potential cardiac complications, particularly after the age of 50[10]. Spontaneous Achilles tendon ruptures without preceding trauma are rare and infrequently reported in the literature, with few cases documenting successful surgical repair and recovery. In our patient, the striking intraoperative finding of black discoloration of the tendon tissue raised immediate suspicion of ochronosis, which was subsequently confirmed through histopathological analysis. This underlines the importance of considering underlying metabolic disorders such as AKU in patients presenting with spontaneous tendon ruptures, even in the absence of other systemic manifestations.

To date, no definitive studies have established the optimal surgical treatment for chronic Achilles tendon rupture associated with AKU. Although several repair techniques exist, none has demonstrated clear superiority. Surgical approach is generally guided by factors such as tear location, defect size, patient comorbidities, and surgeon experience[4]. In this case, modified Krackow repair with distal anchoring achieved satisfactory early healing.

Currently, no treatment definitively prevents the complications of AKU; however, conservative management strategies such as a low-protein diet and antioxidant therapy are often utilized alongside pharmacological options. While pharmacological therapy for AKU remains limited, nitisinone, a reversible inhibitor of 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate dioxygenase, blocks the formation of HGA in the tyrosine degradation pathway. It effectively reduces urinary HGA levels and pigment deposition in tissues. Although clinical trials demonstrate biochemical efficacy, long-term impact on disease progression remains under study. Regular monitoring of plasma tyrosine and dietary protein restriction are necessary during treatment to prevent adverse effects[11].

Early recognition of AKU in atypical tendon injuries allows for timely surgical intervention, metabolic management, and genetic counseling, potentially improving long-term outcomes in these patients.

A limitation of this case report is the lack of biochemical or genetic testing (such as urinary HGA levels, plasma tyrosine measurements, or HGD genetic testing) to confirm the diagnosis. These specific tests are not available at our hospital. Furthermore, sending these tests to outside laboratories is very expensive, and the patient could not afford the high cost. As a result, we based the diagnosis on the clinical signs and surgical findings – specifically the distinct black color of the Achilles tendon, the dark pigmentation of the eyes and ears, and the tissue biopsy results.

Conclusion

Spontaneous Achilles tendon rupture can be the first clinical manifestation of AKU. Recognition of ochronotic changes intraoperatively, combined with histopathological confirmation, is essential for diagnosis. Surgical repair tailored to the degenerative nature of ochronotic tendons, alongside metabolic evaluation and potential use of emerging therapies such as nitisinone, offers the best chance for improved outcomes in affected patients.
