Cowpea lipid transfer protein 1 regulates plant defense by inhibiting the cysteine protease of cowpea mosaic virus
Authors: Jie Ji, Shengli Du, Kun Wang, Ziyan Qi, Chunyang Zhang, Rui Wang, George Bruening, Pengwei Wang, Deqiang Duanmu, Qiuling Fan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceMany plant virus genomes encode proteases that process virus-encoded polyproteins to generate mature proteins that facilitate infection in the hosts. Plant responses to viral proteases are largely unknown. By using the cowpea and cowpea mosaic virus (CPMV) system, we found that cowpea lipid transfer protein 1 (LTP1) interacts with CPMV 24KPro, a cysteine protease, and inhibits its proteolytic activity both in vitro and in vivo. Heterologous expression of cowpea LTP1 in tobacco plants also resulted in the significant suppression of the infection by soybean mosaic virus that encodes a protease. This study identified a type of plant–virus interaction and provides a potential strategy for controlling plant viral diseases by inhibiting viral protease activity using plant-derived protease inhibitors.

Cowpea (Vigna unguiculata) is an important grain legume widely distributed and mainly produced in Africa (1). Cowpea grows in barren soil and is tolerant to abiotic stresses such as drought and salinity. It is an important alternative plant-derived protein source and provides carbohydrates and other nutritious compounds for a healthy diet in developing countries. Various types of biotic stress impair cowpea production. Cowpea mosaic virus (CPMV) is a single-stranded positive-sense RNA virus and is the type member of the genus Comovirus in the Secoviridae family of the order picornavirales (2). CPMV can induce chlorotic lesions or systemic infection in different cowpea varieties, and some cowpea varieties are extremely resistant to CPMV, showing no symptoms after infection (3).

Viral proteases encoded by single-stranded positive-sense RNA viruses of plants and animals are critical for virus replication. Based on the amino acids of enzyme’s active sites and the mechanisms of action, viral proteases are classified into cysteine proteases, serine proteases, aspartic proteases, and glutamic acid proteases (4–6). The primary role of viral proteases is to process viral polyproteins into individual functional proteins to complete the virus replication cycle. Besides the protease activity, plant viral proteases play roles in almost every step of virus infection such as silencing suppression, aphid transmission, and so on (7–9). Several studies reported roles of plant viral proteases in directly inducing plant resistance responses. Mestre et al. (10) demonstrated that the nuclear inclusion a (NIa) protease encoded by potato virus Y (PVY) elicited Ry-mediated resistance in potato. Helper-component protease (HC-Pro) of soybean mosaic virus (SMV) elicited resistance in Rsv1-soybean (11). 24KPro encoded by CPMV is an elicitor of a hypersensitive response (HR) in specific cowpea lines that show extreme resistance to CPMV (12).

Biological functions of proteases of vertebrate RNA viruses have been well studied. For human-infecting viruses such as coronaviruses, flaviviruses, and polioviruses, beyond processing polyprotein, viral proteases also reduce innate immune response by cleaving host factors that are involved in interferon production and signaling pathways (13). Protease inhibitors have been extensively studied and are used as antiviral drugs against animal viral infections (14–16). Compared to the well-characterized proteases of vertebrate-infecting viruses, there are few reports on their counterparts of plant viruses. Xiao et al. (17) reported that potyvirus NIa-Pro cleaved AtEML2, which is involved in plant defense response. Transgenic potato plants overexpressing aspartic protease inhibitor 5 (StAPI5) demonstrated decreased PVY and potato virus A (PVA) accumulation (18). However, mechanisms underlining plant responses to viral proteases are largely unexplored, and protease inhibitors have the potential for plant viral disease control.

The bipartite genome of CPMV consists of two positive-sense single-stranded RNA molecules, RNA1 and RNA2, which encode proteins for virus replication, movement, and encapsulation. 24KPro is the only viral protease, and it functions both in cis and in trans for the maturation of individual viral proteins (19). 24KPro is a type of chymotrypsin-like cysteine proteases, and the catalytic triad includes histidine, glutamate, and cysteine (20, 21). The consensus sequence recognized by 24KPro is AX(A, P)Q/(S, G, or M), which is similar to cleavage site of 3CPro of picornaviruses (4).

Pathogenesis-related (PR) proteins are important in plant defense response to abiotic and biotic stresses. The PR14 family is composed of lipid transfer proteins (LTPs) (22). LTPs are widely distributed in higher plants, participating in seed development, metabolism, and biotic and abiotic stress responses. Plant LTPs consist of two families, lipid transfer protein 1 (LTP1) of 9 to 10 kDa and LTP2 of ~7 kDa. The spatial structure of LTP consists of a hydrophobic cavity for binding and transporting lipids. The cavity is composed of α-helices and is maintained by four disulfide bonds (23, 24).

Besides lipid transferring activity, LTPs also participate in plant immune response (25). LTP expression is induced by plant hormones salicylic acid (SA) and abscisic acid (ABA) and by pathogens (26, 27). LTPs interact with PR1, WIPK (wound-induced protein kinase), and abscisic acid receptor PYL4 to regulate immune responses (28–30). LTPs from a number of plant species such as wheat, potato, ginkgo, and pea have been shown to have antifungal or antibacterial activities (31–33). However, there are few reports regarding roles of LTPs in viral infection. Bamboo mosaic virus (BaMV) does not encode a protease and its infection of tobacco down-regulated NbLTP1 expression, and NbLTP1-knockdown plants showed reduced BaMV replication, indicating that NbLTP1 promotes BaMV accumulation in tobacco (34). Recently, it was reported that LTP6-overexpression up-regulated potyvirus infection in potato by inhibiting the expression of genes in the RNA silencing pathway (35).

In this study of the mechanism of plant recognition of CPMV 24KPro, we identified a cowpea LTP1 that interacts with 24KPro and inhibits the proteolytic activity of 24KPro both in vitro and in vivo. Overexpression of LTP1 repressed CPMV accumulation in cowpea leaves. Upon CPMV infection, LTP1 is relocated from apoplast to intracellular regions of infected cells. We further show that LTP1-transgenic tobacco plants had reduced accumulation of SMV. LTP1 also repressed SMV NIa cleavage activity in tobacco. We conclude that cowpea LTP1 plays a role in plant defense against CPMV by directly inhibiting viral cysteine protease activity.

Results

Cowpea LTP1 Interacts with CPMV 24KPro during CPMV Infection.

To identify host proteins that interact with the elicitor CPMV 24KPro, a cowpea cDNA library was constructed from total RNA of leaves collected at 0 hpi, 24 hpi, 48 hpi, and 72 hpi with CPMV. The cDNA library was screened by yeast two hybrid (Y2H) using 24KPro as the bait, and we identified a set of 286 candidate interacting proteins. In parallel, by immunoprecipitation coupled to mass spectrometry (IP-MS), a separate set of 432 candidate proteins was identified. The two sets had 11 protein candidates in common, including eukaryotic translation initiation factors eIF1A, eIF3B, and eIF3L, 26S proteasome regulatory subunit N3, ubiquitin carboxyl-terminal hydrolase 7, pathogenesis-related protein Bet_v_1 family, harpin-induced protein-like-related, leucine-rich-repeat-containing protein, leucine-rich-repeat-containing protein family, disease resistance family protein/leucine-rich-repeat family protein-related, and LTP1. To validate the interaction between 24KPro and candidate proteins, split-luciferase complementation (Split-Luc) assays were performed by fusing the coding sequences of 11 candidate genes with the N-terminal portion of luciferase (NLuc) and fusing 24KPro or enzymatically inactive 24KPro(C166A) with the C-terminal portion of luciferase (CLuc). Results showed that nine proteins interacted with both 24KPro and 24KPro(C166A), whereas LTP1 was the only protein interacting with 24KPro but not with 24KPro(C166A). The 26S proteasome regulatory subunit N3 did not interact with either 24KPro or 24KPro(C166A) (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1).

The 24KPro activity was important for CPMV replication, and 24KPro could elicit hypersensitive response in resistant cowpea cultivars (12). We focused on LTP1, the specific 24KPro interactor, for the roles in plant–virus interaction. Protein sequence alignment showed that LTP1s from 17 plant species contain two consensus pentapeptides (T/S-X-X-D-R/K and P-Y-X-I-S) that have been proposed to be important for lipid binding (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A) (36). Cowpea LTP1 also has eight conserved cysteine residues and Cys residues of plant LTPs could form four disulfide bonds in a fixed pattern (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A) (37). Phylogenetic analysis demonstrated that cowpea LTP1 is a member of lipid transfer proteins (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). SignalP 5.0 predicted that LTP1 has a signal peptide (SP) of 25 amino acid residues at the N terminus. Transient expression of LTP1-GFP in control and CPMV-infected tobacco plants resulted in the production of two proteins, and we speculated that the two proteins are LTP1-GFP and LTP1ΔSP-GFP (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). LTP1ΔSP-GFP was analyzed by Edman sequencing, which revealed that the N-terminal sequence started with ITCGQ, consistent with the SignalP5.0 prediction result (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 B and C).

The signal peptide might mediate the entry of LTP1 into the endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-mediated secretory pathway, which would generate LTP1ΔSP after removal of the signal peptide. The interaction between 24KPro and LTP1 was further analyzed by Y2H and coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays. In the Y2H assay, LTP1ΔSP interacted with 24KPro but not with 24KPro(C166A) (Fig. 1B). In the Co-IP assay, Flag-tagged 24KPro or 24KPro(C166A) and Strep-tagged LTP1 or LTP1ΔSP were transiently coexpressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves. Signals for 24KPro-Flag, but not for 24KPro(C166A)-Flag, was clearly observed in both LTP1-Strep and LTP1ΔSP-Strep immunoprecipitated samples (Fig. 1C). We also predicted the interaction between LTP1ΔSP and 24KPro with AlphaFold2. The N-terminal helix of LTP1ΔSP contains predicted interface residues (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D). We further used magnetic beads to pull-down 24KPro and found that mainly the processed LTP1ΔSP, not the precursor LTP1, coimmunoprecipitated (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Notably, upon CPMV infection, LTP1 transcript abundance was significantly increased and reached the highest level at 24 hpi and then gradually decreased at 48 hpi and 72 hpi (Fig. 1D).

To confirm the authenticity of the interaction between LTP1 and 24KPro in vivo, we performed a bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay in N. benthamiana leaves with and without CPMV infection. Weak fluorescence signals were observed in tobacco leaves coexpressing LTP1-YFPN and 24KPro-YFPC in the absence of CPMV. However, we observed dramatically enhanced fluorescence signals upon CPMV infection (Fig. 1 E and G). YFP fluorescence intensities were constantly low in tobacco leaves coexpressing LTP1-YFPN and 24KPro(C166A)-YFPC regardless of CPMV infection, suggesting that the interaction is largely abolished by the enzymatically inactive 24KPro(C166A) (Fig. 1 E and G). Coexpression of LTP1-YFPN/YFPC, YFPN/24KPro-YFPC, and YFPN/24KPro (C166A)-YFPC were used as negative controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). Western blot analysis confirmed that YFPN fusion proteins and YFPC fusion proteins were successfully expressed in tobacco leaves agroinfiltrated with indicated protein pairs (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). These results indicate that the infection of CPMV promotes LTP1 and 24KPro interaction.

In epidermal cells, other organelles are pushed by vacuoles to the cellular cortical region which is between the plasma membrane and vacuole membrane. To confirm the subcellular localization of LTP1 and 24KPro interaction, PIP2A (plasma membrane intrinsic protein 2A) was used as a plasma membrane marker (38), and RFP-HDEL was used as an ER marker. We infiltrated PIP2A-CFP or RFP-HDEL together with LTP1-YFPN and 24KPro-YFPC in tobacco leaves which were infected with CPMV, and observed the cortical section of epidermal cells. After CPMV infection, yellow fluorescence signals derived from LTP1 and 24KPro interaction were mainly in the cytoplast, and some punctate signals appeared to overlap with RFP-HDEL (Fig. 1F).

LTP1 Inhibits CPMV Accumulation in Cowpea.

PR proteins are important players in plant immunity response, and LTPs are representatives of the PR14 family (39). In order to elucidate functions of LTP1, we examined cowpea phenotypes through reverse genetic approaches. Agrobacterium tumefaciens cells harboring LTP1 overexpressing (OE-LTP1) or RNAi silencing (Ri-LTP1) constructs were infiltrated into cowpea primary leaves. At 12 h after agroinfiltration of OE-LTP1 or 24 h after infiltration of Ri-LTP1, the infiltrated primary leaves were inoculated with CPMV. At 4 d postinoculation, cowpea leaves overexpressing LTP1 produced fewer and smaller chlorotic lesions than control leaves (Fig. 2A), as quantified by lesion area measurements (Fig. 2B). LTP1 transcript and protein abundance were both enhanced after agroinfiltration of OE-LTP1 (Fig. 2C). CPMV accumulation was quantified by qRT-PCR with primers against the small coat protein (sCP) coding region. Cowpea leaves infiltrated for LTP1 overexpression accumulated significantly less CPMV compared to control leaves at 48 hpi and 72 hpi (Fig. 2D), which was also supported by less abundance of sCP in immunoblot analysis (Fig. 2E). These results showed that LTP1 overexpression reduced CPMV accumulation.

In LTP1-silenced cowpea, CPMV-induced lesion area was significantly increased (Fig. 2 F and G). The LTP1 transcript level in Ri-LTP1 cowpea leaves was significantly repressed compared to that of control (Fig. 2H). CPMV accumulation was significantly increased compared to control plants at 48 hpi and 72 hpi (Fig. 2I), which is consistent with more sCP production in Ri-LTP1 plants than in control plants (Fig. 2J). Collectively, the results indicated that LTP1 expression suppressed CPMV accumulation in cowpea.

CPMV Infection Induces LTP1 Localization Change from Apoplast to Intracellular Compartments.

LTP1 was reported to have multiple localizations in cells and to contribute to plant defense against pathogens (39). We investigated the effects of CPMV infection on the LTP1 subcellular localization. Oryza sativa secretory carrier associated membrane protein1 (SCAMP1) was used as a cellular membrane marker (40). In the uninfected tobacco leaves, signal of LTP1-mCherry was located in a region near cellular membranes (SI Appendix, Fig. S6, Upper). N. benthamiana leaves were inoculated with the sap inoculum containing CPMV virions. The systemic leaves which developed CPMV-induced mosaic patterns were agroinfiltrated to transiently express LTP1-mCherry and SCAMP1-GFP. Under this condition, LTP1-mCherry fluorescence signal was partially overlapping with that of chlorophyll autofluorescence (SI Appendix, Fig. S6, Lower). These results indicate that CPMV infection is able to induce a subcellular location change of LTP1. In the presence of a secretory signal peptide (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C), LTP1 is likely transported exo-cellularly by the conventional secretory pathway. To study whether LTP1 could be secreted out of cells, secretory GFP (secGFP) was used as an apoplastic protein (41). When both LTP1-mCherry and secGFP were transiently expressed in N. benthamiana leaves, LTP1-mCherry fluorescence colocalized with secGFP (SI Appendix, Fig. S7, Upper), suggesting that both proteins were secreted into the apoplast. We next treated tobacco leaves with brefeldin A (BFA). BFA is an inhibitor for ER-Golgi protein trafficking and it induces visible aggregates which are small punctate structures derived from the Golgi and trans-Golgi-network (TGN) (42). In the BFA-treated cells, both LTP1-mCherry and secGFP fluorescence signals showed altered locations and were enriched intracellularly (SI Appendix, Fig. S7, Lower), suggesting that secretion of LTP1 is mediated by the ER-Golgi-dependent secretion pathway.

To clearly observe the apoplastic localization of LTP1, tobacco epidermal cells were stained with Calcofluor white stain for cell wall labeling and were plasmolyzed with 20% sucrose to separate cell wall from plasma membrane. In the plasmolyzed epidermal cells expressing LTP1-mCherry and SCAMP1-GFP, the protoplasm was detached from cell wall and LTP1-mCherry was clearly shown to be located outside of plasma membrane and in the apoplast (Fig. 3A). In CPMV-infected tobacco leaves, the three adjacent epidermal cells were plasmolyzed, shown by cell walls separating from cell membranes in the field of view. LTP1-mCherry was not in the apoplast regions but was present in the shrunken protoplasm (Fig. 3B). Hechtian strands, thread-like structures derived from cellular membrane (43), were observed between the plasma membrane and the cell wall (Fig. 3 A and B). In order to determine which subcellular compartment LTP1 protein is associated with in the presence of CPMV, tobacco leaf epidermal cells expressing LTP1-mCherry were viewed under a superresolution confocal microscope. LTP1-mCherry signal was apparently not colocalized with chlorophyll fluorescence. Instead, most of the signal was at the periphery of chloroplast (SI Appendix, Fig. S8).

To further compare the subcellular distribution changes of LTP1, we isolated apoplast and protoplast fractions from N. benthamiana leaf cells transiently coexpressing LTP1 and the apoplast marker, secGFP. We found that the majority of the LTP1 and secGFP proteins were present in the apoplast fraction in the absence of CPMV (Fig. 3 C, Left), which was consistent with LTP1 apoplastic localization shown in Fig. 3A. In contrast, in the tobacco leaves infected by CPMV, the LTP1 protein was more abundant in the protoplast fraction, whereas the secGFP was still mainly enriched in the apoplast fraction (Fig. 3 C, Left). We next separated the protoplast fraction into chloroplast fraction and nonchloroplast fraction. In the absence of CPMV, a small amount of LTP1 and a slightly larger amount of secGFP were retained in nonchloroplast fraction, possibly because of the partial retention of proteins during trafficking of LTP1 and secGFP through the ER-Golgi-dependent secretion pathway (Fig. 3 C, Right). In the presence of CPMV, LTP1 showed an apparent dual localization in both chloroplast and nonchloroplast fractions, whereas CPMV infection had no significant impact on secGFP distribution (Fig. 3 C, Right). Taken together, these results showed that LTP1 was mainly located in apoplast of uninfected cells through the ER-mediated secretory pathway, but most of the LTP1 was retained intracellularly after CPMV infection and partially localized to chloroplasts.

24KPro Regulates Subcellular Distribution of LTP1ΔSP.

Since 24KPro interacts with LTP1ΔSP (Fig. 1 A–C), we next investigated the subcellular location of LTP1ΔSP in the presence and absence of 24KPro. Calnexin homolog 1 (CNX1) of Arabidopsis thaliana was used as an ER marker. We observed that 24KPro protein was in the cytoplasmic compartment in A. thaliana protoplasts (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). In protoplasts expressing LTP1ΔSP, LTP1ΔSP was associated with chloroplasts. However, in protoplasts coexpressing 24KPro and LTP1ΔSP, LTP1ΔSP showed two types of distribution, both colocalized with 24KPro in the cytoplasm, and in the chloroplasts (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). Subcellular fractionation was also performed on extracts of N. benthamiana leaves expressing LTP1ΔSP-GFP or coexpressing LTP1ΔSP-GFP and 24KPro-mCherry. In agreement with subcellular fluorescence observations (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A), immunoblot analyses indicated that LTP1ΔSP-GFP alone was located in chloroplast fraction (SI Appendix, Fig. S9B), but it was in both chloroplast and nonchloroplast subcellular fractions in the presence of 24KPro-mCherry (SI Appendix, Fig. S9C). These results suggest that 24KPro induces subcellular distribution changes of LTP1ΔSP.

LTP1 Inhibits 24KPro Cleavage Activity.

Our results in Fig. 1 showed that LTP1 interacted with enzymatically active 24KPro but not with the inactive 24KPro(C166A), and Fig. 2 demonstrated that LTP1 expression repressed CPMV accumulation in cowpea. These results prompted us to investigate LTP1’s effect on 24KPro enzymatic cleavage activity. As illustrated in Fig. 4A, CPMV genomic RNA1 encodes protease cofactor (ProC), helicase (Hel), viral protein genome-linked (VPg), 24KPro and RNA polymerase (Pol), and genomic RNA2 encodes movement protein (MP), large coat protein (LCP), and small coat protein (sCP). 24KPro was shown to be able to process the viral polyproteins to release mature individual proteins (44, 45). That is, 24KPro itself is able to cleave at the glutamine/glycine (Q/G) site between LCP and sCP in the precursor LCP-sCP to generate mature coat proteins (46) as diagrammed in Fig. 4A.

The acetone extraction method was used to precipitate all proteins from the reactions, and proteins were then analyzed by immunoblot with anti-Strep, anti-Flag, and anti-24KPro antibodies. An asterisk denotes the unknown protein which might be a cleavage product of 6His-LCP-sCP-Flag.

For the in vivo cleavage assay, N. benthamiana leaves agroinfiltrated with LTP1-Strep or empty vector were also agroinfiltrated with A. tumefaciens containing HA-LCP-sCP alone or together with 24KPro-Flag or 24KPro(C166A)-Flag. Total proteins were prepared for immunoblotting with anti-HA, anti-Flag and anti-Strep antibodies. For leaves coexpressing 24KPro and LCP-sCP, LCP but not the full-length LCP-sCP was observed indicating that 24KPro was able to efficiently process precursor LCP-sCP to produce LCP. As a comparison, LCP-sCP was largely intact when being coexpressed with proteolytically inactive 24KPro(C166A) (Fig. 4B). For leaves coexpressing 24KPro, LTP1, and LCP-sCP, the majority of the full-length LCP-sCP was retained, and only little LCP was accumulated, indicating that LTP1 drastically inhibited the cleavage activity of 24KPro (Fig. 4B). Collectively, these data indicate that LTP1 efficiently inhibits 24KPro cleavage activity on the precursor LCP-sCP in planta.

We further analyzed the impact of LTP1 on 24KPro enzymatic activity in vitro. His-tagged 24KPro and His-tagged LCP-sCP proteins were expressed and purified from Escherichia coli. LTP1-Strep protein was expressed and purified from N. benthamiana leaves. LTP1 protein of increasing concentrations was used in the reaction solution containing 24KPro and LCP-sCP. Immunoblot analysis showed that the higher concentration of LTP1 resulted in a reduced signal intensity of sCP, suggesting that the production of sCP from LCP-sCP was inhibited by LTP1 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4C). A protein band above sCP but below LCP-sCP was also observed and is of unknown origin (Fig. 4C). Both in vivo and in vitro assays convincingly support a role of LTP1 in inhibiting 24KPro protease cleavage activity.

LTP1 Represses Accumulation of SMV.

As LTP1 repressed CPMV accumulation (Fig. 2) and inhibited 24KPro protease activity (Fig. 4), we next examined whether LTP1 could inhibit other plant viruses encoding proteases by using stable transgenic N. benthamiana plants expressing LTP1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S10A). The SMV (a potyvirus) genome encodes the cysteine protease NIa, which is closely related to the CPMV 24KPro protease (4). A. tumefaciens containing viral infectious clone of SMV-GFP was infiltrated into leaves of two independent tobacco lines expressing LTP1 (OE1 and OE2), using untransformed wild-type (WT) plants as control. At 4 dpi with SMV-GFP, the GFP fluorescence signals were remarkably reduced in the leaves of OE1 and OE2 compared to WT plants (SI Appendix, Fig. S10B). Consistently, transcript levels of the SMV gene encoding capsid protein were significantly lower in OE1 and OE2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S10C). At 9 dpi, in WT tobacco, the fluorescence was observed in both inoculated and systemic leaves. In contrast, SMV-GFP fluorescence was observed only in the agroinfiltrated leaves but not in the systemic leaves of OE1 and OE2 lines (SI Appendix, Fig. S10B). These results show that SMV accumulation and systemic spread were significantly suppressed in LTP1-expressing tobacco plants.

LTP1 Inhibits SMV NIa Protease Activity.

Since LTP1 protein inhibited CPMV 24KPro protease activity (Fig. 4) and LTP1-transgenic tobacco plants inhibited SMV accumulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S10), we posit that LTP1 may result in reduced SMV reproduction by repressing SMV NIa protease activity. SMV NIa protease contains catalytic triad His, Asp, and Cys. It recognizes the conserved motif (E or N)XVXXQ/(S or G) and cleaves between Q/S and Q/G (47, 48). LTP1 could interact with the wild-type NIa, and the interaction with the enzymatically inactive mutant NIa(C152A) was dramatically reduced in the Split-Luc complementation assay (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). To test for LTP1 inhibition of NIa protease activity, a modified Aequorea victoria GFP protein was designed to incorporate a cleavage site of SMV NIa, a sequence of seven amino acids (ESVALQS) after the 25th amino acid of GFP as the N-terminal 25 amino acids form the first α-helix and the first β-strand of the tertiary structure (49).

In the absence of NIa, the fluorescence intensity of GFP(ESVALQS) was significantly reduced compared to GFP in both WT and LTP1-expressing tobacco plants [compare EV1+GFP(ESVALQS) to EV1+GFP, Fig. 5 A and B], implying that the GFP fluorescence level was diminished by the inserted ESVALQS sequence. For NIa and GFP coexpression, there was no significant difference in fluorescence intensity between OE1 (or OE2) and WT (Fig. 5 A and B), showing that LTP1 and NIa did not influence GFP fluorescence. In contrast, for NIa and GFP(ESVALQS) coexpression, the fluorescence intensity of WT was significantly lower than that of OE1 and OE2 lines (Fig. 5 A and B), suggesting that NIa remarkably decreased GFP(ESVALQS) fluorescence and LTP1 rescued the fluorescence effectively. These observations indicated that LTP1 inhibited NIa protease activity and GFP(ESVALQS) fluorescence was therefore preserved.

We further analyzed protein accumulation in WT and OE1/OE2 lines agroinfiltrated with GFP-HA, GFP(ESVALQS)-HA and NIa-Flag by immunoblot analyses (Fig. 5C). In WT and OE1/OE2 lines transiently coexpressing GFP-HA and NIa-Flag, full-length GFP was detected (Fig. 5 C, Upper). In WT tobacco leaves expressing both NIa-Flag and GFP(ESVALQS)-HA, we observed only one band, which we interpret as being truncated GFP(ESVALQS) derived from the full-length GFP(ESVALQS) after cleavage by NIa (Fig. 5 C, Lower). Notably, in OE1/OE2 lines expressing both NIa-Flag and GFP(ESVALQS)-HA, both full-length and truncated GFP(ESVALQS) were detected (Fig. 5 C, Lower). These results support the notion that LTP1 inhibits NIa cleavage of GFP(ESVALQS), resulting in stable accumulation of the full-length GFP(ESVALQS).

Discussion

Plants produce protein and peptide protease inhibitors (PIs) that are active against serine, cysteine, or glutamic acid proteinases. Many of these PIs have been demonstrated to defend the plant against pathogens and pests (50). There are more than 6,700 known plant PIs. These are classified into 12 main families (51, 52). PIs inhibit growth of pathogenic bacteria and fungi and retard insect larval development (53). For virus infections, PI’s ability to increase plant resistance to potyviruses has been extensively tested. Rice cystatin reduced PVY and tobacco etch virus (TEV) titers in transgenic tobacco plants (54). Transgenic potato overexpressing a potato aspartic protease inhibitor decreased PVY and potato virus A (PVA) replication (18). Although the resistance to potyvirus was achieved, viral protease processing activity in these transgenic plants was not characterized. Phytocystatin, a cysteine protease inhibitor from several plant species, weakly inhibited plum pox virus (PPV) HC-Pro but not PPV NIa-Pro, suggesting that phytocystatin did not directly inhibit the main potyvirus protease during virus replication (55). Here, we searched directly for host proteins that interact with the only protease of CPMV, 24KPro, and found LTP1, a lipid transfer protein that acts as an inhibitor of 24KPro and throttles down the accumulation of CPMV.

Functional Interactions of LTP1 with Plant Virus-Encoded Proteases.

Of 286 24KPro interactors identified by Y2H and 432 interactors identified by IP-MS, 11 candidates were in common. Only one of these, LTP1, reacted specifically with the cysteine active site, i.e., catalytically active, 24KPro both in vivo and in vitro (Fig. 1). Although the association of LTP1 with 24KPro required the active site cysteine C166, neither LTP1 nor LTP1ΔSP appeared to be cleaved by 24KPro (Figs. 1C and 4B). Using the precursor of the two CPMV coat proteins as a substrate, the LTP1–24KPro interaction resulted in an inhibition of 24KPro protease activity both in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 4). As expected from the 24KPro inhibition results, transient over expression of LTP1 or transient reduction of LTP1 accumulation by RNAi resulted in, respectively, suppressed or enhanced symptoms and CPMV virion accumulation (Fig. 2). Similarly, using an artificial substrate of GFP with an embedded SMV NIa protease cleavage site, LTP1 inhibited SMV NIa activity (Fig. 5) and strongly reduced SMV accumulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S10) in N. benthamiana leaves. LTP activity against other proteases has been reported. In ginkgo seeds, GbLTP1 inhibited pepsin and papain activity in vitro (56). This study reports LTP inhibition of virus proteases. That the small (10 kDa) LTPs should be able to display such a diversity of activities remains to be understood in mechanistic terms.

LTP1 Accumulation and Altered Localization upon CPMV Infection.

Plant LTPs have been observed in the cell wall, cell membrane, plastid, vacuole, and glyoxysome (39, 57, 58). In the apoplast, wheat lipid transfer protein 3 (TaLTP3) formed a TaLTP3–TaPR1a complex that was associated with an enhanced defense response against Puccinia triticina (29). In the apoplast, interaction between LTPs and some ligands can trigger the activation of plant defense responses against bacteria and fungi (58).

If LTP1 is a factor in plant defense against viruses, the accumulation of LTP1 and/or its subcellular location might be altered upon virus infections. When LTP1 mRNA levels were assessed by RT-qPCR at 24 h intervals after inoculation of CPMV to cowpea primary leaves, a ~20-fold increase was observed at 24 hpi, with lesser accumulation at later time (Fig. 1D). In this study, cowpea LTP1 was shown to have an N-terminal secretory signal peptide, and the signal peptide is most likely processed in the secretory pathway, producing LTP1ΔSP. In uninfected plants, LTP1 transient overexpression produced LTP1 which was mainly located in the apoplast (Fig. 3A). After CPMV infection, abundance of the apoplastic LTP1 decreased and more LTP1 was accumulated in the intracellular region, including chloroplasts.

Furthermore, LTP1ΔSP was mainly associated with chloroplasts when transiently expressed in Arabidopsis protoplasts. When coexpressed with 24KPro, LTP1ΔSP was localized in both chloroplast and nonchloroplast fractions (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). CPMV infection induced ER membrane proliferation, and virus replication is associated with these ER-derived membrane vesicles in the cytoplasm (59). Although the mechanism for LTP1 intracellular accumulation in CPMV-infected cells is unknown, it is possible that CPMV-induced ER structure modification affects LTP1ΔSP secretion, and LTP1ΔSP therefore remained in the cytoplast where it interacts with 24KPro. As a result, 24KPro proteolytic activity was repressed and virus reproduction was attenuated (Figs. 2 and 4).

LTP1 Localization to Chloroplast.

Chloroplasts import most of their proteins from cytosol via canonical TOC/TIC machineries (60). Some chloroplast proteins use alternative import pathways such as a pathway involving the endomembrane system and internal targeting signals (61). After CPMV infection, a portion of LTP1 was observed to be associated with chloroplast (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Since LTP1 does not contain chloroplast transit peptide, it is unclear how LTP1 could be transported to chloroplasts. Chloroplasts are crucial players in coordinating defensive responses during plant–pathogen interactions. Under various biological stresses, chloroplasts communicate with the nucleus through retrograde signals, leading to the expression of defense-related genes, including those responsible for the biosynthesis of plant hormones such as SA and JA (62–64). It is possible that chloroplast-associated LTP1ΔSP might participate in the plant defensive response. The specific location of LTP1ΔSP either inside the chloroplast or at the periphery of outer envelope membrane, and the mechanism of LTP1ΔSP redistribution to chloroplast and its ensuing function await further investigations.

In conclusion, our data indicate that in the absence of CPMV, the expression level of LTP1 is low, and LTP1 follows the protein secretion route through ER and Golgi. During this process, the signal peptide is removed, resulting in the transfer of LTP1ΔSP to the apoplast. After CPMV infection, LTP1 expression is dramatically induced. More LTP1ΔSP is present in the intracellular region compared to noninfected plant cells. In the infected plant cells, LTP1ΔSP interacts with CPMV 24KPro to inhibit 24KPro cleavage activity, resulting in repression of virus replication.
