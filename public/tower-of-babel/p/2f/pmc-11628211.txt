Agenesis of the Ductus Venosus and Its Association With Genetic Abnormalities
Authors: Yarin Mash, Ron Bardin, Yinon Gilboa, Yossi Geron, Asaf Romano, Eran Hadar, Dana Brabbing Goldstein, Bella Davidov, Ohad Houri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo investigate the association of agenesis of the ductus venosus (ADV) with genetic abnormalities using genetic studies—Chromosomal Microarray Analysis (CMA) and Exome Sequencing (ES).DesignRetrospective study of all fetuses diagnosed with ADV between January 2013 and December 2022 in a tertiary center.ResultsADV was diagnosed in 33 fetuses. The diagnosis was made at a mean gestational age of 21.2 ± 8.4 weeks. Conventional karyotype was applied in a single fetus (3.0%), CMA was applied in 21 fetuses (66.7%), and five fetuses (22.8%) were additionally tested with ES. ADV was isolated in eight fetuses (24%), whereas in 25 (76%) it was associated with abnormal ultrasound findings, including increased nuchal translucency (NT), intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) and variable structural malformations, mostly cardiac (42%) followed by central nervous system (CNS) and skeletal malformations (24%). Genetic abnormalities were found in six fetuses out of 22 investigated (27%), of which 3 were detected by ES, 3 by CMA and 1 by conventional karyotype. A higher incidence of genetic aberrations was evident among ADVs associated with abnormal ultrasound findings. Genetic abnormalities were indicative of Prader Willi/Angelman syndrome, Noonan syndrome, CASK related disorder, 16q24.3 microdeletion syndrome and Trisomy 21.ConclusionADV associated with abnormal ultrasound findings is commonly correlated with genetic abnormalities and consequently unfavorable pregnancy outcomes. Our study emphasizes the value of genetic studies chiefly among cases associated with abnormal ultrasound findings, enabling early diagnosis of fetal pathologies associated with ADV, and providing better parental counseling.

Background

The ductus venosus (DV) is a unique fetal vascular structure constituting one of the three pivotal physiological shunts through which oxygenated blood circumvents the fetal portal system to enter the heart [1]. Agenesis of the DV (ADV) is a rare congenital anomaly, with a variable prevalence of 0.04%–0.6% [2]. With ADV, blood flows from the umbilical vein through an aberrant vessel that may be extrahepatic, bypassing the liver, or intrahepatic via the portal venous system [3, 4, 5]. ADV is known to be associated with an increased risk for genetic abnormalities, fetal malformations [6], and adverse perinatal outcomes including fetal growth restriction (FGR) and stillbirth [7]. However, genetic abnormalities associated with ADV are hypothesized to be underdiagnosed using conventional cytogenetic studies [8, 9]. In recent years, advancements in genetic testing, namely Chromosomal Microarray Analysis (CMA) and Exome Sequencing (ES), have allowed for comprehensive analysis of the fetal genome, enhancing the prenatal diagnosis of genetic abnormalities associated with ADV. Despite the potential of genetic techniques in the research of ADV, there is a paucity of genetic studies addressing ADV cases in the literature. A recent case series regarding the genetic abnormalities associated with ADV in the exome era was reported [10] in which ES was used in a single case. Our study aimed to investigate genetic abnormalities associated with ADV with the integration of CMA and ES into clinical practice.

Methods

A retrospective cohort study of all fetuses diagnosed prenatally with ADV between January 2013 and December 2022 in our tertiary center.

Study Population

All pregnancies who attended the routine screening were considered eligible. Inclusion criteria were the availability of the first (11–14 weeks) and second (17–22 weeks) trimester ultrasound scan results, including the DV assessment, and the pregnancy outcome. Pregnancies without follow‐up or those with incomplete files were excluded.

Data Collection

Medical records were extracted from the hospital's computerized database. Evaluation of the DV is performed in all women as part of routine anatomical screening, in both low‐ and high‐risk pregnancies; as well as during targeted anomaly scans, for women referred to our ultrasound unit for targeted scans. When ADV was diagnosed in our unit, a full anatomical scan was performed by fetal ultrasound specialist and, in addition, an echocardiogram was made by a pediatric cardiologist. All were referred to genetic consultation, and genetic studies were recommended in all cases, including conventional karyotype, CMA and ES.

We extracted data on gestational age at ADV diagnosis, obstetrical complications, associated abnormal ultrasound findings or genetic findings, pregnancy outcomes as well as complications—namely termination of pregnancy, spontaneous abortion, stillbirth, preterm delivery, and mode of delivery.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SAS software version 34.0 (SAS Corp., Cary, North Carolina, USA). Descriptive statistics are presented by number and percentage for categorical variables and mean and standard deviation for continuous variables.

Ethics

The study was approved by the local Institutional Review Board (049–20‐RMC). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design of the study.

Results

ADV was diagnosed in 33 fetuses. Mean gestational age at diagnosis was 21.2 ± 8.4 weeks (range 12–38 Gestational weeks). In 23 cases (70%) ADV was identified as part of routine anatomy screening, while in 10 (30%) at targeted fetal anomaly scan due to other suspected malformations. Isolated ADV Versus ADV Associated With Abnormal Ultrasound Findings

ADV was isolated in eight cases (24%), whereas in 25 cases (76%) it was associated with abnormal ultrasound findings, including increased NT above 3 mm (24%, n = 6), IUGR (36%, n = 9) and/or structural malformations evident in 17 fetuses (85%). Cardiac anomalies were diagnosed in 15 fetuses (88%) with ventricular septal defect (VSD) being the most common malformation within this category, as detected in five cases (29%). ADV Associated With Genetic Abnormalities

Of the 33 cases of ADV in our study, 22 women (67%) underwent invasive prenatal genetic testing, 17 (77%) by amniocentesis (AC), and 5 (23%) by chorionic villus sampling (CVS). Overall, one fetus (5%) was tested with conventional karyotype, while 21 fetuses (95%) were tested with CMA. Of them, 5 (24%) were additionally tested with ES (Table 3). Among the eight cases of isolated ADV, 6 (75%) were tested with CMA without any genetic abnormalities detected. Out of these six cases, one (17%) continued for ES without genetic abnormalities detected. Among the 25 cases of ADV associated with abnormal ultrasound findings, 16 (64%) underwent genetic studies. 15 (94%) were tested with CMA, of which two fetuses (13%) had abnormal CMA, revealing genetic abnormalities corresponding with genetic syndromes: 15q11.2‐q13.1 microdeletion (Case 1) and 16q24.3 microdeletion (case 2).

Pregnancy Outcomes

In our study, 18 live births (54%) were recorded: six cases (33%) of isolated ADV and 12 (67%) with associated abnormal ultrasound findings; Termination of pregnancy (TOP) opted in nine cases (27%)–eight cases (40%) were among ADV associated with abnormal ultrasound findings, compared with a single case (8%) in the group of isolated ADV. In the group of associated ADV were also recorded, one case of stillbirth (4%) and one (4%) late spontaneous abortion before 22 gestational weeks. Among the 18 women who had a live birth, 14 deliveries (78%) were at term and 4 before term (22%), ranging between 29 + 3 to 30 + 6 gestational weeks. The mode of delivery was vaginal delivery in 11 women (61%) and cesarean delivery in 7 women (39%) (Figure 1).

Discussion

Our study aimed to investigate the association of ADV with structural and genetic abnormalities, focusing on genetic testing (CMA and ES). Among 33 cases of ADV, 8 were isolated, while 25 were associated with abnormal ultrasound findings, including increased NT, IUGR and multiple structural malformations.

Advances in genetic techniques have improved our ability to assess genetic abnormalities in cases of ADV undetectable by conventional testing modalities such as conventional karyotype. However, previous studies lacked data related to the application of prenatal genetic studies of genetic abnormalities associated with fetal ADV [11, 12]. Moaddab et al. [13] published a systematic review of the literature in 2015 describing 259 fetuses with ADV associated with genetic abnormalities as detected by conventional karyotyping performed in 154 cases (59.4%). In 38 fetuses (24.7%), chromosomal abnormality was detected: monosomy X (n = 11, 28.9%), trisomy 21 (n = 10, 26.3%), chromosomal deletions (n = 7, 18.4%), trisomy 18 (n = 6, 15.8%), Robertsonian translocations (n = 2, 5.2%), trisomy 22 (n = 1, 2.6%) and trisomy 13 (n = 1, 2.6%). Pacheco et al. [11] published in 2017 the largest literature review from publications spanning 25 years. The study included 410 fetuses with ADV, of which 70 (17.1%) were isolated and 340 (82.9%) were associated with other abnormalities. Conventional karyotype was performed in 141 cases (41.4%) of which 48 (34%) were abnormal. However, none of these studies included either CMA or ES genetic evaluation. McBrien et al. [10] had the only publication incorporating CMA and ES into the genetic workup of ADV. They described their experience in a small retrospective series of 14 cases in 2020. Only 8 fetuses (57.1%) were tested by conventional karyotype, CMA, or ES. All had associated genetic or structural anomalies. In total, six cases of those tested (75%) were found to have genetic abnormalities. Only two cases of those tested (25%) had aneuploidy, 3 (38%) had single gene variants, of which two were detected by gene panel testing for Noonan syndrome and one was diagnosed through ES (RASA1). Remarkably, in our study, CMA was the standard of care and was employed in all but one instance of ADV in which prenatal genetic testing was performed. Genetic testing enabled the identification of genetic syndromes not listed elsewhere in the literature as associated with ADV, and which would not be detected by conventional karyotyping alone—15q11.2‐q13.1 microdeletion and chromosome 16q24.3 microdeletion syndromes—rare chromosomal abnormalities associated with variable developmental delay, facial dysmorphism, seizures and autistic spectrum disorder [12, 14]. Trisomy 21, which we also identified in our study, is already described in the literature as ADV‐associated [11].

Moreover, we report three cases (14%) among the 21 analyzed with CMA, in which CMA was reported normal, and the LP/P variants were detected only by ES, emphasizing the importance of enhanced genetic studies [15]. Using ES allowed the diagnosis of MICPCH and XL‐ID—a rare syndrome characterized by microcephaly, pontine and cerebellar hypoplasia, postnatal growth retardation, low weight for length, severe intellectual disability and marked motor retardation [16]. To the best of our knowledge, vascular malformations as demonstrated in the fetus (porto‐systemic shunt and ADV) were not previously reported as a part of CASK related syndromes. Therefore, it appears to be an incidental finding that does not explain the fetal phenotype [17]. BRAF [18] and RIT1 P/LP variants correlated with Noonan syndrome/CFC syndrome (Rasopathies), previously described in recent studies in the context of ADV [11], and were also detected in our study using ES. In our cohort, all the cases diagnosed with a genetic disorder had associated ultrasound findings. Therefore, non‐isolated ADV should be offered extended genetic testing. Non‐isolated ADV is associated with genetic disorders, mainly aneuploidy and RASopathies. Widespread use of advanced genetic testing will diagnose these conditions but may also identify other diseases not yet linked to ADV. This could expand current knowledge about the connection between this finding and additional genetic diseases, as possibly suggested by our sample.

In our cohort, 25 fetuses with ADV (76%) were diagnosed with associated abnormal sonographic findings. Among this population, we found variable structural malformations to be associated with ADV, of which cardiac anomalies exhibited the highest prevalence. This finding is also supported by larger studies addressing ADV‐associated structural anomalies [19, 20].

We also found that prenatal diagnosis of non‐isolated ADV is linked to less favorable pregnancy outcomes and a high rate of pregnancy termination. This may be due to the presence of associated fetal anomalies, which impacts parental decision making and is similar to previous studies [4, 9, 10, 11]. Of note, the prognosis for fetuses with isolated ADV is generally favorable, with a lower risk of detecting genetic aberrations.

The mean time of ADV diagnosis in our study was during mid gestation, as part of routine prenatal screening and/or targeted fetal anatomy scans in cases where earlier findings of structural abnormalities were evident. This is in accordance with previous studies [13] reporting a mean GA of 22.9 ± 6.9 weeks at the time of ADV diagnosis. It is worth mentioning, however, that larger studies demonstrated the ability to detect ADV already earlier in gestation during the first trimester when systematically evaluated [5]. However, data in the literature on the efficiency of first trimester screening in diagnosing ADV is sparse, and the prevalence of the anomaly is not clear [21]. The late diagnosis was also probably because our ultrasound unit is a tertiary center where women are referred for dedicated anatomical scanning following the routine anatomical scan in community centers [19, 20, 21, 22, 23].

The strength of our study is the meticulously selected population of cases with ADV, of which most had genetic testing. In addition, pregnancy follow‐up by multidisciplinary staff, including specialized ultrasound obstetricians, clinical geneticists, pediatricians and other specialists in our center, helps to evaluate the cases better. The primary limitation of our study lies in its retrospective nature, which may introduce referral bias. It is plausible that we did not capture all instances of ADV within our population, potentially missing cases that remained undiagnosed. Another significant limitation is the small study size, particularly those cases tested by ES. Furthermore, some of our patients chose not to undergo genetic testing despite the high risk of chromosomal or monogenic disorder due to multiple structural findings. This could lead to probable underestimation of genetic conditions. Further studies with a larger number of participants are needed to confirm our results. ADV is, however, a rare finding, and therefore a multi‐center study may be a better approach in the future.

Conclusion

We demonstrated that 75% of cases of ADV were identified with additional abnormal ultrasound findings, including increased NT, IUGR, and other structural fetal abnormalities. Isolated ADV in our study was not detected with genetic aberrations, whereas ADV with abnormal sonographic findings displayed a 37.5% incidence of genetic conditions with various phenotypes. It is, however, worth mentioning that only 1 of 8 fetuses (12.5%) in the isolated ADV group was analyzed by ES, compared with 4 of 25 fetuses (16%) in the group of ADV associated with abnormal ultrasound findings. Several genetic abnormalities in cases with ADV cannot be detected using CMA or conventional karyotype alone, emphasizing the diagnostic yield of ES. As ADV is subsequently linked with unfavorable pregnancy outcomes, early identification of fetal pathologies affiliated with ADV may provide better parental counseling.
