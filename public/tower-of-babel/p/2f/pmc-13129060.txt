Impact of Risk Factor Combinations on Severe COVID-19 Outcomes Among Outpatients
Authors: Haley J. Appaneal, Vrishali V. Lopes, Laura Puzniak, Kristen E. Allen, Evan J. Zasowski, Aisling R. Caffrey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSeveral risk factors for progression to severe COVID-19 have been identified, but whether multiple concomitant risk factors (risk stacking) further increase risk remains unknown. We sought to identify outpatient populations at the highest risk for severe COVID-19 using risk factor stacking.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of Veterans Affairs (VA) outpatient adults (≥ 18 years) at high risk for severe COVID-19 with a positive SARS-CoV-2 test or COVID-19 diagnosis between April 1, 2022 and August 21, 2024. Severe COVID-19 was defined as COVID-19-related hospitalization or all-cause mortality within 30 days. Logistic regression models were used to quantify the impact of combinations of multiple risk factors (stacked risks) on severe COVID-19. Risk factors, selected based on prevalence and CDC criteria, included age ≥ 50 years, chronic lung disease, cancer, neurological conditions/cerebrovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, chronic liver disease, diabetes mellitus, heart conditions, mental health conditions, obesity, smoking, immunosuppression, hypertension, and not being up to date on COVID-19 vaccination.ResultsAmong 379,144 VA outpatients with COVID-19, 1.5% (5801) had severe outcomes within 30 days. Risk increased with each additional factor from two (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 2.10, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.37–3.23) to 14 risk factors (aOR 43.49, 95% CI 4.94–382.66) compared to one. Overall, 239 significant risk factor combinations were identified. Hypertension appeared in 196 combinations (82.0%), not up-to-date on COVID-19 vaccination in 173 combinations (72.4%), smoking in 168 combinations (70.3%), and heart conditions in 154 combinations (64.4%). The combination with the highest risk (aOR 35.25, 95% CI 16.06–77.37) included age ≥ 50 years, chronic lung disease, neurological conditions/cerebrovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, heart conditions, smoking, hypertension, and not being up-to-date on COVID-19 vaccination.ConclusionsIn this large national cohort, severe COVID-19 risk increased with the number of risk factors present. Patients with multiple overlapping risk factors who were not vaccinated were at the highest risk for severe COVID-19. As recommendations have shifted toward risk-based clinical decision-making, identifying patients with particularly high-risk combinations of conditions is increasingly important to support targeted vaccination and timely antiviral treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-026-01328-1.

Introduction

COVID-19, caused by the SARS-CoV-2 virus, causes a spectrum of disease from asymptomatic infection and mild respiratory tract infection to severe pneumonia with acute respiratory illness and multiorgan dysfunction [1]. There are several well-established risk factors for progression to severe COVID-19, including older age, not being vaccinated or remaining up-to-date on vaccination, and certain medical conditions, such as chronic lung disease, heart disease, and a weakened immune system [2]. Examining the risk conferred by individual comorbidities offers only a limited understanding of their impact on clinical outcomes in COVID-19. While a single comorbidity may increase the risk of severe outcomes, combinations of comorbidities often interact in ways that significantly amplify the overall risk (risk stacking) [3]. Understanding this combined impact is essential to fully understanding the heightened vulnerability of certain patients with COVID-19 and underscores the need for further research to better define how specific comorbidity combinations influence clinical outcomes. We sought to quantify how combinations of 14 CDC-defined risk factors influence the probability of 30-day severe COVID-19 among high-risk outpatients and to identify specific high-risk multimorbidity profiles.

Methods

Design and Study Population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study among adults (≥ 18 years of age) at high-risk for severe COVID-19 in the VA with a positive SARS-CoV-2 test or COVID-19 diagnosis (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification [ICD-10] code U07.1) between April 1, 2022 and August 21, 2024 in the outpatient setting. Patients admitted to the hospital on the same day as SARS-CoV-2 positive test or COVID-19 diagnosis were excluded. Only “active” VA users defined as having at least one VA Healthcare System visit in the preceding 12 months were included. If a patient had multiple COVID-19 episodes, we included the first qualifying COVID-19 episode only. The index date was defined as the date of first positive SARS-CoV-2 test date or COVID-19 diagnosis (if no positive SARS-CoV-2 test was recorded). Those without any risk factors for progression to severe COVID-19, as categorized by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) were excluded [2].

Ethical Approval

Our study complies with all relevant ethical regulations and was determined to be exempt by the VA Providence Healthcare System (VAPHS) Institutional Review Board (IRB) and approved by the VAPHS Research and Development Committee. As this was a retrospective study of existing health records and exempt from IRB review, informed consent requirements are not applicable.

Outcome

The outcome of interest was severe COVID-19 defined as either COVID-19-related hospitalization or all-cause mortality within 30 days of the index date. COVID-19-related hospitalization was defined as hospitalization with a primary admission diagnosis code for COVID-19. Mortality data were obtained from VA administrative sources that integrate death information from multiple federal systems, including VA benefits records, Medicare data, and Social Security death records.

Exposure Variables

We quantified the impact of individual, as well as combinations of multiple risk factors (stacked risks), on severe COVID-19. Selected risk factors were those that commonly occur among the Veteran population and are associated with severe COVID-19 or were categorized as higher-risk medical conditions with conclusive evidence for progression to severe COVID-19 as categorized by the CDC [2, 4, 5]. All risk factors were defined dichotomously (either with or without the factor of interest). The risk factors included (1) age ≥ 50 years, (2) chronic lung disease (asthma and chronic lung diseases), (3) cancer, (4) neurological conditions/cerebrovascular disease, (5) chronic kidney disease, (6) chronic liver disease, (7) diabetes mellitus (type 1 and type 2), (8) heart conditions, (9) mental health conditions (mood disorders, schizophrenia, and post-traumatic stress disorder), (10) obesity, (11) smoking (current and former), (12) immunosuppression (immunosuppressive conditions, immunosuppressive medications, and HIV), (13) hypertension, and (14) not being up-to-date on COVID-19 vaccination.

Underlying conditions were identified using ICD-10 codes in the year prior to the index date, as defined by the CDC “Underlying Conditions ICD-10 Code List Methodology” [2, 6]. Smoking was defined as current or former smoker using health factors or ICD10 codes in the prior year. Obesity was defined based on a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m2 or using ICD-10 codes for obesity if BMI was missing. Immunosuppression included immunosuppressive conditions and HIV as per the CDC ICD-10 list and any immunosuppressive medication (alkylating agents, antibiotics, antimetabolites, antimitotics, monoclonal antibodies, other, immune-modulating agents, tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) antagonist, and steroids) with an outpatient days supply or inpatient administration in the 90 days prior to the index date as per a previous algorithm [6, 7]. Up-to-date COVID-19 vaccination status was defined as at least one dose of XBB vaccine if the index date occurred between September 25, 2023 and August 21, 2024, at least one dose of a BA.4/5 bivalent vaccine if the index date occurred between September 1, 2022 and September 24, 2023, or at least three doses of wild-type COVID-19 vaccine if the index date occurred between April 1, 2022 and August 31, 2022. We created all possible combinations of risk factors (n = 16,834) and defined each combination as a unique variable.

Statistical Analyses

We conducted a series of logistic regression analyses to evaluate the impact of individual and combined risk factors on severe COVID-19 outcomes. We first conducted adjusted analyses evaluating the association between the number of risk factors and severe COVID-19 outcomes, using individuals with only one risk factor as the reference group (e.g., individuals with two versus one risk factor, three versus one risk factor, and so on). We then conducted adjusted analyses for all specific combinations of risk factors (eTable 1). Patients were categorized into mutually exclusive exposure groups defined by specific combinations of risk factors, with each exposure group evaluated in a separate adjusted model and compared against a single, common reference group (individuals aged ≥ 50 years with no additional risk factors), while controlling for covariates. To ensure stable and interpretable effect estimates, combination analyses were restricted to groups with sufficient sample size (≥ 100 patients per exposure group of specific risk factor combinations), adequate cell sizes (≥ 5 in all model cells), and successful model convergence. Interpretable estimates were defined as those with finite confidence intervals that were not excessively wide (e.g., greater than 50), and did not reflect instability due to sparse data or near-separation. Adjusted models controlled for sex (male or female), race (white or other/unknown), ethnicity (Hispanic/Latino or non-Hispanic/Latino or unknown), region (Northeast, Midwest, South, or West), and deprivation (yes [15% most deprived based on the area deprivation index] or no).

We conducted analyses stratified by COVID-19 vaccination status (up to date vs. not up to date) to evaluate whether significant risk-factor combinations differed across vaccination strata. Within each stratum, we identified combinations significantly associated with severe COVID-19 outcomes and summarized the frequency of individual risk factors across these significant combinations. Frequencies were compared descriptively to assess similarity in risk-factor patterns by vaccination status. We also conducted sensitivity analyses using a broader definition of severe COVID-19 that included any hospitalization with COVID-19 listed as a primary or secondary diagnosis to mitigate potential outcome misclassification. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software (Version 9.4 and Enterprise Guide 8.3, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Our study included 379,144 high-risk adults with a positive SARS-CoV-2 test or COVID-19 diagnosis between April 1, 2022 and August 21, 2024 in the outpatient setting (eFig. 1). Median age was 65 years (79.3% were ≥ 50 years of age), 86.0% were male, and 66.3% were not up-to-date on COVID-19 vaccination. The most frequent comorbidity was hypertension (57.5%). Overall, 1.5% (5801) had severe COVID-19 within 30 days of the SARS-COV-2 positive test or COVID-19 diagnosis date.

Compared to those without severe COVID-19, those with 30-day severe COVID-19 were older (age ≥ 50 years 94.4% vs. 79.1%; P < 0.001) and more likely to be male (95% vs. 85.9%; P < 0.001). A higher proportion of those with 30-day severe COVID-19 were current or former smokers (66.3% vs. 56.6%; P < 0.001) and were not up-to-date on COVID-19 vaccination (71.7% vs. 66.2%; P < 0.001), compared to those without severe COVID-19. The proportion of those with obesity was lower in those with 30-day severe COVID-19 than those without (32.8% vs. 50.5%; P < 0.001). Combination Prevalence

Of the 8439 unique combinations of risk factors in our cohort, there were 553 combinations with sufficient sample size and stable estimates for analysis, and 239 (43.2%) were statistically significant (reference group age ≥ 50 with no other risk factors). Figure 2 presents a heatmap of all statistically significant risk combinations. The most frequent significant factor in the significant combinations was hypertension in 196 combinations (82.0% of significant combinations), followed by not up-to-date on COVID-19 vaccination in 173 combinations (72.4%), smoking in 168 combinations (70.3%), and heart conditions in 154 combinations (64.4%). Discussion

Our large national cohort study of 379,144 high-risk outpatients with COVID-19 demonstrated that the risk for severe COVID-19 increased incrementally as the number of risk factors increased, from two up to 14. Our findings confirm prior work that severe COVID-19 risk increases with additional underlying conditions; however, those earlier studies assessed only a limited number of risk factors [8–10]. By analyzing combinations of 14 unique risk factors, our study provides one of the most comprehensive evaluations of multimorbidity and risk stacking for severe COVID-19 to date. Our findings extend earlier work by identifying the highest-risk patient populations defined by their combinations of risk factors, including older adults with heart disease, smoking history, hypertension, and lacking up-to-date COVID-19 vaccination, who may benefit most from targeted prevention efforts. Moreover, our findings are consistent with current CDC vaccine guidance, which identifies these 14 risk factors as individually associated with an increased risk of severe COVID-19 and highlights the importance of updated COVID-19 vaccination for these populations [2, 11] .

Nearly all of our cohort (97.3%) had two or more risk factors for severe COVID-19. This is reflective of the Veterans Affairs population, which generally exhibits a higher prevalence of underlying conditions compared with the non-Veteran US population. Multimorbidity has also increased steadily in the general US population over the last 20 years [12, 13]. Data from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System demonstrated that in 2023 over three-quarters (76.4%) of US adults had one or more chronic conditions and over half (51.4%) had two or more conditions [14]. Although the prevalence of chronic conditions increases with age, in 2023 an estimated 27.1% of individuals aged 18–34, 52.7% of those aged 35–65, and 78.8% of adults ≥ 65 years had multiple chronic conditions [14]. Given the increased risk of severe COVID-19 with multimorbidity observed in our study and the steady rise in the prevalence of chronic conditions, it is increasingly important that patients and providers follow recommendations for vaccination and early antiviral treatment to mitigate the risk of severe COVID-19 outcomes, especially among those with multiple chronic conditions.

Our findings align with previous studies, reinforcing the notion that the number of underlying conditions significantly increases risk for severe COVID-19 [8, 9]. In a CDC study of adults across 800 US hospitals from March 2020 to March 2021, the number of underlying conditions increased the risk of death by 1.53 times for one condition (95% CI 1.41–1.67) up to 3.82 times for ten conditions (95% CI 3.45–4.23) compared to those with no underlying medication conditions, with hypertension being the most common condition (50.4%) [8]. International studies have reached similar conclusions [9, 10]. In a large study of patients hospitalized with COVID-19, the rate of COVID-19-related mortality was 17.2% in those without multimorbidity (≤ 1 condition), as compared to 37.2% in those with multimorbidity (≥ 2 conditions), and 40.3% in those with ≥ 4 conditions, and results were consistent even after controlling for age, sex, and deprivation [15]. Among Italian adults hospitalized with COVID-19, the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was significantly higher among those who died compared with survivors (4.3 ± 0.15 vs. 2.6 ± 0.05, p < 0.001) [9]. Although hypertension was the most common condition (54.9%), it was not identified as an independent predictor of mortality. A study in China found that adults hospitalized with COVID-19 had 1.79 (95% CI 1.16–2.77) times higher risk of an intensive care unit (ICU) stay, invasive ventilation, or death, with at least one comorbidity, and a 2.56 (95% CI 1.61–4.17) times higher risk among patients with two or more comorbidities [10]. Furthermore, in this population, hypertension was identified as a risk factor for severe COVID-19 outcomes (HR 1.56, 95% CI 1.07–2.32).

While evidence for hypertension as a risk factor for severe COVID-19 is mixed [6], hypertension consistently ranks as one of the most prevalent underlying conditions across numerous studies [5, 7–17]. In our study, hypertension was the most frequent significant risk factor observed (present in 82.0% of significant combinations). The absence of hypertension as a highlighted independent risk factor in official CDC guidance may result in missed opportunities to identify and manage at-risk patients, particularly in terms of encouraging these patients to stay up-to-date on vaccination and facilitating access to effective COVID-19 antiviral treatment [16].

In addition to hypertension, heart conditions and smoking frequently emerged as key contributors to severe COVID-19 outcomes, in combination with other chronic conditions. Hypertension most frequently overlapped with heart conditions, not-up-to date on COVID-19 vaccination, and smoking (approx. > 57% of combinations). Additionally, other cardiometabolic conditions, such as diabetes mellitus and obesity, were observed in several of the top 20 combinations of risk factors with the highest risk of 30-day severe COVID-19. Previous work has demonstrated that clusters of cardiometabolic conditions are associated with severe COVID-19 [17, 18]. The frequent overlap of these conditions highlights the importance of coordinated approaches to mitigate their collective impact on severe COVID-19 [19].

Nearly two-thirds of our study cohort was not up-to-date on COVID-19 vaccination, and this risk factor was among the most frequently observed in significant combinations. Moreover, all of the top 20 combinations linked to the greatest adjusted 30-day risk of severe COVID-19 included both age ≥ 50 years and not being up to date on vaccination. Maintaining up-to-date COVID-19 vaccination is critical for preventing severe COVID-19 outcomes and should remain a public health priority to mitigate the burden of COVID-19, especially among older patients and those with underlying conditions [18, 20, 21]. An online survey of 1200 previously vaccinated US adults found that among the most common reasons for not receiving a bivalent booster dose were lack of awareness related to eligibility and availability of the vaccine and overconfidence in perceived immunity against COVID-19 infection and severe disease [22]. As such, public health messaging should emphasize not only the availability of updated COVID-19 vaccines but also the importance of staying up to date with vaccination to reduce their risk of hospitalization and death from COVID-19 [21, 23, 24].

In the context of declining COVID-19 vaccination uptake and an increasing emphasis on risk-based clinical decision-making rather than simple eligibility criteria, these findings are intended to inform prioritization of targeted vaccination outreach and implementation efforts by identifying patient groups in whom the burden of severe disease is greatest. Our data highlight that individuals with multiple “stacked” risk factors, who are at the highest risk for severe COVID-19 outcomes and have long been eligible for vaccination under CDC recommendations, remain undervaccinated [2, 11]. This highlights the need for targeted, system-level outreach strategies that use existing clinical and administrative data to identify high-risk, undervaccinated patients and proactively engage them through vaccination reminders and discussions during routine care, including chronic disease management visits.

Several risk scoring systems have been developed to estimate progression to severe COVID-19 among outpatients; however, most rely on relatively simple models that consider a limited number of variables and do not evaluate the impact of multimorbidity or specific combinations of clinical condition [25–30]. Although these tools may appear more feasible for clinical use, many require assessment of symptoms, vital signs, and laboratory values at the time of diagnosis, in addition to demographic and clinical characteristics, which may limit their utility, particularly for patients managing mild infections at home [25–30]. One previously developed risk score that did not require symptom assessment focused on several clinical conditions similar to those examined here, incorporating 13 broad domains and demonstrating associations with hospitalization; however, like other prior scores, it did not assess the role of multimorbidity or specific risk-factor combination [31]. In contrast to risk scores that often show limited transportability across populations and over time, this combination-based approach focuses on CDC-recognized high-risk conditions and identifies specific multimorbidity patterns associated with the highest risk of severe COVID-19 [2].

Our study has several important limitations. First, our study population was defined based on a positive SARS-CoV-2 laboratory test or COVID-19 diagnosis code in the VA setting which can be subject to misclassification or underascertainment, and any resulting non-differential misclassification would likely bias associations toward the null. Testing or diagnosis of COVID-19 that occurred outside of the VA Healthcare system may not have been fully captured. To mitigate this issue, our study focused on active VA users, who are more likely to rely on the VA Healthcare System for their COVID-19 care. Several limitations related to outcome ascertainment should be noted. Severe COVID-19 was defined as COVID-19-related hospitalization or all-cause mortality within 30 days, and inclusion of all-cause mortality may have captured some non-COVID-19-related deaths, potentially attenuating the associations that were observed. Hospitalization-based ascertainment relies on ICD-10 coding, which has demonstrated high specificity, approaching 100% over time, but only moderate sensitivity [32]. Limitations related to risk factor definitions should also be considered. Modeling age as a binary variable (≥ 50 years) aligned with COVID-19 risk-based guidance and preserved interpretability of the risk-stacking framework, but may mask heterogeneity in risk across the age spectrum. Risk factors for severe COVID-19 are not standardized across the literature and, as such, we may not have assessed all risk factors. However, the risk factors we assessed were categorized by the CDC at the highest level of evidence (higher risk with conclusive evidence), except for hypertension, which is categorized by the CDC as “mixed evidence”. Hypertension, especially in combination with other comorbidities, is associated with severe COVID-19 in VA and non-VA populations [33, 34]. Additionally, we expanded upon the CDC’s definition of mental health disorders by including post-traumatic stress disorder, as previous work has confirmed the importance of this risk factor for worse COVID-19 outcomes among Veterans [4, 35]. We relied on ICD-10 codes to identify risk factors, and coding accuracy may vary across conditions. Any resulting misclassification would likely have been non-differential and would be expected to attenuate observed associations. As with all observational studies, unmeasured and residual confounding may have influenced our findings despite controlling for several sociodemographic factors. We did not assess receipt, timing, or effectiveness of COVID-19-directed treatments, including antiviral therapy, nor care-seeking behaviors. As such treatments are preferentially prescribed to patients at higher baseline risk for severe COVID-19, confounding by indication is possible. To the extent that treatment mitigates progression to severe outcomes, preferential treatment of higher-risk patients may have attenuated the associations observed, biasing results toward the null. Evaluation of the interplay between multimorbidity, treatment receipt, and severe COVID-19 outcomes represents an important area for future research. Finally, the generalizability of our findings also may be limited, as the US Veteran population predominantly comprises older, white men.

Conclusion

Our large national cohort study demonstrated that the risk for severe COVID-19 increased with the number of risk factors present. Patient populations 50 years and older, with heart conditions, hypertension, smokers, and not being up-to-date on COVID-19 vaccination were at the highest risk for severe COVID-19. Not being up-to-date on vaccination appeared in 72.4% of significant combinations, underscoring its critical role in mitigating severe COVID-19 outcomes. Heart conditions, smoking, and hypertension emerged as key contributors to adverse outcomes, both individually and in combination with other chronic conditions. The overlap of metabolic conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, and obesity with heart conditions suggests the need for integrated approaches to address these interrelated risks. Given persistent COVID-19 undervaccination even among high-risk population, these findings support focused vaccination messaging, outreach, and proactive reminders for patients with the highest-risk multimorbidity profiles, particularly during routine and chronic disease management visits. As recommendations have shifted toward risk-based clinical decision-making, identifying patients with particularly high-risk combinations of conditions is increasingly important to support targeted vaccination and timely antiviral treatment.
