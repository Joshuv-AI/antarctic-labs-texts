Coronary septic embolism presenting as acute myocardial infarction: A rare manifestation of infective endocarditis: a case report
Authors: Ahmad H Alhajj Mohammad, Nada Saleh, Alexie Freiji, Dina Badran, Naji Abi Rached, Christoph Sinning, John Kanakakis, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) secondary to septic coronary embolization is an exceptionally uncommon complication of Infective endocarditis (IE). It’s diagnostic and therapeutic complexity poses a unique challenge in clinical management.Case summaryA 79-year-old woman with a history of asthma, dyslipidemia, and breast cancer status post right mastectomy presented to the emergency department with acute STEMI secondary to septic coronary embolization caused by IE of both mitral and aortic valves in addition to the presence of perivalvular abscess. Coronary angiography revealed distal occlusion of the left anterior descending (LAD) artery, and because of the embolic and infectious nature of occlusion, balloon angioplasty without stent placement was performed. Our patient needed urgent surgical intervention; however, surgery was not feasible, leaving prolonged antibiotic treatment as the only possible option. Although treatment with antibiotics alone initially achieved clinical stabilization, the abscess persisted on follow-up imaging, and 2 months later, the patient developed cardiogenic shock and passed away.ConclusionThis case emphasizes the importance of having a high index of suspicion in the diagnosis of STEMI secondary to IE. It also highlights diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, in which early recognition relies on careful clinical assessment and appropriate imaging. Our case draws attention both to the limitations of antibiotic therapy when used alone and stresses the importance of continuous monitoring in patients who are not surgical candidates, although early escalation of treatment, including surgical intervention when indicated, is essential to improve outcomes.Keywords: Acute coronary syndrome, Infective endocarditis, Septic embolism, Perivalvular abscess, Coronary artery embolism, Case report

Introduction

Infective Endocarditis (IE) continues to carry substantial morbidity and mortality.1 Major complications include congestive heart failure (CHF), Peri-annular abscess formation, and Systemic embolization. Rarely, emboli reach the coronaries and precipitate myocardial infarction (MI).2 This is an uncommon (<1%) but clinically serious complication of IE and frequently presents in an atypical manner, complicating prompt recognition.3,4

Case presentation

A 79-year-old woman with asthma, dyslipidemia, and prior breast cancer s/p right mastectomy presented to emergency department with a 3-day-history of worsening dyspnoea at rest and orthopnea, and several hours of acute oppressive chest pain radiating to the left arm. She was febrile (38.5°C) and had a systolic murmur at the mitral area, diffuse crackles with reduced air entry, and bilateral leg oedema. ECG showed diffuse ST-segment elevation (Figure 1).

Electrocardiography demonstrating ST-segment elevation in leads II, III, aVF, V3, V4, V5, and V6.

Laboratory findings include: haemoglobin 8.2 g/dL, WBC 14 000/µL, Crp 88 mg/L, procalcitonin 0.18 ng/mL (<0.5), proBNP 7850 pg/mL, and cTn 93.8 ng/L (<14). Chest CT showed pulmonary oedema with bilateral pleural effusions and no pulmonary embolism. Given the high suspicion of IE, transthoracic and transoesophageal echocardiography (TTE/TEE) were performed, demonstrating severe destruction of the mitral valve, predominantly involving the A3 scallop, with a cystic structure consistent with an abscess and leaflet perforation causing severe eccentric mitral regurgitation. Vegetation was also seen on the non-coronary cusp of the aortic valve with severe regurgitation. Both valves were previously structurally normal. A mild posterior pericardial effusion was present (Figure 2). Coronary angiography revealed septic embolization of the distal LAD artery (Figure 3).

(A) Transoesophageal echocardiogram was performed which demonstrated severe mutilation of the mitral valve and mitral vegetations. (B)TEE showing vegetation on the non-coronary cusp on aortic valve. (C) TEE showing cystic structure on the atrial surface of mitral valve consistent with an abscess. (D) TEE showing the perivalvular abscess in 3D view.

Coronary angiography revealed septic embolization of the distal left anterior descending artery.

Three sets of blood cultures grew Streptococcus bovis. Although urgent surgical intervention was the optimal management, it was deferred due to high operative risk and family refusal because of low socioeconomic status and limited healthcare coverage. She was managed conservatively with intravenous diuretics, blood transfusions, and intravenous antibiotics. Empirical therapy was initiated with intravenous ceftriaxone (2 g every 12 h) in combination with ampicillin (2 g every 4 h). After 48 h, cultures confirmed Streptococcus bovis with high penicillin sensitivity (MIC ≤0.12), and ampicillin was discontinued while ceftriaxone monotherapy (2 g once daily IV) was continued. Clinical status improved, and she was discharged after 2 weeks on intramuscular ceftriaxone (2 g once daily) for an additional 4 weeks, with planned follow-up echocardiography.

One month later, she was readmitted with acute decompensated HF. TTE showed stable findings, and conservative management was continued with intramuscular ceftriaxone for another month. One month thereafter, she was readmitted with cardiogenic shock, deteriorated rapidly, and developed cardiac arrest. Given the poor prognosis, resuscitation wasn’t attempted, and she died.

Discussion

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) commonly results from plaque erosion or rupture. Less frequent causes include microvascular dysfunction, calcific nodules, spontaneous coronary artery dissection, and coronary embolism (CE) (4%–13%).4,5 CE is now classified as a category C subtype of type 1 MI rather than type 2.4,6 It is mainly associated with atrial fibrillation, cardiomyopathy, and malignancy4,7; however, IE is a rare but an important cause.

MI secondary to CE may result from compression of the coronaries by perivalvular extension of infection, mechanical occlusion of the coronary ostia by infected masses, or occlusion by embolic septic material.8,9

Risk factors for coronary embolization in IE include mobile vegetations, size >10 mm, and CRP >40 mg/L,10 all present in our case. Coexisting Systemic embolization occurs in ∼25% of cases, most commonly involving the brain (67%), limbs (25%), kidneys (25%), and spleen (4%).6 Common presentations include a new murmur (88%), leukocytosis (80%), fever (42%), anterior STEMI (63%), and systemic embolization (27%).11

ACS secondary to IE is associated with high mortality and occurs most frequently during the acute phase, particularly within the first 15 days.6 Aortic valve involvement, severe valvular regurgitation, extensive perivalvular disease, abscess formation, and infection with highly virulent organisms (Staphylococcus aureus, fungi) are associated with increased in-hospital mortality, whereas Streptococcus viridans infection and early surgical intervention are associated with better outcomes.12,13

S. aureus is the most commonly implicated organism in IE complicated by CE, our patient was had Streptococcus bovis. Vegetations typically arise from either the mitral or aortic valve, both were involved in our case.8 In a review by Nazir et al.11 including 100 patients with STEMI secondary to IE, LAD was most frequently affected, likely due to coronary anatomy and flow dynamics.8,11 Coronary angiography shows no underlying atherosclerosis in ∼95% of cases.11

Current ESC guidelines recommend the 2023 ESC modified diagnostic criteria for IE diagnosis. It requires integration of clinical, microbiological, and imaging findings.13 The 2023 Duke–ISCVID criteria can also be used.8 TTE and TEE are the imaging modalities of choice for detecting vegetations and perivalvular complications. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) and PET-CT provide additional diagnostic value, particularly in prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) and diagnostically uncertain cases.8,13 Coronary angiography may aid in the diagnosis of septic CE secondary to IE.11

ESC guidelines recommend prolonged intravenous antibiotic therapy as the cornerstone of treatment for IE. Empirical therapy depends on prior antibiotic exposure, native valve endocarditis (NVE) vs. PVE, and the setting of infection (community-acquired, nosocomial, or healthcare-associated), and the most likely pathogen. Regimens for NVE and late PVE should target staphylococci, streptococci, and enterococci. Once identified, therapy should be adjusted according to antimicrobial susceptibility. The usual duration is 4–6 weeks. Early surgical intervention is indicated in cases complicated by HF, uncontrolled infection, or large vegetations with a high embolic risk.8,13

Perivalvular abscess is a serious complication of IE, most commonly associated with PVE, particularly involving the aortic valve, and infection with S. aureus.13,14 Our patient had NVE.

The management of ACS due to septic CE secondary to IE is not standardized and is mainly based on case reports.8 It is more complex than in plaque-rupture ACS. Available evidence supports balloon angioplasty without stent implantation. Stenting carries higher risks of infection and thrombosis; therefore, a stentless strategy may reduce persistent infection, septic aneurysm formation, and recurrent embolization.4,10,11,15

The goal of intervention is to achieve TIMI grade 3 flow using the least invasive revascularization strategy, typically via guidewire manipulation with or without thrombus aspiration, combining mechanical fragmentation and chemical dissolution of the thrombus. A stentless approach is generally preferred.4,15 If TIMI flow remains <2 after aspiration, stent implantation may be considered. If TIMI flow is ≥2, intravascular imaging with intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) or optical coherence tomography (OCT) can assess for underlying plaque pathology.4,6 In the absence of underlying atherosclerotic coronary artery disease, dual antiplatelet therapy is generally not indicated.8

Our case describes STEMI due to septic CE, a rare complication of IE, associated with perivalvular abscess formation. It highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, where early recognition relies on careful clinical assessment and appropriate imaging. Although perivalvular abscess typically requires urgent surgical intervention, surgery was not feasible in our patient, and prolonged antibiotic therapy was the only option. Initial treatment achieved temporary stabilization; however, the abscess persisted on follow-up imaging and the patient died after 2 months.

This case underscores the limitations of antibiotic monotherapy and the need for close monitoring in non-surgical candidates. It also highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion and early treatment escalation, including surgery when indicated, to improve outcomes.
