Sphingosine Kinase 1 Aggravates Liver Fibrosis by Mediating Macrophage Recruitment and Polarization
Authors: Xin Ding, Xiang Zhang, Jiafan Cao, Shiyun Chen, Yinghua Chen, Kai Yuan, Bo Chen, Guizhi Yang, Shengwen Li, Jundong Yang, Guixiang Wang, Frank Tacke, Tian Lan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsSphingosine kinase 1 (SphK1) has distinct roles in the activation of Kupffer cells and hepatic stellate cells in liver fibrosis. Here, we aim to investigate the roles of SphK1 on hepatic macrophage recruitment and polarization in liver fibrosis.MethodsLiver fibrosis was induced by carbon tetrachloride in wild-type and SphK1-/- mice to study the recruitment and polarization of macrophages. The effects of SphK1 originated from macrophages or other liver cell types on liver fibrosis were further strengthened by bone marrow transplantation. The direct effects of SphK1 on macrophage polarization were also investigated in vitro. Expression analysis of SphK1 and macrophage polarization index was conducted with human liver samples.ResultsSphK1 deletion attenuated the recruitment of hepatic macrophages along with reduced M1 and M2 polarization in mice induced by carbon tetrachloride. SphK1 deficiency in endogenous liver cells attenuated macrophage recruitment via C-C motif chemokine ligand 2. Macrophage SphK1 activated the ASK1-JNK1/2-p38 signaling pathway to promote M1 polarization. Furthermore, macrophage SphK1 downregulated small ubiquitin-like modifier-specific peptidase1 to decrease de-SUMOylation of Kruppel-like factor 4 to promote M2 polarization. Finally, we confirmed that SphK1 expression was elevated and positively correlated with macrophage M1 and M2 polarization in human fibrosis livers.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrated that SphK1 aggravated liver fibrosis by promoting macrophage recruitment and M1/M2 polarization. SphK1 in macrophages is a potential therapeutic target for the treatment of liver fibrosis.Keywords: Liver Fibrosis, Macrophage Polarization, Macrophage Recruitment, SphK1

Summary

Liver fibrosis is a common pathophysiological event in diverse chronic liver diseases. Sphingosine kinase 1 promotes macrophage recruitment via C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 and enhances macrophage M1 and M2 polarization by activating ASK1-JNK1/2-p38 signaling pathway and decreasing de-SUMOylation of Kruppel-like factor 4, respectively, thereby exacerbating liver fibrosis.

Liver fibrosis is a common pathway in various chronic liver diseases, including viral hepatitis, autoimmune diseases, metabolic disorders, and cholestatic liver diseases.1 It is characterized by the replacement of the normal liver with collagen fibers formed by the extracellular matrix, leading to organ scarring.2,3 Liver fibrosis further deteriorates to cirrhosis or even liver cancer if left untreated.4 Therefore, it is important to explore the molecular mechanisms underlying liver fibrosis and develop effective therapeutic drugs and strategies for the prevention and treatment of liver fibrosis.

The liver is a central immune organ, and the most critical immune cells are the hepatic macrophages.5 The liver contains macrophages of diverse origins that exhibit high plasticity. The two most important types are Kupffer cells (KCs), which are resident tissue macrophages, and bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs), which infiltrate the liver upon injury and are critically regulated by the C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 and its receptor (CCL2/CCR2) axis.6,7 According to the differences in function, surface markers, and cytokines, macrophages can be broadly categorized into 2 polarized types of macrophages, including classical activated (M1) and alternately activated (M2) macrophages.8,9 Both M1 and M2 macrophages are significantly increased during hepatic fibrosis.10 M1 macrophages have a pro-inflammatory phenotype that responds to infectious stimuli including lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and/or interferon-γ (IFN-γ). Pharmacological inhibition of macrophage M1 polarization attenuates hepatic inflammation and injury, thereby hindering fibrosis pathogenesis.11,12 On the other hand, M2 macrophages promote tissue repair and fibrosis in response to stimuli like interleukin (IL)-4 and/or IL-13.13,14 Recent studies have demonstrated that M2 macrophages contribute to liver fibrosis by secreting transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), a master profibrogenic cytokine.15,16 These studies suggest that targeting macrophage recruitment and polarization may be a potential therapeutic approach for liver fibrosis. Therefore, it is crucial to identify the regulators of macrophage phenotypic changes and recruitment in liver fibrosis for the development of novel treatments.

Sphingosine kinase 1 (SphK1) is a key rate-limiting enzyme to maintain the balance of sphingolipid-rheostat, which participates in cell growth and death, proliferation, and inflammation.17,18 A recent study has shown that SphK1 modulates microglial M1 polarization and proinflammatory cytokine production via sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P)/ sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor (S1PR)3/p38 MAPK pathway in mice with spinal cord injury.19 Our previous study has demonstrated that the activation of SphK1 signaling in hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and KCs contributes to the development and progression of liver fibrosis via CCL2-CCR2 axis.20 However, the role of SphK1 in macrophage recruitment and polarization in liver fibrosis remains unclear. Here, we confirmed that SphK1 was elevated and positively correlated with macrophage M1 and M2 polarization in human fibrotic livers. SphK1 contributes to macrophage recruitment via CCL2 in endogenous liver cells. However, SphK1 in BMDMs promotes macrophage M1 and M2 polarization via Apoptosis signal-regulating kinase 1 (ASK1)-c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK)1/2-p38 signaling pathway and Kruppel-like factor 4 (KLF4) SUMOylation, respectively.

Results

Macrophage M1 and M2 Polarization Increased With Up-regulation of SphK1 Signaling in Human Fibrotic Specimens

To probe the immune microenvironment alteration in liver fibrosis, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis of liver tissues from 6 patients with fibrosis and 3 healthy donors. Liver damage and inflammation demonstrated by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, the fibrotic areas shown by Sirius Red staining and activated HSCs represented by α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA)-positive immunohistological staining were increased in fibrotic human livers compared with healthy controls (Figure 1A and B). Following gene expression normalization for reading depth and mitochondrial read count, a total of 41,814 cells were clustered into 13 clusters based on the gene signatures. These clusters were mainly identified by cellular gene markers of monocytes/macrophages, T cells, NK cells, liver sinusoidal endothelial cells, hepatocytes, B cells, cholangiocytes, and HSCs, respectively (Figure 1C–E). Our previous study has confirmed that SphK1 plays a key role in the development of liver fibrosis.20 Therefore, we detected the expression of SphK1 in all clusters. Interestingly, we found that SphK1 was highly expressed in monocyte/macrophage (Figure 1F and G). Therefore, we focused on analyzing the role of SphK1 in monocytes/macrophages. The tSNE plot revealed that 5 cell clusters (Mono-S100A8, MAC-APOA2, MAC-MARCO, Mono-HLA-DQB1, and MAC-HLA-DRB5) were named according to marker identification and merger. MAC-APOA2 and MAC-MARCO were primarily derived from fibrosis samples, whereas MAC-HLA-DRB5 was mainly derived from normal samples (Figure 2A and B). Noteworthy, we found that SphK1 was evenly distributed in all clusters of monocytes/macrophages, which could be divided into M1 macrophages (highly expressing IL1B, S100A8, S100A9, and C-X-C motif chemokine [CXCL] 8), including Mono-S100A8, M2 macrophages (highly expressing MRC1, CD163, and TGFB1), including MAC-APOA2 and MAC-MARCO, and intermediate stage of macrophages, including Mono-HLA-DQB1 and MAC-HLA-DRB5 (Figure 2C and D). Pseudo-temporal trajectory analysis revealed that the differentiation of macrophages from normal to fibrosis samples was accompanied by a transition from Mono-S100A8 to MAC-APOA2 and MAC-MARCO (Figure 2E). Furthermore, we simulated the trajectory of 4 typical genes (CD68, IL1B, MRC1, and SphK1) and found that CD68 expression was increased, whereas the expression of IL1B was decreased and that of MRC1 was increased, suggesting the skewing of macrophages from M1 to M2 phenotype in the progression of liver fibrosis (Figure 2F). Importantly, we found SphK1 was expressed during both stages of macrophage M1 and M2 polarization (Figure 2F), indicating that SphK1 might play a key role in macrophage M1 and M2 polarization.

To further examine the expression of SphK1 in patients with liver fibrosis and its relationship with macrophage polarization, we performed immunofluorescence staining on liver sections from patients with liver fibrosis; n = 11 subjects (healthy donors); n = 16 subjects (patients with liver fibrosis) (Figure 2G and Table 1). Dual-immunofluorescence staining showed that SphK1 was highly expressed in macrophages, and the co-expression of SphK1 with CD68 was increased significantly in fibrotic livers compared with control livers (Figure 2G). In addition, the messenger RNA (mRNA) levels of SPHK1, CD68, iNOS, and CD206 significantly increased in fibrotic livers (Figure 2H). Meanwhile, the mRNA levels of SPHK1 were positively correlated with the mRNA levels of CD68, iNOS, and CD206 (Figure 2I). Consistently, dual-immunofluorescence staining showed that SphK1 was co-expressed with inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and CD206 in the fibrotic liver of mice (Figure 2J). These results demonstrate the upregulation of SphK1 in fibrotic areas of human liver, alongside the activation and accumulation of HSCs and macrophages.

Deficiency of SphK1 Attenuates Hepatic Macrophage Recruitment and Polarization in Mice Induced by Carbon Tetrachloride

To investigate the critical function of SphK1 in macrophage recruitment and polarization in liver fibrosis, we generated SphK1 knockout (SphK1-/-) mice induced by carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) for 6 weeks. Consistent with our previous report, SphK1 deficiency significantly attenuated CCl4-induced liver injury and fibrosis, as shown by H&E and Sirius Red staining (Figure 3A and B). Immunofluorescence analyses revealed that CD68+ (all macrophages), iNOS+ (M1 macrophages), and CD206+ (M2 macrophages) cells were increased in the livers of wild-type (WT) mice induced by CCl4 compared with the control group. However, immunostaining for CD68, iNOS, and CD206 significantly decreased in SphK1-/- mice (Figure 3A and B). Furthermore, compared with CCl4-induced mice, SphK1 deficiency significantly reduced the mRNA levels of Cd68, M1 macrophage markers (iNOS, Il1-b, and Tnf-a), and M2 macrophage markers (Cd206, Arg-1, Ym-1, and Cd163) (Figure 3C–E). Flow Cytometry analysis revealed that the absence of SphK1 significantly attenuated the increase of BMDMs and KCs in the proportion of CD45+ cells induced by CCl4 in the liver. However, the reduction in BMDMs was more pronounced compared with KCs (Figure 3F and G). The reduced expression of CD68, iNOS, CD206, and arginase-1 (Arg-1) in fibrotic livers from SphK1-/- mice compared with WT mice was confirmed by western blotting (Figure 3H and I). Collectively, these data suggest that SphK1 deletion attenuates hepatic macrophage recruitment along with reduced M1 and M2 polarization in mice with liver fibrosis.

Deficiency of SphK1 Protects Mice Against Liver Fibrosis by Systematically Repressing the Pathways Associated With Fibrosis, Inflammation, and Macrophage Polarization

To understand the basis behind SphK1-attenuated liver fibrosis, we performed RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) on the livers of WT and SphK1-/- mice induced by CCl4. Principal component analysis of gene expression levels revealed distinct segregation between CCl4-treated WT and SphK1-/- mice (Figure 4A). From the volcano plot, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) accounted for 7.4% of the entire transcriptome. Among the 771 DEGs, 237 genes were downregulated and 534 genes were upregulated (Figure 4B). Next, the genetic effects of SphK1 on the main signaling pathways involved in liver fibrosis were investigated. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) pathway enrichment results revealed that cellular signaling pathways related to fibrosis, inflammation, and macrophage polarization were enriched and significantly downregulated in SphK1-/- mice induced by CCl4 (Figure 4C–D). Furthermore, the heatmap indicated that genes related to fibrosis, inflammation, and macrophage M1 and M2 polarization pathways were markedly regulated by SphK1 deletion (Figure 4E). In summary, these results suggest that SphK1 depletion attenuates macrophage polarization, hepatic inflammation and fibrosis induced by CCl4 in mice.

SphK1 in Endogenous Liver Cells Mediates Hepatic Macrophage Infiltration and Liver Fibrosis

To directly investigate the recruitment effect of liver endogenous SphK1 on BMDMs, we transplanted WT and SphK1-/- mice with bone marrow cells from enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) mice, which express green fluorescence and SphK1 protein. Subsequently, we induced liver fibrosis in these mice by CCl4 for 6 weeks (Figure 5A). SphK1 deficiency in endogenous liver cells markedly reduced CCl4-induced liver injury and fibrosis compared with controls, as demonstrated by H&E, Sirius Red, and α-SMA immunohistological staining (Figure 5B and C). Moreover, the number of EGFP+CD68+ macrophages remarkably decreased in SphK1 chimeric mice (Figure 5B and C). Similarly, in vivo optical imaging results showed a significant decrease in bone marrow-derived immune cells in liver following the deletion of SphK1 in endogenous liver cells (Figure 5D and E). Furthermore, SphK1 deletion in endogenous liver cells notably reduced the mRNA levels of genes related to fibrosis (Acta2, Col1a1, and Timp1) (Figure 5F) and macrophages (Cd68) (Figure 5G). In addition, immunoblotting results showed that the protein levels of α-SMA and CD68 were downregulated in SphK1 chimeric mice (Figure 5H and I). As expected, the serum level of alanine aminotransferase was significantly lower in SphK1-/- mice than in WT mice induced by CCl4 (Figure 5J). Together, these data highlight that SphK1 deletion in endogenous liver cells reduces the recruitment of BMDMs and markedly attenuates hepatic injury and fibrosis in CCl4-treated mice.

SphK1 Deletion in BMDMs Attenuates Macrophage M1 and M2 Polarization Rather Than Recruitment

To confirm whether the absence of SphK1 in BMDMs affected macrophage polarization, we generated SphK1-chimeric mice via bone marrow transplantation (Figure 6A). When WT mice were transplanted with SphK1-/- BM (SphK1-/- BM→WT), collagen deposition was diminished compared with that in WT BM→WT mice, as evaluated by Sirius Red staining (Figure 6B and C). Furthermore, the number of iNOS+ and CD206+ macrophages was significantly decreased in SphK1-/- BM→WT mice (Figure 6B and C). Consistently, quantitative reverse-transcription and real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) assays showed that the mRNA levels of fibrotic genes (Acta2, Col1a1, and Timp-1), M1 macrophage-related genes (iNOS, Il-1b, and Tnf-a) and M2 macrophage-related genes (Cd206, Arg-1, and Cd163) were also significantly decreased in SphK1-/- BM→WT mice (Figure 6D–F). Additionally, western blotting confirmed that the protein expression of α-SMA, iNOS, CD206, and Arg-1 was significantly reduced in SphK1-/- BM→WT mice, compared with WT BM→WT mice (Figure 6H and I). Unexpectedly, the hepatic expression of CD68 did not change in both groups (Figure 6B, G, and H). Taken together, these results suggest that SphK1 deletion in BMDMs attenuates hepatic macrophage polarization and collagen deposition, without affecting hepatic macrophage recruitment.

Deficiency of SphK1 in Endogenous Liver Cells Reduces Macrophage Recruitment by Suppressing CCL2 Secretion

To elucidate the potential mechanism by which SphK1 promotes macrophage recruitment, we further investigated the profile of cells highly expressing SphK1 by stratifying the cells in these clusters according to SphK1 gene expression. SphK1pos cells showed higher expression of genes related to macrophage recruitment and cytokine production (CXCL2, CCL2, CLU, DEFB1, and ALB) (Figure 7A). Chemokines are major mediators of macrophage chemotaxis.21 Monocytes/macrophages are recruited to the liver through the CCL2/CCR2 axis and are functionally important for perpetuating hepatic inflammation and fibrogenesis.22,23 To screen for SphK1-mediated chemokines involved in macrophage recruitment, we used the chemokine array to examine liver tissue lysates from SphK1-/- and WT mice induced by CCl4. Among these chemokines, CCL2, CCL27, and CXCL16 were significantly decreased in SphK1-/- mice compared with those in WT mice (Figure 7B). Consistently, the hepatic mRNA levels of Ccl2, Ccl27, and Cxcl16 in SphK1-/- mice were lower than those in WT mice (Figure 7C). As expected, the hepatic mRNA levels of Ccl2 in SphK1-/- mice transplanted with EGFP BM (EGFP BM→SphK1-/-) were markedly reduced compared with those in WT mice transplanted with EGFP BM (EGFP BM→WT) (Figure 7D). However, compared with WT BM→ WT mice, the hepatic mRNA levels of Ccl2 did not change in SphK1-/- BM→ WT mice (Figure 7E). To further examine the role of SphK1 in CCL2-mediated macrophage migration, THP-1 cells were treated with various concentrations of recombinant CCL2 (rCCL2). Transwell assay showed that the migration rate of THP-1 cells was dramatically increased by rCCL2 induction in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 7F–G). However, SphK1 silencing did not abrogate CCL2-induced macrophage migration (Figure 7H–I). Together, these data further demonstrate that SphK1 in endogenous liver cells is required for CCL2 secretion and macrophage migration.

SphK1 Promotes Macrophage M1 Polarization by Activating ASK1 Signaling

To reveal the mechanism of SphK1 in regulating the polarization of macrophages, we induced THP-1 cells and primary mouse BMDMs to M1 polarization with M1 inducers (LPS and IFN-γ), and M2 polarization with M2 inducers (IL-4 and IL-13). Immunoblotting assays showed that the expression of M1 marker (iNOS) and M2 marker (CD206) was significantly induced by M1 and M2 inducers, respectively (Figure 8A and B). Strikingly, the expression of SphK1 was remarkably upregulated in both M1 and M2 macrophages (Figure 8A and B). To investigate whether SphK1 mediates M1 macrophage polarization, we transfected M1 macrophages with SphK1 siRNA or plasmids. As expected, compared with the control cells, the mRNA levels of M1-related genes (iNOS, IL-1B, and TNF-A) in LPS and IFN-γ-treated THP-1 cells were significantly decreased by SphK1 siRNA, but increased by SphK1 plasmid, as examined by qRT-PCR (Figure 8C and D). Consistently, Western blotting also confirmed that the expression of iNOS was reduced by SphK1 knockdown, whereas it was induced by SphK1 overexpression (Figure 8E and F). We also validated that SphK1 deficiency downregulated the expression of iNOS in primary mouse BMDMs treated with LPS and IFN-γ (Figure 8G).

The results of GSEA indicated that the MAPK pathway was inhibited in the livers of SphK1-/- mice induced by CCl4 (Figure 4C and D), and previous studies have reported that MAPK signaling cascades are the central pathway in the M1 polarization of macrophages.24,25 Therefore, we further examined the effect of SphK1 on ASK1, a core upstream kinase involved in the regulation of the MAPK pathway in M1 macrophages. Immunoblotting assays showed that silencing SphK1 significantly reduced the phosphorylation of ASK1 in M1 macrophages. As expected, the phosphorylation of JNK1/2 and p38, the downstream signaling cascades of ASK1, was also abrogated by SphK1 knockdown (Figure 8H). In contrast, SphK1 overexpression remarkably induced phosphorylation of ASK1, JNK1/2, and p38 in M1 macrophages (Figure 8I). In addition, primary BMDMs from SphK1-/- mice were treated with LPS and IFN-γ to further confirm that SphK1 regulates the phosphorylation of ASK1, JNK1/2, and p38 in vitro (Figure 8J). Moreover, pre-treatment with selonsertib (GS-4997), a small molecule inhibitor of ASK1, almost completely suppressed LPS and IFN-γ-induced phosphorylation of ASK1, JNK1/2 and p38 in THP-1 cells after overexpression of SphK1 (Figure 8K). Furthermore, SphK1 overexpression enhanced the expression of iNOS induced by LPS and IFN-γ, but these effects were impaired by pre-treatment with selonsertib (Figure 8K). Consistent with in vitro data, we found that SphK1 deletion abrogated the activation of ASK1-JNK1/2-p38 signaling cascades in the mouse livers induced by CCl4 (Figure 8L). These results demonstrate that SphK1 mediates macrophage M1 polarization through activation of the ASK1-JNK1/2-p38 MAPK signaling pathway.

SphK1 Promotes Macrophage M2 Polarization by Inhibiting the De-SUMOylation of KLF4

To further verify the effect of SphK1 on macrophage M2 polarization, THP-1 cells were transfected with SphK1 siRNA or plasmids, and then cultured with IL-4 and IL-13. qRT-PCR showed that the expression of M2-related genes (MRC1, ARG-1, CD163, and IL-10) was significantly reduced by silencing SphK1 (Figure 9A). In contrast, overexpression of SphK1 further enhanced IL-4 and IL-13-induced mRNA levels of MRC1, ARG1, CD163, and IL-10 (Figure 9B). Consistent with the results of qRT-PCR assay, the protein expression of CD206 and Arg-1 in M2 macrophages was also decreased by SphK1 knockdown (Figure 9C), whereas it was further increased by SphK1 overexpression (Figure 9D). Interestingly, we found that the protein expression of KLF4, a subfamily of the zinc-finger class of DNA-binding transcription regulator, was significantly downregulated by silencing SphK1 (Figure 9C), whereas it was upregulated by overexpressing SphK1 in M2 macrophages (Figure 9D). We also validated that deficiency of SphK1 led to the downregulation of CD206 and KLF4 in primary mouse BMDMs induced by IL-4 and IL-13 (Figure 9E).

Previous studies have demonstrated that most target proteins are modified exclusively by small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO)-1 in vivo, which is the dominant SUMO type among the 4 representative proteins in mammalian cells.26 Furthermore, SUMOylation of KLF4 is induced by IL-4 treatment in M2 macrophages and plays an important role in the regulation of M2 polarization by enhancing Arg-1 gene transcription.27 Therefore, we performed co-IP assays to determine whether SphK1 affects the interaction between KLF4 and SUMO1 in THP-1 cells and primary BMDMs. KLF4 was conjugated with endogenous SUMO1 and SUMO1-modified KLF4 was highly enriched in M2 macrophages induced by IL-4 and IL-13. However, the SUMOylation of KLF4 was significantly reduced by silencing SphK1 in M2 macrophages (Figure 9F–G).

SUMO-specific peptidase (SENP)1 has been identified as a specific protease for KLF4 de-SUMOylation, we further examined the effects of SphK1 on the expression of SENP1.28 Interestingly, we found that SENP1 expression was increased in M2 macrophage by silencing SphK1. However, silencing of SENP1 by infection with SENP1 siRNA abrogated the inhibition of macrophage M2 polarization by SphK1 depletion (Figure 9H). Additionally, consistent with the findings observed in THP-1 cells and primary BMDMs, the absence of SphK1 resulted in a marked reduction in the expression levels of KLF4 and Arg-1, along with an elevation of SENP1 in primary hepatic macrophages (Figure 9I). Taken together, these results suggest that SphK1 regulates the stability of KLF4 by inhibiting de-SUMOylation of KLF4, thereby contributing to M2 macrophage polarization.

Discussion

Liver fibrosis is a common pathological change and an inevitable way for various chronic liver diseases to develop into cirrhosis and liver cancer.29 The pathogenesis of liver fibrosis is complex and without targeted drugs and effective treatment strategies.30 Previous studies have suggested that macrophages, a remarkably heterogeneous population are key drivers of fibrogenesis, fulfilling diverse functions in homeostasis, disease progression, and regression of fibrosis.31 The initiation and persistence of HSC activation are directly regulated by hepatic macrophages.32 In the liver fibrosis stage, under pathological conditions, BMDMs are recruited to diseased tissues and divided into 2 main types of macrophage polarization according to their function, surface markers, and cytokines, including M1 and M2 macrophages.33 In the present study, hepatic macrophages displayed an M2-skewed phenotype in the progression of liver fibrosis in human based on scRNA-seq data. A recent study also demonstrated that hepatic macrophages exhibit M2-preponderant activation in triclosan-induced liver fibrosis.34 Notably, SphK1 was highly expressed by macrophages and upregulated in both stages of macrophage M1 and M2 polarization. Consistent with these findings, SphK1 was co-expressed with CD68, iNOS, and CD206 and positively correlated with macrophage M1 and M2 polarization in human fibrotic livers. As expected, deficiency of SphK1 resulted in a significant reduction in BMDMs recruitment and hepatic macrophage M1 and M2 polarization induced by CCl4 in mice. These data demonstrated that SphK1 is a key regulator of hepatic macrophage recruitment and polarization in liver fibrosis.

Our previous study found that SphK1-/- chimeric mice that expressed SphK1 in endogenous liver cells (SphK1-/- BM→WT mice) and in BM-derived cells (WT BM→SphK1-/- mice) showed different alleviation of liver fibrosis.20 To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying such differences in the current study, we transplanted WT and SphK1-/- mice with BM cells from EGFP mice. We found that the deficiency of SphK1 in endogenous liver cells inhibited the recruitment of the BMDMs and resulted in a marked amelioration of hepatic injury and fibrosis. Interestingly, when we transplanted BM cells from SphK1-/- and WT mice into WT mice, we found that SphK1 in BM-derived cells did not affect macrophage recruitment. These results suggest that SphK1 in endogenous liver cells aggravates liver fibrosis in mice by recruiting BMDMs to the injured liver. Meanwhile, the contribution of SphK1 in HSC activation also is an important driver for liver fibrosis.20

Chemokines are critical for the recruitment and polarization of hepatic macrophages.35 Considering SphK1pos liver cells showed higher expression of CXCL2 and CCL2, we extracted liver tissue proteins from SphK1-/- and WT mice induced by CCl4 for chemokine array analysis to determine the change of chemokine expression in liver fibrosis and whether SphK1 regulates these chemokines. We found that only 3 chemokines (CCL2, CCL27, and CXCL16) were significantly decreased in SphK1-/- mice. Especially, CCL2 is one of the most elevated chemokines and is dramatically downregulated by SphK1 deficiency. Repression of CCL2 gene expression by SphK1 deficiency was further verified by the results of qRT-PCR assays in mice induced by CCl4. Previous reports have shown that CCR2 and its ligand CCL2 promote monocyte subset infiltration into the liver during liver damage, and the central role of CCL2 in macrophage recruitment is well-recognized.9,36 Our previous study confirmed SphK1 mediated CCL2 secretion in KCs, suggesting that SphK1 in KCs may regulate CCL2 secretion to be involved in the recruitment of BMDMs.20 However, when KCs were removed by liposomal clodronate, SphK1 in other liver cells, including HSCs, could still promote the secretion of CCL2. Studies have reported that SphK1 has a distinct role in the activation of HSCs,20 and the expression of CCL2 is increased in activated HSCs,37 indicating that SphK1 in HSCs may promote liver fibrosis by regulating the secretion of extracellular matrix and CCL2. However, the functional differences of SphK1 in different liver cells and their roles in liver fibrosis remain to be further studied. As expected, the migration of THP-1 cells increased upon stimulation with CCL2, whereas silencing SphK1 in THP-1 cells did not affect the chemotactic effect of CCL2. These results indicate that SphK1 in endogenous liver cells promotes hepatic macrophage recruitment through the CCL2-CCR2 axis.

The most important finding of the current study was the regulation of SphK1 in macrophage polarization. We have for the first time found that SphK1 was upregulated in both M1 and M2 macrophages. Furthermore, we validated the gene expression in THP-1 cells by overexpressing or silencing SphK1 and found that genes related to M1 and M2 polarization were markedly affected by SphK1. Based on the RNA sequence data, we found that the MAPK signaling pathway was inhibited in SphK1-/- mice induced by CCl4. The MAPK signaling pathway and its upstream and downstream pathways are involved in the regulation of inflammation and macrophage polarization, especially M1 polarization of macrophages.38 ASK1, an upstream kinase that activates JNK1/2-p38 signaling during inflammation and fibrosis progression, plays a vital role in the M1 polarization of macrophages.39,40 Studies have shown that SphK1 deficiency inhibits the phosphorylation of JNK1/2 and ASK1 in mice with APAP-induced acute liver damage and M1 polarization of microglial cells via the p38 MAPK pathway.41 In addition, inhibition of SphK1 suppresses caspase-1 maturation and il-1b release by repressing nod-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation,42 which is regulated by ASK1.42,43 Our study demonstrated that SphK1 promotes ASK1 phosphorylation and activation of its downstream JNK1/2-p38 signaling pathway involved in the M1 polarization of macrophages.

KLF4 is robustly induced in M2 macrophages in a Stat6-dependent manner and recruited to the Arg-1 enhancer region after IL-4 treatment to promote Arg-1 gene expression.27 A previous study revealed that SUMOylation of KLF4 was induced by IL-4 treatment to promote M2 polarization of macrophages.44 Here, we found that SphK1 deletion significantly inhibited KLF4 SUMOylation and reduced Arg-1 expression by upregulating SENP1.

Furthermore, mitochondria play a central role in metabolic rewiring during macrophage polarization.45 Dars2 encodes mitochondrial aspartyl-tRNA synthetase 2, which is critical for protein synthesis inside mitochondria.46 A recent study has reported that Dars2 deficiency in BMDMs diminishes the response to LPS + IFN-γ and IL-4 + IL-13, thus inhibiting macrophage polarization and activation.47 However, the role of SphK1 in promoting M1 and M2 macrophage polarization via mitochondrial function regulation requires further exploration.

In conclusion, our current study demonstrated the important role of SphK1 in macrophage recruitment and polarization in liver fibrosis, suggesting that SphK1 is a novel mediator of macrophage M1 and M2 polarization. Targeting macrophage SphK1 might be a potential therapy for the treatment of liver fibrosis.

Materials and Methods

Human Liver Samples

Human liver tissue samples were obtained from the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (n = 11 subjects, healthy patients; n = 16 subjects, patients with liver fibrosis. This study protocol conformed to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki. Experiments involving patient samples have been approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (IIT-2021-277). The characteristics of patients with liver fibrosis are listed in Table 1.

Animal

Specific pathogen-free C57BL/6 mice were obtained from the Medical Laboratory Animal Center. SphK1 knockout (SphK1-/-) mice were C57BL/6 background and donated by Richard Proia of the National Institute of Health. All mice were 20 to 25 g in weight, 7- to 8-weeks old, and housed in a specific pathogen-free facility with humane care according to the criteria outlined in the National Institutes of Health “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.” All mice were randomly assigned to control and model group. The control group was performed using an intraperitoneal injection of corn oil (5 ml/kg). The mice in the model group were injected intraperitoneally with 20% CCl4 (corn oil 1:4 diluted) 5 ml/kg, twice a week for 6 weeks. This study protocol of animal experiments was approved by the University Committee on Use and Care of Animals of the Guangdong Pharmaceutical University, Guangzhou, China (gdpulan2018062).

scRNA-Seq Data Analysis of Human Liver Tissue

Filtered gene expression matrices from Cell Ranger were used as input for clustering analysis by Seurat R package (version 4.3.0.1; R version 4.2.3).48 Genes that are expressed in fewer than 3 cells as well as cells that expressed fewer than 200 genes and more than 10% of mitochondria genes were excluded for downstream analysis in each sample. The harmony software (version 0.1.0) was used to evaluate and remove the batch effect in each subgroup.49 After integration, we ran a principal component analysis to perform clustering and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding embedding. Finally, we obtained a total of 13 clusters (resolution level = 0.5). Marker gene analysis was performed using the “FindAllMarkers” function (setting default parameters).

Reclustering of Macrophage and Monocyte

Macrophage and monocyte populations from all clusters were selected for myeloid reclustering. Deriving cells were reclustered using a resolution level of 0.3, producing a total of 5 clusters.

Single-cell Trajectory Reconstruction

Single-cell pseudotime trajectory was generated using Monocle version 2.28.0 (Seurat version 4.3.0.1).50 Expression data coming from Seurat (RNA slot) were uploaded in Monocle with their cluster annotation. Marker genes for each cluster were chosen to define cell progression and the “DDRTree” method (max components = 2) to reduce data dimensionality. After trajectory generation, the root state parameter was set to State 3 to define the starting point.

Bone Marrow Transplantation

Male C57BL/6J wild-type (WT) and SphK1-/- mice were irradiated with X-ray (10.5 Gy) for 6 hours. Liposomal clodronate (10 μl/g, Clodrosome) was used to remove macrophages from SphK1-/- or WT mice, followed by exogenous supplementation with an injection of 1 × 107 BM cells from donor mice. After a 2-month recovery, hepatic fibrosis was induced by CCl4 for 6 weeks.

BMDMs Isolation and Cell Culture

Femurs, tibias, and humeri were dissected in WT mice and SphK1-/- mice, and the shafts were flushed using a syringe with Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium supplemented with 100 U/ml penicillin, 100 μg/ml streptomycin, and 0.2% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The cell suspension was passed through a 40 μm strainer, centrifuged, and the pellets were resuspended in 3 ml of red blood cell lysis buffer, and then incubated for 10 minutes at room temperature. After centrifugation, the pellets were resuspended with 10 ml Dulbecco’s phosphate buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.5% FBS. BMDMs were generated in vitro with macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF, 20 ng/ml, PeproTech) as described, and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum. To polarize BMDMs into M1 and M2 macrophages, BMDMs were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng/ml, Sigma) and IFN-γ (20 ng/ml, PeproTech) or IL-4 (20 ng/ml, PeproTech) and IL-13 (10 ng/ml, PeproTech), respectively.

THP-1 cell line (human myeloid leukemia mononuclear cell) was donated by Prof Bing Liu of Guangdong Pharmaceutical University. There were no commonly misidentified for THP-1 cell line in the study. THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with penicillin-streptomycin and 10% FBS at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. THP-1 cells were induced with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (100 ng/ml) for 24 hours, followed by transfected with 2 μg of SphK1 plasmid or small interfering RNA (siRNA) (0.2 μM, Gene Pharma) using Lipofectamine RNAi/MAX transfection reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 6 to 8 hours of transfection, the medium was changed with fresh medium containing LPS (100 ng/ml) and IFN-γ (20 ng/ml) to induce M1 polarization, or IL-4 (20 ng/ml) and IL-13 (10 ng/ml) to induce M2 polarization.

Chemicals and Reagents

Alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase kits were obtained from Jiancheng Institute of Biotechnology. CCl4 was purchased from Macklin International Ltd. Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) was purchased from Invitrogen Corp. FBS was purchased from HyClone Laboratories, Inc. The penicillin/streptomycin solution, 0.25% trypsin-EDTA, PBS, and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were purchased from Invitrogen Corp. TRIzol reagent was purchased from TransGen Biotech. SYBR Green supermix was purchased from Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.

The secondary antibodies such as anti-mouse-horseradish peroxidase (HRP) and anti-rabbit-HRP antibodies were obtained from Promega. Alexa Flour 568 goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H+L) and Alexa Flour 488 goat anti-mouse IgG (H+L) were obtained from Invitrogen. Enhanced chemiluminescence substrate for the detection of HRP was obtained from Bio-Rad Laboratories.

Immunohistochemical Staining and Histopatholog

Liver specimens fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution were embedded in paraffin blocks. These specimens were sectioned for 4 μm and were processed using a standard immunostaining protocol. Immunostaining was performed on liver sections using standardized methods. Finally, the sections were captured by an Olympus BX51 microscope (Olympus Co). Liver tissues were routinely fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution (4 °C) for 24 hours, embedded in paraffin, and then sectioned (4 μm) for H&E and Sirius red trichome staining. The area of positive staining was measured in high-power fields on each slide and quantified using Image J. Antibodies used for immunohistochemistry were including CD206 (rabbit, 1:200, 18704-1-AP, Proteintech), CD68 (rabbit, 1:200, BA3638, Boster), iNOS (rabbit, 18985-1-AP, Proteintech), SphK1 (rabbit, 10670-1-AP, Proteintech), and α-SMA (mouse, BM0002, Boster).

Quantitative Real-time Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction

The total RNA of liver tissues or cells was extracted using a TRIzol reagent and then subjected to qRT-PCR. From the extracted mRNA, cDNA was synthesized using a PrimeScript RT reagent kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara). qRT-PCR measurements were performed using the SYBR Green Supermix. The primers used were described in Table 2. Genes were normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) or β-actin as an internal control.

Western blotting

Tissues and cells were homogenized in radioimmunoprecipitation (RIPA) lysis buffer. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 30 minutes at 4 °C. Then proteins were resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Millipore Corporation). Afterward, the membranes were blocked with 10% nonfat dry milk, followed by incubation with primary and secondary antibodies. Membranes were detected by Clarity Western electrochemiluminescence substrate (Bio-Rad) in conjunction with a chemiluminescence system (ChemiScope 3300mini, CLinX). Finally, bands were normalized to β-actin or GAPDH as the internal control. Antibodies used for Western blotting included Arg-1 (rabbit, 1:1000, 16001-1-AP, Proteintech), ASK1 (rabbit, 1:1000, 28201-1-AP, Proteintech), JNK1/2 (rabbit, 1:1000, IPB0275, Baijia), KLF4 (rabbit, 1:1000, WL02532, Wanleibio), p38 (rabbit, 1:1000, WL00764, Wanleibio), p-ASK1 (rabbit, 1:1000, 28846-1-AP, Proteintech), p-JNK1/2 (rabbit, 1:1000, BM4380, Boster), p-p38 (rabbit, 1:1000, 4511T, CST), Sumo-1 (rabbit, 1:1000, 10329-1-AP, Proteintech), SENP1 (rabbit, 1:1000, 25349-1-AP, Proteintech), α-SMA (rabbit, 1:1000, Bs-10196R, Bioss), β-actin (mouse, 1:1000, HC201, TransGen), GAPDH (mouse, 1:1000, HC301, TransGen). GAPDH and β-actin were used as internal control.

RNA-sequencing analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), and the RNA quality was assessed on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies) and checked using RNase-free agarose gel electrophoresis. Then, gene expression profiling was performed in Illumina Novaseq6000 by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. RNA differential expression analysis was performed by DESeq2 software between 2 different groups. The genes/transcripts with the parameter of false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.05 and absolute fold change ≥2 were considered differentially expressed genes/transcripts. Downregulated genes and upregulated genes of 5 datasets were then respectively intersected to obtain the candidate genes.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes Pathway Enrichment Analysis

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the online Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) tool. Pathways with P values of less than .05 were defined as significantly enriched pathways. For analysis of RNA-seq data, downregulated DEGs were used as the input for KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/).

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis

GSEA was implemented on the Java GSEA platform. For each KEGG biological pathway, the genes involved were defined as a gene set, and a ranked list and a ‘gene set’ permutation were then generated. Gene sets with a P value < .05 and an FDR value of <0.25 were considered statistically significant.

Chemokine Array

Tissues were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer and collected the supernatant after centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 30 minutes at 4 °C. The protein concentration was diluted to 0.7 to 1 μg/ml with sample diluent and determined by Quantibody Mouse Chemokine Array (QAM-CHE-1, RayBiotech) according to the standardized operation. The signals were detected by InnoScan 300 MicroArray Scanner and analyzed using Mapix.

Co-immunoprecipitation

For coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays, THP-1 cells were co-transfected with the indicated siRNA and then lysed with IP lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4; 150 mM NaCl; 1 mM EDTA; and 1% NP-40) containing an inhibitor cocktail. After centrifugation, the supernatant containing proteins was subjected to IP with protein G agarose beads and the indicated anti-tag antibodies overnight at 4 °C. For endogenous IP, cells were immunoprecipitated with the indicated primary antibody. The beads were washed with NaCl buffer and boiled with 2 × SDS loading buffer prior to analysis by Western blotting.

Transwell Migration Assays

THP-1 cells were induced with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate for 24 hours, then transfected with SphK1 siRNA for an additional 8 hours. Inoculate 200 μl of serum-free RPMI-1640 medium containing 2 × 105 cells into a transwell insert (for migration assay, 8 μm, Corning), of 24-well inserts, afterwards add 750 μl of conditioned media containing 10% FBS and CCL2 to the lower chamber as the chemoattractant. After 12 hours of incubation, the cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 1 hour, stained with DAPI, and captured by a microscope (Olympus BX53).

Statistical Analysis

All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SEM) and statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Statistical differences between the 2 groups were analyzed by unpaired Student t-test, and differences between multiple groups of data were analyzed by 1-way analysis of variance with Bonferroni’s correction. P < .05 was considered statistically significant.
