Addressing Perioperative Needs in Head and Neck Cancer: Piloting a Nurse Practitioner‐Led Preoperative Virtual Visit
Authors: Rebecca W. Gao, Abdullah Adil, Kaitlin Vance, Marie Leginza, Matthew E. Spector, Keith A. Casper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo improve understanding of the postoperative care challenges of patients with head and neck cancer and to help address these care needs before surgery.Study DesignQualitative study with intervention development and process testing.SettingTertiary care academic medical center.MethodsTo examine perioperative patient experiences, we first performed interviews of 28 adult patients with head and neck cancer undergoing resection with microvascular free flap reconstruction. We then developed a unique preoperative virtual visit focused on socioeconomic and perioperative needs. 50 visits were then conducted by advanced practice providers, and visit content was analyzed.ResultsBefore the new preoperative visit, 73% of patients did not plan for postoperative care preoperatively and 73% did not feel comfortable with postoperative care needs. 55% reported unaddressed socioeconomic hardship. Themes included: limited understanding of surgery and limited understanding of postoperative care needs. After the new preoperative visit, 62% of visits addressed significant care‐related concerns not previously identified.ConclusionAn additional preoperative virtual visit by an advanced practice provider may help improve surgical expectations and better prepare patients and their caregivers for perioperative needs.

The perioperative period is a critical window in patient care, and the importance of the perioperative experience and identification of barriers to care is increasingly recognized. Traditional models of perioperative management often rely on in‐person physician visits, which can lead to inefficiencies, care delays, and increased burden on both providers and patients.

There is growing interest in high‐value opportunities to integrate advanced practice providers (APPs) to expand our ability to provide comprehensive care. When combined with telemedicine, APP‐driven virtual care models may help enhance accessibility and improve quality of care. By addressing current gaps in perioperative management, this approach has the potential to enhance perioperative care delivery.

Given significant quality of life changes involved with head and neck cancer surgeries, this patient population can be especially vulnerable to perioperative challenges and miscommunications. The complex nature of the surgeries can limit patient and caregiver understanding of what surgery entails, including the involvement of multiple surgical sites, altered airway anatomy, and feeding tubes. Previous studies have shown that surgical patients in general have poor understanding of important, procedure‐critical details before their surgeries. 1 This can lead to significant perioperative challenges.

While perioperative care is discussed with the head and neck surgeon, these visits may not currently be sufficient. They frequently focus on oncologic management, leaving specific logistics and postoperative care details not fully addressed. However, allotting additional time may not be ideal either, as the amount of information may be too overwhelming for a single visit. 2 , 3 Discussions regarding oncologic care are often emotionally charged, and it can be difficult to process a large amount of detailed logistical information. This may lead to confusion regarding important surgical details.

Even with appropriate conceptual understanding of surgery and postoperative care, however, head and neck cancer patients may experience significant socioeconomic barriers affecting postoperative care and survivorship. 4 , 5 , 6 Patients with lower income and education status have worse overall and disease‐free survival, and this survival disparity may be driven by or significantly influenced by their perioperative care resources and abilities. 5

It thus becomes imperative to gain a better under understanding of the perioperative surgical experience for head and neck cancer patients to identify challenges and barriers to successful care. Currently, there is burgeoning interest on the patient‐reported perioperative experience across surgical specialties, as the importance of identifying barriers to care becomes increasingly recognized for improving patient outcomes. Nonetheless, research on the perioperative experiences of head and neck cancer patient population has been limited.

Our study thus sought to gain a better understanding of the perioperative surgical experience through qualitative patient interviews. Next, to target the identified concerns, we then piloted a novel preoperative virtual visit led by APPs to address care needs.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

Our study was composed of 2 phases: prospectively conducted patient interviews and retrospective review of preoperative virtual visits. The study was classified as exempt by the University of Michigan Institutional Review Board, exemptions HUM00227807 and HUM00267027.

Phase 1: Interviews

The first phase of our research involved qualitative, semi‐structured interviews with patients. To examine perioperative patient experiences, we performed a qualitative analysis of 28 patients at a tertiary care center. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they were 18 years or older and diagnosed with a primary malignancy of the head and neck necessitating surgical resection of their head and neck malignancy with free flap reconstruction and airway alteration (total laryngectomy or tracheostomy). These included composite mandibulectomies, total laryngectomies, hemi to total glossectomies, or larger maxillectomies.

Interview questions and guidelines were collaboratively developed between head and neck physicians, inpatient APPs, case management, and social work. All interviews were conducted by one researcher (RWG) for consistency. This researcher was trained in interviewing skills and qualitative research theory and was not a direct provider of medical care for the study participants. Patients were interviewed about their knowledge and concerns regarding (a) surgery, (b) postoperative care, and (c) psychosocial support. Interview development and study guidelines were conducted using the COREQ checklist. 7

For patients unable to phonate, the patients were given paper or an electronic device for typing. Audio interviews were digitally recorded with the patient's consent and transcribed verbatim. The transcripts were de‐identified per protocol before coding and analysis. The median interview length was 40 minutes. An optional, anonymous socioeconomic and demographic survey was also given, and all participants chose to complete it.

We then used grounded theory, a well‐established analytical strategy commonly used in qualitative research, to detect and explore themes identified in the patient interviews through a more rigorous and structured approach using a coding system. Initial analyses of the themes were compared between the researchers. A catalogue of focused codes was developed, and RWG and AA applied the coding scheme and thematic analysis to the interview transcripts. Themes were iteratively reintegrated into the code book through a constant comparative method. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or by a third‐person review. The data from the surveys were analyzed in Microsoft Excel (Version 16.72.3; Microsoft).

Phase 2: Preoperative Virtual Visits

The themes and analysis from the interviews were then used to develop goals and guidelines for a preoperative virtual visit. Visit content was collaboratively developed by head and neck physicians, inpatient APPs, case management, and social work. We then performed the second phase. 50 preoperative virtual visits were conducted by 2 APPs for 50 patients with head and neck cancer scheduled to undergo resection with free flap reconstruction. A retrospective qualitative analysis of the visit content was performed. Statistical analysis was performed in Microsoft Excel (Version 16.72.3; Microsoft).

Phase 1: Patient Understanding About Surgery

Patients were interviewed about their knowledge of key details regarding their surgery. Limited preoperative understanding of surgery was a recurrent theme noted in the interviews (Table 2). Subthemes included that the focus of preoperative surgical clinic visit centered around cancer removal. Patients expressed that they were often preoccupied with the importance of tumor resection that they did not think about perioperative care needs. Additionally, participants discussed another subtheme of feeling overwhelmed by information and their emotions. This contributed to a feeling of not knowing what questions they should ask or what additional information may be helpful.

Preoperative Preparation and Knowledge About Postoperative Care

Phase 1: Patient Knowledge About Postoperative Care Needs

Participants also discussed their level of understanding of postoperative care needs in the preoperative setting. 61% did not feel like they had a good understanding, and 73% discussed postoperative care challenges (Table 4). 86% of patients did not know they would need someone with them 24/7 to be considered a medically safe hospital discharge after a tracheostomy or laryngectomy. 73% of patients did not start making postoperative care arrangements until after surgery, and many reported a common theme of frustration with communication challenges while trying to coordinate care (Table 2). Around half of patients (43%) discussed with their caregivers where they planned to discharge to postoperatively. The majority of patients (86%) discussed with caregivers who would be designated as a medical decision‐maker.

The limited preoperative understanding and planning of postoperative care needs was encountered as a common theme. Specifically, patients expressed that they met unexpected challenges caring for a tracheostomy or laryngectomy stoma (Table 3). As one patient stated, “I was thinking that I could just go home after the surgery. But then with the trach and all the spit and the suctioning and everything, we didn't think that we could do it at home.” Another patient stated, “I was planning to go home at first, but it was too much for my wife…We didn't really understand how many tubes and bottles and things would be coming all out of my nose and face and everything….We didn't know about all that stuff, so we thought we would heal up most of the way and just be able to go home. But there [was] still so many details and suctioning and all that stuff. We don't know all the medical and technical stuff, so we wanted rehab instead, but then it took a long time to even find a rehab place close to us.”

Phase 2: Development of Preoperative Visit Content

In phase 2, the virtual visit content was collaboratively developed by head and neck physicians, inpatient APPs, case management, and social work to address socioeconomic and perioperative needs. Guidelines were developed, and all preoperative visits covered the following: (1) postoperative care expectation setting and teaching, (2) psychosocial support assessment, (3) discharge planning, (4) nutrition and functional status, (5) alcohol and tobacco use, (6) review of relevant medications and comorbidities.

Phase 2: Issues Addressed at the Preoperative Visit

50 preoperative visits were performed for 50 patients. Visits were performed on average 7 days prior to surgery and averaged 36.5 minutes in length. 54% were attended by one or more caregivers as well. All patients were able to utilize the virtual visit interface.

28% of these visits identified an issue that changed in‐hospital management, such as heavy alcohol use requiring early placement on an alcohol withdrawal protocol. (Table 5). 22% of visits identified information critical for discharge planning, such as limited social support at home. 8% of visits changed the patient's intended discharge location, such as from home to a skilled nursing facility. 22% identified a significant medication concern. One example was a critical medication for a patient's severe neurological disease which was not able to be crushed into a feeding tube, and an expedited discussion with the patient's local neurologist was initiated. 16% of visits corrected an important misunderstanding about surgery, such as informing the patient of a tracheostomy which they were previously unaware of. Overall, 62% of visits identified a significant perioperative care concern.

Discussion

Patients who undergo large surgeries for head and neck cancer may encounter significant postoperative care needs in the setting of complex socioeconomic barriers. We thus performed a qualitative interview study to better characterize and understand the perioperative surgical experience in patients undergoing complex ablations with microvascular free flap reconstructions. We also utilized the themes elicited from the interviews to collaboratively develop and pilot a novel, APP‐led preoperative virtual visit through multidisciplinary input.

Currently, there is limited qualitative characterization of the lived experiences and peri‐surgical needs in patients undergoing these large resections and reconstructions. While there are studies on patients with cancer and on peri‐surgical experience in other surgical specialties, research on our patient population is limited. However, a better understanding of peri‐surgical needs in this population may be especially important, as head and neck surgeries in particular can add an additional layer of postoperative care complexity. Not only are there wound care needs, but also new care requirements around core daily behaviors such as breathing, eating, and speaking.

Our results demonstrate an opportunity to improve preoperative counseling. Our institutional standard of practice is at least one preoperative visit with the multidisciplinary head and neck team, including one‐on‐one time with the surgeon, a speech language pathologist, and a specialized nurse cancer coordinator.

Given our study results, it is interesting to note that our Press Ganey patient satisfaction metrics are consistently ranked amongst the highest. In particular, in response to the prompt “my doctor kept me well‐informed,” the head and neck service is consistently rated one of the top 5, always measuring greater than the 90th percentile. This is important, as it suggests a potential significant mismatch in perceived patient satisfaction through Press Ganey or other quality metrics and patients' actual lived experiences with perioperative challenges. Only through more comprehensive interviews were these challenges appreciated. As such, there may be value for other institutions to also perform more in‐depth analysis of their perioperative practices, even with high patient satisfaction scores and a robust multidisciplinary surgical team.

Overall, 73% of participants reported that inadequate perioperative understanding resulted in patient and caregiver anxiety and amplified hardship in an already socioeconomically vulnerable population (1, 2, 3). The sociodemographic data from our study correlates with the literature on lower socioeconomic status and financial challenges in the head and neck cancer population. 4 , 5 , 8 , 9 While the number of study participants was limited, the socioeconomic data (including 46% earning $30,000 or less and the majority having completed only a high school degree equivalent or less) reflects our institutional experience. Additionally, in our study, third of patients lived alone and 25% lived over 2 hours away. Given this knowledge, earlier identification of patients with transportation or home‐care limitations can allow for earlier referrals to social work for care coordination.

Discharge delays can also be compounded by this patient population's limited ability to communicate postoperatively due to aphonia from altered airway anatomy (eg, laryngectomy). Our study participants expressed frustration that care coordination was exacerbated by the patients' inability to speak (Table 3). This suggests an even greater need for appropriate preoperative counseling while the patient can still communicate more easily. Pre‐emptive care coordination may thus improve patient and caregiver anxiety, avoid discharge delays, and potentially facilitate reduced inpatient stays.

We utilized the themes elicited from the interviews to pilot a preoperative virtual visit. Preoperative visits were performed by our APPs who work exclusively with our head and neck surgery patients on the inpatient setting. The utilization of APP expertise can limit the burden on surgeons who can focus on oncologic management and surgical planning during the already time‐constrained visits. APP‐led visits may also be more financially cost‐effective while also providing more comprehensive care than nurse‐led or patient educator‐led visits. Due to these visits being in the pilot study phase, they were not separately billed for during this study.

Our study additionally scheduled the APP visit after the initial visit with the surgeon and took place on average 7 days prior to surgery. This timing was chosen to reduce information overload and allow patients to process information and gather questions. Information overload may explain why patients report not being aware of key portions of surgery, despite data demonstrating that these topics were indeed discussed.

Given the decision to place the APP visit on a separate day, we chose virtual visits to limit the transportation and financial burden of an additional visit. This is particularly significant in our population, as 71% of patients lived over an hour away, and 25% lived over 2 hours (Table 1). Previous work on telemedicine has demonstrated patient interest and acceptance of the increasing integration of virtual care. 10

The preoperative visits were also able to assess for and address several perioperative concerns. 22% of visits identified information critical for discharge planning, 16% corrected an important misunderstanding about surgery such as whether a patient would have a tracheostomy, and, overall, 62% of visits identified a significant concern impacting patient care (Table 5). Our work helps explore why negative socioeconomic prognostic factors can have such a profound impact on patient outcomes—it influences patient understanding of surgery, support after surgery, and ability to plan for perioperative care needs.

While outside the scope of this study, future research may be able to identify if these visits affect length of stays, satisfaction scores, postoperative complications, time to adjuvant treatment, or postoperative patient phone calls or messages. Preliminarily, the average inpatient length of stay was 6.1 days for patients with the video visit compared to 6.5 days. However, due to the limited sample size and high variability of these variables, this study would not be able to draw meaningful conclusions.

Overall, the primary study limitations include the small sample size and convenience sampling. While the number of patients is comparable to similar interview‐based studies, the limited number is insufficient to draw powered statistical conclusions. We were able to quantitatively characterize the percentage of patients who experienced perioperative issues, but we cannot statistically establish confidence intervals for the larger patient population. Our sociodemographic information was also comparative to similar literature, but too small to be appropriately extrapolated. 5 , 6 , 8 , 9 Our demographic make‐up was majority White‐identifying, which is reflective of the broader head and neck patient population, but did not explore the impact of ethnic or racial identification on perioperative care understanding and needs. 11

Conclusions

Patients reported significant limitations in their understanding of surgery, challenges with postoperative care, and unaddressed socioeconomic hardship. An additional preoperative virtual visit by an APP may help improve surgical expectations and better prepare patients and their caregivers for perioperative needs.
