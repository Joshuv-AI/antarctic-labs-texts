National action on climate and health: developing a strategy – a case report from Argentina
Authors: Antonella Risso, Sol Saliva, Melina García Luciani, Carla Moretti, Cecilia Nicolini, Carla Vizzotti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionNational policies on climate and health were developed and strengthened on the public policy agenda between 2016 and 2023 in Argentina.Case presentationThis report examines the legal and policy framework established to connect the health sector to national and international climate change agendas, focusing on the strategies implemented by the Ministry of Health (MoH) and the State Secretary of Climate Change (SSCC) to develop joint policies on climate and health.DiscussionThe enabling conditions covered in this report include specific international funding available, political decisions as a fundamental requirement to ensure long-term commitment and institutionalization, leadership styles that encourage cooperation and cross-collaboration, national policies with a mandate to create the strategies and plans, and the technical capacity and legal framework already in place. In 2024, a deep political shift dismantled and dissolved a major part of the progress that had been made.ConclusionPolitical decisions and support, as well as transformational leadership and inclusive engagement, remain crucial for strengthening and maintaining effective climate and health policies. The lessons learnt from this experience are valuable for other countries in successfully developing and implementing a National Action Plan and strategy on Climate and Health.

Introduction

The growing evidence on human-caused climate change’s impact on health and livelihoods across all regions and socioeconomic contexts has become increasingly clear. The latest IPCC assessment highlights the alarming rise in climate-related illnesses, premature deaths, malnutrition, and threats to mental health [1]. In Latin America, these effects are particularly severe, with dengue cases rising significantly in recent years and an increasing percentage of the population exposed to extreme heat events, exacerbating health vulnerabilities [2].

Climate change profoundly affects public health systems through extreme weather, vector-borne diseases, and health inequities, necessitating robust public health strategies. To mitigate long-term health consequences, it is vital to prioritize climate action in public health policy with a comprehensive approach, including preventive measures, healthcare adaptation, and community engagement.

At the same time, to achieve the goals established in the Paris Agreement and according to the latest assessments, greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions must drop globally by 43 % by 2030 and 60 % by 2035, aiming for net zero by 2050 [3]. The healthcare sector, which contributes 4–6 % of global GHG emissions, plays a pivotal role in both the problem and the solution [4].

In 2023, the World Health Organization (WHO) updated its operational framework for climate-resilient and low-carbon health systems, outlining ten building blocks to guide its implementation [5]. A key element of this framework emphasizes the importance of climate-transformative leadership and governance. Argentina's collaboration with the WHO and its partners has been instrumental in integrating health into climate policies, paving the way for significant policy and strategic initiatives. However, implementation faced substantial challenges, including low awareness of the health impacts within the health sector and other sectors, and insufficient representation of health in climate change processes (WHO, 2021, p. 4) [6].

Nevertheless, by fostering specific responsibilities and accountability mechanisms for climate change and health within the Ministry of Health (MoH), embedding these priorities in key health policies and programs, and strengthening cross-sectoral collaboration, Argentina fulfilled the necessary criteria to initiate a Readiness project. This preparatory funding mechanism, provided by the Green Climate Fund (GCF) ,1 is designed to support countries in strengthening their national capacities to address climate change. Notably, Argentina’s project was the first of its kind (WHO, 2023, p.32) [4].

This case report documents and reflects upon the development of a climate and health policy framework in Argentina, seeking to contribute to the literature on the subject [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. The Argentine case between 2016 and 2023 is a clear example of how transversal and integrational approaches can be incorporated into the design and implementation of public policy, resulting in the creation of institutional spaces, regulations, projects, and programs focused on health and climate change. This report analyzes the challenges of raising and maintaining collective interest as well as achieving sustainable progress despite changes in administration [9].

Case presentation

In 2016, the first Nationally Determined Contribution2 (NDC) of Argentina was submitted, and the National Climate Change Cabinet was established to develop public policies and raise awareness about the risks associated with climate change [11]. In 2018, the National Response Plan to operationalize the NDC was implemented. In 2019, the Cabinet launched a participatory process to develop Argentina’s National Adaptation and Mitigation Plan. This process brought together ministries, provinces, the academic and scientific sectors, trade unions, the private sector, environmental organisations, and indigenous peoples. This was followed by the Climate Change Act No 27.520 in 2019, which established minimum environmental protection standards and aligned national efforts with global commitments, such as the Paris Agreement, by mandating the development of mitigation and adaptation strategies [12,13].

In 2020, the second NDC was submitted, and collaboration between the Ministry of Health and the State Secretary of Climate Change (SSCC) was consolidated through formal agreements and joint regulations, leading to the creation of the National Program for Reducing Health Risks Associated with Climate Change in 2021 (see Fig. 1). This program led to the development of a specific national climate and health strategy and the establishment of provincial climate and health cabinets in three provinces: Tucumán, Misiones, and Neuquén. This initiative also established the intra-ministerial Climate Change and Health Working Group, bringing together various areas of the Ministry of Health, such as the Environmental Health Directorate, the Vector-Borne Disease Control Directorate, and the National Center for Diagnosis and Research in Endemic Epidemics.Fig. 1Milestones in the development of a climate and health policy framework in Argentina.Fig. 1

This inter-institutional cooperation strengthened the integration of health and climate change, culminating in the creation of the Climate Change, Environment, and Health Unit and the Climate Emergency Response Center (CENDIES) at the ANLIS Malbrán Institute.3 These units, composed of specialists from diverse fields—including climatologists, anthropologists, and biologists—enhanced the monitoring and response to health emergencies exacerbated by climate change, operationalizing the One Health approach [14] at the national level.

In 2022, the Climate Change Cabinet’s efforts culminated in the completion and release of the National Adaptation and Mitigation Plan for Climate Change [15], in which health played a central, cross-cutting role. A special chapter was included, introducing the Health and Climate Change Action Plan as a key component of the overall strategy. That same year, the Cabinet’s External Advisory Council—established by law—began functioning with the participation of a broad range of stakeholders, further reinforcing the plan’s inclusive and multisectoral character [16].

In 2023, an updated National Action and Mitigation Plan for Climate Change4 was developed, incorporating health as a guiding principle and transversal axis in national climate policy rather than another sector [17]. The Ministry of Health played a leading role in broadening the scope of the Plan when it decided to convene for the first time a joint Federal Health Council (COFESA) of health ministers and climate change representatives from all the country's provinces. The plan established key objectives, including reducing morbidity and mortality associated with climate variability and creating a climate-resilient health system. It also promoted sustainable energy use in hospitals.

By strengthening the institutional collaboration between the Ministry of Health and the SSCC, Argentina also adopted and implemented the Safe Hospitals Initiative of the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), which aims to reduce the carbon footprint and improve health systems' resilience to natural disasters and joined the Alliance for Transformative Action on Climate and Health (ATACH), committing to the creation of a climate resilient health care system.

Between 2021 and 2023, the effective inter-ministerial collaboration led to the world's first Readiness program on climate change and health [18], and Argentina became one of the few countries to include the healthcare sector mitigation in its NDC [17], positioning health as a core element of its national strategy. National and subnational policies were developed through cross-collaboration across the climate and health State Secretary of Climate Change and the Ministry of Health, capacities were built, and climate action was effectively promoted.

In Argentina, the Readiness Project was the first of its kind to focus specifically on the intersection of climate change and health. It provided technical and financial support to enable the Ministry of Health to develop climate-informed health strategies, establish intersectoral coordination mechanisms, and pilot subnational implementation in three provinces: Tucumán, Neuquén, and Misiones. As such, it was a foundational element that catalyzed the national Climate and Health Action Plan and positioned Argentina as a regional leader in this policy domain. Building on this Project, the Ministry of Health led the development of recommendations for local implementation, reinforcing the commitment to making climate and health a state policy.

Discussion

Through the initiatives mentioned in the previous Section, Argentina offers an example for other countries in implementing the recommendation made by The Lancet Countdown on Climate and Health of adding attention to the climate and health connection and building intersectional policies in middle-income countries [2]. The COVID-19 pandemic exposed in Argentina the intersection between health and climate, revealing vulnerabilities in the health system's ability to cope with health emergencies related to the climate and environmental crises, poverty, and inequality. This manifestation highlighted the importance of health in government agendas, reminding that "without health, there is no development" [19] and therefore creating the opportunity to work within and across sectors.

International governance played a significant role in supporting Argentina’s efforts. Argentina's active participation in events organized by the PAHO, the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), and the WHO provided a platform to showcase its work, including the federal involvement in the provinces of Tucumán, Misiones, and Neuquén. The synergy between Argentina and these international organizations, particularly through the joint presentation of Argentina’s Readiness Plan, not only validated the country's approach but also highlighted the integration of regional efforts. This collaboration strengthened Argentina’s position within the global health community, demonstrating a coordinated response that bridged national and subnational levels.

The strategy aimed to attract international financing, focusing on support from global financial institutions such as the Global Environment Facility (GEF 8), the Forest Carbon Partnership Facility (FVC), and the Inter-American Development Bank (IDB). However, the disbursement of funds fell short of expectations, revealing the challenge of aligning expectations with outcomes in international cooperation. The lack of financial resources poses a critical barrier to the implementation of effective adaptation strategies. Efforts to secure international funding have not yet met the scale required to address the magnitude of the problem.

Securing internal support for the agenda was also critical. High-level political commitment, backed by concrete results, allowed Argentina to present a compelling proposal. However, implementing such a multi-sectoral and multi-dimensional public policy presented significant challenges. Balancing conflicting interests and securing support from key stakeholders required extensive coordination. A critical factor in this success was the ability of key leaders to personally engage with other ministers, building trust and fostering collaboration across sectors. This approach aligns with the concept of "transformational leadership," which emphasizes the importance of inspiring and motivating stakeholders through vision and trust [20]. One notable characteristic of the government was the influence of female leadership, which adopted a more horizontal leadership style [21,22]. This model fostered collaboration and inclusivity, which are essential for advancing a transversal agenda. Along with national policies with a mandate to create strategies and plans, it facilitated smoother communication between ministries, creating a unified approach to the complex intersection of climate change and health [23].

Public engagement, especially the involvement of subnational governments and civil society, played a crucial role. This aligns with theories of participatory governance, which highlight the importance of inclusive decision-making to enhance legitimacy and policy effectiveness [24]. By involving civil society as an integral stakeholder, the government fostered a sense of ownership and commitment, contributing to the long-term sustainability of the agenda. The engagement of the private sector created a more inclusive strategy, reinforcing its role in addressing global public health and environmental challenges [25]. Nonetheless, in spite of efforts to improve communication and ensure societal understanding and support, difficulties persist in the clarity and accessibility of messages, limiting the impact of policies on the general population.

Lastly, although the technical capacity and legal framework were already in place, the lack of data and capacity-building needs posed considerable obstacles in the development of evidence-based climate and health inter-sectoral policies.

Since 2024, the process has lost momentum, with significant setbacks in institutional and technical capacity amid shifting political landscapes. Achievements were largely dismantled as a result of a change in political administrations. The Ministry of Environment was abolished, and the Environmental Health Coordination within the Ministry of Health was dissolved [26,27]. By July 2025, Argentina had started the process of withdrawing from the WHO and was still considering withdrawing from the Paris Agreement.

The political decision to ensure the continuity of public policies and safeguard technical expertise is crucial for sustaining these achievements and addressing future challenges of the climate crisis. For this reason, a key lesson learned is to create legal instruments that transcend administrations. To achieve this, it is not only important to strengthen the institutional framework of such instruments, but also to encourage the involvement of society to raise collective awareness so that new administrations find barriers to the potential dismantling of public policies on climate change and health that are being implemented.

Conclusion

Argentina aligned itself with global initiatives and agreements on the need to address climate change-related health risks, demonstrating significant progress in adapting health policies to meet these challenges. Notably, the country advanced in fostering an intersectoral agenda that included collaboration with civil society and the private sector, showcasing a comprehensive approach to integrating health into climate change policies. The lessons learnt from this experience are valuable for other countries in successfully developing and implementing a National Action Plan and strategy on Climate and Health.

To secure the success and continuity of its climate change policies, Argentina must address key factors. First, establishing equitable and consistent financial support, both domestically and internationally, to bridge resource gaps. Robust inter-ministerial coordination is equally important to integrate climate and health policies across sectors. Furthermore, sustained investment in scientific research and technical capacity-building is critical for evidence-based policymaking. Ultimately, inclusive engagement with local and sub-national governments, civil society, and vulnerable communities will enhance policy relevance, acceptance, and long-term sustainability.

The previous consensus for sustained investment, strategic national coordination, and international collaboration is today under inquiry, jeopardizing the achievements described in this document to ensure the resilience of the Argentinian health system and population to climate change.
