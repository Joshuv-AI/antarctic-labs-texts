Context is key: how diagnostic and prognostic markers are reshaping gynecologic pathology
Authors: Martin Köbel, Anthony N Karnezis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe field of gynecologic pathology is undergoing a transformative change. The translation of molecular findings from large‐scale genomic studies into clinical practice has been facilitated by robust surrogate assays, particularly immunohistochemistry (IHC). These tools provide scalable molecular proxies with short turnaround times, enabling molecular subclassification and conclusive prognostic biomarker studies. This commentary highlights how prognostic markers now refine diagnoses in challenging borderline areas, where an abnormal result can help exclude benign or precursor entities. However, the utility of these markers is highly context‐dependent, modified by histotype and molecular subtype. Furthermore, the confident diagnosis of rare entities and the study of their precursors have been advanced by defining specific molecular and IHC profiles, opening new avenues for research and therapy. The integration of morphology with molecular features is increasing the robustness of diagnoses and dictating oncology management as never before.Keywords: gynecologic pathology, biomarkers, immunohistochemistry, prognosis, molecular classification, ovarian carcinoma, endometrial carcinoma

Over a decade ago, a common refrain at pathology conferences was ‘The H&E slide remains the gold standard’, a defiant claim from a profession fearing its own obsolescence. Today, we witness the enormous adaptability of our profession, which dictates oncology management as never before. How was this possible? Articles in the Journal of Pathology and Clinical Research over the last 10 years bear witness to this evolution and its underlying reasons.

Scalable molecular proxies with short turnarounds

The translation of molecular findings from large‐scale international genomic studies [1, 2] into routine clinical practice requires robust and practical surrogate assays. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) is an integral part of pathologic diagnostic work up with results usually available within 1 or 2 days. Hence, the optimization of key markers such as p53 IHC, ARID1A IHC, and CCNE1 chromogenic in situ hybridization (CISH) as accurate proxies for the underlying mutational or copy number status [3, 4, 5] paved the way for adequately powered, conclusive prognostic biomarker studies [6, 7, 8]. It also enabled the molecular subclassification of endometrial carcinomas especially through p53 IHC staining (also including POLE sequencing and DNA mismatch repair protein IHC) [9].

Prognostic markers refine diagnoses in difficult borderline areas

A decade ago, using prognostic markers for diagnostic refinement was anathema to diagnostic purists. The textbook definitions of diagnostic, prognostic, and predictive markers are distinct but, in reality, a given marker often has different flavors, and its interpretation is determined by the clinical context. For example, p53 is a prognostic marker in endometrial carcinomas but it can also be used as a diagnostic marker to support serous histotype or a p53 abnormal (p53abn) molecular subtype [9, 10]. Another example is abnormal loss of ATRX and DAXX expression by IHC as a proxy for underlying loss‐of‐function mutations in uterine smooth muscle tumors [11]. ATRX and DAXX are mutually exclusive alterations of the ATRX‐DAXX chaperone complex that is involved in the alternative lengthening of telomeres (alternative to telomerase). In an article by Slatter et al, the authors reported that all (6/6) smooth muscle tumors of uncertain significance (STUMP) that had an adverse outcome showed abnormal loss of either ATRX or DAXX. Therefore, these cases probably were not STUMPs and would be better classified as leiomyosarcoma. ATRX and DAXX IHC are now part of a molecular‐based immunohistochemical algorithm to confirm leiomyosarcomas [12]. Similarly, abnormal loss of CDKN2A/p16 is an unfavorable prognostic marker in low‐grade serous carcinomas [13]. Hence, a case with an indeterminate implant that shows abnormal loss of CDKN2A/p16 is probably a peritoneal low‐grade serous carcinoma and not a noninvasive serous borderline implant [14]. Patients with mucinous borderline tumors with abnormal p53 expression show lower survival rates [15]; hence, a high extent (>30%) of abnormal p53 may not be compatible with a diagnosis of mucinous borderline tumor if the morphology is challenging (e.g., significant degree of confluence, extensive areas of intraepithelial carcinoma, or adenofibroma pattern with moderate to high‐grade nuclear atypia) and consideration should be given to a diagnosis of mucinous carcinoma [16]. Hence, an abnormal finding of robust prognostic markers may help to rule out certain benign or precursor entities in challenging diagnostic situations.

Prognostic markers are dependent on the histotype or molecular subtype context

The prognostic value of a marker is not absolute but dependent on case inclusion, for example, by histotype or molecular subtype. Two publications in the Journal of Pathology Clinical Research showed that, while p53 and p16/CDKN2A are prognostic in ovarian carcinomas overall, they are not associated with survival within tubo‐ovarian high‐grade serous carcinoma (HGSC) [6, 13]. The overall association is driven by HGSC being the most common histotype, with the shortest survival and the highest prevalence of these alterations (p53 nearly 100%, CDKN2A block‐like 56%). The overall associations with survival are replicated in endometrioid and clear cell carcinomas but not in HGSC, where TP53 mutations are essentially a prerequisite for the diagnosis [6, 13]. Therefore, histotype is an effect modifier to prognostic biomarkers in ovarian carcinoma, and studies should analyze candidate biomarkers stratified for histotype [17]. It is noteworthy that, without stratified analysis, the aforementioned association of abnormal loss of CDKN2A/p16 with shorter survival in low‐grade serous carcinoma would not have been uncovered in unselected, aggregate analyses of all ovarian carcinomas.

In endometrial carcinomas, a study by Karnezis et al showed that PR offered further information in the no specific molecular profile (NSMP) molecular subtype, which is potentially very useful because it represents the largest molecular subtype with an intermediate prognosis [18]. Recently, ER has also shown prognostic value in the NSMP subtype, which somewhat confirms this finding [19]. However, this brings us back full circle because ER‐negative NSMP endometrial carcinomas are enriched for nonendometrioid histotypes (e.g., mesonephric – formerly mostly low‐grade endometrioid, and clear cell as well as dedifferentiated carcinomas – high‐grade). This again illustrates that the definition of a diagnostic or prognostic marker is context dependent.

Confident diagnosis of rare entities

A reproducible diagnosis is even more important for rare diseases to enable a more accurate study of their clinical behavior. For example, Tessier‐Cloutier et al showed significant survival differences among stage III/IV dedifferentiated/undifferentiated endometrial carcinomas dependent on whether an alteration in the SWI/SNF complex was detected (4 months median survival) or not (36 months median survival) [20]. This highlights the need for diagnostic scrutiny for cases without alteration in ARID1B, SMARCA4, and SMARCB1, as some of the SWI/SNF intact ‘undifferentiated’ carcinomas harbored a prognostically favorable POLE mutation [20]. Another rare entity, mesonephric adenocarcinomas, can occur in the cervix, uterus or ovary and, at least in the latter as highlighted in a recent study in the Journal of Pathology and Clinical Research, there seems to be a danger of underdiagnosis [21]. This diagnosis requires ancillary IHC support (if available), namely the expression of GATA3 or TTF1 with absent ER and PR. Emphasis has been made on using a low threshold when being confronted with a constellation of morphological clues to order ancillary IHC, which could also include PAX2 as a useful screening marker for mesonephric carcinomas, to avoid misclassification as endometrioid carcinomas given the former's unfavorable clinical outcome [21]. Endocervical adenocarcinomas are also uncommon, with HPV‐independent histotypes particularly rare. Shi et al reported that 15% of their 34 gastric‐type endocervical adenocarcinomas out of 209 endocervical adenocarcinomas in total showed ERBB2 3+ staining by IHC with confirmed amplification by fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) [22]. This compared to only 5% FISH‐confirmed amplified cases among 111 HPV‐associated endocervical adenocarcinomas, usual type [slightly higher rates were observed among HPV‐associated, mucinous type (10%) and 1/1 HPV‐associated signet ring cell carcinoma]. Since ERBB2 amplification was associated with stage III/IV disease, this may open therapeutic opportunities for this uncommon disease [22]. Another study on HPV‐independent endocervical adenocarcinomas shed some light on the prevalence of three immune checkpoint markers (PD‐L1, B7‐H3, and TIM‐3) [23]. While PD‐L1 inhibitors are in clinical use, clinical trials generally do not separate rare histotypes, leaving it uncertain whether effects are homogeneous across all subgroups. The authors reported relatively high but not ubiquitous prevalence rates, with PD‐L1 positivity in 76% of cases by combined positive score and 38% by tumor proportion score [23]. Carcinosarcomas are essentially metaplastic carcinomas in which the sarcoma component arises through epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT, or transdifferentiation) from the carcinoma component. Both components are clonally related, and mutational analyses did not reveal recurrent mutations that would explain the EMT [24]. Chui et al discovered amplification of GPC5 on 13q31.3 as a recurrent event in the sarcoma component of carcinosarcomas [25]. GPC5 belongs to the Glypican family, cell surface proteoglycans that can sequester ligands such as TGF‐beta, potentially influencing cell lineage differentiation. These studies on rare entities show challenges but also many opportunities for future research in this area.

Precursors are an understudied area

Verlaat et al showed in a straightforward study that PIK3CA exon 9 mutations, which are prevalent (37%) and associated with poor prognosis in cervical squamous and adenocarcinoma, are only rarely detected in high‐grade squamous intraepithelial lesion/cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) 3 (2%) [26]. This suggests that PIK3CA exon 9 mutations occur during malignant progression to cervical carcinoma, contrary to serous tubal intraepithelial carcinoma (STIC) where key alterations can already be detected, suggesting an early divergence of distinct molecular subclasses in HGSC [27]. Another study on STIC by Bogaerts et al used AI augmentation to support pathologists in making a STIC diagnosis that resulted in a 9% increase in sensitivity (from 82% to 93%) and increased efficiency (32% reduction in review time) [28]. While the standalone AI model was not yet clinical‐grade, the augmented AI approach demonstrates the technology's potential in clinical practice. Of course, increased sensitivity does not always translate into patient benefit and further study on the identification of high‐risk STIC/early HGSC is required [29].

Conclusion

The inception of the Journal of Pathology and Clinical Research coincided with an exciting era in pathology, where the integration of morphology with molecular features increases the robustness of our diagnoses. The journal has published several impactful open‐access articles in gynecological pathology and has become a go‐to journal for translational researchers in the field, bridging the gap between basic research‐focused journals and those with a more traditional morphology focus.
