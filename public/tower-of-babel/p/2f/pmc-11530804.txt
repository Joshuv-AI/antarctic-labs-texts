10-year natural carbonation data of concretes with limestone, fly ash, volcanic ash, and slag and exposed to tropical climate in India
Authors: Umesh Hule, Sundar Rathnarajan, Radhakrishna G. Pillai, Ravindra Gettu, Manu Santhanam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The adoption of supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) as a partial replacement of Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) is increasing in anticipation of reducing the carbon footprint of cement and concrete industry. The resistance of concretes with SCMs against natural carbonation (a reactive-diffusion mechanism) is still a topic of research. Most literature adopt accelerated carbonation tests (under constant humidity and temperature conditions) to estimate the natural carbonation depth (dCO2) in concrete. This does not always represent the actual scenario in the field structures. Moreover, accelerated carbonation conditions could result in significantly different pore structure than that are formed in the case of natural carbonation; hence, not always reliable to model the service life. Hence, there is a need for data on long-term natural dCO2 to help design reinforced concrete for a target service life. The data presented in this paper contains long-term natural dCO2 data of 45 different concretes prepared using various SCMs like volcanic ash (from Greece), fly ash, slag, and limestone (from France). The concrete specimens were prepared in France, shipped to India by air (in moist burlap and plastic cover), and then kept in sheltered and unsheltered exposure conditions on the terrace of a building in Chennai, India, experiencing tropical climate (warm and humid conditions). The data includes mixture proportions (type of binder, water-to-binder ratio, binder content, % SCMs, fine and coarse aggregate (river sand and gravel) content, and air content). In addition, curing time and compressive strength of concrete mixes are provided in Set A. Sets B and C consist of the natural dCO2 of concrete measured at various time instances between 1 and 11 years of natural exposure. Arguably, this is the first of its kind natural dCO2 database from a tropical climate zone. The natural dCO2 values were measured using a phenolphthalein indicator prepared according to RILEM CPC 18. This data can be used by researchers and practitioners to calibrate the existing carbonation models or to develop new models to estimate natural dCO2 for concretes exposed to tropical climates. Such models can help design concretes to achieve the target corrosion-free service life for reinforced concrete systems.

Background

This study aimed to evaluate the carbonation resistance of various concretes with different binder combinations (incorporating SCMs) and mixture proportions exposed to sheltered and unsheltered conditions in a tropical climate. The tropical climate refer to warm and humid conditions with average temperature ranging from 24° to 33 °C and RH levels of 65 to 84 % with annual rainfall of 1400 mm throughout the discussion [2]. As the carbonation of concrete is a relatively slow and reactive-diffusion phenomenon, most of the studies have adopted accelerated carbonation conditions to predict the carbonation resistance of concrete in a short time (say, within a few months). In accelerated carbonation conditions, concrete specimens are typically exposed to (65 ± 5 % RH, 25 ± 2 °C, and 3 % CO2). However, the natural CO2 concentration is only 0.04 %, and the accelerated studies could influence the carbonated phase formulations, which may not accurately represent natural phenomena. Hence, the long-term natural carbonation data will provide a more realistic carbonation resistance of concrete than accelerated carbonation. Prior research studies focused on collecting long-term natural dCO2 data for concretes, which were collected from laboratories and research centers in temperate climate zones [3]. The comparisons between the carbonation performance of concretes in temperate and tropical climate zones revealed that the latter facilitates faster carbonation progress than the former. Thus, the tropical climate being a critical environment for carbonation progress, more research is required to understand the performance of concretes made with different binder combinations. Vu et al. conducted an inter-laboratory study to measure dCO2 values for 45 concretes mentioned in this data set for a period of five years and published [4]. This paper shows the additional data from exposure of the same specimens for 10+ years. This updated dataset is valuable for calibrating existing models and creating new ones for estimating the carbonation depth of concrete. Such estimates can enable the designers to determine the adequate cover depth required in concrete for achieving target service life in concrete structures exposed to tropical climates.

Data Description

The data contains three sets. Set A comprises the data on material properties and mixture proportion (such as cement type (noted as CmS), water-to-binder ratio (w/b), binder content, percentage of SCMs content, sand and gravel content, and air content). Furthermore, each concrete compressive strength (fc) at 28 days, as per EN 12390-3, is provided. The curing time in (days) with the casting date (in Lyon, France) and exposure start date (at Chennai, India), are given. Set B contains the natural carbonation depths of concrete exposed to sheltered conditions, and Set C consists of the natural carbonation depths data of concrete exposed to unsheltered conditions. In brief, the data set will provide the material properties of 45 different concretes and their natural carbonation depths exposed to sheltered and unsheltered conditions.

Experimental Design, Materials, and Methods

This study used CEM I 52.5 N Ordinary Portland Cement (CmS) conforming to EN 197-1 and CEM I with different % of supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) content like volcanic ash from Greece (PoM) (M stands for Milos), fly ash (FaV) (V stands for Cordemais fly ash), slag (SgF) (F stands for Fos), and ground limestone (LS) from France. A polycarboxylate-based superplasticizer was used to attain workability between 160 and 210 mm. A total of 45 specimens were prepared by varying water-to-cement ratio (0.45 to 0.65), binder content (220–400 kg/m3), and % of SCMs content (between 0 % and 50 %). Set A specifies the duration and type of curing (wet burlap wrapped with a plastic sheet). After the curing, concrete specimens were subjected to sheltered and unsheltered exposure conditions at the Natural Atmospheric Exposure Site at IIT Madras, Chennai, India (see Fig. 1) [2].Fig. 1Natural atmospheric exposure site on the rooftop of the Department of Civil Engineering building in IIT Madras, Chennai, India.Fig. 1

The carbonation depths were measured on the freshly split surface of the prismatic specimens of the size (100 × 100 × 400 mm) (see Fig. 2) using a phenolphthalein indicator prepared with 1 % phenolphthalein powder diluted in 70 % ethanol and 30 % deionized water [5].Fig. 2Schematics representation of prismatic test specimen (t1, t2, etc. indicate test times).Fig. 2

The total 16 carbonation depths (dCO2 (mm)) were measured on each freshly fractured surface of the concrete specimen using a Vernier Caliper (see Fig. 3) in accordance with EN 12390-10 [6]. Limitations

The 45 concretes with different mixture proportions were adopted in this data from 0.45 to 0.65 regarding water-to-cement ratio, 220–400 kg/m3 of binder content, and 0 to 50 % of SCMs content and exposed to tropical climates in Chennai, India. The concretes with mixture proportions outside the scope of this data may not show the same carbonation resistance. As the carbonation of concrete is also dependent on atmospheric conditions, the carbonation depths in tropical climates cannot be directly used for carbonation depth prediction in other climatic conditions.
